## 12. Maren's Reckoning

The guild hall had burned twice in its long life, and both times the Fairweathers had rebuilt it on the same scorched bones, as if the mountain would forgive them for nothing less. Now it was burning a third time, and no one was coming to rebuild it.

Xanthe stood at the treeline and watched the smoke rise thin and gray into the late-morning sky. Not the roaring blaze of an ambush or a raid—this was a slow fire, a deliberate one, set low in the foundations where the peat stores used to be. Someone had wanted it to eat the hall from underneath, quietly, the way rot eats a beam.

"Is she still inside?" Rafael asked beside them. His burned hand was wrapped in clean linen now, Sera's work, but he kept flexing the fingers like he didn't trust them.

"The smokehouse chimney's drawing," Xanthe said. "See how it bends? Wind from the west, and that column's steady. There's a fire tended behind it." They paused. "She's cooking something. Or she was."

"You can tell all that from here?"

"I grew up in that smoke." Xanthe started down the slope before they could think better of it. "It's the only language my family ever spoke honestly."

Sera fell in on their left, bow strung, moving with the loose readiness of someone who expected the trees to be full of hunters. Brannoc brought up the rear with Pip riding his shoulders, her small hands laced under his beard, her eyes fixed on the burning hall with an expression far too old for her face.

"She hears bells," Brannoc rumbled. "I hear stone. And that hall's foundation is weeping, lass. Whatever your elder did down there, the mountain felt it."

Xanthe didn't answer. There wasn't anything to say that wouldn't come out as a wound, and they were saving all of those for Maren.

* * *

The great room was half-gone. The long table where deals had been struck for six generations lay charred down its middle, split like a wishbone. The map wall—every pass, every toll road, every smuggler's cache marked in Fairweather ink for a hundred years—had curled and blackened, the mountains themselves dissolving into ash. Through the hole where the roof had been, snow drifted down onto the embers and hissed.

Maren Fairweather sat at the table's surviving end, alone, with a single lamp and a ledger open before her.

Not just any ledger. Xanthe knew it at a distance of forty feet: oxblood leather, brass corners gone green, the spine cracked from a century of hands. Grandmother Odile's ledger. The one that lived in the locked drawer, the one Maren had told every apprentice—including Xanthe, especially Xanthe—was accounts only, boring as gravel, never to be touched.

She'd been reading it when they arrived. She didn't look up.

"You tracked ash on my floor," Maren said.

"Your floor's already ash."

That got her eyes up. They were the same gray as Xanthe's own, and always had been—the one inheritance Xanthe had never managed to refuse. "You brought a priest, a huntress, a dwarf, and a child who hears gods into the last house of our name. You brought the Crown's war to our door."

"The Crown was already at our door, Elder. You're the one who gave them directions."

Sera made a small sound—not quite a laugh—and moved to cover the doorway without being asked. Rafael stayed near Xanthe, close enough that his shoulder nearly touched theirs. Brannoc set Pip down behind a fallen beam, out of the line of anything, and the girl crouched there watching Maren with unblinking attention, the way she watched everything, as if reading it.

Maren closed the ledger with one hand, slow, deliberate. "So you found it."

"We found the copy you kept in the waystation strongbox. The real one's in front of you." Xanthe walked forward, boots crunching on burnt debris, and stopped at the far side of the ruined table. "Odile Fairweather. Winter of the Long Quiet. Payment received from the Office of Huntsmen: passage rights through three passes, a yearly stipend, and—" their voice caught, and they hated it, hated the catch, pushed through it— "and the terms of the covenant between the mortal realm and the old gods. Written out. Handed over. Sold."

"Yes."

One word. No flinch. Xanthe had prepared for denial, for rage, for the cold procedural cruelty Maren did so well. They had not prepared for *yes*, delivered like a figure being read off a manifest.

"Say the rest of it," Xanthe said.

"There is no rest of it. That's the whole of it." Maren rose, joints cracking, and laid her palm flat on the ledger. "You want me to grovel. I watched my mother grovel over smaller debts and it bought us nothing. So listen instead. When Odile signed that paper, this guild had forty members and eleven of them had starved the winter before. The Crown owned every legal road, the tolls were bleeding us white, and the old gods were *already going quiet*—do you understand? The silence had begun. The covenant was failing on its own. Odile didn't kill it. She sold the corpse before anyone noticed it stank."

"That's not what the letter says."

"The letter says what a frightened woman wrote to soothe herself. What I'm telling you is what's true." Maren's voice didn't rise. It never rose. That was the horror of her; she could order a man's death at a whisper. "The bargain kept forty people fed for sixty years. It kept *you* fed. Every road you ever ran, every coin you ever earned, every meal you ate in that kitchen—it all sat on top of that contract like a house on a grave. So don't stand there with your righteous face and pretend you'd have done differently. You've never once chosen hunger when there was a road around it."

The words landed exactly where she aimed them, because she'd had Xanthe's whole life to learn the aim. Xanthe felt the old obedience stir in their chest, the trained reflex of twenty years: *the elder speaks, the apprentice obeys.* They let it stir. Then they let it die.

"You're right," Xanthe said.

Maren blinked. For the first time in Xanthe's memory, the elder Fairweather looked uncertain.

"You're right that I've never chosen hunger. You're right that everything I had sat on that grave." Xanthe put their hand on the ledger, over hers, and felt her go rigid beneath the touch. "But here's what you never learned, because you were raised by a woman who taught you that survival is the only virtue: the covenant isn't failing because mortals stopped believing. It's failing because mortals stopped *choosing*. Nobody gives loyalty freely anymore—they sell it, or they inherit it, or they obey it. Odile sold ours. You inherited the sale and called it duty. And I—" Xanthe lifted their hand away. "I was raised to be the next link in that chain. Obedience wearing my grandmother's face."

Behind them, faintly, Pip began to hum. A bell-tone, thin and clear, threading through the smoke smell. Rafael turned toward her sharply, and Brannoc put a steadying hand on the girl's shoulder, but she only hummed louder, her eyes on the two Fairweathers across the ruined table.

Maren heard it too. Her jaw tightened. "You think that changes arithmetic? The Crown has a thousand hunters. You have a priest with burned hands and a mute child. When they take you—and they will—I'll have lost the guild *and* the stipend, and in ten years some other desperate family will sign some other contract, and nothing you did today will matter."

"Then it won't matter," Xanthe said. "But it'll be true while it lasts."

Something moved through Maren's face then—fast, quickly buried, but Xanthe saw it, because they'd been trained to read her since before they could read letters. Grief. Real grief, the kind that has nowhere to go.

"You sound like her," Maren said quietly. "Odile. Before the winter. Before the starving. She used to talk about honor the same way. Loud and certain and sure the world would make room for it." She straightened. "The world doesn't make room. The world takes the measure of what you can bear and charges you exactly that."

"Then here's my measure." Xanthe reached into their coat and drew out the thing they'd carried since the waystation, since before Rafael, since the shard first hummed against their ribs: the guild oath-token, iron and worn smooth, stamped with the Fairweather mark. Three generations of elders had passed it hand to hand. It had been pressed into Xanthe's palm at age nine with the words *you are the road now.*

They set it on the table between them, on top of the ledger, with a click that sounded enormous in the ruined hall.

"I release the elder line," Xanthe said. Their voice came out steady, which surprised them. "From me backward to Odile, from me forward to no one. The name Fairweather ends its claim on me, and I end mine on it. Whatever oath binds me to this guild, I unbind it here—because an oath given to liars was never an oath. It was a leash."

Maren stared at the token. Her hand hovered over it and did not touch it. "You don't get to unbind yourself. That's not how blood works."

"No," Xanthe agreed. "Blood doesn't work. That's rather the point. Blood is what Odile spent. What's left is what I choose—and I choose them." They tipped their head back toward the doorway, toward Sera and Brannock and the humming child and the priest who had trusted a stranger with a god's heart. "Every one of them chose me back. That's a bigger guild than yours ever was, Elder. It runs on a different currency."

"And what currency is that?"

"Loyalty freely given." Xanthe smiled, and it wasn't a kind smile. "You should recognize the phrase. It's from the original covenant. The one your grandmother sold."

For a long moment the only sounds were the settling of the fire and the wind coming through the broken roof, and Pip's bell-hum, rising now, insistent, like something answering from very far away.

Maren's hand finally closed over the token. Her knuckles went white around it. When she looked up, her eyes were dry and terrible and, beneath the terrible, relieved—relieved the way a prisoner is relieved when the sentence is finally read.

"Go, then," she said. "Take your truth and your found family and see how far it carries you past the solstice. But know this, child: I sent word to the Crown three days ago. Your route, your numbers, your faces. If you're standing here, it's because I told them the Widow's Bridge and not the ford." She swallowed. "I told myself it was to protect the guild's name. I no longer know if that was a lie I believed or a lie I told."

The floor dropped out of Xanthe's stomach. "You *sold* us."

"I *bargained*," Maren said, and the old cold came back into her voice like water refilling a footprint. "The difference matters to me. It may not matter to you, and that's fine. Go east. Go fast. And Xanthe—" for one heartbeat her voice cracked open, and underneath it was just an old woman in a burning house, "—don't come back. Not even to bury me."

Xanthe turned and walked out through the smoke, past Sera's grim face, past Brannoc lifting Pip to his shoulders again, past Rafael waiting with his burned hand held out—not asking, just offering—and took it.

Behind them, the guild hall settled another inch into its own ashes, and somewhere under the mountain, faint as a memory, a bell rang twice.

East, then. Toward the Widow's Bridge.

Toward whatever Maren had already set in motion.
