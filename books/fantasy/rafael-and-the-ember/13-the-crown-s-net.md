## 13. The Crown's Net

They found her at the edge of the Widow's Bridge, kneeling in the blood of the hunters she'd turned against.

Sera Vane had killed four men that morning. Good men, once—men she'd trained beside, eaten with, shared watch-fires with for eleven years. Corvin was still breathing when she knelt beside him, and he looked up at her with an expression she would carry longer than any scar: not hatred. Confusion. As if the world had simply failed to make sense one last time before it went dark.

"You were the best of us," he said.

"I know," Sera said, and pressed his wound closed anyway, because her hands hadn't caught up with her choices yet. He died holding her wrist.

Now she stood at the bridge's far end and watched the river take the red off the stones, and she did not pray, because prayer was what had started all of this—the miracle in the chapel at Hollowmere, the ember answering a dying priest's whisper, the light that had no business existing in a world where the Crown had spent two centuries teaching everyone the gods were dead.

The gods weren't dead. She'd seen them wake. And everything the Crown had built on their graves was a lie with a uniform on it.

She heard the riders before she saw them. Of course she did; she'd taught half of them to listen for hoofbeats on wet stone.

* * *

The High Hunter came alone to the parley, which was itself a message. Aldous Crane dismounted at fifty paces and walked the rest, unhurried, his grey cloak beaded with mist, his hands visible and empty. He looked less like a hunter than like a magistrate who'd wandered into the wrong story—soft-voiced, precise, with the kind of face you forgot on purpose because remembering it felt like being catalogued.

"Sera." He said her name like a key fitting a lock. "You look terrible."

"I've been sleeping outdoors."

"Among other indignities." He stopped ten paces away, close enough for conversation, far enough that neither of them could pretend this was friendly. "Four dead on the bridge. Two more wounded who'll live to file reports. I've read them. They say you shot first."

"They say true."

"Why?"

It wasn't an accusation. That was the worst part. Crane never accused; he inquired, the way a surgeon inquires into pain, looking for the location of the thing he means to cut out. Sera kept her hand loose near her knife and gave him the truth, or a shape of it.

"I saw something at Hollowmere. When we burned the order's chapel. I saw the god answer."

Crane's expression didn't change. "You saw fire behave strangely in a drafty building."

"I saw a man with half his chest caved in sit up and speak with a voice like a bell, and every candle in that chapel lit at once, and the fire didn't burn me when it passed over my arm." She pushed back her sleeve. The mark was still there—a pale streak across her forearm where the ember-light had touched her, cool as water, leaving nothing but this. Proof. Or delusion. Eleven years of hunting false miracles had taught her the difference, and this was the difference.

Crane studied the mark for a long moment. Then, quietly: "And instead of reporting it, you defected. Mid-pursuit. You put an arrow through Halloway's shoulder and vanished into the high country with a fugitive priest, a smuggler, a dwarf, and—" he consulted nothing, remembered everything, "—a child."

"Pip didn't choose any of this."

"No one chooses anything. That's rather the point of order." He clasped his hands behind his back. "Here is what happens now, Sera, and I'd like you to hear all of it before you decide how to die. Because those are your options today. You die here, and I write a report that costs your family their pension and your name its honor. Or—"

"Or you offer me a pardon," Sera said. "I've read this page before, Aldous. It always ends with the traitor delivering the fugitive and then discovering the pardon was conditional."

Something flickered at the corner of his mouth. Not quite a smile. Respect, maybe, from one professional to another. "Full pardon," he said. "Not conditional. Signed by the Lord Warden, countersigned by the Chancery. Your name restored. Your family's pension reinstated with arrears. And a commission—Hunter-Captain, third rank, which is as high as anyone has climbed from the border companies in forty years."

The words landed exactly where he'd aimed them. Sera let herself feel it—let the wanting rise, hot and shameful, the picture of her mother's face when the letters stopped coming marked *traitor*, the picture of her old cloak with her name stitched at the collar, the life she'd been good at, so good at, before a chapel full of impossible fire had broken it open.

"That's a lot of gold for one priest," she said.

"It's not about the priest." Crane's voice dropped, and now there was something underneath it, something almost human. "It's about the solstice. In nineteen days, either the realms sever and the Crown inherits a clean world, or they don't, and everything we've built for two hundred years comes apart in a season. I don't care about Rafael. I care about which side of that line the Crown is standing on when it breaks." He stepped closer. "You were the best of us, Sera. I'm offering you the chance to be the best of us again, on the right side of history, instead of a footnote in someone else's rebellion."

*Best of us.* Corvin had said the same thing with his last breath. The words were a net, and she could feel the mesh closing.

"And if I refuse?"

"Then I stop being polite." He said it without heat, which made it worse. "I have three hundred hunters in the Thornfells, sappers who can bring down the passes, and a standing order regarding the child that I have chosen not to read aloud. Refuse, and I read it."

Pip. The girl who heard bells, who drew maps of roads she'd never walked, who held Sera's hand on the bad nights and signed *safe* against her palm like a small warm promise. Sera felt the old machinery engage—the duty, the chain of command, the deep grooved habit of obedience—and watched, from somewhere above herself, as it strained against something newer and harder: the memory of fire that didn't burn.

She thought about Rafael, who had watched his entire order slaughtered and still spoke about the gods the way other men spoke about people they loved. About Xanthe Fairweather, who'd taken a stranger's cargo on faith and was learning, badly and bravely, to carry something heavier than contraband. About Brannoc singing to stone until the stone sang back. About Pip, asleep against Sera's ribs two nights ago, murmuring in a language no one had taught her.

"I'll need time," Sera said.

"How much?"

"A week. They move slow in the snow, and they trust me now—but trust is a door you can't kick open. If I come back too fast, too eager, they'll smell it. Give me a week and I'll deliver Rafael to you at the Emberforge approach, alive, with the smuggler's oath-bond intact so the guild can't shelter him after."

Every word was a coin she was spending from a purse that didn't exist. She watched Crane weigh each one.

"A week," he said. "And a token, so my captains know you're mine again." He drew something from his cloak: a hunter's badge, silver, stamped with the Crown's eye. "Wear it where they can see. If you're playing a deeper game than this, Sera—if this is theater—I will know. I always know. And then the standing order applies to everyone you've grown fond of, including the ones who only carry their packs."

He set the badge on a flat stone between them and walked back to his horse without waiting for her answer, because he already knew it. That was the cruelest part. He knew her well enough to know she'd pick it up.

She stood there a long time after he'd gone, the river running cold below, the badge glinting on the stone like a fishhook in still water.

Then she picked it up.

* * *

She caught the party at dusk, two valleys north, in the lee of a fallen watchtower where Brannoc had coaxed a fire from wet wood by singing to it—a trick that still unsettled her, though she was learning that most of what unsettled her was just wonder wearing work clothes.

Xanthe saw her first. The smuggler had sharp eyes and sharper instincts, and Sera watched them catalogue the badge on her collar, the direction she'd come from, the absence of pursuit behind her. Their hand drifted toward their belt.

"Don't," Sera said. "Or do. But hear me first."

"Hear you," Xanthe said flatly. "Standing in our camp. Wearing the Crown's eye."

Rafael rose from the fire, and the ember shard at his throat pulsed once, softly, like a heart noticing a change in the room. Pip looked up from the mending she was pretending to do, and her eyes went from Sera's face to the badge, and she frowned—not afraid, Sera realized. Reading it. The way she read everything, in silence, in signs.

"I met with Crane," Sera said. "He offered me a pardon, a commission, and my name back, if I deliver Rafael."

Brannoc's song cut off mid-note. The silence rang.

"And?" Xanthe said.

"And I told him I needed a week to earn your trust enough to betray it. Which is true. That's exactly what I told him." She held Xanthe's stare. "I'm telling you the same story I told him, word for word, so you know precisely what he believes is happening. He believes I'm his. He believes that in seven days I'll walk Rafael down to the forge approach with a hunter's escort waiting. Every route he gives me, every patrol schedule, every plan—he'll feed them to me thinking they're bait, and I'll feed them to you knowing they're real."

"You expect us to believe that," Xanthe said, "because you said it honestly."

"I expect you to believe it because you have no better option, and because I bled on a bridge for you four days ago, and because—" her voice cracked, just slightly, and she hated it, "—because I have nowhere else in the world to go, Fairweather, and I am asking you, against every instinct you have, to let that matter."

Nobody moved. The fire popped. Somewhere above the treeline, wind worried at the old tower stones, and beneath it—so faint Sera might have imagined it—the mountain hummed.

It was Pip who broke the stalemate. The girl crossed the camp, took Sera's hand, turned it over, and studied the palm the way she studied everything. Then she looked up at Sera and made a sign: two fingers walking, then stopping, then a circle drawn in the air.

*Road. Stop. Again.*

"She's asking," Rafael translated quietly, "if you're coming back to us. After you go to him."

Sera knelt. Up close, Pip smelled of woodsmoke and pine sap, and her eyes were the oldest thing in the mountains. "Yes," Sera said. "I'm coming back."

Pip studied her a moment longer. Then she took the badge in both hands, turned it over, and pressed her thumb hard against the Crown's eye—as if testing whether it would flinch—and gave it back. Her mouth twitched toward something that was almost a smile. She signed again, quick and certain: fist to heart, then fingers walking forward.

Rafael's breath caught. "She says—" He stopped. Started again. "She says the eye doesn't see what the heart keeps. Something like that. She says the gods told her."

"The gods told her," Xanthe repeated, in the voice of someone adding a new weight to a pack already overloaded.

"The gods told her," Sera confirmed, and pinned the badge back on her collar, and felt it settle there like a debt she intended to repay in full, with interest, to the other side.

* * *

The week that followed was the longest performance of her life.

Each dawn she left camp alone and ranged south, and each dusk she returned with intelligence: patrol rotations copied from memory, supply lines, the names of sapper officers. Twice she met Crane's couriers at prearranged cairns and handed over routes—false ones, crafted with Xanthe in long whispered sessions by firelight, the smuggler revealing a genius for deception that Sera suspected had been wasted on mere contraband. A pass that didn't exist. A cache of supplies at a ruin that had burned twenty years ago. A crossing of the Greywater that would drown a company in March meltwater.

Crane swallowed all of it. His replies came back prompt, trusting, detailed—which meant he believed her, which meant every hour she spent in his net was an hour the party gained, climbing north while the Crown's hunters combed empty valleys and drowned imaginary rivers.

But nets are woven from both ends.

On the sixth night, a courier's packet contained more than routes. It contained a single sheet in Crane's own hand, no salutation, no signature:

*The child travels with you. Confirm.*

Sera sat with the paper until the fire burned low. Around her the party slept—Brannoc curled against the warm stone like a cat, Rafael dreaming with the ember shard glowing faintly under his shirt, Xanthe on watch with their eyes on the stars and their thoughts, Sera guessed, on the road ahead. Pip slept closest to the fire, small and breathing, her sketchbook open on her knee to a drawing she'd made that afternoon: five figures on a white road, and above them, drawn in careful strokes, a large unblinking eye with a line struck through it.

Confirm. One word, and she understood the whole architecture of it. Crane didn't need her to deliver Rafael anymore—that was the pardon, the fiction, the leash. What he needed was the child. The gods spoke through her; whoever held Pip held the one channel the severance couldn't close. The standing order he'd refused to read aloud was suddenly very easy to imagine.

If she confirmed, the Crown would move—not on the forge, but on the camp. If she refused, or delayed, Crane would know. Six days of perfect theater, undone by a question with only one honest answer and no safe lie.

She looked at the badge on her collar. At the sleeping child. At the road going north, whiter now with each day's snow, running up toward a mountain that sang.

There was one lie left in her purse, and it had to be shaped exactly right.

At dawn she wrote her reply in a steady hand: *Confirmed. The child is with us. She is the reason I accepted your terms. Deliver her with the priest at the forge approach, solstice eve, as agreed. Do not move early—they have a watcher in the high passes I haven't identified. Early movement spooks them and we lose everything.*

It was good. It was even mostly true—there *was* a watcher; it was her. The false warning would pin Crane in place, force him to funnel everything through her, keep his three hundred hunters leashed for one more day.

One day. Maybe two. Enough to reach the forge, if the weather held and the mountain allowed it.

She sealed the letter, set it in the courier cairn, and walked back north through the falling snow with the Crown's eye on her collar and her heart hammering a countdown.

Behind her, far to the south, she could almost feel Crane reading her words and believing them, the way he'd believed everything, because she had been the best of them, once, and the best of them didn't lie.

Ahead, the mountain hummed against the wind, and the snow thickened, and somewhere above the clouds the Emberforge waited with a door that opened for a keeper of roads—and Sera Vane, double agent, traitor twice over, loyal past all saving, climbed toward it carrying the only cargo she'd ever stolen that mattered:

Time.

She reached camp at dusk on the seventh day, and Xanthe took one look at her face and said, "How long do we have?"

"One day," Sera said. "Maybe less. We climb tomorrow. All of us. Before he learns the child was never for sale."
