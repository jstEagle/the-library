## 14. Ascent to the Emberforge

The mountain began to sing three hours past dawn.

Xanthe heard it first as a wrongness in their boots—a tremor in the stone that wasn't a tremor at all but something closer to a voice, low and sustained, like a note held under the world's floor. They stopped on the narrow shelf of rock where Brannoc had called a halt and pressed one bare hand against the cliff face.

"It's louder here," they said.

Brannoc nodded without turning. The dwarf stood ahead of them on the path, broad as a doorframe, his braided beard crusted with frost, his pick slung across his back unused. He hadn't touched it once since they'd entered the high pass. "Aye. It'll get louder. Stone sings loudest near its own heart." He glanced back, snow catching in the gray of his eyes. "You all remember what I told you at the last camp."

"The mountain tests us," Rafael said quietly.

"The mountain *asks* us." Brannoc held up a scarred finger. "There's a difference, priest, though I grant you it won't feel like one when your turn comes. Every soul that climbs this way gets shown a thing. What they'd give up. What they'd give anything *not* to give up. And the mountain listens to how you answer it."

"And if we answer wrong?" Sera asked. She had her bow unstrung and wrapped—the singing stone, she'd learned, did not tolerate drawn steel—but her hand kept drifting toward it anyway, an old habit clinging like burrs.

"No wrong answers," Brannoc said. "Only honest ones and lies. Lies freeze you where you stand. Honest answers—" he shrugged, a rolling motion of massive shoulders, "—honest answers cost you something. But they carry you up."

Pip tugged Xanthe's sleeve. She signed quickly, small cold fingers shaping the gestures they'd all learned: *What does the mountain ask you?*

Brannoc watched the question travel and something moved behind his weathered face—something old and tired and fond. "Me? Forty years ago, girl, it asked me why I still sang to stone after my clan cast me out for it. And I answered: because nobody else would listen." He turned and started climbing again. "It seemed satisfied with that. Come on. We've light until midday, and the Forge Gate doesn't wait for stragglers."

They climbed.

* * *

Sera Vane heard her name in the stone before anyone spoke it.

Not her name, exactly—her rank. *Hunter.* The word rose out of the rock beneath her boots like heat off a sunstruck road, and with it came the vision, sudden and total, as if she'd stepped through a curtain she hadn't known was there.

She stood again in the courtyard of the God-Hunters' bastion at Cairnmoor, seventeen years old, kneeling on wet flagstones while the High Hunter pinned the silver arrowhead to her collar. Around her, the assembled ranks recited the Oath of Severance: *That which is divine shall be divided from that which is mortal; that which cannot be governed shall not be endured.*

She had spoken the words with her whole chest. She had meant them.

The vision shifted. Now she was in the chapel at Hollowmere, three weeks ago, standing over the bodies of Rafael's order while the ember-light still crawled along the walls like living script. Now she was watching a dying brother of the faith press his scorched hand to her wrist and whisper—not a curse, not a plea, just words: *They're waking up, hunter. We were never guarding against the gods. We were guarding the gods' silence, and the silence was never ours to keep.*

Now she was drawing her bow on Rafael in the scree fields, feeling the shot go wrong in her fingers on purpose, watching the arrow bury itself in stone a hand's width from his heart—and understanding, in that instant, that she had missed nothing. She had chosen.

The vision released her. She was on the mountainside, breath ragged, and the others had stopped ahead of her, watching. Snow on her lashes. Her hands shaking.

"Sera?" Rafael's voice, careful.

She looked at the stone under her feet. "It asked me what I'd sacrifice," she said. Her voice came out flat, hunter-flat, the voice she used for reports. "And I think—I think it already knows the answer. I already gave it. I gave them my oath, my rank, my pardon. Everything I built my whole life on." She laughed, short and joyless. "I keep waiting to regret it. That's my fear. Not dying up here. Dying up here and discovering I regret nothing."

"Then you've answered honestly," Brannoc rumbled from above. "Move."

But Pip crossed the snow to her first, took Sera's shaking hand in both of hers, and pressed it flat against the girl's own sternum. *Still here,* the gesture said. *Still ours.*

Sera exhaled, long and white in the cold. They climbed on.

* * *

Brannoc's turn came at the mouth of a ravine where the walls rose sheer on either side and the singing swelled to something almost choral.

He stopped so abruptly that Xanthe nearly walked into his back. Then the big dwarf went down on one knee in the snow, deliberate as a felled tree, and pressed both palms flat to the stone, and everyone understood at once—without being told—that this was his.

They gave him privacy the only way a mountain allows: by watching.

His lips moved. Whatever the stone showed him, whatever question forty years in exile had left unanswered, Brannoc answered aloud, in the old dwarvish, in a voice like gravel poured slow. Xanthe caught only fragments through the accent and the wind—*clan,* and *song,* and then, clearer, in Common, aimed upward at the peaks as much as at the god beneath them:

"I was afraid my people were right. That a song no one hears is worth nothing."

He knelt there a long moment. The ravine walls hummed. Somewhere far below them, deep in the mountain's throat, something answered in a register Xanthe felt in their teeth rather than heard.

When Brannoc rose, he was crying without any attempt to hide it, tears freezing in his beard, and he was grinning like a man who'd been told good news at a funeral.

"Well," he said, wiping his face with a forearm. "It heard."

* * *

Rafael's test found him in the early afternoon, on a ledge barely wide enough for two, with a drop on one side and a wall of blue ice on the other.

Xanthe was beside him when it happened—one moment walking, the next watching Rafael go still and distant, his burned hand pressed unconsciously against the ice as though steadying himself against a gale. His face did something terrible and private. Xanthe had seen grief before; they'd been raised in a guild that buried people. This was different. This was grief being *relitigated*, argued with, appealed to.

"Rafael." Xanthe gripped his shoulder. "Answer it. Don't fight it—answer it."

His eyes focused slowly, like a man surfacing from deep water. When he spoke, his voice was ruined.

"It showed me my order. All of them. Brother Aldous. Sister Maren—" he caught himself, huffed, "—no relation to yours. It showed them alive, at morning prayers, before. And it asked me—" his jaw worked, "—it asked whether I would trade the covenant's renewal for their lives back. Whether I'd walk away from the forge and let the realms sever, if I could have them breathing again."

"What did you say?"

"I said—" Rafael closed his eyes. "I said yes. I said yes, and I'm ashamed, and I said it anyway, because I loved them, and I am so tired of holy men pretending love is a smaller thing than duty." He opened his eyes and they were wet and fierce. "And do you know what the mountain did? It *sang louder*. It didn't condemn me. Xanthe, I've spent my whole life believing the gods wanted my death. What if—" his voice cracked, "—what if they never wanted sacrifice at all?"

Xanthe had no answer. But they kept their hand on his shoulder, and after a moment Pip appeared at his other side and leaned her small weight into him, and the three of them stood on the ledge together until his legs remembered how to climb.

* * *

Xanthe's turn came at dusk, and it came differently.

There was no vision. There was only a door.

It stood alone on the shelf of rock where Brannoc had called the final camp—an ordinary door, oak and iron, freestanding in the open snow with nothing behind it but mountain dark. Frost ferned across its planks. Xanthe knew it instantly, the way you know your own handwriting: it was the door to Maren Fairweather's study, down to the scratch on the lower panel where Xanthe, age six, had gouged it with a smuggled knife.

The guild hall door. The family door. Standing alone on a mountaintop, waiting.

"Don't," Sera started, and Brannoc put a hand on her arm.

Xanthe approached it slowly. Their breath came shallow. Through the door's seams seeped lamplight and the smell of pipe smoke and ledger-ink—the smell of every evening of their childhood. From inside, faintly, Maren's voice: *You're a Fairweather, child. The roads are our blood. You'll understand when you're older.*

Their hand hovered over the iron latch.

Behind them, the party waited. Rafael with his ruined hands. Sera with her broken oath. Brannoc with his unheard song. Pip, who had no home to be shut out of anymore except the ones they made together. Four people who had, one by one, chosen Xanthe—not the guild, not the mission, but the sharp-tongued, road-worn, terrified person standing in front of a door in the snow.

*Xanthe Fairweather,* the mountain asked, without words, in the deep humming of the stone. *What would you sacrifice?*

"Not them," Xanthe said aloud. Their voice sounded strange in the empty air. "I know what you're offering. A way back. The guild as it was. Her, as she was, before the ledger, before the betrayal. Everyone safe inside." Their hand dropped from the latch. "I'd sacrifice that. I'd sacrifice the door. Because the family I was born into sold the world, and the family I found on the road didn't sell anything—they *gave* everything, freely, and nobody made them."

They turned their back on the door.

Behind them it groaned—wood and iron sighing like an old man settling—and when Xanthe glanced back, the shelf of rock stood empty. Only snow. Only stars coming out over the peaks. Only the singing, which had changed key entirely: warmer now. Almost welcoming.

Pip ran to them and slammed into their middle, arms locking around their waist, and Xanthe held her and laughed, wet-eyed, at the absurdity of crying on a frozen mountainside over a door that had never really existed.

"You passed," Brannoc said. His voice was rough. "Come on, keeper of roads. The gate's close now, and it knows your name."

* * *

They reached the Emberforge Gate as the last light died.

It should have been grand. Instead it was almost humble: a seam in the mountain's face, taller than three men, its edges worn smooth as riverstone, glowing from within with a light like banked coals seen through a cracked stove door. Warm air breathed out of it, carrying the smell of hot metal and, strangely, woodsmoke and bread—as though someone, somewhere inside, had been keeping a hearth lit all these years for company that never came.

The singing had resolved into words. Not words in any language—words underneath language, felt rather than heard, and every one of them pointed the same direction:

*Solstice. Two nights. Keeper of roads. Open.*

Brannoc removed his cap. Sera bowed her head. Rafael went to one knee, and Pip pressed both hands over her heart the way she did when the bells rang.

Xanthe stood before the gate with their family at their back, and felt the ember shard in Rafael's pack pulse once, twice, like a second heartbeat answering the mountain's first.

Then, from far below—down the passes they'd climbed, through the dark they'd crossed—a sound rolled up the stone that none of them needed Brannoc to interpret.

Horns. Many horns. The Crown, come to finish what it started.

Sera's head snapped around, hunter's instinct flooding back into her stance. "They're two days behind us," she breathed. "They can't be. I fed them false routes—"

"They weren't following your routes," Brannoc said grimly. He was looking at the glow of the gate, and his face was shadowed. "They were following the mountain. It sings for everyone now, lass. It doesn't choose whose ears."

The horns sounded again, closer, and beneath the gate's warm light the party stood very still, listening to the future come up the trail after them.
