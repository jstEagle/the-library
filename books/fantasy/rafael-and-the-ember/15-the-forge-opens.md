## 15. The Forge Opens

The last door in the mountain was not a door.

Xanthe had spent their whole life reading stone the way other people read letters—knowing which faces of rock held handholds, which chimneys would take a climber's weight, which walls were walls at all. And so they knew, standing on the ledge where Brannoc had stopped singing, that what looked like a sheer face of black basalt was something else. Something waiting.

The singing had changed an hour ago. All through the ascent, Brannoc's voice and the mountain's had braided together, a low harmonic that lived in Xanthe's back teeth. Now the dwarf had gone quiet, and the mountain sang alone—and it was not humming anymore. It was listening.

"It knows we're here," Pip signed, her small hands quick in the torchlight. She'd been signing almost without stopping for two days, ever since the bells began again in her head, louder with every switchback. "It's been waiting a long time."

"Waiting for what?" Sera asked. Her arm was bound tight against her ribs where the bridge wound hadn't quite healed, but she stood like a woman who'd learned to stand around pain, one shoulder to the wall, eyes moving along the ledge the way she'd once moved them along treelines.

"Us," Brannoc said. He laid his palm flat against the basalt. "Or one of us."

* * *

They made camp on the ledge because there was nowhere else to make it—a shelf of stone barely wide enough for four bodies and a child, with a drop on one side that went down into blue dark and a wall on the other that hummed. The storm that had nearly killed them in the cave was three days behind them now, but its memory lived in all of them: Rafael's cough, deepening by the hour; Sera sleeping sitting up; the way Xanthe caught themself counting the party every morning like a smuggler counting cargo, terrified of coming up short.

Tonight the count came up right. Five. Xanthe let out a breath and passed around the last of the travel bread.

Rafael sat apart, as he had been doing more and more, his burned hands cradled in his lap. The ember shard hung at his throat in its iron cage, and even unlit it seemed to pull the light toward it, a little gravity of orange. He'd barely spoken since the visions in the ascent—the mountain had shown each of them something, and whatever it had shown him, he'd come out of it gray-faced and silent, and he hadn't told anyone what he'd seen.

Xanthe knew better than to ask. They were a smuggler; they knew cargo you didn't open until delivery.

But tonight Rafael spoke first. "I need to tell you all why we're really here."

Brannoc grunted. Sera went still. Pip put down her bread.

"The Emberforge isn't a forge," Rafael said. His voice had that quality it got when he preached—not the performance of it, Xanthe realized now, seeing it clearly for the first time, but the weight. The man carried things when he talked, and set them down carefully. "My order called it the heart of the covenant. The place where the pact between gods and mortals is renewed. Every hundred years, a keeper came here. Every hundred years, the realms held." He looked at the fire, not at any of them. "And I know what the renewal costs. I've known since before the massacre. It's why my brothers are dead—because the ones above me couldn't bear to say it aloud, and the Crown couldn't bear to have it said at all."

"Say it," Sera said quietly.

"The forge demands a soul bound by oath." Rafael finally looked up, and his eyes found Xanthe's and stayed there, and Xanthe felt the ledge tilt under them though nothing moved. "Not blood. Not coin. An oath—a promise kept across a lifetime, freely given, freely sealed. That's what holds the realms together. That's what's been failing. Not because the gods turned away. Because we stopped giving them anything true to hold onto."

The wind moved along the ledge. Below them, somewhere in the blue dark, ice cracked and settled.

"And your vow qualifies," Xanthe said. It wasn't a question. They'd known since the cave, since the confession in the storm-dark when Rafael had finally said it plain: *the covenant wants a death.* A priest's ordination vow was the oldest kind there was—sworn in fire, sworn for life, sworn unto death if death asked. Xanthe had lain awake beside him afterward doing the arithmetic every smuggler does with dangerous cargo: weight, distance, cost of failure.

"It qualifies," Rafael said. "It's the only reason I've been allowed to lead us this far. Vaelis has been patient with me. Kind, even. But the forge opens tomorrow, at solstice, and it opens for one soul, and I—" His voice caught, and he steadied it the way he steadied everything, deliberately, with visible effort. "I am ready. I've been ready since Thornfell. I want you all to know that. I'm not being dragged. I'm walking."

"You're not walking anywhere," Sera started.

"Sera." Brannoc's voice, low, stopping her.

Pip tugged Xanthe's sleeve. When Xanthe looked down, the girl's hands were moving, slow and certain. *"He's telling the truth. But the bells don't ring for him yet. They ring for someone else too."*

Xanthe didn't answer her. They looked at Rafael instead—at the burned hands, at the shard against his chest, at the terrible calm of a man who had organized his entire self around dying well—and felt something crack open in their chest that had no name they wanted to give it.

"I'll take first watch," they heard themself say, level as a ledger line. "Everyone sleep."

* * *

Xanthe did not watch the trail. There was no trail left to watch; behind them was three thousand feet of scree and storm, and below was the drop. Instead they watched Rafael sleep—or fail to sleep, turning and turning on the stone with his hands curled shut like two dying moths—and they watched the ember shard, and they thought about oaths.

Because here was the thing Xanthe had never told him. Here was the thing they had carried since the shard first hummed against their palm in Brannoc's tunnels, since Pip's bells rang for someone else, since the mountain showed them their vision on the climb and Xanthe had lied about it at camp that night with the ease of a lifetime's practice:

*A keeper of roads.*

That was what the forge opened for. Pip had drawn it, weeks ago, in a valley village: a figure on a road, roads branching from it like veins, and beneath it, in her careful silent alphabet, the words the gods gave her. And Xanthe had looked at that drawing and felt the ground open under their life, because there was exactly one person in the world whose entire soul was roads—who had been born into an oath of keeping them, whose guild's founding law was older than the Crown itself: *every road kept, every promise honored, until the road ends or the keeper does.*

Maren had taught them that oath at seven years old, made them speak it into a candle flame. Xanthe had thought it was a smuggler's catechism. It had never occurred to them—it had been made never to occur to them—that it might be a covenant.

The guild hadn't just run contraband. The guild had *kept* things. Old routes that were older than trade. Passes that were passages between worlds. And Maren's grandmother hadn't sold the covenant's secret to the Crown out of greed alone—she'd sold it because the guild had been the covenant's keeper, and someone had decided the toll was too high, and had spent three generations making sure no Fairweather would ever be in a position to pay it again. Sealing the passes. Closing the roads. Dismantling the oath one contract at a time until the guild was a dying smuggling outfit that didn't remember what it was for.

Xanthe understood it all now, understood it with the sick clarity of a lock finally yielding after hours of picking. And they understood what the forge would do tomorrow when Rafael stepped forward with his priest's vow burning in his mouth.

The forge would refuse him.

Not cruelly. But truly. Because his vow, however holy, was not the vow the covenant had been built on. The covenant was built on keepers of roads. It had always been built on them.

Which meant Xanthe had one night to decide what kind of person they were going to be.

Let Rafael try, and watch the forge turn him away—and then what? Step forward themselves, in front of everyone, and bind their life into the ember while the man they loved watched? Or stop him now, betray his choice, take the burden off him by force—which was its own kind of cruelty, because a man who had organized his whole soul around sacrifice did not thank you for stealing it. Or say nothing, let it play out, and gamble five lives and the world's hinge on a theory they'd pieced together from a mute girl's drawings and a dying god's hum.

Xanthe sat with the torch guttering and worked the problem like a route through a closed pass. There was no clean line up. Every path cost. That was the first truth of the mountains and the last truth of love, and nobody had ever told Xanthe those were the same truth.

Behind them, bare feet on stone.

Rafael came and sat down without asking, close enough that their shoulders touched. For a long time neither of them spoke. The mountain hummed beneath them like a struck bell that refused to fade.

"You didn't sleep either," Xanthe said.

"I slept enough." A lie, gently offered. "You've been quiet since the visions. Since before, really. Since the cave." He turned his ruined hands over in his lap. "You know what the mountain showed me?"

"No."

"A funeral," he said. "Mine. Except nobody was mourning. They were building something. Roads, bridges, a town in a pass. And I was—" His breath shuddered. "I was ash on the wind, and the world was fine. Better than fine. And I woke up knowing it was worth it, and I have never in my life hated a vision so much, because it took away the one thing I had left to be afraid of."

Xanthe's throat was tight. "Rafael—"

"Don't." He said it softly. "Whatever you're about to say—don't. Tomorrow is mine. You gave me that in the cave when you didn't turn back, and I won't survive you taking it from me now. Let me try. If the forge refuses me, then we'll know something new, and we'll face it together. But if you stop me—if you spend yourself buying me out—I will walk into that mountain hating you, and I can't do that. I can't carry you and this both." He laughed, wet and brief. "Priest's training. We're very good at making our martyrdoms sound reasonable."

"They are unreasonable," Xanthe said. "All of them. Every martyrdom ever. They're just grief wearing vestments."

"Probably." He leaned his head back against the basalt, and the hum of the mountain ran through both of them through the point where their shoulders met, like a second heartbeat slightly out of time. "Ask me anyway. Ask me if I'm sure. Everyone wants to ask."

"Are you sure?"

"Yes." No hesitation at all. That was the worst part. "Are you?"

The question landed like a knife slipped between ribs—clean, deep, meant. Xanthe stared out into the blue dark and did not lie, because lying to Rafael had become impossible somewhere on the scree fields, somewhere in the cave, somewhere in the long slow fall they'd both been taking since Thornfell.

"No," they said. "I'm not sure of anything except that losing you is unacceptable, and the world doesn't get to make it acceptable just because it's holy."

Rafael was quiet a long moment. Then he reached over with his burned hand—the left one, the worse one—and took Xanthe's fingers, careful of grip, generous of pressure. His skin was hot as banked coals. It had been getting hotter for days. Xanthe had told themself it was the fever. It was not the fever.

"Then we understand each other," he whispered. "Get some sleep, smuggler. Tomorrow we open a door."

He went back to his bedroll. Xanthe stayed on watch, holding the hand-shaped heat in their own hand, and watched the horizon east go from black to iron to the faintest bruised suggestion of dawn, and made their decision the way they made all their worst decisions: completely, and in advance, and alone.

* * *

Solstice morning came clear and cold, the sky scoured to a hard high blue, and the mountain sang like a choir with one note.

The door was not a door until it was. As the sun cleared the eastern ridge and struck the basalt face, the black stone began to change—veins of ember-light spreading through it like frost in reverse, like fire remembering its shape. Brannoc sang the low rising phrase he'd been teaching himself from the stone all week, and the face of the mountain drew apart along lines no chisel had ever cut, folding inward, opening, revealing a corridor of warm gold light and air that smelled of hot metal and, impossibly, of summer.

Pip grabbed Xanthe's hand and signed fast, urgent: *"It's asking. It's asking who comes bound. It wants the answer given inside."*

"Then we go inside," Xanthe said.

They went in single file—Brannoc first, singing the stone wide, then Sera with her bow strung despite everything, then Pip clutching Xanthe's belt, then Xanthe, then Rafael last, and Xanthe hated the geometry of that, hated having him at their back where they couldn't see his face.

The corridor opened into a cavern that no description had prepared them for. It was vast and it was intimate at once, a hollow heart of stone ribbed like a furnace, and in its center, suspended above a basin of molten light, hung the source of everything—the great ember, the first coal, the heart Vaelis had pressed out of his own chest when the worlds were young and handed to mortal hands saying *keep this lit.* Its light was not bright so much as total, filling every shadow, touching every face, and in its light Xanthe could see the others clearly: Sera's jaw set like a woman marching to a battle she'd already lost once; Brannoc on his knees, tears cutting through the dust on his face; Pip standing very straight, listening to something with her whole body.

And Rafael walked forward. Of course he did. He walked forward the way he'd done everything since Thornfell—with that deliberate, carrying weight, setting down what he brought.

He stopped at the edge of the basin and lifted his burned hands, palms out, and his voice filled the cavern the way it filled a chapel.

"Vaelis, heart-of-the-mountain, hearer of prayers. I am Rafael of the Order of the Undying Flame, last of my brothers. I come bound by my vow—sworn in fire at my ordination, sworn for my life, sworn unto death if death should ask. I offer it freely. Take it, and let the covenant be renewed."

The great ember pulsed.

And then it dimmed.

Not rejection, exactly. Not violence. Something sadder. The light drew back from Rafael like a tide withdrawing, and the warmth in the cavern cooled ten degrees, and Pip made a small broken sound and covered her mouth with both hands.

*"It's not refusing him,"* she signed, tears streaming, hands shaking. *"It's grieving him. It says his vow is beautiful and it isn't the vow. It says—"*

She stopped. Her eyes came up. Found Xanthe.

And Rafael turned too, slowly, following her gaze, and Xanthe watched him understand in real time—watched the color leave his face, watched his mouth form the word *no* without any sound coming out of it, watched three thousand feet of climb and one storm-bound night of honesty collapse into betrayal in his eyes.

"You knew," Rafael said. His voice was very quiet. "The drawing. The bells. You knew it was never going to be me."

The ember pulsed again—slow, patient, ancient—and the heat of it pressed against Xanthe's skin like a hand, and every road they had ever walked rose up in them at once: every pass, every promise, every candlelit catechism of a childhood oath spoken into flame.

Xanthe stepped forward.

"Every road kept," they said, "every promise honored—"

"Xanthe, *don't*—"

"—until the road ends," Xanthe said, "or the keeper does."

And the forge opened like an eye.
