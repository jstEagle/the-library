## 16. Vaelis Waking

The forge took him the way a river takes a leaf.

Rafael had expected fire. He had expected, if he was honest with himself, an ending—something with weight to it, something he could point to afterward and say *there, that was the moment I paid the debt*. Priests are trained for endings. We rehearse them. We practice our deaths the way soldiers drill with blunt swords, over and over, until the real thing feels almost familiar.

What he got instead was warmth.

Not heat—the forge's heat had been enormous, pressing against his skin like a hand as Xanthe pushed open the door of iron and singing stone—but warmth, the kind that comes from a hearth you've been walking toward all your life without knowing it. The light inside was not orange or gold but ember-red deepened to something older, the color of coals at midnight, and it moved. It breathed. It had a rhythm, slow and vast, and Rafael realized with a lurch that he was standing inside a heartbeat.

*So this is what it sounds like from the inside,* he thought, absurdly. *All those years of prayer, and I never once wondered how we sounded to it.*

"Vaelis." His voice came out small in the enormous dark. He had imagined this moment since he was a novitiate kneeling on cold flagstones: the god's voice like thunder, the god's presence like a sun. He braced for annihilation.

"I am here," said Vaelis.

The voice was not thunder. It was tired. It was the voice of someone who has been waiting alone in a house for so long they've stopped setting two places at the table, and it came not from above or below but from everywhere at once, gently, the way snow comes down.

"You know why I've come," Rafael said. He knelt—not because the god demanded it but because his knees decided for him, thirty years of liturgy folding him down onto warm stone. "The covenant is broken. The severance comes at solstice. And a broken covenant requires—" His throat closed around the word he'd carried across half a mountain range, through scree and snow and ambush and grief. "It requires a death."

Silence. Long enough that Rafael's heart stumbled twice.

"Oh," said Vaelis, and the god sounded—there was no other word for it—*sad*. "Oh, child. Is that what they taught you?"

* * *

Rafael's order had kept its doctrines in a vault beneath the monastery at Cindermarch: vellum scrolls chained to iron stands, each one copied by hand by three different scribes who compared their work letter by letter. He had spent eleven years among those scrolls. He could recite the Rite of Emberkeeping backward. He knew the exact number of times the bell rang for matins (nine) and the precise angle at which a priest's palms must be held during consecration (open, upward, receiving).

He had never once questioned any of it. Questioning was for heretics, and heretics burned.

Now the god whose doctrine he'd memorized said, "Sit. Truly sit. This will take longer than your courage has prepared you for," and Rafael sat on the warm stone of a god's heart and listened while everything he believed was turned over like a stone in a garden, showing him the pale, blind things underneath.

"The covenant was never a bargain struck once," Vaelis said. "Your scribes wrote it down as though it were a contract—terms, conditions, a price. Mortals love contracts. They feel safe inside them." A pause, and the ember-light dimmed and brightened, dimmed and brightened, the great breath of it. "But a covenant is not a contract. A covenant is a *choosing*. Made again every day, or not made at all."

"Then the terms—"

"There were never terms. There was only this: we would walk among you, quietly, and you would choose us—not out of fear, not out of obligation, but freely—and as long as the choosing continued, the door between the realms would stay open. That is all. That was always all."

Rafael felt something crack in his chest that had nothing to do with wounds. "Then why did the old rites demand sacrifice?"

"Because," Vaelis said, and the sadness in the god's voice deepened into something ancient and weary, "your ancestors stopped choosing us, and could not bear to admit it. They still wanted the door open—they needed the rain, the harvest, the mountain's mercy—and so they invented a price. They reasoned thus: if the gods require blood, then blood given is proof of devotion, and devotion can be scheduled, ritualized, performed without ever once being *felt*. Sacrifice was not the covenant's requirement. Sacrifice was the covenant's replacement. A counterfeit. Do you understand? Your order did not keep the faith. Your order embalmed it."

The word landed like a blow. Rafael thought of the vault, the chained scrolls, the three scribes comparing letters while the meaning rotted unnoticed between their careful hands.

"And when mortals stopped even performing it," he said slowly, working it through aloud because saying it made it real, "when the rites grew thin and then stopped entirely—"

"We began to starve. Not of worship—we never wanted worship. Of *company*. Of being chosen." The light pulsed, and Rafael felt the pulse pass through him like a wave through still water. "A god forgotten does not die, child. A god forgotten goes to sleep. And the longer the sleep, the more the door between the realms seals itself, grain by grain, like frost closing over a window. The severance is not a punishment. It is simply what happens when no one knocks anymore."

* * *

Rafael stayed on the stone a long time after that, and the god let him.

He thought about the night his order died. He had been in the scriptorium, copying—for the thousandth time—a passage from the Rite of Emberkeeping describing the proper disposition of sacrificial ash. The screams started in the courtyard. He remembered running, and he remembered, worse than the running, the moment in the smoke when Brother Aldous grabbed his arm and pressed the ember shard into his hands and said, *Take it to the mountains, boy. Take it home.* Home. As though the forge were not a place of execution but a place of return.

He had spent every mile since believing himself a courier of a terrible necessity—that he carried a god's heart to a god's altar so that a death could be purchased and the world saved, and that the saving would cost exactly one priest, and that this was right, this was holy, this was the whole architecture of meaning his life had been built to support.

"They slaughtered my brothers," he said finally. His voice came out flat and strange. "The Crown's hunters killed forty-one people in that monastery. Because of the shard. Because of *why*."

"Yes."

"Was it worth knowing? Whatever Brother Aldous discovered—whatever made them come—is it worth forty-one lives?"

Another long silence. When Vaelis answered, the god's voice had changed—sharper now, and underneath the weariness something that might have been anger, banked but alive.

"Aldous found the first scroll. The true one. Not the rite—the record. From before the counterfeit. It says what I have told you: that the door opens for the willing and nothing else, that sacrifice was always mortal invention, mortal cowardice dressed in robes. And do you know what the Crown did with that truth?" The ember-light flared. "They buried it. Because a god who requires only to be chosen cannot be controlled by fear—and a god who cannot be controlled is a rival throne. Your brothers did not die for heresy, Rafael. They died for a secret too dangerous to be true: that the divine asks nothing except that you want it."

Rafael laughed. It came out cracked, half sob. Forty-one dead for the punchline of a joke ten centuries long.

"I carried this shard across a kingdom," he said. "I let a smuggler bleed for me. I let a huntress turn her back on everything she was. I walked into this forge ready to burn, and it was all—" He pressed the heels of his burned hands against his eyes. "It was all for nothing. The whole martyrdom. The whole beautiful, terrible story I told myself on the road. There was never a debt. There was only—" 

"Only what?" Vaelis asked softly. "Finish it."

"Only whether anyone still wanted you." He lowered his hands. "And someone does. I do. I always did—I just didn't know that wanting was the whole of it."

*Yes,* said the god, and for the first time the voice was warm all the way through, and the heartbeat around them quickened like a sleeper waking. *That is the entire mystery. That is the thing your scribes chained in a vault and your kings buried under law: that the covenant survives not on altars but on affection. Love against duty, child. Every age, some few remember it. Every age, most forget.*

"But the severance still comes at solstice," Rafael said. "You said the door seals itself. If there's no sacrifice required, then how do we—"

The light shifted. And Rafael understood, before the god spoke, that he was not going to like the answer—because the answer would not be a price he could pay once and be done with. It would be harder than dying. Dying was a single act; he had been training for it his whole life.

"The forge can renew the covenant," Vaelis said. "But not with blood. Blood was always the counterfeit. It renews the way it began: with a soul bound by oath, freely given, who will *keep choosing*. Not one death, Rafael. A life. Every road kept. Every promise honored. Every single morning, rising and choosing again."

The words settled over him, and Rafael—who had rehearsed his death until it fit him like a coat—felt terror of a kind no liturgy had ever prepared him for.

Because he understood, then, why the god had sounded sad. Why the forge breathed instead of burned. Why the door had opened for him so easily.

It wasn't waiting for a martyr.

It was waiting for a keeper.

And somewhere behind him, beyond the door of iron and song, he heard footsteps on warm stone—Xanthe's uneven stride, the stride of someone who had already guessed, and was coming anyway.
