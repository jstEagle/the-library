## 17. The High Hunter Arrives

The mountain had been singing for an hour before Sera heard the horns.

She stood at the mouth of the Emberforge's approach, where the tunnel opened onto a shelf of black stone above a chasm full of cloud, and she felt the song come up through her boots—deep, patient, older than any anthem the Crown had ever taught her. Behind her, faint through the rock, came the sounds of the others: Brannoc's voice rising and falling in the old cadences, Pip's small hands signing shapes to Rafael that made him laugh in a cracked, wondering way. Xanthe's voice, low, steady, asking questions.

Sera had spent eleven years learning to listen for danger. She heard it now, far below and closing fast: the double-beat of Crown signal horns, the crunch of many boots on frozen scree, the thin metallic music of chains being uncoiled. God-hunter kit. The kind they used on things that didn't want to be caught.

They'd believed they had until solstice. They'd believed the storm would buy them two more days.

Sera should have known better. She'd fed the High Hunter false routes for six days, and he had swallowed every one of them with a smile—and all that time he'd been letting her run, because a traitor leads you straight to what you love. He hadn't needed her maps. He'd needed her heart, and she had handed it over wrapped in a bow of good intentions.

*You always were the best of us,* he'd said, when she'd knelt in his tent and sworn to deliver Rafael alive. *That's why I chose you.*

She turned from the chasm and ran back into the mountain.

* * *

The forge chamber was not what she expected. She'd imagined fire, industry, some great engine of the old faith. Instead it was a cavern like the inside of a struck bell—a dome of veined stone, walls ribbed with channels that glowed a slow ember-orange, and at its center a floor of polished basalt where the light pooled like standing water. Warm. Not hot. The warmth of a hearth in a house where someone is waiting for you.

Rafael stood at the edge of the pool of light with the ember shard cupped in his burned hands, and even from across the chamber Sera could see it was no longer a shard. It had grown—or woken—into something like a coal the size of a fist, pulsing in rhythm with the mountain's song. His face was wet. He was smiling.

"It knows me," he said when he saw her. "Sera—it never knew me before. All those years of prayer, all those vigils, and now—" His voice broke and reformed. "It's like coming home to a house you only dreamed existed."

Xanthe stood beside him, arms crossed, wearing the expression Sera had learned to read as terror managed by competence. "How long?"

"I don't know. The forging—I think it has to happen at the exact moment of severance. Solstice midnight." Rafael looked up at the dome, where veins of light were brightening one by one, like windows being lit in a dark city. "We're close."

"Then we're out of time." Sera crossed the chamber, boots ringing on stone. "The High Hunter is in the tunnels. Sixty men, maybe more. Chains, cold-iron, severance lances—the works. He let me lead him here."

The words landed in the group like stones into still water. Pip went still, her hands freezing mid-sign. Brannoc set down the tuning-stone he'd been holding and pressed his palm flat against the wall, eyes closing, listening with his whole body.

"Aye," the dwarf said quietly. "I hear them. Iron on my stone. Like teeth on bone." He opened his eyes, and Sera saw something in them she'd never seen there before—not fear. Grief. "This chamber is the mountain's heart. If they break the veins, if they spill their cold iron into the song—" He shook his head. "It willna just kill the forge. It'll deafen the mountain forever."

Xanthe looked at Sera. Just looked. And Sera braced for the accusation she deserved—for the lie, the false routes, the whole rotten architecture of her betrayal—but it didn't come.

"You bought us six days," Xanthe said. "Whatever it cost you to do it, I watched you pay it. So we spend what's left well." A pause. "Can we hold this chamber?"

"With five of us? Against sixty?" Sera almost laughed. "No. But we don't need to hold it. We need to make him come to us on our ground, in our time, and we need the forge awake when he does." She pointed at the single entrance, the long throat of tunnel behind it. "One way in. That's a choke point. I've held worse with less."

"And the rest of us?" Brannoc asked.

"The mountain holds the rest." Sera turned to the dwarf. "You said the stone sings. Can it fight?"

Brannoc's grin was slow and terrible. "Lass, I've been waiting my whole life for someone to ask me that."

* * *

They worked like people who had done this together for years, though it had been weeks—weeks of roads and storms and shared bread, and apparently that was enough to build a thing worth defending.

Brannoc went to the walls and sang to them, low and grinding, and the stone answered. The tunnel throat narrowed, widened, narrowed again—a series of killing pockets where three men could pass and thirty could not. He raised a lip of basalt at the entrance, waist-high, cover for whoever held it. He did it all while the mountain's song deepened around him, and sweat cut clean lines down his dusty face, and he laughed twice, both times at nothing anyone else could hear.

Pip refused to leave. Her parents' letter was sewn into her coat, and she had shown it to Rafael with the fierce dignity of a child delivering an ultimatum: *she stays where the bells are.* So they gave her the deepest alcove, behind a spur of stone Brannoc raised for her himself, and told her—honestly, because she would have known a lie—that her job was to listen. If the gods spoke, she was to sign it to Rafael, fast, whatever it was.

Rafael tried to give the shard away twice. Both times Xanthe took it, weighed it, and handed it back without a word, and the second time Rafael stopped trying.

Sera checked her bow, counted her arrows—nineteen—and took her place at the raised lip of stone. Below her, through the narrowing throat of tunnel, she could see the first flicker of hunter-lanterns. Cold white light. Light with nothing warm in it at all.

"Xanthe," she said, not looking away from the tunnel.

"Yeah."

"When this is over—if it's over—if I'm still standing. I'm going to need to answer for the bridge. For all of it. Don't let them make it easy on me."

Xanthe was quiet a moment. "When this is over," they said, "you're going to eat too much stew and complain about my cooking, same as everyone else. Answering can wait for spring."

"That's not how justice works."

"No," Xanthe agreed. "But it's how family works. Ask me again in spring."

The lanterns rounded the last bend.

* * *

The High Hunter came last, as Sera knew he would. First came the vanguard—eight men in the new pattern armor, moving with the careful economy of soldiers who trusted their equipment more than their luck. Cold-iron nets coiled at their hips. Severance lances slung across their backs, each one tipped with a head of grey metal that seemed to drink the lantern-light.

Sera put her first arrow through the leading man's shoulder joint, dropping his lance arm, and the battle began.

It was ugly and it was close and it was everything she'd trained her whole life to do, and she hated every second of it, because these had been her people. She knew their names. She'd drunk with half of them. The man whose shoulder she'd ruined was Aldric, who'd once carried her ten miles through fever-country on his back.

"Hold!" someone shouted below. "Vane is up there! Vane has turned!"

"She hasn't turned," said another voice, younger, uncertain. "She's seeing something we're not."

"Then she's dead with the rest of them!"

They came anyway, because the High Hunter's word was law and doubt was treason, and the tunnel did what Brannoc had promised. Three abreast in the killing pockets, bunched and blind, and Sera shot from the lip of stone with the calm of a woman spending a currency she could never earn back. Fourteen arrows. Eleven. Nine.

Behind her, the mountain fought. She heard it rather than saw it—Brannoc's voice dropping into registers that vibrated in her back teeth, and then the stone itself moving, a groan, a shift, a section of tunnel ceiling settling downward with a sound like a giant exhaling, and screams, and the terrible grinding quiet after. The dwarven songs of her childhood had been about gems and gold. No one had ever told her the stone-songs had war verses.

Twice the hunters broke through to the final stretch of tunnel, and twice the mountain closed on them. The third time, they stopped coming through at all, and instead the tunnel filled with smoke—not woodsmoke but something chemical and grey, severance-smoke, the stuff they burned to strip magic from air—and the song of the mountain stuttered.

In the chamber, the veins of light dimmed.

"No, no, no—" Brannoc staggered back from the wall, hands pressed to his ears. "It's no' pain, it's *silence*, they're cutting the song out of the air—"

And through the smoke, walking unhurried, came the High Hunter.

He had removed his helmet. That was the worst part. He wanted her to see his face—calm, grey-eyed, the face of a man doing arithmetic. He stepped over a fallen soldier without looking down, and his voice carried through the chamber with the easy authority of a lifetime of command.

"Sera Vane." He sounded almost fond. "You've cost me forty good hunters tonight. Do you know what the tribunal will call that? Treason squared. Betrayal compounded by incompetence." He surveyed the chamber—the glowing veins, the pool of light, Rafael standing at its edge with the ember burning in his hands—and something moved behind his eyes that was not arithmetic at all. Hunger, maybe. Or fear. "There it is. A god's heart, loose in the world. Do you understand what happens if it finishes whatever it's doing? Every miracle, every omen, every whispering shrine from here to the coast—the Crown spends the next century drowning in fires it cannot tax and cannot command. My grandmother watched the old gods burn her village for a bad harvest. I watched the covenant's silence bring fifty years of peace. *Fifty years.* And you five would trade that for—what? Songs in the stone?"

"The peace was rented," Rafael said quietly. "Not owned. And the rent is due."

"Then we default." The High Hunter drew his sword—cold iron, grey as grave-frost—and nodded to the ranks filing in behind him through the smoke. Thirty remained, maybe more, filling the chamber's edges like water finding its level. "Take the priest. Break the shard. Kill the rest if they resist." His gaze found Sera one last time. "Last offer, Vane. Kneel, deliver him, and walk out pardoned. You know I keep my bargains."

"That's exactly why I can't kneel," Sera said, and drew her last arrow.

What happened next, she would spend years trying to describe, and never fully succeed.

Pip screamed—or didn't scream; her mouth was open but the sound that came out wasn't hers. It was layered, harmonic, several voices braided into one small body, and every vein of light in the chamber flared at once. The smoke burned away like fog under sun. And the hunters—every hunter in the chamber—stopped.

Because their oaths rose up out of them.

Sera felt it happen to the room: the Crown's oath, spoken at every commissioning, *to serve the realm above all gods*—and the realm, the true realm, the land itself, was standing right there in the form of a waking god, and the oath had nowhere honest to point. Men staggered. Weapons drooped. One young hunter dropped his lance entirely and sat down on the stone, weeping, saying *I didn't know, I didn't know* like a prayer.

But oaths are iron as well as spirit, and the High Hunter's was forged deeper than most. He shook off the weight of it like a man shrugging off a cloak, snarled, and drove forward toward the pool of light, toward Rafael, sword high—

—and Sera loosed her last arrow.

Not at him. At the severed chain-link hanging from the winch above the entrance, the one Brannoc had sung loose hours ago and left hanging as a trap. The link snapped. Two tons of counterweighted basalt dropped across the tunnel mouth with a boom that shook dust from the dome, sealing the hunters' reinforcements in the throat, leaving the High Hunter alone in the chamber with a god's heart, a failing oath, and five people who had decided, separately and together, that he would not win today.

He didn't even look at the sealed tunnel. He looked at Rafael, and smiled, and raised his sword.

And Rafael—who had spent his whole life preparing to die, who had walked toward this moment like a bride toward an altar—took one step forward to meet him.

"No," Xanthe said.

Sera heard it and turned. Xanthe was already moving, already past the pool of light, already pulling something from inside their coat—the guild oath, the old paper, Maren's ledger-page folded and refolded until it was soft as cloth. Their face was lit from below by the ember's glow, and it was the calmest, most terrifying face Sera had ever seen.

"Not your vow," Xanthe said, stepping between Rafael and the descending sword. "Mine."

The sword fell. The ember flared. And somewhere beneath them, deeper than stone, deeper than song, something vast and patient opened one eye and began, at last, to listen.
