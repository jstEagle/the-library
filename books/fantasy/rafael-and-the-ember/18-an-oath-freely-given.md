## 18. An Oath Freely Given

The forge opened like a wound in the world.

Not with fire—Xanthe had expected fire—but with light that moved the way water moves under ice, slow and deep and older than anything with a name. It poured up through the mountain's spine and filled the great hollow of the Emberforge, and every surface caught it: walls veined with gold that pulsed like breath, a floor of black glass that showed reflections Xanthe didn't trust, and at the center, rising from a pit of coals that had never gone cold, an altar.

The altar was simple. That was the worst part. After everything—the sealed passes, the hunters, Maren's ledger, Sera bleeding on the Widow's Bridge so they could run—Xanthe had imagined something grander than a slab of stone no bigger than a smuggler's table, worn smooth by hands that had stopped coming centuries ago.

Rafael stood before it, and the ember shard in his burned hands was singing.

Xanthe felt it in their teeth. In the scar tissue along their palms where rope had taught them its lessons. The shard had hummed for them once, back in Brannoc's tunnels, when they'd touched it without thinking—a small, startled note, like a bell struck by accident. Now it wasn't humming. It was *calling*, and the whole mountain was calling back, and Rafael was walking toward the altar with the expression of a man who had rehearsed his death so many times it had become a kind of peace.

"No," Xanthe said.

Rafael didn't stop. Of course he didn't stop. He'd been walking toward this since the night his order burned, since the night his brothers and sisters died for knowing why the gods had gone quiet, since the night he pressed a shard of living god into a stranger's hands and said *take me north*.

"Xanthe." His voice was gentle, which made it worse. "You knew."

"I suspected. Knowing's different." Xanthe crossed the black glass floor, boots ringing wrong against it, each step echoing a half-beat late as if the forge were considering whether to let them through. Behind them, at the mouth of the passage, Brannoc stood with his hand flat against the singing stone, his face wet. Pip had her face buried in Sera's shoulder, her small fingers signing fast—*no, no, no*—a word she'd never needed until now. Sera held her and watched the altar with a hunter's stillness, one hand on a bow she hadn't strung, because there was nothing left to shoot.

"You knew," Rafael said again, softer. He reached the altar. Set the shard down on the stone, and it flared, and the light climbed the walls like ivy. "You've known since the shard sang for you. You could have turned me over to the Crown a dozen times and saved yourself all of this. Instead you walked me here." He smiled, and it broke Xanthe's heart clean down the middle, because it was the smile of a man setting down a weight he'd carried too long. "This is what I'm for. I've been dying to be useful my whole life. Let me finally do it properly."

"That's not—" Xanthe stopped. Breathed. The forge waited around them, patient as winter. "That's not what I meant when I said I'd keep you safe."

"I know." Rafael laid both burned hands flat on the altar's edge. The scars there were old ones, from his first communion, from a god who had loved him enough to mark him. "But you can't keep everyone safe by standing between them and what they choose. You taught me that. On the scree, remember? You said a road isn't a cage. A road is a promise that goes both ways."

The shard began to rise.

It lifted off the stone an inch, then two, turning slowly, shedding sparks that didn't fall but hung in the air like a held breath. And the voice—if it could be called a voice; it was more like heat given language, more like the feeling of standing too close to a bonfire and being glad of it—filled the forge.

***A SOUL BOUND BY OATH.***

Rafael straightened. "I am bound," he said, and his priest's cadence came into his voice, the formal liturgy of Vaelis's order, words Xanthe had heard him whisper in the dark of caves and snowbound camps. "By vow and by blood, by the rite of my ordination, I give myself—"

***NO.***

The word hit the forge like a door slamming. The light shuddered. Sparks scattered. Rafael staggered back from the altar as if struck, and Xanthe lunged forward without deciding to, catching his arm, holding him up.

"What—" Rafael's voice cracked. "I don't understand. I'm bound. My vows—"

"Your vows were given *for* you," Brannoc said quietly, from the passage. The dwarf's voice carried strange in the forge, doubled by the singing stone. "That's how your order worked, lad. Infancy, dedication, the whole ladder of it. Given by others. Sworn on your behalf." He shook his head slowly. "The mountain doesn't want a gift someone else wrapped."

***A SOUL BOUND BY OATH,*** the fire-voice repeated, and now Xanthe heard what it had been saying underneath all along, the way you hear the second melody in a round only after years of listening: ***FREELY GIVEN. FREELY KEPT. THE COVENANT WAS NEVER BLOOD. IT WAS CHOICE. AND YOU STOPPED CHOOSING.***

And Xanthe understood.

Understood it the way they understood a pass in bad weather—not from maps or elders' warnings but from the feel of the ground under their boots. The covenant hadn't broken because mortals failed some ancient test. It had broken because somewhere in the long generations, choosing had curdled into obligation, obligation into ritual, ritual into rote—and a covenant kept by habit is no covenant at all. It's just a lock nobody remembers opening.

Maren's grandmother hadn't sold the covenant to the Crown. She'd sold its *corpse*. The real thing had died of neglect long before, and no one had noticed because the silence of the gods sounded so much like peace.

"Rafael." Xanthe turned to face him, still gripping his arm. Up close, in the forge-light, his eyes were terrified. Not of dying. Of failing. Of standing at the last door of his life's purpose and hearing *no*. "Step back from the altar."

"Xanthe—"

"Step *back*." They gentled it, barely. "Please. Trust me one more mile."

He searched their face. Whatever he found there made him sway, made him close his eyes, made him—slowly, like a man dismantling himself—lift his hands from the altar and step aside.

The shard flared brighter.

Because it wasn't looking at him anymore.

It was looking at Xanthe.

* * *

There is a moment on every dangerous road—the moment Xanthe had lived a hundred times—when retreat stops being possible. A bridge behind you cut. A pass ahead closed by weather. The point where the only way out is through, and fear either breaks you or burns off, and what's left underneath is simpler than courage. It's just *yes*.

Xanthe stood at that moment now, and for the first time in their life, no one had built the road for them. No guild route. No elder's orders. No client's coin. Just a slab of stone and a god's heart hanging in the air, waiting.

They thought about the oath.

Every Fairweather learned it at seven, kneeling on the cold floor of the guild hall, Maren's grandmother's grandmother's voice written out in ink gone brown: *Every road kept. Every promise honored. Every cargo delivered, though the mountains fall and the Crown take the passes and the gods themselves forget the way.* They'd recited it a thousand times without thinking, the way you recite anything inherited. Duty. Blood. The weight of a name.

But oaths weren't the problem. Xanthe saw that now, blazing clear. Oaths were never the problem. The problem was who did the choosing.

They stepped forward.

"Xanthe, no—" Rafael's hand found their shoulder, and Xanthe covered it with their own, held it there a heartbeat, then let go.

"You told me once," Xanthe said, not turning around, "that your order believed the gods answered prayers again because someone, somewhere, prayed honestly. Not correctly. Honestly." They looked up at the shard, at the light that was watching them with the patience of mountains. "I've never prayed in my life. But I've kept promises. Every single one. Through sealed passes and Crown hunters and winters that should have killed me. Nobody made me. Nobody ever made me. I chose every road I ever walked, and I chose them *well*."

They put their hands on the altar.

The stone was warm. Warm as a living thing, warm as Rafael's burned hands, warm as the kitchen of the guild hall on the one morning a year Maren had allowed herself to be soft. The light gathered around Xanthe's wrists like manacles that hadn't closed yet, asking.

"So here's my prayer," Xanthe said. "Hear me, Vaelis. Hear me, all of you sleeping old things. I'm not giving you my blood. I'm not giving you my death. Those were never mine to give—they belong to the roads I haven't walked yet, the people I haven't come home to yet." Their voice didn't shake. Later, they would marvel at that. "I'm giving you the only thing I've ever owned outright. My oath. Freely spoken, freely kept, for as long as I draw breath and past it if the roads go on without me."

They drew breath, and spoke the words they'd known since they were seven, and for the first time they were not reciting. They were *vowing*.

"Every road kept. Every promise honored. Every soul entrusted to my care delivered safe to where they need to be—though the mountains fall, though the Crown take the passes, though the gods themselves forget the way." A pause, and then, because honesty was the whole point, the part that was never in the guild's version: "*I will not forget yours.*"

The forge went silent.

Utterly silent—the singing stone stilled, the coals hushed, even the wind in the high passages holding itself—and in that silence Xanthe felt the full attention of something vast turn upon them, the way a lighthouse turns, the way winter turns, and they understood that they were being weighed. Not their worth. Worth was a mortal arithmetic. Something older: their *willingness*. Whether the yes in them ran all the way down, or only skin-deep.

Xanthe thought of Pip's small hands signing *stay*. Thought of Sera's arrow going wide on purpose, and wide again, until going wide became her truth. Thought of Brannoc singing stone awake, of Rafael in the cargo hold saying *please* to a stranger with a knife at his throat. Thought of Maren, and forgave her, and refused her, both at once, because both were true.

*Yes,* they thought, pouring it into the warmth under their palms. *All the way down.*

The forge accepted.

Light detonated upward through the mountain—not fire, never fire, but dawn, the first dawn, gold climbing the veins in the walls until the whole Emberforge blazed like a heart remembered beating—and the oath left Xanthe's mouth and did not end. It *continued*. They felt it settle into their bones like a second skeleton, like a road map drawn on the inside of their skin, every promise they'd ever kept lighting up one by one, and beyond them, waiting, every promise they had yet to make. It didn't hurt. That was the astonishing thing. It felt like coming home to a house they'd been building their whole life without knowing it.

***KEEPER OF ROADS,*** said the fire-voice, and it was not terrible anymore. It was tired, and grateful, and glad the way the very old are glad. ***THE COVENANT STANDS. NOT IN BLOOD. IN YOU.***

The shard descended, gently, onto the altar—and sank into the stone like a coal sinking into a banked fire, and the light spread outward instead of up, flowing out through the mountain's roots, through the singing stone, through every pass and valley and hidden road in the Thornfells, and far away Xanthe felt—or imagined, or didn't—old things stirring in deep places, stretching, waking not in wrath but in relief.

Then it was done, and the forge was just a warm bright room, and Xanthe was still standing, still breathing, still themselves—except for the road mapped in their bones, and the quiet certainty that they would never walk alone again.

Rafael caught them as their knees buckled.

"I had it," he whispered into their hair, and his voice was wrecked, and his arms were the strongest thing in the world. "I was supposed to—I trained my whole life—"

"I know." Xanthe gripped him back. "I'm sorry. I know exactly what I just took from you."

"Don't apologize." He pulled back far enough to look at them, and his face was doing several things at once, grief and fury and a dawning, disbelieving joy. "Don't you dare apologize. You didn't take it. You *out-oathed* me." A laugh tore out of him, ragged and wet. "Nobody's ever out-oathed a priest of Vaelis. There isn't supposed to be a way."

"There's always a way," Xanthe said. "That's the first thing the guild teaches."

From the passage came a sound—Pip, laughing, actually laughing, a bright cracked sound like a bell learning to ring—and then Sera's voice, sharp with alarm: "Xanthe! Trouble. The High Hunter's found us. Torches on the lower switchbacks—dozens of them. They'll reach the forge before solstice midnight."

Xanthe rose. The new oath hummed in their bones, steady as bedrock.

"Good," they said. "Let them climb. This road is kept."
