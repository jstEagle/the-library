## 20. Ashes and Embers

Rafael had spent his whole life learning how to die.

He knew the liturgies. He could recite the Rite of the Returning Flame in his sleep—had, in fact, done so more than once in the years since Vaelis's silence began, waking in cold cells and colder hostels with the words already on his tongue like a fever. He knew which prayers to whisper as the blade came down. He knew that a martyr's last breath was supposed to smell of incense, and that the ember-god would gather it up like a spark from a struck flint, and carry it home.

He had rehearsed his death so thoroughly that when the forge refused to take it, he had no idea what to do with his hands.

They were just hands now. Unburned. The old scars—the ones he'd carried since the night at the sanctuary, when the fire took his brothers and left him kneeling in the ashes with a shard of god's heart cupped in his palms—were fading. He kept looking at them. Turning them over in the forge's amber light, expecting the pain to come back, expecting the weight of the offering he had spent eleven years preparing himself to make.

The forge had looked at him with something ancient and patient behind its fire, and it had said—not in words, but in the way warmth moves through stone—*not you. Not this.*

And then Xanthe had stepped forward, and spoken their smuggler's oath into the heart of the world, and everything Rafael understood about his own life had quietly collapsed.

* * *

The others were sleeping. Or trying to.

The Emberforge's great chamber had softened around them as the solstice passed—as if the mountain itself had exhaled after holding its breath for three hundred years. The walls still glowed, but gentler now, the color of banked coals rather than open flame. Warmth pooled on the floor where they'd dragged blankets and bedrolls from the hunters' abandoned camp. Someone had found bread in the Crown's supply packs. Someone else had found wine. Pip was asleep against Sera's side, her small hands curled loosely, and even in sleep her fingers twitched sometimes, signing fragments of dreams.

Rafael couldn't sleep. He sat with his back against a warm wall and watched Brannoc work.

The dwarf stood at the edge of the forge-pool, barefoot on stone that should have been too hot for anything living, singing under his breath. It wasn't a song with words—or if it had once had words, the mountain had worn them smooth over generations. It was more like the sound a cave makes when water finds a new path through it. The stone sang back to him in harmonies below hearing, and where the two songs touched, the cracks left by the High Hunter's assault were closing. Slowly. Like skin over a wound.

"You're going to stay," Rafael said. It wasn't a question.

Brannoc didn't stop singing. He let one phrase finish itself, then said, "Aye." His voice was rough with exhaustion; he'd been at it since dawn, and the light in his eyes said he'd be at it until the mountain told him to stop, which might be never. "She's awake now. Been asleep three hundred year, and somebody's got to teach her how to talk again without cracking her own teeth."

"The mountain has teeth?"

"Everything has teeth, priest. Ye just haven't met most of 'em yet." Brannoc grinned, and there was ash in the creases of his face, and something in him had changed since the climb—since the vision the singing stone had shown him, whatever it was. He hadn't spoken of it. None of them had spoken of what they'd seen on the ascent. Some confessions were meant for the dark alone.

"I don't know what I am," Rafael said, because it was late, and the wine was thin, and Brannoc was the only one awake to hear it. "That's the truth of it. Eleven years I was a vessel. A wick, waiting for the flame. My order trained me for one purpose and I was good at it—I was *good*, Brannoc—and now the purpose is gone and I'm just..." He turned his hands over again. "Just a man who knows a lot of prayers nobody needs anymore."

The dwarf considered him for a long moment. Then he tapped the stone beside him, twice, an invitation.

Rafael came and sat. The rock was warm as a hearthstone and hummed faintly against his spine.

"Ye know what a keystone is?" Brannoc asked.

"Architecturally, or metaphorically? Because my order loved metaphors."

"Both, probably." The dwarf snorted. "It's the stone at the top of an arch. All the weight comes down through it. Take it out and the whole thing falls." He nodded toward the forge-pool, where the ember-light turned slowly like something breathing. "Everyone thinks the covenant was the god's work. Vaelis holds the door, mortals keep the faith, realms stay stitched together. But that's not how arches work. The god was never the keystone." He pointed one blunt finger at Rafael's chest. "Ye were. Every priest before ye. Folk who chose to carry the weight. The god didn't hold the covenant up—he leaned on it same as everyone else. And when your order died, the arch didn't fall, but it started to sag, didn't it? Started letting the cold in."

"And Xanthe is the new keystone."

"Xanthe is the new keystone," Brannoc agreed. "Which means ye're free, lad. First time in eleven years, nobody's leaning on ye." He said it like a gift. To Rafael, in that moment, it felt like standing at the top of a cliff with nothing to hold.

* * *

Sera found him near morning, out on the ledge above the forge where the first true sunlight of the new season was coming up over the Thornfells—clean light, unfiltered by stormcloud, catching the snowfields until they blazed.

She moved like a hunter even now, even wounded: favoring her left side where the spear had gone through, her hand resting easy near a knife she probably didn't need anymore. She sat down next to him without asking, which was its own kind of miracle. A month ago she'd been shooting at him across a scree field.

"Deliberately missing," he'd learned later. "Mostly deliberately." She still wouldn't tell him the whole story of the massacre at the sanctuary—what she'd seen that made her turn—but he'd stopped needing to know. Some things you recognized in a person without the telling.

"Pardon arrived at dawn," she said.

Rafael blinked. "What?"

"Rider came up as far as the lower pass. Wouldn't cross the threshold—couldn't, maybe. Left this." She held out a scroll, sealed with the Crown's sunburst, the wax unbroken. "Full pardon. Restoration of rank. Command of my own company, if I want it. All I have to do is confirm the death of the fugitive Rafael of the Order of the Returning Flame, and bring in the girl for questioning."

The word *questioning* did something ugly in Rafael's chest. He looked at Pip, still asleep inside, small and dreaming in a room full of gods who spoke to her in bells.

"What are you going to do?" he asked.

Sera looked at the scroll for a long time. Then she stood, walked to the edge of the ledge, and dropped it into the void. It fluttered down like a dying moth, white against the blue shadow of the mountain, and was gone.

"Tell them yourself," Rafael said.

"They know. The rider saw enough." She shrugged, and winced, and sat back down. "Let them put it in their reports. *Vane defected. Vane is lost to us.* Let the High Hunter choke on it." She was quiet a moment. "I spent nine years being a weapon for people who thought the gods were a problem to be solved. I'm not going back to that. Whatever I am now—" she gestured vaguely at the mountain, at all of it— "it's not theirs."

"It might not be anyone's," Rafael said. "That's the thing nobody tells you. After."

Sera glanced at him sidelong. "You sound like a man who knows."

"I sound like a man standing on a cliff with nothing to hold," he admitted, and she laughed—a short, surprised bark—and for a moment they were just two defectors sitting in the sun, unemployed by destiny.

Inside, Pip stirred, and her sleeping hands made a shape Rafael was starting to recognize: the sign for *home*. Or maybe for *fire*. In her language the two were nearly the same.

* * *

Midday, the mountain spoke through Brannoc, and everyone gathered.

The dwarf stood at the rim of the forge-pool, both palms flat on the stone, and when he lifted his head his eyes had gone coal-bright—which Rafael understood now was not possession, not exactly. More like translation. The mountain needed a mouth, and Brannoc had volunteered, and the price of volunteering was written all over him in lines of deep tiredness that no sleep would touch.

"She says," Brannoc began, and paused, listening to something, "the roads will open at springmelt. All of them. The passes the Crown sealed, the ways that've been closed for three hundred years—she'll open them. Gods want to walk among folk again. Quietly, mind. No burning bushes, no voices out of whirlwinds. Just... presence. Old neighbors coming back to call."

"And the covenant?" Rafael asked. "The oath Xanthe bound—does it hold?"

"It holds." Brannoc's grin was fierce. "Held through the High Hunter's best shot, didn't it? She says the keeper's oath is woven through every road in the Thornfells now. Every path, every pass, every hidden trail. Wherever folk walk, the covenant walks with 'em." He cocked his head. "She also says the keeper hasn't slept in two days and is currently reorganizing the hunters' abandoned supply packs by *weight class*, and asks if someone might intervene."

"That sounds like Xanthe," Sera said dryly.

Rafael went looking, and found them exactly where Brannoc had promised: in the antechamber, surrounded by neat rows of rations and rope, moving with the restless energy of a person who had reforged the covenant between realms two days ago and had not yet figured out what to do with a body that was allowed to rest.

"You're supposed to be sleeping," Rafael said.

"So are you." Xanthe didn't look up. They were repacking a satchel with the particular violence of someone avoiding a conversation. "I'll sleep when the inventory's done."

"The mountain sent me."

That stopped them. Xanthe finally looked up, and Rafael saw it in their face—the thing they were both carrying, the thing neither of them had said aloud since the forge closed around their joined hands and the world declined, miraculously, to end. Xanthe looked tired in a way that had nothing to do with sleep. The look of someone who had offered everything and gotten everything back, and didn't know where to put it.

"Come here," Rafael said.

"Rafe—"

"*Come here.*"

They came. He pulled them down onto the bench beside him, and for a while neither of them spoke. The forge hummed through the floor. Somewhere deeper in the mountain, Brannoc's song rose and fell like tide.

"I had a speech prepared," Rafael said finally. "Eleven years of speeches, actually. For the moment before the sacrifice. What I'd say to Vaelis. What I'd say to whoever was left behind." He laughed, softly, helplessly. "Every version ended the same way. *Tell them I did what I was made for.* That was the whole point of me. The sum total. I was a man shaped entirely like his own ending."

"And now?"

"Now I'm standing in the wreckage of my own survival and I don't know a single prayer for this. There's no liturgy for *waking up tomorrow*. Nobody taught me how to want things." He turned his ruined-and-healing hands palm-up between them. "You gave me back my life, Xanthe. Do you understand how terrifying that is? A death I could carry. It had weight, it had shape, I knew exactly where to put it down. But a life? An open life? I'm holding it like it's going to blow away."

Xanthe was quiet for a long moment. When they spoke, their voice was low and rough and utterly certain—the voice that had talked a god into accepting an oath instead of a corpse.

"First lesson of the road," they said. "Free lesson, since you're family now. You don't hold a life like it's going to blow away. You hold it like cargo you've sworn to deliver. One day at a time, one mile at a time, and you don't set it down till you get where you're going." Their hand covered his. Warm. Callused. Steady as a packed trail. "And you don't know the destination yet? Fine. Neither do I. We'll map it as we go. That's the whole trade, Rafe. Nobody ever knows the destination. They just keep walking anyway."

"And if I'm bad at it?" he whispered. "At being alive?"

"Then you'll be bad at it with company." Xanthe's thumb traced the fading scar across his knuckles. "That's the other thing about roads. You never walk them alone unless you choose to."

Outside, far below, the wind came up the pass—and it carried something it hadn't carried in three hundred years. Bells. Faint, silver, unmistakable. Pip's sleeping laughter drifted through the stone, and Brannoc's song caught the sound and wove it in, and the mountain rang like a struck chord.

Rafael closed his eyes and listened, and for the first time in eleven years, prayed nothing at all.

He just listened.

Spring was coming down off the peaks, and every road it opened led somewhere none of them had ever been—and for the first time in his life, Rafael found that he wanted to see.
