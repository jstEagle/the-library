## 1. The Valley That Magic Forgot

The rain came to Hollowmere the way debts did — slowly, grudgingly, and never quite enough.

Rafael Vasquez stood in the doorway of the Gilded Ox with his collar up, watching it fall in thin gray threads across the market square. Ten years of rain like this. Ten years since the god went quiet, and the sky had forgotten how to do anything but apologize.

"You're staring again," said Odette from behind the bar, not looking up from the tankard she was scrubbing. "Staring at weather won't make it better."

"Staring at weather is free," Rafael said. "Everything else in this valley costs."

He came inside properly, shaking the damp from his shoulders, and took the corner table by habit — back to the wall, sightline to both doors, one hand's reach from the hearth iron. Old habits. The kind you kept even when there was nothing left to guard against but boredom.

The contract lay on the table between two rings of old ale. A merchant named Corvin Hale wanted a rival's warehouse watched for three nights, no violence unless violence found him first. Fifty silver. It was the sort of work that came to men like Rafael precisely because it required nothing special — no charms, no wards, no whispered words over a blade. In the valley, magic didn't work. Hadn't worked in a decade. Spells died at the border stones like moths against glass, and so the valley had become a place where problems were solved the old way: with eyes, hands, patience, and occasionally blood.

Mercenaries who'd made their names elsewhere called it exile. Rafael called it home, though he'd have been hard-pressed to say why. He'd never known anything else. Born here, raised here, sworn here — to a god whose name he still carried on his tongue like a stone he couldn't spit out. *Vasquez, marked and sworn.* He'd spoken the oath at fourteen, kneeling in the shrine at Cael's Foot while the god's presence had washed through him like warm water. Two years later, the warmth stopped. No word. No sign. Just silence, thick as valley fog, going on ten years now.

The priests said the god was testing them. The farmers said the god was dead. Rafael mostly tried not to think about it, which was its own full-time occupation.

Marguerite arrived at sundown, letting in a gust of cold air and louder noise than she was worth. She was small, sharp-faced, and dressed like a rumor — three layers of stolen-looking green, hair pinned up with what appeared to be a fork. She dropped into the seat across from him, stole his ale without asking, and drank half of it.

"Well?" she said. "Is it boring?"

"Fifty silver to watch a warehouse."

"Gods below." She set the tankard down with a clack. "We've sunk so low we're being paid to be furniture."

"We were paid worse to be furniture last month."

"That's not the comfort you think it is." She flagged Odette for another, then leaned forward, dropping her voice beneath the tavern's murmur. "I heard something today. Down at the border stones. A carter swears he saw a man come through from the east side — walking wrong, he said. Like the road was arguing with him."

"People walk strange all the time."

"Not like this. Carter says the man's shadow moved before he did." She turned her tankard in a slow circle. "Which is impossible. Because shadows don't cross the border stones. Nothing crosses the border stones. That's rather the point of this entire miserable valley."

Rafael looked at her. Marguerite told stories the way other people spent money — freely, often, and with no intention of accounting for all of it. But under the theater there was always a ledger, and the ledger said she only brought him things that mattered.

"If a man crossed," he said, "he'd be dead or empty by now. Magic doesn't survive here. Whatever he was carrying would burn out of him before he reached the square."

"Yes," Marguerite agreed pleasantly. "Isn't it interesting, then, that I can't stop thinking about it?"

* * *

It was near midnight, the Gilded Ox down to its last six drinkers and Odette stacking chairs, when the door opened and the past walked in wearing a stranger's coat.

Rafael saw him first because it was his job to see things first. A man, tall, gaunt, moving with the careful deliberation of someone carrying something breakable inside his own body. His coat was fine once — deep blue, silver thread at the cuffs — and it was ruined, scorched along one side as if lightning had used him for practice. He stood in the doorway a moment, swaying, and the lamplight did something wrong around him. Bent toward him, almost. Like a candle leaning into wind that wasn't there.

Then Rafael saw the light coming through the man's shirt.

Not reflection. Not lantern-glow. Light, actual light, seeping out between the buttons of his scorched coat the way blood seeps through a bandage — slow, pulsing, gold-white, bright enough to throw the man's shadow huge and shivering against the floorboards. One of the remaining drinkers swore. Another knocked over his stool getting up. Odette went still behind the bar with a mug frozen halfway to the shelf.

"Magic," someone whispered, and the word landed in the room like a dropped knife. Nobody had seen magic in the valley in ten years. Magic couldn't live here. That was the whole covenant of the place, the whole mercy of it.

The man took three steps into the tavern. Each one cost him visibly. The light pulsed faster, guttering, and where it dripped from his sleeve it hissed against the floorboards and left small scorched stars.

Rafael was on his feet before he'd decided to stand. Twenty years of soldiering will do that — the body moves ahead of the mind, sorting threat from casualty. This one was a casualty. This one was dying, and dying fast, and some animal part of Rafael already knew it.

"Easy," Rafael said, crossing the room. "Sit down before you fall down."

The man's eyes found him. They were extraordinary eyes — pale, almost colorless, lit from within by the same gold bleeding through his coat — and they went wide with an expression Rafael had seen maybe twice in his life and never wanted to see again. Recognition. Not of a face. Of a name.

"Vasquez," the man said. His voice was ash and river-stones. "Rafael Vasquez."

Every hair on Rafael's arms stood up. "You have me confused—"

"No." The man's legs gave. Rafael caught him — and flinched, because the light was hot, not burning exactly but *present*, humming against his palms like a struck bell. He lowered the man into a chair. Up close he could see the damage properly: the scorch-marks weren't random. They radiated outward from the man's chest in deliberate lines, like frost-flower, like something had been *growing* through him. Something that was eating him alive to keep itself lit.

"They're watching the roads," the man rasped. "Behind me. Small ones, silver-eyed, they count the crossings—" He coughed, and the cough produced light, drifting motes that rose toward the rafters and winked out. "I ran the border at Gray Ford. It nearly took everything. Nearly took—" His hand shot out and seized Rafael's wrist with shocking strength. "Listen. There isn't time. There has never been time, that's how they keep it, that's how they *win*—"

"You need a healer—"

"There are no healers for this." Almost a laugh. "There are no healers for me anywhere. I am spent, mercenary. Spent and spilled. But the debt isn't." His grip tightened. "Your mother—"

The word went through Rafael like a spear through wet canvas.

"My mother," he repeated, very quietly, "has been dead eleven years."

"I know what she did." The man pulled him closer, close enough that Rafael could see the light flickering behind his teeth when he spoke. "I know what she *paid*. I carried the ledger. I carried it thirty years, waiting for the account to come due, and the account has your name on it, Rafael Vasquez. Your *true* name. The name she buried so deep even you don't remember it—"

"What are you talking about?"

But the man wasn't listening anymore. He was fumbling inside his coat, and the motion tore the scorch-lines wider; light poured out of him now in earnest, pooling in his lap, running off the chair. With fingers that shook and smoked, he drew out a folded thing — leather, oilcloth-wrapped, dense — and pressed it hard against Rafael's chest with both hands, the way you'd press a wound.

"Take it," he said. "Take it *down*. Beneath Cael. Follow it to the end. And Rafael—" His colorless eyes fixed on Rafael's face with terrible tenderness, as if he were looking at someone else entirely, someone long dead or long awaited. "She sang to you. Before she gave you away. She sang the whole night through, and the court still remembers the song. They play it in the vault. Every night. For *you*."

The light in him surged — one great pulse, gold flooding the whole tavern, throwing every face into sharp relief, Odette's horror, Marguerite's white stare from the corner table — and then it went out all at once, like a snuffed candle, and the man slumped forward into Rafael's arms weighing suddenly nothing at all, nothing, a coat full of ash and finished business.

For a moment nobody moved. The rain ticked against the windows. Somewhere upstairs, a floorboard creaked.

"Rafael," Marguerite said carefully, from very far away. "What did he give you?"

Rafael looked down. The oilcloth package sat in his hands, warm as a living thing. Slowly, because his fingers didn't entirely want to obey, he peeled back the wrapping.

A map. Ink on skin-pale vellum, drawn in a hand fine as needlework: the valley, the border stones, Mount Cael — and beneath Cael, descending in spiraling lines through the mountain's roots, passages marked in a script he didn't know, annotated in margins crowded with notes in three languages. And at the top, in the same fine hand, a title:

*The Account of Raffaello Vásquez-del-Agua, sold in the year of the dry stars.*

His childhood name. A name he hadn't heard spoken aloud since he was four years old, since his mother used to sing it into his hair at bedtime, syllables soft as water — a name that had gone into the ground with her, that no living soul should have known.

The tavern was silent. The rain fell. Somewhere beyond the border stones, far to the east, something silver-eyed was counting crossings, and had perhaps just counted one too few.

Marguerite crossed the room slowly and looked down at the map over his shoulder. When she spoke, her voice had none of its usual theater in it at all.

"Rafael," she said. "Why does a map to hell have your name on it?"
