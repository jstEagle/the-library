## 2. A Debt Written in Ash

Marguerite had read contracts written in blood, in bone, in the ash of burned cities, and once — memorably — in the tears of a man who had sold his own shadow. She was not easily impressed by strange ink.

But she had never seen a map that bled.

She'd laid it flat on her worktable at dawn, weighted at the corners with candlesticks and a chipped teacup, and for three hours it had done nothing but sit there, smug as a closed door. The parchment itself was wrong in ways she couldn't name: too warm to the touch, like skin; faintly pulsing when she pressed her ear against it, like something sleeping beneath. And the margins — the margins were full of writing that refused to be read. Not invisible. She could see every curl and stroke of it, sharp and deliberate, but whenever her eyes tried to follow a line, the meaning slid away like water off oiled leather.

Which meant it wasn't ordinary script. It was script with an opinion about who should read it.

"Fine," she muttered, dipping her pen. "Be difficult."

Marguerite's method for stubborn documents was simple: stop reading them as documents. A map was a claim. A claim had a structure — grantor, grantee, consideration, condition. Whoever wrote this wanted someone to find Mount Cael and *not* understand what they were carrying there. So she stopped trying to read the words and started reading the hand. The pressure of the strokes. Where the writer hesitated. Where the quill had dug deep enough to score the parchment, and where it had gone feather-light, almost apologetic.

The apologetic parts were the interesting ones.

By noon she had a system: she copied the margin-script blind, without looking at it directly, letting her hand move while her eyes stayed fixed on the candle flame. It was a trick from a forger in Vell Marrow who could write confessions in languages he didn't speak. Her copies came out looking like moth-wing scribbles, but scribbles had shapes, and shapes repeated, and one shape repeated more than any other.

A name.

Not spelled out — *rendered*. Folded into the flourishes the way a face is folded into a crowd. But once she saw it, she couldn't unsee it, because it was a name she knew better than her own. A name she'd been calling across taverns and battlefields and bad decisions for eleven years.

*Rafael.*

No. Not Rafael. The letters were older than that, arranged differently, a child's name — the kind of name a mother gives before anyone else has a chance to shorten it. She sat back, and her chair creaked, and the map pulsed once beneath its candlestick weights, patient as a heartbeat.

*Collateral,* said the next line, once she finally cracked the cipher around dusk — a substitution wheel keyed to phases of the moon, which was obscene, which was theatrical, which told her exactly what kind of creature had written it. *Held in trust against the debt of rain. Sold before first breath. Value: whole and unspent.*

She read it four times. Then she read it aloud, quietly, in the empty room, because some things don't become real until you hear them say them.

"*Sold before first breath.*"

Outside, the valley went on being the valley — the godless valley, the dry-boned valley, where magic came to die and mercenaries were worth their weight precisely because they were made of nothing but meat and stubbornness. Somewhere out there, Rafael was probably drinking. Or sharpening something. Or doing that thing he did where he stood in doorways looking like a man waiting for a god to answer a letter he'd stopped sending years ago.

She thought about his mother. Everyone in the valley had a story about Elena Vasquez — the woman who walked into the hills during the drought years and came back with rain. The woman who never said how. The woman whose son grew up asking questions she answered with silence, until even the silence had a shape, and the shape was a locked door.

Marguerite had always assumed the story was embellished. Droughts end. Women walk into hills. Coincidence makes saints out of survivors.

*Value: whole and unspent.*

"Oh, Rafael," she said, to no one. "You absolute idiot."

Because here was the thing she understood now, sitting alone with a bleeding map and a cold cup of tea: he would go. Of course he would go. He'd spent ten years serving a god that had gone quiet, taking contracts in a valley where his sword was the only miracle on offer, and somewhere under all that ordinariness he still believed — she'd seen it, in the way he flinched at church bells — that the silence meant something. That it was a message he hadn't decoded yet.

And now someone had sent him a letter after all. Written in his mother's handwriting, or close enough to break his heart.

She fed the fire and thought about burning it right then. Just — done. Map in the grate, story over, Rafael never knowing. He'd sulk for a month. He'd call her a thief. He'd get over it, because that was what people did, and he'd live out his life as an ordinary man in a valley where ordinary men got to grow old, and someday he'd die in bed with his boots off, which was more than most mercenaries got.

It was, objectively, the kindest thing she could do.

Her hand hovered over the grate for a long time. The firelight made the parchment glow from within, and for a moment she could have sworn the margin-script rearranged itself — not into words, but into a look. The look of a thing that knew what she was considering and found it quaint.

She took her hand back.

"No," she told it. "I'm not doing this for you. I'm doing it so he hears it from me."

* * *

He heard it from her at the Broken Antler, in the corner booth with the wobbly leg, because Marguerite believed bad news traveled best with beer.

She put the map on the table between them, face down, and kept her palm flat on top of it like a woman holding down a lid.

"I need you to listen to all of it before you say anything," she said.

Rafael looked at the map, then at her, and she watched him do the arithmetic he always did — cataloguing exits, weighing threats, deciding whether the person across from him was a problem. Eleven years of partnership, and he still ran the numbers on her first. It used to annoy her. Now she found it almost tender, the way you find a dog's habit of guarding the door tender.

"You've been reading it," he said.

"I've been reading it." She turned it over. "The margins are a contract. Moon-keyed cipher, Unseelie handiwork, very old. It's a bill of sale, Rafael. For a soul. Yours. Your mother sold it before you were born — 'sold before first breath' is the exact phrase — and the payment was rain. The drought ended because of you. You weren't blessed. You were *purchased.*"

The tavern noise went on around them — dice, arguments, a fiddler murdering a reel — but their booth might as well have been underwater. Rafael didn't move. His face did something complicated and then did nothing at all, which was worse.

"The valley," he said finally. "Magic can't cross the boundary. That's why we're hired. That's why spells fail here."

"That's why your soul isn't *here*, maybe. Maybe it's the other way around. Maybe the valley is dry because—" She stopped herself. "I don't know. What I know is that the map leads under Mount Cael, to the court beneath it, and the contract names you as collateral held in trust. They've had you in a vault since before you drew breath, and now something up there wants you delivered."

"So we deliver me." He reached for the map.

She slapped her hand over it. "No."

"It's my name on it, Marguerite."

"Exactly! It's your *name*, written by things that eat names!" She leaned across the table, voice dropping to the register she saved for horses and hopeless men. "Listen to me. Everything about this document is designed to be followed. The cipher was hard enough to feel clever, easy enough to be broken. The route goes through country no sane mercenary enters. This isn't a map, it's a *funnel.* Something under that mountain has been waiting ten years — or thirty — for you to come collect yourself, and I promise you the price at the counter is not going to be coin."

"My mother—"

"Your mother traded you for rain and then never told you. Never wrote you. Never left you so much as a note explaining why. Ten years of silence, Rafael. She chose that silence over you, and now a piece of enchanted paper shows up wearing her handwriting, and you want to *follow it home?*"

"She's dead," he said quietly. "She died two winters ago. I never got to ask her anything. This is the only letter she ever sent me, even if she didn't send it." His jaw worked. "You burn it, and I spend the rest of my life not knowing. I've done ten years of not knowing. I'm not doing forty more."

"Burn it," she said anyway, because she had promised herself she'd say it plainly at least once. "Burn it and let's take the Harrow contract, the boring one, the one that pays. Let's be ordinary. Ordinary is *safe*, Rafael. Ordinary is the whole point of this valley."

"And you?" Something flickered behind his eyes — not anger. Hurt, arriving dressed as anger, the way it always did with him. "Since when do you care about safe? You forged a bishop's seal to win a bet. You fought a duel over a mispronounced word. You've never once taken the safe road in your life, and now, the moment the road leads somewhere that matters to *me*, suddenly you're a farmer?"

"That's not fair."

"No," he agreed. "It's not. None of this is fair. My whole life was decided before I was born and nobody asked me." He pulled the map gently out from under her hand. She let him. "I'm going. Tomorrow, at first light, through the eastern pass. You don't have to come."

And there it was. The trap she'd built for herself, sprung shut with perfect precision: he was going, and she could either let him walk into a funnel alone or walk into it beside him, and both options were terrible, and neither one was a choice at all.

She drained her beer. She thought about the fire, and her hovering hand, and the kindness she hadn't committed.

"If you die under that mountain," she said, "I'm going to be so angry at you. Do you understand? I will hold grudges beyond death. I will haunt *you.*"

The corner of his mouth moved. Not quite a smile. Close enough. "That's the spirit."

"This is the stupidest thing we've ever done, and I'm including the bishop."

"We haven't done the mountain yet."

"Don't remind me." She stood, slinging her pack over her shoulder, and paused with her hand on the booth's edge. "One condition. If it comes down to a bargain — if something down there offers you power, or answers, or your mother's voice, or whatever else they keep in the dark — you tell me before you shake hands. Promise me that much."

He looked at the map, curled now in his fist like a sleeping animal.

"I promise," he said.

Later — much later, walking home through streets that smelled of woodsmoke and dust, past the chapel where the god's silence hung like weather — Marguerite admitted to herself what she'd known since noon: she wasn't coming along to protect him from the court.

She was coming along to protect him from himself. From the part of him that had liked the silence, that had built a religion out of waiting, that would hear the word *belonging* whispered in the dark and mistake it for home.

At the valley's edge, tomorrow, the magic would come flooding back. She'd seen travelers cross the boundary once, years ago — seen them stagger like drowning men surfacing, drunk on a world they'd forgotten existed.

Whatever was waiting for Rafael under Mount Cael wouldn't have to fight him.

It would only have to welcome him.
