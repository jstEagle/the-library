## 3. Where the Map Ends

The valley ended the way all endings do: gradually, then all at once.

For three days we walked the old drover's road north, past fields gone gold and stubbled, past shrines where the god's face had been carved into standing stones and weathered now to something featureless and patient. Marguerite walked behind me with the map open across her saddlebow even though there was nothing left to read. She'd memorized it. I think she wanted me to know she'd memorized it — that if this went wrong, she would be able to find our way back without asking anything of me but my legs.

"You're quiet," she said on the third morning.

"I'm always quiet."

"You're quiet like a man rehearsing an argument he's already lost." She folded the map with a snap. "Say it anyway."

I didn't have an argument. What I had was a feeling in my chest like a held breath, ten years long, and the sense that at the valley's edge something was waiting to exhale through me. The stranger in the tavern had bled light. He had died saying my name — not Rafael Vasquez, mercenary, but *Rafael*, the small unguarded version, the one only one person alive had ever used. My mother. Who had gone silent the same year the god did, and never told me why.

"Keep walking," I said.

"That's what I thought you'd say."

* * *

We felt the boundary before we saw it. There was no wall, no marker, no line drawn in the dirt — just a change in the air, the way a room changes when someone opens a door onto a warmer one. The birds stopped. Then everything else did too, the whole world holding its note, and I understood that for ten years I had been living inside a silence so complete I'd mistaken it for peace.

Then we crossed, and magic came back like water through a broken dam.

It hit me first as sound — a chord struck somewhere below hearing, vast and dissonant and *alive*. Then as color: the grass flared green beyond green, the sky deepened to a blue with weight to it, and every stone on the roadside glimmered faintly, as though remembering it had once been molten. My knees nearly went. I put a hand against a boulder to steady myself and the boulder hummed under my palm like a struck bell, warm, welcoming, *aware*.

"Rafael." Marguerite's voice came from very far away. "Rafael, your eyes."

I couldn't answer. Something was rising in me — something that had been lying dormant so long I hadn't known it was there, the way you don't know you've been carrying a stone until you set it down. It unfurled along my spine like heat. It knew things. It knew the names of the trees. It knew that the stream ahead of us ran over buried silver, that the hawk circling above had been born twice, that the road itself was older than the mountain and remembered every foot that had ever pressed it. Ten years of numbness peeling away all at once, and underneath —

Underneath, I was *more*. That was the terrible part. Not different. More. Every fear sharper, every hunger louder, every memory lit from within like a lamp turned up past its wick. I could feel the map in Marguerite's pack the way you feel your own heartbeat. I could feel the direction of the court beneath Mount Cael as surely as I felt north. And threaded through all of it, faint as a vein of ore, I could feel *myself* — the sold thing, the collateral, stirring in whatever dark place it had been kept, turning toward me like a child waking in a crib.

I liked it.

God help me, I liked it. Ten years of being ordinary, of being hired precisely because spells slid off me like rain off wax, of being *nothing special* in a valley where nothing was special — and here, one step past the border, I was the most extraordinary thing on the road. Power sat in me like wine in an empty stomach. It whispered that I had been robbed. It whispered that whoever had kept it from me owed me ten years of interest.

"Rafael." Marguerite had my shoulder. Her face was pale, her freckles stark. "You're glowing. Just slightly. Around the edges. Like a bad candle."

I made myself breathe. In. Out. The glow guttered. The chord beneath the world softened to a murmur, though it never quite stopped — I understood now that it never stopped out here, that everyone who lived beyond the valley simply learned to stop hearing it, the way you stop hearing your own blood.

"It's still there," I said. My voice sounded wrong. Richer. "Inside me."

"I know." She said it flatly, which was how she said the things that frightened her most. "And when we're done — whatever we find down there — you give it back or you bury it or you do whatever has to be done. Because I've seen men find their true selves, Rafael, and it's never once made them easier to drink with."

I laughed. It came out cracked. We walked on, and the murmur walked with us, and I caught myself listening to it the way a starving man listens to a kitchen.

* * *

Mount Cael did not look like a mountain. It looked like a wound that had healed wrong — a mass of black granite shouldering up out of the earth, its peak lost in cloud, its lower slopes glassy-smooth as if something enormous had once flowed over them and cooled. No trees grew on it. No snow settled on it, though the wind coming off its heights carried cold enough to ache in the teeth. As we climbed the last mile of scree, the murmur of the world's magic thinned and changed key, dropping into something low and mournful, a dirge played underground.

The gate was a crack in the mountain's flank, tall as a cathedral door, framed in stone carved with faces — thousands of them, generations of them, mortals and others, all with their eyes closed. All asleep. Except that as we approached, I swear the eyes began to open, one by one, following us.

And in front of the gate, coiled around the threshold like a moat of scales and shadow, lay the dragon.

She was dying. There was no mistaking it, no polite way around it. She had been crimson once — I could see it in the deep garnet gleam of the scales along her spine — but the color had drained from her the way color drains from a fire settling into ash. Her wings were folded tight against ribs that rose and fell too slowly. Her eyes, when they opened, were the exact orange of a coal an hour after the flames have gone: beautiful, banked, and nearly finished.

"*Mortals,*" she said. Her voice was a landslide heard through a wall. "*Two of them. Walking toward the Unseelie court with their chins up. How charming. How brief.*"

Marguerite's hand found her knife. Mine, I noticed, stayed exactly where it was, because some animal part of me — the new part, the awake part — recognized what she was and refused to insult either of us by pretending otherwise.

"We're looking for the way down," I said.

"*Everyone who comes here is looking for the way down.*" One vast eye rotated toward me, and the coal-bright gaze fixed on my chest, on the place where the stirring thing lived. The dragon's nostrils flared. Smoke curled out, thin and gray, more breath than fire. "*Oh,*" she said softly. "*Oh, little stolen one. You smell of hothouses. You smell of the queen's garden.*"

My skin crawled. "You know what I am."

"*I know what everyone is. It is the one advantage of being old enough to remember when the mountain was open and the courts still traded fairly.*" She shifted, and the movement cost her — I saw the tremor run down her neck, the effort of lifting her head. "*I am Emberlast. Last of the fire-drakes. I guard this gate because guarding is the last work left to me, and because what sleeps below must not come up. But you are going down, and I cannot stop you, and I find I don't wish to. Which interests me.*"

"Why?" Marguerite asked. Straight to it. That was Marguerite.

Emberlast regarded her for a long moment. Then she extended one foreleg — a terrible slow unfolding, joints cracking like green wood — and opened her talons.

In her palm lay an ember.

Not a coal. An ember the size of a robin's egg, glowing a white so pure it hurt to look at, radiating heat I could feel from six feet away — real heat, ancient heat, the heat of the first fires, of volcanoes and forge-hearts and the sun's own childhood. It pulsed slowly, like a heart. Like a very tired heart.

"*This,*" Emberlast said, "*is the last of my fire. When it goes out, I go out. There will be no more dragons, and the world will be a colder place by exactly one warmth, and no one will ever know the difference.*" The great eye closed briefly. When it opened again, something had moved behind it that I was not meant to see and could not unsee. "*The queen collects flames. She keeps them in her vaults, pretty and caged, trophies of the age when things burned freely. If your soul is in her keeping, little stolen one, then you will stand in her hall. And I am asking — I, Emberlast, who has begged nothing of anyone in four hundred years — I am asking you to carry my ember down with you. To hold it in her presence. To let me see it burn in that place, one last time, before the end.*"

The wind came down off the mountain and smelled of iron and old snow.

"And the price?" I asked, because there is always a price, and I had learned that much from the valley if nothing else.

"*The price is that you carry it,*" she said. "*That you keep it close to your skin, where your new-woken soul can feel it. That is all. That is everything.*"

I understood then, in the wordless way one understands a trap only after stepping in it, that this was the true toll. Not labor. Proximity. She was handing me temptation wrapped in grief, and she knew it, and she was doing it anyway, because dragons are honest that way and dying creatures can afford honesty.

I took the ember.

It weighed nothing and everything. Its heat sank through my palm and up my arm and met the stirring thing inside me, and the two of them — the ember and the soul — recognized each other like siblings separated at birth. For one blinding moment I felt what the world had felt before the courts, before the debts, before the god went silent: whole, burning, unafraid.

Then it settled against my chest, banked and patient, and the feeling faded to an ache. An appetite.

"Rafael," Marguerite said quietly. "Your eyes are doing the thing again."

Behind the dragon, the carved faces on the gate had all opened their eyes now, and every one of them was looking at me, and far below — down through miles of bone-lit stone — something in the queen's garden had just turned toward the surface, the way a flower turns toward a window.

Emberlast lowered her great head onto her forelegs, closed her eyes, and began, very softly, to sing a dirge for herself.

The gate stood open. We went in.
