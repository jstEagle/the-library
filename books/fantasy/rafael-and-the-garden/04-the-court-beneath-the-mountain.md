## 4. The Court Beneath the Mountain

The tunnels began where the map ended, which Marguerite had decided was the map's way of being clever.

They went down through a fissure in Mount Cael's southern flank, a crack barely wide enough for Rafael's shoulders, and the first thing she noticed was that the dark was wrong. Not empty-dark, not cave-dark — *lit* dark. Pale light seeped from the walls themselves, and when she dragged her glove along the stone to steady herself, her fingers came away understanding why.

Bones. The walls were made of bones, fused and polished until they gleamed like riverstone, thousands upon thousands of them laid in courses like masonry. Ribs arched overhead in rib-vaults that were not a pun but an architecture. Femurs formed columns. Skulls had been set into the walls at intervals, facing inward, their eye sockets filled with something luminous and slow-moving, like honey given light.

"They're watching," Rafael said behind her.

"They're *lighting*," Marguerite corrected. "Watching is extra."

She heard him exhale through his nose — his version of a laugh when he was too tired or too frightened to commit to one. She kept walking. It was easier than admitting her hands were shaking, and it was much easier than telling him what she'd read in the map's margins three nights ago, by candlelight, while he slept with his sword across his knees like always.

*Collateral,* the margins said, in a hand so fine it might have been etched with a needle. *Sold before first breath. Held in trust against the debt of rain.*

She had held the page over the candle flame after that. She'd held it there for a long time. And then she'd folded it back up, because burning it would only mean he never learned, and Rafael Vasquez did not forgive secrets kept *for* him. He forgave almost anything else.

"Marguerite." His voice came low. "Stop."

She stopped. Ahead, the tunnel opened into a chamber where the bone-light pooled like milk, and standing in that pool were two figures that had no business standing anywhere.

They were tall the way reeds are tall — thin, jointed wrong, dressed in garments that moved as if underwater. Their faces were beautiful the way knives are beautiful: all edge and intention. One of them held a lantern made from what looked disturbingly like a birdcage, and inside the cage, instead of a bird, there was a small writhing light that beat itself against the bars.

A soul. Or close enough to one that Marguerite's stomach turned over.

"Brokers," the taller figure said. Its voice was pleasant, which made it worse. "You've come to trade."

It wasn't a question, but Marguerite answered it anyway, because that was the plan, and the plan was hers, and if the plan died here then everything else she'd traded to be here died with it.

"We've come to sell," she said. "There's a difference. Sellers get paid."

Something flickered across the creature's face — amusement, maybe, or the memory of it. "And what do you sell?"

"Access," she said smoothly. "Valley-brokers. You know how it works below. Magic can't cross the border, souls can't be carried out, but *names* pass freely. We collect names. Debts, contracts, inheritances — paper promises on mortal souls, worth their weight in gold down here, and you can't dig for them yourselves because your kind burns at the valley line." She let that hang. "We're the only bridge you have. We're expensive."

It was a good lie. She'd built it over four days of descent, testing each plank, and it held — mostly because it was built on truth. Mortals who worked the valley's edge really did broker in names and debts; she'd spent two years among them before Rafael, learning exactly this kind of fluency.

The shorter creature drifted closer to Rafael. He stood very still, the way he did around anything dangerous, which was to say the way he did around everything.

"This one smells of the valley," it murmured. "Of dust and iron and—" It paused. Its head tilted at an angle no neck should allow. "*Familiar.*"

"He's my muscle," Marguerite said. "He doesn't smell of anything but work."

But the creature was still looking at him, and Rafael was looking back, and Marguerite saw something pass over his face that she had seen only once before — the night the stranger died in the tavern with Rafael's name on his lips. Recognition running the wrong direction. Being known by something you had never met.

"Proceed," the tall one said at last, and gestured with its cage-lantern, and the wall of bones ahead of them simply opened, vertebrae sliding apart like a lock accepting a key.

* * *

The court beneath the mountain was the most beautiful thing Marguerite had ever seen, and she hated it immediately and completely.

The cavern could have swallowed the whole valley. Stalactites hung like chandeliers, each one crusted with crystals that burned cold blue-white, and between them hung gardens — actual gardens, upside-down, growing from the ceiling in cascades of black roses and silver-leafed vines whose flowers chimed softly when the air moved. Water fell somewhere in the distance, but it fell upward, she realized; sheets of it climbing the far walls in slow silver ribbons.

And the people. The court was full of them — the tall jointed folk of the gate, yes, but also others: things with antlers of glass, things wearing faces like porcelain masks that occasionally blinked, and scattered among them, mortals. Living mortals, dressed finely, moving carefully, their eyes holding the particular blankness of people who had stopped expecting to leave.

Everywhere, the light-cages. Dozens of them. Hundreds. Hung from hooks and carried on poles and worn at belts, and inside each one a small bright struggling thing, and Marguerite understood with a lurch that they weren't decorations.

They were currency. Mortals were currency. A man nearby paid for a glass of something smoking with a cage containing a light that flickered like a candle in wind, and the vendor took it without ceremony, the way a baker takes a coin.

"Don't look at them too long," Rafael murmured. His voice was steady. His knuckles, she noticed, were white around the strap of his pack.

"I'm not looking at them," she said. "I'm counting exits."

"How many?"

"One. Behind us. Through several hundred things that want us dead."

"So we make new ones."

"That's the spirit."

They crossed the floor toward the raised dais at the cavern's heart, and Marguerite played her role — chin up, ledger under her arm, the walk of a woman whose time costs money. Around them the court watched with polite, terrible interest. A child-shaped thing offered Rafael a sweet from a dish of what appeared to be tiny folded stars, and he declined with such careful courtesy that Marguerite nearly laughed despite everything.

Then the crowd parted, and the queen came down from her throne, and every thought Marguerite had ever organized abandoned her at once.

The queen of the Unseelie court was small. That was the shock of it. Small and slight, wrapped in grey silk that moved like smoke deciding to be cloth, with hair the color of frost forming and eyes like the moment just before snowfall. She looked young. She looked old. She looked like every age at once, the way a portrait looks like you if you stare long enough.

She walked past Marguerite as though Marguerite were furniture.

She stopped in front of Rafael.

"There you are," the queen said softly.

The court went silent. Even the upside-down roses stopped chiming.

Rafael didn't move. Marguerite watched him not-move with the whole of her attention, cataloguing every detail the way she'd been trained to: his weight settled back on his heels, ready to retreat; his right hand loose near his belt; his breathing even, deliberately even, the breathing of a man performing calm.

"Do I know you?" he asked.

"No," the queen said, and smiled, and it was the smile that undid Marguerite — because it wasn't hungry, it wasn't cruel, it was *warm*. It was the smile of an aunt at a wedding. Of a mother at a door. "Not yet. But I have known you since before you knew yourself. I rocked you," she said, "before you had ears to hear it. I kept what was given to me safe and warm all these long years, and now here you come, walking down into the dark all on your own, straight to me." Her head tilted. "Home should feel like something, little collateral. Tell me — does this feel like nothing?"

"*Don't answer her,*" Marguerite said sharply.

The queen glanced at her then, finally, and Marguerite felt the look go through her like a needle finding a nerve. "Ah. The broker. You carry a heavy ledger, child. So many pages torn out." Something flickered in those snowfall eyes — curiosity, or appetite. "We'll speak again."

Then she turned back to Rafael, took his hand in both of hers — small, cool, gentle as turning soil — and pressed it briefly against her cheek.

"Welcome home," the queen said.

And Rafael — Rafael who flinched from kindness, who trusted nothing free, who had spent ten years serving a god that never once answered him — Rafael's face *broke*. Just for a heartbeat. Just enough. Marguerite saw it happen: the longing rising in him like floodwater, the terrible naked wish to belong somewhere, anywhere, to be claimed by something that claimed him gladly.

The court began, very quietly, to applaud.

Marguerite stepped forward and put her hand on Rafael's arm, and felt him trembling under her grip like a struck bell.

"We should go," she whispered.

"Yes," he said, and didn't move. His eyes were still on the queen. The queen was still smiling at him like family.

And above them, hanging from the great crystal chandeliers, hundreds of caged lights had begun — all together, all at once — to sing.
