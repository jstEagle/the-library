## 6. Marguerite's Bargain

The court did not sleep. That was the first thing Marguerite understood about it, and the thing she hated most.

She had spent three nights in a guest chamber carved from pale bone-glass, lying awake beneath a ceiling that shimmered like the inside of a shell, listening to music that never quite ended — it only faded, the way a fire fades before flaring again. Somewhere below, the Unseelie danced. Somewhere above, Rafael sat at the queen's right hand, laughing at jokes he should not have found funny, wearing clothes someone had laid out for him, drinking wine that tasted of whatever the drinker wanted most.

That was the trap of it. Not chains. Comfort.

Marguerite had known Rafael Vasquez for eleven years. She had pulled an arrow out of his shoulder in a ditch outside Harrowmere while he made jokes about her stitching. She had watched him bury clients, bury enemies, bury a dog whose name he still wouldn't say aloud. She knew how he took his coffee — bitter, no sugar, because sweetness was for people who hadn't earned their mornings. She knew that when he lied, his left hand curled shut like a fist around a coin that wasn't there.

And for three days, his left hand had been open. Relaxed. Empty.

He was not lying to her. That was the horror. He wasn't lying at all. The court had simply made forgetting feel like arriving.

*You could leave,* she told herself on the third night, standing at the window of the guest chamber, watching phosphorescent things drift through tunnels far below like lantern-fish in a black sea. *You could walk out the way you came in.*

But Emberlast's ember sat in its iron lantern by the door, dimming. Each day it burned a little lower, a little more like a coal remembering it had once been a sun. And each day Rafael came back from the queen's hall with something new in his face — wonder first, then ease, then a kind of quiet happiness that Marguerite had never once seen on him in eleven years of contracts and cold camps and shared bread.

He looked happy. That was the worst weapon anyone had ever used against her.

* * *

The broker kept shop in a side tunnel off the Court of Small Debts — a niche hung with brass bells that rang without wind, lit by candles that burned blue and gave off the smell of snow. The broker itself was small and grey and sexless, wrapped in layers of moth-eaten velvet, with eyes like two coins at the bottom of a well.

"You are the mortal who is not collateral," it said when Marguerite ducked under the curtain. Its voice sounded like pages turning. "Interesting. Everyone here is something. What are you?"

"Looking for directions."

"Everything is directions," the broker said. "The question is what you can pay."

Marguerite set the lantern down between them. Emberlast's ember pulsed once, weakly, and the broker drew back with a hiss like steam escaping a pipe.

"Not that. Never that." She'd rehearsed this part in the dark for hours, and now that the moment came, her voice held steady, which surprised her. "I want the way into the Vault of Sold Names. The real way. Not the front door with its silver bells and its alarms."

"The vault has no back door," said the broker. "It has only prices. Everything in my court has only prices."

"Then name one."

The broker's coin-eyes turned slowly, considering her. Around them the brass bells shivered though there was no draft. Marguerite felt suddenly, acutely aware of what she was: a mortal woman in a court where mortals were currency, offering to negotiate.

"You have little gold," the broker said. "Little magic — none, I think, though you carry the ember's warmth like a second heart. You have your sword, your wits, and—" it leaned forward, and its velvet wrappings rustled like dry leaves, "—your memories. Eleven years of them, if I read you rightly. Rich seams. The court pays beautifully for mortal memory. It tastes of time."

"No," Marguerite said. Too fast. She heard herself say it too fast and hated it.

"Of course not," the broker agreed pleasantly. "No one ever says yes on the first night. They come back. They always come back. Memory is the one treasure mortals guard worse than gold, because they cannot see it spending."

"I'm not coming back."

"Mm." The broker poured something dark and steaming into a cup that might have been a skull or might have been ceramic; it was impossible to tell, and Marguerite decided it was kinder not to know. "Then ask me a smaller question. Ask what the vault costs to enter, rather than the way in. Knowledge of the price is cheaper than knowledge of the path."

"And what does that cost?"

"One hour," said the broker. "One hour of a memory of your choosing. A small one. An hour you would not miss."

Everyone says that, Marguerite thought. Everyone thinks there's an hour they wouldn't miss.

But she needed the price, and the price was cheap, and cheap things in this court were the most expensive things of all — she knew that too, knew it in her bones, and yet.

"Fine," she said. "An hour. But I choose which."

"Naturally." The broker slid the cup across the table. "Drink, and think of the hour as you drink. My craft will find it."

Marguerite looked at the cup. Steam curled up in shapes that almost meant something. Somewhere far away, faint through miles of bone and stone, she could hear the court's endless music starting up again, rising toward another night that would never end.

Rafael had been at that music every night. Laughing. His hand open.

She drank.

It tasted of winter and copper and — absurdly — coffee, bitter, no sugar, and for one half-second she was in a camp outside Harrowmere with dawn coming grey over the hills and Rafael complaining about the stitching in his shoulder, saying *you do that on purpose, Marguerite, you enjoy watching me suffer*, and her saying *yes*, and both of them laughing—

The broker inhaled sharply, like a man smelling money.

"Oh," it said softly. "Oh, that is a *rich* seam. Eleven years, all threaded together. Do you know what the collectors would pay for a friendship like this? Whole centuries of it. Mortals make so little that lasts."

"I asked for one hour."

"You asked the price of the vault." The broker's coin-eyes glittered. "Here is the answer you bought: the vault opens for no key and no force. It opens for *recognition*. A soul knows its own bottle. Your friend must stand before ten thousand sold names and call his own — but the bottles are warded against theft, and the warding drinks intention. He cannot go in wanting. He cannot go in planning. The vault must catch him *unguarded*. Distracted. While his attention — and the attention of everyone who would shield him — is elsewhere."

Marguerite's mouth had gone dry. "Meaning what?"

"Meaning the vault will open for him only when no one is watching him. Including you." The broker folded its grey hands atop the table. "That is the price of the knowledge. Was it worth the hour?"

"That wasn't an hour," Marguerite said slowly. "That was instructions."

"All instruction is memory," said the broker. "All memory is instruction. Now — the second payment."

"I paid one hour. One. We agreed."

"We agreed nothing. You drank. I read." The broker smiled, or did something adjacent to smiling. "But be at peace. I take only what you were already willing to lose. That is the law of my shop, and the queen enforces the law of shops more fiercely than any other law she keeps." It leaned close, and its breath smelled of old paper. "You have been carrying it since you arrived, mortal. The thought you keep pushing down. Say it, and it will weigh nothing at all."

Marguerite said nothing.

"You have been thinking," the broker said gently, horribly kindly, "*if it comes to it, if the choice is his soul walking free or mine staying whole — I know which I would pick.*"

The candlelight bent. The blue flames guttered and stood still.

"Don't," Marguerite said.

"It is a beautiful thing you're carrying. So heavy, and so freely given. The court has not seen its like in nine hundred years." The broker extended one grey hand, palm up. "Give it here, and I will give you the last piece: the hour the vault's warding thins. The court sleeps shallowest on the turning of the water-clock — three nights hence, between the last bell and the first. That is when he goes. That is when you must not be watching."

"I'm not giving you eleven years."

"No," agreed the broker. "Only the thread that holds them. The shape of him in your mind. The reason you stay. Keep every hour, every campfire, every scar — keep the arrow at Harrowmere, keep the dog's name. Only give me the *why*. When you wake, you will remember everything and understand nothing. You will look at this stranger, this Rafael Vasquez, and you will not know why you followed him under a mountain." The coin-eyes gleamed. "And you will protect him anyway. Because some debts run deeper than remembering. That is the trade, mortal. Take it or leave it, and either way, we are finished talking."

Marguerite stood there for a long moment. The music swelled somewhere distant, beautiful and bottomless.

Eleven years. She thought of all of them — the ditches, the contracts, the bad wine, the good bread, the nights they'd split a single blanket in the pass at Vell because neither would admit being cold. She thought: *he'll walk out of here with his soul in his hands or he won't walk out at all.* She thought: *and he can't do it while I'm standing next to him ready to die for him, because the vault drinks intention, and mine would drown us both.*

There was no version of this where she kept herself and saved him. There was only the version where she chose, and then forgot she had chosen, and woke up loving a stranger for no reason she could name.

"Take it," she said.

The broker's hand closed around something invisible and pulled, slow, like drawing a splinter from deep tissue. It didn't hurt. That was the obscene part — it didn't hurt at all. There was just a moment where Marguerite felt the shape of her own life tilt, like furniture moved one inch to the left in a familiar room, and then —

— then she was standing in a candle-lit shop across from a small grey creature, holding a lantern with a dying ember in it, knowing three things with absolute certainty:

The vault's warding thinned three nights hence, between the last bell and the first.

The man called Rafael Vasquez had to be in that vault at that hour, unguarded, unwitnessed.

And she would die before she let anything happen to him.

"Why?" she heard herself ask the broker. Her voice came out rough, bewildered. "Why do I care what happens to him? Who *is* he to me?"

The broker was already wrapping its velvets closed, already losing interest, already turning to its blue candles.

"A stranger," it said. "A stranger you are protecting. Go home, mortal. Sleep. You have work to do."

* * *

She found Rafael in the corridor outside the queen's hall, sitting on a bench of woven silver, looking tired in a way he never let the court see. He glanced up, and his face did the thing it always did — softened, just slightly, the way a door eases open.

"There you are," he said. "Been looking for you everywhere. You missed dinner. The queen sent up—"

"I don't need her food," Marguerite said. "Come on. We need to talk. Tonight. Before the last bell."

Something flickered behind his eyes — suspicion, maybe, or hope — and his left hand began, slowly, to curl shut.

"Why?" he said. "What's tonight?"

Marguerite opened her mouth and found, to her astonishment, that she had no idea. Only the certainty, burning steady as a coal in her chest: *three nights hence, between the last bell and the first, and no one watching.*

"I don't know yet," she said honestly. "But I know it matters. Trust me."

He studied her for a long moment — eleven years of studying her, though she couldn't have said why that number felt true — and then he nodded, and got up, and followed her into the dark.
