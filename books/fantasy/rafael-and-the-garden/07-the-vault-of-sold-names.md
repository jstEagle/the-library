## 7. The Vault of Sold Names

The ember led us down.

It floated a hand's width ahead of Rafael's chest, no larger than a coal from a hearth, and it burned without heat — a small red heart pulsing in the dark, throwing shadows that moved wrong, that leaned toward the walls as if listening. Emberlast's last fire. She had pressed it into his palm at the mountain gate with a claw like a drawn sword and said, *Carry me to the burning place. Let me see flame once more before I go out.* He had promised. He was beginning to understand that promises made to dying things did not stay small.

The tunnels beneath Mount Cael were not carved. They were grown — ribs of black stone arching overhead like the inside of some enormous throat, slick with moisture that gleamed when the ember passed. The air tasted of iron and honey. Somewhere far below, music played: thin, sweet, endless, the kind of melody that could go on for a hundred years without repeating itself and never once be beautiful.

Marguerite walked beside him in silence. That was the wrongness he kept stumbling over, the loose stone his thoughts turned on every few steps. Marguerite silent. Marguerite three paces back instead of at his shoulder, watching him with the polite blank attention of a stranger on a coach.

She had traded her memories of him. Of them. Ten years of partnership, every argument, every shared fire, every time she'd pulled an arrow out of his arm and called him an idiot while she did it — gone, spent like coin to buy him a path through the vaults. The broker had told her nothing. She had woken knowing only two things: that the man beside her was named Rafael Vasquez, and that she would die before she let anything happen to him.

*Why?* she'd asked him once, on the descent, not accusing — genuinely puzzled, which was worse. *Why do I trust you?*

*I don't know,* he'd said.

And she'd nodded, satisfied, because whatever she'd bought with herself, she'd apparently also bought the ability to accept that answer.

"Close now," she murmured. Her voice came out flat in the dark. "I can feel it. Like standing near a beehive."

Rafael felt it too. Not sound exactly — pressure. A hum in his teeth, in the marrow of him, in the hollow space behind his sternum where a soul should sit and where, for thirty years, there had been only quiet. The closer they came to the vault, the louder the quiet got. It had a shape now. It had always had a shape; he simply hadn't been able to hear it from the valley, where magic couldn't follow and neither, presumably, could the thing that had been sold out of him before his first breath.

The corridor opened.

He had imagined a vault — stone, locks, iron. What lay before him was a cathedral of glass.

Thousands upon thousands of bottles, each no bigger than a fist, hung from the ceiling on silver threads or sat nested in alcoves cut into the living rock, tier upon tier rising into darkness beyond the ember's reach. And each one held light. Not firelight, not moonlight — something warmer and more particular, the way every face is particular. Some glowed gold. Some blue as deep water. One near the entrance flickered a sickly green and shivered whenever Rafael looked at it, like a dog that knew it was being watched.

Souls. Sold names. A harvest of everything the court beneath the mountain had ever been paid.

"They're singing," Marguerite whispered.

They were. Faintly, below hearing, below feeling — a chord made of ten thousand voices, none of them words, all of them longing. Rafael stood in the doorway with the ember trembling above his heart and understood, suddenly and completely, why the queen had smiled at him like family. This was her larder. Her garden. Every bottle here had been given freely — that was the horror of it, the thing he couldn't stop turning over. No one had been robbed. They had all been *paid*, or thought they were. Rain for a valley. A cure for a child. One more winter of warmth. And in exchange, the smallest, truest part of themselves, bottled and hung up in the dark to ripen.

His mother had stood somewhere in this arithmetic. Desperate, dust-choked, a valley dying around her and a god who had stopped answering. She had carried something precious down this throat of stone and set it on a table, and the court had weighed it and found it sufficient, and the rain had come.

He had spent his whole life believing his god had abandoned him. It had never occurred to him that his mother might have sold him first.

"The queen's own vault is deeper in," Marguerite said. She had the map — the dead stranger's map — spread against her forearm, though she didn't need it anymore; the broker's price had included the way. "Third gallery past the white roots. There'll be wards."

There were wards. Rafael saw them as they moved into the third gallery — seams of silver light running along the floor like veins of ore, humming that same hive-note, brighter now, angrier. He crouched and studied them the way he'd study any trap in any mercenary job: dispassionately, professionally. That was the trick of himself he'd learned in the valley. Magic failed there, so men like him learned to look at the impossible and ask only one question: *what is it made of, and what breaks it?*

Silver. Everything here was silver. Silver threads, silver seals, silver alarms waiting to sing.

"Emberlast," he said quietly. "A little fire."

The ember drifted down into his cupped hands and pulsed, once, like a creature waking. He held it to the nearest vein of light. For a moment nothing — then the dragon's last flame licked out, thin as a candlewick, and touched the silver, and the silver *remembered what heat was*. The ward-seam softened, sagged, went dark. Fire was older than the court. Fire was older than bargains. Somewhere above them, he felt rather than heard Emberlast stir against the mountain gate, her vast body shifting, her ruined wings dragging stone.

*Hurry,* the ember seemed to say, in the language of cooling coals. *I am not long for this.*

* * *

He found his soul in the deepest alcove, on the highest shelf, apart from the others.

Not because it was grander. Because it was *younger*. The bottles around it held souls gone amber and dense with decades of storage; his sat small and pale and restless, glowing like the first minute after dawn, and it was moving. Turning. Turning inside its glass the way a sleeper turns toward a window.

Rafael reached for it and stopped, his fingers a breath from the glass, shaking.

Because it was singing.

A lullaby. His mother's lullaby — four notes descending, then a pause, then the fourth note held too long, the way she'd sung it when he was small enough to fit against her collarbone, back before the drought, back before the silence, back before she stopped meeting his eyes. The court hadn't just stored his soul. They had *kept* it. Fed it. Nurtured it like a hothouse flower, the queen had said, and he'd heard the words without understanding until now: they had sung to it, for thirty years, in the only voice it knew.

His voice. His mother's voice, kept alive in glass by strangers, while the woman herself grew old and silent in a valley that magic forgot.

"Grief later," Marguerite said softly behind him. Not unkindly. She didn't know why she said it. She said it anyway, because whatever remained of her, down under the bargain, still knew him. "Take it and grief later."

He took the bottle.

The moment his skin met the glass, the singing stopped — and started again *inside him*, pouring up through his palms, his wrists, his ribs, filling the hollow behind his sternum like water finding a dry riverbed. He gasped. It wasn't pain. That was the terrible thing. It was *homecoming*, it was thirty years of quiet ending all at once, and he felt himself become more in a single heartbeat — sharper, vaster, lit from within. The dark of the vault receded as if politely. He could feel the threads of every ward in the mountain. He could feel Emberlast's failing heart two miles above. He could feel the court's music, and underneath it, faint and strained, something enormous holding the mountain shut from within, groaning like a hinge left too long under weight.

*Oh,* he thought, drunk on it. *Oh, so this is what I was.*

The silver screamed.

Every seam of light in the gallery flared white at once, and the hum became a bell, became a thousand bells, and the bottles around them began to rattle on their threads like teeth chattering. Somewhere far above, doors he had slipped past were slamming shut. The court knew. The court had always known; perhaps it had only been waiting to see what he would do with it.

"Run," Marguerite said — and then, as silver light spilled down the corridor behind them and figures began to form within it, tall and glittering and unhurried, she stepped *in front of him*.

He grabbed her arm. "You don't even know why you're doing this—"

"No," she agreed, drawing her blade, calm as Sunday. "But I'm doing it."

The first of the court's guards came on, beautiful and faceless, and Marguerite met it like a landslide, and Rafael ran — ran with his soul singing in his chest and the ember blazing at his shoulder like a small furious star, up through galleries of sleeping light, past ten thousand stolen dawns, while behind him a woman who had forgotten she loved him bled to keep the door open anyway.

Above, through miles of stone, he felt the great hinge strain — felt the silent god feel him coming.

And felt, cold and patient as winter, the queen begin to laugh.
