## 8. What the God Was Doing All Along

The tunnels screamed silver behind them.

Rafael ran with the bottle clutched to his chest like a newborn — because that was what it was, in every way that mattered. Inside the glass, his soul sang his mother's lullaby, soft and cracked and patient, as if it had been waiting ten years in the dark for someone to come carry it home. The sound of it made his eyes burn. He did not have time to weep. He had never had time to weep; that was the whole shape of his life.

Ahead of him Marguerite ran without looking back, her boots striking sparks off the bone-lit stone. She didn't know why she was doing it. He'd seen it in her face when she'd thrown herself between him and the vault's wardens — a stranger's courage, borrowed from somewhere she couldn't name. She had sold herself to buy him an open road, and the price was that she no longer knew there was anything to forgive.

*Marguerite,* he wanted to say. *You called me "Vasquez the Unlucky" the day we met. You stole my boots as a joke and gave them back full of snakes. You are the only person who has ever known me all the way through.*

But she would look at him with those polite, puzzled eyes, and he could not survive that twice.

So he ran, and the mountain ran with them.

That was the first wrongness he noticed, once the initial terror burned down to something he could think inside: the mountain was moving. Not shaking — *breathing*. The walls contracted and released around the tunnel like the ribs of some vast sleeping animal, and each exhalation pushed warm air up past them toward the surface, and each inhalation pulled at their heels like a tide going out. The bone-lights guttered in rhythm with it. The whole of Mount Cael was keeping time.

And beneath the breathing, so faint he felt it more than heard it, was a second pulse. Slower. Wearier. A heartbeat like a bell rung by someone too tired to lift the hammer properly.

Rafael knew that pulse.

He had grown up under it. Every prayer he had ever said in the valley had fallen into silence, and he had assumed — they had all assumed — that the silence meant absence. That the god of the valley had died, or left, or never been there at all, and that this was why magic curdled at the valley's borders like milk in thunder weather. Why mercenaries were hired precisely because they were ordinary. Why Rafael had spent thirty years being nothing in particular in a place where nothing in particular was the safest thing to be.

*Silent*, he had sworn. *Abandoned.* Those were the words he had built himself out of.

They were lies. They had always been lies, and the truth had been holding the roof over his head the entire time.

* * *

They broke through into a cavern the size of a cathedral and stopped, both of them, as if the air itself had thickened into glass.

The chamber was not carved. It was *held*. Great slabs of granite hung suspended in a slow spiral around a central column of light — no, not light. Rafael squinted, and understood, and wished he hadn't. It was a hand. A hand of pale stone and older darkness, fingers spread wide against the underside of the world, pressing upward against a seam in the rock above where daylight leaked through thin as thread. Where the fingers met the seam, the stone glowed white-hot with effort, and flakes of the god's own substance drifted down like snow — grey ash, falling forever, piling in dunes around the wrist.

The god of the valley was not absent. The god of the valley was *underneath*, arms raised, holding the mountain shut from within — holding the court sealed in its burrow like a cork in a wound — and had been doing so for ten years without rest, without word, without one scrap of strength to spare for prayers or rain or the small mercies a god is supposed to provide.

Ten years of drought. Ten years of silence. Ten years of Rafael kneeling in the dust asking *why have you forsaken us* while the answer strained overhead, finger by finger, holding back everything that wanted out.

"It's dying," Marguerite said quietly. She didn't know the god. She didn't know him. And still she said it like a eulogy, because some things you can read off a room the way you read weather. "Look at the ash. It's spending itself. Every year the court takes another soul, the seal gets thinner, and it spends more of itself to compensate." She turned to him, and her puzzlement had sharpened into something almost like grief. "Whoever's up there is losing."

"The queen knows," Rafael said. His voice came out flat and strange. "She's not trying to break out. She's just eating. She eats, the seal weakens, the god spends more, the seal weakens further. She doesn't need a door. She only needs patience." He looked down at the bottle against his chest, at the small warm light of it. "And then I walked in carrying the last piece she needs to finish it."

Above them, through the thread of daylight, came a sound like silk tearing, and the queen stepped through it.

She did not descend so much as arrive, unfolding out of the crack in the world with the unhurried grace of someone who owned the crack. Her gown moved like poured ink. Her crown was a circlet of frozen breath. Behind her came no soldiers — she had no need of them, and the insult of that needlessness was sharper than any blade.

"Vasquez," she said warmly, and the warmth was the worst thing in the mountain. "Look how far you've climbed. Your mother used to climb like that. All heart, all hurry, no plan whatsoever."

"My mother," Rafael said, "sold me."

"She *saved* a valley." The queen drifted closer, and the ash-snow bent around her, avoiding her the way water avoids oil. "Do you know what she offered us first? Rain. Her years. Her memory. We refused it all — too little, too thin, too mortal. But a soul, a whole unbought soul, given freely before its first breath?" She smiled, and it was a family smile, and he hated that he recognized it. "Freely given is the only coin we cannot counterfeit. She paid. It rained. Your neighbors lived. And we kept you the way gardeners keep a seedling — patiently, lovingly, waiting for exactly this day."

"I'm not here to be harvested."

"No." The queen tilted her head, and for a moment the performance fell away, and what looked out of her face was old beyond language and tired beyond pity. "You're here because the god is dying, and you've realized it, and you're doing the arithmetic. So let us do it together, honestly, as family should."

She gestured upward, at the straining hand, at the seam of light, at the whole trembling architecture of the world's mercy.

"That is the last grip your god has. When it fails — days, perhaps hours — my court walks free, and we are hungry, and there is nothing above us but a valley of ordinary people who have forgotten what hunger means. Unless." She extended one pale hand, palm up. An invitation. "Unless the seal is fed. A soul freely reclaimed is worth ten souls freely given — the ledger loves symmetry. Give me what you carry, Vasquez. I will pour it into the seal myself, and your god will live another century, and your valley keeps its quiet, and you go home to your tavern and your contracts and your ordinary, unmagical, beloved life."

"And if I keep it?"

The queen's expression did not change, which was how he knew the answer would be terrible.

"If you keep it, you become the most powerful mortal in the world — do not pretend you haven't felt it, that song in your chest since you crossed the border, that *appetite*. You could take the crown from me; I might even enjoy watching you try. But power must be fed. Yours would feed on the same coin mine does. And your god will die, and the mountain will open, and every stolen soul in my vault will go into the winter along with everyone you have ever fed, cheated, drunk beside, or loved." She let the silence sit a moment. "There is no version of keeping it, child, in which anything survives but you."

Rafael looked at the bottle.

Inside, his soul sang the lullaby, and the lullaby had words he was only now letting himself remember: *sleep, my heart, the rain will come, the rain will come tomorrow.* His mother had sung it while the fields died. His mother had sung it while she walked to the mountain to trade him away. The song wasn't a comfort. It was a promise — the promise she couldn't keep any other way, folded into melody and left with the part of him she sold, so that somewhere in the dark, whatever remained of him would know it had been loved.

Give it back, and the valley lived, and he went home hollow and safe and ordinary, and the god went on holding up the sky with two bleeding hands until the next debt came due.

Keep it, and he became her. He saw it now with perfect clarity, the way you see the bottom of a river when you finally stop splashing: the throne, the crown, the centuries, the appetite — and Marguerite's empty eyes looking at him across a winter with no end.

Every choice cost everything. That was the lesson of the whole mountain. There had never been a door marked *free*.

"There's a third option," Rafael said.

The queen's stillness sharpened. "There is no third—"

"You keep thinking in ledgers," he said, and closed his hand around the bottle, and felt his soul press against the glass from inside like a bird testing a window. "My mother taught me better math than that."

Above them, the god's failing grip shuddered — and the seam of daylight split wider, and through it came the smell of smoke, and wings, and the last ember of the last dragon in the world, falling toward them like a seed.
