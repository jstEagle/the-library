## 9. The Last Ember

*Rafael*

The mountain's throat was a wound of light.

They had climbed for hours through tunnels that grew warmer with every step — not the warmth of stone, but something older, breathing, like the inside of a closed fist. Above them, muffled by a thousand feet of rock, the god's grip on the mountain groaned and shifted. Rafael could feel it now that he knew what to feel: a vast weariness pressing down through the stone, holding shut a door that wanted very badly to open.

Emberlast walked between them, slower than she had been at the gate. Her scales had gone the color of old coals, grey shot through with dying orange, and each breath she took was shorter than the last. But her eyes were bright. Terribly bright.

"How far?" Marguerite asked, for the third time in an hour.

"Close." Rafael touched the bottle at his chest. His soul sang inside it, faint and sweet, his mother's lullaby worn smooth as river stone from ten years of singing in the dark. "I can hear it."

"Hear what?"

"The god. It's not silent up here. It's *tired*."

The tunnel opened without warning into a cavern so vast their torchlight simply gave up, swallowed by dark above and dark below. In the center of the floor — if it could be called a floor — stood a pillar of pale stone, and wrapped around the pillar like a hand around a throat, chains of silver light. They pulsed. Slowly. Like a heartbeat counting down.

And standing before them, in a gown of frost and shadow, her crown a circlet of black ice, waited the Queen of Small Debts.

"You brought it out of my vault," she said. Her voice filled the cavern the way cold fills a valley at dusk. "I confess I wondered if you would. Most thieves stop at gold. But you—" She smiled, and it was the smile Rafael remembered from the court below, warm as a hearth, wrong as a hearth in a house of corpses. "You were always going to come all this way. I raised your soul myself, little Rafael. I know exactly what you are."

"You don't," Rafael said. He stepped forward, putting himself between her and Marguerite, between her and Emberlast. "You kept it in a jar. That's not knowing someone. That's owning a song you never learned to sing."

Her smile did not waver. "Give it back, and I will let the god live. You have seen what it costs me to be merciful." She gestured, delicate, at the straining chains. "It is dying, Rafael. Every year it weakens, every year my court grows fatter and brighter on what leaks through the cracks. Return what is mine, and I will seal the mountain gently. Your valley keeps its silence. Everyone keeps everything they love."

"And if I keep it?"

The temperature dropped. Frost bloomed across the pillar in ferns and feathers.

"Then the god dies screaming, the mountain opens, and winter comes down off Cael like a wolf coming off a hillside. And you—" her voice softened, went almost tender, "—you will still be holding the only soul in creation that has been tended for thirty years in the dark of my court. Do you understand what that means? Power doesn't corrupt, child. Power *ripens*. And yours has ripened beautifully."

Behind him, he heard Marguerite's breath catch. He heard Emberlast's claws scrape stone as the old dragon lowered herself, gathering.

Rafael reached into his coat and drew out the bottle.

His soul looked back at him through the glass — a swirl of gold and green, valley-colored, alive. And it *reached* for him. He felt it the moment his fingers closed around the neck of the bottle: a flood of belonging so total it buckled his knees. Every cold night of his life answered. Every empty prayer. Here, here, here — you were never abandoned, you were only waiting, take yourself back and you will never be small again, never be ordinary, never again stand in a tavern while magic passes over your head like weather—

He wanted it.

Gods forgive him, he wanted it more than he had ever wanted anything, and the wanting felt like coming home.

"Yes," the Queen whispered. "Yes. Feel it. That is what I have kept safe for you all these years. No one else could offer you this. Not your silent god, not your sharp-tongued friend. Only me. Only—"

"*Now,*" Emberlast roared, and the world caught fire.

* * *

*Marguerite*

She didn't remember deciding to trust him.

That was the strange thing, the thing she'd stopped questioning somewhere in the bone-lit dark: she'd woken in the court below knowing nothing about the man beside her except that his name was Rafael and that she would follow him into hell before she'd let him walk there alone. The broker's price was gone from her mind like a tooth pulled clean — no ache where it had been, just smooth absence. She didn't know what she'd paid. She only knew she'd pay it again.

So when the dragon bellowed *now*, Marguerite moved.

Emberlast's fire came first — not the guttering candle-flame of the gate, but something vast and ancient uncoiling from the last deep chamber of the dragon's heart, a furnace opened one final time. The heat hit Marguerite like a wall, drove her behind a spur of rock, and lit the whole cavern noon-bright. The Queen shrieked — actually shrieked, frost flashing to steam off her shoulders — and for one glorious instant the Queen of Small Debts was only a woman flinching from the light.

"THE EMBER!" Emberlast bellowed, and Marguerite understood. The dragon wasn't attacking. She was *spending*. Every scale on her body glowed white-hot; her final fire poured out of her in a torrent, and it wasn't aimed at the Queen at all. It was aimed at the ceiling — at the thousand feet of stone between them and the sky — no. At the chains. At the silver light wrapped around the pillar, which blazed under the fire's touch like a wire drawn taut.

"She's feeding the god!" Marguerite shouted. "Rafael! The fire goes to the god!"

But Rafael wasn't listening. Rafael was on his knees in the center of the fire, untouched, the bottle open in his hands, and the light pouring out of it was wrapping around him like a second skin.

Marguerite ran.

She didn't think about it. Later — if there was a later — she would not be able to explain it, because the part of her that contained the explanation had been traded away in a glittering booth beneath the mountain. She only knew that the man on his knees was hers to protect, that she had always known it, that she would always know it even when she forgot why. She put herself between him and the Queen, drew both knives, and grinned at a creature older than kingdoms.

"Well?" she said. "Come on, then."

The Queen looked at her the way winter looks at a match.

"Mortal," she said, almost pitying. "You don't even know why you're doing this, do you?"

"No," Marguerite said cheerfully. "But I've got two knives and no sense, and you're wearing a dress. My money's on me."

The Queen moved faster than sight. Marguerite's knives met air; cold closed around her throat like a fist; she was lifted off her feet, boots kicking, the world tilting — and through the roaring blood in her ears she saw Rafael rise.

* * *

*Rafael*

He rose, and he was *vast*.

His soul poured into him and he poured into it, and for a moment there was no difference between the man and the thing inside the bottle. He could feel the mountain's roots. He could feel the god above them, a great exhausted hand pressed flat against the underside of the world. He could feel the Queen's stolen court below, thousands of bottled lights singing in the dark. He could feel Marguerite's pulse hammering under the Queen's frozen grip, and he could stop it or save it with a thought, and the thought was *easy*, that was the horror of it, it was the easiest thing he had ever done—

Power didn't corrupt. It ripened. And he could feel himself ripening, sweetness spreading through him like rot through fruit, and some distant part of him — the part that took routine contracts in a valley where spells failed, the part that was hired precisely because he was ordinary — watched all this happen and whispered:

*This is how she started.*

The Queen turned to face him, and for the first time there was fear in her eyes, and beneath the fear, hunger, and beneath the hunger — recognition. Family looking at family.

"You see it now," she breathed. "You see what you are. Take my hand, Rafael. We will not fight. We will *reign*. Winter forever, and you and I its king and queen, and nothing small will ever hurt you again—"

He looked at his hands. They were full of light.

He thought about the valley. About rain that fell because a mother had sold her unborn child to buy it, and children who grew up never knowing the price. About a tavern where nobody cast spells because nobody could, and mercenaries who got work because they were ordinary, and Marguerite — Marguerite, who had followed him without knowing why, whose reasons he owed her back. About a dragon spending her last fire not to save herself but to feed a god she'd never met.

About a garden. He didn't know why he thought about a garden. He'd never planted one. But he suddenly, achingly, wanted to.

"Nothing small," Rafael said, "is the whole point."

And he crushed his soul in his fist.

* * *

*Marguerite*

The cold left her throat all at once and she dropped, rolled, came up with a knife ready — and stopped.

Rafael stood in the center of a detonation of gold-green light, arms spread, mouth open in a cry that was grief and relief in equal measure. The light didn't burn. It *went* — everywhere at once, up through the stone, down through the dark, into the chains, into the dragon, into the Queen.

The Queen screamed. Not in pain — in *loss*. Because the light was passing through her court like spring through a locked house, and every bottled soul it touched burst open, and the cavern filled with voices, thousands upon thousands of voices singing free, rising past them through splitting rock toward a sky Marguerite could suddenly see — the ceiling had cracked wide, stars pouring in, real stars, cold and enormous.

The chains around the pillar flared white and snapped. And from above, from the mountain's very crown, something immense exhaled — a sound like a held breath released after ten years — and the god's presence settled over the mountain not failing but *whole*, renewed, fed by a soul freely given away.

The Queen staggered backward as the light took her crown, her gown, her court, her hoard of small debts, dissolving them into drifting sparks. She looked at Rafael with something that might, in another creature, have been respect.

"What," she said, "did you just do?"

"I refused," Rafael said hoarsely. He swayed. Marguerite caught his arm. "Turns out it's harder than it sounds."

Below them, far down in the dark, stone ground against stone as the mountain sealed itself, door after door after door, until the Unseelie court was only a story told in valleys where magic couldn't follow.

Beside them, Emberlast lay still. Her scales had gone grey as ash, all her fire spent — but as the freed souls streamed upward past her, one small golden mote broke from the stream and settled on her snout, and the old dragon smiled, and closed her eyes, and was smoke on the wind.

Marguerite squeezed Rafael's arm. "You want to tell me why I can't remember the last three days, and why I'm crying, and why I'd do all of it again?"

He looked at her — ordinary, exhausted, alive — and something in his face broke open.

"Ask me again tomorrow," he said. "We're going home. And Marguerite — I'm going to teach you to garden."

Far overhead, soft as snowfall, the god spoke for the first time in ten years: *"Thank you."*

Then it rested.
