## 10. Seeds in Ordinary Soil

The memories came back to Marguerite the way rain comes back to a valley: not all at once, but in a long, soft insistence that would not be refused.

She was standing at the edge of the market in Harrow's Bend, holding an apple she had no memory of buying, when the first of them returned. It arrived as a smell — tallow and woodsmoke, the particular stink of the Drowned Lantern tavern — and with it a face she had spent some unknowable stretch of time protecting without knowing why. Rafael Vasquez, younger by years than the stranger she'd been trailing through her own blurred days, glaring at her over a hand of cards he was losing on purpose because he liked watching her gloat.

Then more. The map pressed into her hands, inked in a dead man's light. The argument in her rooms, his voice low and stubborn: *It has my name on it, Marguerite.* The valley's edge like a curtain of cold water. The dragon on the mountainside, vast and dying, her scales gone the grey of old coals. The tunnels lit by bones that remembered being candles. The queen's smile, sweet as rot beneath honey.

And last — last and worst and best — the broker's parlor, where she had laid her memories out on a velvet tray like a woman selling jewelry, and watched them lifted away one by one while she recited, again and again, the only instruction she'd permitted herself to keep:

*Protect him. You will not know why. Protect him anyway.*

Marguerite sat down hard on a barrel beside the fruit-seller's cart. The apple rolled from her fingers. She caught it without looking, an old mercenary reflex, and laughed — a cracked, astonished sound that made the fruit-seller edge away from her.

She remembered everything.

She remembered trading away the ten best years of her life for a corridor of stone and a locked door, and she remembered — this was the part that made her laugh again, helplessly, into the crook of her elbow — that she would do it exactly the same way twice.

"You absolute idiot," she said aloud, to no one, to herself, to the girl who had walked into that parlor with her chin up and her heart already grieving what it was about to lose. "You beautiful, calculating idiot."

The freed souls were still passing overhead. They had been doing it for three days now, the whole valley watching: small bright shapes rising off the mountain like sparks off a struck flint, drifting east and west and south, going home to whatever waited past the world's edges. Some of them, folk said, paused on their way. One had hovered for a full hour over the cradle-house in Lowmarsh, and the child born there that night did not cry once. Another had settled briefly on the shoulder of old Tam Wicker, who had wept without knowing why and then baked forty loaves of bread and given every one away.

They were not ghosts. Marguerite understood that now, standing in the market with ten lost years pouring back into her like water into a dry field. They were *paid debts*. Every soul the court had ever bottled, loosed at last, and the whole sky over the valley glittering with the settling of accounts older than anyone living.

She bought two more apples, because the fruit-seller looked like he needed the transaction to mean something ordinary, and started walking toward the mountain.

Toward Rafael.

* * *

The mountain was different. That was the first thing. Mount Cael had always had a look of leaning — a suggestion, felt more than seen, that its whole mass was braced against something, straining, a door held shut from inside by someone whose arms were shaking. Ten years of that strain had written itself into the landscape below it: crops that came up thin, birds that flew around the high valleys instead of over them, a silence where a god should have been singing.

Now the mountain simply *sat*. Immense, indifferent, finished. Whatever door it had been holding shut was held shut forever now, sealed from both sides, and the stone had relaxed into itself the way a man relaxes after setting down a weight he has carried since boyhood.

Marguerite climbed the pilgrim path — new-worn, already, though the sealing had been three days ago; people could not seem to stay away — and found him halfway up the southern slope, on his knees in the dirt, with his hands full of soil.

He did not hear her coming. That alone told her how much he had changed. The Rafael she remembered heard everything; a decade of contracts in a godless valley had tuned him like a wire, and even asleep he'd slept like a man expecting the bill to come due. This one — this man kneeling in a patch of turned earth with seed packets lined up beside him in careful rows — was humming. Badly. Some tavern song about a miller's daughter.

"Rafael."

He startled so badly he dropped the handful of soil down his own shirt. Then he turned, and his face did something complicated — relief and guilt and grief and joy all arriving at once, none of them willing to wait their turn — and he stood up too fast and knocked over the water bucket.

"Marguerite." He said her name the way a man says the name of something he'd been afraid he'd imagined. "You're— your memory. It came back?"

"All of it. Three days ago. In the middle of the market." She held up an apple. "I apparently bought fruit I couldn't afford to eat, protected a stranger I couldn't stand to lose, and forgot the entire best friendship of my life. So." She tossed him the apple. He caught it, barely. "You're welcome."

"Marguerite—" His voice caught. He looked down at his hands, filthy to the wrist, and when he looked up again his eyes were wet and furious about being wet. "I didn't know. About what you traded. Emberlast told me after — after, and I've been waiting up here like a coward because I couldn't think of anything to say to you that wouldn't be—"

"Groveling?"

"Groveling," he agreed.

"Good instinct. Keep it." She crossed the turned earth between them — noting, with a professional eye, that the soil had been worked properly, double-dug, ash folded deep into it, black and rich as nothing in this valley had been in ten years — and punched him in the arm. Not hard. Hard enough.

"That's for forgetting me," she said. "This is for coming back." And she hugged him, brief and fierce, the way mercenaries hug: fast, because the world is short, and hard, because the world is cruel, and then done, because sentiment unwatered goes to rot.

When she stepped back he was smiling, that rare unguarded thing, and she gestured at the slope around them. Rows of turned earth marched across the scarred ground where the mountain's throat had been torn open and then closed forever. Small stones marked the rows. A rough fence of branches kept out the deer. And scattered everywhere, mixed into the soil until it was almost invisible, a fine grey powder that she recognized with a catch in her chest.

Ashes.

"She asked me for it," Rafael said quietly, following her gaze. "Before the end. She said dragons don't get graves — they get *uses*." He knelt and picked up a seed packet. Turnips. He said the word with tremendous dignity, as if announcing the coronation of a king. "There are four hundred souls' worth of grief buried under this hill, Marguerite, and the court sealed behind a mile of stone, and a god sleeping under all of it like a banked fire. And I'm planting turnips on top."

"What else would you plant?"

He laughed — really laughed, from somewhere loose and new in him — and shook his head. "That's the thing. Everyone keeps asking. The magistrate sent a clerk up here yesterday to offer me *land*. Official land, with a deed. The pilgrims want me to bless things. There's a woman in Harrow's Bend who swears the rain came back because I willed it, and she's telling everyone, and I cannot make her stop." He turned the seed packet over in his hands. "A month ago I would have taken the land. Would have wanted the blessing. Would have let her believe it, because being believed is—" He stopped. Started again, slower. "Do you know what the queen offered me? Power, permanence, a throne, a court that sang my name. And do you know what I wanted, standing there with all of it held out to me like a plate of fruit?"

"What?"

"This." He spread his dirty hands. "Dirt under my nails. A bad harvest I can complain about honestly. Somebody who remembers me even when I'm not useful." He glanced up at her sideways. "Turnips, Marguerite. I fought a queen and shattered my own soul and I want *turnips*."

"It's a good crop," she said gravely. "Underrated. Very honest vegetable. No ambitions whatsoever."

"That's why I respect it."

She sat down on the fence rail while he went back to work, and watched him plant, and let the strangeness of peace settle over her like a coat she'd forgotten she owned. Below them the valley lay green and washed, the rain having come at last — real rain, patient rain, the kind that soaked in instead of running off. Somewhere downslope a bell was ringing vespers, badly, the ringer out of practice. Smoke rose from chimneys in straight, unhurried lines.

Her memories were still settling, silt finding its levels. She kept catching fragments at the corners of the day: the broker's voice saying *you will not know why*, and her own answer, steady as bedrock: *I don't need to know why. I know who.* She had walked into that parlor knowing she would forget him, and she had gone anyway, and the version of her that had lived those blank months — loyal to a stranger, fierce on behalf of a name she couldn't place — had been no less herself for the missing years. If anything—

"If anything," she said aloud, surprising herself, "the woman who guarded you without remembering why might have been the better half of me. She didn't have ten years of grievances to weigh it against. She just chose."

Rafael looked up, soil on his cheek. "She was entirely you," he said. "That's the thing about you, Marguerite. You could empty you out to the walls and you'd still be you. I think it infuriates you."

It did. Infuriatingly.

* * *

The god spoke at dusk.

Not to the valley — the valley got nothing but wind and the smell of rain coming. Just to the two of them, sitting by the small fire Rafael had built at the garden's edge, sharing a supper of hard cheese and the last of the road-bread. The voice arrived the way warmth arrives through a wall on a sunny afternoon: not heard, exactly, but *arrived-with*, present in the fire and the soil and the space between one breath and the next.

**THANK YOU FOR THE GARDEN,** it said. **I HAVE WATCHED TEN THOUSAND YEARS OF KINGS AND QUEENS AND NEVER ONCE SEEN ANYONE PLANT TURNIPS OVER MY GRAVE. IT IS THE BEST THING THAT HAS EVER HAPPENED TO ME.**

Marguerite choked on her cheese. Rafael didn't startle this time. He just smiled at the fire, tired and fond, the way you smile at an old friend who has finally, finally come home from a long war.

"We're glad you're resting," he said.

**RESTING,** the god agreed, and the word carried a depth of relief that made Marguerite's eyes sting. **THE COURT IS SEALED. THE DEBTS ARE PAID. THE RAIN WILL COME WHEN IT COMES, WHICH IS ALL ANY VALLEY HAS EVER BEEN PROMISED. YOU DID NOT SAVE ME, RAFAEL VASQUEZ. YOU FREED ME. THEY ARE NOT THE SAME THING, AND NO ONE THANKS PEOPLE FOR THE SECOND ONE OFTEN ENOUGH.**

"And the souls?" Marguerite asked. "The ones still drifting?"

**GOING HOME. SOME OF THEM ARE GOING TO BE BORN AGAIN. ONE OF THEM ASKED ME TO TELL YOU BOTH —** the warmth in the air shifted, became almost sheepish — **THAT THE LULLABY WAS NOT HIS MOTHER'S. SHE LEARNED IT FROM HER MOTHER, WHO LEARNED IT FROM HERS, BACK AND BACK UNTIL THE FIRST WOMAN WHO EVER SANG IT WAS SINGING IT TO A CHILD IN A VALLEY VERY MUCH LIKE THIS ONE, DURING A DROUGHT VERY MUCH LIKE YOURS, WONDERING IF THE RAIN WOULD EVER COME. IT ALWAYS CAME. IT JUST NEVER CAME WHEN ANYONE WANTED.**

Silence. Fire crackle. Somewhere down the slope, a nightbird trying out three notes of something.

**I AM GOING TO SLEEP NOW,** the god said. **FOR A LONG TIME. PERHAPS FOREVER. IF YOU NEED NOTHING, PLANT SOMETHING. IF YOU NEED SOMETHING, PLANT MORE. THAT IS THE ENTIRETY OF MY THEOLOGY AND YOU MAY TELL ANYONE WHO ASKS.**

The warmth withdrew gently, like a hand drawn back from a shoulder, and the fire burned on, ordinary fire, and the stars came out over the mountain in their ancient, unhurried way.

* * *

Later — much later, when the fire had gone to embers and Marguerite had claimed the flatter side of the fence rail for sleeping — she woke once in the dark and saw him still awake, sitting cross-legged in the ash-rich dirt, turning a single seed over and over between his fingers.

"You could have kept it," she said quietly, into the dark. "Your soul. Whole. Yours. You could have been—" She gestured vaguely at the sky, the mountain, everything.

"I know." He set the seed into the earth and covered it with both hands, pressing it down with a care most men reserve for wounds or infants. "That's how I knew not to. Anything that tempting, that easy to hold onto — it wasn't mine. It was just the shape of what other people wanted me to be." He wiped his hands on his knees and lay back, looking up at the wheeling stars. "Turnips tomorrow. Onions after. Maybe, if the rain holds, an apple tree in the corner there. Takes seven years to bear."

"Seven years," Marguerite said. "You're planning to stay."

"I'm planning to stay."

She settled back against the rail, ten years of memory warm behind her ribs and the rest of her life, unremarkable and unspectacular and utterly hers, opening out ahead like fresh-turned ground.

"Well," she said. "Someone has to guard the turnips."

Above them, faint as a heartbeat, the mountain breathed — once, slow and easy, like something sleeping soundly at last — and did not stir again.
