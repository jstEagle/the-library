## 1. The God Who Stopped Answering

The stag had been dead for two days when Hazel finally found it, and by then the wolves had taken most of what the contract was worth.

She stood at the edge of the ravine in the grey hour before dawn, breath fogging, and looked down at what remained: a red smear across the frost-bitten ferns, a rack of antlers tangled in bramble like a crown someone had thrown away. The pelt was ruined. The meat was gone. Somewhere below, still gorged and careless, the wolf pack was sleeping off their theft.

*Two hundred yards east,* she'd prayed, three nights ago, kneeling in the dark with her palms pressed flat against cold earth. *Show me where it beds down. Just once more. Like you used to.*

Silence. The same silence that had answered her for four years now, patient and absolute as snowfall.

Hazel Underhill had been bound to her god since she was nine years old, the way every child born inside the Hollow was bound—oath spoken over hearth-smoke, a name whispered into the valley's ear, and something vast and wordless settling into the chest like a coal that never cooled. Her mother had wept at the ceremony. Everyone's mother wept; it was supposed to be beautiful. *The Hollow keeps us,* they said. *The god keeps the Hollow.* Magic could not cross the valley's rim, could not take root in its soil, could not touch its children—and in exchange, the god asked only faith, and in exchange for faith, the god gave back guidance. A warmth behind the sternum when a path was true. A cold prickle at the neck when a road led wrong. Every mercenary in the hall swore by it. Every hunt, every contract, every gamble of life and limb, hedged with that small divine voice.

It had stopped answering the winter Hazel turned nineteen.

Not gradually. Not with warnings or fading, the way a fire dies. It had simply gone quiet between one prayer and the next, like a door closing in an empty house, and no one else in the valley seemed to have noticed. When she'd mentioned it—carefully, once, to the old priest at the shrine—he'd smiled with wet eyes and said the god tested those he loved most. When she'd mentioned it to the other mercenaries, they'd laughed and told her she was praying wrong, or too much, or not enough, or about the wrong things.

Four years of praying wrong. Four years of testing. Hazel crouched now at the ravine's edge, pulled her knife, and began the long, unglamorous work of salvaging what she could from the carcass, because gods did not fill contracts and rent did not pay itself.

The wolves came at mid-morning.

There were five of them, gaunt with winter, flowing down the ravine wall with the particular boldness of animals who had already eaten this kill and considered it theirs. In the old days—in the days when her prayers were answered—the god would have warned her. A prickle at the neck, a cold breath on the spine: *behind you, girl, move.* She would have turned with her spear already leveled and the fight half-won before it started.

Instead she heard them the ordinary way, with ordinary ears, late, and had to fight the whole ugly business on even terms.

It lasted perhaps ninety seconds and cost her a gash along the forearm that would need stitching and a spear shaft cracked clean through. She killed two—the pack broke when the second one dropped—and drove the rest off with blood on both sides, hers and theirs, steaming in the frost. Then she sat down hard on a stone, laid the broken spear across her knees, and did the thing she had sworn she wouldn't do again until at least spring.

She prayed.

*I know you're there,* she thought, staring up through bare branches at a sky the color of dirty wool. *Or I don't. That's the point, isn't it? I don't know anything anymore. I've kept your oath. I haven't left the valley. I haven't spoken your name outside these borders. I've done everything—*

Her throat closed. She made herself finish.

*Everything I promised. And you can't spare me one word. Not one. Not even a lie.*

Nothing. No warmth, no cold, no coal in the chest, nothing but her own heartbeat and the wind moving through the trees like a long exhalation from a mouth that would never speak again.

And here was the thought she had been circling for four years the way a tongue circles a broken tooth, and which finally, in the bloody quiet of the ravine, stepped forward and showed its face:

*Maybe there was never anyone there at all.*

Maybe the warmth had always just been courage, and the cold just fear, and the whole architecture of her inner life—a god's voice guiding her hand since childhood—had been nothing but herself, talking to herself, in the dark. Maybe the entire valley knelt before a silence and called it love. The thought should have shattered her. Instead, strangely, it settled over her like the frost itself: cold, totalizing, and oddly clarifying. If there was no god, then no one had abandoned her. If there was no god, then the silence wasn't punishment.

It was just the world, being the world.

She laughed—one short, ugly bark of sound that startled a crow out of the brush—and got up, and shouldered what remained of the stag's rack and hide, and walked home through a valley that suddenly felt very large and very empty, like a house after a funeral.

* * *

The mercenary hall at Duncarrow smelled of woodsmoke, tallow, and thirty years of spilled ale soaked so deep into the floorboards that no amount of scrubbing would ever get it out. Hazel had always found the smell comforting. Today it just smelled old.

The contract clerk took one look at the ruined hide and the broken spear and winced on her behalf. "Wolves?"

"Wolves."

"Full rate's out. You'll get salvage."

"I know how contracts work, Tam."

He pushed the coins across the counter without meeting her eyes, and Hazel understood, distantly, that she had become one of those people people didn't like to look at—not because of any scandal, but because everyone in the valley somehow knew about her without knowing how they knew. *The Underhill girl who lost her god.* Faith was the water everyone swam in here; a swimmer who claimed the water had vanished made everyone else's lungs hurt to think about.

She was counting coins she didn't need to count when Brannoc lowered himself onto the bench beside her with the groan of a man whose knees had opinions.

Old sergeant Brannoc had trained half the hall's current roster, including her, back when his shoulders were wider than the doorframes and his voice could stop a bar fight at forty paces. Age had pared him down to leather and gristle, but his eyes were still quick, and they went straight to the bandage on her arm, then to her face, and stayed there.

"Rough one," he said. Not a question.

"Five wolves on a two-day-old kill. Shouldn't have happened." She tied off the coin pouch. "I should have known they'd doubled back. Any decent tracker—"

"Any decent tracker would've moved on the first night instead of sitting on her hands waiting for permission." He said it mildly, but it landed anyway, because it was true. Three nights she'd spent camped at the treeline, praying for confirmation, praying for the warm yes in her chest, and each morning waking to silence and telling herself *today*, today the voice would come back, today she'd be sure enough to move.

"You don't understand," she started.

"I understand fine." Brannoc leaned in, and his voice dropped to the register he'd used for twenty years to keep green recruits alive. "Listen to me, girl. You've got a look about you lately. The look you get right before you say something out loud that ought to stay in your head."

"I'm not going to say anything."

"You're going to say everything, eventually. People like you always do. The ones who actually feel the absence." He tapped the scarred wood of the table with one knuckle, twice, like punctuation. "You know how many mercenaries I've served with in forty years? More than I can count. And I'll tell you what they all learned, some faster than others: the ones who ask questions disappear."

"That's superstition."

"That's arithmetic." His eyes flicked, briefly, toward the hall's front windows, toward the street beyond, and came back. "You think nobody notices who stops attending shrine? Who stops saying grace before a job? You think the wardens don't keep lists?" He shook his head slowly. "I'm not telling you the god's listening or isn't. I'm telling you that in this valley, the question is more dangerous than any answer. Keep your head down. Do your contracts. Pray if it comforts you, and if it doesn't—" he shrugged, enormous and weary— "pray anyway, and keep your mouth shut about it."

Hazel looked down at her hands. The knuckles were split from the fight. There was wolf blood dried black under the nails.

"What if there's nothing there, Brannoc?" The words came out smaller than she intended. "What if I've been talking to myself my whole life?"

For a moment the old sergeant didn't answer, and in that moment something moved across his face—something quick and complicated and gone, like a fish under ice. Later, she would turn that moment over and over in her memory and wonder what it meant. Now, she was too tired to see it at all.

"Then you've been keeping yourself alive this whole time," he said finally, "and that's more than most men can claim." He rose, joints crackling, and clapped her shoulder with a hand like a weathered plank. "Go get that arm stitched. And Hazel—" he paused at the edge of the lamplight, not turning around— "whatever's eating at you, bury it deep. Some holes in this valley are better left unopened."

He walked away into the smoke and noise of the hall, and Hazel sat alone with her coins and her bandaged arm and the enormous, ringing silence where a god used to live.

Outside, the light was already failing. Winter was coming down off the mountains, and somewhere far away—at the valley's rim, though no one in Duncarrow knew it yet—a stranger was lacing up his boots for the last long walk of his life.
