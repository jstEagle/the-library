## 2. A Stranger at the Treeline

The Hollow's edge was a place Hazel Underhill knew the way other people knew their own hands. She had walked the treeline since she was nine years old, first trailing her mother's heels, then alone, then with a contract blade at her hip and a god in her ear that had stopped speaking years ago. The boundary itself was invisible—no wall, no fence, no carved stone marking where the valley ended and the wild country began. You felt it instead. A pressure behind the eyes. A dullness in the air, like a room long shut. The old folk called it the Hush, and they spoke of it the way farmers speak of weather: with respect, without question, and never, ever with curiosity.

Hazel had questions. That was the problem with her. Brannoc said so often enough.

She was walking the eastern stretch that morning because a wool merchant from Lowmark had paid the hall twelve silver to learn why his drovers refused to camp near the pines there—said they heard singing, said their mules went mad-eyed and wouldn't drink. Hazel didn't believe in singing trees. But twelve silver was twelve silver, and a contract was a contract, and besides, she had wanted the walk. Wanted the cold clean air after a week of the hall's smoke and Brannoc's snoring and the particular silence of a god who would not answer.

She had prayed at dawn. Old habit, older than sense. Kneeling on the frozen mud behind the hall, palms open, whispering the words her mother had taught her before her mother stopped teaching anything at all: *Vael, keeper of the quiet, walk with me.* A hundred mornings she had done it since the voice went out. A hundred mornings of nothing.

This morning, for the first time, she hadn't felt nothing. She had felt *absence*—a shape where something should have been, the way you feel a missing tooth with your tongue. It had unsettled her enough that she'd cut the prayer short, which was its own small blasphemy, and taken the eastern road early.

So perhaps it was fitting—or cruel, or both—that it was on this morning, of all mornings, that the sky above the treeline tore open.

There was no better word for it. One moment the air over the pines was grey and ordinary; the next it split along a seam no eye should have been able to see, and light came through—not sunlight, not lightning, but a white so total it made the snow on the branches look dirty. The sound arrived a heartbeat later: not thunder but a *peel*, high and wrong, like a bell struck inside her skull. Every bird in the valley went up at once, a black ragged sheet of them, and the horses in Lowmark's distant paddock began to scream.

Hazel dropped into a crouch behind a granite outcrop, hand on her hilt, heart hammering against her ribs like something trying to get out. Because she knew what she was seeing, even if every lesson of her life insisted she couldn't be seeing it.

Magic did not work in the Hollow. That was the whole point of the place. Wards failed at the treeline. Charms curdled. Sorcerers who crossed the boundary found their power gone as suddenly as a snuffed candle—and most of them turned around and left, muttering about cursed ground, and the valley let them go and counted itself safe. It was the one true thing everyone agreed on, priest and mercenary and farmer alike. The curse protected them. Whatever else the Hollow took from them—the voices of gods, the wonders travelers described—it kept out the fire too.

And yet the seam in the sky was burning, and something was coming through it.

It came badly. That was the thing she would remember afterward, when she tried to make the memory hold still: how *badly* it came. Not striding through in triumph but dragged, or falling, or both—a dark shape tumbling down through the light like a man thrown down stairs by an unseen hand. The seam snapped shut behind him with a crack that shook frost from the pines, and the white light died all at once, and the man hit the snow at the edge of the treeline and did not get up.

For a long moment Hazel stayed where she was, crouched behind the rock, breathing fog, doing the arithmetic mercenaries do. Distance: two hundred yards. Cover: poor. Threat: unknown. Retreat: easy, and sensible, and what any professional worth her salt would do—ride back to the hall, report a strange light, let the elders argue about it.

But the man was moving now. Crawling. Dragging himself through the snow toward the valley's interior with the blind persistence of a dying animal, leaving a furrow behind him, and—she could see it even at this distance, even through the shimmer of cold air—smoke rising off his back.

Smoke. Off a living man. In the Hollow, where magic was supposed to be impossible.

"Damn it," Hazel said quietly, to herself, to the silent god, to whoever was listening, which was no one. Then she stood up and went to him.

* * *

Up close he was worse. He was maybe fifty, grey-bearded, dressed in travel-stained clothes of a cut she didn't recognize—foreign, certainly, and expensive once, though they were ruined now. Ruined was the word. The back of his coat was burned away entirely, and beneath it his skin was scorched in long wealing stripes, as if he'd been flayed by lines of fire. But the burns were wrong. Hazel had seen burn wounds—kitchen accidents, a hay fire in Duncarrow, the aftermath of a siege once, years ago. These weren't like them. They glowed faintly, the way coals glow under ash, and they moved. Slowly. Creeping along his arms like ivy growing in fast time, reaching for his throat.

He had magic in him. Magic that shouldn't exist here, still eating at him, and the Hollow wasn't killing it—the Hollow was killing *him*, slowly, while the magic rode him like a rider.

His eyes found her when her boots crunched the snow three feet away. They were pale, nearly colorless, and terribly clear. Clearer than a dying man's eyes had any right to be.

"You're hers," he rasped. His accent was thick, unplaceable, vowels rolled soft. "Marked. I can see the mark."

Hazel's hand went, without her deciding it, to the small scar on her inner wrist—the oath-scar, cut there when she was thirteen, the sign of Vael's service. Nobody saw that scar. Nobody *recognized* it; outside the valley, Vael was a name nobody so much as whispered anymore.

"Who are you?" she said. "What was that light? How did you cross the Hush?"

He laughed, and the laugh became coughing, and the coughing brought blood to his lips, bright and shocking against the snow. "Questions," he managed. "Good. She said you'd be the one who asks." He fumbled at his chest, fingers clumsy, and Hazel knelt—knelt without meaning to, snow soaking through her knee—and helped him, because whatever else he was, he was dying, and the creeping fire had reached his collarbone and she could smell it now, sweet and foul, like meat left out.

Beneath his coat, strapped flat against his ribs, was a leather case. Long, narrow, sealed with wax stamped by a sigil she didn't know: a circle broken by a vertical line.

"Take it," he said.

"What is it?"

"The only map," he breathed, "that tells the truth." His hand closed around her wrist, over the oath-scar, and his grip was shockingly strong for a man half-consumed. His colorless eyes bored into hers. "Listen to me. They will come for it. Robes. They wear robes and they smile and they will tell you they serve your god. Do not give it to them. Do not let them—" A spasm took him. The glowing stripes flared white-hot along his neck. He screamed through clenched teeth, and then, horribly, he smiled.

"Beneath," he said.

Just the one word. Just that. And then the strength went out of his grip all at once, like a rope cut, and Hazel was kneeling in the snow holding the wrist of a dead stranger while the last of the strange fire guttered out beneath his skin, leaving the burns black and still and ordinary, and ordinary was somehow worse.

* * *

She sat with him for a while. She wasn't sure how long. The valley was very quiet around her—the birds still gone, the horses far off and silent now, the pines holding their snow and their secrets. Somewhere behind her, Lowmark's bell began to ring the noon hour, and the sound seemed to come from another world.

Professionalism said: search the body, take the case, report to the hall, send word to the elders. Professionalism said: this is a contract now, the strangest of your life, and contracts are handled by procedure.

Her hands shook as she unbuckled the case anyway.

Inside, wrapped in oilcloth, was a map. Not drawn on paper but on something thinner and tougher, skin or membrane, and not drawn at all in the way maps are drawn. Every map Hazel had ever seen showed the valley from above—the way a bird sees it, the river a silver thread, the town a scatter of little houses. This showed the valley from *below*. From underneath. As if the artist had lain beneath the earth itself and looked up through it, rendering the ground as a translucent ceiling: the chapel at Duncarrow hanging down like a root, the river a vein of light, and threaded through the dark of the underworld, tunnels. Dozens of them. Hundreds. A warren beneath the whole valley, converging—every passage, every shaft, every crawl narrowing toward a single point beneath the ruined chapel, where the cartographer had drawn a door.

A door, standing alone in the dark, rendered in careful strokes. And around it, band after band after band, markings in no language Hazel knew—characters like hooked nails, like birds' feet, like things written by a hand that was not entirely a human hand. They circled the door the way a knot circles a rope. The way a cage circles a prisoner.

At the bottom corner, in cramped common script, someone had written a single line in a language she could read:

*If the seal breaks, remember: it was mercy, once.*

Hazel sat in the snow with a dead man's blood drying on her sleeve and read the line until she had memorized it, and then read it again, because it changed nothing and explained less. Beneath the valley. Tunnels. A door. A seal. Mercy, once.

The curse that kept magic out. The Hush she'd lived inside her whole life, the silence her god had vanished into. What if it wasn't a wall at all? What if it was a lid?

"No," she said aloud, to the empty morning. It was reflex, nothing more—the word you say when the ground moves under you. The valley was safe. Everyone knew the valley was safe. That was the one true thing.

She looked down at the dead man, and his clear pale eyes seemed to look back at her even closed, and she thought: *She said you'd be the one who asks.* Someone had sent him. Someone had known about her—about the mark, the oath, the questions. Which meant someone, somewhere beyond the treeline, knew things about the Hollow that no one in the Hollow knew. And those someones wore robes, and they were coming.

Hazel wrapped the map carefully, buckled the case, and slung it across her back where a sword usually hung. Then she stood, brushed the snow from her knees, and looked east along the treeline, where the morning lay pale and innocent and full of strangers yet to arrive.

Twelve silver for singing trees, she thought. Brannoc was going to say this was what happened when you asked questions.

She turned toward the road, and that was when she heard it—footsteps in the snow behind her, quick and stumbling and utterly wrong, coming from the direction of the scorched seam in the air, where no one else should possibly be.
