## 3. The Man Who Fell Out of Nowhere

The ink was still wet on the coastline when the world ended.

Rafael didn't know it had ended, of course. He knew only that his hand cramped, that Master Ivo's shop smelled of tallow and salt and the particular sourness of old vellum, and that the third copy of the Meridian Charts was due to a merchant in Tessel by week's end. He sat at the long table beneath the window where the afternoon light came slantwise through panes warped by age, and he drew the same jagged shore for the fourth time, because Master Ivo said Rafael's coastlines were too pretty—too *invented*—and that a map was a promise, not a poem.

*A map is a promise,* Rafael thought, dipping his pen. *And no one has ever promised me anything.*

The thought arrived with its usual quiet bitterness, the kind he'd learned to fold small and keep behind his ribs where it couldn't be seen. Six years in Ivo's shop. Six years of sweeping floors and grinding pigments and being told his work was competent, which was the cruelest word in any language. He was nineteen and he had never left Corvassa, this city of bridges he could draw from memory but had never been allowed to see past the harbor walls.

He pressed the nib to the vellum.

And the ink ran *upward*.

That was the first wrong thing. The black line lifted off the page like smoke reversing, threading back into his pen, into his fingers, and Rafael had time to feel not fear but a strange professional offense—*that is my work, that took hours*—before the second wrong thing happened, which was that the window went white.

Not bright. White. As if someone had taken the world and blotted it, the way you blot a mistake, and what remained was paper-blank and humming. The hum wasn't sound exactly. It lived in his teeth, in the marrow of him, and it spoke without words in a voice that felt like the moment before a sneeze, the moment before a fall—

*carry*

—he would swear later, all his life, that he heard one syllable. Not a command. A request. Almost polite.

Then everything that was Rafael Vess was pulled out of himself like thread off a spool, and the shop, the charts, the city, the whole known curve of the world, unraveled behind him.

* * *

He woke face-down in grass.

This was wrong for several reasons, the first being that he had fallen asleep at his desk if he had fallen asleep at all, and Master Ivo's floorboards did not grow grass. The second reason was the smell—wet earth and pine resin and something green and living and *enormous*, the smell of a forest that had never been cut. The third reason was the cold, a damp cold that got under his coat and settled against his skin like a creditor.

Rafael rolled onto his back and looked up at trees so tall their crowns dissolved into mist. Not Corvassan oaks. Not anything from any chart he had ever copied. His breath came out ragged and white.

"Right," he said aloud, because saying things aloud made them real, and this needed very badly to be real or not real, either would do. "Right."

He sat up. His satchel was beside him—his actual satchel, with its waxed canvas and its brass buckles—and inside were his pens, his inkhorn, his folding rule, and, absurdly, half-finished, the sheet of the Meridian coastline he'd been copying when the world blotted itself. He clutched it like scripture. It was proof. Whatever had happened, he hadn't dreamed the shop; the shop existed; he existed; there was a before.

There was an after, too. He just didn't know where it was.

He walked. There was nothing else to do. He followed the slope downhill because water runs downhill and towns sit near water—that was lesson one of cartography, lesson one of survival, the first thing Master Ivo ever taught him: *follow the water, boy, and the water will take you to people, and people are where the mistakes get corrected.* He walked through ferns taller than his waist and past trees furred with moss, and the light thickened toward evening without him once seeing the sun, and the hum from before—the hum in his teeth—faded slowly, reluctantly, like a note held past its measure.

It was full dark when he saw the lights. A scatter of them below, warm and ordinary, lamplight in windows, woodsmoke rising straight up in the windless air. A town. Rafael nearly wept with relief, which embarrassed him even though no one watched.

He was twenty paces from the first fence post when the arrow hit the gate beside his head.

* * *

The thunk of it was very loud in the quiet. Rafael stood perfectly still, hands rising on their own, and watched a figure detach itself from the shadows of the fence line—a woman, broad-shouldered, moving with the unhurried economy of someone who had done this many times and expected to do it many more.

"Don't." Her voice was low and flat. She had a bow drawn, the arrowhead steady on his chest, and her eyes in the lamplight were the color of wet slate. "Hands where they are. Turn around. Slowly."

"I'm not—I don't have any weapons—" Which was true and useless. He turned anyway, palms out, feeling the night air on his neck and the specific animal misery of being aimed at.

"The case," she said. "On your shoulder. Open it. Tip it out."

"It's just drawing tools. Pens. Ink—" He tipped it anyway, and his belongings scattered in the dirt: the inkhorn, the rule, the pens, and the half-finished chart, which unrolled in the mud with the coastline facing up.

The silence that followed had a texture to it. Rafael risked turning his head and saw the woman crouch, pick up the vellum, and hold it to the light. He saw her go very still in a way that had nothing to do with assessing a weapon.

"Where did you get this?"

"I drew it. This afternoon. Or—" His voice cracked and he hated it. "I don't know how long ago. I don't know where I am. Please. I was in a shop in Corvassa, in the Cartographers' Quarter, and then there was a light, and a sound, and then I was here. That's everything I know. I swear it on—" He almost said *on my master's name*, and realized he didn't want to spend that coin. "I swear it on whatever you hold sacred."

She stood. Up close she was younger than her bearing suggested, mid-twenties maybe, with a sword at her hip worn the way other people wore old boots, and a small wooden pendant at her throat she touched once, absently, like a man touching a scar. She studied the chart again, turning it over, and something in her face was doing arithmetic that kept refusing to sum.

"This is the valley," she said finally. "Drawn from above. Accurately." Her eyes came up, and they were not friendly eyes. They were frightened ones wearing armor. "Nobody draws this valley. Nobody maps it. No one comes in, no one goes out, and every soul born here knows better than to put it on paper."

"Well, someone taught it to me by accident," Rafael said, "because I've never seen it before tonight."

"That's not possible."

"I'm aware." A laugh climbed out of him, thin and slightly unhinged. "Do you think I *chose* this? Look at me. I'm covered in mud. My best pen is broken. Three hours ago I was worried about whether a merchant in Tessel would accept a delivery late, and now I'm standing in—" he gestured helplessly at the enormous dark trees, the blank starless sky above the mist— "wherever this is, being shot at by someone who thinks geography is a crime."

"You fell out of nowhere," she said flatly.

"That is genuinely the most accurate description anyone has offered me all day."

Something moved behind her eyes—not softening, exactly. Recalculation. She lowered the bow but didn't sling it. "Hazel Underhill," she said, as though the name cost her something.

"Rafael. Rafael Vess. Apprentice cartographer, Guild of Corvassa, third year of—" He stopped. "Formerly, I suppose."

"Pick up your things." She stepped back, still watching him the way one watches a lit fuse. "You're coming with me. And Rafael Vess of Corvassa—if you're lying to me, if you're one of *them*, whoever sent you—"

"Sent me?" He laughed again, and it came out worse than the first one. "No one sent me. That's the whole problem. For the first time in my life, nobody wants anything from me, and I'd give anything to be wanted somewhere, just not—" He stopped, hearing himself, hearing too much of himself in it, and shut his mouth.

Hazel Underhill looked at him for a long moment. The lamp in the nearest window flickered. Somewhere in the town behind her, a dog began to bark, then stopped as if scolded.

"Beneath," she said quietly.

"What?"

"Nothing. A dead man's last word. You wouldn't know anything about that."

But she was already turning, already walking, and Rafael scrambled to gather his scattered tools—the inkhorn, the rule, the ruined chart—and hurried after her through the gate of a town whose name no one, she said, had ever thought to put on a map.

Behind them, at the treeline where he had fallen out of the world, the mist lay over the grass in a perfect circle, and inside that circle, faint as breath on glass, the ground was scorched black.
