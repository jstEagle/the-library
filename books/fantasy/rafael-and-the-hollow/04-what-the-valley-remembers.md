## 4. What the Valley Remembers

Hazel had knelt in the mud of a hundred battlefields and never once felt the ground move beneath her. Now, standing over a scrap of vellum by candlelight, she could not shake the sensation that the whole valley was tilting.

The map case lay open on Brannoc's old table in the back room of the mercenary hall, though Brannoc himself was three tables away in the common room, drinking with men who still believed the world was simple. Hazel had waited until the hall emptied of anyone who might ask questions. She was good at waiting. She was less good at what came after.

The stranger's map showed the Hollow from beneath. That was the only way to describe it. Not a chart of the valley as a bird would see it—roads, fields, the river threading down from the high passes—but as something *under* it. Tunnels branched like roots through the page, converging on a single point near the valley's heart. There, drawn with a care that bordered on reverence, was a door. A rectangle of hatched lines, and around it a ring of symbols in no alphabet she knew—not Low Trade, not High Vessic, not the knot-script the mountain clans scratched into their lintels. The symbols were older than that. They looked less like writing than like scars.

And they circled not just the door but the entire valley. When she held the page to the candle and let the light pass through, fainter lines emerged: a second map laid over the first, showing the Hollow itself, its ridgelines traced in that same alien script, each peak and river named with characters that hurt to look at too long, the way a bright forge hurts.

A ward. Her mind kept circling back to it, refusing the word and returning to it. The curse on the Hollow—the one every child learned before they learned their letters—was supposed to keep magic *out*. That was the story. That was why her god's voice did not reach here, why no hedge-witch's charm worked past the treeline, why the valley had survived every war that had burned the world beyond its walls. Protection. She had built her whole life inside that word.

But wards did not have doors. Cages did.

"You're going to burn a hole in that, staring so hard."

Rafael stood in the doorway, arms crossed, still wearing the borrowed shirt that hung wrong on his foreign frame. Three days since he'd fallen out of nowhere into her patrol's path, and she still caught herself watching him for signs of sorcery—as if a man who wept when he thought no one saw could be an agent of anything.

"I'm going to find where this tunnel starts," she said.

"Under the chapel." He crossed the room without being invited, which was another thing about him she hadn't decided how to feel about. He tapped the convergence point on the map. "I've been over the surveyor's charts your hall keeps. The old chapel on the hill—ruined before anyone living was born. Its foundations sit directly above this." His finger rested on the hatched rectangle. "Whoever drew this knew exactly where they were standing."

"And you know chapels because you're a mapmaker's boy."

"Because I'm a mapmaker," he said, with a flash of pride that softened almost immediately into something more wounded. "Apprentice. Was." He looked at the alien script and shook his head slowly. "This isn't any tradition I've catalogued. And I've catalogued everything my master owned, and half of what the guild archive owns. This is older than the guilds. Older than—" He stopped.

"Say it."

"Older than the gods people worship now." He met her eyes. "You prayed to your silent god for years, yes? In this valley?" He didn't wait for her nod; he'd asked before, in the ugly first hours, and she'd answered with a blade's worth of hostility. "Then whoever wrote this was praying to something else entirely. Or wasn't praying at all. Maybe they were *building*."

Hazel rolled the map closed with hands that wanted to shake. "Tomorrow," she said. "Before dawn. If there's a door under that chapel, I want to see it before anyone else does."

"Anyone else," Rafael repeated quietly. "Like whoever killed the man who carried this?"

She didn't answer. She banked the candle herself and left him in the dark.

* * *

They went up through the fog before the sun cleared the eastern ridge, and the ruined chapel rose out of it like a ribcage.

Hazel had passed it a thousand times. Every child in the Hollow had. It sat on its bald hill above the mill road, roofless, its walls furred with lichen, and everyone knew you didn't play there because the curse was strongest on holy ground—or so the miller's wife said, or so somebody said; the stories had worn smooth as river stones from handling. Hazel had always kept her distance without knowing why. Now she understood: the place had always hummed at the edge of hearing, a pressure behind the ears she'd mistaken for wind.

Up close, the humming was a voice.

"Do you feel that?" Rafael whispered. He had gone pale in the grey light.

"I've always felt it. I thought it was the hill."

"It's not the hill." He crouched at the chapel's threshold and pressed his palm flat to the threshold stone. "There's script under the moss. Look." He scraped with his thumbnail, and beneath fifty years of green, lines emerged—fine-cut grooves in the same alien hand as the map, running along every stone of the threshold and, when they followed them, up the doorposts and across the fallen lintel and around the base of every wall. The whole ruin was stitched with it. The building hadn't been a chapel at all, or if it had, it had been built on top of something whose purpose the chapel's builders had forgotten.

Or chosen to forget.

"The curse keeps magic out of the valley," Hazel said. Her voice sounded strange to her own ears, flat and careful, like a woman reading someone else's eulogy. "That's what we're taught. That's why my god doesn't answer here. That's why—"

"That's what you're *taught*, yes." Rafael walked the wall, tracing, counting under his breath the way he counted everything. "But a ward against magic would be woven all through the valley, wouldn't it? Evenly? Like a net?" He stopped at the chapel's far corner, where two walls met, and looked back at her. "This isn't even. It's thickest here. It runs *downward*, Hazel. Every line I can follow goes down. This isn't a wall keeping something out." He swallowed. "It's a stake holding something in."

The words landed in her chest like stones dropped down a well. She thought of the stranger, scorched as if lightning had loved him, dying with the word *beneath* on his lips. She thought of a hundred unanswered prayers, and the shape of the silence they'd left in her.

"Show me the floor," she said.

The chapel's nave was choked with rubble and bramble, but beneath it the original flags remained in patches, and in the center of the crossing—where an altar should have stood—there was a slab. Round. Wide as a wagon wheel. Unmarked by moss while everything around it wore a green coat, as if the stone refused to let anything live on it.

Rafael knelt and brushed the dirt from its edge. Grooves again. And set into the slab's face, worn nearly smooth, a symbol: the same ringed mark that circled the door on the map.

"It's a seal," he breathed. "Or the top of one. The map shows tunnels below—there has to be an entrance, a stair, something—"

"There's no stair," said a voice behind them. "Because I filled it in with rock and my own two hands, thirty-one years ago, and I'll thank you both to step away from it very slowly."

Brannoc stood in the broken doorway with a hunting crossbow leveled at them, and his face was the face of a man who had aged ten years overnight.

* * *

He made them sit against the far wall while he kept the crossbow up, which was absurd—he'd taught Hazel everything she knew about arms, and she could have disarmed him before his next breath—but neither of them moved. Some things outrank skill.

"Thirty-one years ago," Hazel said carefully. "You were already sergeant then. You disappeared for a season. They said you'd taken a contract in the lowlands."

"They said that because I told them to say it." Brannoc's aim did not waver, but his voice did, just barely. "There was a man. A traveler, like your corpse at the treeline. He came down out of the passes with a map case and burns on him that no fire makes, and he found me because I was the only soldier in this valley who'd ever served outside it and might believe him. His name was Aldous. He stayed in my quarters six weeks. He taught me enough of that script to read the warnings, and then the Vestals' predecessors came for him, and he ran, and I sealed the stair behind him so they couldn't use it." He exhaled. "And then I spent thirty-one years hoping I'd die before anyone else came down out of those passes carrying the same case."

"You knew," Hazel said. The word came out of her like a tooth. "You knew what the curse was. You knew what I—" She stopped, because the rest was too large. You knew I'd spent my life praying to a god who was never listening. You knew my faith was built on a lid.

"I knew what Aldous told me, which is not the same thing." Brannoc lowered the crossbow at last, and he suddenly looked every one of his sixty-odd years. "He said the valley wasn't cursed, girl. He said it was *sentenced*. There's a difference. A curse is punishment. A sentence is containment. And he said the ones who built the ward weren't priests of any god you'd recognize. They were jailers." He looked at the round slab in the chapel floor as if it might hear him. "And he said the thing they jailed had once been worshipped here, before the jailers came, and that it had many voices, and that some of the voices were kind."

Rafael had gone very still. "The Vestals," he said. "The ones who came for Aldous. Are they—"

"Still alive? A few. Old now. Fewer every year." Brannoc rubbed his eyes. "Which is why I need to know, and I need you to answer true, Hazel Underhill: who else knows you have that case? Who saw the stranger die?"

"No one. We buried him ourselves. No one—"

The sound came up the hill from the town below: bells. Not the alarm pattern, not the market peal. The slow, spaced tolling that meant visitors of consequence were entering the gates.

Brannoc went to the broken window and looked down at the mill road, and Hazel watched the color leave his face.

Three figures in grey robes were walking up through the morning fog toward the gate, unhurried, their cowls raised against the light. At the gate, the guardsmen were already bowing, because robed strangers who arrive unannounced are either pilgrims or trouble, and trouble is safer bowed to.

"They're early," Brannoc said softly. "Aldous bought us thirty-one years, and they're *early*." He turned from the window, and when he looked at Hazel, it was the look of a man handing over a weight he could no longer carry. "They'll be asking after a dead traveler by nightfall. And when they do, girl, everything I just told you becomes the most dangerous secret in this valley—so decide fast whether it's a secret you're going to keep, or one you're going to finish digging up."

Below, the bells tolled on, and beneath Hazel's boots—faint, patient, almost like breathing—the hill hummed.
