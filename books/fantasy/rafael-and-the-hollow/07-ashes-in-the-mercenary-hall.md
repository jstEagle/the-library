## 7. Ashes in the Mercenary Hall

The fire started at the hour when the town's lamps were lit, so that for a few moments no one could tell which light was which.

Hazel was three streets away, walking home from the well with a yoke of water across her shoulders, when she smelled the pitch. She knew that smell. Every mercenary did—tar and tallow and the sweet reek of oiled timber going up all at once—and her body understood it before her mind did. The yoke cracked against the cobbles as she dropped it and ran.

The Mercenary Hall of the Hollow had stood for two hundred years. It had survived the border skirmishes, the famine winters, the year the river froze to its bed. Its beams were black oak, older than the chapel on the hill, older than any name anyone still used for anything. Now its great doors were open and vomiting flame like a mouth, and figures in grey robes stood in a silent half-circle before it, watching, their hands folded, their faces calm as men at a funeral they had paid for.

*Vestals.*

Hazel stopped in the street. Her hand went to the sword she wasn't wearing, because no one wore swords to fetch water, and the absence of it felt like standing naked in a snowfield.

There were six of them this time. Before there had been two—the polite ones who had come asking about a dead traveler, who had smiled with their mouths while their eyes took inventory of everything Hazel owned. Now there were six, and behind them, held between two robed women by his arms, knelt Brannoc.

They had beaten him. Not badly—not enough to kill, only enough to demonstrate competence. His left eye was swollen shut, his lip split, blood drying in a dark line down his chin. But when he saw Hazel in the crowd that was gathering, screaming and weeping and hauling buckets that would do nothing, he shook his head. Slowly. *Don't.* The old sergeant's whole vocabulary of command compressed into one gesture.

"You have it," said the eldest Vestal. She stepped forward out of the firelight, and Hazel saw that her robes were scorched at the hem, that she had personally carried the torch. Her voice was not unkind. That was the worst part. It was the voice of a woman explaining arithmetic to a slow child. "Three days. Bring the map to the old chapel at dusk on the third day, or we open the seal ourselves, and we open it *our* way. You have seen what sleeps beneath your valley, Underhill. Would you rather it woke angry or awake?"

"It's not yours," Hazel said. "It's not theirs. It belongs to no one."

"Nothing belongs to no one." The woman tilted her head. "You of all people should understand service. You prayed to our god for twenty years. He never answered. Do you wonder why? Because his voice is *down there*, girl, pressed under stone, and every prayer you ever wasted was a coin dropped into a well with no bottom. We can give you back the god you loved. All you must surrender is a piece of leather."

The crowd had heard. Hazel felt them hearing it—the shift in the air, the way backs stiffened and eyes slid toward her. Old Marta the baker, whose bread Hazel had bought every market day since she was twelve. The cooper's sons, whom she'd once pulled out of the flooded quarry. Faces she had bled for, faces that had drunk with her, all turning by degrees like weathervanes.

"She brought them here," someone said. Not loudly. Just loud enough.

"The stranger came to *her*," someone else answered. "She should have burned the map that night."

"We should have burned *her*."

Brannoc made a sound then—a roar, muffled through broken lips, and one of the women struck him across the head with something short and heavy, and he sagged, and Hazel's vision went white at the edges.

"Three days," the eldest Vestal repeated. "And Underhill—grief makes people stupid. Don't be stupid."

They walked into the dark with their hostage between them, unhurried, and the hall collapsed inward with a sound like a felled forest, and sparks rose over the Hollow higher than Hazel had ever seen anything rise, and no rain came to meet them because the Hollow kept even the sky on a short leash.

Hazel stood in the crowd of her own town and let them hate her, because hating her was easier than being afraid, and she understood that, and could not even blame them for it.

* * *

Rafael found her at dawn, sitting on the steps of the gutted hall, still in yesterday's dress, soot in her hair where the heat had reached her even across the square.

He had spent the night doing what cartographers' apprentices do: making lists. On the walk over he had rehearsed what he'd say. He'd been up until the small hours drafting it, actually, the way he drafted everything—carefully, twice, with margins for error. *This began with me. The spell that dragged me here used the dying man's last breath as fuel, but I was the cargo. If I leave—if I draw them after me, away from the valley, away from you—*

He had imagined her nodding. He had imagined relief. What he had not prepared for was the look on her face when she turned: not grief exactly, though grief was in it somewhere, deep down under the ash. It was the look of a woman who has finished counting her losses and arrived at a number she can carry.

"They took Brannoc," she said, before he could speak.

"I know. Half the town knows. They're saying—" He stopped.

"Saying I brought this doom down on us. Yes. They're right, more or less." She rubbed her palms on her knees, leaving grey streaks. "Which brings me to your part in it, which is none, and mine, which is considerable, and the fact that you've been standing there holding a speech behind your teeth since you rounded the corner. I can see it. You hold your shoulders like a man reciting."

Rafael flushed. "I was going to offer to leave."

Her eyebrows rose a fraction. Encouraging, possibly.

"The Vestals want the map. They followed the stranger's trail, and the stranger's trail ended where I appeared. If I'm gone—if I draw them after me—they have no reason to stay in the valley. No reason to hold Brannoc. No reason to burn anything else." He had practiced this part too, and now that he delivered it, it sounded thin, the way rehearsed things always sound when they meet the air. "My master has contacts in the lowland cities. People who deal in... contested documents. I could take the map south, lead them off, and—"

"And hand them the key to the door," Hazel said.

"—*distract* them from the—"

"Hazel," she corrected, automatically, the way she always did when he called her Underhill. Then: "Rafael. Sit down."

He sat.

"Do you know what I did for twenty years?" She didn't wait for an answer. "I prayed. Every contract, every hunt, every morning before the fog lifted off the fields—I prayed to a god who never once, not one single time, answered me. And I told myself that was faith. Silence was just how he spoke. Silence was discipline. Silence was love wearing work clothes." She laughed, short and bitter, a sound with no humor in it anywhere. "Turns out the silence was a scream with the volume turned down. Turns out I was praying to a prisoner and calling it devotion."

"That doesn't mean—"

"Everything means something now. That's the problem." She looked at him straight on, and Rafael realized, with the peculiar clarity of exhaustion, that she was younger than he'd first thought. Or perhaps just tired in a way that aged people temporarily, like frost on glass. "You want to leave because leaving feels like strategy. I know. I've done it my whole life. Walk away from the burning building, call it professionalism. But if you walk out of this valley with that map in your pack, they will follow you, and they will catch you—you're a boy who reads maps, not a fighter—and they will take it from you, and they will come back here anyway, except now they'll also know exactly where you hid it and how long I hesitated before sending you. There is no version of leaving that ends well. I've checked. Believe me, I spent the whole night checking."

Rafael looked down at his hands. Ink-stained, pencil-calloused. Hands that drew borders; that had never once defended one. "Then I'm useless," he said quietly. "I'm a liability with a good memory for coastlines."

"You're the only person in this valley who understands what that map actually shows."

"A door isn't complicated—"

"Not the tunnels." She leaned forward. "The *projection*. I showed you the markings and you read them in an hour. It took me two days to learn the alphabet and you were correcting my transcriptions by candlelight. Rafael, those Vestals have been hunting this thing for generations and they still need *us*. Why do you think they haven't just taken it? Why do you think they're giving three days instead of three hours? Because the map needs a reader, and they think I'll break first." Something kindled behind her eyes, banked but live. "Let them think it."

The sun was coming up properly now, gilding the smoke haze that hung over the square, catching the wet black skeleton of the hall. Somewhere a rooster crowed, absurd and indifferent. Rafael thought of his master's shop four thousand miles away—the smell of size and vellum, the drawer where his own cot was chalked out on the floor plan, the life that had fit him the way a borrowed coat fits no one. He had crossed the world in a stranger's dying breath and landed in the one place magic couldn't reach, and somehow, impossibly, it was the first place that had ever made room for him.

"If I stay," he said slowly, "I need to say something first."

"Say it."

"What happened to Brannoc—it's because of me. Because the stranger chose me, or chose near me, or whatever the truth of it is. And what happened to your hall, and your reputation, and—" He gestured helplessly at the ruin. "All of it traces back to me landing here like a stone through a window. So I need you to know that I would go. Today. Gladly. If I thought it would save one beam of that building or one hair of that old man's head. I'm not staying because I have nowhere else. I'm staying because you asked."

"I didn't ask," Hazel said.

"You're about to."

A long silence. A cart rattled past on the far side of the square; the driver spat when he saw them and did not slow down. Hazel watched him go, and Rafael watched her watch him, and understood that he was witnessing a woman renegotiating terms with the entire world.

"Stay," she said.

It came out rough, unused, like a hinge that hadn't swung in years. She cleared her throat.

"Stay. Please. I don't— I've spent my whole life alone in a crowd, Rafael. Oaths don't keep company. Contracts don't. Gods certainly don't." She was looking at the ashes now, not at him, as if it were easier to address the ruins. "Three days. I intend to spend them taking back what's ours, and I cannot do it alone, and for the first time in my miserable career I am asking instead of ordering. Stay."

Rafael found that his eyes were stinging, which was ridiculous, and that he was grinning, which was worse, and that neither thing seemed inclined to stop.

"Well," he managed. "Someone has to redraw the valley's maps anyway. This one's clearly out of date. It doesn't show any of the tunnels."

"No," Hazel said, and stood, and for a moment the dawn light caught her face and stripped ten years of silence off it. "But it shows where they're keeping him. And it shows a second entrance to the chapel undercroft that those grey-robed bastards don't know exists, because it's not on their precious original—it's on the surveyor's margin, in pencil, the way real cartographers hide things."

Rafael stared at her. "How do you know that?"

"Because you talk in your sleep," said Hazel Underhill, and picked up her sword from where it leaned against the ruined doorframe, and turned toward the hills where the enemy waited with her oldest friend between them. "Two days, apprentice. Let's go steal a miracle."
