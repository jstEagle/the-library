## 8. The Bargain at the Door

The Vestals' camp sat in the old quarry above the mill road, white tents pitched in a ring like a hand closing into a fist. Hazel saw it from the ridge at dawn, the smoke of their cookfires rising straight and thin in the windless morning, and she thought: *they camp like a prayer. Everything ordered. Everything waiting.*

Brannoc had been theirs for two days now. She knew because they wanted her to know—the sergeant's cloak hung from a pole at the camp's edge where anyone passing on the mill road could see it, a flag flown backwards, a message written in the only language fanatics ever learned to speak fluently.

Rafael stood beside her in the gorse, his breath fogging, the map case heavy against her ribs beneath her coat. He hadn't slept. Neither had she, but he wore it worse; his hands kept moving, tracing invisible coastlines on his knee, the habit of a boy who'd spent years drawing places that weren't real because the real ones had never wanted him.

"You're not actually considering this," he said.

"I'm considering everything."

"They'll kill you."

"Probably." She watched a robed figure move between the tents, unhurried, certain. "But they won't kill me before they've used me. That's the difference between them and the thing under the chapel. The Vestals still think I'm worth something alive."

"And Brannoc?"

She was quiet a moment. Below, the mill wheel turned without water, creaking its slow complaint—she'd heard it every morning of her life and never once listened to it until now.

"Brannoc taught me how to hold a blade when I was nine and too small for it," she said. "He told me the trick wasn't strength. It was deciding, before the fight started, what you were willing to lose. Nobody ever decides that. They wait until the fight tells them." She stood, brushing the wet gorse from her knees. "I'm deciding now."

"Hazel—"

"Three days, they said. It's been two. If I go in there and trade myself for him, we get him back, and you keep the map, and you get one more night to study it properly. If I don't go, they take all three of us and the map and the valley besides." She looked at him then, and something in her chest ached with an old loneliness finally put down. "You said your master used to say a map is a promise about a place. Well. This is mine."

* * *

They let her walk down the mill road alone, as she'd demanded, hands empty and visible, the map case left behind in Rafael's keeping—or so the Vestals believed. Sister Veyrel met her at the quarry's mouth, the same grey-robed woman who had marked them both as heretics in the town square, though today her hood was back and her face was older than Hazel remembered, seamed and tired, a face that had been praying a long time to a god that did not answer.

"You came alone," Veyrel said.

"I came to bargain. Him for me. That's the whole of it."

"And the map?"

"Stays where it is. You get me instead. I've carried it for weeks—I know its roads, its door, its markings. Whatever you mean to do at the seal, I'm worth more to you than leather and ink."

Veyrel studied her with pale, patient eyes. Behind her, two younger Vestals flanked the tent where Brannoc was held, and beyond them the quarry dropped away into terraced shadow, old cuts in the hillside where men had once torn stone out of the world's face. Hazel thought: *we are all of us digging at something.*

"You love him," Veyrel said. Not a question.

"He's my family. The kind you choose."

Something moved across the woman's face—there and gone, like a fish under ice. "Then you understand better than most why we do what we do." She stepped aside. "Come. You'll want to hear what we have to say before you decide what you're worth."

* * *

They gave her tea she didn't drink and a stool by a brazier whose coals glowed a color Hazel had never seen fire burn—faintly blue at the heart, magic-fed, the first true sorcery she'd ever witnessed inside the Hollow. It should have been impossible. The ward should have strangled it. Unless—

Unless the ward was already thinning. Unless the entity below was already pressing its fingers through the weave.

Veyrel sat across from her, hands folded, and for a long moment simply looked at her the way one looks at a wound one is deciding whether to lance.

"You prayed to it," Veyrel said. "All your life. The god of the Hollow. The Silent One."

"Every contract. Every hunt. Every night before sleep, for twenty years."

"And it never answered."

"No."

"It answered us." Veyrel's voice didn't rise; it went quieter, which was worse. "Once a generation, at the consecration, the voice would come. Guidance. Blessing. A single sentence, sometimes only a word, and we built our whole lives around those words. My grandmother heard it speak her name. Do you understand what that does to a person? To be *known* by a god?" Her hands tightened in her lap. "And then, sixty years ago, the voice stopped. Mid-sentence, the records say. Our last high vestal wrote down the final words: *'Do not open the door, for I am—'* And nothing after. Sixty years of silence."

Hazel's mouth had gone dry. "Because it died."

"Because it *split*." Veyrel leaned forward, and the brazier's blue light carved hollows under her eyes. "That is the truth we have guarded since the founding, girl, the truth the map-bearers were meant to carry and none of them lived long enough to deliver. Listen to me, because no one else will tell you plainly. There were two of them, in the beginning. Or one of them twice. The scholars argue. What matters is this: the voice that spoke to the faithful, and the thing sealed beneath the valley—they are the same god. It divided itself to end the war. Put its power below, behind the door, and kept only its voice above, to shepherd the people it had saved."

"That's—" Hazel started, and stopped. The chapel tunnels. The weeping. The voice that had spoken to her through the stone in *her own god's cadence*, pleading, grieving. She had told herself it was mimicry. A trap. A lie wearing a beloved face.

"The seal doesn't just cage the power," Veyrel went on. "It cages half of *it*. Half its mind. Half its heart. The voice above has been speaking with half a soul for a thousand years, and now even that half is failing, because the ward feeds on it—every year the containment holds, the voice grows thinner, and every year the prisoner below grows louder. They are pulling against each other like two men hauling on opposite ends of one rope." She spread her hands. "Restore the voice fully—break the seal, let the halves reunite—and the war begins again, because the power comes up with the mind. Keep the seal—and the god dies slowly, in pieces, and takes the valley's blessing with it. There is no door out of that room, Hazel Underhill. We have searched for sixty years. There is no version where the god speaks again and the valley survives."

The brazier crackled. Somewhere outside, a Vestal was singing the dawn office, a low looping melody Hazel recognized from childhood—from festivals, from funerals, from every ceremony where the faithful had lifted their faces toward a silence and called it holiness.

"You knew," Hazel said. Her own voice sounded far away. "Your order has always known. And you let us pray anyway. You let children kneel and ask a dying half-god for help with their sick mothers and their lost goats, and you never told them—"

"What would telling them have done?" For the first time Veyrel's composure cracked, and underneath it was not cruelty but exhaustion, vast and geological. "Take away the faith of a valley and you leave it nothing but fear. We chose to give them the silence and call it mystery. Yes. We lied. Every generation of us has lied, and every generation has hated itself for it, and every generation has concluded that the lie was kinder than the truth." She rose, and her shadow fell long across Hazel in the blue light. "You called us fanatics. Perhaps we are. But we are fanatics of a dying sentence, girl. *'Do not open the door, for I am—'* We have spent sixty years trying to finish it. Some of us believe the missing word is *sorry*. Some believe it is *dying*. And some—" her eyes went to Hazel's coat, to the place where the map would be if Hazel had been fool enough to bring it, "—some believe the sentence ends with the prisoner free, and the war ended properly this time, on our terms, with the faithful armed and ready. Three days, Underhill. Then we stop asking."

They took her to see Brannoc before dusk. He sat bound in the tent, one eye swollen shut, and when he saw her he laughed—a wet, cracked sound.

"Told you," he rasped. "Asking questions is how mercenaries disappear."

"I'm not disappearing," Hazel said, kneeling to cut his bonds with a stolen knife she'd palmed from the mess tent, her hands steady, her heart anything but. "I'm trading. You're going to walk down that hill, find Rafael, and you're both going to be at the treeline by tomorrow's dark."

"And you?"

She thought of the sealed door. Of the weeping. Of a god cut in half to save a world that worshipped its wound. Of twenty years of prayers falling into a silence that was not abandonment but *amputation*.

"I'm going to reach the seal first," she said quietly, so only he could hear. "Before they do. Before it wakes. Because I finally understand what I'm willing to lose, old man, and it isn't you, and it isn't him, and it isn't even me."

Brannoc searched her face with his one good eye. "And the god?"

Hazel cut the last cord. Outside, the dawn office had ended, and the quarry rang with the ordinary sounds of a camp preparing for an extraordinary sin: canvas snapping, boots on stone, the coals of a blue fire being fed.

"There was never a god listening," she said. "There was only half of one, screaming politely. Go."

He went. And Hazel turned back toward the heart of the camp, toward the terraced dark of the quarry and the door beneath the world, carrying the only weapon the Vestals hadn't thought to search her for: the truth, and the grief that came with it.
