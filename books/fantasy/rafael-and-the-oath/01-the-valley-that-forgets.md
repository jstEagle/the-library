## 1. The Valley That Forgets

The dead, Gideon had learned over ten years of listening, were mostly terrible gossips.

Old Marta's femur, which he was carving that morning into a memorial rose for her granddaughter's mantel, would not stop complaining about the weather. Not the current weather—Marta had been in the ground eleven years—but the weather of her whole life, catalogued and ranked. The flood of her fortieth year. The hail that ruined the plum harvest when she was a girl. The winter she'd spent certain the sun had simply given up on them all.

*Cold then,* she murmured through the bone under his chisel, in the thin, papery voice only he could hear. *Cold now. You'd think being dead would settle a person's temperature.*

"It settles most people," Gideon said quietly, turning the bone against the light. It was a good piece, dense and honey-colored, taken with permission from the family's ox rather than from Marta herself—the valley folk had opinions about that, and Gideon honored them. "You're just contrary."

*I am thorough,* said Marta. *There's a difference.*

The workshop smelled of linseed oil and bone dust and the particular sweetness of beeswax warmed by the little stove. Outside the window, Aldervale went about its morning: smoke rising straight from chimneys in the windless air, goats complaining their way up the terraced lanes, old Tam Hollis arguing with his son about fence posts in voices pitched to carry across three fields. Ordinary. Unhurried. The valley held its people like cupped hands.

It held other things out. That was the arrangement.

Gideon set down his chisel and flexed his fingers, working stiffness from the knuckles. Ten years ago, on a night he still could not think about without his chest going tight as wet leather, his mother had gripped his wrist with the last strength she had and made him swear.

*Never leave the valley, Gideon. Whatever comes to the door, whatever calls you past the stones. Swear it. Swear it on my name.*

He had sworn. He had been nineteen and hollowed out with grief, and he had sworn because she asked, and because her voice had been afraid in a way he had never heard before—not afraid of dying, but afraid of something on the other side of it. He hadn't understood then. He understood only that the oath had weight, that it settled over him like a second skin, and that the valley—which everyone knew was magic-dead, which was why the grieving came down from the high country to bury their dead where nothing could whisper back—had become his fortress whether he wanted one or not.

Most days he didn't mind. Most days there were bones to carve and bread to bake and the dead to keep company, and the dead, whatever else they were, were never lonely in quite the way the living managed to be.

*The girl coming today,* Marta said suddenly. *The one with my rose. Tell her I liked the yellow dress better than the grey one. She'll know what it means.*

"You hated the yellow dress."

*I lied to her about hating it. There's a difference.* A pause, papery and dry. *Tell her anyway. Dead women have so few chances to be kind.*

Gideon smiled despite himself and reached for the fine rasp. This was the part no one paid him for, and the part he did best: the messages. The valley folk brought him bones to carve because he was skilled, and skilled carvers were common enough in the high country too. But they came back year after year because the memorials he made felt *right*, somehow. Because widows wept at his roses and said, *it's as if she's in it*, and sons held his carved hands and swore they could almost hear—

They never finished the sentence. In Aldervale, you didn't finish sentences about hearing things. The valley forgot its magic the way a body forgets pain, thoroughly and on purpose, and the forgetting was the point.

Only Gideon remembered. Only Gideon heard.

He was sanding the curve of a petal when the light changed.

Not dimmed—changed, the way it does when a cloud crosses the sun except there wasn't a cloud, and the air itself seemed to lean toward the door. The stove flame stood very still. Even the dust motes hung suspended, and beneath them Gideon felt something he had not felt in ten years: a crack. A hairline fracture running through the numbness of the valley, thin as a scratch across varnish, and through the crack seeped a smell of pine and cold iron and something older underneath, something like a cellar full of years.

Then footsteps on his path. Uneven ones. Dragging ones.

The door burst open—it was never locked; who locked doors in Aldervale—and a man fell through it.

He was tall, or had been built tall, though he came into the room at a steep sideways angle, one hand pressed to his ribs where his coat was dark and slick. His face was grey as tallow, his lips already going the blue of deep water, and his eyes—his eyes were the worst part, because they were *clear*, terribly clear, the eyes of a man who has run a very long way from something and knows exactly how far behind him it is.

"Door," the man rasped. "Light. Saw the light."

"There's a healer two lanes down," Gideon said, already moving, catching the man's arm before he folded. The arm was cold. Cold as riverbed stone in a way that had nothing to do with blood loss, and through his palm Gideon felt the crack in the valley widen, just slightly, like ice groaning before it gives.

"No healer." The man let himself be lowered onto the workbench stool, then further, half-collapsed against the wall, and laughed—a horrible, wheezing sound with blood in it. "No healer in any world fixes what I've got. But you—" His clear eyes fixed on Gideon's face and went wider. "*You.*"

"I'm Gideon Almeida. Bone-carver."

"Not your name." The man shook his head slowly, and there was something almost tender in it, which frightened Gideon more than the blood did. "I know your name. Your real one. The one she called you across the garden when you were small enough to ride her shoulders." He swallowed, wetly. "*Little Wren-Song.*"

The workshop went silent. Not quiet—silent, the way the valley made everything silent, but inside-out, pressing in. No one living knew that name. No one living had ever known it. His mother had used it perhaps a dozen times in his whole childhood, always softly, always like a secret passed palm to palm, and she had carried it with her into the grave, and Gideon had never spoken it aloud since, not once, not even alone.

"How do you—" His voice came out strange. Thin. "Who are you?"

"Corvin." The man slid further down the wall, and Gideon lunged to ease the descent, lowering him flat along the bench, coat and all, blood smearing the sawdust. "Corvin Ashe. And I haven't much time, so listen, Little Wren-Song, because I ran a long way to say this and I'll only get to say it once."

"Let me at least—"

"*Listen.*" Corvin caught Gideon's sleeve with shocking strength. Up close, Gideon could see the wound now, and wished he hadn't: not a blade-cut, not an animal's tearing, but a neat round hole bored through the man's side, edges smooth as if drilled, and around it the flesh was pale as candle wax. Something had taken a piece of him. Something precise. "I sold something I shouldn't have. Down under the mountain, there's a court, and the court keeps accounts, and I owed, and I ran, and they collect, Little Wren-Song. They always collect. Thirteen moons, and the ledger comes due, and everything unpaid goes home to them."

"You're raving. There's no court under the mountain. There's nothing under the mountain but rock."

"That's what the valley wants you to think." Corvin smiled, and it was the kindest, saddest expression Gideon had ever seen on a dying face. "That's what it's *for*. This whole valley. All that lovely numbness. Don't you wonder, boy? Don't you ever wonder why nothing magical can follow you here? Why you're safe?"

Gideon's mouth had gone dry. Through the window, Aldervale went on—goats, smoke, Tam Hollis's voice raised over fence posts—as if the world were intact. But the crack in the air was widening, and through it Gideon could hear, faintly, for the first time in ten years, something that was not a dead voice. Something that hummed below hearing, patient and vast, like a great beast turning over in its sleep beneath the mountain's roots.

"My mother," Gideon said. His heart was slamming. "What do you know about my mother?"

Corvin opened his mouth—and stopped. His eyes rolled back. His grip on Gideon's sleeve went slack, then clenched again with a final surge of will, and with his other hand he fumbled inside his coat and pressed something hard and small into Gideon's palm, folding Gideon's fingers over it with both of his own shaking ones.

"Dawn," Corvin whispered. Blood threaded from the corner of his mouth. "Come dawn, I'm theirs. Unless—" His gaze drifted past Gideon's shoulder, toward the mountain visible through the window, blue and distant and harmless-looking, and his whisper dropped to almost nothing. "Unless someone steals us back."

"Steals *us*?" Gideon said. "Who is *us*?"

But Corvin's eyes had found Gideon's face again, and what was in them now was recognition, and grief, and something that looked unbearably like pity.

"She never told you," he breathed. "Oh, you poor cub. She never told you what she traded."

Outside, the light failed all at once, though it was barely midday. The stove flame guttered. Somewhere far above the valley, beyond the rim of stones Gideon had never once crossed, a moon that should not have been visible in daylight showed itself briefly through the crack in the sky—a thin white sliver, watching.

And in Gideon's palm, the thing Corvin had given him grew warm as a living thing.

* * *

He got Corvin onto the cot in the back room, propped on pillows, and did everything he knew, which was little. Water, mostly. The man drank it like it was an apology.

The object in Gideon's pocket was a sliver of bone, flat and long as a knife, and carved across its surface in lines fine as spider silk was a map. He had not dared look at it properly yet. Every time his fingers brushed it through the fabric, it warmed, and somewhere at the edge of his hearing—below Marta's papery complaints, below the valley's blessed silence—something answered.

Corvin slept in fits. Between them, he talked, in fragments, in a voice growing thinner by the hour.

"The court keeps souls in lanterns," he murmured once, eyes closed. "Little lights, each with a label. Mine says *one debt, unpaid*. Yours—" A wet, rattling laugh. "Yours isn't written yet, Little Wren-Song. Isn't that a mercy. Isn't that the only mercy left."

"What did my mother trade?" Gideon asked for the fourth time.

Corvin only smiled and turned his face to the wall.

Toward evening, Gideon stepped outside to fetch more water and found the lane empty in a way lanes are never empty. The goats had gone quiet. Tam Hollis's argument had died mid-sentence. Down the street, Widow Pram stood at her gate staring up at the mountain with an expression Gideon had never seen on her broad, cheerful face—an expression of vague, animal unease, like a hen that has noticed the shadow of a hawk without understanding what shadows are.

She blinked, shivered, and went inside without a word.

The valley was watching the mountain. The valley, which forgot everything, was remembering something.

Inside, near midnight, Corvin began to speak names. Dozens of them, hundreds, a litany whispered into the pillow—some Gideon recognized as the names of the dead whose bones had come through his workshop over the years, and some he did not, and one, repeated over and over, soft and wondering, that stopped Gideon's breath:

*"Lena. Lena, you clever, brave, foolish woman. What did it cost you? What did it cost you?"*

His mother's name.

Gideon sat down hard on the stool beside the cot. The stove ticked. The map-bone burned warm against his ribs like a second heartbeat. And in the silence of the magic-dead valley, in the silence that had kept him safe for ten years, Gideon Almeida heard—for the first time—the sound of the silence cracking.

Dawn was four hours away.
