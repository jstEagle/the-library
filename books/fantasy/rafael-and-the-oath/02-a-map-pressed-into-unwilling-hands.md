## 2. A Map Pressed Into Unwilling Hands

The stranger lasted until dawn, which was longer than Gideon had expected and shorter than he'd hoped.

He had spent the night in the chair beside his workbench, watching the man's chest rise and fall in shallow, rattling intervals, and in those hours he had learned three things. First: the wound in the stranger's side was not a knife wound, though it had been made by something with an edge. Second: no poultice, no willow bark, no prayer Gideon half-remembered from his mother's mouth could touch it. And third — this was the one that kept his hands busy, sanding a piece of sheep bone that did not need sanding — the dead were already gathering around the bed like moths around a lamp that hadn't been lit yet.

They always came early for the dying. It was one of the small cruelties of his gift, or curse, or whatever you called a thing that let him hear the voices of the dead held in the bones he carved. The recently dead were the worst. They hadn't learned quiet yet. They hovered at the edges of the workshop, murmuring questions — *Is it cold? Is it dark? Does it hurt?* — as if Gideon were some kind of ferryman who'd forgotten to mention the fare.

But tonight they were silent. All of them. Drawn back like a tide going out, leaving only the stranger's ragged breathing and the tick of the stove.

That silence frightened Gideon more than any voice ever had.

* * *

The light came up gray through the workshop's single window, the color of old linen, and with it the stranger opened his eyes.

"You're the carver," the man said. His voice was dry leaves and gravel. "The Almeida boy."

"I'm the carver," Gideon agreed carefully. He was thirty-one years old and had not been anyone's boy in ten years, but there was no point arguing with a dying man about grammar. "You said a name last night. Before you collapsed."

"I said a great many things." The stranger tried to sit up, failed, and settled for propping himself on one elbow with the dignity of a man pretending the effort cost nothing. Up close, Gideon could see what the dark had hidden: the man was hollowed out, cheekbones like tent poles, skin the color of tallow. Not starving. *Emptied.* "Did I say your name?"

"You said 'Little Wren-Song.' That was—" Gideon stopped. Swallowed. "That was what my mother called me. No one living knows that name."

"No," the stranger said softly. "No one living does."

The stove ticked. Somewhere outside, the first cart of the morning rattled over the bridge stones, and old Marta could be heard scolding her goat with the particular venom she reserved for Tuesdays. The valley went on being the valley: safe, dull, wrapped in its numbness like a blanket pulled to the chin.

"Who are you?" Gideon asked.

"Corvin." He said it like a man setting down something heavy. "I was a mapmaker, once. Then I was other things. Then I was a debtor." He smiled, and it was the worst smile Gideon had ever seen, because it was genuinely warm. "You have your mother's frown, Little Wren-Song. She frowned exactly like that when I told her she was making a mistake."

The world tilted slightly. Gideon gripped the edge of the workbench. "You knew my mother."

"I knew of her. We never met. Debtors rarely meet their benefactors." Corvin's hand crept beneath his coat, moving with the slow deliberation of a man whose body had begun charging interest on every motion. "She bargained well, your mother. Better than I did. I sold my soul outright — foolish, greedy, I wanted things. She only pawned a piece. A clever woman. But the court keeps accounts, and accounts come due, and when they do—" He drew out his hand, and in it was a long, thin sliver of bone, yellowed and dense, carved so finely the marks looked like frost on a window. "—they send collectors."

"What is it?"

"A map. To the court beneath the mountain. Every road, every door, every tithe-tunnel. Carved into the femur of a man who owed them more than I did." Corvin pressed it toward him. "Take it."

"No." Gideon stepped back. The bone sat in the stranger's palm like a splinter working toward the heart. "I don't want it. I don't want anything to do with courts or debts or whatever my mother—"

"She didn't tell you." Corvin's eyes were pitying now, and pity from a dying man is a terrible currency. "Of course she didn't. She bought your safety with her silence, boy, and paid for ten years of it with interest, and you think the valley's numbness is just weather." He laughed, and the laugh turned into coughing, and the coughing brought blood to his lips, thin and bright. "The thirteenth moon turns soon. When it completes, all unpaid debts are collected. Hers included. Mine included." He seized Gideon's wrist with shocking strength. "Take. It."

And because the dead were silent, and because the man's grip was iron, and because some traitor part of Gideon had been waiting ten years for permission to be afraid — he took it.

The bone was warm. Bone should never be warm.

Corvin sagged back against the cot, spent. "There's a gate," he whispered. "It opens to a willing soul. I meant to use it. I meant to give myself back, somehow, make them unmake the bargain. I ran out of moons." His breath whistled. "Don't run out of moons, carver."

Then he died, the way candles go out — one moment present, the next just smoke and the memory of warmth.

* * *

Gideon buried him at noon, behind the workshop, in ground that was soft from rain. He carved the marker himself, quick and rough, a simple stone-bone hybrid that would hold a name for a hundred years. He did not listen while he worked. He refused to listen.

Which lasted until sunset, when the voice started.

It came from everywhere and nowhere, the way the dead speak when they've only just crossed — thin, frayed, echoing off the inside of Gideon's skull rather than arriving through his ears. The valley's numbness usually muffled them to whispers. This voice came through clear as a bell struck against his teeth.

*Carver.*

"I'm not listening," Gideon said aloud, to an empty workshop. "You're dead. Dead men don't get to make requests."

*I'm dead,* the voice agreed, and there was something almost amused in it. *Dead men are the only ones who know how anything works, and no one listens to us. You see my problem.*

"I'm sorry you died. I'm sorry you're stuck. I'll carve you a finer marker tomorrow, and you can rest in it, and that's the whole of what I can offer."

Silence. Long enough that Gideon almost believed he'd won.

*She's still singing,* Corvin said then, gently, terribly. *Your mother. Her voice-soul hangs in the Queen's ledger hall like a lantern. Ten years, carver. Do you know what it's like to hang?*

Gideon's hands stopped moving. He had been cleaning his tools, one by one, the ritual that ended every day, and now the burnishing cloth lay still in his palm.

"The dead lie," he said. "Or they exaggerate. Or they want things, and they use the living to get them."

*All true,* Corvin admitted. *And I do want something. I want what you want, which is rare between the dead and the living. I want my soul back. And yours wants its mother's voice, even if the rest of you hasn't admitted it yet.* A pause, and when the voice came again it was quieter. *I begged, at the end. In the court. I begged, and the Queen showed me my own entry in the ledger, and the price column, and she said, "You set the price yourself." I did, carver. That's the joke. That's the whole rotten joke. We set our own prices.*

"Go away."

*I can't. I'm tied to the map now. Where it goes, I go.* Another pause, and Gideon could have sworn he felt the dead man settle in somewhere behind his left ear, like a cat finding a warm spot. *Get used to me. I'm told I'm excellent company.*

Gideon threw the burnishing cloth across the room. It hit the wall and slid down, and the workshop was very quiet except for the stove, ticking, and the voice that wasn't using the air at all.

* * *

At midnight he took the map to the hearth and tried to burn it.

This was, in retrospect, predictable. He had watched Corvin press it into his hands; he had felt the weight of obligation settle onto his shoulders like a yoke; and Gideon Almeida had spent ten years learning that obligations, unlike bones, could sometimes be destroyed. Fire had seemed reasonable. Fire had seemed almost comforting — the idea that the whole terrible machinery of courts and debts and thirteenth moons could be reduced to ash and smoke and a bad smell.

He built the fire hot. He fed it split oak until the flames stood up straight and roared. Then he held the bone map over the heat with a pair of tongs and waited for it to blacken.

It did not blacken.

He held it there for one minute. Five. Ten. The flames licked around it and bent away, the way water bends around a stone, and the bone stayed cool and yellow and faintly, obscenely warm in the tongs' iron grip. The fine carved lines caught the firelight and glittered, and Gideon realized with a lurch that the lines had changed since dawn — new roads surfacing like veins rising under skin, the map redrawing itself, keeping current, keeping count.

*It knows where it's needed,* Corvin observed from behind his ear. *Maps are like that. The good ones.*

"It's a debt instrument," Gideon said grimly. "It's not a map. It's a bill."

*Those are often the same thing.*

He pulled it from the fire and set it on the workbench, where it lay gleaming among his honest tools — the chisels, the rasps, the little hooked knife his mother had given him at fourteen, saying, *This is for kindness, Little Wren-Song. Everything else is for craft.* The map did not belong here. It belonged nowhere. It belonged beneath the mountain, in a hall full of hanging lanterns, and the thought arrived unbidden and unwelcome and absolutely certain.

Outside, the valley lay silent under the stars. Too silent. Gideon had lived here ten years and knew its sounds the way he knew his own heartbeat — Marta's goat, the bridge stones, the wind combing the barley. Tonight there was nothing. No goat. No wind. As if the valley itself were holding its breath.

In the morning, he understood why.

They came at first light — three elders, walking slowly up the lane in their gray wool, faces folded shut like letters no one wanted to read. Old Tam at the front, oldest of them all, his cane tapping a rhythm that Gideon had heard at every funeral and festival of the past decade. They stopped at his gate and did not knock, because elders did not knock; they simply stood, and waited, and let their presence do the talking.

"Gideon Almeida," Tam said. "Something entered the valley two nights ago."

"A traveler," Gideon said. "He was wounded. He died. I buried him."

"We know." Tam's eyes, pale as skimmed milk, moved past Gideon's shoulder to the workshop window, to the workbench, to the thing lying on it that did not belong. "We felt him come through. We felt the hole he left. The valley has been quiet a long time, carver. Quiet is a garment we weave, thread by thread, year by year. Your guest tore it."

"He was dying."

"Dying things carry debts." Tam said it without malice, which somehow made it worse. "The valley keeps you numb and safe, boy. That was the bargain. Whatever he gave you, whatever he pressed into your hands — it is not numb. It hums. We can feel it humming from the chapel green." He leaned on his cane, and for a moment the old man looked tired instead of stern, ancient instead of authoritative. "Burn it. Bury it deep. Give it back to whatever pit it crawled out of. And then, Gideon—" the pale eyes came back to his face, and held there, gentle and merciless, "—forget it. That is what the valley is for. Forgetting."

They turned and walked away, three gray shapes down a gray lane, and Gideon stood in his doorway holding the handle of a door that suddenly felt like a bar across a cage.

Behind him, the map hummed on the bench. Behind his ear, patient as winter, the dead man waited.

*Well?* Corvin asked. *What's it to be, carver? The blanket, or the mountain?*

Gideon looked at his mother's little hooked knife, lying in its place of honor. *This is for kindness,* she had said. He had always assumed kindness meant the grieving widow, the carved remembrance, the soft word at the graveside. He had never once considered that kindness might mean climbing down into the dark after someone.

Four moons, the map's new-carved lines seemed to say. Four moons until the thirteenth turns.

Gideon closed his eyes. "Damn it, Mother," he said quietly. "What did you do?"

And from behind his ear, warm as a hand on his shoulder, Corvin began — at last, and with evident relish — to tell him.
