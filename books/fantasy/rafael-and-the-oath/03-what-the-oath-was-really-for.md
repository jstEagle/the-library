## 3. What the Oath Was Really For

Sefa had been walking for eleven days when the valley finally let her in.

That was how it felt, anyway. The road narrowed at a ridge of grey stone, and on the far side of it the world went quiet in a way she had never experienced—not the quiet of empty places, but the quiet of held breath. Her oath-sense, which had hummed against her ribs like a second heartbeat since the day she was bound to the order, simply stopped. She pressed her palm flat against her sternum and waited. Nothing. Just Sefa, alone in her own body, for the first time in twenty years.

It should have been a relief. It was not.

"Charming place," she told the empty road. "Very restful. I hate it."

The valley did not answer. Valleys never did, though she'd had arguments with three hills and one suspiciously opinionated river over the course of her career.

She had come hunting a debt. Corvin Ashe, oath-breaker, soul-seller, and—she now knew—the reason her exile had teeth. Three years ago, when Sefa had stood before the Conclave of Keepers and read aloud the ledger of Vow-Warden Hestia Marrow, showing how the woman had been quietly selling the souls of debtors to something beneath the eastern mountain, half the hall had gasped and the other half had already known. What Sefa hadn't known then was who had taught Hestia the trick: a soft-spoken mapmaker named Corvin, who had learned the court's language first and sold it like a recipe.

For that truth-telling, Sefa had lost her office, her cloak, and her right to speak oaths on anyone's behalf. She kept the last part out of spite, speaking them anyway, badly, at weddings of strangers who didn't know better.

Corvin's trail had gone cold twice, then warm again, and finally led here—to a valley where magic pooled nowhere and oaths meant nothing, which was exactly where a man running from a soul-debt would go. Clever, Corvin. Except debts don't die just because you stop being able to feel them.

The village at the valley's heart was small and pale, built of timber the color of old milk. People watched her pass with the flat, polite blankness of folk who had agreed long ago not to notice things. She asked after a wounded stranger. They looked through her. She asked after a bone-carver named Almeida, because Corvin's last recorded purchase—a horse, a week back—had come from a boy who mentioned the carver's workshop by the millstream.

*Everyone knows everyone here,* the boy had said, *and no one says so.*

The workshop smelled of tallow and dust and, faintly, of something older underneath—bone, yes, but also the particular cold sweetness of things that had once been alive and were now only useful. Sefa paused in the doorway.

A man sat at the workbench with his back to her, broad-shouldered, dark-haired, carving a small curved thing with the total absorption of someone who had done nothing else for years. On the bench around him lay memorials in every stage of becoming: a bird mid-flight, a child's hand, a dog curled nose-to-tail.

"Master Almeida?" she said.

He didn't startle. That was her first clue that he'd heard her coming—which was impossible, because she'd walked the last mile on gravel like a professional.

"You're not from the valley," he said, without turning. His voice was low and careful, like a man used to being listened *to* rather than listening.

"No one is, apparently. You're hard to find. Your neighbors are excellent at not knowing things." She stepped inside. "I'm looking for Corvin Ashe. Tall, grey, talks like a book that's been rained on."

Now he turned. He was younger than his hands suggested—hands do their own aging—and there was something in his face that made her chest tighten, some rawness barely scabbed over.

"He died," Gideon said. "Three days ago. In this room."

Sefa closed her eyes briefly. Eleven days of walking, and she'd missed him by three days and one corpse. "Then I'm sorry, and also I need everything he left behind. Papers. Maps. Anything bone."

"Why?"

"Because he owed a debt that doesn't expire, and I'm the poor fool sent to collect it." She hesitated, then decided honesty was cheaper than lies in a valley that ate them. "Exiled oath-keeper. Former. I exposed a corruption in my own order and they thanked me by taking my cloak. I've been chasing Corvin's ledger-line ever since. If he died owing the court beneath the mountain, whatever he carried matters."

Gideon set down his knife very slowly. From beneath a cloth on the shelf behind him, he drew a flat object wrapped in linen, and even before he unwrapped it, Sefa's skin prickled with recognition.

A map. Bone, thin as vellum, etched with roads and chambers and a spiral descending into darkness. And threaded through its grain, faint as a pulse, the signature of a bargain.

"That's a tithe-map," she said quietly. "Court-made. It doesn't show you the way *to* the mountain. It shows the mountain the way to *you*."

"I tried to burn it." His voice was flat. "It wouldn't burn. Nothing I did touched it."

"Of course not. It's not an object, it's a contract with a handle." She took it carefully, turning it in the light. "Where did he—"

And then she saw it. There, near the map's lower edge, worked into the border scrollwork in letters so fine they might have been decoration: a name. Not Corvin's.

*GIDEON ALMEIDA.* And beneath it, smaller, in a different hand—a woman's hand, cramped and hurried:

*Paid in full. Voice entire. Hold harmless: one son, invisible, unclaimed, until claimed by another's default.*

The workshop went very still. Somewhere outside, the millstream kept up its idiot cheerful noise.

"Gideon," Sefa said, and heard her own voice change, the way it always did when she read a true vow aloud. "Who was your mother?"

"She died ten years ago." His jaw tightened. "What does that say? I can't—" He stopped. Started again. "I've read it a hundred times. The words won't hold still. They slide off. Like the valley doesn't want me to know."

The valley didn't want him to know. No—the *bargain* didn't want him to know. Sefa had seen protections like this once before, in Hestia Marrow's ledger, and she had hoped never to see one again.

She thought about lying. She was, after all, good at truth and bad at kindness, and this was going to hurt him in a place no craft could reach.

But she had been exiled for telling one truth too late. She wasn't going to make a habit of it.

"It says your mother bought your life," Sefa said. "From the court beneath the mountain. With her voice."

Gideon stared at her. "My mother could talk."

"Not anymore. Not anywhere that counts." Sefa laid the map down between them as gently as if it were a sleeping animal. "Listen to me, carver, because I'm going to say this once and then we're both going to wish I hadn't. Ten years ago, someone came for you—or would have. A debt of yours, or your father's, or simply bad luck with good hearing; the court collects all kinds. Your mother went to them and offered the only coin they value. Her voice-entire. Her self-that-speaks. In exchange, they wrote you out of every ledger. Invisible. Unclaimable. Safe."

"That's—" He laughed, one short ugly note. "That's nonsense. My mother sang. She sang while she cooked, terribly, constantly. She talked the whole valley into—" He stopped. His hand had drifted, without his noticing, to the small carved bird on the bench. "She died of a fever. Quietly. Everyone said so."

"Everyone said so because the valley forgets what bargains touch. That's what these places are *for*. Magic can't follow you here, but neither can memory, not properly. You've spent ten years in a numbed cradle thinking it was peace." Sefa kept her voice level, almost gentle, though gentleness sat in her mouth like a stone. "And here is the part I truly hate telling you. Court contracts run in cycles of thirteen moons. At the thirteenth turn, all accounts come due—all of them, everywhere, at once. Any soul still unpaid gets collected. Defaulted contracts transfer to the next of kin."

"Transfer," Gideon repeated.

"If Corvin's debt isn't settled by the thirteenth moon, the court takes his soul *and* calls due anything held in trust nearby. Including yours. Including—" she tapped the border script, "—the collateral. Your mother's voice. It was never freed, carver. It was *held*. Payment suspended, pending your continued invisibility. And you stopped being invisible three days ago when a dying man said your name in a valley that isn't supposed to know it."

Gideon sat down. Not gracefully—he simply folded, the way timber goes when the grain has been cut wrong. For a long moment he said nothing, and Sefa, who had faced down a Conclave without flinching, found she couldn't look directly at him.

"She gave up her voice," he said finally, "so I could be safe. And I thought—" His thumb moved over the little carved bird, back and forth. "I thought the oath she made me swear was fear. Never leave the valley. I resented it. Ten years, I resented it. I thought she wanted to keep me small."

"Maybe she did," Sefa said. "People are allowed to be wrong while they're being brave. Both things are usually true at once."

That earned her a look—wounded, furious, grateful, all fighting for the same face. "You came here to collect a debt."

"I came here to collect a debt," she agreed.

"And now?"

Sefa looked at the map, at the spiral descending into the mountain's roots, at the thirteenth-moon mark glowing faintly at its center like a slow countdown. Four turns left. Maybe five. She thought about her cloak, hanging in a Conclave closet somewhere, and about how she'd sworn—badly, at a stranger's wedding, out of spite—that she would never again stand by while a ledger ate someone.

"Now," she said, "I appear to be collecting a different kind of debt entirely. One I owe myself." She straightened. "Here's what I know about the court, carver, and it's not much, but it's more than you have. They deal only in willing payments. Every soul they hold was given, traded, or defaulted—they cannot take by force. Which means every soul they hold can, in principle, be *bought back*, argued free, or out-bargained. It's been done. Once. By a fool whose name got struck from every record, which is why I know it happened and nobody else does."

Gideon lifted his head. Something had changed in his face—not hope exactly, hope was too tidy a word—but the fierce, stubborn attention of a man who had spent ten years listening to the dead and had just been told the dead include his mother.

"My mother's voice is down there," he said. "In that court. Hanging somewhere. Waiting."

"Probably labeled," Sefa said, and immediately wished she hadn't, because his expression cracked open another inch. "Sorry. I'm told I do that."

"Four moons," Gideon said. He stood, and picked up the map, and his hands had stopped shaking. "You know the road. I know what I'm carrying." He met her eyes. "I'm going. Tell me why I shouldn't."

Sefa considered lying to him—telling him it was suicide, that the court ate oath-keepers for breakfast, that love made terrible currency. All true. All beside the point.

"Because you'll die," she said instead, "and because I'm coming anyway, so we may as well argue about provisions on the road. Do you own a pack? You strike me as a man who owns one pack and uses it for bones."

"I own one pack," Gideon admitted, "and I use it for bones."

"Wonderful. We leave at dawn." She was already moving toward the door, because if she stood in this workshop one moment longer, surrounded by ten years of carved grief, she was going to say something kind, and kindness made her itch. At the threshold she paused. "Carver. When we get your mother's voice back—what will you say to her?"

Gideon looked at the little bird in his hand, and then at the door of the workshop his oath had forbidden him to leave, and Sefa watched him understand, all at once, that the oath had never been a cage. It had been a wall built around something precious, by someone paying for it with the only thing she had.

"Nothing," he said quietly. "I'm going to listen."

Outside, far to the east, behind the shoulder of the world, the thirteenth moon began, silently, to turn.
