## 4. Thirteen Turns

The night I broke my mother's oath, I did it the way she always said I did everything: too carefully, and far too late.

I had spent three days telling myself I wouldn't go. Three days of carving a memorial hawk for old Marta's husband, of listening to the dead man's voice inside the bone—*tell her the roof leaks, tell her I fixed it before I died, tell her—* while Sefa sat in my workshop sharpening a knife that had never once needed sharpening, watching me with the patience of a woman who had already done her own arithmetic.

"Four moons," she said on the third evening, not looking up from the blade. "That's what's left on your mother's debt. The thirteenth moon turns, and everything unpaid goes into the ledger forever."

"I know."

"You know, but you're still sanding that hawk's wing like it has somewhere to be."

"It does have somewhere to be. Marta's mantelpiece."

Sefa set down the knife. "Gideon. Your mother sold her voice to buy you ten years of safety in a valley where nothing magical can follow. That bargain ends when the thirteenth moon turns. And when it ends—" she paused, choosing the words like someone stepping across ice, "—you stop being invisible to them. You become *findable*. Do you understand what that means?"

I understood. What I said was: "Marta will want the wing a little longer on the left. Her husband always flew left-handed."

Sefa made a sound that was half laugh and half prayer, which I would learn was simply how she laughed.

But that night, after she'd gone to sleep in the loft, I sat with the bone map Corvin had pressed into my hands as he died, and I listened—not to the dead, for once, but to the living silence of the valley. The numbness my mother had bought me. Ten years of quiet so complete I had mistaken it for peace. And beneath the quiet, faint as a heartbeat under snow, the map pulled. Northward. Toward the mountain. Toward her voice, hanging in some candlelit court like a lantern with a price tag.

*Tal,* my mother used to call me. Tal. A name I hadn't spoken aloud since her funeral, because saying it felt like opening a door I'd nailed shut.

"Tal," I said to the empty workshop. My voice cracked in the middle of it, embarrassingly, like a boy's.

Then I packed a bag, woke Sefa, and broke an oath ten years old.

* * *

You would think breaking a sacred oath would feel like thunder. It didn't. It felt like walking out of a warm house into winter—the first step is just cold, and then all at once you realize how much of you had gone numb without noticing.

We crossed the valley's edge at dawn, where the hills rise up gray and ordinary and then, quite suddenly, aren't. There's no wall. No marker. Just a line of junipers, bent by wind, and beyond them a world that had been waiting for me my entire life.

It hit me halfway between the junipers.

Magic, flooding back into a body that had spent a decade in the dead valley, is not gentle. It came up through my boots and into my teeth and behind my eyes, and I went down on one knee in the frost with Sefa's hand fisted in my collar, holding me upright.

"Breathe," she said. "Breathe through it. Everyone thinks the valley protects you from magic. It doesn't. It protects magic from *you*. You've been a stopped-up well for ten years, Tal. Wells don't uncork politely."

She wasn't wrong. But she also hadn't mentioned—and I would remember this later, when forgiveness was required—that hearing the dead again was only half of it.

Because the moment the flood crested, I heard the *living*.

* * *

No one had warned me about the living.

The dead are quiet, mostly. They repeat themselves; they grieve in loops; they ask small things. I had spent my whole life among them and thought I understood voices.

The living were a riot.

Every traveler on the north road came at me like weather. The drover passing us thought about his daughter's fever and hated himself for thinking about his mule's price in the same breath. The pilgrim woman wanted, with a ferocity that made my ears ring, to be *somewhere else*, anywhere else, and underneath that want ran another, older one—to be looked at, truly looked at, just once. Two soldiers argued aloud about rations while silently rehearsing apologies to each other, both of them, simultaneously, for a fight neither could remember starting.

I stopped walking. I put my hands over my ears, which accomplished precisely nothing, because none of it was sound.

"Gideon." Sefa crouched in front of me. "What is it? What do you hear?"

"Everyone." My voice came out thin. "They're so *loud*. They want things. All of them, all the time, they want—"

"Yes," Sefa said, and something crossed her face—not pity exactly. Recognition. "Welcome to being an oath-keeper. Now you know why we're all slightly mad. Every vow anyone ever swears near you, you can hear whether they mean it. Every lie has a flavor." She offered her hand. "Can you walk?"

I took her hand. Her surface thoughts were armored—I'd learn later that oath-keepers train themselves into silence the way monks train themselves into stillness—but beneath the armor, one thing rang clear as a struck bell: *don't let him break, don't let him break, he's the first honest thing I've found in six years.*

I decided not to mention I'd heard that. Some things you keep.

We walked. It got easier—or rather, I got better at standing in the river instead of drowning in it. By midday I could sort the noise into streams: wants here, fears there, the low constant hum of people hoping nobody notices them. By evening I could almost enjoy it. Almost. There was something in the wanting that was terrible and beautiful at once, the way fire is.

"You're smiling," Sefa said suspiciously.

"The mule drover forgave his daughter's fever for making him afraid. He just did it now, walking. He doesn't even know."

"That," said Sefa, "is either very beautiful or very rude to listen to."

"Both. I'm learning both is most things."

* * *

The map, I should say, behaved less like a map every hour and more like a creature.

Corvin's bone—it was carved from his own finger bone, Sefa told me, which explained a great deal—lay flat in my palm at rest. But when we drifted off the true road, it warmed. When we turned toward the mountain, it tugged, gently, insistently, like a dog on a lead that had spotted home.

"It's pointing," Wren would have said, if Wren had existed yet. She didn't yet. Hold that thought; the world was saving her for us.

"It's pulling," Sefa corrected, watching it strain against my fingers. "Bone remembers what it belonged to. His soul is under that mountain, and the finger wants to go home to it." She glanced at me. "Does it bother you?"

I considered. "My whole trade is listening to bones that miss their owners. This is just the first one that's asked for directions."

At dusk we made camp in the lee of a fallen oak, and I built a fire the way I'd read about in books, since building fires was not a skill the valley had ever demanded of a bone-carver. It took four attempts and a great deal of Sefa pretending not to watch. On the fifth attempt, flame caught, and I sat back feeling like a man who had taught himself to fly using only spite.

"There," Sefa said. "First fire of your real life."

I held my hands to it. And that was when I noticed the quietest thing of all: the fire had no voice. Neither did the wind, nor the frost, nor the mountain waiting black against the stars. For ten years the dead had filled every silence I'd ever stood in, and now, outside the valley, the world itself was mute—and only people, living and dead both, sang.

"Ask me why I never left," I said suddenly. "Before tonight. Ask me if it was fear."

Sefa fed a stick to the flames. "Was it?"

"My mother made me swear on her deathbed. Never leave the valley. I thought—" I turned the bone map over in my fingers, watching it lean north like a flower toward light. "I thought she was keeping me from danger. All these years I thought the oath was a cage she built around a coward."

"And now?"

"Now I know she built it around a door." I looked up at the mountain, where four moons from tonight a ledger would come due, and my mother's voice—her actual voice, the one that sang to me, that scolded me, that called me *Tal*—hung waiting to be collected. "She wasn't afraid I'd be hurt out there, Sefa. She was afraid of what I'd hear. And she was right. It's unbearable. Everyone wanting, everyone grieving, everyone lying beautifully to themselves—"

"But?"

I hesitated. Then, because she had let me hear her one honest bell, I gave her mine.

"But this morning a soldier apologized to his friend in his heart, over and over, forty times, and never said it aloud. And I thought: someone should carve that. Someone should make a bone that holds the apology he couldn't speak, so it isn't lost when he dies with it still in his mouth." I swallowed. "Ten years I carved memorials for the dead. Out here I could carve for the living. I didn't know that was allowed."

Sefa was quiet a long moment. The fire popped.

"You're going to be insufferable," she said finally, "once you get good at this."

"I'm already insufferable. You've known me four days."

"Five. And yes. I've been counting." She lay back against the oak. "Sleep, Tal. We reach the foothills tomorrow, and the foothills have thieves, toll-keepers, and worse. You'll want your wits."

I slept. Or nearly. And in the gray hour before dawn, half-dreaming, I heard it—a thread of sound so far off I might have invented it. Not the dead. Not the living. Something between: small, quick, fox-bright, singing a song about being lost.

I sat up. The map in my hand had gone cold and still, pointing not north anymore.

Pointing east. Toward a border town, and a pickpocket who did not yet know she was waiting for us.

"Sefa," I whispered into the dark. "Change of route."
