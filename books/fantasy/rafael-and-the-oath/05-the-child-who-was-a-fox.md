## 5. The Child Who Was a Fox

The trouble with being a fox was that everyone assumed you were still one.

Wren had hands now. She had a mouth that made words instead of yips, and feet in boots two sizes too big, and hair she cut herself with stolen scissors so it hung ragged around her ears like something she'd dragged through a hedge. But the town of Marrow's Edge — last town before the mountain road, all slate roofs and suspicion — looked at her and saw *vermin*. A small dirty thing that moved too quick. Something to be shooed.

Which was fine. Being underestimated was the best trick she had left.

She crouched on the wall behind the tannery, watching the two travelers cross the market square, and her nose — her useless human nose, which was a tragedy — told her nothing, but her eyes told her plenty. The tall one walked like his bones hurt. He carried a satchel against his chest the way other people carried babies, one arm curled around it, always. The woman walked like a drawn bow. Sword at her hip, but worn wrong — too high, too easy to reach, the habit of someone who'd been robbed of it once and never again.

Oath-keeper, Wren thought. Or used to be. The badge on her cloak had been scratched out, but scratched out badly, in a hurry, by someone who couldn't quite bring themselves to do it properly.

And the tall one's satchel. Wren's fingers itched. It wasn't greed, exactly. It was more like thirst. The bag *hummed* to her — not with sound, but with that feeling she got sometimes, deep under her skin where the fur used to be, that said *this matters, this matters, this is a thing that matters*. She'd learned to trust it. It had never once steered her toward anything boring.

She waited for the bellows at the smithy to groan — loud, covering everything — and dropped from the wall into the crowd like water finding a crack.

* * *

Later — much later, when everything had gone sideways — Wren would try to explain what happened next, and she'd never quite manage it. But here is what happened:

She bumped the tall man's hip with her shoulder, apologized with her whole heart ("Beg pardon, sir, sorry, sorry—"), and lifted the satchel strap off him in one smooth motion that her body remembered better than she did. Fox-hands. They knew things her head didn't.

Then she ran.

She ran because running was joy, pure and simple, even in stupid boots, even on two legs. She ran through the fish stalls and over the low wall and up the gutter-spout of the chandler's shop, and she was three rooftops away and laughing before she heard the shouting start.

She stopped in the shadow of a chimney and opened the satchel, expecting coin. Maybe a knife. Maybe something shiny enough to trade for a week of bread.

Inside was a map made of bone.

It was thin as a leaf and pale as milk, and it was *warm*, and when her thumb crossed its surface the humming in her chest turned into singing. Not with her ears. With the place under her skin. A voice like a voice inside a voice, like a song sung down a long tunnel, and the words were—

*Small one,* sang the bone-map, or something through the bone-map, *small fox-child, I hear you too.*

Wren dropped the satchel.

That was her first mistake. Her second was not running when she heard the rooftop tiles clink behind her.

"Give it back," said the tall man, "please."

He didn't sound angry. That was the horrible part. Everyone who caught her thieving sounded angry — it was practically law — but this man just stood there at the roof's edge with the wind pulling at his coat, looking at her like she was a puzzle he already felt sorry for.

"You're supposed to yell," Wren said. "You're doing it wrong."

"I've been told." He climbed onto the roof properly, which took him a while; he was all elbows and knees, like a heron in a coat. Behind him, on the street below, the ex-oath-keeper stood with her arms crossed, radiating the specific patience of someone who had already decided not to climb anything. "I'm Gideon. This is Sefa. And you have something of mine."

"It hummed," Wren said, defensive. "Things shouldn't hum."

"No," Gideon agreed quietly. "They shouldn't."

And then he did the third-worst thing anyone had ever done to her, after the court took her fur and her parents sold her debt: he *looked* at her. Really looked. The way you look at a door you hadn't known was there.

His face went strange.

"What?" she snapped.

"Nothing," he said, in the voice people use when it is absolutely not nothing.

* * *

They sat on the rooftop because Wren refused to come down — down meant walls and hands and the town constable — and because Gideon said fine, we'll sit, and Sefa said *we'll* do no such thing, and then sat anyway, muttering about oath-breakers and thieves and why she'd ever left the capital.

Wren kept the satchel in her lap, hostage-style, though they both knew she'd already lost. Her stomach growled loudly enough to startle pigeons.

Gideon produced bread and hard cheese from his coat without comment and set them between them.

This was also horrible. Kindness was much harder to steal from than a satchel.

"Eat," he said. "Then tell me your name."

"Wren."

"Just Wren?"

"Just Wren." She tore the bread with her teeth, fox-fashion, and dared him to mention it. He didn't. "What's in the bag? Why does it sing?"

Sefa's head came up sharply. "It sings?"

"To her," Gideon said. His eyes hadn't left Wren. "Not to me. Not exactly." He hesitated, and Wren watched him decide something, watched the decision move across his face like weather. "I hear the dead," he said. "It's my gift. Was my gift. The map carries voices — souls, or pieces of them. You heard it because—" He stopped. Started again, gentler, the way you'd approach a bird. "What happened to you, Wren? What are you?"

"A girl," she said immediately.

"Yes," he agreed. "And?"

Her ears — her round, human, traitor ears — went hot. Nobody had asked. In two years, nobody had *asked*. They'd chased her, cursed her, thrown things, once offered her a copper to dance like an animal (she'd taken the copper; she could still dance like an animal; it was humiliating how well). But no one had ever sat on a cold rooftop and said *and?*, as if the answer might be worth hearing.

"My parents owed a debt," she said, to the cheese, because it was easier than faces. "To the court under the mountain. Everybody up here owes somebody, but most debts get paid in grain or daughters' labor or whatever. Ours was big. My da gambled. My ma let him." She shrugged, and the shrug cost more than she wanted it to. "So the tithe-collectors came, and there was a ledger, and the ledger said our family owed a soul. And I was the smallest."

The rooftop was very quiet.

"They didn't take me all the way," she went on, faster now, because stopping would be dying. "Taking costs them, see, I figured that out later. Full taking is expensive. So they took most of me. The — the *me* me. And left the rest to run around and grow up and be their little reminder. Like a receipt." She laughed, and hated the sound of it. "A walking receipt. That's me."

"What did they leave you?" Gideon asked.

"Teeth." She grinned, sharp and real. "And the running. And dreams. I dream about snow and mice and my mother's back, warm, when I was small enough to sleep against it. Sometimes I wake up and my hands feel wrong. Too flat. Like mittens full of stones." She looked at her palms. "Sometimes I catch myself trying to talk and it comes out as — almost-barking. Just at the edge. The shape of a bark with no bark in it."

She expected pity. Pity was everywhere, thick as mud, and just as useful.

Instead Gideon leaned forward slowly and said, "May I? I won't touch you. I just need to listen."

"To what?"

"The dead aren't the only ones who echo," he said. "Everything the court takes leaves a hole, and holes ring. If your soul is still theirs, it's still *singing*, wherever they keep it. Souls can't help it. It's all they are." He closed his eyes. "Hold still."

Wren held still. Which, for her, was nearly a miracle, and she noted it with some surprise.

Gideon tilted his head, listening to something far beneath the town, beneath the ground itself, and his expression did a thing Wren had no name for — grief and wonder fighting over the same territory.

"Oh," he breathed. "Oh, *there* you are."

"What. What. Is she okay? Am I okay?"

"She's singing," Gideon said, and his voice cracked clean down the middle. "Wren — she hasn't stopped singing since they took her. Two years in a lantern in a dark court, and she's been singing the whole time. A fox-song. It sounds like snow. It sounds like—" He opened his eyes and they were wet. "It sounds like someone waiting to be found."

Wren discovered she was crying, which was annoying, because crying was such a human thing to do and she'd been managing perfectly well without it.

"That's cruel," Sefa said quietly. She was staring at Gideon like she'd never seen him before. "You know that's cruel, telling her that."

"It's true," Gideon said. "She deserves true."

"And what do you propose?" Sefa's voice hardened into the old steel; Wren could hear the oath-keeper under the exile, like a sword under a rag. "We're four moons from the thirteenth turn. We're carrying two souls' worth of debt already — Corvin's, and Gideon's mother's. The court knows we're coming. Every mile north is a mile closer to a ledger that wants us all. And now you want to add a *third* theft? A child's soul, on top of everything?"

"Yes," Gideon said.

"Gideon—"

"Sefa." He turned to her, and Wren saw something pass between them, some old argument suddenly given new weight. "My mother bought my safety with her voice. Corvin died on my floor with a map in his teeth, trying to buy back what he sold. I spent ten years believing an oath was the same thing as love, and it isn't, and I'm done pretending I don't know the difference." He looked back at Wren. "Nobody chose this for her. So somebody should choose *against* it. That's all. That's the whole plan."

"It's a terrible plan," Sefa said.

"Most good ones start that way."

Wren wiped her face on her sleeve, sniffling in the least dignified way possible, and cleared her throat.

"For the record," she said, "I'm sitting right here. And I'm coming whether you say yes or not. I know every smuggler's path from here to the mountain, I can fit through gaps the size of a loaf of bread, I can steal anything that isn't nailed down and several things that are, and I bite." She bared her teeth to demonstrate. They were excellent teeth. "Also, if you leave me here, I'll just follow you, and then you'll have to worry about me the whole way *plus* feel guilty. So really I'm saving you effort."

Sefa pinched the bridge of her nose. "We are going to die."

"Probably," Wren agreed cheerfully.

"But not tonight," Gideon said. He held out his hand — palm up, open, the way you offer a hand to a strange dog, and Wren understood that he understood exactly what he was doing. "Come with us, Wren. We'll get her back. Your soul. I swear it — not as an oath. Oaths are what people hide behind. As a promise. Those you make awake, and keep on purpose."

Wren looked at the hand. Warm, calloused, faintly dusted with bone-shavings.

Foxes didn't take hands. Foxes decided, moment to moment, free as weather, and anyone who thought otherwise got bitten.

But the song under her skin — hers, hers, *hers*, still singing in the dark — leaned toward him like a plant toward light.

She shook it. Once, hard, like a handshake between equals.

"Deal," she said. "But I get the window bed. And you're teaching me to read the map. And if either of you dies, I'm keeping your boots."

Below them, in the square, the evening bells began to ring — and underneath their bronze sweetness, faint as a heartbeat through a floor, Wren felt the mountain hum back.

Something up there had noticed them.

Something up there had started counting.
