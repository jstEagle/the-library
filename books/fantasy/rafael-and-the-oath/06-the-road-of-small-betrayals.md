## 6. The Road of Small Betrayals

Sefa had been an oath-keeper for nineteen years, and in that time she had learned exactly three things about betrayal: it always smelled like bread, it always came from someone you'd fed, and it was almost never the dramatic kind. Nobody stabbed you on a moonlit road. They simply left the gate unlatched and went to bed early.

So when the village of Hollowmere opened its gates to them with suspicious warmth—stew, straw beds, a fire already lit—she counted the exits before she touched the soup.

There were two. The main gate, which would be barred by now, she'd wager her last oath-ring on it. And a gap in the eastern palisade where the wood was newer, paler, patched recently. Villages that patched their fences in autumn were villages expecting visitors who didn't knock.

"You're staring at my fence," said the headman, a broad, cheerful man named Doss with flour on his sleeves and not one honest line in his face.

"I'm admiring your carpentry," Sefa said.

"We had a bad storm."

"In summer."

"Terrible summer," Doss agreed, and beamed at her, and ladled her more stew.

Across the fire, Gideon sat with Wren asleep against his shoulder, his long bone-carver's hands wrapped around his bowl as though it were something precious. He looked tired. He looked trusting. He looked, Sefa thought with a familiar sour twist in her chest, exactly like a man who had no idea he was tonight's special.

The tithe-collectors had been behind them for two days. She'd seen the signs: crows flying wrong, a shrine with its offering bowl smashed rather than emptied, the particular silence of a forest that knew it was being walked through by something with a ledger. The court beneath the mountain did not send soldiers after runaway debts. It sent clerks. And clerks were worse, because soldiers wanted your blood and clerks only wanted your *account*.

Three souls owed. One thirteenth moon turning. And somewhere behind them, patient as arithmetic, the collectors closing in.

"Eat," Doss urged. "You look half-starved, all three of you. Travelers are rare blessings out here."

*I'll bet they are,* Sefa thought. *Rare. Blessed. And probably delicious.*

She ate the stew anyway. Refusing food was an accusation, and accusations got gates barred early. Besides, if she was going to sell a man to a subterranean court, she preferred to do it well-fed. It seemed only professional.

* * *

She didn't sleep. She sat up in the loft of the guesthouse with her back to the wall and her knife loose in its sheath, listening to the village settle into that particular hush—not sleep, but *waiting*. Below her, Gideon breathed slow and even. Wren murmured fox-words in her dreams, small yipping sounds that should have been ridiculous and were instead heartbreaking.

And Sefa did the arithmetic she'd been refusing to do since Hollowmere's gates opened.

Here was the truth she kept buried under nineteen years of honorable service: she had been exiled for telling the truth once, and it had cost her everything, and she had sworn—privately, bitterly, in the ashes of her old life—that she would never again pay for someone else's vow. Gideon's mother had made her bargain alone. Corvin had made his alone. These were not Sefa's debts.

But if she gave them Gideon—

No. Not even that, quite. If she simply *failed to warn* him. There was a difference, and the difference was the whole of her soul, and she knew because she'd spent nineteen years learning exactly how thin that line was and exactly how many people crossed it while calling it wisdom.

If the collectors took Gideon tomorrow, they might take only him. Debts were precise things; she'd read enough ledgers to know. But if they found all three of them together—a stolen soul-child, a disgraced keeper, a marked debtor—they would take the lot, and the court did not do refunds.

One soul offered might satisfy them. Might buy passage. Might buy *her* passage.

He'd never know, a small voice pointed out. He'd be below the mountain before dawn. Wren would grieve, children grieve and mend, and you'd walk free with your own name still yours.

Sefa pressed her palms against her eyes until she saw stars.

"Nineteen years," she whispered to the dark. "Nineteen years, and this is what I am when it counts."

Below her, Wren turned over in her sleep and said, very clearly, "Sefa keeps us safe."

Not Gideon. Not the dead mother whose bargain had started all this. *Sefa.* As if it were a fact already written, already true, as settled as the color of the sky.

Sefa sat very still for a long time.

Then she climbed down the ladder, quiet as an oath-breaker, and shook Gideon's shoulder.

* * *

He woke the way stone wakes—slowly, completely, without panic. She'd noticed that about him. Ten years of listening to the dead apparently taught a man not to startle.

"Collectors," she breathed. "Two days back, maybe less. And this village—" she nodded toward the window, where lanterns were moving between houses, too many lanterns, moving with purpose, "—feeds travelers to the mountain. It's how they pay their own tithe. We're the payment, Gideon. Tonight."

He looked at her for a long moment. "You could have let me sleep."

"Yes," Sefa said. "I could have." And found, to her horror, that her voice cracked slightly on the word, so she covered it with briskness. "Eastern palisade. New wood. Get the child."

But Wren was already awake—Wren was always already awake, a habit Sefa suspected came from having once been small enough to be carried off by hawks—and blinking at them both with those amber eyes that saw far too much.

"Are we running?" she whispered, delighted.

"Quietly," Sefa said.

"Running *quietly*," Wren repeated, in the tone of someone being promised cake and told not to enjoy it.

They went out through the guesthouse's back door into a night thick with lantern-light and the smell of bread—fresh bread, at midnight, in a village that expected no travelers. Sefa heard the ovens before she understood them: great communal ovens being fired, fed, stoked hot. Her stomach turned over. She decided firmly not to wonder what Hollowmere baked for the mountain's clerks, and moved faster.

The palisade patch was exactly where she'd read it. Three pale boards, nailed fresh, and behind them—the map in Gideon's hands had begun to glow faintly, tugging eastward—a gap in the earth. A tunnel mouth, old and stone-lined, breathing cold air that smelled of deep places.

"The tithe-tunnels," Gideon murmured. The bone map shimmered, lines rising off its surface like heat-haze, tracing passages down and through and under. "Corvin knew these. They run beneath the whole valley. Under the collectors' road."

"They'll be watched," Sefa said.

"Everything will be watched," Gideon said. "But under is better than through." He glanced at her then, and there was something in his face she couldn't parse—gratitude, yes, but also a kind of gentle, terrible knowing, as if a man who heard the dead had heard something in her voice tonight that she hadn't meant to say aloud. "Thank you, Sefa."

"For what?"

"For choosing."

She had no answer for that, so she lifted Wren through the gap first, then followed, then helped Gideon pry the boards wide enough and replace them crookedly behind them—two minutes' delay, maybe three, bought with shaking arms.

It was enough. Barely. As they dropped into the tunnel's throat, she heard the guesthouse door burst open above, and Doss's cheerful voice, no longer cheerful at all, calling into the dark: "They're gone! Wake the ovens—wake everyone—the debt walks!"

Then darkness, cold stone, and the map's pale glow lighting three faces as they ran.

* * *

The tunnels went down the way grief goes down: gradually, then all at once. The walls changed from cut stone to older stone to stone that had never known daylight at all, veined with something faintly luminous, like moonlight soaked into rock and forgotten there.

Wren trotted ahead, nose-first, literally sniffing the air. "Fox ways," she declared. "These are fox ways. Old ones. Before men put doors on the world."

"Do foxes have tunnels?" Sefa asked, despite herself.

"Everything has tunnels if you're small enough and want it badly enough." Wren glanced back, amber eyes catching the glow. "That's what the court forgot, you know. It counts everything, but it never counts wanting."

Sefa filed that away next to the other impossible things the child said, in the growing drawer labeled *true anyway*.

Gideon walked with the map held out before him like a blind man's hand, and Sefa watched him and thought about the oath he'd broken to leave his valley, and the oath his mother had broken to bind him there, and the strange, ruinous mathematics of love—that people kept paying each other's debts without ever asking whether the debt was wanted.

"You nearly didn't wake me," Gideon said quietly, falling into step beside her. Wren was ten paces ahead, pretending not to listen and failing completely.

"No," Sefa said.

"Why?"

Because you trusted me and I despise that I noticed, she didn't say. Because a child said my name like it was already true, she didn't say. Because I spent nineteen years keeping other people's oaths and I will not spend the rest of my life breaking my own.

"Because," she said instead, "if the court takes you, it takes the map-reader. And I can't read bone."

Gideon smiled—a small, tired, entirely unconvinced smile—and said nothing, which was somehow worse than arguing, because it meant he'd heard the real answer anyway, the way he heard everything, the way he'd hear it even after the hearing was gone.

Ahead, the tunnel forked, and the map flared bright, and Wren stopped dead, ears—ordinary human ears, but standing alert as any fox's—pricked toward the dark.

"What is it?" Sefa whispered.

Wren turned around, and her face in the glow was grave beyond her years. "Someone's singing," she said. "Down deep. Lots of someones. And one of them—" her voice wavered, went young, went small, "—one of them is singing *my song*. The one I forgot."

The map pulsed in Gideon's hands like a heartbeat. Down the right-hand passage, the luminous veins in the rock ran brighter, thicker, converging somewhere far below toward a light that was not moonlight at all.

The court was close. And it already knew they were coming.
