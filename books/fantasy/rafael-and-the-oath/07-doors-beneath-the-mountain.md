## 7. Doors Beneath the Mountain

The map stopped humming on the third night of the climb.

I had grown used to its song by then—a thin, dry rattle like a knuckle dragged across a rib—which is a strange thing to grow used to, but grief makes strange roommates. For six days it had pulled us upward through pine and scree, tugging at my hands as though Corvin's ghost were still in there somewhere, impatient to finish his errand. Then, just past a waterfall frozen mid-cascade into something like a wedding veil, the bone went quiet.

"It knows we're close," Sefa said.

"Or it's dead," Wren offered cheerfully. "Like most of the things you two carry."

"The map is not dead," I said.

"You'd know," she said, "being an expert."

There was no arguing with that. I had spent ten years listening to dead things whisper through my hands—teeth, femurs, the little finger-bones of infants that mothers brought me wrapped in linen because they could not bear to bury them silent. The map's silence was different from theirs. It wasn't the silence of something finished. It was the silence of something holding its breath.

We made camp in the lee of the falls, and I sat up while the others slept, turning the map over in the firelight. It was carved from a single flat piece of some animal I had never identified—not sheep, not deer, nothing with a name I knew. The mountain itself was rendered in shallow relief near its center, and beneath the mountain, so faint you had to tilt it against the light, ran corridors like veins. And at the heart of those veins, a small round chamber no bigger than my thumbnail, where Corvin had once scratched a mark with something sharp and desperate.

A door. A door drawn from memory by a man who had sold himself through it and spent the rest of his short life trying to find his way back.

"Corvin," I said quietly, to the fire, to the dark, to whatever was left of him that still clung to the bone. "What did you do?"

The dead don't answer questions. They only repeat what they died holding. But the map grew warm in my palms, just slightly, the way a stone does when a bird has been sitting on it, and I chose to take that for an answer.

* * *

The gate was not what I expected, which is a foolish thing to say about anything beneath this mountain, where nothing has been what I expected since the day a dying man said my childhood name in a voice like ash settling.

I had imagined iron. Chains, portcullises, the teeth of some great beast. Instead we came around the shoulder of the mountain at dusk and found a doorway set directly into living rock—smooth, black, taller than three men—and before it, a table.

Just a table. Long, plain, oak gone silver with age, as if someone had carried it up from a farmhouse kitchen and left it here out of laziness or reverence. On it sat a single clay cup.

"That's it?" Wren whispered. "That's the terrifying door of the soul-eating court?"

"There's a cup," Sefa said grimly, as if the cup were worse than any chain.

There were no guards. There was no wall. The rock rose sheer on either side, but the door itself stood free, unattached to anything, which offended some deep part of me that believed doors belonged in walls the way hearts belong in chests. As we approached, I felt the valley-quiet again—that numbness I had lived inside for ten years—but thinner here, stretched, like a drum skin worn nearly through. Beyond that door, magic pooled thick as marrow. I could feel it pressing against the far side of the stone the way water presses against a dam.

And I could feel something else. Something watching. Not eyes—weight. The particular weight of being *counted*.

Sefa stopped ten paces out and put her arm across Wren's chest. "Nobody moves until we understand the terms."

"I understand the terms," I said. My mouth had gone dry. "Corvin told me. The gate opens only to a soul willingly offered." I looked at the door, at the smooth black of it, and heard my own heartbeat. "You give yourself, and it opens. That's the toll."

"Yes," Sefa said slowly. "That is exactly what it means, and I want you to say aloud, right now, that you are not planning to do the stupid thing."

"Sefa—"

"Gideon Almeida. Say it."

Wren looked between us with the expression of a child watching two adults argue over who has to eat the burned bread.

"If I offer myself," I said, "the gate opens. We go in. I find their souls—Mother's, Corvin's, Wren's. And then—" I faltered, because here was the part I had been not-thinking-about for six days, carrying it like a stone in my boot. "Then I would be theirs. But you two would be free. You could walk out."

"And do what?" Sefa's voice cracked like green wood. "Bury you? Tell your mother's freed soul what her bargain bought?" She stepped closer. "I have kept oaths my whole life. Broken ones too—I broke mine, that's why they exiled me, and I have regretted it every day since, and I will tell you the one thing I learned: *a sacrifice nobody agreed to is just a betrayal with better posture.*"

That silenced me. She had a way of doing that—of saying things so plainly they went straight through you like a needle through cloth.

"Wren," I said, turning, because I was grasping and she was the softer hand to grasp. "You remember being a fox. You know what it is to be caught in something—"

"Don't you dare use my trauma as rhetoric," Wren said, with the crisp authority of a much older person temporarily renting a small body. "Sefa's right. It's a terrible plan. It's the worst plan. It's the kind of plan a man makes when he thinks he deserves less than other people."

I opened my mouth.

"And before you say it," she went on, "yes, maybe you do think that. Maybe you've thought it for ten years, sitting in that valley carving bones for everyone else's grief because you weren't allowed any of your own. But we didn't walk all this way"—she counted on her fingers—"through the tithe-tunnels, past the village of the extremely polite cannibals, up a mountain, in winter, *in these boots*"—she held up a foot; the boot was held together mostly by hope and Sefa's stitching—"to watch you hand yourself over like a coat check."

I laughed. I couldn't help it. It came out ragged and surprised, and Sefa glared at both of us, and Wren grinned her gap-toothed fox grin, and for one moment the weight of the counting thing above us seemed, very slightly, confused.

Then Wren's grin faded, and she tilted her head—that fox tilt, ear toward a sound no one else could hear—and said:

"Wait."

She walked past Sefa's reaching hand, past the warning grip I tried to make on the air itself, right up to the table. She stood on her toes and peered into the clay cup. Then she turned around, eyes enormous.

"It's empty," she said. "But it smells like honey. Like something sweet *used* to be in it." She looked at me. "Gideon. What if the cup isn't for putting a soul *in*. What if it's for pouring one *out*?"

* * *

It took us half the night, huddled behind the frozen falls with the map spread between us, to understand what Wren had tasted in the air of that empty cup. It was Corvin who gave us the key, in the end—or his bones did. I pressed my palms flat against the map and let myself listen the way I hadn't dared listen since crossing the valley's edge, and his last hours came up through the grain like water through chalk.

He had come to this gate twice. The first time, he had been dragged, already sold, already hollow, and the door had opened for him the way a throat opens—because he belonged to them. But before that, before the selling, he had stood here with his soul still his own, and he had scratched his mark on the map, and he had *planned*. He had learned the gate's grammar the way you learn the grammar of any hungry thing: by watching what it ate.

Souls willingly offered—it opened for those. Souls already owned—it opened for those. But there was a third meal, rarer, and Corvin had died trying to serve it: a soul freely given *back*. A debt returned. A captive released by choice, not by force. The court's whole architecture was built on taking, and taking was all it understood—except that somewhere in its oldest ledgers, older than the Queen herself perhaps, there was a rule written by whoever wrote rules before her: *what is restored outweighs what is owed.*

"They can't refuse it," Sefa breathed. "If we bring them a soul given back freely, the gate has to open. It's not a loophole, it's a *law*, an old one, older than their cleverness—"

"So whose soul do we give back?" I asked. "We don't have any souls to return. That's rather the entire problem."

Silence. Then Wren, very quietly: "Corvin's."

We all looked at the map.

"He's dead," Sefa said. "His soul's already in there. On their shelf. In their lantern rack, or wherever they keep—"

"But it's *labeled*," Wren said. "Everything in there is labeled with its price. His price was paid—he sold it, they own it fair and square. But what if we walk up to that door carrying him—" she tapped the map, "—carrying his bones, his name, his *last wish*, which was to undo what he did—and we offer to give him back? Not steal him. Give him back. To himself. Freely. Doesn't that count?"

I felt it before I understood it—the map warming under my fingers, that held-breath silence breaking at last into something almost like singing. Corvin's voice, thin and frayed, repeating the only thing he had ever died holding: *let me take it back, let me take it back, let me take it back.*

"He never got to try," I said. "They took him before he could. But his wish is still in the bone. Wishes don't expire. I should know—I've been keeping one alive for ten years."

Sefa stared at the map a long time. When she spoke, her voice was rough. "So we walk up to the gate of the Court of Counted Souls, and we tell them we're delivering a debt-payment, and the payment is *giving a soul away for free.*"

"When you say it like that it sounds insane," Wren said.

"How else would I say it?"

"Honestly? Same way."

I picked up the map and held it against my chest, and Corvin's warmth soaked through my shirt like a small stubborn sun. Somewhere above us, the counting-weight shifted, attentive now, curious. The thirteenth moon was four nights from full. My mother's voice hung in a lantern somewhere behind that black stone, and I had promised her grave, and her ghost, and my own stubborn heart, that I would carry it home.

"All right," I said. "We knock."

We rose together and walked to the table. I set the map down upon the silvered oak, beside the empty cup, and laid my palm flat on Corvin's cold carvings, and said, clearly, in the voice I used for the grieving:

"We've brought a soul to give back."

For a long moment, nothing. The wind moved. The frozen falls glittered like a held breath.

Then the black door shivered—from top to bottom, like the surface of a struck bell—and swung inward without a sound, onto a stairway lit by candles that burned with no smoke at all, descending into a warm gold dark. And from far below, rising up the stairs like perfume from a kitchen, came a sound that stopped my heart and started it again in the same instant.

Someone was humming.

And beneath the humming, layered under it, faint as a lullaby through a wall—hundreds of voices, hanging in the dark, waiting to be counted.

One of them was my mother's.
