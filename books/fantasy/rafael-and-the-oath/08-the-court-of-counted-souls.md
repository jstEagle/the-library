## 8. The Court of Counted Souls

The door behind them closed with the sound of a very large book shutting, which was exactly what it was. Wren had watched it happen: the gate wasn't stone at all but a spine of bone taller than a cathedral, and when it closed, letters crawled across its surface like ants settling in for the night.

She decided not to think about that.

"Stay close," Sefa murmured. "Touch nothing. Agree to nothing."

"I'm going to touch everything," Wren whispered back, "and agree to nothing."

Gideon made a sound that might have been a laugh or might have been a prayer. He'd been making both since they'd come down through the tithe-tunnels, and honestly he looked terrible — pale as candle wax, his hands shaking around the bone map that had led them here. But his eyes were doing something new. Something fierce.

Wren liked it. It was a fox look. She knew fox looks from the inside.

They came out of the tunnel into the court itself, and Wren forgot to be clever for a whole minute.

Souls hung everywhere.

Not floating free like she'd imagined — she'd imagined them like dandelion seeds, like breath in winter — but hanging from hooks and chains and delicate black branches that grew out of the ceiling and had no business growing anywhere. Each one glowed. Each one was different. Some burned white and steady, some flickered like moths, one near the far wall pulsed a deep embarrassed red, as if it knew it had been caught doing something.

And under every single one, on a small brass plate, was a number.

"That's a price," Gideon said hoarsely. "Every soul has a price."

"It's a shop," Wren said. "It's the worst shop in the world."

"It's a ledger," said a voice like pages turning.

* * *

The Queen of Ledgers did not walk so much as arrive, the way winter arrives — gradually, and then all at once, and everywhere. She was tall in the way that made Wren's neck hurt, robed in something that wasn't cloth but seemed to be made of lists, and her face was beautiful the way an empty house is beautiful: perfect, and waiting for someone to fill it.

Her eyes found Gideon first. Then Sefa. Then, last, Wren — and stayed.

"You smell of forest," the Queen said.

"I *am* forest," Wren said. "Some of me. The best parts."

Something moved behind the Queen's empty-house face. Not a smile. The place where a smile would be built later, if she ever ordered the materials.

"A fox-child," the Queen said. "Debt of the Aldermere family, paid in part. You are item four hundred and eleven."

"I'm *Wren*."

"You are four hundred and eleven," the Queen repeated, gently, the way you correct a child who has miscounted. She turned away, and Wren felt it like a door closing, and hated it more than anything the court had done yet. Being a number was bad enough. Being a number nobody bothered to argue about was worse.

But then Gideon stepped forward, and put himself between Wren and the Queen, and said, "Then let's talk numbers."

* * *

He bargained badly, Wren thought later. Or maybe bravely, which sometimes looks the same from the outside.

"My mother," he said. "Maren Almeida. Her voice-soul. And Corvin Ashe — he died at my door. His whole soul, not just pieces. And the child's." He put his hand on Wren's head, briefly, warm and certain. "Three souls. I've come to take them home."

The Queen did not laugh. Wren got the feeling the Queen never laughed; it would have required equipment she hadn't installed.

"The Almeida debt is old," the Queen said, and somewhere above them a lantern flickered, and Gideon flinched as if the flicker had happened inside his chest. "A voice, freely sold, to purchase a hiding place for a son. Ten years of interest accrues nightly. The Ashe debt is young but compound. The child's—" she glanced down at Wren again, that door-closing glance— "is partial, and partial debts are the most tedious kind."

"But they can be paid," Sefa said. Her oath-keeper voice, the flat iron one. "That's what this court runs on. Debts can be settled. That's the whole point of you."

The Queen considered her the way a cat considers a closed door.

"They can," she agreed. "And here is my offer, carver, since you have come so far and your hands shake so beautifully. Three souls hang in my court tonight. My ledger will accept two. Choose which two leave with you, and which stays to balance what remains."

The silence afterward was enormous.

Wren watched Gideon's face do several things very quickly. She watched him look up — really look — at the lanterns, finding them, she supposed, because he could hear them even if they made no sound she could catch. Somewhere up there was his mother's voice. Somewhere up there was Corvin, who had walked into a valley to die at a stranger's door because a stranger was the only person left who could hear him beg.

And somewhere up there, or maybe right here being stared at by a queen, was Wren herself, item four hundred and eleven, half a soul, mostly girl, partly fox, entirely unwilling to be left.

"Don't choose," Wren said.

Gideon looked down at her.

"If you choose," she said, and her voice came out smaller than she wanted, so she made it bigger, "then whoever you leave — you'll hear them forever. Every night. In your bones. You're the man who hears the dead. You don't get to make dead people and then just *live with it*." She swallowed. "I don't want to be the thing you live with."

"Wren—"

"No. Don't choose. Be stubborn. It's your worst quality and I'm asking you to use it."

And Gideon Almeida, bone-carver, oath-breaker, man with shaking hands, did the most stubborn thing Wren had ever seen anyone do in her entire life, including everything she had done as a fox, and foxes are *very* stubborn about chickens.

* * *

"No," he said to the Queen.

The lanterns dimmed, all together, like the court holding its breath.

"The ledger accepts two," the Queen said. "That is generous. Generosity is not a thing I am known for, and I would hate to ruin my reputation before breakfast."

"Then keep your reputation and keep your souls," Gideon said. "I'm not choosing. I didn't come down here to do arithmetic. I came to bring all three home, and I will, and if you want to know how — watch my hands."

Wren didn't understand. Neither, clearly, did the Queen, whose empty-house face developed its first crack: a small frown, like a hairline fracture in fine porcelain.

"Watch your hands?"

"I'm a bone-carver," Gideon said. "It's what I *am*. And in ten years in a valley where nothing magical can follow, I learned something the mountain never taught you." He knelt, slowly, and drew from his pack the last piece of Corvin — the small splinter of wrist-bone the man had pressed into his hand along with the map, the piece Corvin had asked him to keep, *so somebody remembers I was a person.* "You count souls. You price them. You hang them on hooks and write numbers under them. But no one — I'd bet my other hand on this — no one has ever shown you what a soul looks like from the outside. What it looks like to someone who loved it."

The Queen went very still.

"Carve," she said at last, and the word came out hungry.

* * *

So he carved.

Wren sat cross-legged on the cold floor and watched, and it was better than any story she'd ever stolen out of a peddler's coat pocket. Gideon worked the way water works — no wasted motion, everything going where it must — and the little splinter of Corvin's bone turned under his knife into a face. Not a grand face. A tired one. A man with a crooked nose and laugh lines he'd probably earned dishonestly, and eyes that even in bone managed to look like they were about to ask you a favor.

"That's him?" the Queen asked. She had drifted closer. Queens should not drift; it undignified them, and she either didn't know or didn't care.

"That's him," Gideon said. "Corvin Ashe. Who sold his soul to save his daughter, and died apologizing to a stranger for the trouble." He turned the carving over. On the back, in tiny sure strokes, he'd cut a single line: *He was afraid, and he came anyway.*

The Queen reached out one long finger and touched the bone.

Every lantern in the court flickered at once.

"Oh," the Queen said quietly. It was such a small word for such a large creature. "Oh, that is — that is *wasteful*. All these years. All these souls, and I only ever counted them." Her finger traced the laugh lines. "You can see the whole of a person in this. The counting tells me what a soul weighs. This tells me what it *cost*."

"Now you understand why I don't choose," Gideon said softly. "Because I've held every one of them. Their weight isn't in the ledger. It's in the hands."

Wren's chest did something complicated. She looked at Sefa and found the oath-keeper staring at Gideon with an expression Wren had never seen on her before — something soft and ruined, like a woman watching a door she'd nailed shut swing open on its own.

The Queen straightened. For a moment Wren thought — hoped — that the crack in the porcelain face might spread, that the whole empty house might finally get furniture.

But queens are queens, even underground, even made of lists.

"A lovely trick, carver," the Queen said, and though her voice was smooth again, her eyes kept returning to the little bone face, again and again, like a tongue returning to a sore tooth. "Art does not settle debts. The moon turns whether you carve or weep or beg. Four hours remain, perhaps less. When it completes, the ledger comes due, and due it must be paid — all accounts, all at once, in full."

"Then I'll pay," Gideon said. "All three. Watch my hands."

He picked up his knife again. But this time he wasn't looking at the bone.

He was looking up — at the lanterns, at the thousands of small burning prices, at whatever he alone in the room could hear singing — and Wren saw him take a slow breath, the way you breathe before jumping off something high.

"Gideon," Sefa said sharply. "What are you doing?"

"Settling accounts," he said.

And above them, every soul in the court began, very faintly, to sing.
