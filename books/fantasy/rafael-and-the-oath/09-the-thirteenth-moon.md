## 9. The Thirteenth Moon

The candles in the Court of Counted Souls did not flicker. That was the first thing Gideon had noticed, days—or what felt like days—ago, and it was the thing he returned to now as the thirteenth moon reached its fullness somewhere far above the mountain's roots. Candles that did not flicker were candles that had forgotten what wind was. Everything down here was like that: the Queen of Ledgers, her courtiers with their ink-stained fingers, the great hall where souls hung from hooks of black iron like lanterns at a festival no one danced at. Nothing moved unless it was owed.

And tonight, everything was owed.

Gideon stood before the Ledger itself—a book the size of a coffin, its pages turning of their own accord, each page whispering a name. Beside him, Sefa had gone very still, the way she went still when she was calculating odds she didn't like. Wren pressed against his leg, small and warm and smelling faintly of pine needles even here, even underground, as if the forest refused to be fully taken from her.

"Read them," the Queen said.

She sat upon a throne carved from a single vertebra of something enormous and long dead, and her voice was not cruel. That was the horror of it. It was patient, the way winter is patient, the way a ledger is patient. She wore robes the color of candle smoke, and her crown was a ring of tiny bells that never rang.

Sefa stepped forward, because Sefa always stepped forward when stepping forward was the worst available option. "Three debts," she said, and her oath-keeper's voice filled the hall like a struck bell. "Corvin Ashe, who sold his soul for passage he never used. The woman Almeida—Maren Almeida—who sold her voice to buy her son's invisibility." She paused, and Gideon heard the pause cost her something. "And the child. Wren, traded by her parents for the forgiveness of debt."

"Three souls," the Queen agreed. "Three prices, paid or unpaid. The moon turns tonight. Unpaid debts are collected." Her eyes, pale as tallow, found Gideon. "You have stood in my court for three days, bone-carver, carving trinkets, stalling. Did you think I hadn't noticed? I notice everything. It is my only virtue."

"You have others," Wren said suddenly, in the fierce whisper she probably thought was quiet. "You liked the portrait. You kept touching it."

The courtiers stirred. The Queen's hand drifted, almost involuntarily, toward the small bone portrait Gideon had made her—the Queen rendered not as a collector but as a woman looking out a window at a sky she could not reach. He had seen her touch it four times when she thought no one watched. He had been counting on it.

"I liked the *craft*," the Queen said coldly.

"Of course," Gideon said. "That's why you keep looking at it."

Her gaze snapped back to him, and for one moment—one dangerous, human moment—something moved behind the tallow-pale eyes. Not warmth. Recognition, maybe. The look of someone who had been shown a mirror after centuries of counting.

"The moon," she said, recovering, "is nearly turned."

Gideon stepped forward.

He had spent ten years in a valley that numbed everything—the magic, the grief, the dead murmuring through every bone he touched. He had thought the numbness was his prison. Then he had learned it was his mother's purchase, bought with her voice, her soul hanging above him right now in this very hall, third lantern from the eastern wall, glowing a soft amber that he somehow knew was hers the way you know your own name. And he had spent the whole journey here rehearsing this moment in a hundred different ways, and in none of those rehearsals had his hands been steady.

They were steady now. That surprised him most of all.

"Then let me offer a payment," he said.

"There is nothing you have that covers three souls."

"There is one thing." He lifted his hands, the carver's hands, scarred and steady. "I hear the dead. Every bone I've ever touched has spoken to me. Their voices, their last thoughts, their unfinished sentences—I have carried them since I was a child. My mother sold her voice to the court. But she never told you what her son had instead, did she? Because if she had, you would have priced it."

The hall went silent in the way only the dead can make a hall silent—even the ledger's pages stopped turning.

The Queen rose slowly from her throne. "A gift of hearing," she said softly. "The silence of the living world, given over entirely to the dead."

"Not given over," Gideon said. "*Traded.* You take it, and I go deaf to them forever. No more voices in the bones. No more listening. Three souls erased from your ledger—Corvin's, Wren's, and hers—" he pointed upward, at the amber lantern, and his voice cracked on the word *hers*, and he let it crack, because some things should not be carved smooth— "and in exchange you take the one thing I have that is truly mine."

"Gideon, no." Sefa's hand closed on his arm. Her grip was iron; her voice was not. "There are other ways. There's always another way—that's the whole point of oaths, that's what I *was*—"

"This is the other way." He looked at her. "You exposed a corrupt vow because you believed vows should serve people, not the other way around. So tell me, oath-keeper: what does this vow serve?"

Sefa's mouth opened. Closed. Her hand fell away.

Wren tugged his sleeve. She was crying without any of the noise children usually permit themselves, tears running silently down a face that still sometimes, in certain light, showed the ghost of whiskers. "Will it hurt?" she whispered.

"Yes," Gideon said honestly. "But so does losing you. And I've done enough of the second kind."

He turned back to the Queen. "Well? The moon is turning."

The Queen descended from her throne. Up close she smelled of ink and cold stone, and her footsteps made no sound at all, which meant that even here, even she was something owed to the mountain. She stopped before him and studied his face the way she must once have studied faces before she became only a counter of souls.

"Do you understand what you offer?" she asked. "Not the price. The *thing* itself. To hear the dead is to never be alone. It is why carvers weep at their work and smile at funerals. You will lose all of it. The valley will feel to you what it feels to everyone else—like an ending."

Gideon thought of his mother's deathbed, ten years ago, her hand on his cheek, her lips shaping the words *never leave* with a voice already half-spent. He had thought she was afraid for him. She had been afraid *of* this—afraid he'd follow her someday into the dark, following her voice.

"I understand," he said. "Better than you think. I've been alone in a crowd of voices my whole life. I'd like to try being accompanied."

Something happened to the Queen's face then. It was very small, and it might have been the candlelight, but Gideon chose to believe it was the memory of being a person. She extended her hand, palm up, and above it the air began to shimmer like heat over a forge.

"The ledger accepts," she said. "All three debts. Erased."

The amber lantern flared.

It happened fast, faster than breath. Gideon felt the hearing leave him—not torn away but *poured out*, like water finding its level, like a held note finally released. Ten years of whispers, of murmurs, of the dead leaning close to say their unfinished things—all of it draining out through his ears into the Queen's open palm, and with it, strangely, the last echo of his mother's voice saying his childhood name, the one Corvin had died whispering, the one no one alive had known until a stranger stumbled bleeding into his workshop and changed everything.

Then silence.

Not emptiness. Silence—the kind that lives inside music between notes, the kind that means someone has finished speaking and is waiting to see what you'll do next.

He swayed. Sefa caught him. Wren wrapped both arms around his waist and held on with the desperate strength of a child who had once been a fox and remembered how to hold on to things that mattered.

Above them, three lanterns unhooked themselves from the black iron.

Corvin's soul came down first—a gray-blue light that circled Gideon's head once, twice, in something that felt embarrassingly like gratitude, and then shot upward through the mountain's stone as though stone were a rumor. Wren's soul was different: it dove straight into her chest, and she gasped, and her eyes went wide and gold for one impossible heartbeat, and when she blinked she was only a girl again—but a girl who grinned with slightly too many teeth and said, "Oh. Oh, I remember *running*."

And his mother—

His mother's soul descended slowly, the way she had done everything in life, carefully, thoroughly, leaving nothing unfinished. It hovered before Gideon's face. He could not hear her. For the first time in his life, standing in the court of the dead, he could not hear the dead, and the loss hit him like a physical blow, and he raised his hand anyway, cupping the amber light the way he had once cupped her face.

She pressed against his palm. Warm. Once. Wordless.

Then she rose, and the darkness took her gently, and she was gone—not collected, not counted. *Free.*

Gideon stood in the silence and wept, openly, in front of a queen and a court of ink-fingered accountants, and discovered that no one laughed, and that the Queen herself had turned away, one hand resting on the little bone portrait, looking out its carved window at the sky she could not reach.

"Go," she said quietly. "Before I remember my virtues are only the one."

They went. Through the great doors, up the tithe-tunnels, past the gate that had opened for a soul freely given back and now stood ajar as if unsure whether to be a door or a wound. Behind them the ledger's pages resumed their whispering, but none of the names they passed were theirs.

They climbed out beneath the thirteenth moon, full and white and turning complete, and Gideon lay back on the cold grass of the mountainside and listened.

Wind. Sefa breathing hard beside him. Wren's heartbeat, quick as a bird's, against his shoulder. Somewhere downslope, a nightjar calling its two-note question into the dark. The world, entire and alive and unbearably loud with living.

He heard every bit of it.

"Well?" Sefa said at last. "Was it worth it?"

Gideon considered the question seriously, the way he considered everything, and then he laughed—a rough, rusty sound, because he couldn't remember the last time he'd laughed at something only the living had said.

"She's free," he said. "They're all free. And I can't hear a single dead person complaining about it."

Wren giggled. Sefa shook her head, but she was smiling, that reluctant crooked smile she rationed like bread in a famine.

They had a long road home. A valley to return to, a workshop to reopen, memorials to carve for the grieving that Gideon would make with his hands and his heart and never again with his ears. But that was tomorrow's work.

Tonight there was only the moon, and the grass, and the two heartbeats beside his—and Gideon Almeida, deaf to the dead at last, listened to the living, and found it was enough. More than enough.

It was, he realized, the first silence of his life that felt like a beginning.

Far below them, deep in the mountain, a bell in a queen's crown rang—once, softly, for the first time in a thousand years—and no one was there to hear it but her.
