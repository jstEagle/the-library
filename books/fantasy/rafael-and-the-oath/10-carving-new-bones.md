## 10. Carving New Bones

The valley had not changed, which was the cruelest and kindest thing about it.

Sefa felt the change the moment they crossed the ridge—the way Gideon went still on the path ahead of her, the way his shoulders dropped like a man setting down a load he'd carried so long he'd forgotten it was there. Wren, walking between them with a stick she'd named Sir Bramblewood, looked up at him.

"Does it hurt?" she asked. "Coming back?"

"No," Gideon said. He turned his face to the valley as if seeing it for the first time—which, Sefa supposed, he was. "That's the strange part. It used to be quiet here. Now it's just... quiet."

"Same thing," said Wren.

"Not even close," said Gideon, and smiled, and Sefa's chest did something complicated and unwelcome that she decided to ignore for at least another week.

She knew valleys like this one. Oath-keepers were taught about them in training—places where bargains pooled and thickened, where magic drained away like water into sand. They'd been taught to mark them on maps with a small grey circle and move along. No one had ever told her what it was like to live inside one, deaf by design, safe by subtraction. No one had told her that a mother could love her son so much she'd hollow herself out to keep him hidden.

Sefa had spent ten years believing oath-keeping meant holding people to their words. She'd exposed a corrupt vow—a magistrate who'd sworn protection to a village while selling its harvest-tithes—and the order had stripped her rank for embarrassing them rather than praising her for being right. Rightness, she'd learned, was not the same as goodness. Sometimes it wasn't even in the same village.

But watching Gideon Almeida walk down into the place that had been both his cage and his shield, carrying nothing but a satchel of carving tools and two freed souls' worth of gratitude, Sefa thought maybe she was finally learning the difference.

* * *

The workshop smelled of dust and bone-meal and ten years of absence.

Gideon stood in the doorway for a long moment before entering, and Sefa pretended to be very interested in the eaves so he could have his moment without an audience. Wren had no such manners. She marched straight past him, announced "I claim this chair," claimed it, and immediately fell asleep in it, Sir Bramblewood clutched to her chest like a sword surrendered after a long war.

"She snores," Sefa observed.

"She purrs," Gideon said. "Sometimes. When she forgets not to."

He moved through the workshop touching things—his father's old bench, the racks of finished carvings, a jar of river-smoothed pebbles he apparently collected for reasons he didn't explain. His hands were steady. That was new. In the mountain, when he'd offered up his silence, his hands had shaken so badly Sefa had been sure the Queen of Ledgers would refuse the payment as damaged goods. But the Queen had weighed his silence in her candlelit scales and found it sufficient—more than sufficient, since silence freely given outweighed silence taken, and Gideon's had been offered with all three debts named aloud.

Three souls had flown out of that court like startled birds. Sefa had watched Corvin's soul go first—it had paused beside Gideon just long enough to press something like gratitude against the air, and then it was gone, unburdened, unpaying, free. And somewhere in the dark beneath the thirteenth moon, a voice that had been sold away ten years ago had found its way back to its owner.

"You're going to reopen it, then," Sefa said. It wasn't quite a question.

"The workshop?" Gideon set a chisel down in its old groove, where the wood had worn a hollow shaped exactly like the tool. "I don't know how to do anything else. I carve bones for the grieving. That's—" He stopped. Started again. "It used to be that I carved, and then I listened, and then I could tell the mourners what their dead wanted said. The listening was the gift. The carving was just... the shape of it."

"And now?"

"Now the carving is the whole of it." He picked up a half-finished piece from the rack—an old commission, a sparrow, wings folded, made for some mourner who'd never come back to collect it. "They'll bring me their dead, and I won't hear anything. Just bone, and my hands, and whatever the grieving tell me themselves. And somehow that feels more honest."

Sefa leaned against the doorframe. "You'll be a fraud, then. A bone-carver with no second sight."

"I'll be a craftsman," Gideon said. "There's a difference, and I intend to spend the rest of my life learning it."

She believed him. That was the strange thing. Sefa had spent her career detecting lies in oaths—in the tremor of a voice, the hedge-word tucked inside a promise—and Gideon's words rang clean as struck bronze.

"There will be gossip," she warned. "The elders marked you. They know you left."

"They know I left and came back." Gideon shrugged. "Let them gossip. I've faced a queen of ledgers. Elder Hessa's disapproval is going to have to wait its turn."

Despite herself, Sefa laughed. It came out rusty, like a gate opening after winter.

* * *

The first customer came three days later: a widow named Marta whose husband had drowned in the mill-race, bringing a knucklebone wrapped in linen because that was all the river had returned to her.

Sefa watched from the corner, ostensibly sweeping, actually eavesdropping shamelessly.

"I heard you could speak with the dead," Marta said, wringing the linen. "Before. Everyone said."

"I can't anymore," Gideon said simply. "I gave that away."

Marta's face fell. "Then what good is—"

"Tell me about him," Gideon said, and pulled his stool up to the bench, and began to work.

And oh, it was something to watch. Sefa had seen oath-craft performed by masters, had seen the old rites done properly in the high houses, but she had never seen anything like this: a man listening—not to ghosts, but to a widow. Marta talked for two hours. About how Harl hummed when he milled, off-key always; about how he'd proposed by falling into a creek; about the argument they'd never finished, the night before the river took him, over whether to plant barley or oats. Gideon carved while she talked, asking small questions—did he hold his cup like this? was his laugh more cough than sound?—and slowly, slowly, the sparrow became a different bird entirely: a wren-shaped thing with a crooked wing, because Harl, Marta said through tears and laughter both, had broken that wing climbing a tree at seven years old and it had never healed right.

When she left, clutching the little crooked-winged bird to her chest, she paused in the doorway.

"He would have liked you," she said. "He talked too much, too."

After the door closed, Wren emerged from behind the flour sacks, where she had absolutely been hiding instead of doing the chores she'd been assigned.

"That's better than the ghost-words," she declared. "Ghost-words were always sad. That lady laughed four times."

"You counted?"

"I count everything," Wren said, with the gravity of a general reviewing troops. "It's a fox thing."

"You were a fox for eleven months," Sefa said.

"It leaves marks." Wren held up her chin, daring contradiction, and got none.

* * *

On the evening of the ninth day, the light in the workshop went gold and slow, and Gideon went quiet in a way Sefa recognized from the mountain—from the moment before the ledger came due, when every soul in that candlelit hall had seemed to hold its breath.

She looked up from the accounts she was keeping (someone had to; Gideon charged what mourners could pay and forgot to charge at all otherwise, and Wren's economic philosophy was "finders keepers"). He was standing at the window, utterly still.

"Gideon?"

"She's here," he said. His voice cracked down the middle. "My mother."

Sefa set down the ledger. She couldn't hear anything—the freed voice-soul made no sound she could catch—but the room had changed temperature, or pressure, or something older than either. The air felt the way a house feels when someone who loved it has come home.

Wren woke in her chair without seeming to wake at all. Her eyes opened, amber-bright. "She smells like bread," the child whispered. "Warm bread. And lavender. And—" her brow furrowed, listening to something no one else could hear— "and she says... no. She doesn't say anything." Wren blinked, puzzled. "She doesn't need to."

Gideon raised one hand, palm open, the way you reach toward a fire you're afraid will go out.

Sefa had heard the story by then—all of it, told in pieces across the road and around fires: how Ilse Almeida had gone beneath the mountain with her own voice in her teeth and traded it away, silence for silence, so that her son would be too small a thing for the court to notice. How she'd bound him with a deathbed oath to keep him inside the numbness, inside the safety, never telling him why. How she had paid for ten years with the very voice Gideon now realized he'd inherited—the hearing of the dead, the family gift, passed down and purchased back at a price no ledger should have allowed.

Love sworn in fear, the Queen had called it. Love chosen freely, the Queen had conceded, outweighs it.

Now the fear was spent, and the bargain broken, and the voice had come home only to say goodbye.

Gideon wept without noise, which seemed fitting, and Sefa stood very still by the door and let him, and Wren climbed down from her chair and pressed herself against his side, and the warm-gold-lavender presence moved through the workshop like sunlight through water. It touched the bench. It touched the jar of river pebbles. It touched, briefly and gently, the tops of all three of their heads—Sefa included, though she had no right to it, and felt the touch land anyway, wordless and warm, an acknowledgment that needed no language because it was made of the thing underneath all language.

Then it thinned. Brightened. Went.

Gideon stood in the fading gold light with his hand still raised, and lowered it slowly, and when he turned around his face was wet and calm.

"She stayed longer than she owed," he said. "That was the whole point, I think. Staying longer than you owe."

* * *

Winter came early that year, and the workshop filled up.

Word traveled, as word does: the bone-carver who no longer hears the dead but listens anyway; the disgraced oath-keeper who keeps the accounts and, when travelers try to shortchange the shop, holds them to their word with terrifying precision; the child who was a fox and counts everything, including the number of customers who cry (all of them) and the number who come back (most).

Elder Hessa came in the first snow, stiff with disapproval, prepared to deliver a formal censure for the breaking of a deathbed oath. She left two hours later having commissioned a memorial for her sister and, according to Wren's careful tally, having cried twice, though she denied it at the door.

Gideon taught Wren to sand bone on evenings when her hands were restless and her dreams ran on four legs. He taught Sefa the names of the tools, and she pretended not to care and then caught herself sharpening the small chisels at midnight because dull tools offended her now. The map of bone—Corvin's map, the one that wouldn't burn—hung above the bench, its roads faded to faint silver lines, pointing nowhere anymore. Or perhaps, Gideon said, pointing everywhere. Maps did that, once you'd been where they led.

One night in deep winter, the three of them sat around the stove while a memorial-dove dried on the bench—a piece for a shepherd, commissioned by his flock, which Wren had insisted on counting personally. The wind worried the eaves. Wren was asleep, purring faintly. Sefa was pretending to read.

"Gideon," she said. "Why did you offer your silence? Truly. Not the noble version."

He was quiet a while, turning his cup in his hands.

"In the court," he said finally, "when the Queen showed me the three lanterns—my mother, Corvin, Wren—I understood that I'd been keeping my hearing the same way my mother kept her oath. Holding onto something out of fear of what losing it would mean." He looked into the fire. "She silenced herself to protect me. I was silent already and called it a life. Offering it up was the first thing I ever did that was mine—chosen, not owed. And it bought back everyone I—" He stopped. Started over, plainer. "It bought back my family."

"We're not your family," Sefa said. "We've known each other two seasons."

"Yes," Gideon agreed comfortably. "Family's like that. It happens faster than you'd think, and slower than you'd want."

Sefa opened her mouth to argue—she was, professionally, excellent at arguing—and found she had nothing. Outside, the snow came down soft as bone-meal, and the valley lay quiet around them, and for the first time in ten years the quiet was just weather.

In the morning there would be commissions, and accounts, and Elder Hessa's suspiciously generous payment, and Wren's ongoing campaign to have Sir Bramblewood listed as an apprentice. But that night, Sefa sat in the warm gold light of a workshop full of honored dead and living love, and thought that if this was what failing at oaths led to, the order had exiled her straight into the best vow she'd ever keep:

the kind no one made her take,

and no one could take away.
