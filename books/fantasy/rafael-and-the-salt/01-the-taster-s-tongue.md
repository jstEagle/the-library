## 1. The Taster's Tongue

The poison in the pheasant tasted of almonds and wintergreen, which meant foxglove steeped too long, which meant an amateur. Yara Eriksen let the morsel sit on her tongue for the count of five—long enough to be convincing, short enough to keep the dose manageable—and then she swallowed it with a small, appreciative nod, as though the chef had done something clever with the glaze.

"Her Majesty will want to know it's good," said Lord Aldous Vane, standing far too close behind her chair. His breath smelled of candied ginger and older sins. "You're certain?"

"I'm certain I'm still breathing," Yara said.

He laughed. Everyone at the king's table laughed when she said that; it was a joke worn smooth as a river stone from ten years of handling. She kept her face arranged into its usual expression—the mild, faintly bored look of a woman whose entire profession was dying slowly and not making a fuss about it—and beneath the table, where no one watched, she pressed her thumbnail into the meat of her palm until the pain gave her something clean to hold.

Foxglove. A gentle poison, if you could call anything gentle that stopped a heart. The kind chosen by someone who wanted deniability. And Lord Vane had asked for the pheasant to be served to Duchess Imre's side of the table first. Yara filed this away with all the other things she carried: the Count of Serevain who salted his rival's wine with antimony every third feast, the Lady Marchetti whose perfumes had a bitterness under the roses that Yara's tongue catalogued even when her nose missed it, the bishop who never ate at court at all because he was afraid of everyone and right to be.

She was the king's taster. Officially, her duty was to die instead of him, should it come to that. Unofficially, she was the most complete record of murder in the kingdom—a living ledger of who hated whom, and by what means, and how badly they cooked.

The dish passed inspection. Servants bore the pheasant down the length of the table to where the old queen consort sat with her ladies, and Yara allowed herself three slow breaths while the foxglove began its quiet argument with her pulse.

It would take hold within the hour. It always did. Her heart would stumble like a dancer missing a step, recover, miss another. By midnight she'd be pale and clammy and counting beats in the dark, and by morning she would drink the bitter green draught she brewed herself—measured against tonight's dose, adjusted for the bread she'd eaten, the wine she hadn't—and by midday she would be functional again. This had been the arithmetic of her life for so long that she no longer thought of it as survival. It was simply maintenance. The way one maintained a roof, or a knife, or a lie.

"Yara." The steward's voice, low, at her shoulder. "His Majesty requests you taste before he sits."

Of course he did. The king had grown cautious in his middle years—or rather, someone had grown cautious on his behalf, because the man himself had lately seemed less like a cautious creature than an absent one. Yara rose, smoothed her grey servant's dress, and walked the familiar distance down the table, past candle flames that bent as she passed, past faces she knew better than their own mothers knew them: which ones slept badly, which ones wept after supper, which ones were lying about everything.

The king stood at the head of the room, talking to a man Yara didn't recognize, and that itself was strange enough to snag somewhere behind her ribs. New faces at court were rare. Rarer still was the way the stranger stood—not bowing quite deeply enough, not smiling quite correctly, like a portrait of courtliness painted by someone who had only heard it described.

"Your Grace," Yara murmured, and knelt to the tasting stool set beside the king's place.

The soup was venison and dark ale, heavy with pepper. She took her spoonful, held it, swallowed. Beneath the pepper—clever, hiding everything under pepper, the coward's camouflage—she caught something else. Not poison. Not exactly. A taste she had no name for, flat and grey, like drinking the memory of water. It slid down her throat and left nothing behind, no warmth, no weight, as if her mouth had briefly forgotten how mouths worked.

She frowned at the bowl.

"Well?" the king said. His voice was pleasant. His voice was always pleasant. But when she looked up at him, his eyes were fixed slightly to the left of her face, focused on some point just behind her shoulder, and for a moment—an unaccountable moment—she had the impression that whatever was looking out through the King of Cael Harad's face did not entirely understand what a spoon was.

"It's good, Your Majesty," she heard herself say. "Rich. Perhaps a touch heavy on the pepper."

"Heavy on the pepper," the king repeated, in the tone of a man learning the phrase. Then he smiled, warm and kingly, and the strangeness was gone—or folded away somewhere, or eaten—and he clapped the unfamiliar stranger on the shoulder and said something about the harvest, and the room resumed being a room.

Yara finished the rest of her duties mechanically. Soup, bread, the honeyed figs the king favored, the small sweet wine from the southern coast. Nothing else tasted wrong. Nothing else tasted like nothing. She told herself the earlier moment had been fatigue—she had slept badly, she always slept badly after dosing nights—and told herself it convincingly, the way she convinced herself of many things, and went home through streets slicked with evening rain while the foxglove played its slow percussion against her ribs.

* * *

Her lodging was a narrow house above a chandler's shop in the Saltrow, chosen for reasons no one asked about: tasters were expected to live modestly, and modesty was excellent cover for a woman who came and went at odd hours carrying locked boxes.

She lit no lamp. She knew the stairs by heart, eleven steps, the fourth one that groaned, the landing where the chandler's stock perfumed the wood with beeswax and smoke. In her back room, she moved the wardrobe aside—it weighed almost nothing to her anymore; she had long since stopped noticing how strong the poisons had made her daily labor—and knelt at the floorboards.

The trapdoor opened on cold air and the smell of paper, ink, and brine.

The cellar archive ran farther than her house did, deeper than her street's foundations, carved into the old city stone that predated every map anyone sold. Shelves descended into darkness in ranked rows, and on those shelves, wrapped in oilcloth and wax-sealed, stacked in crates marked with the guild's sigil—a circle bisected by a single horizontal line, like a horizon seen through a keyhole—sat the inventory of the Salt Guild.

Impossible things. Bottled grief, stoppered in grey glass that wept condensation even in dry weather. Borrowed names, folded into letters that rustled when no draft moved. Years of life, measured out in grains and vials and, in one notorious case, an entire decade decanted into a cask like wine. First loves, last words, the sound a dead woman's mother made when she learned the news—all of it traded, priced, shelved, catalogued.

And every crate, every vial, every sealed and secret thing passed through Yara's hands.

She was the ledger-keeper. Had been for nine years now, since old Master Quill's hands shook too badly to write and Corvane had looked at the guild's youngest immune asset with those flat grey eyes and said, *You have a careful tongue, Eriksen. Let's see if you have a careful hand.*

By day she tasted death for the crown. By night she recorded the trades of men and women who bought and sold the intangible. She wrote the entries in her own cipher, cross-referenced them in three separate indices, and kept the master ledger chained to her wrist when she worked—which was why, descending now into the dark with the lamp finally lit, she felt the familiar small tug at her arm, the chain's soft greeting, like a dog acknowledging its owner.

There was a delivery waiting. There was always a delivery waiting.

Tonight it was a grief, standard grade, sold by a widow in the Hillmarket who had decided that mourning was more than she could afford to carry. Yara unpacked it with the neutral efficiency of long practice: grey glass bottle, thumb-sized, stoppered with black wax stamped with the guild's mark. When she lifted it, the bottle was cold and faintly wet, and inside it something shifted—not liquid, but the suggestion of liquid, the way a shadow suggests a body.

She held it up to the lamplight and, for a moment, allowed herself the luxury she permitted herself perhaps once a month: she imagined what was inside. A woman's whole sorrow for her husband, thirty years of shared mornings, distilled to two inches of grey glass. Somewhere a buyer waited—some lordling who had never loved anything and wished to know what it felt like, or some other mourner hoping a borrowed grief might fill the hole where their own had been cut out.

Yara entered it in the ledger. *One (1) grief, marital, 31 yrs duration. Condition: good. Seller: H., Hillmarket. Buyer: pending.* Her pen moved in the cipher that only she and Corvane could read, neat and small, each letter identical to its brothers. She had once believed her handwriting revealed nothing about her. Lately she suspected that was its own revelation.

She worked for two hours. Two more deliveries arrived through the chute from the shop above—a vial of borrowed courage, a lock of hair that remembered its owner's dreams—and she logged both, wrapped both, shelved both. The work steadied her the way it always did. Here, at least, the world made sense. Everything had a price. Everything had a place on a shelf. Even grief could be put in order, if your columns were straight and your hand was steady.

It was only afterward, sitting with the ledger closed on her knees and the lamp turned low, that the loneliness arrived, punctual as pain.

It always came at this hour. The court thought of her as furniture with a tongue. The guild thought of her as furniture with a hand. Corvane spoke to her in instructions and corrections, and the other immunes—the handful of orphans like her, scattered through the palaces and great houses of the realm—exchanged letters so coded and infrequent that they might have been writing to strangers. No one touched her except to press a cup into her hands. No one knew her name except as an entry in a book she herself kept chained to her wrist.

*Yara Eriksen,* the guild called her. But that wasn't true, not quite. The guild owned her name the way it owned the widow's grief—collateral, taken when she was seven years old and debt-ridden and alone, held in trust against her service. What she answered to was a working label. What she actually was, officially speaking, was an entry. Line forty-one, third column, the master registry. Property of the Salt Guild, term of indenture: indefinite.

She had never once, in twenty-three years, heard anyone say her name aloud.

The foxglove chose that moment to remind her of its presence. Her heart stumbled—caught—stumbled again, and she breathed through it, palm flat against her sternum, counting. One. Two. On the fifth beat it found its rhythm again, sullen and slow, and she reached for the flask of antidote in her coat and drank a measured swallow, wincing at the bitterness.

"You'll kill yourself doing this," she said aloud to the empty cellar, in the voice she used for jokes no one heard. "One of these days you'll miscount, and there'll be a lovely funeral, and nobody will come, and the guild will enter it in the book. *One (1) taster, expired, cause: arithmetic.*"

The shelves said nothing. The bottles gleamed. Somewhere in the dark, a grief she had shelved last winter wept its thin condensation onto the stone.

She climbed the stairs, replaced the wardrobe, washed her face, and lay down in her narrow bed to wait out the poison, staring at the ceiling beams as her pulse slowed and her skin went cold and clammy and her thoughts grew the particular glassy clarity that foxglove gave her—that terrible, honest clarity in which every wall she'd built around herself stood visible, brick by brick, and she could see all the way down to the small girl at the bottom of her, seven years old, holding out her hand to a stranger in a grey cloak and saying, *I don't have anything, but I'll owe you.*

Everyone owes someone, the stranger had said. We just make sure it's written down.

Sleep took her near dawn, dreamless, merciful. She woke to grey light and the chandler's boy hammering at the door, calling that she had a visitor, which never happened, which had never once happened in nine years.

"She won't say anything," the boy reported through the door, aggrieved. "Just stands there. Covered in mud. Won't say a word."

Yara dressed quickly, heart already quickening with something that had nothing to do with foxglove, and opened the door onto the grey morning—and onto a child.

A girl, perhaps six years old, barefoot despite the season, soaked to the waist in river mud. Her hair was pale as salt. She stood perfectly still in the doorway, looking up at Yara with an expression of terrible patience, like someone who has traveled very far to deliver a message and intends to deliver it.

In one small fist, mud-caked fingers clenched white around it, she held a coin. Yara knew coins. She had spent her life among currencies, official and otherwise, and she knew every mint mark in the kingdom.

This was no coin she had ever seen. It was white as bone, and stamped upon it was a circle bisected by a single line.

The guild's mark. But older. Wrong somehow, the way the stranger at court had been wrong, the way the king's eyes had been wrong. As if the sigil had existed long before the guild, and the guild had merely copied it without understanding what it was copying.

The girl opened her mouth and spoke.

The sounds meant nothing—no language Yara knew, no trade-tongue, no high speech, none of the dockside dialects she'd gathered over the years like dropped coins. But the moment they touched her ears, every scar on Yara's body ached at once: the faded burn on her forearm from the antimony year, the needle-marks along her veins, the old break in her little finger that had healed crooked. All of them flared together like instruments answering a tuning fork, and Yara gripped the doorframe and did not fall, and the child watched her fall-not-falling with those patient salt-pale eyes, and said it again, slower, the way one speaks to someone struggling to remember.

And the worst of it—the thing that made Yara's blood move strangely in her chest—was that some wordless part of her, some cellar-deep part below all the ledgers and the numbness and the careful columns, understood that the girl was not speaking gibberish.

She was speaking Yara's name.
