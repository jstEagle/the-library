## 2. Ledgers of Impossible Things

The archive beneath the Salt House smelled of cold stone and old paper and, faintly, of the sea—though the nearest coast was a hundred leagues away. Yara had never decided whether that smell was real or whether it was simply what memory was supposed to smell like, and after six years of keeping the ledgers she had stopped asking questions of that kind. Questions of that kind were how people disappeared.

She carried the lamp low, so its light pooled on the flagstones rather than climbing the shelves. The shelves went back farther than the building above them should have allowed. She had measured once, pacing heel-to-toe with a tallow stub in her fist, and counted forty-one paces where the Salt House's cellar could only have held twenty-two. She did not measure again. Some arithmetic you performed once and then had the good sense to leave alone.

Tonight's work sat waiting on the reading table: a delivery crate from the Vellum Road house, three days late, sealed with black wax stamped by a hand she didn't recognize. That was the first wrongness. Guild seals were like guild members—you learned them the way a shepherd learns his flock—and this one was new, or new-cut, or cut by someone who wanted it to look new. Yara turned the crate twice under the lamp, then set her thumb against the wax and broke it.

Inside, wrapped in oilcloth gone slick with age, were eleven items and their papers.

She began, as she always began, with the papers.

*Item one.* A stoppered vial the size of a sparrow's egg, glass so old it had gone violet at the shoulders. The accompanying slip was written in a clerk's careful hand two generations dead: *Grief, bottled. Source: widow of the ferryman Osk, taken at the moment of interment. Quantity: sufficient for one mourning, whole. Note: do not decant near open flame; grief is flammable when fresh.*

Yara held the vial up to the lamplight. Something inside moved—not liquid, nothing so honest as liquid. It shifted the way a shadow shifts when the candle gutters, leaning toward the flame and away from it at once. She had catalogued grief before. She knew better than to uncork it. The last keeper who had—there was no record of what happened to him, which was itself a kind of record, if you knew how to read absence.

She wrote in the master inventory, her pen moving in the small, exact script the guild required: *Grief (bottled), one mourning, provenance Osk/ferryman's widow. Condition: intact. Shelf: Violet Row, third niche, behind the salt-ward.* Her own handwriting looked strange to her tonight, thinner than usual. Court did that. Court thinned everything.

*Item two* made her sit down.

It was a plain wooden box, unremarkable except that it hummed—or seemed to hum, or seemed to want to hum, the way a struck bell wants to keep ringing after your palm has stilled it. The slip inside read: *One decade, stolen. Taken from a debtor of the Copper Street house, years eleven through twenty of his life, per contract signed in blood and ink. Contents: ten years, unspent. Buyer: undisclosed. Warning: box must remain closed. Opened, the decade will attempt to live itself.*

Yara set the box down on the far side of the table, away from her lamp, away from her hands, and found that she had stopped breathing somewhere between *stolen* and *unspent*. Ten years. Somewhere a man walked around with a hole in him shaped like his twenties, and he had signed for it, because debt made signers of us all. She knew about signing. There was a page in a ledger with her name on it—her guild-name, her collateral-name—and below that name a sum that had been paid on her behalf when she was nine years old and orphaned and worth exactly what someone had decided she was worth.

She did not think about that page. She thought about it every night, but she did not think about it now.

*Item three* was smaller than the others and worse. A locket, silver, tarnished green at the hinge. Inside, where a portrait ought to be, there was only a smudge of light, like breath on glass that refused to fade. The slip said: *First love, one (1), extracted voluntarily. Seller requests it not be returned. Seller requests, further, that no record be kept of whose it was. Request honored.*

Someone had loved once, wholly, the way you love the first time—badly, hugely, without armor—and had chosen to have it scooped out of them like a stone from a peach, and had asked that even the ledger forget. And the ledger had agreed. Yara stared at the little green-hinged thing for a long time. In all her years of cataloguing she had never seen an entry ask to be forgotten and get its wish. The guild forgot nothing. Forgetting was the one trade they didn't make.

Except apparently it was.

She wrote: *First love, extracted, anonymous per seller's request,* and then, after a moment, in the margin, in a shorthand only she used: *who?*

It was a small rebellion. It was almost nothing—a single word, in code, in a book only three living people could read. But her heart beat faster writing it, and she noticed that, and noticing it shamed her, and the shame felt strangely like being alive.

* * *

The rest of the crate went quicker, and stranger. A jar of last words, seventeen of them, still warm. A ribbon that had been tied around a dying man's wrist and now untied itself whenever anyone lied within ten feet of it. A child's tooth that dreamed aloud if you left it in the dark—Yara could hear it murmuring through its velvet pouch, tiny and contented, dreaming whatever teeth dream, and she put it on the shelf quickly because listening felt like theft.

By the third hour her neck ached and the lamp had burned down to a coin of oil, and Corvane came down the stairs the way he always did: without sound, announced only by the cold.

He was a long man, dry as a December branch, with a face that had been handsome before something had eaten most of it—not flesh, she'd always thought, but expression. What remained was patient and gray and very hard to lie to, which was inconvenient, because lying to Corvane was occasionally a professional requirement.

"You're behind," he said.

"The crate was behind. I'm on schedule." She didn't look up from the inventory. "New seal on the delivery. Black wax, unmarked die."

"I know."

"Whose?"

"Does it matter?"

"It matters that you know and I don't." She finished the line she was writing, blotted it, and then looked up, because looking up was also a kind of professional requirement—the taster's discipline applied to everything: meet the poison, meet the man, meet the moment, eyes open. "Eleven items. One of them asked to be forgotten and the books let it. That's not in any rite I've been taught."

Corvane crossed the room and stood over the table, reading her entries upside down, which he could do, which everyone in the guild above a certain rank could do, another fact she was not supposed to find strange. His finger stopped—not on the grief, not on the decade, but on her marginal word. *Who?*

He looked at her for a long moment. Outside, far above, a cart rattled over the cobbles of Saltmonger's Lane and faded, and the silence afterward was enormous.

"Yara," he said.

He almost never used her name. Names were currency here; spending them meant something.

"When you finish the inventory," he said, "you close the book. You climb the stairs. You eat something with butter in it, because you're thin as a liturgy and the court season is starting and I need my taster upright. And you do not—" his finger tapped the margin, once, gently, the way you'd tap a sleeping animal you didn't want to wake, "—read past your own entries."

"I wasn't reading. I was recording."

"You were asking." He straightened. "There's a difference, and the books know the difference, and the books are less forgiving than I am."

"They're books, Corvane."

"Yes," he said, and something moved behind his face, some old weather. "That's what everyone says, right up until they understand what that means."

He gathered the crate's wrappings, folded them into a square too neat for a man his size, and paused at the foot of the stairs with his back to her.

"Your entries end at the salt-line," he said. "Page ninety-one. Everything past page ninety-one is older than your employment, older than mine, older than the kingdom that pays your king. You're a good keeper. You're the best I've trained. Don't make me train another."

Then he was gone, and the cold went with him, and Yara sat alone in the enormous cellar with a guttering lamp and a rule she had just been handed like a knife handle-first.

* * *

She should have climbed the stairs. She should have eaten something with butter in it.

Instead she sat for a quarter of an hour doing sums she already knew the answers to, the way a gambler counts cards he isn't playing. Page ninety-one. Her entries ended there because her employment began there—six years ago, a girl of nineteen with steady hands and a tongue that had already survived four assassination attempts by accident of birth and poverty, brought down into this cellar and shown the books and told: *you will keep them, and they will keep you, and the line between those two things is the whole of your life.*

Her entries ran pages sixty-three through ninety-one. Twenty-eight pages of bottled things and borrowed names and debts recorded in blood-ink, all of it passing through her hands, none of it hers. Before page sixty-three: other keepers, going back and back. After page ninety-one—

After page ninety-one there was nothing she had ever been permitted to see.

The archive's oldest shelf stood at the far end of the impossible forty-one paces, past the Violet Row, past the niches where the salt-wards hung like dried fish, in a recess where the lamplight behaved badly—arriving late, leaving early, as though the dark there had negotiated different terms. On that shelf, behind a door of black iron with no lock (which was worse than a lock; locks admitted that doors could be opened), stood the First Ledger.

Every keeper learned its name before they learned their letters. No keeper in living memory had read it. It was not forbidden, precisely. Forbidden things had rules, and rules could be broken cleanly, with a clear conscience, in the dark. The First Ledger was simply *not discussed*, the way one does not discuss a parent's grief or a god's reasons.

Corvane's warning rang in her ears, and it was a good warning, and she honored it for nearly a minute.

Then she took the lamp and walked the forty-one paces, counting them aloud under her breath like a child crossing a churchyard, and stood before the black iron door, and did not open it—she wasn't ready to be that kind of rebel, not tonight, maybe not ever—but pressed her palm flat against the cold metal instead.

Through the iron, faint as a pulse in a wrist, she felt the First Ledger.

Not humming, like the stolen decade. Not warm, like the jar of last words. *Reading her.* She was certain of it—certain the way a taster is certain, that deep pre-verbal certainty in the tongue and the gut that says *this one, this bite, this is the one that kills.* Something on the other side of the door had registered her touch, had noted it, had perhaps—she would remember this later, in the bad hours, in the hour before dawn when the court's poisons sat in her belly like swallowed stones—had perhaps *turned a page.*

She snatched her hand back. The lamp flame stood perfectly still, unnaturally still, the way flames stand when something nearby is paying attention to them.

"Recording only," she whispered to the dark, to the door, to herself. "I was only recording."

The dark did not answer, which she told herself was reassuring.

* * *

She finished the inventory properly. She shelved the grief behind the salt-ward, and the decade in its lead-lined niche where the humming couldn't travel, and the locket—last, deliberately last—in the smallest compartment of the anonymous case, and she closed the master book, and blotted it, and squared it on the table, and did everything right.

At the top of the stairs she paused, one hand on the rail, and looked back down into the lamplit dark. Forty-one paces of shelves. Centuries of traded lives, filed and dusted and kept, griefs and decades and first loves, all of it bought and sold and stored like grain against a famine nobody named.

And underneath the thought, rising through it like a thread of mercury through water, the question she'd written in the margin, the one small word, the seed of every rebellion ever grown:

*Who?*

Whose grief was flammable? Whose decade sat unspent in a wooden box? Whose first love had begged the books for oblivion—and why had the books, which forgot nothing, agreed?

And what, she wondered, climbing now, taking the stairs two at a time because the cellar's cold had gotten into her spine and wouldn't leave—what was on page ninety-two?

She reached her rooms above the chandlery as the bells rang the small hour. She ate bread with butter in it, because Corvane had told her to and because obedience, in small things, bought freedom in large ones. She banked her fire. She lay down.

She was nearly asleep—drifting in that border country where thoughts loosen their laces—when the knocking started at the street door below. Soft. Irregular. Not a knock, quite. More like small fists, or small palms, patting the wood, patiently, over and over, as though whoever stood outside had knocked at a great many doors tonight and expected, this time, to be let in.

Yara opened her eyes.

Below, in the chandlery, the candle stock had begun—very faintly, impossibly—to smell of the sea.
