## 3. The Child at the Door

The knock came at the hour when even the lamps in Saltrow gave up pretending.

Yara heard it through a half-dream of copper coins and bitter almond, the taste still ghosting her tongue from the evening's service—foxglove in the marzipan, subtle, meant for Lord Aldery's daughter-in-law, though Aldery himself had eaten two pieces before anyone thought to look. She had swallowed her counter-draught in the shadow of the serving archway, as she always did, and smiled at the chamberlain as if her mouth were not a battlefield.

Now someone was knocking on her door at midnight, and no one knocked on Yara Eriksen's door at midnight unless they wanted something the daylight would not permit.

She rose without lighting a lamp. Years of guild training had taught her that light was a confession; darkness was a door held slightly open. She crossed the single room of her lodging above the chandler's shop, stepping over the loose board where the day-ledger slept beneath the floor, and put her eye to the crack beside the frame.

Nothing. Rain, and the gutter running loud along the eaves, and the grey smear of the street below.

Then she looked down.

A child stood on her landing. Small—six years old, perhaps seven—soaked to the skin, plastered in river mud to the knees as though she had waded the whole length of the Vell to reach this door. Her hair was dark and lank with water. Her eyes were the strangest thing about her, and it took Yara a moment to understand why: they were not afraid. Children who arrived soaked and starving at strangers' doors were always afraid. This girl looked at Yara's door the way pilgrims looked at shrines.

"Go home," Yara said through the wood, though the child could not possibly hear her, and anyway had nowhere to go. Yara knew what orphaned children looked like. She had been one, and the mirror had never quite let her forget it.

The girl raised her fist and knocked again—not the frightened tapping of a beggar but three measured blows, spaced like a ritual. Like a summons.

Yara should have turned away. Every instinct the guild had ground into her said *turn away, report nothing, involve yourself in nothing*. Corvane's voice lived in her head like a second conscience: *We are salt, Eriksen. We preserve. We do not rescue.*

But her hand was already lifting the latch.

* * *

The cold came in with the child, and the smell—river silt, wet wool, and beneath it something else, faint and wrong, like the air inside a sealed room. Yara shut the door and barred it, then turned and crouched to the girl's level, keeping her weight on her heels the way one does with strange dogs and stranger children.

"You're frozen," Yara said. "Where is your mother?"

The girl tilted her head. And then she spoke.

It was not Low Court, nor High, nor the dockside pidgin Yara had learned young enough to keep. It was not the border-tongues she'd catalogued for the guild, nor the merchant-cant of the Salt Roads, nor anything from the ledgers' long appendix of human speech. The sounds came out round and heavy, rooted deep in the chest, each word landing like a stone dropped into a well—and Yara felt them land somewhere under her own ribs.

"I don't understand you," she said gently.

The girl frowned, tried again, slower. Same language. And this time—

This time Yara's scars woke.

She had eleven of them, small white crescents scattered across her forearms and collarbone, the record of her apprenticeship: controlled doses, graduated poisons, each scar the anniversary of a night she'd spent curled around a chamber pot thanking whatever gods tolerated tasters for surviving. They had been quiet for years. Dormant as old debts. Now they burned—not the surface sting of healing but a deep ache, as if the poison in them remembered something, recognized something, and was calling out across her skin.

Yara sat back hard on her heels.

The girl watched her with those unafraid eyes and said a third phrase, softer now, almost apologetic, and pressed something into Yara's palm.

A coin.

Not any coin. Yara had handled currency from nine kingdoms and could blind-identify most of it by tooth and thumb. This was none of them. It was pale as bone, cool as riverstone, and lighter than it had any right to be—salt-white, unmarked by any mint. Except it *was* marked. Stamped dead center, worn nearly smooth but unmistakable to anyone who had spent ten years filing its paperwork:

The guild's mark. The closed circle with the vertical line—the seal of the Salt Guild, the sign that hung over no door and appeared on no document that daylight would ever see.

Yara's blood went very slow and very cold.

There were perhaps forty people alive who had ever held one of these. They were not currency. They were credentials—writs of passage into the guild's inner business, carried only by handlers, masters, and the rare courier moving something too valuable to trust to paper. A child did not carry one. A child could not *know* one existed.

"Where," Yara said, and her voice came out steadier than she deserved, "did you get this?"

The girl answered at length, earnestly, in the impossible tongue, gesturing—river, bridge, dark streets, and then, twice, with terrible clarity, she pointed at the coin, then at her own chest, then made a shape in the air with both hands: a circle closing around a line. *Mine. I am marked.* Or perhaps *I belong to this.* Or perhaps something older than either.

Then she pointed at Yara, and at the coin again, and said one final word—a short, falling syllable—and every scar on Yara's body ached at once, a chord struck on an instrument she hadn't known she was.

* * *

She fed the child because there was nothing else to do with her hands. Bread, hard cheese, the last of the honey Yara kept for masking bitterness after bad doses. The girl ate like someone who had learned hunger as a craft—thoroughly, without greed, wasting nothing—and watched Yara the whole time with an attention that felt less like a child's curiosity and more like an auditor's.

Yara sat across from her and turned the coin over and over.

Think. That was the discipline. Fear was just information arriving too fast; slow it down and sort it. So: the coin was genuine. She'd stake her immunity on it—she had held Corvane's own seal once, briefly, when he'd needed a second hand to break a wax bond, and the weight and grain of this matched exactly. Which meant the guild had issued it, recently enough that the stamp had not fully worn. Which meant some handler had given it to a six-year-old and pointed her across the city in a storm.

Or the girl had taken it. But children who robbed handlers did not survive to spend the take, and children who were sent did not arrive looking like pilgrims.

And the language. Yara got up, lit the lamp after all—darkness had stopped being useful the moment she opened the door—and pulled down the false-bottomed box from behind the loose brick in the chimney breast. Inside, wrapped in oilcloth, lay her working papers: the cipher keys, the cross-reference tables, the thin index of tongues the guild used to authenticate foreign contracts. She went through it page by page while the girl watched, licking honey from her thumb.

Dockside cant. Border dialects. The high script of Vail and the low of the Fenmarches. Nothing. Not a single root shared, so far as Yara could tell—and yet the girl's cadence nagged at her, familiar in a way she couldn't place, like a song heard once in childhood and lost. When the girl spoke, Yara's scars ached. When she stopped, they settled into a dull throb, like weather coming.

That made no sense. Scars did not have opinions about languages. Poison did not remember.

Except that everything Yara knew about poison said otherwise. Poisons were patient. They lodged, they waited, they answered to their kin. Whatever had been in those eleven doses had left its signature in her, and something in this child's voice was calling that signature by name.

The girl finished eating, set the crust down precisely at the plate's edge, folded her hands, and looked at Yara with an expression of such grave expectation that Yara laughed—a short, startled sound she hadn't made in her own lodging in months.

"Yes, you can sleep here," she said. "I clearly wasn't going to get rid of you."

She cleared the end of her bed, found a dry shift that would fit the girl like a sack, and turned her back while the child changed. In the lamp's small light Yara saw what the mud had hidden: marks on the girl's arms. Not bruises. Faint pale lines, thin as hair, tracing patterns down from shoulder to wrist—too regular for injury, too organic for writing. Old scars, healed long ago, in shapes Yara almost recognized.

Almost. That word again. The whole night was built out of *almosts*.

The girl climbed into the bed without being told, arranged herself with the economy of a soldier, and was asleep between one breath and the next—which itself was strange, Yara thought. Children fought sleep in strange places. This one surrendered to it like someone reporting off duty.

Yara sat up with the lamp trimmed low and the coin in her hand, and let herself finally think the thought she'd been circling all night:

*I know this mark. I keep its books. And I have never seen it given to a child.*

Tomorrow she would have to decide. Report the find to Corvane, as law demanded—every guild-marked object that surfaced belonged in guild hands within a day, and every guild-sworn keeper who concealed one forfeited her recorded name. Ten years of careful, invisible, immaculate service, weighed against one mud-soaked girl who spoke the language of Yara's own scars.

Or she could say nothing. Hide the coin. Break the oldest rule she had and see what the guild did about it.

The girl murmured in her sleep. One word, repeated, soft as tide going out.

Yara leaned close, telling herself she was checking on the child, knowing perfectly well she was listening.

The word was the same falling syllable the girl had spoken at the door, the one that had struck every scar like a bell. Spoken asleep, it sounded less like language and more like a name—someone's name, held carefully, the way you hold a coal you mean to carry a long way.

Outside, the rain stopped all at once, mid-fall, as if the sky had been told to hold its tongue.

And far across the sleeping city, in a palace Yara would walk into at dawn, a king sat alone at his supper table, pushing food around a golden plate, unable to remember what anything tasted like.
