## 4. What the King Ate

The king's breakfast arrived on a silver salver carried by a boy who could not have been more than fourteen, and Yara Eriksen tasted all seven dishes before the sun had fully cleared the eastern towers.

She worked in order of danger, as she always did. The honeyed figs first—honey was where assassins liked to hide things, because sweetness masked bitterness and no one suspected a fruit so humble. Then the smoked river-trout, then the soft-boiled quail eggs with their dusting of gray salt, then the porridge laced with almond milk, then the small mountain of white bread, then the candied violets that Lord Aldous Vane sent every morning from his glasshouses south of the city, and last the cup of warmed wine cut with water from the palace cisterns.

Nothing. No copper at the back of the throat, no numbness blooming along the gums, none of the telltale prickles that meant nightshade or the slow warmth that meant something worse. She let each mouthful sit on her tongue for a full ten count, cataloguing, dismissing, swallowing.

"Clear," she said to the chamber steward, a narrow man named Pell who had served the royal table for thirty years and had never once looked her in the eye. "All of it."

Pell nodded and waved the boy forward, and the salver went up the steps to the dais where the king sat alone at the long table, because Queen Isolde took her breakfast in her own apartments this season, and the hall was too large for one man, and everyone pretended not to notice how small he looked in it.

Yara stood at her station by the wall—tasters did not sit; sitting implied belonging—and watched King Aldric eat.

That was when she saw it.

He lifted a fig to his mouth and bit into it, and there was no change in his face at all. Not the small pleasure of a man eating well, not even the absent tolerance of a man eating from duty. Nothing. His jaw moved. He swallowed. He reached for the trout as though the fig had been a stone he'd been obliged to move out of the way.

She had tasted for the king's table for six years. She knew his appetites the way a wife knows them, better than the queen knew them, probably. Aldric of House Merren ate like a man in love with the world. He closed his eyes over the smoked trout. He broke bread with his fingers and dragged it through the yolk of the quail eggs and made a low sound of contentment that the courtiers pretended not to hear and secretly found comforting, because a king who enjoyed his food was a king at peace, and a king at peace meant no levies, no purges, no sudden wars.

He did none of that now. He ate the way a man feeds a fire he cannot feel the heat of. Mechanical. Thorough. Empty.

* * *

By midmorning she had written nothing down, which was itself unusual. Her habit was to record everything—the day's dishes, the day's poisons or absences thereof, the small politics of the table—in a cipher of her own devising inside a plain merchant's ledger that any inspector would find boring beyond endurance. But this morning her pen kept hovering and returning, hovering and returning, because what she wanted to write was not poison, and the ledger was for poison.

In the end she wrote: *His Majesty ate all courses. No adverse reaction. Appetite: nominal.*

*Nominal.* A word that meant *in name only*. She stared at it for a long moment before deciding she had not chosen it on purpose, and then deciding that was a lie, and leaving it.

* * *

The midday meal was worse, because at the midday meal the court attended, and Yara could watch the king among people.

The great hall filled with its usual weather of silk and scheming. Lord Vane presided over the fish course with the satisfaction of a man whose violets had survived the frost. Duchess Maren of the eastern marches sat too close to Master Corvel of the Treasury and laughed at things that were not funny, which meant either an alliance or an extortion, and Yara noted it automatically, the way she noted everything, because a taster who knew which noble wished which rival dead lived longer than a taster who didn't. The Chancellor, old Hesk, drank three cups of the summer wine and grew loud about grain tariffs. Ordinary. All of it ordinary. The court turned like a wheel in a millstream, grinding the same grain it had ground for centuries.

And above it all, at the high table, the king moved through the meal like a man underwater.

She tested him, quietly, the way she tested everything. She had learned long ago that the palate was the most honest witness in any room. Grief changed how a man tasted. Fear did. Love did. A guilty conscience made sweet things taste metallic—she had proven that once, years ago, with a chancellor who was embezzling and didn't know she knew. The tongue reported what the face concealed.

So she watched what he reached for. Aldric loved sharp flavors. Pickled things, mustard, the bitter greens from the kitchen garden that no other royal person would touch. Today he left the pickled beets untouched and ate three servings of the blancmange, which was the blandest thing on the table, which was the kind of thing you ate when you could not taste anything at all and were only going through the motions of fueling a body.

Then there was the salt.

A taster notices salt. It was practically theology in her trade—salt was the one thing that appeared at every meal, in every dish, the common denominator, the thing you calibrated against. And the king salted his fish. And then salted it again. And then again, until the crust of it glittered gray-white across the surface like frost on a windowpane, and Lord Vane, beside him, made a small joke about preserving the man along with the herring, and the king laughed.

The laugh was wrong. That was the second thing. It came half a beat late, like thunder after lightning that hadn't happened yet, and it used the right muscles but not quite in the right order, the way a puppet uses strings. Around the table nobody else noticed. Courtiers see what they need to see. But Yara had spent six years training herself to catch the difference between a poison that killed in a minute and a poison that killed in a year, between a tremor of fear and a tremor of palsy, between a man laughing and a man performing laughter, and every instrument she owned told her the same thing:

Something was wearing the king loosely. Like a coat cut for a bigger man.

* * *

"You're staring," said Sefa, under her breath.

Sefa was the queen's taster, five years in the post, a broad-shouldered woman from the coast with a scar through her eyebrow and the emotional range of a harbor wall. They shared a wall in the tasters' quarters and, occasionally, a flask of cheap brandy, and they trusted each other exactly as far as two women in their profession could afford to, which was further than most and less than it looked.

"I'm watching," Yara corrected.

"You're watching like my grandmother watched the sea the winter the boats didn't come back." Sefa tore a piece of bread and didn't eat it. "What is it?"

Yara weighed it. Six years of discipline said say nothing, record everything, trust no one with an observation that couldn't be unobserved. But Sefa had pulled her out of the cistern channel the time the oyster course went wrong, had held her hair back through the worst of the antidote nights, had never once asked what the guild paid her or why a taster needed money beyond her wage.

"The king isn't tasting his food," she said.

Sefa glanced up at the dais, casual as checking the weather. "He's eating plenty."

"Eating isn't tasting." Yara kept her eyes on the hall, on the wheeling courtiers, on anything but the dais, because looking directly at a thing you feared gave it power over your face. "Watch the salt. Watch what he reaches for. He had the blancmange twice."

"So he's got a sweet tooth this season."

"Aldric Merren once sent the blancmange back because it offended him." She heard her own voice go flat and precise, the voice she used for measurements. "He called it *a coward's pudding*. In front of the Vane delegation. It caused a diplomatic incident I had to taste my way through. He does not have a sweet tooth, Sefa. He has never had a sweet tooth. Something has happened to his tongue."

Sefa was quiet for a moment. "Or his head. Kings go strange. It's the weight of the crown."

"Perhaps." It was the sensible answer. She hated it. "Taste the evening courses carefully tomorrow. Both kitchens. If something's being dosed to him slowly—if someone's feeding him something that deadens the palate—I want to know before whoever's doing it knows we know."

"And if it's not in the food?"

Yara looked at the king. He was smiling at something the Duchess Maren had said, and the smile arrived on schedule, and behind his eyes there was nothing arriving at all.

"Then I don't know what it is," she said. "And I hate not knowing worse than I hate arsenic."

* * *

That night, in her lodging above the chandler's shop on Candlewick Row, she unlocked the floorboard and took out the ledgers.

There were three of them, stacked in order of age. The top one was the merchant's ledger, dull and legal, full of ciphered entries about dishes and doses. Beneath it lay the guild book—the Salt Guild's running inventory, the record of impossible things bought and sold and held in bond, the ledger she kept because the guild required a keeper who could not read its contents without permission and would not want to. And beneath that, wrapped in oilcloth, the oldest volume, the one she was forbidden to open past her own entries, the one whose marginalia sometimes kept her awake.

Tonight she worked by candlelight with the merchant's ledger open, doing what she always did: recording the day. Dishes tasted. Poisons found: none. Court intelligence: Vane and Maren, note; Hesk drinking heavily, note; the treasury delegation arriving Thursday, note.

And then, because her hand seemed to move without consulting her, she began a new column.

She titled it nothing. Just ruled a line down the margin and started writing, in the same cipher, the same dry administrative voice she used for everything—as if dread could be disguised as duty if you dressed it properly.

*Day 1. His Majesty reports no preference at table. Salting pattern abnormal (3x baseline). Sweet courses preferred over sharp. Laughter delayed approx. half-second. Eyes: pupils responsive but slow. Blink rate low. Did not touch pickled beets.*

She stopped. Read it back. It sounded like the log of a sick animal. It sounded like the notes a physician keeps on a patient he already knows will die.

*Day 1,* she wrote again, underneath, as though repetition could make it ordinary. *No cause identified. Food supply clean. Continue observation.*

Continue observation. That was the whole of her creed, distilled. Taste, record, survive. Do not interpret. Interpretation was how tasters died—you interpreted, you concluded, you acted, and acting drew attention, and attention was the one poison no immunity could cover. For six years she had watched the court the way she watched a dish: attentively, dispassionately, swallowing what she found and saying *clear*.

But her pen hovered over the page, and she thought about the fig.

Not the poison in it—there had been no poison in it. She thought about the way he had bitten it. A man's first bite of the day tells you everything. Hunger is honest. Pleasure is honest. Even misery is honest, in its way—a man eating breakfast through grief bites differently than a man eating through fear. She had catalogued a thousand first bites. This one had belonged to none of them.

It had belonged to no one. That was the horror of it. There had been no one behind the bite at all. The jaw had moved because jaws move. The body had swallowed because bodies swallow. And whatever looked out through the king's eyes had regarded the fig with the mild, distant interest of a scholar examining an artifact from a country he intended to erase.

*Eyes: pupils responsive but slow,* she had written. Coward's phrasing. What she had actually seen, in the moment the king's gaze had crossed hers down the length of the high table—one accidental, ordinary, courtly glance—was this: recognition without knowledge. He had known there was a person there. He had not known what a person was for.

She blew out the candle and sat in the dark and listened to the chandler's stock cooling and cracking below her floor, wax contracting in the night, thousands of small voices ticking as they hardened into shapes.

* * *

She dreamed of the cellar archive, which was not unusual, and in the dream she was shelving jars, which was also not unusual—bottled grief, a stolen decade, somebody's first love in a stoppered flask the color of dawn—and the unusual part was that the jars were empty. Every one. Shelf after shelf after shelf, hundreds of flasks, all of them empty, all of them labeled in her own handwriting with things that should have been inside them: *Grief, black, one measure. Decade, stolen, years 12–22. First love, unopened.* Empty vessels, perfectly preserved, waiting to be filled.

And somewhere below the lowest shelf, below the archive's stone floor, something vast and patient was drinking.

She woke before dawn with her heart slamming and the taste of ash in her mouth, which was absurd, because she had eaten nothing since noon, and sat up in the dark repeating the taster's liturgy to herself like a prayer: *Taste, record, survive. Taste, record, survive.*

It did not help. Because for the first time in six years, the liturgy had a hole in it, and through the hole she could see the question she had been refusing to ask since the fig:

If the king could not taste—if the king could not *feel*—then what, precisely, had she been tasting for? Whose safety was her tongue guarding? Whose body was that, moving through the ceremonies of appetite like a clockwork figure wound by someone who had read about kings but never met one?

She lit the candle. She opened the merchant's ledger to the new column. And beneath *Day 1*, in her careful cipher, she added a final line, the most dangerous sentence she had ever written, though she would not understand how dangerous for some weeks yet:

*Day 2. Subject continues. Whatever sits behind his eyes is learning to imitate him. It is getting better at it.*

She blotted the page, locked the ledger away, and put her hand flat on the floorboard over the hidden books, feeling the cool wood like a pulse.

Outside, the bells rang for prime, and the city woke around her, ten thousand people rising to lives built out of memory—streets they remembered, names they remembered, a king they remembered—and none of them, not one, had noticed anything missing yet.

Neither had she. Not quite. But the ash was still in her mouth, and she could not remember eating anything at all.
