## 5. Corvane's Warning

The summons came the way all guild business came: folded into something ordinary.

Yara found it at dawn, tucked inside a delivery of river eels from the fishmonger on Tanner's Row—a man who had never sold her an eel in her life and never would. The paper was cheap, unmarked, wrapped around one of the eels like a second skin. She unwound it standing in her lodging's narrow kitchen, the girl still asleep behind the curtain, and read the message twice before her hands stopped being steady.

*The cellar door opens at moonrise. Bring what you carry. — C.*

No seal. No signature beyond the initial. The Salt Guild did not believe in seals; a seal could be forged, but Corvane's handwriting could not, because Corvane had not written a letter by hand in eleven years—he dictated, always, to scribes who then burned their notes. This was his own script, cramped and left-leaning, the letters of a man who had learned to write in secret as a child and never unlearned the habit of hiding the words inside other words.

*Bring what you carry.*

They knew about the girl.

Of course they knew. Yara had told herself for three days that the coin might have slipped past them—that a mud-soaked child with a salt-white mark in her fist might wander the city unnoticed by an organization that noticed everything. But the guild weighed every salt-coin struck, every mark circulated, every debt entered and discharged. A coin of that denomination appearing in a commoner's hands was not an oversight. It was an alarm.

She folded the note and held its corner to the stove flame until it curled into black lace, then crushed the ash between her fingers the way she'd been taught—ash carried no words, only weight.

Behind the curtain, small sounds. The girl waking.

Yara drew the cloth aside and found her sitting up, knees drawn to her chest, watching Yara with eyes the color of rain over slate. Three days now the child had slept in her bed while Yara took the floor, eaten from her larder, sat silent through the nights. Three days without a single word—not the strange flowing language either, nothing. As if whatever had spoken through her at the door had been spent, or had decided silence was safer.

"Good morning," Yara said. Useless words. The girl didn't understand them, or pretended not to, or understood them perfectly and simply saw no reason to honor them with reply. She was perhaps seven years old, though there was something in her stillness that made age impossible to guess—the stillness of deep water, old wells.

Yara set out bread and honey. The girl ate neatly, completely, wasting nothing. When she finished, she looked at Yara's hands—at the faint silver scarring across the knuckles where years of micro-doses of arsenic and worse had written their history in her skin—and touched her own wrist, once, deliberately.

The scars along Yara's forearms ached. They'd been aching since the night at the door, a low burning she could not salve, as if the poisons sleeping in her blood had recognized something in the child and stirred.

"I know," Yara said quietly. "I don't know what you are either."

She washed the dishes. She braided her hair flat under her taster's cap. And she made a decision that would have been unthinkable forty-eight hours ago: she would not bring the girl to moonrise. She would lie to the Salt Guild.

The thought sat in her chest like a swallowed stone. Eleven years she had served them. Eleven years of perfect entries, perfect deliveries, perfect silence. Her loyalty was not sentiment—it was architecture, load-bearing, the frame that held up everything else she was. The guild had bought her debts when she was nine years old and starving in a debtor's gutter; the guild had trained her tongue until poison became a flavor profile rather than a death; the guild had taken her name into its ledgers as collateral, which meant the guild *held* her, in the way a bank holds a deed. You did not lie to the institution that owned the record of your existence.

And yet.

She looked at the child, who was watching her with that patient, bottomless attention, and felt the ache in her arms deepen.

"Moonrise," Yara said, and tapped the floor twice, and pointed down, toward the cellar. Then she pointed at the girl, and shook her head slowly, side to side. *Not you. Stay.* Whether any of it communicated, she couldn't know. But the girl's gaze flicked to the floorboards, then back up, and something almost like understanding settled over her face.

Or perhaps Yara only needed it to be so.

* * *

The day passed in the long ritual of court tasting, and Yara performed it flawlessly, because a liar's first duty is to seem unchanged.

The king dined lightly—white soup, quail in vine leaves, a custard flavored with burnt sugar—and Yara stood at his shoulder with the silver spoon and tasted each dish before it touched him, holding each mouthful on her tongue for the count of thirty while the whole hall watched her throat for the twitch that meant death. Nothing twitched. The food was clean. The food was always clean now, which should have been reassuring and was instead the most frightening thing in the palace: somewhere in the kitchens, someone had learned that poison was no longer necessary.

Because the king tasted nothing anyway.

She watched him eat. She had watched kings eat for eleven years and knew the choreography—the small involuntary pleasures, the closing of eyes at a good sauce, the flicker of the tongue against the palate. The man wearing the crown performed none of it. He lifted the spoon, he chewed, he swallowed, and his face remained as smooth and attentive as a portrait. Once—only once—his gaze slid sideways and caught hers, and for a half-second Yara felt the way a rabbit feels beneath a hawk's shadow: seen entirely, assessed, catalogued as *food or not-food*. Then the moment passed, and he was smiling at the queen, and the hall went on with its noise.

She logged it that evening in her private book, beside the poison records, in her own compressed cipher: *Day 41. His Majesty consumed 3 courses without gustatory response. Pupils slow to contract. Asked the Lord Chamberlain's name twice within the hour. Second time, called him by his father's name.*

Beside it, in the margin, without deciding to, she wrote: *Something is eating him from the inside out. Something is eating us.*

Then she crossed it out—but not thoroughly enough to make it unreadable. Some part of her wanted it legible. Some part of her was already keeping evidence for a tribunal that did not yet exist.

At dusk she walked home the long way, doubling back twice through the tannery stink of the low quarter, checking for followers the way Corvane had taught her in her first year. No one followed. Which meant either no one cared, or whoever watched her was better than she was, and both possibilities curdled her stomach equally.

She fed the girl. She taught her two more words of trade-tongue—*eat*, *sleep*—by pointing and repeating until those gray eyes flickered with something that might have been wry acknowledgment. *Eat. Sleep.* As if Yara were the simpleton, learning to speak one word a day while the child withheld a whole language like a miser withholding coin.

Then the moon rose, and Yara went down to open the cellar door.

* * *

The archive lay beneath the chandlers' row, behind a door disguised as a coal chute, down stairs that smelled of tallow and cold stone and, underneath everything, faintly of the sea—salt soaked so deep into the masonry that no amount of lime could draw it out. The guild had built here two hundred years ago and the building above had changed hands eleven times, burned twice, been rebuilt in three different styles. Only the cellar endured. Cellars were honest. Cellars remembered.

Yara descended with her lantern raised, and the shelves rose around her in the dark like cliff faces—hundreds of them, thousands, receding beyond the lantern's reach, every one laden with ledgers. Not books exactly. Ledgers: bound in gray hide, clasped in dull pewter, spines labeled not with titles but with entries. *Grief, bottled, 4 drams — Aldous Merrin, paid in full.* *Name: "Sable," borrowed, 6 months — returned damaged.* *First love, entire — withdrawn by owner, no repayment possible.* *Ten years, unspent — seller deceased, stock intact.*

The inventory of impossible things. Yara had catalogued it for six years, row by row, shelf by shelf, and still there were alcoves she had never audited, ledgers whose clasps had never been broken in her tenure. The guild traded in everything people were foolish enough or desperate enough to sell, and it kept meticulous accounts of all of it, because the guild believed—Corvane believed—with religious fervor that *what is recorded cannot truly be lost.*

Tonight, walking those aisles toward the reading room, Yara felt the belief crack like river ice under her boots.

Corvane was waiting in the reading room, seated at the great oak table with a single candle before him, and the sight of him hit her with its usual double blow: comfort and dread, in equal measure, because he had been her handler since she was nine years old and he was the closest thing to a father the guild had ever permitted her to have.

He was not a large man. He was made of angles and gray wool, with a face like a closed ledger itself—long, ruled, ruled again, every line accounted for. His hair had gone white at the temples early and stayed there, refusing further surrender, as if even his body kept strict accounts. He looked up when she entered, and his eyes did the thing they always did: read her completely, in silence, top to bottom, entry by entry.

"You're late," he said, though she wasn't.

"The streets were watched."

"They're always watched." He gestured to the chair opposite. "Sit."

She sat. The candle between them threw their shadows huge against the shelving. Somewhere deeper in the archive, water dripped from stone to stone with the patience of centuries.

"A salt-coin," Corvane said. "White issue. Struck before the founding of the current dynasty." He laid something on the table between them: a sketch, done in a clerk's quick hand, of a round coin stamped with the guild's mark—the coiled serpent swallowing its tail, crowned. "Recovered this morning from a moneylender on Cordwainer Street who tried to pass it. He described the person who gave it to him. A child. Female. Approximately seven years of age. Mud to the knees. Carrying herself," and here Corvane paused, and the pause was louder than anything he'd said yet, "carrying herself like a courier carrying something worth more than her life."

Yara kept her face still. Thirty-count hold, tongue flat, no twitch. The same discipline she used at the king's table, deployed now against her own handler.

"The coin was reported to me," Corvane continued, "because white-issue coins do not circulate. They are struck for internal accounting only. There are perhaps forty in existence, and I can account for thirty-eight of them." He turned the sketch toward her. "Which means either two have gone missing—which I would already know—or this one came from outside the ledger entirely. From *before* the ledger. Do you understand what I'm telling you?"

"That the coin is old," Yara said.

"That the coin is *impossible*." For the first time, something moved behind Corvane's eyes—something that in another man would have been fear, and in Corvane was merely a faster pulse of calculation. "The guild does not deal in impossibles, Yara. We deal in rare things, painful things, things people misprice. But everything we trade obeys the law of accounts. Everything balances. A coin predating our own mint is a debt with no recorded creditor. It is a hole in the book." He leaned forward. "And holes in the book are how organizations die."

"What would you have me do?" she asked, and heard how level her voice was, and hated how good at this she'd become.

"The child." He said it plainly, without cruelty, which somehow made it worse. "Wherever she is, whoever holds her—you will locate her and arrange delivery. To me, personally, not through channels. Within four days."

"For processing," Yara said. The phrase was standard guild language. Assets were processed. Debts were processed. People, occasionally, were processed.

"For processing," Corvane confirmed. "There are masters who want her examined by the archivists, and masters who want her delivered upward to—" he stopped himself, jaw tightening, "—to parties I am not authorized to name. I would prefer the archivists. I would prefer it very much. So find her quickly, and give her to me, and we may keep this contained at the level of ledgers rather than let it rise to the level of *rituals*." 

He studied her a moment longer. The candle flame bent, straightened, bent again, though there was no draft.

"You've gone quiet," he said. "You go quiet when you're composing. I taught you that tell myself, which means I know precisely how much practice you've had hiding it." A dry pause. "You are wondering whether you can lie to me. Let me save you the arithmetic. You cannot. Not because I'm clever—though I am—but because you've never done it, and the first lie is always too heavy to lift gracefully. Whatever you're carrying right now, set it down. Give it to me the way you gave me your debts at nine years old. That transaction saved your life. This one can too."

And there it was—the whole machinery of her loyalty, laid out on the oak table between them like a dissected thing. Nine years old. Starving. Sold, signed, saved. Every debt forgiven in exchange for every future owed. Corvane had been the hand that pulled her from the gutter, and Corvane was now the hand extended again, offering salvation on the same terms: give me the child, and remain safe, and remain *mine*.

It would be so easy. It would cost nothing but a stranger.

Yara looked at the candle flame, and thought of gray eyes watching her from behind a curtain, patient as deep water. Thought of the scars aching. Thought of a king eating ash with a smile on his face, calling his chamberlain by his dead father's name.

"No," she said.

The word dropped into the room like a coin into a well. Corvane didn't move. The drip of water in the dark aisles went on, unhurried.

"No," she said again, steadier, because the first had cracked and the second must not. "I haven't found the child. I've looked—I've walked Cordwainer Street, the fish market, the orphan stairs at Saint Verre's. Beggars, pickpockets, no girl matching the description. If she exists, she's already gone—passed to a caravan, a river boat, out of the city. Or she never came through my district at all, and someone sent you a false description to watch us chase shadows."

Every word was true except the architecture of them. This was the trick of lying, she discovered, mid-sentence, with her heart slamming against her ribs like a trapped bird: build the lie entirely from true bricks. The searches were real—she *had* walked those streets, on her own time, hunting for any trace of where the girl had come from. The possibility of flight was real. Only the conclusion was counterfeit, and conclusions were hard to audit.

Corvane listened without blinking. When she finished, he sat back, and for a long moment the only sound was the candle and the dripping dark.

"There it is," he said softly. "Your first lie. And look—still breathing. Still seated. The world didn't end." He rubbed one angle of his face with one angle of his hand. "I'm going to choose to believe you, Yara, for reasons that have nothing to do with belief. Because the alternative is to open a formal inquiry, and inquiries have a way of consuming everyone in them—including nine-year-old debts I paid off twenty years ago and have never once regretted." His eyes pinned her. "But understand what you've just become, even if only provisionally. A keeper of two sets of books. And the guild has a saying about keepers of two sets of books."

"We burn them," Yara said.

"We burn *them*," Corvane corrected. "The books. The keepers we recycle." He rose, buttoned his gray coat with three precise movements, and took the candle with him, so that the darkness came forward across the table like a tide. At the edge of the light he paused.

"Four days," he said. "If she surfaces anywhere in this city, bring her to me before anyone else finds her. Not for the guild's sake. For yours. There are masters upstairs who would process that child the way we process a bottled grief—and there is at least one master who would process *you* the same way, the moment he suspected you'd hidden her. Sleep with your knife loose in its sheath, taster. And Yara—" the darkness held his voice a moment after his shape had gone, "—whatever you think you're protecting, ask yourself whether it can survive what's coming. Ask yourself what the coin was paying *for*."

His footsteps faded down the aisle, unhurried, counted, gone.

Yara sat alone in the black reading room, listening to her own heartbeat slow. Two sets of books. One life, double-entry.

Then, from the deep shadow between the shelves—an aisle she had checked, an aisle that had been empty when she passed it—a small voice spoke in the flowing, older-than-kingdoms language, and Yara's blood went to ice, because the child should have been home behind a locked curtain three districts away.

A shape detached itself from the dark. Small. Mud-stained at the hem. Gray eyes catching the last gray light from the high vent like a cat's.

The girl stepped into the reading room, crossed to Yara's chair, and pressed something into her palm: a second salt-white coin, warm as if freshly struck, stamped with the coiled serpent swallowing its tail—and beneath the serpent, in tiny letters of the Root-Tongue Yara had never been able to read but suddenly, terribly, almost understood—

*a date. Three days hence.*

* * *

Yara did not remember deciding to hide the girl in the archive. Deciding implied deliberation, options weighed, and there had been none. There had been only the coin in her palm and the child's hand withdrawing and the absolute certainty, rising through her like floodwater, that the guild must not find what she was carrying.

She moved fast now, moving the way she'd been trained to move during audits—efficient, silent, treating panic as a luxury item she could not afford. The reading room had alcoves. Behind the third alcove, sealed by a false shelf-back she herself had designed six years ago for storing entries too sensitive for general access, lay a space barely larger than a coffin. She had built it to hide ledgers. It would hold a child.

"Listen to me." She crouched, took the girl by the shoulders, and pointed: *you, here.* Then she mimed sleep, silence, stillness. Then she held up four fingers, and folded them down one at a time. *Four days. Four nights.* The girl watched with that unnerving patience, and then—this was the worst part—she reached up and touched Yara's scarred wrist again, gently, tracing one silver line with one muddy finger, and nodded once.

Consent. Or prophecy. With this child, Yara suspected they were the same word in different languages.

She stocked the hiding place with water in a stoppered flask, bread, a blanket of the coarse gray wool the guild issued for wrapping fragile inventory. At the last moment she added her private logbook—the one with the king's symptoms, the uncrossed-out line about the thing that eats. If they found the girl, they would find everything anyway. If they found neither, the book was safer here than at her lodging.

Before sealing the false shelf, the girl caught her sleeve. Pointed at Yara's mouth. Touched her own lips. And spoke—one soft phrase in the Root-Tongue, three syllables, rising and falling like water finding its level—and Yara felt the words land somewhere below language, in the scarred and poisoned country of her body, and understood without translation:

*Keep my secret, and I will keep yours.*

Yara sealed the shelf. Sat down at the great oak table in the dark. And waited to see whether she had just committed treason, or survival—or whether, in the economy of the Salt Guild, where everything balanced and every debt came due, the two had ever really been different things.

Above her, through the vent, the moon was crossing toward morning. Three days, the coin had said. She turned it over in her fingers, and in the dark, faintly, it was still warm—as if something far below the city, older than the ledger, older than the crown, had just begun counting.
