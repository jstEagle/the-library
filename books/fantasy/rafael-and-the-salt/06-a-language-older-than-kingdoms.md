## 6. A Language Older Than Kingdoms

The girl did not sleep so much as she went somewhere else.

Yara watched her from the cellar stairwell, a candle cupped in her palm, and could not decide whether what she saw was rest or rehearsal. The child lay curled on the pallet of sacking beside the ledger shelves—Yara had moved her there on the second night because the loft above was too near the street, too near listening ears—and in her sleep her lips moved constantly, shaping small hard syllables that sounded like stones dropping into deep water. *Keth. Orenn. Keth-va-sul.* The same sounds over and over, patient as tide.

They made Yara's scars ache.

That was the strangest part. Not fear—the girl was four feet of mud and hunger, hardly a threat—but this low, wrong ache along the inside of her left arm where the guild's first immunity training had burned its lessons into her. Corvane had called the scars her "savings account." She had never once thought of them as something that could *listen*.

She climbed down the last three steps and crouched by the pallet.

"Can you understand me?" she asked, as she had asked every night since the door. "Nod if you can. You don't have to speak."

The girl's eyes opened without any transition from sleep, which unsettled Yara more than the language had. Children surfaced slowly or not at all. This one arrived fully, like a diver breaking water.

She looked at Yara for a long moment. Then she lifted one small hand—not nodding, not shaking her head—and pressed two fingers to Yara's scarred forearm, gently, the way you touch something to learn if it is warm.

And she said a word.

It was one of the sleeping words. *Sul.* It came out clear and unhurried, and it meant nothing to Yara in any tongue she knew—Aldish, Low Court, the harbor cant, the three years of High Aldish she'd traded a winter of taster's wages to learn—and yet her arm *ached* when it landed, a deep bell-tone through the scar tissue, and behind her eyes something stirred that had no name but felt horribly like recognition.

"Don't," Yara said, and heard her own voice crack. She softened it. "Please."

The girl lowered her hand. She did not look sorry. She looked like someone who had confirmed a measurement.

* * *

Corvane would come at dusk. He always came at dusk, when the archive lamps were lit and the guild's business wore its evening face, and Yara had perhaps six hours to solve a problem she could not name aloud, because naming things in the Salt Guild was the same as selling them.

So she worked.

The archive occupied the full undercroft of the drop-house on Candlemaker's Row—a long stone belly lined with shelving, cool and dry as a tomb, holding the ledgers in their ranked centuries. Yara had catalogued them all. That was her function, her value, the reason the guild tolerated a food-taster with ink-stained fingers: she kept the inventory of impossible things. Bottled grief, stoppered in blue glass. A stolen decade, folded flat between pages like a pressed flower. Someone's first love, sold twice, which had caused an unpleasantness in Year 214 of the current reign that Yara had spent three weeks untangling.

She knew the collection the way other people knew their own hands.

Which was why, before dawn on the third morning, she pulled the oldest ledger off its shelf.

It had no title. It predated the numbering system by so many centuries that the numbering system simply refused it, the way a map refuses the ocean's edge. Its cover was salt-white hide gone ivory with age, soft as cloth, and its spine made no sound at all when it opened—which was wrong, because every other book in the archive complained. The archivists who had come before Yara had called it the First Ledger, though *first* implied there had been a second, and there was no evidence of one. It was kept on the lowest shelf, in the darkest corner, wrapped in oilcloth, and her instructions regarding it were absolute: dust it, weigh it once a season to confirm no pages had been removed, and never read past your own entries.

*Never read past your own entries.* Corvane's voice in her head, gentle as ever, gentle the way a hand on the back of the neck is gentle.

Yara unwrapped it anyway.

The vellum inside was older than the binding, palimpsest upon palimpsest, and here was the thing she had never told anyone: she had always been able to read a little of it. Not fluently. But certain characters rose off the page for her the way faces rise out of a crowd—familiar without introduction, meaningful without translation. She had assumed for years that this was some trick of her training, some subliminal alphabet the immunity drills had burned in alongside the poison-signs. She had never mentioned it. In the Salt Guild, unexplained aptitudes were not talents. They were liabilities, and liabilities got processed.

Now she laid the First Ledger open on the reading table, set the candle close, and waited.

The girl woke on her own as true dawn grayed the high slit windows. She padded across the cold floor barefoot, silent even in movement, and stopped at the table's edge, and looked at the open book.

Then she looked up at Yara, and her whole face changed.

It was not wonder. Yara had seen wonder on children's faces, at festivals, at fireworks over the harbor. This was narrower and more terrible: it was the look of someone finding a door in a wall they had believed solid all their life.

The girl reached out—one finger, hovering above the page, not quite touching—and spoke.

*Keth. Orenn. Sul.*

The same words from her sleep. And Yara's eyes went where the girl's finger pointed, and the ache sang up her arm again, and she saw it.

There, in the margin of a page whose main text recorded—she could make out fragments, *binding*, *under*, *crown*, *feed*—in a script of angular, root-like characters that branched and forked like something grown rather than written, the same shapes repeated. The same syllables. *Keth. Orenn. Sul.* Written in a hand far older than the girl, older than the kingdom, older than the paper's own main text, cramped into the margin by some scribe who had been alive when this language was not dead.

"It matches," Yara said aloud, to no one, because saying it made it real and she badly needed one thing tonight to be real. "You're speaking marginalia. You're speaking—" She laughed, and it came out wrong. "You're speaking the *footnotes of the world*."

The girl tilted her head at her tone, not the words. Then she did something Yara had not seen her do before.

She smiled.

It transformed her. Under the mud and the hollow cheeks there was, briefly, a child of perhaps eight who had been pretty once, in some place Yara couldn't imagine, before whatever road had brought her to a food-taster's door in the mud-rain of a spring night. The smile lasted two seconds. It was the loneliest thing Yara had ever seen, because it was clearly a smile the girl had learned from watching others and was deploying now, deliberately, as a bridge across the gap between them.

Yara sat down heavily on the stool.

"All right," she said. "All right. Let's see what you know."

* * *

They built the lesson out of what the archive offered.

Yara could not teach the girl Aldish—there was no shared ground to build on, no pointing game that stuck, or none that survived past breakfast. But the First Ledger gave them a third country to meet in. Yara copied marginal characters onto scrap vellum in fat charcoal strokes; the girl studied them, corrected Yara's clumsy renderings with a firm little hand—*no, the fork goes downward, always downward, roots don't reach for the sky*—and then, crucially, supplied sound. Sound, and something more than sound. When she spoke each word she gestured, and the gestures were consistent, and slowly, over hours, a grammar of hands and marks assembled itself between them.

*Sul*: the gesture was a hand pressed flat against the sternum. When the girl used it toward Yara, her scars ached. Toward the candle flame, nothing. Toward the ledger itself, a fainter echo. Yara wrote in her working notes, in careful cipher: *SUL — self? substance? that-which-is-held-inside? Applies to persons, books, fire(?). NOT applicable to objects.* She underlined it twice. In her experience, words that applied to people and books and fire but not to tables were either poetry or danger, and in the Salt Guild those were usually the same category.

*Orenn*: both hands cupped, then opened outward, like releasing a bird or spilling water. The girl used it when Yara poured her broth. Used it again, pointedly, at the memory-bottles on their shelf—the blue glass ones, the griefs. Yara's pen hovered a long time over that entry before she wrote: *ORENN — to give out? to pour? to release what is held?*

And *keth* she could not get at all, except that the girl used it most often while touching her own chest, just below the collarbone, and each time she did, the candle flame leaned toward her. Every time. Without fail, without wind, without draft. Yara tested it eleven times, moving the candles, sealing the room, checking the vents she knew better than the guild's own masons. Eleven times the flame bent toward the small muddy child like a listener leaning in.

By late afternoon Yara's hands were shaking, and it was not entirely the poison-residue tremor she'd carried since her twenty-fifth year.

Because here was what she had not written down, what she barely admitted to herself between tests: the marginalia of the First Ledger was not random. Scribes do not annotate at random. Whoever had written those branching characters in the margins of the guild's founding book had been doing what annotators always do—arguing, warning, correcting, grieving. And scattered among the syllables Yara now recognized, there were recurring sequences she'd catalogued years ago and filed, dutifully and stupidly, under *ornamentation*. Sequences that appeared beside the word she could now half-read as *crown*. Sequences that appeared beside *hunger*. One sequence, appearing seven times across four hundred pages, always in the same cramped urgent hand, always underlined—underlined, in a book whose owners forbade annotation—with a character the girl, when shown it, touched to her own mouth and said:

*Vess.*

Yara wrote that one down and stared at it until the charcoal wore blunt.

"That's a word," she said. "Or a name." The girl watched her with those old eyes. "Is it yours? Are you Vess?"

The girl considered the question with a seriousness that no eight-year-old should possess. Then she touched her mouth again, then her sternum—*vess, sul*, mouth and self, spoken-word and held-substance—and shook her head, and nodded, and shook it again, caught in some distinction the gesture-language couldn't carry. Finally she took the charcoal stub from Yara's fingers.

"Careful," Yara started, "that's—"

But the girl wasn't writing. She was drawing.

She worked fast and sure, hunched over the scrap vellum, tongue-tip between her teeth, and Yara let her, watching the shape emerge stroke by stroke. A circle first, closed with a double line. Then points rising from it, five of them, spaced with an accuracy no child freehand-draws: the crown. The king's crown—she knew it instantly, had seen it glinting at the head of the high table a hundred times, had tasted death from dishes set before it. Five points, double circlet. Drawn from memory by a mud-soaked foreign child who had never, as far as anything in Yara's records showed, set foot within forty leagues of the palace.

Beneath the crown, the girl drew a serpent.

Not coiled beneath it, not crowned by it, not in any heraldic arrangement Yara had ever seen on a signet or a standard. The serpent was *underneath*—its body drawn as a single continuous line passing below the crown's band, and the crown's band resting *on* it, the way a yoke rests on a neck. And the serpent's head rose up at the right side, and its open jaw closed around the lowest point of the crown. Around the fifth point. Gently. Patiently. The way a thing eats when it has all the time in the world.

The girl shaded the serpent's eye solid black. Then she tapped the drawing—crown, serpent, crown—and looked up at Yara, and said, in her root-and-branch language, a full sentence. Several sentences. Her voice stayed level; her hands did not. They shook as she spoke, and the candle flames in the archive—all twelve of them, every lamp on every shelf—leaned toward her at once, and guttered, and steadied, and leaned again.

Yara caught maybe one word in five. *Crown. Sul. Hunger—* no, not hunger, the gesture was different, cupped hands opening inward instead of out, the reverse of *orenn*. Taking-in. Swallowing. And a word she didn't know at all, repeated three times, each time accompanied by a slow dragging gesture, fingertips pulling backward through the air as though gathering something invisible off a table—

As though erasing something.

When the girl finished, the silence in the archive had weight. Dust hung motionless in the lamplight. Somewhere above, on Candlemaker's Row, the evening bells rang the hour, and the sound seemed to arrive from very far away, as though the city itself had stepped back.

Yara looked down at the drawing.

She looked at the marginalia of the First Ledger, open beside it, at the sequence that appeared seven times in four hundred pages beside the half-glimpsed word *crown*—and with the girl's sounds still ringing in her scarred ear, she suddenly heard how the sequence would be voiced, and her blood went cold and stayed there.

It matched. The urgent underlined warning in the guild's oldest book and the last word of the mud-child's trembling sentence were the same word. Whatever the scribe of four centuries ago had been trying to say about kings and crowns and the thing that eats them, he had said it in the language this girl dreamed in—and the girl had just said it back across all that dead time, pointing at the palace on the hill.

"No," Yara said quietly. It was not disagreement. It was the sound a person makes when a floor gives way an inch and holds.

The girl watched her. Then, deliberately, she picked up the charcoal again and drew one final mark: a small figure standing apart from the crown and the serpent, at the edge of the vellum. A stick-figure with two lines of hair, and beside it—in Yara's own borrowed hand, mimicked with unnerving accuracy—the character Yara now knew.

*Sul.*

You. It concerns you. Or: you are part of what is held inside. Or, in the grammar of gestures Yara had only begun to parse, with its terrible ambiguities: *you are being swallowed too.*

The cellar door at the top of the stairs opened.

Boots on stone. The particular cadence Yara knew better than her own heartbeat—unhurried, heel-first, a man who had never once needed to hurry anywhere in his life. Corvane's voice drifted down ahead of him, mild as milk:

"Yara. You missed the summons at dusk." A pause, exactly long enough. "I see we have a guest."

The candle flames all leaned toward the stairs.

Yara stood, placing herself—without deciding to, her body simply doing it—between the child and the light.
