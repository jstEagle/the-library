## 7. The Thing Wearing the Crown

The scrying-glass lived in a room that did not officially exist.

Yara had known about it for six years—the way she knew about most things in the Salt Guild's architecture: by counting steps, memorizing doors, noting which corridors smelled of tallow and which of cold stone. The room sat behind the archive's eastern wall, past the shelf where the guild kept its bottled griefs in rows of dark green glass, each one labeled with a name and a date in Corvane's cramped hand. The door had no handle. You opened it with salt pressed to the seam, and it only answered for someone whose name was written in the ledgers.

Hers was. Somewhere. She'd never been allowed to see the entry.

Tonight she came down after midnight, carrying Vess wrapped in a blanket against her chest like contraband, because leaving the girl alone in her lodging had become unthinkable. The child slept against her collarbone, breathing in small, even sighs, one fist curled around the salt-white coin. Yara had tried three times to pry it loose for safekeeping. Three times the girl's fingers had tightened in her sleep, and Yara had stopped trying.

*You're getting soft,* she told herself. *Soft tasters don't survive.*

But the thought had no teeth. It hadn't since the girl arrived.

She pressed her palm—salt ground fine between skin and stone—to the door seam. The wall exhaled, a breath of air that tasted of old iron and something sweeter underneath, like fruit going bad in a locked room, and the door swung inward on darkness.

Inside, the scrying-glass filled an entire wall.

It was not mirror-glass. Mirrors showed you what was there. This showed you what was *true*, which was a different country entirely, and the guild guarded the difference jealously. The glass was older than the building around it, older than the guild hall, salvaged—so the rumor went—from a shipwreck that predated the kingdom's founding. Its surface never quite settled. Even now, standing before it in the dark, Yara watched slow shapes move beneath the silver like fish under ice.

There were rules. Corvane had drilled them into her during her first year, when she'd been promoted from taster to ledger-keeper and granted access to the deep rooms.

*Never scry yourself. Never scry the guild masters. Never scry the crown.*

Three rules, and tonight she intended to break the third.

She set Vess down gently on the bench along the far wall, tucked the blanket around the small body, and stood a moment watching the girl sleep. In the glass's faint light her face looked carved from candle-wax, smooth and unfinished, as if whoever made her had run out of time before adding the details. Except the eyes, when they opened—which they did now, suddenly, without any drowsy transition—were finished all the way down. Older than the face they sat in.

Vess looked at the glass. Then at Yara. Then back at the glass.

"No," Yara said quietly, in the trade-tongue, though she knew the girl didn't understand the words. "Stay there. Sleep."

The girl did not sleep. She sat up, coin in her fist, and watched with the patient attention of something that had been waiting a very long time to see this exact thing.

Yara turned to the wall of true-silver.

"Show me the king," she said.

Her voice sounded smaller than she wanted it to. The glass took the words anyway, swallowing them the way still water swallows a dropped stone, and the surface shivered. Silver rolled like fog. Shapes gathered.

At first it was only the king's bedchamber, seen from a corner near the ceiling, the way a spider might see it. Canopied bed, banked fire, the heavy curtains drawn against the winter dark. King Aldric lay on his back with his hands folded on his chest, and for one merciful moment he simply looked like a sick man—a fifty-year-old ruler worn thin by fevers, breathing shallowly, gray in the face.

Then the light shifted.

It came from nowhere Yara could identify. It moved across the bed like the shadow of a cloud crossing a field, and where it passed, the king's face *changed*. Not morphed, not melted—changed the way a portrait changes when you tilt it, when the painter's trick reveals a second face hidden in the first. The slack features firmed. The gray flesh took on color. The closed eyes opened, and they were not the king's eyes.

They were old.

That was the only word her mind would hold onto, and it held it uselessly, because *old* meant nothing at this scale. The eyes that opened in the king's face had been open when the mountains were soft. They had a patience in them that made Yara's knees go strange—not cruelty, cruelty would have been easier, cruelty was human—but the calm of something that had eaten so long it had forgotten hunger was an appetite. It simply ate, the way a river simply runs.

The thing sat up in the king's body. It moved well. Better than the king had moved in months—it wore him the way a skilled rider wears a horse, and Yara understood with a lurch that every stiff joint, every fever, every moment of royal frailty she'd witnessed at court had been theater, or perhaps adjustment, the thing learning its new fit.

It walked to the window. It looked out over the sleeping capital.

And it began, very softly, to eat.

Yara saw it happen and could not afterward describe how she saw it, because there was nothing visible—no light, no motion, no sound. But the glass told the truth, and the truth was this: the city below the window grew *thinner*. A street that had been there—she could see it, cobbles and lamplight and a drunk singing outside a tavern—simply became less certain, less *insisted-upon*, until the eye slid off it the way it slides off a word read too many times. The drunk's song faltered mid-verse. He stood in the street with his mouth open, trying to remember what came after the second line, and then he stopped trying, and wandered home to a bed he half-recognized, and the verse was gone from the world forever.

The thing at the window sighed. It was a satisfied sound. It was the sound Yara herself made over a perfect sauce, and that similarity was the most obscene thing she had ever perceived.

"It eats memories," she said aloud. Her voice cracked on the second word.

Behind her, Vess made a sound—a low, urgent note in the Root-Tongue, one of the few words Yara had begun to recognize. She thought it meant *see*. Or possibly *run*.

"I see," Yara whispered.

* * *

She stayed until the fourth bell, and what she learned in those hours rearranged the world the way an earthquake rearranges a room: everything in its familiar place, but nothing where you could trust it anymore.

Because once she saw the feeding, she started seeing it everywhere—and worse, she started seeing where it had *already been*.

She asked the glass for the city, wide view, and the glass gave her the capital from above, and she made herself look at it honestly. There were gaps. Not ruins—gaps. Districts where the rooftops continued but the streets between them had a faint grayness, a hesitation, like ink applied by a tired hand. She counted seven streets she could not name. Seven streets in a city she had navigated by heart since she was nine years old, and when she reached for their names her mind produced a smooth blankness, the particular blankness of a thing that had never existed.

Except it had. She could feel the absence the way you feel a missing tooth—tongue going again and again to the gap, expecting what isn't there.

She asked the glass for documents. This was harder; the true-silver preferred faces and places, but the guild had fed it enough paper over the centuries that it could manage. And there she found it laid bare, the quiet catastrophe, recorded in the guild's own copies of royal charters and parish rolls:

Names fading.

A deed from the Merrow district, forty years old, listing a landholder whose signature had degraded—not physically, the ink was intact, but the *name itself* had gone illegible in a way that made no sense, the letters present and unreadable, as if the concept of the man had been chewed away and only the spelling remained. A marriage register with a bride's name smudged into nothing while the groom's stood clear beside it. A muster roll from the border wars with an entire company struck through—not crossed out, *eroded*, eleven names worn to ghosts, and no record anywhere of whether those men had ever come home.

The realm was being edited. Slowly. Carefully. One memory, one name, one street at a time, and nobody noticed because the editing included the noticing. How do you mourn a street you cannot remember? How do you audit a theft when the thief steals the knowledge of what was stolen?

Yara sat down on the cold floor because her legs declined to continue being responsible for her.

*How long?* she asked the glass, and the glass, which did not answer questions so much as offer truths adjacent to them, showed her a sequence: the same charter, copied fresh every decade in the guild's records, and across two hundred years of copies the landholder's name decaying letter by letter, generation by generation, like a coastline eroding under a patient tide.

Two hundred years. The thing had been eating since before her grandmother's grandmother. Every wonder the guild traded—every borrowed name, every stolen year, every bottle of grief—had passed through hands that touched, somewhere upstream, this hunger. She thought of the inventory shelves, the neat labels, the tidy economy of impossible things, and felt the first cold thread of a suspicion she refused yet to pull: *where do all those traded things actually go?*

No. Not tonight. Tonight had already taken too much.

She looked back at the bed in the king's chamber. The thing had returned to it, folded the king's hands on his chest, arranged the slack gray mask of illness over its own alertness, and lain down to wait. For what? For more? For the realm to ripen?

As if it heard the question—as if the glass carried her attention to it the way a wire carries current—the thing's eyes opened in the dark of the royal bedchamber.

It looked directly into the scrying-glass.

Directly at her.

Yara's whole body went rigid, taster-still, the trained stillness that had saved her life a hundred times at the poison table, and she needed every hour of that training now because the eyes were *considering* her. They had the quality of eyes that had just found a new flavor in an old dish. The gaze held for three heartbeats, five, ten—

—and then the thing smiled with the king's mouth, a small, private, knowing smile, and closed its eyes, and the glass went dark from the inside out, silver collapsing to black like a pond freezing top-down.

Yara discovered she had been holding Vess's hand without deciding to. The girl stood beside her, having crossed the room silently, and her grip was surprisingly strong, and her ancient eyes were fixed on the dead glass with an expression Yara could finally, horribly, read across the barrier of language:

*Now it knows where we are.*

"No," Yara said. "No, it doesn't. It can't—" But even as she said it she was moving, snuffing the single candle she'd allowed herself, pulling the blanket around the girl's shoulders, listening to the archive above them with her whole body. The guild hall at night had a hundred sounds and she knew them all: settling timber, wind in the chimney flues, the bottled griefs on their shelves making their faint tidal murmur. All normal. All ordinary.

For now.

She climbed the stairs with Vess on her hip, past the green bottles, past the shelves of stolen years, and paused at the long table where the working ledgers lay open under their weights. Habit put her hand on the current volume—her own neat columns, the nightly record of poisons survived and symptoms logged, the king's entry from last night: *refused honeyed wine; did not taste it; pupils slow.*

Duty said: log tonight. Log what you saw. A ledger-keeper records; recording is armor; what is written down can be checked, cross-referenced, proven.

But her pen hovered over the page and would not fall.

Because a new thought had arrived, quiet and terrible, and it would not leave: *if the world can be edited—if names can fade off deeds and streets can be unwritten—then what protects a ledger?*

What protected the record of Yara Eriksen, food-taster, orphan, property of the Salt Guild, from being edited too?

She looked down at her own entries, hundreds of nights of careful ink, and for the first time in six years of keeping the guild's books, Yara Eriksen was afraid of what she might find written in them.

She dipped her pen. Her hand shook. She wrote a single line—*the king does not eat; the king is eaten*—and as the ink dried she noticed, at the bottom of tonight's page, a faint indentation in the paper. A ghost of writing, pressed through from some page that should not exist, in a hand that was almost, but not quite, her own.

She turned the page to find what had made it.

The next entry was dated three days from now.
