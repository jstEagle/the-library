## 8. Entries That Shouldn't Exist

The archive kept its own weather.

Yara had learned that years ago—the cellar beneath the Salt Guild's counting-house breathed like a living thing, cool and mineral-sweet, the air thick with brine and old paper. Candles guttered here without wind. Shadows pooled in the corners of the shelves as though the dark itself had been decanted into jars and left to settle. Tonight the weather felt wrong. She could not have said how. The lamps burned steady, the salt-dryers hummed their low eternal note, and still the hairs on her arms stood up like a dog's before a storm.

She told herself it was exhaustion. She had not slept properly since the girl arrived—since Vess, curled now on a pallet behind the false shelf in the far alcove, breathing the small even breaths of a child who had finally stopped being afraid long enough to sleep. Three nights Yara had smuggled her down here past Corvane's rounds. Three nights of lies stacked like coins, each one heavier than the last.

Tonight she would work. Work was the one prayer the guild permitted, and Yara had made an art of it.

The quarterly audit was due in six days, and if Corvane found discrepancies he would ask questions she could no longer afford to answer honestly. So she worked backward from the newest acquisitions, cross-referencing intake against payment, payment against collateral, collateral against the master index—a discipline so ingrained that her hands moved through the ledgers like a blind woman's through familiar rooms.

*One grief, bottled, amber glass, sold to Lady Maren of the Weeping Stair.* Paid in full: two years of the seller's life, transferred and verified. *One borrowed name, gentleman's quality, lightly worn.* Returned by the borrower; the original owner had died before reclaiming it, which meant the name now sat in limbo inventory, ownerless, faintly humming when you held the vial to your ear. *One first love, pressed and dried, condition fair.*

Yara recorded each in her own hand, in the cipher only ledger-keepers were taught, and told herself the small cold feeling in her chest was just the cellar.

It was nearly midnight when she reached the drawer that did not belong.

Every keeper knew the archive's grammar. Drawers ran A through Zed by seller's surname, then the numbered vaults for high-value trades, then the sealed cabinet for First Ledger material that no one below the rank of master touched. This drawer sat between *Halloway* and *Harrow*, where *Hanover* should have been—and there was no Hanover. There never had been. She had catalogued this wall herself, twice a year, for nine years.

The drawer slid out with a sound like a held breath released.

Inside: a single folio, bound in grey calfskin, unmarked. No guild seal. No intake stamp. No chain of custody, which was impossible—every object in the archive carried its history in wax and ink the way a tree carries rings. This book had simply *appeared*, the way a stone appears in a shoe.

Yara looked over her shoulder. The lamps burned. Vess slept. Somewhere above, the counting-house settled and creaked, Corvane's boots crossing the floor overhead in his nightly circuit—there, fading, gone.

She opened the folio.

The handwriting was hers.

Not similar. Not an imitation. It was her hand exactly—the slight forward lean of her letters, the way she crossed her *t*s with a single impatient stroke, the little hook she put on the tail of a seven that no writing-master had ever managed to beat out of her. She stared at it the way one stares at one's own face in a window at night: recognition arriving a half-second after wrongness.

The entries were dated. That was the second impossible thing. Ledger entries were dated at intake, always, because a trade without a date was a trade without proof, and the guild did not deal in the unprovable. But these dates ran forward. Tomorrow. The day after. And—

Three days from now.

Yara read the entry three times before the words would stay still on the page.

*Item: one death, natural causes, accelerated. Seller: Eriksen, Yara. Collateral received: full remainder of recorded lifespan, thirty-one years, four months, eleven days. Buyer: [entry sealed]. Witnessed by: —*

There was no witness line filled in. There was no buyer named. And there was a detail that turned her mouth dry as ash: the entry described her death the way the guild described everything, in flat inventory language, but it included particulars. *Subject expires in the drop-house on Ropewalk Lane, between the third and fourth bell of morning. Cause recorded as cardiac failure consistent with cumulative toxic exposure. Body recovered by guild enforcers within the hour.*

The drop-house on Ropewalk Lane. She knew it. Every taster knew it—it was where the guild took delivery of street-bought goods, a low room that smelled of tallow and wet wool, where she had gone perhaps forty times in her career. Between the third and fourth bell. That was the dead hour, the hour when even the rats slept.

Her hands were shaking. She set the folio down on the desk, carefully, the way she handled the poison vials at court—one tremor away from catastrophe—and pressed her palms flat against the wood until the shaking slowed.

Someone had written this. Someone with access to the archive, access to her cipher, and either a perfect copy of her handwriting or—

Or nothing. There was no *or*. Handwriting could be forged; she forged writs herself often enough to know. But the *particulars*. The guild's inventory language bent around truth like water around a stone; you could fake an entry, but faking the texture of a real one—the little redundancies, the verification marks, the way a genuine entry always smelled faintly of the salt used to fix the ink—was another matter entirely.

She lifted the page to her nose. Salt-fixed ink. Genuine.

*Between the third and fourth bell.*

A sound behind her—small, soft—and she spun, heart slamming, one hand already at the knife in her boot. Vess stood at the edge of the lamplight in her borrowed nightshirt, hair wild, eyes enormous and dark in her thin face. The girl said nothing. She never said anything in any tongue Yara knew, though sometimes at night Yara heard her murmuring to herself in that old rolling language, words that made Yara's scars ache like weather coming.

Vess was looking at the open folio.

"No," Yara said quietly. "Back to bed."

The girl didn't move. Her gaze stayed fixed on the page, and slowly her small face changed—not frightened, Yara realized. Grieving. The look of someone reading a letter from a friend who had not yet died.

"Vess." Yara crossed the space between them and knelt, taking the girl's cold hands. "It's just a book. Books lie all the time. I should know—I keep them lying for a living."

Vess looked at her then, and something passed between them that needed no language: the child squeezed her fingers once, hard, and shook her head. *No.* Not agreement. Refutation. *Books don't lie,* the shake said. *This one is telling the truth, and you know it.*

Then she turned and padded back to her pallet, and Yara was alone with the lamp and the folio and the sound of her own blood.

* * *

She did not sleep. Instead she did what she always did with a problem too large to hold: she broke it into pieces and audited the pieces.

Piece one: provenance. If the folio had appeared in the archive, someone had placed it there. The outer door required two keys—hers and Corvane's—turned together. The inner wards were keyed to the masters' seals. Nine years, and she had never once seen a breach. Which meant either the breach came from above, from the masters themselves, or—

Piece two: the other entries. She went back through the folio page by page, forcing herself to be systematic, because terror without method was just drowning with extra steps. There were eleven entries total. All in her hand. All dated forward, clustered across the next two weeks. And every one of them was about her.

*Entry: subject requests leave of absence from court duties. Granted.*

*Entry: subject discloses archive contents to unauthorized party, male, scribe's calluses, age approximately twenty-two.* — Yara read that one twice, and something hot crawled up her throat. She didn't know any scribe. She hadn't disclosed anything. Yet.

*Entry: subject attempts to remove First Ledger material from premises. Intercepted.*

*Entry: subject surrenders the child.*

That one she read until the letters blurred. *Surrenders the child.* Dated eight days out. As though it were already decided, already filed, already true—as though her whole future were a shipment moving through the guild's hands toward its destination, and these pages were merely the manifest.

She thought of Corvane's voice at their last meeting, mild and terrible: *Never read past your own entries, Yara. Some doors only open from the inside.* She had always taken it as a warning against curiosity. Now she understood it might have been a warning against exactly this—against finding out that somewhere in the deep arithmetic of the guild, her life had already been entered, balanced, and closed.

Piece three: the handwriting. She fetched her own current ledger and set it beside the folio, comparing stroke by stroke under the magnifying lens she used for detecting forgery. Identical. Not close. Identical—including a flaw she had never shown anyone: a tiny hesitation-ink-blot she made on capital *E*s when writing quickly, a stutter of the pen she'd tried for years to train out of herself. It was there. On every capital E. *Eriksen, Yara.*

Which meant the entries had been written by her hand, or by something that knew her hand better than she did.

Or—her mind supplied the thought with horrible gentleness—by her. By some future version of her, writing backward into the books the way the guild wrote everything: in advance, in certainty, in salt.

She laughed. It came out wrong, a dry cracked sound in the silent cellar, and she clapped her hand over her mouth and looked toward the alcove. Vess slept on. Above, the counting-house was quiet. The world continued, indifferent, while Yara Eriksen sat in a pool of lamplight reading the date of her own death.

*Thirty-one years, four months, eleven days.* She did the arithmetic without wanting to. It matched. Of course it matched—it was the exact figure from her own intake record, the remainder of her recorded lifespan, the number the guild had assigned her the day they bought her debts and claimed her name. Whoever wrote the entry had simply taken her balance and marked it *withdrawn.*

The guild dealt in years of life. She had known that since her first week, had watched vials of shimmering decades change hands between nobles who wanted to see their grandchildren wed. She had never once wondered, in nine years, whether her own years might be a commodity like any other—whether somewhere a buyer might already hold a claim on them, waiting only for the paperwork to clear.

*Buyer: [entry sealed].*

Sealed entries required a master's seal. Only five men in the city could break one, and every one of them outranked her the way a mountain outranks a stone.

* * *

Dawn found her still at the desk, the folio closed beneath her palms as if she could pin it to the world, keep it from doing whatever it intended to do. The lamps had burned down to stubs. Through the high slit of a window near the ceiling, the darkness was going the grey-green of old bronze.

She had decided things in the night, the way decisions come in the small hours—quietly, completely, without negotiation.

First: she would not surrender Vess. Whatever the eighth-day entry said, whatever certainty it wore, she would tear that page out with her teeth before she let enforcers carry the girl to *processing.* The guild had made her invisible, and invisibility had uses. She had hidden contraband griefs and stolen names under the masters' noses for nine years. She could hide a child.

Second: she would find out who had written the folio. There were only so many hands that touched the archive. Corvane. The five masters. The intake clerks, none of whom could forge her hand. And whoever had keyed the wards to admit a book that had never been checked in. She would start with the ward-records—they were kept in the antechamber, and Corvane reviewed them monthly, which meant they existed, which meant they could be read.

Third—

Third, she admitted, staring at the folio's grey cover, that she was afraid in a way she had not been since she was seven years old and an orphanage matron had explained, kindly, what happened to children nobody claimed. She had built her whole self out of controlled numbness, out of the taster's discipline: hold the poison on your tongue, count to ten, swallow or spit, never let your face tell the story. She had thought herself immune to fear the way she was immune to arsenic.

But arsenic immunity fails eventually too. Every taster knew that. The doses accumulated. The body kept its own ledger, and one day the account came due.

She opened the folio one last time, to the death-entry, and forced herself to read it as an auditor rather than as its subject. Look for the flaw. Every forgery had one; every lie had a seam.

*Subject expires in the drop-house on Ropewalk Lane, between the third and fourth bell of morning.*

She read it again. And again. And on the fourth reading, cold spreading through her chest like groundwater rising, she saw it.

The entry was dated three days from now. But the drop-house on Ropewalk Lane had burned down eleven months ago. She had tasted there a dozen times; she had walked past its black shell in the spring. The guild had never rebuilt it. There was no drop-house on Ropewalk Lane anymore—no room, no tallow smell, no third and fourth bell.

So either the entry was a forgery after all, sloppy in its details—

—or it was accurate, and the world it described was one in which the drop-house still stood, and somewhere between now and three days hence, the difference would be erased. Rebuilt. Restored. Written back in.

Like a road that vanished from the maps. Like a name fading from a document. Like the king eating dish after dish and tasting nothing at all.

Yara sat very still in the grey light, holding the folio, understanding at last that this was not a prophecy and not a threat. It was a record. The guild's records did not predict the future. They *fixed* it. And something, somewhere, had already fixed hers—had entered her death into the books with her own hand, in her own cipher, salt-fixed and witnessed by no one, waiting only for the world to catch up to the page.

Unless the page could be caught first.

She was still sitting there, dawn strengthening along the shelf-tops, when she heard the sound that froze her worse than any entry could: footsteps on the cellar stair. Two sets. Heavy, unhurried, descending.

Corvane's tread she knew like her own heartbeat. The second set she did not know at all—and no one, in nine years, had ever come down those stairs unannounced except to collect.

Yara closed the folio, slid it into the drawer, pushed the drawer home, and moved between the desk and the alcove where Vess slept—just as the lamp at the bottom of the stairs swung its light across the first face, and she saw that it wore a master's mask of white salt, and that it was looking directly at her.
