## 11. Court of Ash

The palace smelled different now.

Yara noticed it before she passed the outer gate, before the guards waved her through with the bored familiarity of men who had forgotten her face a hundred times and would forget it a hundred more. It was a smell like a room after a fire that had been put out too late—char beneath the lilies, ash under the beeswax. The courtiers' perfumes lay over it in bright, futile layers, orange blossom and civet and something new from the southern trade, but underneath all of it ran that gray thread of smoke-that-was-not-smoke.

She had tasted poison for eleven years. She knew what it was to carry death in a body and smile around it. The palace was doing the same thing now, she thought. Carrying something dead at its center and perfuming the air above it.

*You are a taster,* she told herself as she crossed the marble of the Gate of Hours. *Taste everything. Trust nothing.*

She had left Vess locked in the cellar archive with three days of bread, a jug of well water, and instructions written twice—once in Common, once in the halting Root-Tongue characters Yara was only beginning to shape correctly. The girl had watched her go without expression, salt-white coin clenched in one small fist, and Yara had carried the image of her up out of the dark like a coal held in cupped hands. If the guild came while she was away—and they would come; Corvane's summons had been a warning shot, not the war—the false entry she'd built would have to hold. A taster who lied to the Salt Guild lasted about as long as a king who ate carelessly. She was doing both now.

*One day at a time,* she thought. *One dish at a time.*

* * *

The morning presentation was fish.

It arrived on a platter of hammered silver, six river trout glazed with honey and bitter orange, arranged nose-to-tail in a curve like a broken necklace. The Master of Kitchens stood sweating behind it. The Chamberlain announced it with the flat ceremony of a man reciting words whose meanings had been sanded off. And at the high table, beneath the banners of the Ninefold Crown, sat the king.

Yara took her place at the tasting board—a low table set always within the king's sightline, because a taster who died invisibly was no use to anyone—and made herself look at him the way she looked at an unfamiliar dish: thoroughly, coldly, part by part.

He was dressed beautifully. That was the first wrongness. Aldric IV had never cared for dress; the tailors despaired of him; he wore state robes the way a man wears a coat someone else is holding. Today every button was fastened, every chain laid true, and his hair—his hair had been going gray at the temples for two years, a fact the portraitists were paid to soften—was dark again. All of it. Uniformly dark, like paint.

His hands moved correctly through the rituals of eating. His face performed the correct expressions. But watching him was like watching a puppet operated by someone who had studied puppetry from a book: each motion arrived a half-breath after it should, each smile assembled itself rather than appearing.

And he did not taste.

Yara had watched this man eat for eleven years. She knew the small liturgy of his pleasure—the pause over the first bite, the half-closed eyes, the grunt he gave for the kitchen's benefit when something pleased him. This morning he ate the trout the way a man fills out a form. Chew, swallow, next. When the honey-glaze—Master Ollen's pride, four hours of reduction—touched his tongue, nothing moved in his face at all. Not displeasure. Nothing. As if flavor were a language he had heard described but never learned.

*Food passes his lips like ash.* She wrote it later in the margin of her poison ledger, in the cramped cipher she used for observations that could hang her: *K. eats. K. does not taste. Third day confirmed. Eyes: pupils slow. Left hand knows the fork; right hand forgets it between courses. He laughed at Lord Cassel's joke one beat too late and looked at the jester afterward as if checking whether laughing had been correct.*

Beside those entries, in a column she had begun only that week, she logged the other things. The road east of the city that appeared on no map younger than five years. The seamstress in the Lower Ward who had stopped mid-sentence Tuesday and could not remember the name of her own daughter, only that she loved her. The way the palace archives had begun returning requests marked *no record found* for documents everyone swore existed.

The realm was being eaten from its edges inward. And the king's mouth was the door.

"Yara Eriksen."

She looked up. The Chamberlain stood over her tasting board, and behind him, flanked by two household guards, was a woman she had never seen before—or rather, a woman she had seen a thousand times and never once *seen*, because the Queen of Cael Saren did not come to the tasting board. Queens did not descend to poison and sweat. Queens received their food already blessed, already safe, already made real by other people's deaths.

"The Queen desires your attendance," the Chamberlain said. "At her convenience."

Her convenience, in practice, meant now. Yara rose, wiped her hands on the cloth every taster carried, and followed.

* * *

The queen's solar occupied the tower's south face, all pale stone and long light, and it smelled of nothing at all—which was, Yara realized with a small cold shock, the strangest thing yet. Every room in the palace had a smell. This one had been scoured of scent the way a ledger page is scraped clean for reuse.

Queen Isolde stood at the window with her back turned. Up close, without the forty feet of throne dais between them, she was smaller than her portraits and younger than her years, a woman built out of northern stock and southern patience. Her ladies had been dismissed. That, too, was unprecedented.

"You are the taster," the queen said, still facing the glass. "Yara Eriksen."

"Yes, Majesty."

"How long have you served at my husband's table?"

"Eleven years, Majesty."

"Eleven years." The queen turned. Her face was composed the way a battlefield is composed after the fighting—order imposed over ruin. "Then you will know. You will be able to tell me." She crossed the room with quick, unregal steps and took Yara's wrist—not a queen's gesture at all, a frightened woman's gesture—and her grip was stronger than her frame suggested. "Our wedding day. Tell me about our wedding day."

"Majesty?"

"What season was it. What did I wear. What—" Her voice snagged, caught, tore slightly at the seam. "What was the weather. Everyone says it rained. They say it rained and that we laughed about it. But I remember—" She stopped. Drew breath. Started again from somewhere flatter. "I remember standing in the Great Hall. I remember being happy. I remember being happy the way you remember a sum is correct. Do you understand me? I know the answer. I cannot find the working."

Yara understood. Gods help her, she understood completely. It was how she remembered her own mother's face: she knew there had been one, she knew it had mattered, and when she reached for it she found a smooth blank space shaped exactly like a memory, like a coin rubbed featureless by handling.

"It rained," Yara said quietly. "It rained all morning and cleared at noon, and the whole city came out anyway, and they said the wet streets shone like silver so that the procession walked on light. There are songs about it. The Silver Wedding, they call it."

Something moved through the queen's face—hope arriving and then, horribly, failing to find anywhere to land. "Yes," she whispered. "That's—it sounds true. It sounds *true*, do you see? But hearing it is not the same as—" She released Yara's wrist and stepped back, and the queen reassembled herself piece by piece, spine first, chin last. "I am told I wept at the vows. I am told my father wept worse. I look at the portraits and I feel devotion. I feel it very strongly." Her eyes fixed on Yara with terrible directness. "Devotion is not remembering. Devotion is what they give you instead."

*They.* Yara's blood went thin and cold. "Majesty, I don't—"

"I know what I sound like." The queen laughed, short and bloodless. "The physicians say grief. Grief for what? I have a husband. I see him daily. He is kind to me in the manner of a man reading kindness from instructions." She moved to the window again, and below them the capital spread out in its autumn smoke, ten thousand roofs, and Yara watched the queen look at her own city the way a woman looks at a stranger wearing her sister's coat. "Three nights ago I woke and could not recall the name of the woman who taught me to ride. Yesterday I found my wedding letters and read them like another person's mail. The words were mine. The love in them was mine. I felt it like warmth off a fire in a house I've never entered." She turned. "You taste his food. You watch him more closely than any living soul. So I am asking you, taster, and I will deny asking if you ever repeat it: is my husband ill?"

Every instinct Yara owned screamed at her to give the safe answer. *The king enjoys robust health, Majesty. Long life to him.* Eleven years of survival lived inside that sentence.

But the queen was standing in a scoured room holding a featureless coin where her wedding should be, and asking, and *denying she would ask*, which meant some last unedited corner of her knew that asking was dangerous—that the thing she needed protection from was the same thing that had taken her memories, and that it sat on a throne downstairs wearing her husband's darkened hair.

"He is changed," Yara said. Each word cost her something and she spent it anyway. "I don't know what ails him. But it began at midsummer, and it is getting worse, and it is not an illness of the body."

The queen closed her eyes. When she opened them they were wet and steady. "Thank you," she said. "That is the first honest thing anyone has said to me in a month. Perhaps longer. I wouldn't know how much longer." She lifted her chin, and the queen returned like armor closing. "You will speak of this to no one."

"No one, Majesty."

"And you will keep tasting." Something flickered under the formality then, quickly buried—fear, or its cousin. "Keep tasting, taster. Whatever else changes at that table, keep tasting."

* * *

The summons came at dusk, and it did not come through the Chamberlain.

A serving girl Yara had never seen brought it to the tasters' quarters: a folded card of heavy cream stock, sealed with black wax, bearing no crest. Inside, in a fine clerk's hand:

*His Majesty desires the taster's judgment upon a private supper. Nine o'clock. The old dining room. Come alone.*

The old dining room. Yara stood holding the card and felt the temperature of the words. The old dining room was in the west range, in the wing built by Aldric's grandfather, disused for thirty years. No kitchens serviced it. No suppers happened there.

Private. Alone. Nine o'clock.

Corvane's warning from the archive rang in her skull—*the guild sees everything, little keeper, and what the guild sees, others pay to see*—but this was not the guild's style. The guild summoned through handlers, through marks on doors, through children with coins. This was the palace speaking in its own voice. Or the thing that wore the palace.

She considered refusing. She catalogued the refusal and its consequences in the ledger of her mind and found the column short: a taster who declined the king's table ended her career at best. And besides—if it wanted her, hiding would only delay it, and delay would spend time she did not have. Vess was hidden. The ledgers were warded. Tam—no. Not Tam. She had no one else. That was precisely why it could summon her alone and expect obedience.

She dressed carefully. Plain wool, serviceable boots, hair bound back. Nothing that said ambition, nothing that said fear. She ate a full meal beforehand—old taster's discipline, never meet danger hungry—and she slipped her smallest knife into her sleeve, knowing even as she did it that a knife would matter exactly as much against this as a spoon.

At nine o'clock she presented herself at the old dining room's doors.

* * *

They opened before she knocked.

Candles burned everywhere—hundreds of them, a field of small flames down the length of a room built for feasts of fifty, and the long table was set for two. Two places, facing one another, midway along all that emptiness. At the far place sat the king.

He had dismissed the servants. He rose when she entered, which kings did not do for tasters, and gestured to the near chair, and his voice—Aldric's voice, warm and slightly rough, a voice she had heard bless harvests and declare wars—said, "Mistress Eriksen. Thank you for coming. Sit. Please."

She sat. The distance between the chairs was four feet. She measured it the way she measured everything now: in escape routes and seconds.

"I'm told you have served me eleven years," the king said. He poured wine himself, unhurried, the dark hair falling perfectly across the perfect forehead. "Eleven years of keeping my body safe. That is a rare devotion. I wanted to thank you properly. A private supper. A dish of my own choosing." He smiled, and the smile fit his face the way a mask fits a face—edges aligned, nothing behind it moving. "I hope you're hungry."

The covered dish between them steamed gently. He lifted the lid with both hands, tenderly, a father unveiling a cradle.

Underneath was a plain bowl of stew. Brown broth, root vegetables, dark meat, a scatter of barley. Peasant food. The kind of thing sold in the Lower Ward from carts, the kind of thing Yara had eaten as a starving child in the years before the guild bought her debts.

"Taste," the king said softly.

She picked up the spoon. Her hand was steady; eleven years of training saw to that. She took a mouthful of broth, held it on her tongue, and her trained palate went to work automatically—beef, no; older than beef, mutton? No. The meat tasted like nothing she had a word for. Not gamey, not sweet, not foul. It tasted the way the queen's solar smelled: like a room with the memory scrubbed out of it. The vegetables were carrots and parsnips and a third root she recognized and could not name, a root she was certain she had eaten a hundred times in childhood, a root whose name was simply *not there*, a hole in her tongue shaped like a word.

The broth was delicious. That was the horror of it. It was the most delicious thing she had ever tasted, and every flavor in it pointed backward at something missing, the way a scar points at the wound.

Across the table, the king had not touched his own bowl. He was watching her eat with an attention that had nothing human in its patience, and slowly, sweetly, in Aldric's gentle voice, he asked:

"Do you recognize it?"

Yara swallowed. Set down the spoon. Kept her face arranged in the mild, professional blankness that had kept her alive through three succession crises and a poisoning attempt by her own predecessor.

"A hearty stew, Majesty. Well-made. The meat is—" She paused, honestly, because the pause was true. "Forgive me. The meat is unfamiliar. An old recipe, perhaps. Regional."

"Regional," the king repeated, tasting the word. He leaned forward into the candlelight, and for just a moment the light fell wrong into his eyes—not deep, the way eyes are deep, but *far*, the way a well is far, a depth with no bottom that reflects nothing back. "It is a very old recipe. Older than regions. My grandmother's grandmother kept it, and hers before her, all the way back to the founding. Every ingredient in it was grown in this valley, from seeds this soil has forgotten how to make. It is the taste of this kingdom, mistress taster. Its true taste. And do you know—" he picked up his spoon, stirred the stew without eating, "—no one alive can name the third root anymore. I checked. I checked *everywhere*. Cooks, farmers, herbals, archives. The knowledge is simply gone. Eaten, if you like. And yet here it is, in the bowl, still growing in a garden that no longer exists, in a valley that remembers what it is even though no one in it does."

He looked up at her, and Aldric's kind face smiled, and beneath the smile something vast adjusted its weight.

"You taste for a living," he said. "So tell me, Yara Eriksen. When you put that spoon in your mouth—what was it you were actually tasting?"

The candles burned. Four feet of air lay between them. Somewhere in the cellar archive across the city, a mute girl slept clutching a salt-white coin, and Yara understood suddenly, with total clarity, that this supper was not a courtesy and not a test of her palate.

It was a test of whether she would lie well enough.

She met the far, bottomless eyes, and thought of the queen's featureless wedding, and chose her answer the way she chose which poison to survive: precisely, and with her heart hammering like a fist on a locked door.

"Hunger, Majesty," she said. "It tastes like hunger. Whoever cooked it was very hungry indeed."

For a long moment the thing wearing her king said nothing at all. Then it laughed—Aldric's laugh, warm and rough and entirely wrong—and pushed the stew aside.

"Well answered," it said. "We'll dine again soon, mistress taster. I find I'm developing quite an appetite for your company."

And as she bowed and backed toward the doors, it added, pleasantly, in the voice of a man remembering a small errand:

"Oh—and give my regards to the archivist. Corvane, isn't it? Do tell him the guild's ledgers are overdue for inspection. I've let the arrangement run unexamined for such a long time. One does hate to seem neglectful of one's oldest... *partnerships*."

The doors closed behind her. In the corridor, Yara discovered her hands were shaking—and that she was already, helplessly, composing the entry: *It knows about the ledgers. It knows about Corvane. It knows I lied.*

And beneath it, in cipher, the sentence she would spend the rest of the night staring at:

*It didn't ask about the child. Which means it doesn't need to ask. Which means it already knows where she is.*
