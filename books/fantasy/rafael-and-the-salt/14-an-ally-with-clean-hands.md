## 14. An Ally With Clean Hands

The scribe's name was Tam, and he was going to get himself killed.

Yara had been watching him for six days before she admitted it to herself. He sat at the third desk from the window in the Chancery of Records, the one with the warped leg that rocked when he leaned too far into his work, and every afternoon between the bells of Nones and Vespers he did something no scribe in the palace had done in living memory: he checked his own work against the past.

She saw it first as a small habit, the kind of thing a meticulous young man does to avoid a whipping—flipping back through a register to confirm a date, running his finger down a column twice. But then she noticed what he checked *against*. The palace kept its records in triplicate: the working copy, the fair copy for the chancery shelves, and the sealed archive beneath the Salt Vault where nothing was ever meant to be read again. When Tam found a discrepancy—a harvest figure that differed between copies, a funeral date that appeared in one ledger and not another—he didn't correct it. Correcting was what any obedient scribe would do. Instead he copied both versions onto scraps of vellum and hid them inside the spine of a psalter so battered no one had opened it in years.

He was building an archive of errors. A private museum of things the world had forgotten on purpose.

Yara knew what that was because she kept one herself.

* * *

She approached him on the seventh day, in the rain, under the pretense of official business.

The taster's office did have business with the chancery—it was one of her quiet duties, confirming in writing which dishes had been cleared for the royal table, so that if a noble died of something other than poison, the paperwork would already be in order. She carried the confirmation roll herself rather than sending a page, because pages talked, and because she wanted to see his hands.

Hands told you everything about a scribe. Tam's were ink-stained to the second knuckle, with the callus of a man who cut his own quills rather than wait for the chancery's blunt stock ones. There was a burn scar across the heel of his right palm—wax, probably, from a careless moment over a sealing candle—and his nails were bitten short in the way of someone who thought better when his mouth was occupied.

He looked up when she set the roll on his desk, and she watched him do the arithmetic everyone did when they saw her: *food-taster, guild-collateral, invisible.* His eyes touched her face and slid off, polite and incurious, the way you look past a chair.

"Confirmation for the feast of Saint Aldric," she said. "Three courses cleared, one withheld."

"The boar." He said it without looking up, unsealing the roll. "Word is the king sent it back untouched last night."

Yara kept her face still. "Kings are entitled to their appetites."

"Mm." He dipped his quill, signed, dated. Then, as she reached for the roll, he added—quietly, not looking at her, in the tone of a man testing ice before stepping onto it—"Though it's strange. Last month he sent back the honeyed lamb. And the week before, the river eels. My grandmother always said a man's palate goes before his teeth do."

"Your grandmother sounds wise."

"She also said the roads out by Miller's Ford used to run through the orchards, not around them." Now he looked up, and his eyes were not polite anymore. They were careful, and underneath the care, afraid. "Except there aren't orchards at Miller's Ford. Never have been. I asked my mother. She laughed at me like I'd said the moon was made of cheese." He turned his hand over on the desk, palm up, the wax scar pale against his skin. "But I remember them. I can still smell them. Blossom and rotting windfalls. So either I'm mad, mistress taster, or something is eating the world a bite at a time. And I've been wondering for about two months now which it is."

The rain ticked against the window. Somewhere down the row of desks, another scribe coughed.

Yara thought about Corvane's voice in the cellar archive three nights ago, flat as slate: *Bring me the child, Yara. The guild will process her. That is not a request.* She thought about the lie she had told—the first lie of her career, delivered smoothly, without a tremor, and how it had felt less like betrayal than like finally setting down a weight she hadn't known she was carrying. She thought about Vess, asleep just now in the false alcove behind the shelving for the tax rolls, curled around the salt-white coin like it was warm.

*You are either very brave or very stupid,* Corvane had said once, years ago, when she'd asked her first forbidden question about the ledgers.

Both, she'd answered. It's the only way anything gets done.

"I don't know which it is either," she said to Tam. "But I keep notes."

His breath went out of him slowly, like a man who'd been holding it for two months.

* * *

They met that night in the disused counting room above the old granary, a space that smelled of dust and mouse droppings and, faintly, of the salt that had passed through the granary decades ago and never quite left the walls. Yara chose it because it had one door, two windows, and no history in the guild's books—which meant, as far as the ledgers were concerned, it did not exist. Unrecorded ground. She was learning to think in those terms now, the way a hunted animal learns to think in terms of shadow.

Tam arrived twenty minutes late, soaked, and carrying the psalter.

"You followed me," he said, breathless, standing in the doorway with rain dripping off his nose. "After the chancery. Tuesday. You followed me home."

"Yes."

"Why?"

"Because you hid your notes in a book instead of burning them." She lit the lamp on the counting table. Its light caught the salt ghosting the walls and turned it briefly silver. "A man who burns his notes has decided to be safe. A man who hides them has decided to be ready. I needed to know which one you were before I put my neck next to yours."

"And which am I?"

"That depends on what you do in the next hour."

He came in and shut the door behind him, and she saw him take the room in the way she took in rooms—one door, two windows, sightlines, exits. Not a trained man. Just a careful one, self-taught, the way she had been. Something in her chest loosened a fraction, the way a knot loosens when you stop pulling at it.

He set the psalter on the table and opened its broken spine, and out came the scraps of vellum—dozens of them, thin as onion skin, covered in his small precise hand. He laid them out in rows with the unconscious reverence of a priest laying out relics.

"Here." His finger stopped on the first scrap. "Harvest tallies, south granaries, eight years back. The working copy says four thousand bushels. The fair copy says thirty-one hundred. Same year, same granary, same clerk's initials on both. Nine hundred bushels of grain that grew and then didn't."

"Or were remembered differently."

"That's what I thought at first—that someone fudged the numbers to skim. But skimming leaves a trail. Someone pockets the difference, buys something, the money shows up somewhere. This doesn't. The nine hundred bushels didn't go anywhere. They just—" he made a small gesture, fingers opening like a bird released, "—stopped having happened. And it's not only numbers. Look."

Scrap after scrap. A wedding recorded in the parish rolls of Saint Ferren's that appeared in no couple's family papers. A bridge toll listed in the exchequer accounts for a bridge that stood now over dry ground, the river having moved—or having been moved in the record. A master mason named Osk who signed the cathedral's foundation stone and whose name appeared nowhere else in forty years of guild rolls, as though a man could build a cathedral and then be unwritten from the doing of it.

"Names are worst," Tam said quietly. "Numbers you can argue with. But there was a woman who sold chestnuts outside the chancery when I was a boy. Burn scar on her left arm, sang while she worked, off-key. I could draw her for you today. And when I asked after her—she'd have been old by now, but someone would remember—no one did. Not the guards, not the other vendors, not my own mother, who bought from her every market day for years." He looked up. "I started writing people down after that. Everyone. Every face I see more than twice, I write down their name and some detail, and I hide it, because I have this—" his laugh was short and had no humor in it, "—this horrible feeling that one morning I'll read my own list and find half the names gone from everyone's mouths but mine. And then I'll start doubting myself. And then I'll stop writing. And then I'll be gone too."

The lamp flame bent in a draft. Yara found that her hands had gone cold on the table's edge.

Because this was the thing she had seen through the scrying-glass, the truth she had swallowed alone for weeks now, unable to say it aloud even to herself except in the dark: the roads going crooked, the documents fading, the city's edges graying like ink left in rain. The thing wearing the crown was eating, and it ate memory, and Tam—clean-handed, careful, terrified Tam—had noticed the crumbs.

"You're not mad," she said.

"No?"

"No. And you're not wrong. And if you keep asking questions in the chancery, in daylight, where the wrong person can watch you ask them, you will die badly and quickly, and your notes will burn, and it won't matter that you were right." She pulled out the chair opposite him and sat, and made herself hold his eyes. "So. Which are you? Safe, or ready?"

He was silent a long moment. Rain ran down the windows. Then he gathered the scraps of vellum back into the psalter's spine, carefully, all of them, and held the book against his chest like a shield.

"What do you need me to do?" he said.

* * *

What she needed, it turned out, was exactly what he had already been doing—only aimed.

"The sealed archive under the Salt Vault," she told him, on their third night, drawing the layout from memory on the dusty tabletop: corridors, doors, the stair down. "It holds the triplicate copies of everything the palace has ever recorded. Officially, no one reads it. Unofficially, certain entries get amended there—quietly, at night, by clerks who don't sign their work. I need to know if amendments have been made to any record touching the king. His health, his meals, his household, his movements. Especially the last two years."

"You want me to steal from the sealed archive."

"I want you to *compare* the sealed archive. Stealing implies taking something away. I want you to leave everything exactly where it is and remember what it says."

"And if I'm caught down there?"

"Then you're a scribe who got lost looking for a misfiled roll, and you'll be fined a week's wages, and that will be the end of it—if they believe you. If they don't believe you—" She stopped. There was no kind version of the rest of that sentence.

Tam studied the map she'd drawn. "You're guild," he said suddenly. It wasn't a question. "I've seen the mark on coin-stamps come through the exchequer. Salt-white, the spiral-and-shard. You carry their seal; I've seen the impression on your confirmation rolls. So why are you telling me the archive's secrets? Why risk your brotherhood for a chestnut-seller's worth of proof?"

The question sat between them in the lamplight, and Yara understood that this was the true test—not his courage, hers. Trust was a door you opened knowing you couldn't close it again. Corvane's face rose in her mind, slate-flat and tired. *The guild is older than the kingdom, girl. The kingdom is a tenant. We are the house.*

"The guild keeps ledgers," she said slowly. "Of trades. Things that shouldn't be tradable—grief, years, names. I keep those ledgers. I have kept them for eleven years, and I believed we were a dam against something worse. A necessary evil holding a line." Her jaw tightened. "Two months ago a child came to my door speaking a language older than any kingdom, carrying our mark on a coin, and since then I have learned that the line we're holding may be the thing's leash, not its cage. That we might not be the dam." She heard her own voice drop. "We might be the pipe."

Tam didn't flinch, didn't scoff, didn't reach for the rational explanations a sensible man would grab at. He just nodded slowly, the way a man nods when a tooth finally breaks and the pain at least has a shape.

"My grandmother," he said, "used to say the king's grandfather wasn't the same man either. Everyone called him by the right name. But she swore the eyes changed." He rubbed the wax scar on his palm. "I thought that was old-woman talk. I don't anymore, do I."

"No."

"All right." He rolled up the map and tucked it into his shirt. "The sealed archive. Two nights from now, after the changing of the vault watch—I've learned the rotation; the second watch drinks. Give me three days."

"Two."

"Two and a half. I'm careful, not fast."

Despite everything—the child asleep in her cellar, the death-entry waiting in her own ledger with her name on it, the crowned thing wearing her king like a coat—Yara felt the corner of her mouth move. "Two and a half, then."

He paused at the door on his way out, rain starting again beyond the glass, and looked back at her with an expression she couldn't entirely read—something open and unguarded that she realized, with a small internal jolt, she hadn't been on the receiving end of in eleven years. People looked *through* a taster. People looked *at* a ledger-keeper with fear or greed. No one looked at her like she was a person who might be worth worrying about.

"Eat something tonight," he said. "You look like you forget to."

And then he was gone into the rain, and Yara stood alone in the salt-ghosted room, absurdly, unforgivably warm.

* * *

He was good. Better than good. On the fifth night he brought her the comparison, written in cipher of his own devising—simple substitution, but keyed to a psalm verse, which was cleverer than anything the chancery taught—and she decoded it by lamp-light in the counting room while he watched her hands and pretended not to.

"Amendments," he reported. "Forty-one touching the king's household in the last two years. Meals struck from the record—dozens of them, whole feasts that officially never occurred. Physicians' visits removed. And here's the one that turned my stomach." He tapped the page. "The queen's household accounts. There was a jeweler's commission, seven years ago, a wedding anniversary gift—a locket with a miniature portrait inside. The commission exists in the working copy. In the sealed copy, the page is blank. Not crossed out. *Blank*, like the ink was never there. And when I pulled the jeweler's own guild records, he remembers making a locket for the queen, but not what was in it. He thinks maybe pearls."

"A portrait," Yara said quietly. "Of the king. Before."

"Before what?"

Before the thing wore him. Before his eyes went wrong in small ways. Before food began passing his lips like ash. She folded the cipher page closed. "That's what I intend to find out. And there's something else you should see. Not a record. A person."

His eyebrows went up. "Here?"

"In the archive. Hidden in a false entry I built for her. She's—" Yara searched for words that wouldn't sound mad and gave up, because lying to this man felt suddenly worse than any danger. "She's a child. She speaks a language that predates the kingdom. She remembers things that haven't happened yet and places that no longer exist, and when she says certain words aloud, things come *back*. A street. A song. I've watched it happen, Tam. She spoke a word in the Root-Tongue last week and the fountain in Chandler's Square started flowing again after three years dry, and every person in that square remembered water being there, and no one questioned it, because remembering is easier than doubting."

The silence stretched. Tam looked at his own hands, the ink stains, the wax scar, as if checking they were still real.

"Show me," he said.

* * *

Vess liked him immediately, which unsettled Yara more than suspicion would have. The child emerged from the false alcove blinking in the lamplight, took one look at Tam, and walked over and patted his knee twice—the solemn, deliberate gesture she used for things she deemed *true*. Then she held up her salt-white coin and showed him the spiral-and-shard.

Tam crouched to her level. "Hello," he said gravely. "I'm Tam. I write things down so they can't be forgotten."

Vess considered this. Then she pointed at the psalter clutched under his arm, and at him, and said a short phrase in the Root-Tongue—two syllables, soft, round—and though Yara had been studying the old script for weeks and could parse perhaps one word in five, she caught this one, because it was the word Vess used most often, the word that seemed to sit at the center of everything:

*Remember.*

Tam's throat moved. "Yes," he said. "Exactly that."

And Yara, standing in the doorway of the false entry she had built to hide a soul in a jar of paper, felt the dangerous warmth spread through her chest again and did not fight it. Trust was a wound that healed into strength, her mother might have said, if she had ever had a mother who said things. All she had was a guild that owned her name and a child who was teaching her a language that cost memories, and a scribe with clean hands who had just knelt in the dust of a forbidden archive and promised a mute girl he would remember.

It was nearly enough to make her feel brave.

It was the bell of Matins, gray-blue and cold, when Tam climbed the archive stair to leave—and stopped dead at the top, his hand shooting out to catch Yara's arm.

Below them, in the reading room they had just left, lamplight moved where no lamp should be. Voices—two, low, professional. And then the scrape of a shelf being searched, methodical, shelf by shelf, moving toward the tax rolls.

Toward the false entry.

Toward Vess.

Corvane's voice, flat as slate, cut the dark: "Guild business, keeper. Open the alcove, or I open it for you."
