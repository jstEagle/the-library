## 15. The Price of Immunity

The first sign was the wine.

Yara knew it before she touched it—knew it the way a sailor knows weather in the ache of old bones. The goblet sat at the king's left hand, and the crowned thing wearing him lifted it with fingers that moved a half-beat too smoothly, and Yara stepped forward as she always did, and drank.

Almond. Bitter beneath the honey and the black cherry of a good Vellorine red. Cyanide dressed in courtship, dosed by someone who understood subtlety—which narrowed her suspects to perhaps six nobles and one bishop—and she stood there with the taste blooming on her tongue like a cold flower opening, and she waited for her blood to do what her blood had done ten thousand times before: turn aside, shrug, refuse.

It didn't.

The tremor started in her hands first. Small, private, hidden against the folds of her serving-skirt. Then a ringing in her ears like a struck bell that would not finish dying. The hall's candlelight smeared sideways. She counted—one, two, three, four—anchoring herself to numbers because numbers had never lied to her the way flesh could, and on five she bowed to the king and said, "It is safe, majesty," and her voice came out level, and no one saw anything wrong at all.

That was the trick of her life. No one ever saw anything wrong at all.

She walked out through the servants' corridor on legs that felt borrowed from someone older, and in the shadows past the buttery she put her palm flat against the cool stone and breathed until the ringing faded to a hum. Her heart stuttered twice, like a bird striking a window, then found its rhythm again.

*Noted,* she thought, with the terrible calm of her profession. *Cyanide tolerance: failing.*

*Weeks, not years.*

---

The archive smelled of salt and dust and candle-tallow, as it always did, and tonight it smelled like sanctuary. Yara descended the spiral stair with a lamp in one hand and her body held together with will and posture, and at the bottom, among the shelves that went back further than the kingdom itself, Vess looked up from the floor where she sat cross-legged with a stub of chalk.

The girl had drawn another word. Root-Tongue, the script that predated crowns and borders, the language that lived only in marginalia and in this small mud-streaked child who had arrived at Yara's door with a salt-white coin and no name anyone could pronounce. The character looked like a river deciding to be a tree.

"Vess," Yara said, setting down the lamp. "I need to work. Can you be quiet?"

Vess tapped the chalk against the flagstone twice—their signal for *yes*—and then pointed, with the direct unerring aim children have, at Yara's hands.

Yara looked down. They were still trembling. Faintly, like the last shiver of a plucked string.

"It's nothing," she said, which was the second lie she'd told in her career. The first had been to Corvane, about this same child, and it still sat in her chest like a swallowed stone. Lies were supposed to get easier. This one didn't. "Drink your water. Sleep when you're tired."

Vess's dark eyes stayed on her a moment longer—too long, too knowing, the way they always were—and then the girl returned to her chalk, and Yara climbed the ladder to the shelf where her own records lived.

Her section was modest. Thirty-one volumes, bound in gray calfskin, indexed in her own tight hand. Most ledger-keepers kept one book per principal; Yara kept thirty-one because the king's table required thirty-one separate chains of custody, thirty-one suppliers, thirty-one webs of grudge and greed that she mapped the way other people mapped coastlines. Poison was never just chemistry. Poison was *who*, and *why*, and *when the harvest failed and whose brother insulted whose wife*. She had built her immunity dose by dose over nineteen years—a grain of this, a drop of that, calibrated against her body's slow education—and she had recorded every calibration, because a taster who trusted her memory alone was a taster who died of arithmetic.

Tonight she pulled volume twenty-two and opened it flat on the reading desk, and began, for the first time in her career, to audit herself.

---

The numbers were patient. That was the horror of them. Numbers didn't flinch.

She worked backward from tonight. Cyanide exposure, standard court dose, response time eleven seconds longer than her baseline from three months prior. Tremor onset. Auditory disturbance. Two arrhythmic events—she'd felt both, and now here they were in ink where she couldn't pretend them away. She turned pages. Found the earlier warnings she'd catalogued without understanding: the fatigue she'd blamed on the archive's poor air, the nosebleed after the Feast of Saint Aldric that she'd blamed on dry weather, the way certain foods had begun tasting of tin.

Each entry, alone, was nothing. A taster's body accumulated complaints the way a road accumulated stones.

Together, laid out across nineteen years in her own handwriting, they made a shape. A curve. And curves had ends.

Yara sat very still in the lamplight and let the shape speak.

Immunity was not armor. She had known this in the abstract the way one knows the sea is deep. Immunity was a bargain struck between her liver and her blood and every venom the guild had fed her since she was nine years old—a bargain maintained by constant payment, and the currency was herself. Each dose taught her body to survive that poison by spending something irreplaceable. The guild's trainers had called it *the tithe*. They'd said it lightly, over cheap beer, young orphans all of them: *the tithe, the tithe, pay it now so you don't pay later.* None of them had asked what happened when the purse ran empty.

Her projections—in volume nine, dated six years ago, when she'd been thorough enough to frighten herself—had given her until her forty-fifth year. Eleven more years of margin. Enough time to buy out her contract, enough time for the guild to release her name, enough time to become a person again somewhere far from courts and cups.

The curve on the page in front of her bent the wrong way.

She took fresh paper and recalculated, using the last eighteen months of data. Her hand was steady now—the tremor had settled into a fine vibration she could almost pretend wasn't there—and the figures came out clean and merciless.

*Six weeks,* the numbers said. Perhaps eight if she stopped taking doses immediately, which she could not do, because an immune taster who lost her immunity lasted exactly as long as the next poisoning, and the next poisoning was always coming; the court was a machine that ran on it.

Perhaps eight weeks. In which she must somehow protect a child the entire Salt Guild wanted delivered to whatever wore the king's face, while continuing to drink poison nightly for a king who was not a king, while keeping two lies alive against an organization that had forgotten more ways to kill than most kingdoms had ever learned.

Yara closed her eyes and listened to her heart. It beat. Stubborn, irregular, hers—for now.

"Well," she said aloud, to the shelves, to the salt-dark air. "That explains the tin."

From the floor, softly, Vess made a sound—not a word, not quite. A hum, low and resonant, the note she made when she was frightened for someone. Yara opened her eyes and found the girl watching her across the lamplight with those old, old eyes, and something in Yara's chest cracked along a seam she hadn't known was there.

"I'm fine," she said. Third lie. They weren't getting easier at all.

---

* * *

Tam found her at dawn.

He came down the archive stairs with his scribe's case under one arm and ink on his fingers and the particular careful brightness he wore when he'd discovered something and hadn't yet decided whether telling her would save her or doom her. He was twenty-three, precise, brave in the specific way of men who had never yet been tested and suspected they might fail—and he had noticed, all on his own, that the realm's records were being eaten. Roads that vanished from survey rolls. Names fading off deeds mid-sentence. A parish register where an entire wedding party's surname had gone blank between one page and the next.

"The palace physician's logs," he said, without preamble, sitting across the reading desk from her. "I've been copying them for the chancery. There are entries going back four years—complaints the king made. Numbness of the tongue. Food tasting of ash. He complained about it to the physician eleven times before he stopped complaining altogether." Tam's eyes came up. "Eleven times, Yara. And then nothing. As if he simply… accepted it. Who accepts that?"

"A man who can no longer hold the thought long enough to be afraid," Yara said quietly.

"That's what frightens me. That's exactly—" He stopped. Looked at her properly for the first time, and she watched the scribe in him take over from the ally: the flick of attention to her face, the cataloguing. "You look ill."

"I'm always pale. Tasters are kept out of the sun; it's policy."

"You're grey. Your lips are grey." He leaned closer, and up close she could see the sleeplessness in him too, the cost of knowing what he knew. "When did you last eat a real meal? Not taste one. Eat."

"I eat."

"When?"

She didn't answer, and his jaw tightened in a way that was becoming familiar, that did something complicated to her chest that she refused to examine with the same rigor she gave her ledgers.

"Yara." His voice dropped. "What's happening to you?"

And there it was, the fork in the night. She could give him the professional non-answer—*fatigue, nothing, the season*—and keep her mortality locked in volume twenty-two where it belonged, a private debt between her and her own blood. Or she could say it aloud, in front of witnesses, in front of Vess's listening silence, and make it real in the way spoken things became real, irreversible, heavy.

She was so tired of carrying things alone. Nineteen years alone. It occurred to her, with a strange floating clarity, that she might have eight weeks left and had spent most of her life acting as though she had forever.

"My immunity is failing," she said. "Nineteen years of dosing. The body keeps accounts of its own, and mine is calling them due. Weeks, Tam. Not years. Weeks."

The lamp flame leaned in some draft neither of them felt. Tam sat back slowly.

"Weeks," he repeated.

"Six. Possibly eight." She heard her own voice and marveled at it—level, clerical, reporting figures. "I've recalculated three times. I'm hoping I've made an error. I don't think I have."

"What does the guild say? They trained you—they must have physicians—"

"The guild says nothing, because the guild doesn't know, and it stays that way." She met his eyes. "A taster whose immunity is failing is a taster worth less than her debts, Tam. They don't treat that. They *write it down*. They record the depreciation, and then they decide whether I'm still worth feeding. Do you understand? If Corvane learns this, I stop being a person in their books and start being a line item. And I cannot—I *will* not—be a line item while that child is under my roof."

Silence. Somewhere above them, the city was waking—cart wheels, a gull, the bells of the low quarter marking the hour with their usual indifference.

Then Tam did something she did not expect. He reached across the desk and took her trembling hand—her traitorous, failing, poisoned hand—and held it, ink-stained fingers laced through hers, as simply as if they had done it a hundred times.

"Then we don't tell them," he said. "We tell no one. You'll eat real meals—you'll eat them sitting down, I'll bring them myself, I know the cook in the west kitchen and she owes me for three letters of petition. And we find another way. Whatever the guild's books say about you, they're wrong. I've read enough falsified records lately to know one when I see it."

"They're not falsified," Yara said. Her voice came out rougher than she intended. "I wrote them myself."

"Then you wrote them about a woman who thought she was alone," Tam said. "You're not anymore. Write new ones."

Yara looked down at their joined hands, at the small warmth of it, and felt something dangerous move through her—not hope exactly, hope was a currency she'd learned early never to hold—but something adjacent. Something that made the eight weeks feel less like a sentence and more like a sum that had not yet been finished.

Behind them, chalk whispered on stone.

They both turned. Vess had risen from her corner and crossed the room without either of them hearing—she moved like that sometimes, like a memory moving—and she stood at the edge of the lamplight holding up her slate. On it, in the flowing root-script, was a single character, and beneath it she had copied, carefully, the shape of Yara's own hand as it lay in Tam's grip.

"She's been drawing that word for days," Yara murmured. "I haven't found it in any of the indexes."

Tam squinted at it. "What does it mean?"

"I don't know yet." But the old scars along Yara's forearms—the ones the child's words had always ached toward, the ones that had ached since the night Vess first spoke at her door—had begun, faintly, to warm. "But I intend to find out. Tomorrow I start translating properly. Every marginal gloss, every pre-kingdom fragment in the First Ledger's commentary. Whatever she's trying to tell me, it matters enough to make my scars itch, and everything that makes these scars itch has turned out to matter *enormously*."

Vess set the slate down, and then, deliberately, she took Yara's free hand and pressed it flat against the chalk drawing—against the image of her own held hand—and looked up at her with an expression that was not a child's expression at all. It was the expression of someone delivering news.

Then the girl pointed at Yara's chest. At the heart behind the grey lips and the stumbling pulse.

And said, in her tongueless tongue, one soft syllable that made the lamp flame stand perfectly still:

*"Home."*

Yara's breath caught. Because she had heard that word before—not in sound but in sense, in the ache of her scars—and because the oldest ledger in the archive, the sealed one chained shut on its iron stand, had that exact character pressed into its lock in silver.

The lock, she realized, that was shaped like a hand.

Hers fit it. She had known that for years and had never once dared to try.

"Eight weeks," Yara said to the dark, to Tam, to the child, to herself—to whoever was keeping the true account of her life, the one no guild recorded. "Then we'd best not waste a single morning."

Above them, muffled by stone and salt, the palace bells rang the hour—and somewhere in the king's chambers, she knew, the thing wearing his crown was waking hungry, and the city's memories were a little thinner than they had been yesterday, and the countdown she had just written for her own body had begun.

She picked up the lamp and turned toward the chained First Ledger, and her scars burned like a lit fuse all the way across the archive floor.
