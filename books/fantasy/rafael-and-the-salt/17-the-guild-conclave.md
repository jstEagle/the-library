## 17. The Guild Conclave

The summons came at dusk, folded inside a wheel of salted cheese the way the guild delivered everything—hidden in the ordinary, carried by hands that never remembered carrying it.

Yara found the slip while cutting wedges in her kitchen above the archive: a strip of grey vellum, unmarked except for a single pressed grain of black salt. She held it to the lamp and watched the words surface slowly, like drowned things rising.

*Conclave. Third bell past midnight. Bring your keys. Leave your face.*

She read it twice, then fed it to the stove and stood watching it curl, thinking of Vess asleep upstairs with Tam's spare coat dragged over her like a tent, thinking of the Root-Tongue syllables she'd copied that afternoon, thinking of Corvane's voice two nights ago: *They know something is wrong, Yara. When the masters call a conclave out of season, someone has already decided what the vote will be.*

"Leave your face," she said to the empty kitchen. Her laugh came out flat as a dropped plate.

She went to wake Tam.

---

He was sleeping badly these days—she could tell by the state of his desk when she passed his room, the ink pots uncapped, the forgery practice sheets crumpled into balls and hidden under blotting paper as if shame could be filed away too. He'd taken to practicing the chancellor's hand at night, over and over, until his fingers cramped. He said it was so he'd be ready when they needed a writ. Yara suspected it was because his hands needed something honest to do while his mind chewed on fear.

She shook his shoulder gently. "Tam. I have to go out."

He surfaced from sleep all at once, the way he always did, eyes finding hers before anything else. "The conclave?"

"You weren't supposed to know about that."

"I wasn't supposed to know about any of this." He sat up, hair wild, and rubbed his face. "You're going alone? Into a room full of masked guild masters who've just learned their ledger-keeper has been lying to them?"

"They don't know I've been lying."

"They will." He caught her wrist—not gripping, just resting there, warm and deliberate. "Yara. If they ask you where the child is—"

"They won't ask. That's not how it works. The ledger-keeper isn't a person at a conclave. The ledger-keeper is furniture that can count." She tried to make it sound like reassurance. It came out sounding like what it was.

"Then be very good furniture," Tam said, "and come home."

It was such a small thing to promise. She gave it anyway, because his hand was still on her wrist, and because promises were the only currency she had left that wasn't salt.

---

Vess was awake when Yara checked on her, sitting cross-legged in the dark, watching the door with those old, old eyes.

"Stay here," Yara told her, kneeling. "No matter what you hear tonight. No matter what you feel. The city might get strange around the edges—the masters do their work in the deep hours sometimes—but you stay under the floor, in the archive, behind the false entry. Can you do that?"

Vess considered her for a long moment. Then she reached out and touched Yara's collarbone, right over the faded scar from her first immunity dose, and spoke three words in the Root-Tongue.

Yara had learned enough now to translate them without the marginalia, and the translation went through her like cold water: *Do not forget yourself.*

"I won't," she lied softly, and kissed the girl's forehead, and tasted salt.

---

The conclave met beneath the Saltmarket, in a hall that did not appear on any surveyor's map because the surveyors had sold their memory of drawing it.

Yara descended by the traders' stair with her hood up and her keys heavy at her belt—nine keys, one for each of the sealed ledgers, plus the little iron key to her own entries that she wore on a cord against her skin. Around her, other figures converged through the fog-wet streets: broad men and narrow women, a hunchbacked shape she recognized by its gait as the debt-broker Old Sennet, a pair walking so close together they might have been one person wearing two coats. None of them greeted each other. Greeting required names, and names were collateral, and collateral stayed home on conclave night.

At the bottom of the stair stood the Doorwarden, a mountain of a man in a mask of grey felt, holding a bowl of brine.

One by one, each arriving master dipped two fingers in the bowl and touched them to the mask's mouth. Yara had done it a hundred times. The brine was guild-worked; it took a thin coat of memory off whatever it touched—tonight's walk, the smell of the street, the color of the sky—and kept it in the bowl, so that no one entering could later describe the road they'd come by. She dipped her fingers. The brine was cold as a confession. For half a heartbeat afterward she genuinely could not have said whether it had rained that evening, and the smallness of that theft, the casualness of it, made her want to weep or scream or both.

Instead she bowed her head and went through.

The conclave hall had been carved out of the city's bones long before the city had a name—it was older than the palace above it, older than the dynasty, its walls of raw dark stone veined with white like frozen lightning. Salt ran in those veins. Yara had always assumed it was decorative. Tonight, standing beneath the vaulted dark with forty masked figures arrayed around a circular table of black basalt, she looked at the veins properly for the first time and understood that the hall wasn't built *near* the salt. The hall was grown *from* it. The whole chamber was a geode, and they were meeting inside the crystal.

Nine chairs stood empty at the table. Nine masters absent. She counted twice, uneasily, and wondered if absence meant death or simply that some business was too foul even for a conclave.

Above the table hung the guild's oldest relic: a glass sphere the size of a child's head, filled not with water but with slow-drifting grains of salt that moved against every current, rising when they should fall, circling when nothing stirred them. The Heartglass. Every apprentice learned the rhyme about it—*what the glass gathers, the guild keeps; what the guild keeps, the crown eats*—and every apprentice was beaten for reciting it aloud. Yara had never seen the Heartglass move with purpose before.

Tonight it was moving with purpose. The grains were spiraling, slowly, steadily, all in one direction. Down and inward. Like water finding a drain.

"The ledger-keeper stands ready," announced the Master of Keys, whose mask was white bone polished to a shine. "Bring forward the record."

That was her cue. Yara stepped to the table's edge, unlocked the satchel of records she'd been commanded to bring, and laid out the documents with steady hands: the audit rolls, the tribute schedules, the inventory of recent acquisitions. And threaded among them, disguised as routine entries, the reports she had been quietly assembling for weeks—the vanished roads, the songs no one could finish humming, the parish registers with names fading off the page like breath off glass.

She had brought them as evidence. She realized, watching the masked heads bend over the pages, that she had no idea whether she'd brought them as evidence *for* the truth or *against* it. Both, perhaps. A taster's habit: lay out every dish and let the room reveal which ones are poisoned.

"The realm thins," said the Master of Keys, when the reading was done. "Say it plainly, keeper. What does your record show?"

"It shows consumption," Yara said. Her voice sounded strange in the geode dark, flattened by the salt walls. "Memory leaving the capital at a rate beyond natural decay. Roads unwritten. Names dissolving from legal instruments. A season of births in the Lowbridge parishes that no mother remembers bearing." She paused, and made herself say the rest. "And the king. His tongue is dead. Nothing tastes to him anymore. Whatever sits on the throne, it does not eat food."

Silence. In it, the Heartglass whispered—a sound like sand pouring through an hourglass the size of the world.

"The king's appetite," said a voice from the far side of the table, dry as old vellum, "is not the matter before this conclave."

Yara turned toward the speaker. The mask was black lacquer, featureless but for a single slit, and she knew the posture beneath it even without a name: the Master of Debts, the eldest of them, the one who had signed her own indenture thirty years ago. She would know that stillness anywhere. It was the stillness of a spider that has already finished spinning.

"The matter," the Master of Debts continued, "is the vessel."

The word went through the hall like a knife through wet cloth.

"There is a child," the Master of Debts said, "in this city. Unregistered. Uncollateralized. Speaking a tongue our own archives predate. The First Ledger speaks of such children. The binding speaks of such children. And the terms of the bargain—" a pause, delicate as a scalpel— "are due for renewal."

Yara kept her face still behind her plain grey mask. It was the cheapest mask in the room, the ledger-keeper's mask, deliberately anonymous. But her heart was slamming against her ribs hard enough that she feared the salt walls themselves might taste it.

*They know.* Not everything—they didn't know where Vess was, or they wouldn't be convening, they'd simply be taking—but they knew she existed. Someone had talked. Some watcher at some door, some bribed ear in some market, some thread pulled loose from the tapestry of Yara's careful lies. Or worse: the ledgers themselves had whispered. Entries that shouldn't exist had a way of surfacing, like bodies in spring thaw.

"The bargain," said Old Sennet, and his hunched shadow swayed with something that might have been hunger, "has kept this city fed for four hundred years. Kept *us* fed. While kings rose and rotted, while plagues walked through and walked back out, the guild has traded, and the trade has flowed, and the flow has been *paid for*. You all know the arithmetic. Four hundred years of prosperity against—" he gestured vaguely upward, toward the palace, toward the throne, toward the thing that wore it— "against a modest tithe."

"A modest tithe," Yara repeated. She hadn't meant to speak. The words came out of her like blood from a reopened cut. "You call it a tithe. The season the thing ate last month—that was harvests. Funerals. Weddings. There are people in this city who reached for their children's faces last week and found blankness where the love used to be. That is your modest tithe."

"Keeper." The Master of Keys' voice was soft, warning.

"No." She stepped away from the table, into the open floor, where all forty masks could see her. "I keep the ledgers. It is my right—I would say my duty—to speak to what the numbers mean. And the numbers mean this: we are not merchants. We are not even thieves. Thieves take things people can live without. We are *farmers*. We raise this city like livestock, fat and trusting, and we milk it, and when the yield drops—" she pointed at the Heartglass, its grains spiraling down and down— "*we feed it*."

"Do not," said the Master of Debts, and for the first time the stillness in that black lacquer cracked, just slightly, showing heat underneath, "presume to teach this conclave its own history."

"Then teach me." She turned to face him fully. "Teach me what the founders bound. Teach me why the First Ledger is chained shut. Teach me what we're actually feeding, because I have read every unsealed page in nine ledgers and nowhere—*nowhere*—does it say what sits on the throne. Only what it pays. Only what it eats. A merchant knows his goods, Master. What are we selling?"

The silence that followed had weight. The Heartglass poured its whisper into it.

Then the Master of Debts rose from his chair, unhurried, and Yara saw that the other masters were rising too, one by one, in order of seniority, and her stomach dropped through the geode floor into whatever darkness lay beneath the city's bones, because she recognized the ritual. Every apprentice learned it. It was performed only once in a generation, and it was called the Renewal, and it ended with a vote.

"The question before this conclave," the Master of Debts intoned, "is put in the old form. The realm thins. The flow falters. The bargain requires renewal, and renewal requires a vessel of the old blood, speaking the old tongue, freely surrendered." He let the word *freely* hang there like a hook baited with rot. "A vessel has been found. The conclave will vote: deliver the vessel, and the bargain holds for another century—or refuse, and watch the flow fail, and the city starve of itself, memory by memory, until nothing remains but animals in fine clothes."

"And if the vessel cannot be found?" Yara asked. "If it stays lost?"

Something moved behind the slit in the black lacquer. Something almost like pity, which was infinitely worse than cruelty.

"Then the guild sends finders," the Master of Debts said. "We are very good at finding things, keeper. It is, after all, what we trade in."

The vote was conducted as it had been conducted for four centuries: each master in turn pressed a palm to the Heartglass. Consent left the hand warm; refusal left it cold. No names. No voices. Just warmth and cold, gathered into the sphere, weighed in grains of salt.

Yara watched the first palm touch the glass. The grains shivered, brightened, drifted *up*.

Warm.

The second master. Warm. The third. Warm. Old Sennet pressed his knotted hand flat and the sphere flared like a lamp being fed. Warm, warm, warm, down the line of seniority, and Yara stood apart with her keys at her belt counting warmth after warmth after warmth, feeling the vote assemble itself around her like a wall, like a closing fist, like the interior of a mask.

There were thirty-one voting masters present. She counted twenty-nine palms before hers was called for.

Twenty-nine warm.

Her own vote, as ledger-keeper, was advisory only—one grain among many, weighted less than a debt-broker's, less than a smuggler's. She could press her palm cold and change nothing. She could press it warm and keep her place, her access, her usefulness, the fragile cover over the girl hiding beneath her floorboards.

Or—

She thought of Vess's fingers on her collarbone. *Do not forget yourself.*

She pressed her palm to the Heartglass, and she thought, with all the force she had ever used to hold poison on her tongue without swallowing, *cold*, and the grains where she touched dimmed, guttered, sank.

One cold grain in a sea of warm ones. The Master of Keys recorded it without comment, without even turning his bone-white mask toward her. One dissenting grain. Noted. Filed. Remembered.

The vote stood: deliver the vessel.

"So resolved," said the Master of Debts. "The finders go out at first light. The vessel will be surrendered within nine days, or the guild will consider every hiding place, every ally, every lie—" and here the black lacquer turned, slowly, deliberately, until the slit faced Yara across the width of the table— "an act of theft against the guild. And the guild, keeper, collects its debts. Even from furniture."

The conclave broke. Masters filed out in reverse order of arrival, dipping their fingers in the brine bowl on the way, surrendering the evening's memory piece by piece until they emerged into the foggy street as strangers to their own treason. Yara lingered, relocking her satchel with hands that wanted to shake, and in the emptied hall she looked up one last time at the Heartglass.

The grains had stopped spiraling inward.

Now they were drifting toward her side of the chamber. Slowly. Curiously. Like something smelling blood in the water.

She got out before it finished deciding what she was.

---

Corvane was waiting for her at the top of the traders' stair, hood up, face gray in the lamplight, and she knew before he spoke.

"You voted cold," he said.

"You saw."

"I see everything that happens in that room. It's my job to see." He fell into step beside her, and for a block neither of them spoke; the fog muffled the city around them, and somewhere a drunk was singing a song that had lost its middle verses, everyone in the street humming along to a hole in the melody. "Yara. The finders muster at dawn. Twelve of them, and they don't use doors. They'll start with the places you'd hide her, because everyone knows the ledger-keeper's habits better than she thinks."

"Then I'll break my habits."

"That's not—" He stopped her with a hand on her arm, and in the lamplight she saw how old he suddenly looked, how the years he never spent seemed to be collecting on him all at once, like debts called in. "Listen to me. There's something else. Something I should have told you at the conclave, in front of all of them, and didn't, because I'm a coward with thirty years of salt in my veins." His grip tightened. "The vote wasn't the only resolution tonight. After you left the floor, the Master of Debts convened the inner circle. Nine of them. And they passed a second motion, sealed, unwritten, off the record."

The fog seemed to close in tighter. "What motion?"

Corvane's voice dropped to almost nothing.

"They didn't vote to wait for the finders, Yara. They voted to send the crowned thing a message *tonight*. To tell it the vessel exists. To tell it where the search begins." He swallowed. "They told it the vessel is hidden by the ledger-keeper. They gave it your name."

Above them, across the sleeping city, the palace windows were dark.

Except one.

High in the east tower, one window had lit—though no servant kept hours that late—and stood glowing, patient and yellow, like an eye opening.
