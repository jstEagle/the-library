## 18. Stolen Pages

The First Ledger lived behind a door that did not officially exist.

Yara had known this for eleven years—had catalogued its neighbors, weighed its dust, recorded the exact number of paces from the archive's cold hearth to the iron-banded panel disguised as a wine rack. She had never opened it. No ledger-keeper had. The guild's law was older than her handler, older than the conclave, older than any living master: *the first book is not for reading.* It was not even for looking at. Corvane had once told her, in his flat way, that a keeper who touched it would be processed like anything else the guild acquired.

She had believed him. For years she had believed him, the way she had believed the king's food was merely dangerous and not, in some deeper sense, wrong.

Now she stood before the false wine rack with Vess asleep on a pallet of archive blankets at her back, and Tam across the room with an ink-stained apron over his palace livery, and the whole weight of what they were about to do pressing down like water in the lungs.

"The writ," Yara said quietly. "Again."

Tam exhaled and unrolled the parchment he'd been practicing on all evening—a requisition of sealing wax, of all things, the most boring forgery in the world. His hands were steady. That surprised her; scribes were supposed to tremble. But Tam wrote the way other people prayed, with total absorption, and when he bent over the page he stopped being a frightened young man committing treason and became something older: a hand guided by habit.

"Master Aldous Fenwick, Keeper of the West Receiving Room," he recited as he worked, copying the cadence of the royal chancery. "By order of the Under-Chamberlain, access is granted to the sealed holdings of the Salt Guild archive for purposes of inventory reconciliation, pursuant to the audit of—" He glanced up. "What date do I want?"

"Three days ago. Before the conclave met. If anyone checks, the writ should predate their vote."

"Which means I'm forging a document that authorizes us to have forged documents." He said it without drama, just to see how it sounded in the air. It sounded terrible. He kept writing anyway.

"You can still walk away," Yara said. She said it every night now, and every night he ignored it, and she kept saying it because it was the only honest thing she could offer him. "You're a palace scribe. You noticed discrepancies no one else saw—that's already more courage than most. You don't have to go further."

"I counted forty-one roads out of the capital," Tam said, not looking up. "The city maps say thirty-three. I checked the surveyor rolls; the extra eight were struck through in different inks, decades apart, by different hands. Someone has been editing the kingdom, Yara. I spent two years thinking I was mad." His pen paused. "I'd rather be guilty than mad."

She let him work.

The lock on the false wine rack was not a lock but a judgment—it read the blood of whoever pressed their palm to it and compared that blood against the ledgers' records of debts. Every person in the guild's books was collateral. Every drop of them was accounted for. Yara had long ago worked out the flaw in the design, the small arrogance at its heart: the door trusted its own accounting absolutely. And Yara was the one who kept the accounts.

She had spent three nights preparing the entry. A transfer of custody, dated properly, sealed with the conclave's own cipher—which she should not have been able to reproduce, except that she had copied ciphers the way Tam copied handwriting, obsessively, for years, because a ledger-keeper who couldn't forge her own guild was a ledger-keeper who couldn't protect herself. The entry named her as authorized custodian of the First Ledger for reconciliation purposes. It was a lie wearing perfect clothes.

Her palm met the iron. She felt the faint pull of the mechanism tasting her, checking her against itself—and then, with a sound like a held breath released, the panel swung inward.

Cold came out. Not temperature-cold. Something older than weather, a cold like the inside of a word no one had spoken in centuries.

Inside, on a lectern of black salt-stone, lay the First Ledger.

It was smaller than she expected. Smaller than any of the working volumes in the archive—barely larger than her two hands, bound in hide that might have been goat or might have been nothing born on this earth, its pages the color of old teeth. There was no title. There didn't need to be. Everything else in the guild was downstream of this book; everything else was sediment.

Vess stirred on the pallet. The girl had begun sleeping less since the conclave, waking in the dark hours with words gathering behind her teeth like weather. Yara had taken to keeping a lamp lit for her. Tonight, mercifully, the child slept on.

"Copy everything," Yara told Tam. "Even what looks like nonsense. Especially that."

"And you?"

"I'm going to find the binding."

* * *

The ritual occupied pages near the middle of the book, and Yara knew it the moment her eyes found it—not because she understood the Root-Tongue script, though Vess had been teaching her, word by costly word, but because the ink itself was different. Every other entry in the First Ledger was written in the same brown-black pigment, dried centuries ago. The binding ritual was written in something darker, something that still seemed faintly wet, as if the page refused to let it finish drying. As if the writing were still happening.

She laid fresh vellum beside it and began to copy.

Her hand shook at first. Then the taster's discipline took over—the same discipline that let her lift a poisoned spoon to her lips with a courtier watching and show nothing on her face. Steady the breath. Steady the wrist. One stroke at a time. She copied the angular Root-Tongue characters, and beneath each one, in her own small precise hand, the phonetic guesses Vess had given her, and beneath those, translations where she had them. Where she didn't, she left space. The girl would fill the gaps tomorrow, and the girl's lessons would cost Yara another memory—she could feel the toll already, a thinning at the edges of her childhood, a summer gone soft and gray—but there was no other way. Some prices you paid forward.

The ritual had seven movements. She could see that much even through the language barrier: seven stages, each requiring something given freely. Words. A name. A witness. Her eye caught on the third requirement and her stomach dropped—the text called for *a name freely given by one whom the bound one trusted*, and beneath it, in a later hand, someone had added a note in ordinary guild-script:

*No qualifying giver remains. See addendum.*

See addendum. She flipped pages forward, hunting, and found the addendum torn out. Not removed cleanly—torn, hurriedly, the stub of the page still visible in the binding. Someone, sometime in the last few centuries, had ripped out the answer and left the question.

"Yara." Tam's voice, low and strange. "Come look at this."

He had reached the earliest pages of the ledger while she worked the middle, copying backward and forward in alternating stretches so the sound of their pens never overlapped into rhythm. Now he turned the book toward her, and she saw why his voice had changed.

The first entries were not trades.

They were deaths.

Names, dates, causes—all in that same brown-black ink, all in a script that predated the guild's formal hand. *Maren of the Ash Rows, taken winter of the Long Hunger. Dev, ferryman, taken at the river crossing. Sella, age six, taken from her mother's kitchen.* Taken. Not sold, not traded, not borrowed. Taken. Page after page of the taken, hundreds of names, and at the bottom of each entry, in smaller script, a single recurring notation that made Yara's skin crawl:

*Fed.*

"They weren't merchants," Tam whispered. "The founders. They weren't building a trade. They were—" He couldn't finish it. He didn't have to. She had seen the scrying-glass; she had watched the crowned thing eat. The guild hadn't invented its commerce. It had *domesticated* the feeding, dressed it in contracts and ciphers, taught it manners. Bottled grief. Borrowed years. All of it, every transaction in every ledger in the archive, was the same act as those early pages—memory taken and delivered to the thing beneath the crown—only laundered through paperwork until even the guild's own masters had forgotten what the washing was for.

Or hadn't forgotten. Or had chosen, generation after generation, not to look.

"We're copying evidence," Tam said. His pen had gone still. "This isn't a ritual anymore. This is a confession."

"Then we copy faster."

They worked. The candles burned down. Somewhere above them the city slept its half-edited sleep, citizens dreaming of streets that no longer existed, singing songs whose second verses had been eaten. Vess slept too, curled small, one hand closed around the salt-white coin she had carried out of whatever wilderness had made her. Yara copied until her eyes burned, until the darker ink of the ritual seemed to watch her back, until she had filled nine sheets of vellum with the shape of the world's oldest crime and its possible undoing.

She was reaching for the tenth sheet when the lamp at the archive door went out.

Not guttered. Extinguished—one instant burning, the next dark, the way a candle dies when a hand closes over it.

Corvane's voice came out of the darkness, quiet as dust. "Don't stop writing on my account. Finish the line."

* * *

He stepped into the light of their remaining candles, and Yara's whole body went rigid with the old training—the stillness of the taster, the face that shows nothing. But her heart was hammering against her ribs like something trying to get out.

Corvane looked exactly as he always looked: gray, spare, precise, his coat buttoned to the throat, his expression a locked door. Except his eyes. His eyes moved from the open First Ledger to the vellum sheets to Tam's frozen pen to the sleeping child, and something in them was not locked at all. Something in them was breaking, slowly, the way ice breaks on a river—you couldn't see it move, but you could hear it groaning.

"How long have you known about the door?" he asked.

"Eleven years," Yara said. There was no point lying. He had trained her; he would taste a lie the way she tasted arsenic.

"Eleven years." He said it like a man doing arithmetic he didn't like. "I told you once, when you were new, that keepers who touch the First Ledger get processed. Do you remember?"

"You also told me the guild protects the realm's memory. I've stopped believing both in the same week."

Something flickered at the corner of his mouth. Not quite a smile. The ghost of one, maybe, or the scar tissue where one used to be. He crossed the room without hurry and stood over the lectern, looking down at the pages of the taken, and Yara watched him read his own religion's original sin.

"I was recruited at fourteen," he said, to the book rather than to her. "Debts of my father's. You know how it goes." He did not ask if she knew how it went. Of course he knew she knew; he had pulled her file himself, once, in the years before she understood what she was. "My handler told me the guild was ancient and necessary. That memory is a resource like timber or grain, and someone must steward it, or the realm would drown in its own griefs. I believed him for fifty years." He touched the edge of the First Ledger, lightly, the way you'd touch a wound to check if it still hurt. It clearly still hurt. "Fifty years, Yara. Do you know what I did in those years? I processed a woman's grief over her drowned son so a merchant could buy calm. I took a man's memory of his brother so a rival could take his business. I told myself it was mercy. The grievers slept better. That's what I told myself."

"And now?"

"Now I count roads." He looked up at her. "Eight roads that don't exist. An entire season missing from the city's books—I audited it myself after the decoy child was delivered. Births unwritten. Funerals unwritten. I have spent my life keeping accounts, and last month I discovered the account has no bottom." His jaw tightened. "And tonight I find the youngest keeper I ever trained copying out the truth with a palace boy and a Root-Tongue child, preparing to do what fifty generations of masters were too well-fed to do."

Tam had risen halfway off his stool, poised between flight and foolishness. Yara put a hand out—not toward Corvane, toward Tam. Stay.

"You're going to report this," Yara said. It wasn't a question. It was the sentence she had been rehearsing since the lamp died, and her voice came out steadier than she felt. "So say it, and be quick. The conclave meets at dawn."

Corvane was silent for a long time.

When he finally spoke, he did not report. He reached inside his coat and drew out a folded paper—official stock, chancery watermark—and laid it on the table between them. Tam leaned forward despite himself and went pale. It was a summons. A guild summons, bearing Corvane's own seal, ordering the immediate surrender of the Root-Tongue child to conclave custody for delivery to the crown.

Dated tomorrow.

"I wrote it tonight," Corvane said. "Before I came down here. I told myself I would deliver it whether or not I found you in violation, and that finding you in violation would make it easier." He turned the paper over, showing its blank back. "Instead I find you copying the binding ritual out of the First Ledger, which no keeper has dared in four hundred years, and I find that I cannot write the second half of my own sentence."

"Why?" Tam asked. It burst out of him, young and honest. "Why look away? You'll be processed yourself if they learn you knew. You must know that. This—" he gestured at the stolen pages, the open forbidden book, the sleeping vessel of a king's soul—"this is death for all three of us, and worse for you, because they trust you."

Corvane regarded him for a moment with something almost gentle.

"Because I altered a ledger once," he said. "Thirty years ago. A single entry. A death-date moved by one hour, in a book I was sworn never to touch." His eyes found Yara's, and held, and she felt the floor tilt under the weight of what he was saying—what he had said without saying, what she suddenly understood had been done for her, and what it had cost. "The guild believes its books are perfect. Let them go on believing it a little longer. I've already spent what reporting you would cost me. There's nothing left in me they can take that matters."

Yara's throat was tight. "Corvane—"

"Don't." He raised a hand, sharp, almost angry. "Don't thank me. I didn't do it for gratitude. I did it because I looked at your file thirty years ago and saw a girl the guild had hollowed out and labeled, and I have regretted my part in that every day since, and I am *tired*, Yara. I am tired of being the digestive system's favorite clerk." He straightened his coat, and the locked-door expression returned, but imperfectly now, like a mask with a crack running through it. "Take the western stair when you go. The enforcers changed the rotation at midnight; the east corridor walks past the conclave chamber. And take these."

He set a ring of keys on the table. Archive keys. Master keys—keys to doors Yara had catalogued for a decade but never been permitted to open.

"The sealed stacks," she said. "Why would I need—"

"Because the ledgers anchor it," Corvane said quietly. "You've worked that out, or you will. Every debt recorded in this place is a hook in someone's memory, and every hook feeds upward. If you mean to do what I think you mean to do, you'll need to reach all of them." He paused at the threshold of the dark, an old gray man silhouetted against an older darkness, and for one moment he looked less like her handler than like a ghost attending his own funeral. "I bought my father's freedom with fifty years of this work. I'd like to think the last purchase spends better."

Then he was gone, and the lamp relit—or perhaps he relit it; she never learned which—and the three of them stood in the sudden ordinary light among the stolen pages, breathing.

Tam sat down slowly. "He gave us the keys."

"He gave us the keys," Yara agreed. Her hands, she noticed, were shaking now that it was safe to shake. She pressed them flat against the vellum, against the dark wet ink of the binding ritual, and felt the future rearrange itself around her like furniture in the dark.

Nine sheets. Seven movements. One torn-out addendum naming a giver who supposedly no longer existed.

And outside, somewhere above them, the city slept on, forgetting itself street by street, while dawn—and the conclave, and the hunt for a child—came on like a tide.

"Pack everything," Yara said. "We have until sunrise. Then we run."
