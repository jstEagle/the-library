## 19. The Queen's Empty Eyes

The summons came on a card of heavy cream stock, sealed with the queen's own signet—a swan crowned in laurels—and delivered by a page who would not meet Yara's eyes.

*Her Majesty requests the presence of the taster at the ninth bell. Come alone. Come quietly.*

Yara read the card three times by candlelight while Vess slept curled in the false entry Tam had forged for her, a girl named "Maren Ostwick, ward of no one, employed nowhere," who existed only as ink and cross-reference. The real girl breathed slow and even beneath a blanket stitched with salt-white thread, her small hands open, palms up, as if even in sleep she were receiving something.

*Nine bells.* That was the hour when the palace emptied of ceremony. When the courtiers went to their suppers and their schemes, and the corridors belonged to servants and shadows. It was also, Yara noted with a cold twist in her stomach, the hour recorded in an entry she had not written—the one that described her death. Three days past now. She had survived it because someone had altered the ledger, because Corvane had spent thirty years of his own recorded life to buy her a wrong hour. But the habit of dread did not unlearn itself so quickly. Every summons felt like a page turning without her consent.

She dressed plainly. Taster's grey, hood down, no guild marks—there were none to wear anyway; the Salt Guild branded nothing but ledgers. She left Vess with instructions whispered in the fragments of Root-Tongue the child had taught her: *Stay. Count. Wait.* Vess had pressed one salt-white coin into her hand before she went, wordless, solemn, and Yara had carried it against her palm the whole way through the palace gates like a stone she could not put down.

The queen received her in the Orangery—not a chamber Yara knew well, though she had tasted for banquets held there. In daylight it was all glass and green and the smell of citrus oil. At night, lit by a handful of lamps, it became something else: a room full of growing things straining toward light that wasn't there. The queen sat among them in a plain chair, dressed in mourning grey that was not officially mourning, because officially no one had died.

"Your Majesty." Yara knelt, as protocol demanded.

"Get up," Queen Isolde said. "Please. There is no one here to see."

That was the first wrongness. The second was her voice. Yara had heard it across banquet halls for years—clear, musical, a voice trained to carry warmth over distance. This voice was frayed at the edges, like cloth rubbed thin.

"You asked for me," Yara said carefully.

"I asked for you specifically." The queen studied her with eyes that were too steady, the way a person stares when they are holding something heavy and are afraid to set it down. "Do you know why?"

"No, Majesty."

"No. Of course not. No one knows why, because I haven't told anyone, because—" The queen stopped herself. Pressed her lips together. Started again, slower. "Because every time I try to tell someone, I forget what I was trying to tell them. Do you understand what I'm saying, taster? I stand in front of my lord chancellor and I open my mouth and the thought is *gone*. Like smoke drawn up a chimney. And then I smile, because smiling is what remains when everything else is taken, and I ask him about grain tariffs instead."

The lamps hissed softly. Somewhere in the glass dark, a night-bird struck the pane and fell away.

"Your Majesty," Yara said, "I am only a taster."

"You are the only person in this palace whose whole profession is noticing what is wrong with things before anyone else can see it." The queen rose. She moved to the nearest orange tree and touched its leaves with something like desperation, as if the fruit might confirm the world still worked. "So notice me. Look at me and tell me what you see."

Yara looked. She had spent ten years learning to read faces across a dinner table—to see the micro-flinch of a man watching his rival drink, the too-calm hands of a woman who had already chosen her poison. She applied that craft now to the most guarded face in the kingdom.

What she saw frightened her.

Not illness. Not madness. Something subtler. The queen's face was *complete*—every feature correct, every expression available—but it was like a portrait of the queen rather than the woman. And around her eyes, where grief or love or fury should have pooled, there were smooth places. Places worn flat. Like stones in a riverbed where water had run for years.

"You're grieving," Yara said slowly. "But you don't know what for."

Queen Isolde made a sound that was half laugh, half wound. "Yes. *Yes.* Gods, yes. Do you know how that feels? To wake weeping into your pillow and have no memory to hang the tears on? My women say it is melancholia. My physicians bleed me. My confessor prescribes prayer. And I kneel and I pray to be relieved of a sorrow I cannot name, and I rise, and by noon I've forgotten I prayed at all—until the next morning, when the pillow is wet again."

She turned from the tree. In the lamplight her composure cracked clean through, and Yara saw beneath it: a woman exhausted in a way sleep could not touch.

"Something happened to my husband," the queen said.

"Majesty—"

"Don't. Don't soothe me. Listen." She crossed the floor and took Yara's hands, and her grip was stronger than her voice. Her fingers were cold. "I will tell you what I have, and you must hold it for me, because I cannot hold it myself. Every time I reach for it, it slips. So I am asking you to be my memory. Will you do that? Will you keep what I give you?"

Yara should have refused. Every instinct trained into her by the guild said: *the less you know of royals' secrets, the longer you live.* But the queen's hands were shaking, and Vess slept three miles away under a name that didn't exist, and somewhere below the palace foundations a thing wearing a crown was eating the world one memory at a time. Yara had already chosen her side, weeks ago, in a cellar full of impossible things. She simply hadn't known it yet.

"I'll keep it," she said. "Every word."

The queen exhaled, long and shuddering, and led her to a bench beneath the largest tree. They sat like two women at a market stall, shoulder to shoulder, conspiratorial in their smallness.

"The wedding," the queen began. "Tell me—what do people say about our wedding? You were there. You tasted the wine."

"They say it was beautiful, Majesty. The feast of the century. Forty courses. The bride wore silverthread and the whole city drank at the crown's expense."

"Yes. Yes, that's what they say." The queen's jaw tightened. "And what do *I* remember?"

Yara waited.

"Nothing." The word came out flat and terrible. "I remember standing at an altar. I remember vows—I think I remember vows, or I remember that there were supposed to be vows. And then there is a gap, a white space, and after the gap I am queen, and everyone tells me how happy I was, and I smile and thank them." Her voice thinned. "Ten years married to the most documented man in the kingdom. Ten thousand portraits, a hundred chroniclers, more ink spilled on him than on any king in four dynasties. And I cannot recall his *laugh*, taster. I cannot picture his face when he was young. I know he had a favorite hound—I've read it in a book—but I don't remember its name, and when I ask the old kennel-master, he frowns and says, 'A dog, Majesty? His Majesty keeps no dogs.'"

"He doesn't," Yara said quietly. "Now."

"Now. Yes. Now." The queen laughed, brittle. "Everything is *now* with everyone. Have you noticed? Ask any servant about last winter and they'll describe this spring. Ask them about a dead friend and they'll pause—just a heartbeat, a little hitch, like a cart wheel catching a rut—and then talk about the weather. I used to think it was grief, everyone grieving privately. Now I think—" She pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes. "Now I think something is *eating* us."

There it was. Said aloud, in the queen's own voice, in a room full of blind orange trees. Yara kept her face still. Ten years of tasting had taught her that.

"And the king himself?" Yara asked. "You live beside him. What do you see?"

For a moment the queen didn't answer. When she did, her voice dropped to almost nothing, as though the walls had ears that reported upward.

"He is kind to me," she said. "Perfectly kind. He remembers our anniversary. He brings me flowers—he always brings white ones, I've noticed, never red, and I don't know why that matters but it does, it matters terribly. He sits with me in the evenings and asks about my day and listens, truly listens." A pause. "And when he thinks I'm asleep, he stands at the window for hours. Just stands. And once—once, months ago—I woke and found him watching me, and his face was—" She struggled. "You've seen a man eat, taster. You watch them at table all day. Tell me: when a man eats, what's in his face?"

"Hunger," Yara said. "Or pleasure. Sometimes both."

"Yes. Hunger or pleasure." The queen nodded slowly. "His face was neither. It was *accounting*. Like a merchant counting coins that weren't his. And I thought—no. I thought, you're mad, Isolde, you're sleep-sick and sorrow-mad. But I wrote it down. I've been writing things down, hiding the pages, because paper remembers even when I can't. Would you like to know what I've written?"

"Majesty, I—"

"*Would you like to know?*" Not angry. Begging. That was the horror of it: a queen begging a food-taster in an empty orangery, gripping her sleeve like a drowning woman grips driftwood.

"Yes," Yara said. "Tell me."

Isolde pulled a small book from the folds of her grey dress—a slim volume bound in blue leather, disguised as a book of devotions. She opened it to a page dense with her handwriting, which grew shakier as it descended.

"I write what I lose," she said. "It started as a game. Then it became a map. Listen: 'The king no longer eats. He performs eating. Watch the jaw—it moves correctly, but the throat does not swallow until it must.' 'He called me by a pet name tonight. I don't have a pet name. He invented one, and I answered to it, and afterward I nearly wept and couldn't say why.' 'The east gallery has eleven windows. I counted them at my wedding. Today I counted nine, and the two missing ones leave no gaps in the wall, and no one else finds this strange.'" She looked up. "Eleven windows, taster. I stood in that gallery yesterday and counted *nine*, and I could not remember ever counting eleven, except that my own hand had written it. My own hand is the only witness I trust, and some mornings I wake and find entries I don't remember making, in handwriting going strange at the edges, like mine but *tired*."

Yara's skin crawled. Beside her poison records, in her own hidden log, she had written the same observations of the king: the ash-pale tongue, the eyes that catalogued. The queen had arrived at the edge of the truth alone, armed with nothing but a devotion-book and a stubbornness that grief had honed instead of dulled.

"Your Majesty," Yara said, choosing each word like a step across ice, "if I told you I believed you—if I told you I had seen signs myself, in the king's taste, in the city—what would you do with that?"

"Do?" The queen blinked. "I would act. I would—"

She stopped.

Yara watched it happen. Watched the thought form behind the queen's eyes—saw her lean toward it, saw her mouth begin to shape the next words—and then saw the smooth places around her eyes go smoother, like water closing over a thrown stone. The queen's gaze drifted, caught on the orange tree, and softened into pleasant blankness.

"—I would speak to my lord chancellor," she finished, in a different tone entirely, mild and distant. "Grain tariffs are a pressing matter this season. Forgive me, taster, I seem to have lost the thread. Why did I summon you?"

The lamps hissed. The night-bird hit the glass again, softer, and slid away.

Yara's chest ached in a place she hadn't known was exposed. This was worse than anything Corvane had shown her in the scrying-glass. The thing below the palace didn't just erase memories—it had learned to *tidy* them, to seal the wound with a pleasant thought so the victim never bled enough to notice. The queen was a fortress being dismantled from within, and each time she raised a banner, the enemy painted it her own colors.

"You summoned me," Yara said gently, "because you wanted a taster's opinion on the anniversary feast. The wines."

"Ah. Yes. The anniversary." The queen smiled, warm and hollow. "Ten years. He always remembers."

"He does," Yara said. "Majesty—may I offer one piece of advice? Unasked."

"Of course."

"Keep writing. Even when it seems foolish. Especially then. And hide the pages in more than one place, and never tell anyone where—including yourself, if you can manage it." Yara met her eyes and poured everything she could not say into the look: *Hold on. Someone is coming. Someone is trying.* "Paper remembers, Majesty. You said so yourself."

Something flickered far back in the queen's gaze—a fish turning in deep water, there and gone. "Did I say that?" she murmured. "How odd. It sounds like something worth saying."

She rose, and the audience was over; the mildness had closed over her like pond ice, safe and terrible. At the door she paused, one hand on the frame, and without turning said, very quietly, in a voice that was hers again for exactly one breath:

"If my husband is lost, find him. Whatever it costs. I would trade everything I have—even this, even *me*—to have one true day back."

Then she glided away down the corridor, grey silk and empty eyes, and a waiting lady-in-waiting fell in beside her speaking brightly of tariffs, and the queen laughed at something, easily, like a woman with nothing buried anywhere at all.

* * *

Yara walked out through the sleeping palace with the queen's words folded inside her like a coal wrapped in cloth.

*I would trade everything I have—even me.*

In the cellar archive, hours later, Vess woke when the door opened and read Yara's face in the lamplight with those ancient, child-dark eyes. The girl reached up and touched Yara's cheek, once, and spoke a single word of Root-Tongue—one of the few Yara had learned, one that cost her a summer afternoon and her mother's humming whenever she used it.

*Mourning,* the word meant. *Grieving for what remains.*

"Yes," Yara said hoarsely, sitting down among the ledgers. "That's exactly it."

She pulled the First Ledger's copy toward her—the binding ritual, the sealed pages Tam had risked everything to help her take—and smoothed it flat beneath her hands. The queen wanted her husband back. The queen would trade herself for one true day. And the ritual's final line, the one Yara had read forty times and not understood, stared up at her now from the yellowed vellum:

*A binding holds by a name freely given. An unbinding requires the same.*

Someone had to give a name. Freely. Completely. And Yara was beginning to understand, with a cold clarity that felt almost like relief, whose name it would have to be.
