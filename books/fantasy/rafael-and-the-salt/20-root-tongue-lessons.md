## 20. Root-Tongue Lessons

The archive at midnight smelled of salt and old paper and the faint mineral bite of the candles Corvane preferred—tallow rendered with brine, so that even the light tasted of the guild's trade. Yara had always found it comforting. Tonight it felt like standing inside a mouth.

She spread a wool blanket over the cold flagstones between the shelves of the First Collection, the oldest ledgers, the ones chained shut with links stamped by hands three centuries dead. Vess sat cross-legged on the blanket, scrubbed clean for once, her mud-colored hair drying in pale rings around her face. In her small palms she held a stub of white chalk.

"Again," Yara said, and sat down across from her.

Vess drew a single mark on the flagstone: a vertical line, forked at the top like a young tree, with a dot nested in the fork.

"*Sael*," she said. The word came out of her throat strange—not childish at all, but deep, resonant, as though the girl's body were a cave and something older than caves was speaking through it. "*Sael.*"

Yara repeated it. "*Sael.*"

And the world took something from her.

It happened the way a pickpocket works—she felt nothing leave, only noticed afterward that her pocket was lighter. She had been thinking, a moment ago, about the smell of her mother's kitchen. Woodsmoke and onions. A copper pot with a dented lid that rattled when it boiled. She had been *about* to think of it, reaching for it the way you reach for a warm thing in the dark—

—and her hand closed on nothing.

The kitchen was gone. Not faded. Gone, the way a page is gone when it has never been written. She knew she had had a mother; she knew there had been a kitchen somewhere at the start of her life. But the smell, the pot, the rattle of the lid—all of it had simply never existed, and the absence was so smooth she could run her mind along it without catching on a single edge.

"Sael," Vess said again, gently, watching her. "Home."

"I know what it means," Yara said. Her voice came out steady. Years at the king's table had taught her that: whatever happens behind your eyes, keep the voice level. "I know what it cost, too."

Vess looked at her with those gray eyes, old as well-water, and nodded slowly. Then she reached out and touched Yara's knee—the first time she had initiated contact—and left her hand there, a small warm weight, an apology or a promise. Perhaps both.

They had discovered the price two nights ago, quite by accident. Yara had learned four words before either of them understood what was happening. *Aren*, water. *Doln*, bread. *Mira*, sleep. *Hessa*, the word for a door that opens outward. It was only when she'd tried to remember the name of the orphanage matron—the woman who'd beaten her for stealing crusts, whose name Yara had carried like a stone for twenty years—that she'd found the stone missing too. Four words, four vanishings. Clean surgical absences, all of them from childhood, all of them from the years before the guild.

"The language eats to feed," Vess had said, in her broken court-tongue mixed with Root-Tongue, and Yara had understood: memory was the only coin the old words accepted. The guild traded in bottled grief and borrowed years, but the Root-Tongue went deeper than any ledger. It traded in what made a person.

She should have stopped then. Any sensible woman would have stopped.

Instead she had come back the next night. And the next. And tonight she sat down on the blanket and said, "Teach me more."

Because the unbinding needed the Root-Tongue. Because the king's true name lived somewhere in these words. Because every lesson brought them closer to the under-court, to the thing wearing the crown, to the nine days that were now eight and would soon be seven.

And because—she admitted it only to herself, in the honest dark of the archive—because when Vess spoke, when the old syllables filled the room, Yara felt less alone than she had ever felt in her life. The language was older than loneliness. Speaking it felt like being remembered by the world itself.

She was paying for that feeling with her childhood, one word at a time, and she kept coming back anyway.

* * *

"Doln," Vess said, drawing the sign: a circle open at the bottom, resting on a short stem. "Bread."

"Bread," Yara repeated, and lost the taste of the first loaf she ever stole—a warm roll off a baker's windowsill in the Tanner's Quarter, eaten in an alley with her heart hammering, so hot it burned her fingers, so good she had nearly wept. She knew she had stolen bread as a child. She could not remember how it tasted. The knowledge remained; the experience was amputated.

"It is strange," she said, when she could speak again. "I don't mourn them while they're going. I only notice afterward. Like—" She searched for the shape of it. "Like walking downstairs in the dark and your foot expects one more step than there is. Just for a moment. Then nothing."

Vess considered this with the gravity of a much older creature. Then she said, in careful court-tongue: "My teacher. Before. She said—" The girl frowned, hunting for words. "Root-Tongue is not learned. It is *traded*. Everything true is. The guild knows this. That is why the guild is what it is." Her small chin lifted. "You are trading better than they do. They take from other people. You take from yourself."

"That doesn't make me noble," Yara said. "It makes me the only one with anything left to spend."

"No." Vess shook her head, firm. "It makes you *saelsa*. Willing." She drew another sign, quick and sure: two parallel strokes joined by a curve, like a yoke. "Willing. The word the binding needs. The king must be unbound by someone *saelsa*—someone who gives freely, knowing the price. The thing beneath the crown cannot be fought. It can only be out-given."

Yara stared at the little chalk yoke. "Out-given."

"It ate a kingdom's memories because no one ever gave it anything. It only knows taking. Taking is all it understands." Vess's voice dropped into that cave-deep register again, and for a moment the child seemed like a doorway with wind moving through it. "So we give until it chokes."

Outside the archive, somewhere far above, a bell marked the third hour. Yara counted silently. Eight days now. Maybe seven by the time they climbed back up.

"Again," she said.

* * *

They worked until the candles guttered. By the fifth hour Yara had spent six more memories and gained eleven words, and she had begun to map the exchange rate with a ledger-keeper's precision:

*Aren*—water—had cost her the memory of rain on the orphanage roof, the drumming sound she used to fall asleep to. She missed it more than she expected. Sleep had gotten harder since.

*Vosh*—fire—had taken the bonfires of some festival whose name was already gone; she remembered that festivals had happened, remembered orange light on happy faces, but the faces had no features now, and the warmth was only information, not sensation.

*Kell*—the word for *name*, the most important word, the one Vess saved for last—had cost her the single most precious thing she owned: the sound of her own mother's voice saying *Yara*. She hadn't known it was still in her. She hadn't known she still carried it, folded small, all these years. When it left, she felt it go like a tooth pulled from the jaw—a shocking absence, a new hollow she kept touching with her tongue. She knew her name. She could write it. But the sound of it in her mother's mouth, the particular way it had been sung rather than spoken—

Gone. Spent on a word meaning *name*, which was almost funny, if anything was.

She did not cry. She had trained herself out of crying at eight years old, at the same table where she'd trained herself out of dying. But her hands shook as she copied the eleventh sign into her notebook, beside her poison records and the king's symptoms, and Vess watched the shaking and did not pretend not to see.

"You are almost empty," the girl said quietly.

"I'm not empty. I'm a taster. Emptiness is the job." Yara capped her ink. "Besides, everything I'm losing happened before the guild. Before I was anyone. What's left—" she tapped her chest— "is all mine. Bought and paid for."

Vess crawled across the blanket and put her arms around Yara's waist, pressing her face into the wool of her dress. She was so small. She smelled of chalk and river water and, faintly, of the salt-white coin she still wore on its cord around her neck.

"*Saelsa*," she said into the fabric. Willing.

Yara held her and looked at the candle flame and thought: *This is what they mean. This is why the old stories warn you about bargains. Not because the price is high. Because you'll pay it gladly.*

* * *

Tam found them at dawn.

He came down the archive stairs with his arms full of copied writs and his collar askew—he'd slept at his desk in the records office again, three nights running—and he stopped halfway down when he saw them: the royal food-taster and the mute-turned-articulate child sitting on a blanket among the chained ledgers, chalk marks covering the flagstones like frost.

"I knocked," he said. "Upstairs. For a long time."

"We didn't hear you." Yara gathered the chalk pieces into their box without looking up. "What have you got?"

"Two things. One bad, one worse." He came the rest of the way down and crouched beside the blanket, and Yara noticed—she had been noticing for weeks, and pretending not to—that he looked at Vess first, always first, checking she was whole, before he looked at anyone else. "The bad: the palace kitchens received a special order yesterday. A feast for the conclave masters, day after tomorrow. Which means the conclave is convening early, which means they've decided something."

"They've decided to deliver her." Yara's voice stayed level. Inside, something turned over like a boat.

"Or they're debating it. Either way, they're meeting, and meetings end in decisions." Tam ran a hand through his ink-stained hair. "The worse thing: I pulled the household rolls, like you asked. Cross-referenced against the census fragments from before the gray-outs." He hesitated. "Yara, the losses aren't random anymore. They're organized. Whole districts are going blank in sequence—riverside first, then the markets, then up the hill. It's eating inward. Toward the palace. Toward the throne."

"Toward the strongest memories," Yara said. "The densest ones. It's starving, so it's going for the marrow."

"And the census clerks don't even see it. I write the corrections into the margins and by morning my own corrections are gone and I've forgotten writing them. I only know because I started leaving myself notes sealed with wax under my boot heel." He showed her, absurdly, the crumpled wax seal hidden in his shoe. "I'm keeping a diary my future self won't believe."

Despite everything, despite the hollow places where her childhood used to be, Yara laughed—a short, real laugh that surprised all three of them. Vess smiled up at Tam with the unguarded delight of a child, and Yara saw him go slightly pink, and catalogued that too, in the place where she kept things too dangerous to feel yet.

"Teach me one," Tam said suddenly.

Both of them looked at him.

"A Root-Tongue word." He set his jaw the way he did before forging a signature. "If you're both spending yourselves on this, I'm not going to stand outside the shop window. Teach me one."

"No," Yara said, faster than she meant to.

"Why? Because it costs?"

"Because it takes from *before the guild*. From childhood. Yours is—" She stopped. Recalibrated. "You still have all of yours. Every memory you lose will be real loss. Mine are mostly ruins already."

"That's not your choice to make for me." He said it gently, but he said it. "You taught me that, actually. Or you didn't teach me—you just *are* it. Someone has to keep the honest record, Yara. Let me pay for my part of it."

She looked at Vess. Vess looked at Tam. Something passed between the girl and the scribe that Yara couldn't read, some assessment conducted in a currency older than kingdoms.

"One word," Vess said. "You choose blind. That is the rule. *Saelsa* means you cannot choose what you give."

Tam swallowed. Nodded.

Vess drew a sign on the flagstone: a horizontal stroke crossed by three descending ticks, like a bird's footprint in snow. "*Noven*," she said. "It means—" she glanced at Yara, and something mischievous and ancient moved in her gray eyes— "it means *stay*."

"*Noven*," Tam repeated.

Yara watched it happen. Watched the color change very slightly in his face, the way it did with all of them—one breath of vertigo, one foot expecting a step that wasn't there. She wondered which memory he'd paid. His father teaching him letters? A summer afternoon? Some small golden ordinary thing?

"What did you lose?" she asked, before she could stop herself.

"I don't know," he said. And then, wonderingly: "That's the trick of it, isn't it. I don't even know what I had." He looked at the chalk sign a long moment. "*Noven*," he said again, softly, testing the weight of what he'd bought. "Stay."

Then he cleared his throat and became practical again, spreading his copied writs across the blanket, and the three of them bent together over plans and names and timings while the dawn came gray through the archive's high slit-windows.

But twice that morning, Yara caught him looking at her—quick, unfinished looks, the look of a man trying to locate a word on the tip of his tongue—and she understood that whatever he'd spent, he'd spent it gladly, and that the word he'd bought was already doing its work.

* * *

That night, alone in her lodging above the chandler's shop, Yara took out her notebook and made an entry she had never made before. Not poison records. Not guild inventory. A private ledger, one line long:

*Memories remaining: unknown. Estimate: fewer than forty. Of those, perhaps ten belong to the years before the guild. Spend them wisely.*

She read it twice. Then, beneath it, in smaller script, she wrote the thing she had been circling for days without landing:

*The crowned thing takes because it is empty. The guild trades because it is empty. I am learning a language that empties me further, and I have never felt fuller. There is a lesson in that. I think it is the whole lesson.*

She blew out the candle.

In the next room, Vess slept in the narrow bed Yara had given her, and talked in her sleep—not nightmares, but recitation, a slow stream of Root-Tongue syllables rising and falling like tide. Yara stood in the doorway listening, hugging her shawl around herself, and tried to make out the words. Most were beyond her eleven. But one phrase recurred, over and over, soft as a lullaby, and she had learned enough by now to catch its shape:

*Saelsa doln. Saelsa doln mira.*

*Willing bread. Willing bread, sleep.*

Or—no. She turned it over. Word order in Root-Tongue ran backward to court-tongue. It wasn't *willing bread* at all.

It was *the bread gives itself willingly.*

Yara stood in the dark a long time, holding that phrase the way you hold a live coal, understanding that Vess was not talking in her sleep at all.

She was rehearsing.

And far away across the sleeping city, in the palace on the hill, bells began to ring—an unscheduled peal, urgent, wrong—and Yara was moving for her boots before the second toll, because in her bones, in the hollows where her childhood used to be, she already knew what news traveled on those bells:

The conclave had voted.
