## 21. The Bargain Renewed

The decoy arrived at the archive an hour past midnight, wrapped in a wool blanket that smelled of someone else's hearth.

Yara had not been told her name. That was the first cruelty of it. Corvane stood in the doorway with the bundle in his arms the way a man carries something he has already begun to grieve, and when Yara reached to pull back the blanket, his hand closed over hers—not rough, but final, like a door shutting.

"Don't," he said. "You'll want to know her name. Don't."

The girl beneath was perhaps seven, hollow-cheeked, sleeping with the terrible slackness of a child who had been given something to make her sleep. Her hair was pale as unbleached linen. Around her wrist, tied with guild-knot cord, hung a small lead tag stamped with the Salt Guild's mark: a circle broken by a vertical line, the sign of a thing sealed and a thing passing through.

"She's from the foundling house on Candlemaker's Row," Corvane said, and his voice did the thing it always did when he spoke of guild business—went flat and even, sanded smooth, like driftwood. "Debt-cleared. Collateralized at birth. The conclave voted this morning. Eleven masks for, three against, one abstention." He paused. "I abstained."

"You abstained." Yara heard her own voice come out strange, thin as wire. "She's a child, Corvane. Not a ledger entry. She has a name somewhere—someone gave it to her once."

"Someone gave it to her and then sold her debts twice before she could walk." He set the bundle down on the long table among the open ledgers, among Yara's careful columns of poison and symptom, and would not look at the child while he spoke. "The masters call it *renewal*. The bargain requires feeding, and feeding requires a vessel of sufficient... innocence. Unwritten things. A life that hasn't accumulated enough memory to be missed."

"And Vess?"

"Vess stays hidden. That part you've won—for now. The thing in the crown will accept what we give it or it won't. If it doesn't—" He finally looked up, and Yara saw that behind the flatness his eyes were wet and furious and old. "If it doesn't, then everything we've hidden was for nothing, and it takes the city anyway, and us with it. So pray it accepts."

* * *

They delivered her at dawn.

Yara insisted on going. She told herself it was duty—the taster's role extended now beyond food, beyond poison, into the tasting of intentions, and someone needed to watch the crowned thing's face when the gift was presented. But she knew the deeper truth as she dressed in her court grays and pinned back her hair: she wanted to see. She wanted to look into that wrong-eyed face and understand what they were buying with a seven-year-old girl, so that she could hate it precisely.

The palace in early morning was all pale stone and long shadows, servants moving through the corridors with the hushed efficiency of people who had learned not to remember anything worth remembering. Tam met her at the scullery stair, ink-stained, sleepless, his collar crooked.

"It's true, then," he whispered. "The whole hall's been scrubbed since midnight. New rushes. They're treating it like a coronation."

"Where?"

"The lesser audience chamber. The one with the salt-glazed windows." He hesitated, then caught her sleeve. "Yara. Whatever happens in there—you can't let your face do anything. You know that. You're the best I've ever seen at nothing, but today of all days—"

"I know what I am," she said, and hated how much it sounded like a boast, and hated more that it was true.

The audience chamber smelled of lilies, which was wrong; lilies were funeral flowers, and no one had ordered them, and yet forty stems stood in bronze urns along the walls as if the room itself had decided to hold a wake and lacked only the body. Courtiers lined the sides in their morning silks. The queen was absent. Yara noted that absence the way she noted flavors—a bitter note under the sweetness, something that didn't belong.

Corvane entered from the east door carrying the sleeping girl, and the chamber went silent in a way that felt rehearsed.

And then the king came in.

Yara had been watching him for weeks now, logging his small wrongnesses beside her poison records: the way food left his lips untouched by appetite, the way his laugh arrived a half-beat after the joke as though translated from another language, the way his shadow sometimes moved a fraction late. She had thought she was prepared. She had thought she understood what wore him.

She was not prepared for how *gracious* it looked.

The thing wearing King Aldric crossed the chamber like water finding its level, robes whispering, and there was warmth in its smile, real-seeming warmth, and it took Corvane's hand in both of its own and said, in the king's beloved voice, "You have served my house faithfully, Master Corvane. My house remembers."

*Your house does not remember,* Yara thought. *It eats.*

The girl was transferred to the king's arms with a formality that made Yara's stomach turn—arms outstretched, a bowing exchange between Corvane and the crowned thing, the child passed over like a dish at table. She did not wake. Whatever they'd dosed her with, it was thorough. Her head lolled against the king's shoulder, and the thing in the crown looked down at her with an expression of such tender hunger that Yara had to fix her eyes on the salt-glazed windows and count the panes to keep her face still. One. Two. Three.

"What is her name?" the king asked softly.

No one answered. The silence had been arranged. Yara realized, with a cold sliding feeling, that the guild had chosen a child whose name had already been traded away—sold, resold, filed in some ledger until the original record crumbled. A nameless vessel. Nothing to miss.

"How convenient," the crowned thing murmured, as if it had heard her thinking. Its eyes lifted and found Yara across the chamber—found her with the ease of a fisherman finding the one spot where the water moves—and it smiled at her with the king's mouth. "Taster. You look pale. Have you eaten this morning?"

"I break my fast after court, majesty," Yara said. Her voice came out level. Somewhere far away she admired it.

"Good," it said. "I should hate for you to miss a meal. Meals are how we come to know each other, aren't they? Every dish a little confession." It turned away, the girl still asleep against its chest, and Yara understood suddenly, viscerally, that it had known about her all along—that her double life, her ledgers, her hidden child were all known and permitted, permitted the way a farmer permits the fox near the henhouse because the fox amuses him. "Come to supper tonight," the thing called back over its shoulder. "All of you. We'll celebrate the season."

*What season?* Yara thought. And then the doors closed behind the king and the girl, and the chamber exhaled, and the courtiers began to murmur, and no one—not one soul in that room—could say what season they were celebrating, or why, only that it felt right to feel glad.

* * *

The loss came at moonrise, and it came everywhere at once.

Yara was in the archive with Vess when it happened, copying the day's observations by lamplight while the girl slept curled in the alcove behind the false entry that hid her from guild sight. There was no sound. No tremor. The world did not shudder. What happened was quieter than that and infinitely worse: the lamp flame bent sideways, though there was no draft, and every candle in the cellar leaned the same direction, toward the palace, like grass bending under wind.

Then the ink on the page in front of Yara began to fade.

Not smear. Not blot. *Fade*—the letters paling from black to gray to the ghost of gray to nothing, line by line, as though the page were being read backward by something patient and enormous. She snatched the quill up and wrote frantically over the vanishing words—*harvest recorded, autumn, the granaries full, the vintage pressed*—and watched her new ink hold for a moment, shimmer, and then dissolve like sugar in rain.

"No," she said aloud. "No, no—"

Vess woke. The girl sat up in the alcove, and her eyes were wide and streaming, and she began to speak in the Root-Tongue, rapid and distressed, her small hands shaping words that Yara had only begun to learn. She pointed at the ceiling. At the city above. She said a word Yara knew—*gone*, the word meant gone, the word for unwritten—and then she said it again and again, a litany, keening it.

Yara ran up the stairs two at a time and out into the street.

Candlemaker's Row was still there. The houses stood. The cobbles lay where cobbles lie. But the season was gone.

She knew it the way you know a tooth has been pulled—not by seeing the gap but by feeling the tongue keep returning to it. Summer had ended weeks ago; autumn stood around them in the cold stars and the smell of woodsmoke. And yet the entire warm half of the year had been scooped out of the world like the flesh of a fruit. She stopped a night-watchman and asked him what month it was, and he frowned at her with genuine confusion and said, "Month? It's autumn, mistress. It's been autumn always, near enough. Why d'you ask?" And when she asked him about the harvest—had it been good?—he scratched his head and said the granaries were full, which was odd, because he couldn't recall anyone filling them, and wasn't that strange, and then his face did something frightening: it slid off the question entirely, like water off glass, and he smiled and wished her a good night and walked on, content, empty where the summer used to be.

She ran. Through the market square, where the fountain still played but no one could say when it had been built. Past the chandler's, where the chandler's wife sat on her step weeping without knowing why, wringing her apron and saying, "There was a funeral. Wasn't there? Someone died this year. Someone I—" and then losing the thread, the grief dissolving mid-sob, her face smoothing into puzzled calm, and Yara had to walk away because if she stayed she would grab the woman by the shoulders and shout names at her and it would change nothing.

At the Temple of the Ledgered Saints—ironic ground, she thought wildly—a priest stood in the doorway reading the book of births aloud to himself over and over, faster each time, because the entries kept fading under his thumb and he kept re-reading to catch them before they went, and by the time Yara reached him the book held only blank vellum where June and July had been, and he looked up at her with the naked terror of a man watching his god die and said, "Mistress, were there children born this year? Tell me there were children born this year."

"There were," Yara said. "I'm sure there were."

"Then why can't I—" He looked down. The last line vanished as they both watched. He closed the book gently, the way you close the eyes of the dead.

Tam found her at the temple steps. He was white as paper, clutching a fistful of loose documents.

"The chancery," he gasped. "Contracts, deeds, marriage rolls—it's not just fading, Yara, it's *reorganizing*. The records are rewriting themselves around the hole. There's a whole season missing and the paper has decided it never happened. I watched a marriage contract seal itself blank. The couple's still married—they think—but neither of them remembers the wedding, and the witnesses—" His voice cracked. "My master signed as witness at six births this summer. He can't remember a single child. He thinks he's been ill. He's *glad* he's been ill, because being ill explains the gaps, and people would rather be ill than erased."

Behind him, across the square, a woman was calling a name into the dark—"Tolla! Tolla!"—walking in slow circles, certain she had lost someone, unable to say who Tolla was or whether Tolla had ever existed, only that her arms remembered holding weight and were now empty of even the memory of it.

Yara took Tam's arm and steered him toward the archive, because standing still felt like being eaten.

"They fed it a child," she said, and her voice came out low and shaking. "Eleven masks voted for, and they fed it a child, and this is what a season costs. One season, Tam. For one small vessel. Do you understand what that means? When the bargain runs short again—and it will run short again, sooner now, it's starving faster—they'll feed it something bigger. A district. A decade. Us."

"Then we get Vess out of the city," Tam said. "Tonight. Now."

"And go where?" Yara pushed open the archive door, and the lamplight below was steady again, and Vess stood at the bottom of the stairs waiting for them, small and grave, holding out the salt-white coin she had carried to Yara's door on the first night. "Every road out of this realm runs through memory, Tam. Every map, every milestone, every innkeeper who'd remember their faces. If it can eat a season, it can eat the roads behind us while we walk them." She descended the stairs slowly. "There's nowhere to run. There's only forward. Down, if Vess's memories are true. To the under-court. To the binding."

Vess pressed the coin into Yara's palm and closed her fingers over it, and then the girl did something she had never done before: she spoke a full sentence in the common tongue, halting and accented, each word placed like a stone across a stream.

"Not down first," Vess said. "First the crown comes off the wrong head. Or there is no down to go to."

From the shelf behind her, the First Ledger—sealed, chained, older than the kingdom—gave a single soft sound, like a page turning itself.

And in the lamplight, faint but unmistakable, fresh ink was blooming across its cover: a single new line, written in no hand Yara recognized, dated three days hence.

Her death-entry. Amended.
