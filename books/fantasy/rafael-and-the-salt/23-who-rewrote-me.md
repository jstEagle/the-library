## 23. Who Rewrote Me

The drop-house still smelled of the ambush. Blood had soaked into the floorboards between the tables, and no one had come to scrub it out—guild property, guild mess. Yara stood in the doorway with Vess's small hand locked in hers and counted the ways she should have died.

Three enforcers. Two crossbows. One knife that had found her ribs at an angle shallow enough to be luck or incompetence. The entry in her ledger had named the hour of her death precisely: *the ninth bell of evening, the third day of the Salt Moon.* She had walked into the drop-house at the ninth bell because some stubborn, fatalistic part of her had wanted to see whether the book was right.

She was still breathing at the tenth bell. Someone had made a mistake.

Or someone had made a choice.

* * *

Tam found the alteration first, because Tam found everything first—that was his gift and his curse, the scribe's eye that could not pass over an inconsistency without snagging on it like a nail in cloth. He sat in the archive's lamplight with the death-entry flattened before him, magnifying lens in hand, and he did not look up for a long time.

"Yara." His voice was strange. Careful. "Come here."

She came. Vess was asleep in the corner among the ledger crates, curled like a comma, one thumb hooked in her mouth—a child's habit that seemed impossible for something as old as Vess was. Yara knelt beside Tam and looked where his finger pointed.

Her own entry. Her own hand, from years ago, recording the standard death-notice format the guild used for collateralized members: name, debt standing, projected date of collection. Except this was not the entry she remembered writing. This was not the entry she had read a hundred times, the one that had haunted her since the night she'd discovered pages about herself she never wrote.

"This says the ninth bell," she said slowly. "It always said the eleventh."

"No," Tam said. "Look closer. It said the eleventh. Then someone changed it to the ninth."

He tilted the page toward the lamp. And there it was—the thing she would have missed, the thing almost anyone would have missed, because forgery of this quality deserved admiration before it deserved suspicion. The ink matched. The pressure of the quill matched. Even the tiny hesitation-stroke Yara herself made when crossing a *t* had been reproduced faithfully.

But paper remembers. Under the lens, in the raking light, the fibers showed a faint ghost beneath the surface—a shadow of different strokes, scraped away with a blade and rewritten. The original hour had been lifted off the page like a stain drawn out of linen.

"The eleventh bell," Yara whispered. "They were supposed to come at eleven. I went at nine. If I'd gone at eleven—"

"They'd have been waiting two hours early," Tam finished quietly. "You'd have walked into them exactly on schedule. Exactly as written."

Silence filled the archive, the particular silence of old paper and older secrets. Somewhere above them, the city went about its evening, losing another street name, another song, another face in a crowd, while three people crouched over a forged line of ink and understood that someone had spent something enormous to keep one woman alive.

"Who has access to my entries?" Yara asked, though her stomach already knew.

"Ledger-keepers. Handlers. Conclave masters." Tam set down the lens. "And one other person who's audited your file more times than any record shows he should have."

* * *

Corvane kept rooms above the salt warehouse on Cindermarrow Lane, a place Yara had visited perhaps six times in fifteen years—always for business, always with the door open, always with the sense that the man behind the desk was less a person than an institution wearing a person's coat. He answered her knock on the second try, and when he opened the door, the lamplight behind him caught his face, and Yara saw what exhaustion looked like on a man who had spent decades pretending he didn't have one.

"You found it," he said. Not a question.

"You knew I would find it."

"I hoped you wouldn't. I prayed you would." He stepped back to let her in. "Both things. That's what being a handler is, Yara. Holding two true things until they cut your hands."

The room was spare to the point of penance: a bed, a desk, a shelf of ledgers that were, she realized with a small cold shock, all blank. Empty books. Hundreds of them, waiting to be filled or never filled, their pages white as salt.

"Thirty years," she said.

Corvane closed his eyes.

"I checked the exchange rates," she went on, and her voice stayed level because fifteen years of tasting poison had taught her voice to stay level while everything under it screamed. "Life-years trade against recorded time at the standard conversion. Thirty years of a handler's recorded life buys—" She stopped. Recalculated. "Buys one alteration of one entry, if the alteration is undetectable by conclave audit. Which yours nearly was."

"Nearly," Corvane agreed. He crossed to the window. Below, the city flickered—literally flickered, these days, streets graying at the edges like old photographs left in the sun. "Ask me why."

"Why?"

"That's the question you're supposed to ask. Everyone asks how. You're the only one who asked why." He laughed, and there was no humor in it, only rust. "I don't know if I can answer. I've been trying to answer it myself for three days, sitting here with these empty books, wondering if I'm going mad."

"Then tell me what you know."

He turned. And for the first time in fifteen years of service, Yara watched Corvane take off the institution and stand there in just the man—older-looking suddenly, smaller, his hands hanging at his sides like tools whose purpose had been forgotten.

"When you were eight years old," he said, "you were brought to the guild with a debt of four silver marks and a fever that should have killed you. Do you remember?"

"I remember the fever."

"I sat with you. Three nights. It wasn't my job—I had a dozen collaterals that season—but I sat with you anyway, and on the third night you woke, and you looked at me, and you said—" His throat worked. "You said, 'Are you the one who owns me?' And I said yes, because it was true, and you nodded, very seriously, like a little judge, and you said, 'Then you should decide what I'm worth.'"

Yara's breath caught. She had no memory of this. The fever had burned most of that year away.

"And I thought," Corvane continued, "that I had never heard anything so terrible or so true in thirty years of this work. Because I *should* decide. We all should. Every one of us who keeps the ledgers, every master behind every mask—we should all be forced to say aloud what a person is worth, instead of hiding behind numbers and conversions and the founding bargain." He shook his head. "And I couldn't do it. I couldn't put a number on you. So I did what cowards do. I deferred the judgment. Year after year. Your audits came due and I filed extensions. Your immunity training finished and I assigned you to the king's table, which was dangerous but also—it kept you *visible*, do you understand? A taster who dies is a tragedy. A taster who lives is an asset. Assets get maintained."

"You protected me."

"I *postponed* you." His voice cracked on it. "There's a difference, and the difference is thirty years of my life, apparently, because when the conclave ordered your collection—" He stopped. Started again. "When they voted, I went back to the archive that night, and I took out my own ledger, and I looked at what remained of my recorded life. Sixty-one years, banked and traded and borrowed against, the way we all do it, the way the guild taught us to think of ourselves as sums." He held up his hands, and Yara saw they were trembling. "And I thought about the boy I was before. Before the guild bought my debts. I had a sister, Yara. Did you know that? I had a sister who sang badly and loved plum cakes and died at nineteen because the guild collected her years to pay a debt our father owed, and I joined the guild afterward because I wanted to understand the arithmetic that killed her. And I understood it. That was the worst part. I understood it completely, and I stayed, because understanding felt like a kind of loyalty."

The lamp flame bent in a draft. Vess stirred in Yara's memory—not here, but somewhere safe—and Yara thought of a mud-soaked child speaking a language older than kingdoms, and of a king unbound from his own body, and of a guild that fed its god with the world's remembering.

"So when they came for you," Corvane said, "I finally decided what you were worth. And the number was everything I had left."

"Why?" It was her turn to ask it properly. "Truly. Not the fever story. Why spend thirty years on someone you postponed for fifteen?"

Corvane looked at her for a long moment, and then he did something she had never seen him do. He sat down. He sat on the edge of his narrow bed like a man whose legs had finally presented their invoice, and he put his face in his hands, and when he spoke, his voice came out muffled and old.

"Because you remind me of who I was before the guild. Not in what you do—in what you refuse. You taste poison every day and you feel nothing, and everyone believes you feel nothing, and I have read your entries, Yara, hundreds of them, and I know the truth. You catalog grief so carefully because you have so much of it and nowhere to put it. You memorize which noble wants which rival dead because you cannot bear that murder should be *casual*. You keep perfect records because somewhere inside you still believes that if the truth is written down precisely enough, it will win." He lowered his hands. His eyes were wet. "That was me. Before. I believed that. And the guild taught me better, and I let it, and I have hated myself in a quiet, professional way for forty years. But you—you never let it teach you. They dosed you with numbness and you stayed human underneath it, like a coin at the bottom of a well, still gold, just far down." He stood, abruptly, as if sitting had cost him too much. "So no. I didn't spend thirty years on you. I spent them on the idea that I might not be entirely lost. On the last evidence that the person I was before still exists somewhere in the world, walking around, tasting poison, keeping true books."

The room was silent except for the city's faint gray flicker below the window.

"The conclave will audit eventually," Yara said. "They'll find the ghost-strokes under the ink. When they do—"

"They'll collect what remains of my recorded life. All of it. What's left after the thirty." He shrugged, a terrible casual shrug. "Eleven years. I'll be collected down to nothing, Yara. Erased from every record, forgotten by everyone whose memory is anchored to a ledger entry. I'll become what the crowned thing eats." He met her eyes. "Worth it. Ask me again tomorrow and I'll say worth it again."

"Then come with us." The words were out before she could weigh them. "The archive, the under-court—all of it. You know the binding ritual's location in the First Ledger. You know the conclave's movements. You know things about the founding bargain that aren't written anywhere I can reach."

"I know things that would get you killed faster."

"You're already dying by inches. Eleven recorded years, Corvane. Spend them *moving*."

He turned to the window again. Below, a lamplighter worked his way down Cindermarrow Lane, and where his flame passed, the street held its shape a moment longer than the ones around it—as if fire itself were reluctant to forget.

"There's something else," Corvane said quietly. "Since you're asking me to spend my last years on a lost cause, you should know what the conclave voted yesterday. After the ambush failed. After they realized someone had altered your entry." He did not turn around. "They didn't suspect me. They suspected *you*—that you'd forged your own escape, that you have abilities the ledgers never recorded. So they've done something I've never seen in the guild's history."

"What?"

"They've opened the First Ledger." His reflection stared at her from the dark glass, hollow-eyed. "The sealed book. The founding bargain itself. And Yara—the masters are reading it aloud. Page by page. In the conclave chamber. Tonight."

"Why would they—"

"Because the First Ledger doesn't just record the bargain," Corvane said. "According to the oldest handlers' rumor, the one we're all told is superstition—it *is* the bargain. The words on those pages are the binding. And they're reading it aloud to something." He finally turned, and his face in the lamplight was the face of a man watching his life's work burn. "They're not feeding it a child anymore, Yara. They're renegotiating. And whatever new terms they strike tonight—there may not be an archive left to save. There may not be a *city*."

Outside, the lamplighter reached the end of the lane, and Cindermarrow Street began, very slowly, to fade.
