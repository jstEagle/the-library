## 24. Into the Under-Court

Vess woke them before dawn, standing at the foot of Yara's cot with her small hand pressed flat against the floorboards, the way she did when something beneath them was calling.

"Again," Yara said, and her voice came out rough with sleep and dread. "You're hearing it again."

The girl nodded. In three weeks she had not spoken a word of Common, but she had learned to make herself understood all the same—her hands, her eyes, the terrible patience of a child who remembered being much older than she looked. Now she pointed down, once, twice, insistent, and then drew in the dust on the hearthstone the shape Yara had come to know too well: a crown, and beneath it, coiled, a serpent with its tail in its mouth.

"She says it's louder," Tam said from his corner, already dressed. He had taken to sleeping in his clothes. They all had. "It's been getting louder every night since the conclave. You told me that yourself."

Yara swung her legs off the cot and pressed her palms against her eyes. "I was hoping I'd imagined agreeing to this."

"You didn't agree." Tam's voice was gentle. "You decided. There's a difference. Agreements can be renegotiated."

* * *

They went into the palace through the tasters' door, because it was the only door in the whole sprawling pile of stone where no one questioned Yara Eriksen's presence. The kitchen corridors were already waking—the first bakers sweating over their ovens, the scullery boys hauling water—and Yara moved through the steam and noise with her ledger-sense open the way Corvane had taught her twenty years ago: *count everything, trust nothing, and when you must be invisible, be busy.* She carried a tray of morning dishes she had no intention of delivering. Tam carried his scribe's case. Vess rode on Yara's hip like a parcel, her face buried in Yara's neck, her small body humming with a tension Yara could feel through her own ribs.

The other tasters saw them pass and said nothing. That was the pact they had sworn in Maren's cellar two nights past, six orphan-born men and women pressing their scarred tongues to a shared dish of salted bread: *we are each other's eyes now.* Sera had walked the eastern stair an hour earlier and reported it clear. Odo had marked which guards took bribes and which took pride, and neither kind would stop a food-taster carrying breakfast to whoever paid for it.

"The undercroft stairs behind the wine cellar," Yara murmured to Tam as they descended past level after level of stored wealth. "Every servant knows about the wine cellar. Nobody thinks about what's under it."

"And what is under it?"

"I don't know yet. That's rather the point."

The truth was that Yara had known for years that something lay below the palace foundations—every guild ledger-keeper knew, because the oldest entries referenced it obliquely, in the careful circumlocution the First Masters had used for dangerous things. *The lower court. The place of the first signing. The room where the salt was struck.* She had assumed, in the comfortable way one assumes about foundations, that it was rubble. A sealed relic. A story the guild told itself about its own antiquity.

Then Vess had begun remembering.

It happened in fragments, always at night, always with the girl sitting bolt upright and speaking that flowing consonant-heavy language while the candle flames bent toward her as if listening. And every word she spoke did something. A street three districts over had reappeared out of nowhere—a whole crooked lane called Tanner's Walk that no map recorded and forty people suddenly swore they'd lived on all their lives. A lullaby came back to a midwife who had forgotten she'd ever had children. Each fragment Vess recovered was a wound in the world closing, and each closing made the thing beneath the crown hungrier, angrier, more desperate—and made Vess's memories come faster, as though the two of them were pulling on opposite ends of the same thread.

Last night, in the middle of one of her speaking fits, the girl had drawn the way down. Not a map exactly—an account. Ledger-style, Yara realized later, with a start. Columns and entries, the palace written as a stack of layers, and at the bottom, in Root-Tongue characters Yara could only half read but fully feel, a single word that translated, as near as she could manage, to *beginning.*

* * *

The door behind the wine racks was older than anything it touched.

That was the first wrongness Yara noticed: everything above was pale limestone, palace-cut, maybe two centuries old. But the frame she found when Odo's borrowed key turned and the great rack swung out on its hidden hinge—that was black granite, veined with white like frozen smoke, and the stone was worn smooth in the center of the door as if by ten thousand hands. Or by one hand, ten thousand times.

Beyond it, stairs went down. Not palace stairs. These were cut in a spiral, wide enough for four abreast, and they had been built for processions—for crowds, for ceremony, for the movement of many bodies toward something important. Torches still stood in iron brackets every dozen steps, and when Tam lit the first one, the flame burned strangely steady, as if the air itself were holding its breath.

"How far down?" he whispered.

"Until it stops being the palace," Yara said.

Vess slid off her hip and took her hand. Then, deliberately, the girl took Tam's hand too, and led them downward.

The temperature dropped. The smell changed—from torch-smoke and cold stone to something mineral and old, a smell like the inside of a geode, like salt left too long in a closed box. Yara's tongue, that ruined, trained, poisoned instrument, began to ache. It was the same ache she'd felt the night the child arrived at her door, the ache that meant *old magic, concentrated, close.* Her mouth flooded and she spat, discreetly, and tasted salt. Just salt. But underneath the salt, faint as a ghost note in a heavy sauce, something sweet and rotten that made her hindbrain scream *do not swallow.*

"What do you taste?" Tam asked. He had learned to ask her that the way other men asked the time.

"Preservation," she said. "Something down here has been kept fresh."

The walls began to change as they descended. At first there were only tool marks, ancient chisel strokes. Then came carvings—shallow, eroded figures that might have been dancers or mourners or reapers. Then, lower still, writing: rows of Root-Tongue characters marching around the spiral like a tide line, and Yara stopped at the first full panel and held her torch close, and her blood went cold in a way that had nothing to do with the temperature.

"It's a ledger," she breathed.

"What?"

"This—" she traced the columns with a finger, not touching, never touching—"it's formatted exactly like our books. Debits. Credits. Terms of exchange. This isn't decoration, Tam. This is the original contract. Carved into the bedrock so it couldn't be edited." She laughed, and hated the sound of it. "Do you understand? We kept paper because paper can be rewritten. The founders wanted this version permanent. Which means somewhere in these lines is the bargain itself, in full, unredacted—"

"Yara." Tam's voice had gone very quiet. He was looking further down the wall, where the carved text continued, and where, here and there, entire passages had been defaced—chiseled out, gouged smooth, in patterns too systematic to be vandalism.

"Some of it's been erased," he said. "From stone."

"Nothing gets erased from stone," Yara said. "Not without enormous effort. Not without someone being *terrified* of what it said."

Vess pulled both their hands. Downward.

* * *

They heard the water before they reached it—a vast, slow dripping, each drop landing with the resonance of a struck bell. The spiral opened at last into a gallery, and the gallery opened into the room.

Yara had prepared herself for a crypt. What she found was a throne room.

It was smaller than the king's hall above—intimate, almost, a chamber perhaps eighty feet long—but every proportion of it was designed to make the body feel like an intruder. The ceiling descended in stepped coffers to a dark apex. The floor was black granite polished to a mirror, and their torchlight doubled and tripled across it until it seemed they stood on the edge of a lake of flame. Along both walls ran tiers of stone benches, empty, waiting, the parliament of some polity older than any kingdom Yara could name. And at the far end, on a low dais, stood a throne.

It was not grand. That was the horror of it. The throne above, in the king's hall, was gilt and towering and ridiculous; this one was a plain chair of black wood, salt-white with age, its arms worn concave by use. Whoever had sat here had sat here often, patiently, for a very long time. Behind it, carved deep into the living rock, rose the serpent with its tail in its mouth—the circle, the eating of oneself—and within the coil of its body, at the height of a seated figure's chest, was a recess shaped precisely like the salt-white coin Vess had carried to Yara's door.

The coin. Yara's hand went to her pocket, where she had carried it every day since. It was warm. It had never been warm before.

"He sat here," she said aloud, and her voice fell into the room and did not echo—it was absorbed, eaten, the acoustics drinking sound the way sand drinks water. "The true king. Before. This was his seat. This was the first court, before there was a kingdom to hold a court." She walked forward slowly, reading the walls, and the ledger-keeper in her was translating even as the woman in her recoiled. "And this is where they bound it. Right here. In front of witnesses." She gestured at the benches. "A whole assembly. They watched the signing happen. And then they built a city on top of it and forgot, because forgetting was part of the price—"

She stopped.

On the dais, directly before the throne, the floor was stained.

Not with age. With salt. A great spreading bloom of white crystals, furred and glittering in the torchlight, growing out of a seam in the black stone like frost given permanence. It spread in a rough circle perhaps ten feet across, densest at the center, thinning at the edges—and at its heart, half-swallowed, half-preserved, lay things.

Bones, first. Small ones. Yara knelt despite every instinct, and her trained eye read them the way she read dishes: bird, mostly, and one cat, and something larger she chose not to name. But among the bones were objects that made her stomach turn over. A child's wooden horse, its paint miraculously bright. A wedding ring fused to a finger-bone. Dozens of letters, tied in bundles, their ink faded but legible. A soldier's medal. A lock of hair braided with ribbon. A shoe. One small leather shoe, salt-stiffened, its buckle still bright.

"These aren't offerings," she whispered. "These are *remains.*"

Tam crouched beside her, and his face, in the torchlight, was the face of a man doing arithmetic he didn't want finished. "Things people couldn't bear to lose," he said slowly. "Or things that were taken from them. Memories leave... residue, maybe. When they're eaten. When they're traded." He looked up at the serpent on the wall. "This is where it fed first. This is its feeding place. Everything it ever swallowed left its bones here."

Vess walked into the circle of salt without hesitation.

"Vess, no—" Yara started forward, but the girl turned and shook her head, calm, certain, and knelt at the center of the bloom, and laid both palms flat against the crusted white ground.

What happened next, Yara would remember all her life in pieces, because it did not proceed in order.

The salt *sang*. A tone too low for ears, felt in the teeth and the breastbone, and every crystal in the vast circle lit from within with a pale gold light, and images rose off the floor like heat-shimmer: faces, hundreds of faces, thousands, flickering past—people laughing, grieving, working, holding children, none of them pausing, none of them looking out, all of them *being remembered* by the room for one suspended moment before dissolving back into the white. And in the middle of the flood, one image held: a man on the black throne, young-faced, tired-eyed, wearing a simple circlet instead of a crown, and he was looking directly at Vess with an expression of such naked grief and love that Yara's throat closed.

He raised one hand—not in blessing, not in warning. In *recognition.*

Then the light died. The salt went gray and silent. Vess swayed, and Yara caught her, and the girl was weeping without sound, great shaking silent sobs, and clutching at Yara's collar with both fists.

"She saw him," Tam said hoarsely. "She saw—"

"I know what she saw." Yara held the child and stared over her bowed head at the empty throne, at the serpent coiled above it, at the recess in the carving that fit a coin she now understood had been a key all along. "He put her outside before it took him. He cast out the last piece of his own soul and hid it in a body small enough to walk unnoticed, and he gave her a coin and sent her to find the one person in this kingdom who keeps accounts." Her laugh cracked in the middle. "Because he knew. Even then, he knew that whatever else happened, someone would have to *read the terms.*

The torches guttered, all together, though there was no draft.

Above them—or around them, or *through* them—something shifted in the fabric of the room. The mirrored floor darkened. The salt bloom seemed to lean, all at once, toward the throne. And from the black apex of the ceiling, from the stepped darkness where the coffers met, came a sound that Yara felt before she heard: a slow, wet, patient exhalation, like the settling of a house, like the turning of a page in a book that had been waiting a very long time for its reader.

Vess lifted her tear-streaked face and spoke a single word of the Root-Tongue, and this time Yara understood it without translation, the way she understood poison:

*Found.*

Down in the dark beneath the dais, behind the throne, in a place where no door had been, something answered.

It knocked.
