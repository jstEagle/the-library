## 26. The Crowned Thing Speaks

The scrying-glass did not sleep, and neither, it seemed, did I.

I had told myself I would rest. After what the First Ledger had shown me—the guild as gullet, the trades as meals, centuries of borrowed grief and stolen years funneling down into that patient, buried hunger—I had walked home through streets gone gray at their edges, past houses whose owners' names had slipped out of the tax rolls like water through fingers, and I had lain down on my narrow bed with Vess curled against my side like a warm coal, and I had closed my eyes.

Sleep lasted less than an hour.

The glass sat in the corner of my lodging where I'd hidden it behind a false panel, wrapped in wool to muffle whatever it was glass did when no one watched. But I felt it before I heard it—a pressure behind my eyes, the same sensation I got when a poison touched my tongue a half-second before taste confirmed it. Recognition without evidence.

I rose. Vess stirred but did not wake; she slept the way she always did now, deeply, greedily, as though every hour of untroubled rest were a debt being repaid to her. I unwrapped the glass.

It showed me the throne room.

Not the real one—the one above, where petitioners shuffled and heralds droned—but lit for no occasion I recognized. Candles burned in their hundreds, and the light they gave was wrong, too steady, the way candlelight looks in paintings rather than life. And on the throne, chin propped on one hand in a posture the true king had never once assumed in all my years of tasting for him—

He was looking directly at me.

"You keep strange hours, little ledger-keeper," said the mouth that wore my king's face. "But then, you always have."

Every instinct I owned told me to put the glass down. The guild's oldest training lived in my spine: never engage the mark, never let the mark see your fear, never let anything see your face twice. I had spent twenty years becoming furniture to powerful people. Furniture does not speak. Furniture is not spoken to.

I spoke anyway.

"Who am I talking to?"

The thing smiled with the king's mouth, and the smile was fluent, practiced, worn smooth as a river stone from centuries of use. It did not reach the eyes. The eyes were the problem. They had been the problem since the first dish passed the king's lips untasted—eyes like wells you could drop a lantern down and hear nothing hit bottom.

"Such a blunt question." It rose from the throne, unhurried, and paced toward the glass until its face filled it entirely, close enough that I could see the pores of skin it was borrowing, the faint stubble along the jaw, the small scar above the left brow that Rafael Aldemar had earned at fourteen falling from a horse. All of it perfect. All of it a costume fitted so long ago the wearer had forgotten it was a costume. "You know the answer. You've read the answer. You held the First Ledger in your clever hands not six hours ago and learned exactly what has been eating at this kingdom's table. So let us skip the theater. You are tired of theater. I can see it in you—you have been tired of theater since you were a child swallowing other people's poisons."

My hand had gone to my throat without my permission. To the old scars there, the ones Vess's words had made ache on the first night.

"Here is what I propose," the thing said. "A trade. One last trade, between professionals. I understand you better than anyone alive understands you, Yara Eriksen. Better than Corvane, who thinks he knows you because he bought your debts. Better than the scribe who loves you and doesn't yet know why he can't stop thinking about you. I know you because I have been reading the ledgers longer than your guild has existed. Every entry. Every name surrendered. Including—" and here it paused, delicately, the way a surgeon pauses over the exact spot to cut— "*yours.*"

"I don't make trades," I said. My voice came out level. Twenty years of tasting had taught me that trick if it taught me nothing else: how to keep the voice flat while everything underneath screamed.

"No," it agreed pleasantly. "You *record* them. Which makes you worse than a trader, little keeper. That makes you the memory of the trade itself. And memory—" the smile widened a fraction— "is my favorite food."

* * *

It talked for a long time. I let it.

That was the taster's discipline again. When a dish might be poisoned, you do not spit at the first wrong note; you let the whole flavor assemble itself, because sometimes the poison hides beneath sweetness, and sometimes beneath bitterness, and only the complete architecture of the taste will tell you where. So I stood in my cold room with the wool-wrapped glass in my hands and I listened to the crowned thing lay out its offer like courses at a state dinner.

"Your name," it began. "Let us start there, since we both know it is the wound that never closes."

The room seemed to tilt. I gripped the windowsill.

"The Salt Guild took your name when you were nine years old and wrote it into a book as collateral against bread and shelter and immunity training. You have lived thirty years as a woman who technically does not exist. No document bears you. No deed, no registry, no parish roll. If you died tonight, the city would bury a body belonging to no one." The king's face tilted, almost tenderly. "I can give it back. Not merely return the entry—I can do better than the guild ever dreamed. I can write you into the world's memory itself. Every person who ever brushed past you would suddenly remember you were there. Your childhood neighbors would recall the girl who fed sparrows on their step. The matron at the orphanage would remember your birthday. You would be *real*, Yara Eriksen, in a way no ledger-entry has ever made anyone real. You would be remembered by everyone."

"And in exchange," I said, "you want Vess."

"In exchange," it said, "I want what I was promised. What your precious founders bound themselves to deliver. A vessel. A fragment of soul adrift in a borrowed shape, carrying memories that do not belong to her. She is a loose thread, little keeper. She unravels my meal. Every street she remembers back into existence, every song she restores to some washerwoman's lips—that is food taken from my mouth and handed back to cattle. Do you understand what she is doing to this kingdom?" For the first time, something moved behind the well-eyes—not anger, something older and hungrier than anger. "She is making them *full*. Full people do not need me. Full people do not forget, and forgetting is the door I walk through. She is locking the door from inside the house."

"She's a child," I said.

"She is a *key*," it said, "and keys are made to be used. Your own guild understood that once. Before sentiment rotted them." It resumed its seat on the throne, arranging the king's limbs with a proprietor's ease. "But let us move to the second course. Your health."

My breath stopped. Not dramatically—just stopped, the way a clock stops, mid-tick.

"Thirty years of dosing," it murmured. "Arsenic in wine. Nightshade in broth. Mercury salts in the sauces, ground pearl in the sweetmeats. You built an immunity the way a mason builds a wall—one stone at a time, each dose a little death refused. Admirable. Truly. But walls crack, Yara Eriksen. Your liver is failing. Your blood carries more metal than water. You have perhaps two months of clear-headedness left, and then the tremors begin, and then the blindness, and then—" it spread the king's hands, gracious, generous— "well. You've recorded enough deaths to know how the final entries read. I can undo all of it. Every dose, reversed. Thirty years returned to your body. You would live to be ninety, hale and sharp, and you would die in a bed, old, with people around you who knew your name. Isn't that the dream you've never permitted yourself to think? Not wealth. Not power. Just—to be someone, and to last."

I became aware that my hands were shaking. I folded them, the way I did at the tasting table when a dish was dangerous and delicious at once, when part of me wanted it desperately and the discipline was all that stood between wanting and taking.

"And Tam," the thing said softly. "Third course. Shall I describe him? Young, ambitious, honest in a way that should have gotten him killed years ago and would have, if honesty weren't so rare the powerful mistake it for usefulness. He has noticed discrepancies in the palace records. He has begun asking questions. Questions are dangerous here. And he asked them for *you*." The head cocked. "He is currently asleep above the scriptorium with a half-forged writ in his desk drawer and my patience wearing thin as vellum. Keep refusing me, and I will not need to harm him. I will simply let the machine do what machines do. The guild will find him. The conclave will decide he knows too much. There will be an entry in a ledger, and within the month, a scribe nobody remembers will have died of a fever nobody questioned. Clean hands. Clean books. Or—" the smile again, the terrible fluent smile— "surrender the vessel, and he lives to a ripe age beside you. Both of you. Named. Remembered. Whole."

The silence afterward was very long.

I want to record honestly what happened inside me during that silence, because dishonesty is the guild's trade and I have broken with it. What happened was this: I wanted it. All of it. The name like sunlight after thirty years underground; the body unpoisoned, unbreaking; Tam alive and near; a kitchen somewhere, maybe, a plain room, honest food. The vision assembled itself so completely that I could smell the bread in it. The thing wearing my king had studied me the way I studied dishes, found every fault line, and poured its sweetness precisely into them.

And underneath the sweetness, faint but unmistakable, I tasted the poison.

Because I had read the First Ledger. Because I knew what every gift from this creature cost. Because I had spent my whole life learning that the most dangerous dish is the one you crave—and that craving is exactly how it gets past the tongue.

"You're offering me myself," I said finally. "My name, my years, my—people. Everything the guild took and everything time is taking. You've priced it perfectly." I looked up into the glass. "Which tells me you're afraid."

Something flickered across the borrowed face. Fast. Gone.

"Afraid," it repeated.

"You need Vess delivered willingly, or the guild would simply have seized her weeks ago—they've had enforcers hunting her since the autumn. You need *me* to choose it. A freely given surrender. That's the shape of the old bargain, isn't it? Everything with you must be given, not taken, because taken things carry no nourishment. Stolen memory is gristle. Only what's surrendered freely feeds you." I stepped closer to the glass. "So you're not a king making offers. You're a starving thing begging at my door. And you hate that I can see it."

For one moment—one naked, endless moment—the mask slipped. The face in the glass was still Rafael Aldemar's face, but nothing human looked out of it. It was vast and hollow and old beyond kingdoms, and it looked at me the way winter looks at the last green field.

Then the warmth returned, smoother than before, and with it, the threat.

"Nine days," it said. "In nine days I finish this kingdom's memory regardless. Every road unwritten. Every name dissolved. Every mother looking at her child and seeing a stranger's face. The vessel cannot stop it alone—she is a fragment, a crumb of the loaf, and crumbs do not argue with hunger. Only you stand between those outcomes, little keeper. Give her to me freely, and I will honor every word I've spoken—on the binding itself, which even I must keep. Refuse, and lose everything I offered *and* everything else besides." It leaned close, and the king's voice dropped to something almost gentle. "You of all people know how this ends. You've read the entry."

The glass went dark.

I stood in the cold a long while, listening to my own heart work, counting the beats the way I counted tastes—cataloguing, recording, refusing to flinch. Then I wrapped the glass in its wool and slid it behind the panel, and I went and knelt beside Vess, and I watched her breathe, this small fierce fragment of a soul that a starving god had offered me everything to betray.

Two months of life in my body. Nine days in the world.

And somewhere above the scriptorium, a young man with clean hands and a forged writ in his drawer was sleeping, unaware that his name had just become currency.

I woke Tam at dawn.
