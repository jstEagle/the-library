## 27. Refusal

The king's mouth smiled at me from across the drowned throne room, and it was the worst thing I had ever seen.

Not because it was cruel. Because it was kind. It wore his face the way a good forger wears a signature—every line correct, every warmth in its proper place—and beneath that correctness was nothing at all. The torchlight did not quite reach it. The water at our feet did not ripple where it stood. Even the dark seemed to lean away from it, as though the under-court itself remembered what it was.

"Name your price," it said again, in the voice I had spent years tasting for. "You have only to speak it."

Vess's small hand was locked in mine. Her palm was cold. Tam stood behind us with Corvane, and none of them breathed. In the flooded dark of the first throne room, with the First Ledger open on its stone plinth between us like a wound that would not close, the crowned thing had made me an offer, and now it waited with the patience of something that had waited centuries before.

I thought about what it had offered. That was the trap of it—it knew exactly which doors in me were unlocked.

My name back. *Yara Eriksen*, spoken by someone other than a ledger, owned by no guild, written into no book. A self that belonged to me the way my scars belonged to me.

My health. The ledger of my own body, which I kept as faithfully as any guild record, said I had perhaps two seasons left before the poisons finished their slow accounting. It offered me decades. It offered me an old age.

Tam safe. Vess safe. Everyone I had come to love, guaranteed, signed, sealed.

It was a merchant's offer. Of course it was. The Salt Guild had learned everything it knew from this thing—had been, if the First Ledger told true, its first customers and its first meal. And a merchant knows that you do not sell a thing to someone who wants it. You sell it to someone who is afraid of losing something else.

"You've read me," I said. My voice came out steadier than I felt. "All these years, reading everyone else. Which noble wants which rival dead. What the queen can't bear to lose. What the king dreams about." I looked at the face wearing him. "You read people too."

"I have read deeper than any ledger," it said. The king's eyes did not blink often enough. "I read the girl who ate poison so she could not be poisoned. I read the woman who keeps accounts because numbers cannot leave her. I read the orphan who was bought for the price of a winter's bread and has never once asked whether she was worth more." The mouth curved. "I am offering you the answer to a question you have never dared ask. *What are you worth?* Name it. I will pay it."

The water lapped at the steps. Somewhere far above us, the palace went on with its hollow day—the false king holding court, the queen smiling her blank devotion, the ministers debating grain tariffs for a harvest that might already be unwritten. Nine days, it had said. Or rather: it had not said yet. That part was still coming. I could feel it coiling under the courtesy like a snake under a rug.

"You're right," I said. "I never asked that question."

Vess's grip tightened.

"Because the guild answered it for me when I was six years old. They wrote a number next to my name and closed the book. And I have spent my whole life living inside their arithmetic—what I cost, what I'm owed, what I'm worth on the open market of impossible things." I took one step forward. Tam made a small sound behind me and strangled it. "So here is what I think of your offer."

I reached out—not toward the thing, but toward the First Ledger, lying open on the plinth between us, its ancient pages soft as skin—and I put my hand flat against the entry that named the bargain. The binding. The feeding. The trade that had built a kingdom's shadow economy on the slow digestion of memory.

"You priced me," I said. "And I refuse the price."

* * *

For a moment, nothing moved. Then the torches guttered all at once, every flame in the under-court bending sideways as if a door had opened somewhere into a much larger cold.

"Do you understand," the thing said, and the kindness drained out of the king's voice like wine from a cracked cup, leaving something underneath that made my teeth ache, "what you are refusing?"

"I understand exactly."

"You understand *nothing*. You hold a fragment—a scrap of a soul, a child-shaped remainder—and you think it is a key. It is a *crumb*. I have eaten kingdoms, taster. I ate the language your little vessel speaks; I ate the people who spoke it; I ate the word for the color of their sky. I have been patient. I have dined slowly, course by course, decade by decade, while your guild set my table and called it commerce." The king's face leaned close, and up close I could see that his eyes were not wrong in some small way—they were wrong in the way a painted eye is wrong, depth without anything behind it. "And you would trade all of that—the safety of your scribe, the years of your own life, the name you weep over in the dark—for a crumb?"

"Yes," I said.

"Why?"

"Because it isn't a crumb." My hand was still on the Ledger. The pages were warm under my palm, warmer than paper should be, warm like something alive and dreaming. "It's the last piece of him. And pieces are how everything starts. I know that better than anyone—I was a piece once. A starving girl nobody counted. And somebody wrote my debt down instead of letting me vanish, and that single entry—" I stopped, because my throat had closed, and finished anyway, quieter. "That single entry is why I'm standing here refusing you. Small things are not small. That's the whole secret of ledgers. Every enormous theft begins as a line item."

The silence that followed had texture. Weight. The water around the throne began, very slowly, to move backward—ripples flowing uphill toward the thing's feet, as though the room itself were being drawn into its lungs.

"So be it," it said.

And then it took the offer off the table. I watched it happen—the way a merchant sweeps the goods back into his case when the buyer haggles too long. The warmth went out of the king's face. The gentleness folded itself away like expensive cloth. What remained was older than the face, older than the crown, and it spoke in a voice that came from everywhere in the flooded room at once, from the water and the stone and the space between my own heartbeats.

"Then hear the new terms."

* * *

"Nine days," it said.

The number landed in the room like a stone dropped down a well. I heard Tam exhale. I heard Corvane murmur something in the old guild cadence—an involuntary prayer, or maybe an inventory.

"In nine days, I finish my meal. Not the scraps your masters have fed me—not the bottled griefs and borrowed names and stolen years your guild has dripped into my mouth for three hundred years, keeping me tame, keeping me *hungry*. No. In nine days I take the city entire. Every road. Every name. Every mother's face in her child's eyes. I will drink this capital down to its foundations, and when I am done there will be a great and prosperous city where nothing remembers itself—where the bells ring at hours no one chose, for reasons no one knows, and the people smile their empty smiles and call it peace." The king's head tilted, birdlike, considering me. "Unless."

"Unless," I said.

"You deliver the vessel. The fragment. The crumb." Its smile returned, thin as a knife laid flat. "Bring her to me before the ninth dawn, and I will content myself with her alone. One small soul, freely given, and the city keeps its memories. Its streets. Its songs. Its dead in their graves and its children in their beds." The painted eyes moved to Vess, and I stepped bodily between them, and the thing laughed—a sound like pages tearing. "Oh, taster. You shield her with your body? I do not eat bodies. Stand aside. It changes nothing."

"It changes everything," I said, "to me."

"Then let me make the arithmetic simple, since you love your columns so dearly." It raised the king's hand, and in the air above the black water, images assembled themselves out of mist and memory-stuff—thin, gray, half-erased, like sketches someone had rubbed nearly away. A market street. A wedding procession. A woman lifting an infant. As I watched, each image faded a little more, colors draining, edges dissolving, until they were barely suggestions of themselves. "This is what remains of your city's remembering. Nine days of it. I have already begun—you have seen it. Roads that vanish. Names that fade from documents. Your scribe found the discrepancies; your queen feels the holes and cannot look at them long enough to grieve. I am simply telling you the schedule."

The images collapsed into water and were gone.

"Deliver the child, and I stop. Refuse, and in nine days no one in this city will remember anyone. Including—" the voice softened into something almost tender, which was somehow worse than the threat, "—including you, taster. Though I suspect that loss will trouble you least. You have practiced being forgotten your whole life."

Corvane moved then. I felt him come up beside me, and when he spoke, his voice was the flattest I had ever heard it, which from Corvane meant it was burning.

"The conclave voted to deliver her," he said. "They'll come for her within days. You know that. Whatever game you're playing—"

"I play no games. I keep appointments." The thing's attention slid back to me, and the weight of it was physical, a hand pressing down on the crown of my skull. "Nine days, keeper of ledgers. Bring me the vessel, or bring me nothing, and lose everything else. There is no third column in this account. There is no clever cross-reference that erases this debt. I wrote the first ledger. I know every clause."

"Then you know one clause better than I hoped," I said.

Its stillness changed quality. Interest. The painted eyes narrowed a fraction.

"The founders were your first victims," I said. "That's what the First Ledger says. They didn't bind you out of power—they bound you out of desperation, and the binding became a feeding trough, and the trough became a guild, and the guild became the digestive system of a god. Three hundred years of your meals, arranged and delivered by the descendants of the people you ate first." I lifted my chin. "But bindings cut both ways. Whoever struck the original bargain wrote terms. And terms can be *unwritten*. That's why you fear her. Not because she's a crumb. Because she's the piece that doesn't fit in your mouth—the last fragment of the man you swallowed, and fragments remember how they were broken off."

For the first time, the thing wearing the king stopped smiling.

It lasted only a heartbeat. Then the mask reassembled, smooth and courteous and utterly cold, and the king's body turned from us with unhurried grace, wading back toward the drowned throne as though the water were a carpet.

"Nine days," it said, without turning around. "Count them well, Yara Eriksen. I gave you back your name just now, free of charge—did you notice? So that you will know precisely what it is you are losing." At the throne it paused, one hand resting on the armrest worn smooth by a real man's grip, centuries ago. "When the city forgets, it will forget you first. That is my gift to you. You will die unrecorded, unnamed, unmourned—exactly as you always feared, exactly as you always lived. And the last thing this kingdom will remember of you is a food-taster who refused a generous offer."

The dark took it. Not gradually—all at once, the way a candle goes out, the king's shape simply ceasing to be where it had been, and the water settling flat, and the torches standing up straight again as though nothing in the world had bent them.

* * *

Nobody spoke for a long time.

The under-court held us in its wet silence—the broken throne, the plinth with its terrible open book, the faint gray light seeping down through cracks in the foundation stones like the memory of daylight. Vess's hand was still in mine. She hadn't let go through any of it. When I finally looked down at her, she was looking up at me, and her eyes—those too-old eyes in a child's face—were wet.

She said something in Root-Tongue. Soft. Two syllables.

"What did she say?" Tam asked hoarsely.

"She said—" I had to stop and start again. "She said *not your fault*. That's what it means. Roughly." I crouched down to her level, mud and floodwater soaking through my skirts, and took both her hands. "Listen to me, Vess. What that thing said in there—about being delivered, about being a price—I need you to hear me clearly. Nobody is delivering you anywhere. Not the conclave. Not the enforcers. Not me. Do you understand? You are not currency. You are not a crumb. You are the realest person in this whole rotten kingdom, and I will burn every book in that archive before I let them write you into one."

She studied me with that grave, bottomless attention children sometimes have, as though they are checking your words against something truer than words. Then she reached up and touched my cheek—one small muddy finger, tracing the scar along my jaw—and said another word in the old tongue. One syllable this time. Low and certain.

"What now?" Tam asked quietly.

"Now?" I stood. My knees ached. My chest ached, in the place where the poisons had taken up permanent residence, and I thought: *nine days*, and thought: *my ledger gives me eight weeks, and the world is giving me nine days, and for once the arithmetic is simple.* "Now we go up. Now we count."

"Count what?"

"Everyone the thing forgot to count." I turned to Corvane. He looked ten years older than he had an hour ago, and he was watching me with an expression I'd never seen on him—something stripped bare, a man standing in the ruins of the only church he'd ever had. "How many tasters does the palace employ?"

He blinked. "Currently? Eleven. Why—"

"How many of them are guild-collateralized orphans?"

The pause stretched. I watched him do the arithmetic, watch him realize I'd already done it, had probably done it years ago, in the margins of ledgers no one was supposed to read past their own entries.

"All of them," he said. "Every one. That's how it's always—"

"That's how it's always done. Yes." I picked up the lantern. Above us, through the cracks in the ancient stones, the palace was waking into another hollow morning—the false king, the empty queen, the ministers shuffling papers full of names that were fading even as they read them. Nine days. Let the conclave send its enforcers. Let the thing keep its schedule. It had made one mistake, the same mistake every merchant makes eventually: it assumed that because I had been bought once, I could be bought again.

It didn't know about the others. The invisible ones. The girls and boys it had priced at a winter's bread, scattered through the palace kitchens and corridors, tasting its poisons, memorizing its tables, waiting all these years without knowing what they were waiting for.

"They think we're property," I said, and started up the stairs. "Let's show them what the property remembers."
