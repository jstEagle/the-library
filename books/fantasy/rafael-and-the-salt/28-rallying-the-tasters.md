## 28. Rallying the Tasters

There were six of us who tasted for the crown, and we had never once, in all our years, sat at the same table.

That was by design. The Salt Guild did not gather its assets. Assets that gathered could conspire; assets that conspired could compare ledgers; and every one of us had a ledger. I knew this better than anyone alive, because I kept them. In the cellar archive beneath the Salt House, in drawers of yellowed vellum organized by year and collateral value, there was a page for each taster with her name struck through it—struck through, because the guild preferred us nameless even on paper—and beside each struck name, a figure: what we'd cost to raise, what we'd earned back, what remained owed.

Maren of the west kitchens: eleven years served, four still owed.

Dessin, who tasted for the queen's table: nine years, six owed.

Old Sefa, who had survived three reigns of poisoners and now taught the youngest among us how to hold wine on the tongue without swallowing: paid in full, they said, but no one left the guild paid in full. Her page simply read *retained*.

And me. My page was the thickest. My page had been edited twice that I knew of—once by Corvane, once by whoever had written my death into it before he un-wrote it again—and I had stopped trusting anything written about me except what I wrote myself.

Six days since the crowned thing had given its ultimatum. Nine days it had promised; four had already burned away like paper held too near a flame. Four mornings I had woken in the archive with Vess curled against my side, her small hands cold as coins, and counted what remained. Five days. Then four. The city grayed a little more each dawn. On the third morning the Fountain of the Nine Sisters had been dry—not broken, not drained, simply *forgotten*, its basin full of dust and its name gone from every sign and song. People walked past it without seeing it. You cannot miss what you cannot remember.

Tam had made a list of everything vanishing. He kept it hidden inside a treatise on grain tariffs, which he rightly judged to be the most unreadable document in the palace. The list grew longer every day. Streets. Lullabies. A whole quarter's worth of funerals, unwritten, so that mourners woke with wet eyes and no idea whom they grieved.

"We can't fight it," Tam said that night, hunched over his list by candlelight while Vess slept. "You said it yourself. It eats memory. An army forgets why it's marching before it reaches the gate."

"Then we don't send an army," I said. "We send people it can't see."

He looked up. "No one is invisible to a thing like that."

I thought of the tasting hall at court—the long table where we stood behind the king's chair in our undyed grey, faces trained blank, tongues scarred and calloused, moving through the palace like furniture that breathed. I thought of Maren slipping a note into my sleeve once, years ago, without either of us ever acknowledging it: *the stew on Thursdays comes from Lord Havel's cellars, don't ask why.* I thought of Dessin holding a poisoned cup in her mouth for a full ten seconds before spitting it into the silk, buying the queen time to be elsewhere, and walking out of the hall with her expression unchanged, because that was the first thing they taught us and the last thing we ever unlearned.

"No one sees us," I said. "That's the point. That's always been the point."

* * *

Finding them was the easy part. We had been trained to find each other without seeming to look—a glance held half a beat too long across a courtyard, a hand brushed along a doorframe in a particular rhythm, the guild-sign scratched small into the underside of a serving tray where only a taster would ever run her thumb. I had spent fifteen years pretending those signals meant nothing. Now I used them all in a single afternoon.

Maren first. She was checking the evening's sweetmeats in the west kitchen when I came to stand beside her at the long bench, close enough that our sleeves touched, and I pressed two fingers flat against the wood—*ledger, ledger, ledger*, the old signal for *accounts must balance*. Her knife stopped mid-slice. She didn't turn her head. Fifteen years of training held her face perfectly still, but I saw her swallow, and I knew she understood that something had finally come due.

"The old drying shed," I murmured, barely a breath. "Behind the tannery row. Tonight, after the second bell. Come alone, come unseen, come angry."

A pause. Then, softly, with the ghost of the girl she'd been before the guild smoothed her flat: "I've been angry for twenty years, Yara. I'll bring extra."

Dessin took longer. She was careful in a way Maren wasn't—careful the way I was careful, the way you learn to be when your handler reads your face for a living. I caught her at the well in the servants' court, drawing water neither of us needed, and passed her the signal under the pretense of steadying her bucket. She looked at me then, really looked, and there was fear in her eyes and something underneath the fear that was worse: resignation.

"You're going to ask me to do something that gets us all erased," she said quietly. "Not killed. Erased. I've seen the streets going grey. I know what's doing it. And I know the guild is feeding it, because Corvane came to my lodging last month and asked me questions about the child."

My blood went cold. "What did you tell him?"

"Nothing. There was nothing to tell." A bitter almost-smile. "But I've known something was wrong with the king for two years, Yara. Two years. I taste for the queen's table—I watch him across the hall every night eating food that might as well be ash, and everyone else smiles and calls it piety, his indifference to pleasure. And I say nothing. Every night I say nothing." She set down the bucket. Her hands, I noticed, were shaking. "So yes. Tell me where. Tell me when. But understand—if this fails, they won't kill us. They'll open our pages and write us out, and there will be nothing left of us anywhere, not even grief, because no one will remember to grieve."

"That's true," I said. "It's exactly true. And I'm asking anyway."

She closed her eyes. When she opened them, the resignation was still there—but it had moved over, made room for something harder.

"Second bell," she said. "Don't be late. I've waited long enough."

Sefa I did not summon. I went to her directly, because Sefa was owed directness if anyone was. She lived in a narrow room above the spice market, surrounded by jars of antidote bases she still brewed out of habit, though no one had tried to poison a taster in a decade—they knew better than to waste good venom on us. She was seventy, maybe older; she had watched three kings eat and outlived two of them, and her tongue was so scarred it had gone smooth as river stone.

She listened to everything—the crowned thing, Vess, the ledgers burning in my future, the countdown—without interrupting once. Then she poured two cups of willow-bark tea, pushed one across the table, and drank hers in the old training way: hold, breathe, spit into the cup. A ritual with no poison in it. Just the shape of survival.

"Do you know what they told us, the first day?" she said. "Each of us, separately, so we'd never compare notes? They told us we were chosen because we were special. Immune. Marked." She turned her cup slowly. "It took me thirty years to understand what marked meant. It means *collateral*. It means the guild bought us as infants the way merchants buy futures in grain, and immunity isn't a gift—it's maintenance. You maintain a tool so it lasts until the debt is paid." Her eyes came up to mine, and they were dry and terribly clear. "The debt is never paid, child. You know that. You keep the books."

"I know," I said. "That's why I'm here. The books are going to burn, Sefa. All of them. And when they do, everything the guild holds goes free—including us. But I need the tasters to get me into the palace on the last day, past the enforcers, down to the under-court, and I need them to do it without being seen or remembered. Who better?"

Sefa was quiet for a long moment. Outside, the spice market called its wares in voices that would, in five days' time, perhaps be forgotten mid-word.

"There were twelve of us, in my training year," she said at last. "Twelve orphan girls in a cellar, learning to die slowly and call it a career. Seven are dead. Two are scattered beyond finding. Three of us are left in this city, and one of them keeps the ledgers." She rose, joints cracking like green wood, and began pulling jars from her shelves with sudden purpose. "If we're going to walk into that palace, we go armed. Not with steel—with antitoxins, emetics, coagulants. Everything I've brewed and hoarded for forty years against a day I told myself would never come." She thrust an armful of jars at me. "Well. Here's the day, girl. Help me pack."

* * *

We met in the old drying shed behind tannery row, the six of us—Sefa, Maren, Dessin, Tam, Vess, and me—by the light of a single shielded lamp, surrounded by hanging racks that smelled of salt and hide and decades of other people's secrets. I had chosen it because tannery row was already greying; the thing wearing the crown had eaten most of the street's memory, and what the thing forgot, it did not watch. Even monsters have blind spots. Ours was made of their neglect.

Vess sat on a crate in the lamplight, and I watched the tasters see her properly for the first time. They had heard rumors—the mute child, the strange coin—but hearing is not knowing. Maren knelt to her level the way you'd approach a skittish animal, and Vess studied her with those old, old eyes, and then reached out and touched Maren's scarred hand with one small finger.

Maren went very still.

"What did—" she started, and stopped, because Vess had spoken. One word, in the Root-Tongue, and the word had landed somewhere in Maren's chest like a stone dropped down a well. I felt it too, faintly, the way you feel a distant bell through a floor. "What did she say?"

"She said *sister*," I said. "In the language from before kingdoms. It's how she says *I know what you are*." I looked around the lamp-lit circle at their faces—Sefa's carved calm, Dessin's white-knuckled resolve, Maren's dawning wonder, Tam's quiet, stubborn devotion. "She knows what all of us are. Orphans bought and branded and taught to be invisible. She was cast out of the king's own soul for the same crime: being the part that couldn't be owned."

Tam spread the map he'd drawn of the palace service ways—every corridor, every dumbwaiter, every blind stair that the guards had forgotten because the guards themselves were forgetting. "Four nights from now," he said, "the conclave delivers whatever decoy they've prepared, and the thing will feed, and the city will lose another season. After that feeding, it will be strongest—and busiest digesting. That's our window. While it swallows, we descend."

"And if it catches us," Dessin said flatly, "it offers us our hearts' desires and then unmakes us. You've met it. You know."

"I know," I said. "Which is why none of us goes alone. From tonight, we move in pairs. We use the tasting signals—no spoken plans, nothing written, nothing the ledgers can read off our lips or our ink. Sefa arms every pair with enough antitoxin to survive the guild's favorite tricks. And we spend these four days doing what we were bred to do." I let the pause sit. "Tasting. Testing. Finding the poison in things and naming it silently, and surviving it. The whole palace is poisoned now—its king, its memory, its bargains. Nobody knows that better than us. Nobody is better built to swallow it and live."

Maren laughed—a short, startled sound, the first laugh I'd heard from any of us in years. "Fifteen years they told us we were nothing. Furniture. Grey dresses with tongues." She shook her head. "And it turns out they were building exactly the weapon this moment needed."

"They weren't building it," Sefa said. "They were building *against* it. That's the joke of it. Every orphan they collateralized, every tongue they scarred, every blank face they trained—they were forging the one kind of person the crowned thing cannot easily digest. People with nothing left to remember being taken." She looked around at us, and for a moment the old teacher's iron gentled into something like pride. "We are the invisible. Let's go be seen."

We sealed it the only way we knew how. Six cups, filled from Sefa's plain kettle—no poison, no test, just water. We raised them, held each mouthful on the tongue the way we'd been taught, feeling it fully, honestly, for perhaps the first time in our lives. Then, together, we swallowed.

Vess watched us do it, and smiled, and spoke a single word of Root-Tongue that made the lamp flame bend toward her like a listener.

Tam leaned close. "What did she say?"

"She said *enough*," I translated, and my throat was tight. "As in: it has taken enough."

Outside the shed, the second bell rang across a city with holes in its memory, and somewhere above us, in a palace built on a buried throne room, the thing wearing the king's face finished another day of eating.

Three days remained. And for the first time since the child had come mud-soaked to my door, I was not alone.
