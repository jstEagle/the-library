## 29. Tam's Choice

The letter came at the hour when the chancery emptied and the candles guttered down to their last thumb-width of wax, which was the hour Tam had learned to love best, because it was the hour when no one watched him.

He knew the seal before he broke it. Royal green wax, pressed with the double crown-and-quill of the Office of Records—not the routine stamp used for requisitions and rota assignments, but the heavy ceremonial die that appeared perhaps twice a year, when the Crown wished to remind a man that his life belonged to the kingdom. He had copied that seal a hundred times in his apprenticeship. He had never held one addressed to himself.

*Tam of the Lower Scriptorium,* it began, and even his own name looked strange written in another hand. *By order of His Majesty's Chancery—*

*His Majesty.* The words snagged like a fishhook. Three weeks ago he would have read past them without a thought. Now he heard Yara's voice in his head, quiet and terrible: *The thing wearing him doesn't blink enough. Count the blinks, Tam. Kings blink.*

He counted the blinks now, always. He had begun noticing other things too—the way documents from certain years had gaps in them like teeth knocked out of a jaw, the way clerks argued about streets that no longer appeared on any map, the way the queen's signature had changed after her wedding day and no one else remembered what it had been before. He had brought these observations to Yara the way a man brings found money to the one person he trusts not to rob him. She had looked at his notes for a long time and then said, "You see the edits. Most people can't. Most people were never meant to."

That was three days ago. He had walked home afterward through the Lamplighters' Quarter feeling taller than his body, as if someone had slipped an extra inch of spine into him while he wasn't looking.

Now he read the letter by candlelight, and the extra inch of spine folded flat.

*—is pleased to offer you appointment as Under-Chancellor of the Royal Archive, with all attendant honors, stipend, and lodging within the Palace Precinct. This appointment shall take effect immediately upon acceptance, whereupon all prior obligations, debts, and encumbrances recorded against your name shall be struck from the rolls and considered satisfied in full.*

All prior obligations. Tam read the sentence four times. His debts were not large—they never had been—but they were old, older than his apprenticeship, inherited from a father who had signed away three years of his son's labor to a wool merchant and died before the ink dried. Then there was the second debt, the one he never spoke of: the fee owed to the Salt Guild for the schooling they had advanced him, quietly, when no other school would take a debtor's son. Everyone in the Lower Scriptorium carried some version of that second debt. It was how the guild kept its hands clean and its ledgers full. You never saw the contract. You just felt the weight of it every time you signed your name to anything official—a small tug behind the ribs, like a hook set in soft meat.

And here was a letter promising to cut him free of both. Struck from the rolls. *Satisfied in full.*

There was more. Of course there was more. The Crown did not spend ceremonial seals on charity.

*The Under-Chancellor shall have direct oversight of all archival corrections, restorations, and reconciliations as directed by the Office of the Crown, and shall report weekly in person to the Chancellor regarding any irregularities observed in the course of his duties.*

Tam set the letter down. His hands were steady—he prided himself on steady hands; a scribe's hands were his fortune—but his mouth had gone dry as old vellum.

*Any irregularities observed.* He knew exactly which irregularity they meant. He could see the shape of it between the lines the way he could see watermarks held up to a window: Yara Eriksen. The taster who asked questions. The woman who had been seen speaking with him in the palace corridors, in the records room, once—once—on the servants' stair, standing closer than propriety allowed, her hand briefly closing around his wrist like a cuff fastening.

Someone had been watching. Someone had decided that the cheapest way to deal with a curious scribe was not to silence him but to promote him—to buy him, elevate him, wrap him so deep in honor and salary and obligation that he would drown willingly. And if the promotion happened to sever his connection to the taster, well. The Crown did not waste purchases.

They were offering him everything he had wanted since he was nine years old copying letters on a slate in his father's cold room. Under-Chancellor. His own office. His father's debts dissolved, the guild's hook pulled out of his ribs. He would be *Tam the Under-Chancellor* for the rest of his life, and no one would ever again look at his worn cuffs or count the coins in his purse when he entered a room.

And all it would cost was every irregularity he failed to report.

He sat with the letter for a long time. The candle burned down. Somewhere below, the night-watch called the half-hour, and the sound rolled through the scriptorium's dark like a stone dropped into a well.

He thought about his father, who had died owing money to two merchants and the parish, and whose last recorded act was signing his son's future away because the alternative was watching the boy starve. His father had spent his whole life being bought. Every choice Tam had ever made was built on top of that fact, the way a house is built on top of a grave.

Then he thought about Yara's face when she had shown him the entry dated three days from now—the one describing her death—and how she hadn't trembled. She had looked at her own murder, written in advance in her own handwriting, and said, "Well. At least my penmanship holds up under pressure." That was the moment he had understood that he was lost, thoroughly and permanently, the way a man is lost at sea who has stopped looking for the shore.

If he accepted the appointment, he would report her. Not immediately—maybe not even consciously. But the position would require it, week after week, in person, before the Chancellor, and the Chancellor answered to whatever wore the king, and eventually Tam would sit in that gilded office and say the words *the taster has been asking questions about archival discrepancies*, and he would watch them move against her with the smooth institutional patience of a tide coming in, and he would tell himself he had had no choice.

He had seen what happened to people with no choice. He had been raised among them.

Tam rose, took the letter, and walked the length of the empty scriptorium to the great iron stove that heated the drying-presses, still warm from the day's work. He stood before it with the royal seal in his hand and found, to his surprise, that he was waiting. Waiting for a reason. Waiting for some argument to arrive that would make burning it unnecessary—some legal loophole, some way to keep the appointment and keep Yara too, some clever clause only a scribe would find.

No clause came. There was no cleverness here. Only the oldest transaction in the world, laid out plainly: everything he wanted, in exchange for the one person he—

He didn't finish the thought. He didn't need to. He fed the letter into the stove.

The green wax caught first, bubbling and hissing, the crowned quill swelling and blackening as the flame took it. Then the vellum curled, and the words he had read four times went gold at the edges and lifted upward as ash, *Under-Chancellor, Under-Chancellor, satisfied in full*, dissolving into the dark of the flue like a name spoken backward. Tam watched until the last corner fell apart. It took less time than copying a single line.

When it was done he felt lighter and poorer simultaneously, the exact sensation, he imagined, of a man cutting off a limb that had been caught in machinery—grief and relief arriving in the same instant and refusing to be separated.

He went back to his desk, took out a fresh sheet, and wrote the acceptance declining itself—proper form required a written refusal, and proper form was the one weapon they'd given him and could never take back:

*Tam of the Lower Scriptorium respectfully declines His Majesty's most gracious offer, citing obligations of a personal nature that cannot be discharged by any office of the Crown.*

Obligations of a personal nature. Let them chew on that. Let the Chancellor read those five words and understand that the scribe had known exactly what was being purchased, and had declined to be currency.

He sealed it with his own cheap grey wax, the kind apprentices used, and left it on the Chancellor's desk for morning.

* * *

Yara was waiting in the archive cellar when he came, as arranged, near midnight. Vess slept in the nest of blankets behind the false ledger-shelf, breath whistling faintly, one fist curled against her cheek. Corvane was absent—summoned, he'd said, though his eyes when he left had suggested the summons was a summons in name only.

"You're late," Yara said, without looking up from the First Ledger. "You're never late."

"A detour." He sat down across from her at the worktable. Up close she looked worse than she had a week ago—the poison-years were calling in their debts now, visibly, a grayness under her skin like frost forming inside glass. It frightened him more than the crowned thing did. Monsters you could fight. Her body was simply keeping honest books, and honesty, he was learning, could kill faster than treachery. "I received a letter today."

"From whom?"

"The Crown." He told her all of it—the seal, the title, the debts struck from the rolls, the weekly reports on irregularities. He watched her face as he spoke, and saw the moment she understood, and saw something flicker behind her eyes that might have been guilt.

"They offered you my head as a signing bonus," she said.

"They offered me a life," he corrected. "A good one. The one I've been working toward since I was nine years old. All I had to do was sell the only person who ever—" He stopped. Recalibrated. "I burned it."

Yara set down her pen very carefully, the way she did when her hands wanted to do something else entirely. "You should have taken it."

"Probably."

"Tam." She said his name like a diagnosis. "Do you know what's coming? In eight days, maybe fewer, that thing is going to finish eating this city's memory unless we stop it. The odds are—" She made a small, precise gesture with two fingers, the gesture she used at the king's table when a dish passed inspection. *Clean. Acceptable. Barely.* "And you are not immune. You're not collateralized, you're not guild, you're not protected by anything except being beneath notice. Taking their appointment would have put walls around you. Burning it leaves you standing in the open with me."

"I know what it leaves me standing in."

"Do you?" For the first time since he'd known her, her composure cracked—not dramatically, just a hairline fracture, visible only because he had spent weeks learning where to look. "Everyone who stands near me ends up in a ledger. Dead ones, mostly. I have entries, Tam. Whole pages of people who trusted the taster and got recorded."

"Then record me," he said. "Write it yourself. Right now, in whatever book you want—*Tam, Lower Scriptorium, chose badly, died of it.* If it comes to that, at least the entry will be true, and at least I'll have chosen it instead of being sold. My father was sold, Yara. Before he could walk, practically. Every decision of his life was made by whoever held his paper. I have spent twenty-six years making sure that when my turn came, I would get to make one decision that was entirely mine." He leaned forward. "This is it. This is the one. Don't take it from me because you're afraid for me. Fear isn't yours to carry alone either—I've noticed you try, and I'm telling you it doesn't work. I can read marginalia. I see what you write in the margins when you think no one checks."

Her jaw tightened. "What do I write?"

"*Don't let him be another entry,*" he quoted softly. "*Last line, page forty-one, yesterday's date.*"

For a long moment neither of them moved. Above them, faintly, through stone and floorboards, the city murmured in its sleep—eight days from becoming a city that murmured about nothing, that remembered nothing, that loved without objects the way the queen did.

Then Yara reached across the table and took his hand, and hers was colder than he expected, and shaking, very slightly, the way a steady hand shakes when it has finally been given permission.

"All right," she said. "Then you stay. And when this is over—if it's ever over—you'll owe me a dinner. A real one. Nothing traded, nothing tasted, nothing recorded. Just food."

"That," Tam said, "is the first honest debt I've ever taken on."

Behind them, in her blanket nest, Vess stirred and sat up, eyes wide open in the dark—wide open and fixed on the stairwell door, on which, faint but unmistakable, someone had begun to knock.

Three knocks. A pause. Then three again.

Corvane's signal—except Corvane had left hours ago, and Corvane never knocked twice.

Vess spoke a single word in the Root-Tongue, low and urgent, and Yara went white as salt.

"What did she say?" Tam whispered.

"'They're already inside,'" Yara said, and snuffed the candle.
