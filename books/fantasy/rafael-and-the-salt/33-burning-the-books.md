## 33. Burning the Books

The smoke reached Yara before the fire did.

It curled up through the archive's stairwell in gray ribbons, tasting of vellum and hot salt and something sweeter beneath—burning sugar, almost, the smell of a hundred years of bottled grief going to ash. She stood at the top of the stairs with Vess's small hand locked in hers and understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, that there was no way back down.

Behind them, the under-archive was burning. Ahead of them, Corvane was shouting her name—a name that had thirty seconds ago been hers, and might not be for much longer.

"Yara!" His voice cracked on the second syllable. "Tell me you're not—tell me you didn't—"

"I did." Her own voice sounded far away. "I burned it."

* * *

It had begun, as all catastrophes do, with a small thing: a ledger page that refused to die.

They had fought their way out of the archive's reading room while the enforcers came through the delivery doors—six of them in guild-gray, masked, moving with the terrible economy of men who had done violence to books before. Tam had thrown the conclave's stolen seal through a window to buy them a heartbeat. Vess had screamed one word of Root-Tongue and every lamp in the cellar had guttered black, and in that darkness Yara had swept the nearest shelf of ledgers off its brackets and into the brazier by the door, mostly to make noise, mostly to make light, mostly because she could not think of anything else to do.

And then she had seen what happened when the first ledger caught.

A man screamed.

Not an enforcer. Not anyone in the room. The scream came from inside the fire—from the pages themselves, from the entry that was curling and blackening—and Yara knew the voice. She had heard it a hundred times across the guild-house counters: Master Ellery Voss, who traded his stutter for a silver tongue in the year of the Double Winter, who walked with a limp the guild had recorded and then sold away, whose grief over his drowned daughter sat stoppered in a blue bottle on shelf nine. The scream was old and young at once, the sound of a life being unwritten in reverse, and when the flames took the page whole, the screaming stopped—not ended but *completed*, like a debt paid, like a knot cut.

In the sudden quiet, Vess turned to Yara and spoke in Root-Tongue, and though Yara had learned perhaps forty words of the old language, she did not need any of them. The child's face said everything. *You see now. You see what they are.*

The ledgers weren't records.

They were anchors.

Every trade the guild had ever made—every bottled grief, every borrowed name, every decade of life siphoned from one desperate soul and poured into another—all of it hung from those pages like meat from hooks. The crowned thing beneath the palace did not feed on memory in the abstract. It fed *through* the books. The Salt Guild had spent four centuries building the most efficient harvesting machine ever devised, and its engine room was this cellar, these shelves, these ten thousand entries written in Yara's own careful hand.

She had catalogued them all. She knew where every hook was set.

"Yara." Tam grabbed her arm in the dark, enforcers cursing somewhere behind them, lamps dying and flaring. "We have to move. We have to move *now*."

"Yes," she said. And then, hearing herself say it, feeling the words leave her body like something expelled rather than spoken: "But not the way we came."

* * *

Corvane caught up with them at the top of the stairwell, half-carrying, half-dragging a wounded taster named Odile whose shoulder was bleeding through her coat. His mask was gone—she had never seen his bare face in daylight, only in the scrying-glass, and even then softened—and without it he looked suddenly, shockingly mortal. An old man. A tired man. A man watching his life's work climb the stairs behind him as smoke.

"The east stacks are already gone," he said. His voice was very level, which is how she knew how much it cost him. "The Second Ledger. Fourteen thousand entries. I can hear them."

"You can hear them?"

"They're singing," he said simply. "Didn't you know they sing? When a binding breaks. All those years I told myself it was the wind in the vents." He closed his eyes. When he opened them again, they were wet and furious and, underneath both, relieved in a way that frightened her more than the fire. "How long has this been in your head, Yara? How long have you known what we'd have to do?"

"Since the First Ledger showed me the founders' names." She pulled Vess closer against her hip; the girl's cheek was hot against her side, feverish with remembering. "I've known since the under-court. I just didn't let myself finish the thought. Because finishing the thought meant—" She stopped.

"Meant your name," Corvane said.

The stairwell shuddered. Below them, the fire found the shelf of bottled names—the physical vessels, the little salt-white coins and stoppered vials the guild kept beside their paper shadows—and something detonated softly, a sound like a held breath released after four hundred years, and the air filled with whispers. Names, she realized. Hundreds of names, freed at once, rising through the smoke like birds startled from a reed bed. Somewhere in the city above, people were stopping in the street, touching their own mouths, saying sounds they'd forgotten they owned.

Vess laughed. It was the first time Yara had heard her laugh, and it was such a small, cracked, human sound that Yara had to look away or come apart right there on the stairs.

"My name," Yara repeated. "Yes."

* * *

Here is what no one tells you about being unmade: it does not feel like anything, until it feels like everything.

Yara had spent her whole adult life as a woman defined by absence. No family. No debts she didn't owe to the guild. No name in any record that mattered—because the guild held her name as collateral, sealed in iron and salt in the deepest vault, and a person whose name is collateral is a person who cannot run. That was the cage Corvane had once described to her, gently, the night she'd signed: *We don't take your freedom, Yara. We take your name. You'll find you can live without it. Most people never notice the difference.*

She had believed him. She had built a whole self on the belief: the numbness, the discipline, the double life, the careful hand that wrote other people's souls into columns while her own stayed blank. Blankness had felt like safety. Blankness had felt like power. *No one can sell what no one can find.*

Now she stood at the top of the burning stairs and understood that blankness had only ever been hunger wearing her shape. The thing beneath the crown ate memory. The guild fed it. And Yara Eriksen—or whatever her name had been before the guild bought it, before some clerk scratched it out and filed it under *assets*—had made herself the perfect servant of that appetite. Invisible. Unrecorded. A hole in the world shaped exactly like a woman.

"No," she said aloud, to the smoke, to Corvane's waiting face, to herself. "That's not—I'm not doing this to disappear. I'm doing it so no one else ever has to be *kept*."

"I know," Corvane said.

"I need you to know that."

"I've known since I rewrote your death-entry." He said it plainly, the confession she had never thanked him for, the thirty years of his own recorded life he had spent to buy her one wrong hour. "You think I did that because I love you? I did it because you're the first honest thing this guild has produced in two centuries. Burning it isn't destroying you, girl. It's the only thing that's ever been worthy of you."

"Flattery," Tam said hoarsely, "after the war."

"After?" Corvane looked past them, up toward the ground floor, where the fire was now visible through the floorboards as a pulsing amber glow, like a heart beating under the guild-house skin. "This isn't after anything. This is the middle. Maybe the beginning. The masters will feel every ledger go. They'll know within the hour who did it and why, and they will come for that child with everything they have left, because if the archive dies before the bargain renews, the crowned thing starves angry—and a starving god doesn't negotiate. It takes."

"How long do we have?" Tam asked.

"Hours," Corvane said. "Perhaps less. Which means you three need to be gone, and I need to stay."

"You'll burn with it."

"I'm ninety-one years old in recorded time, boy. I burned decades ago." He smiled—it transformed his ruined face into something almost young. "Someone should keep a single honest record of tonight. One page. No trades. Just what happened, and who did it, and why. Someone who remembers how to write the truth." He pressed something into Yara's palm: cold, small, round. A salt-white coin, stamped with the guild's mark—the same mark the child had carried to her door a lifetime ago, in another season, when the world still pretended to be whole.

"What's this?"

"Your receipt," Corvane said. "For everything you're about to pay."

Then he turned and walked back down into the smoke, upright, unhurried, a keeper going to check his shelves, and Yara did not watch him go because if she watched she would follow, and she could not follow. She had a king to unbind and a countdown to outrun and a child's hand to hold.

* * *

They came up out of the guild-house into a city that was holding its breath.

The fire behind them lit the alley walls orange. Above, the sky was the bruised violet of false dawn, though dawn was hours away yet, and Yara understood with a lurch that the sky itself had gone strange—that the crowned thing, feeling its food burn, was pulling harder at the realm's memory, dragging color and certainty up out of the world like a drunk draining a bottle. Streetlamps flickered in rhythms that spelled nothing. Somewhere a church bell rang a hour that didn't exist. A woman ran past them clutching a swaddled bundle, sobbing, *I had a daughter, I know I had a daughter, why can't I remember her face*—and Vess broke from Yara's grip and caught the woman's sleeve and spoke six syllables of Root-Tongue, soft as a lullaby.

The woman stopped. Blinked. Looked down at the bundle in her arms, which Yara now saw was not a bundle at all but a small sleeping girl of perhaps three, who had been fading at the edges like wet ink until Vess's word pinned her back into the world.

"There you are," the mother whispered. "There you are. I'm sorry. I'm sorry, I'm sorry—"

"Go home," Yara told her. "Lock your doors. Hold her all night. Say her name until sunrise. Names are rope right now—don't let go of yours."

The woman fled. Tam watched her go, then watched the sky, then looked at Yara with an expression she was learning to read as well as any ledger: fear doing arithmetic.

"It's accelerating," he said. "The losses. Yesterday it was streets and songs. This morning the queen couldn't hold her wedding day. Now it's taking children mid-embrace. If the archive burns completely—if every anchor goes at once—"

"Then the thing has nothing left to sip through and starts drinking from the cup itself." Yara touched the coin in her palm. "Corvane's right. We have hours. Maybe fewer."

"And your name," Tam said quietly. He always went at it sideways, her scribe, circling the wound before naming it. "When the last ledger burns. Your entry. The one with the name the king gave you."

*Rafael.* She hadn't let herself say it yet, even alone, even in the dark. A name written in a dead man's hand thirty years ago, in the margin of a debt-entry: *for the orphan girl, let her be called Rafael, so she is never only what they made her.* A name that existed nowhere else in the world. Not in parish rolls. Not on any tongue. Only in ink, in the vault, in the vault that was currently an oven.

"If I burn my entry," she said, "that name dies unread. No one will ever have said it. It'll be like it never was."

"Unless someone says it first."

She looked at him. Firelight moved on his face. Behind him, Vess stood very still in the mouth of the alley, mud-stained and solemn, watching Yara with those ancient eyes that had once been a king's eyes, and Yara saw the thought complete itself in the child's expression a heartbeat before it completed in her own.

"You," Yara said to Vess. "You read the ledgers. In the archive—you used to put your hand on the pages and just *know*. You read my entry. You've read all of them."

Vess nodded slowly.

"Can you carry it?" Yara crouched to the girl's height, took her small hands, felt how cold they were. "Not on paper. Paper burns. Can you carry my name in here"—she touched the child's chest—"where the fire can't reach? Can you keep it safe for one more day? Just until the king wakes. Just until there's someone left to give it back."

Vess considered her with the gravity of the very old and the very young, which are the same gravity. Then she opened her mouth and spoke a single word of Root-Tongue—the fortieth word Yara had learned, the one that had cost her the memory of her mother's kitchen, the smell of bread, the sound of rain on a roof she could no longer picture—and the word settled into Yara's bones like a key turning in a lock she hadn't known was in her.

It was not her name. It was older than her name. But she understood it, the way you understand a language you dreamed in as a child:

*I will keep you.*

Yara stood. Her legs shook. She found that she was crying and could not remember starting, and that the tears did not feel like grief—they felt like a wall coming down, like a cellar flooding with clean water, like every unbottled grief in the burning building below her finding its way home through the only door left open.

"All right," she said. She wiped her face with the heel of her hand, once, hard. "Tam. The palace. The under-court. We go now, while the masters are still digging their enforcers out of the smoke."

"And the coin?"

She looked down at the salt-white disc in her palm, at the guild's mark, at the mark that had started everything. Then she drew back her arm and threw it as far as she could, out over the rooftops, into the violet dark, and did not listen for where it landed.

"Forget the receipts," Yara said. "Tonight we settle accounts."

Behind them, the guild-house roof groaned and gave, and the fire stood up into the sky like something finally allowed to rise, and the whispering above the city grew louder—thousands of names ascending at once, a chorus four centuries deep—and ahead of them the road to the palace lay open and gray and getting grayer, landmark by landmark, memory by memory, as the starving thing beneath the crown began, at last, to eat the capital itself.
