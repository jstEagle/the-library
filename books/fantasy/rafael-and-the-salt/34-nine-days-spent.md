## 34. Nine Days, Spent

The bells stopped on the sixth day.

Not because the ringers had forgotten how to ring them—though that came later—but because there was no one left in the bell-tower who remembered why the bells mattered. Yara stood at her window in the grey hour before dawn and listened to the silence where the morning peal should have been, and understood that the city had begun to die in earnest.

Below her, the Saltmarket was still there. Stalls, awnings, the fishmonger's cart with its reeking barrels. But the colors were wrong. Not faded—*unwritten*. The red of a fruit-seller's canopy looked like a description of red rather than red itself, as if someone had summarized it and discarded the original. A woman bought onions from a man whose face Yara could not hold in her mind for longer than a blink. She looked away, looked back, and the man's face was smooth as an egg.

She had seen this before, in small doses. A street name missing from a signpost. A song no one could finish. But now it was wholesale. The thing beneath the crown had stopped rationing itself.

"Yara." Tam's voice from the stairwell, breathless. "The Fountain of Aldous. You have to see it."

She knew what she would find before she got there. She went anyway, because going was all that was left to do.

* * *

The fountain stood in the center of Cordwainer's Square, or something stood there—a shape of weathered stone, dry-basined, its carved figures worn to suggestion. Yara remembered the fountain. Everyone remembered the fountain. It had been gilded at the spouts; children had ridden the bronze dolphin for four generations; lovers had thrown coins and sworn by them. She remembered it so hard her teeth ached.

But the square did not remember it back.

People walked past the grey shape without seeing it. Not around it—*past* it, through the space it occupied, shoulders passing through stone like smoke through smoke. A baker set down his tray directly in the fountain's basin and arranged his loaves there, in the dry bowl where coins had glittered for two hundred years, and he did not notice, because to him there was nothing there but pavement.

"It's not just being forgotten," Tam said quietly. He had his scribe's satchel over his shoulder and ink stains on his fingers, and he kept touching the strap like a talisman. "It's being *removed*. The memory goes first, then the object loses its anchor. Corvane warned us about this—the ledgers don't record the world, they *hold* it up. Every entry is a nail. The thing is pulling nails."

"How many days?"

Tam didn't answer immediately. He took out his slate instead, where he'd been keeping the count in his own hand, in his own ink, because written things lasted longer than thought things now. He turned it toward her.

**Three**, it said. Then, beneath, in smaller letters: *maybe less.*

Three days since the conclave's vote. Three days since the guild had delivered their decoy child to the palace and the crowned thing had accepted the offering—and taken an entire season as payment. Autumn was gone. Not ended: gone. There were no harvest festivals in anyone's memory, no funerals for those who'd died in the reaping months, no births registered between the first frost and the first snow. Whole families had simply never been born, and their houses stood empty and unremarked, doors open on dust.

And the thing was still starving. The decoy had been ash on its tongue. Now it fed openly, gluttonously, dragging its mouth across the capital like a net across a riverbed.

"We need to move tonight," Yara said. "Tomorrow at the latest. Whatever's left of the plan."

"The under-court route through the cisterns is flooded past the second junction. Corvane says—"

"I know what Corvane says." She softened it with a hand on his arm. His sleeve was thin; he'd lost weight; they all had. Fear ate faster than any poison she'd ever tasted. "We'll go through the archive sub-levels. I know passages in the ledgers' cross-references that even Corvane doesn't. Where do we stand on Vess?"

"She slept," Tam said. "Actually slept, for the first time in two days. She's teaching herself words while she dreams—I can hear her murmuring in that language when I check on her. Root-Tongue." He hesitated. "Yara. When she talks in her sleep, things happen. Last night she said a word over and over, and in the morning the baker on our street remembered his mother's lullaby. Just that one song. He sat in his doorway singing it and crying and he didn't know why."

Yara looked at the grey shape in the square, at the baker arranging his loaves inside it without knowing, and felt the terrible arithmetic of it settle over her: one word restored one song, while the thing devoured a season per hour. They were bailing the sea with a thimble.

"Then we get her to the throne room," she said, "and we let her say every word she has."

* * *

They crossed the city in the manner of ghosts, which was fitting, because the city was becoming a city of ghosts who hadn't yet noticed they were dead.

The great landmarks were going out one by one, like candles snuffed by a walking draft. The Hall of Records stood open and hollow, its shelves bare—not looted, Yara realized with a lurch, but *never filled*, the documents unwritten retroactively until the building itself had become a shell with no reason to exist. The Triumphal Arch had lost its inscriptions; the names of the victorious dead slid off the stone like rain off glass. By midday the Cathedral of Saint Verene was only a suggestion, a grey sketch of buttresses against the sky, and people detoured around the empty air where its doors had been without knowing why they detoured.

Worse than the stones were the people.

Yara saw it in the Weavers' Row, and she would see it every night afterward behind her closed eyes: a woman kneeling in the street, holding a small boy by the shoulders, shaking him gently, saying, *I know you're mine, I know it, tell me your name, tell me your name*—and the boy looking up at her with polite, blank courtesy, the way one regards a kind stranger. The woman had tied a string around his wrist with his name knotted into it, and the boy wore the string, and the string meant nothing to him. Names written on paper lasted a little longer. Names held only in flesh were going out like sparks.

A father forgot his daughter mid-embrace. Yara watched it happen outside a chandler's shop: the man lifted the girl onto his shoulders, laughing at something, and then, halfway through the laugh, his arms loosened, and he set her down with the careful neutrality of a man handling someone else's child, and stepped back, and frowned at her, and walked away. The girl stood in the road. She did not cry. That was the worst part—that she seemed to understand, in some animal way, that crying would change nothing, that grief itself required a memory to stand on.

Vess made a sound Yara had never heard from her—a small cracked noise, half word and half wound—and pulled against Yara's grip, trying to run to the girl.

"No." Yara knelt, took the child's face in her hands. Mud-colored eyes, old as the ground under kingdoms. "Look at me. *Vess.* If you go to her, the enforcers will find you, and then everyone forgets everything. Do you hear me? Everyone. Her too."

The child's jaw trembled. She said something in Root-Tongue—three syllables, low and resonant, and Yara felt them in her sternum like a struck bell—and though Yara had learned barely a hundred words of the old language, she understood this one, because some words translate themselves:

*It isn't fair.*

"No," Yara said. "It isn't. Come on."

She picked the child up and carried her, and Vess pressed her face into Yara's shoulder, and behind them the little girl in the road turned in a slow circle, looking for someone, and then stopped looking, because she had already forgotten there was anything to look for.

* * *

Corvane met them at the drop-house door, and Yara stopped dead in the alley, because Corvane was *old*.

He had been aging since the night he spent thirty years of his recorded life to rewrite her death-entry—she'd watched it happen in increments, a new line at his eyes each week, his hair going from iron to ash—but now the process had accelerated horribly, as if the world's unraveling had loosened whatever held his borrowed years in place. He leaned on the doorframe. His hands, always steady enough to balance a ledger page on one fingertip, shook.

"Don't," he said, before she could speak. "Don't waste time on it. I spent my years buying yours; I'd spend the rest the same way. That's not sentiment, that's accounting." He pushed off the frame and straightened as much as he could. "Come inside. Both of you. And bring the girl—we have a problem, and it has her face on it."

Inside, the drop-house was dim and smelled of tallow and old salt. Two of the other tasters were there—Maren, broad and silent, and quick-fingered Osk, who had once detected henbane in a broth by smell alone through three feet of steam. They nodded to Yara. Around them, spread across three tables, lay the last of the guild's records—not ledgers anymore, but scraps: pages torn from burning books, notes in Tam's hand, copies of copies. An archive in exile.

"The masters aren't waiting for the nine days," Corvane said. "That's the problem. When the decoy failed, the conclave panicked. They've convinced themselves the bargain can be renewed if the offering is *true*—a real fragment of the king's soul. They voted an hour ago. Enforcers move on the girl at moonrise tonight."

"Moonrise." Yara looked at the shuttered window, at the pale seam of daylight around its edges. Hours. "How many enforcers?"

"All of them. Every blade the guild has left—they've recalled them from the districts. Which means the districts are unprotected, which means—" he gestured vaguely at the greying world beyond the walls, "—that hardly matters anymore. Yara. Listen to me. The masters believe delivering Vess will save what's left of the city. They're wrong—it will only feed the thing long enough to finish eating. But they believe it, and belief makes men fast."

"Then we're gone before moonrise." She was already moving, rolling the record-scraps into oilcloth, her hands doing the work while her mind ran the routes. Archive sub-levels to the cistern junction, junction to the foundation galleries, galleries down to—

"To where?" Maren asked flatly. "You're taking her *toward* it. To the under-court. To the throne room. That's not escape, that's delivery with extra steps."

"It's the only place the unbinding can be done," Tam said. "The binding was struck there. The First Ledger says—"

"The First Ledger burned with the rest of the archive," Maren said.

"A copy survived," Yara said. "Mine. In my head, where no fire reaches and no master reads." She tied the oilcloth bundle shut and looked around at them: the old man spending his final years standing upright, the scribe with ink-stained fingers and no debt left except the ones he chose, the two tasters who owed the guild nothing but their training and had come anyway. "I won't lie to any of you. What's down there wears the king's face and speaks in his voice, and it will offer each of us exactly the thing we want most, and it will make the offer sound like mercy. Some of us may not walk back up. And if we fail—if Vess fails—there is no version of tomorrow where any of us remember why we went."

Silence. Somewhere above, faintly, a bell rang—one bell, uncertain, as if the ringer had started and then forgotten the pattern.

Osk spoke first. "I tasted for the crown eleven years. Poison after poison after poison, so that a man I never met could eat dinner without fear." He shrugged. "Feels right to finish the job."

Maren said nothing. She picked up the heaviest bundle, which was answer enough.

Corvane looked at Yara for a long moment, and in his ruined, aged face she saw something she had never seen there in fifteen years of ledgers and warnings and cold professional distance: pride, worn as openly as a scar.

"When my entry was young," he said, "before the guild, I was a schoolmaster. Did you know that? I taught children their letters." He smiled, and it hurt to look at. "I keep forgetting that. The guild took it. But lately, with the world coming apart, things leak *back*. Strange, isn't it? The thing eats memory, and what escapes it comes home." He took up his coat. "Perhaps that's the weapon none of us counted. Perhaps remembering is contagious."

* * *

Moonrise found them in the foundation galleries beneath the palace, moving by Tam's lantern through corridors of black stone older than any kingdom—older, Vess signed to them in the gesture-language she used when words were too costly, than the *first* kingdom, older than the idea of kingdoms at all.

Behind them, above them, muffled by fathoms of rock, the city was making its sounds: shouting, running feet, the crash of a search party turning over the drop-house they'd left twenty minutes ahead of the enforcers. And beneath all of it, growing steadily louder as they descended, a sound Yara had no name for and suspected no language did—a vast slow grinding, like a millstone the size of the sky, turning, turning, hungry.

Twice, the passage grayed around them.

The first time, it was a stretch of wall—carved reliefs, kings and saints and the founding of the city, suddenly flattening into blankness, the figures smoothing over like wax under a thumb. Tam touched the blank stone and pulled his fingers back as if burned. "Those were the First Kings," he whispered. "I read them in a chronicle. Their names are already gone from every book. Now the faces."

The second time, it was Osk.

He was walking beside Yara, lantern raised, and then he wasn't walking—he was standing, perfectly still, the lantern drooping in his grip, his face emptied of everything, and for three heartbeats Yara looked at a man with no one home behind his eyes. Then he blinked, shuddered, and said, "Sorry. Lost the thread. What were we—where—" and Maren caught his arm and said his name, firmly, twice, three times, until it caught on something in him and he came back, gasping, white-faced.

"It reached down here," Maren said. "It shouldn't reach down here."

"It's not reaching," Yara said quietly. She had been watching the dark ahead of them, where the corridor bent down toward the under-court, and she had finally admitted to herself what the grinding sound was doing. It wasn't random. It was methodical. Working outward from the palace foundations, district by district, landmark by landmark, name by name. "It's tidying. Clearing the table." She looked at Vess, riding on Tam's shoulders, her small hands gripping his hair, her ancient eyes reflecting the lantern flame. "It knows we're coming. It's clearing a path. It wants us to arrive."

"Then it's made a mistake," Tam said. His voice shook, but he said it. "It thinks we're bringing it a meal."

Vess slid down from his shoulders. She stood in the black corridor, in the lantern light, this mud-streaked scrap of a child who was the last living piece of a king's soul, and she looked down the passage toward the throne room where her larger self had been bound and buried and eaten from for three hundred years.

Then she spoke aloud, in Root-Tongue, one full sentence, and the lantern flames stood up straight, and the grinding above them faltered—for just a moment, one ragged instant, like a heartbeat skipping—and somewhere far overhead, Yara would swear afterward, a bell remembered its pattern and rang true.

Yara didn't know the whole sentence. But she knew its last word. Every orphan of the guild knew that word in every language, because it was the first word the guild ever taught them and the last word it ever let them keep.

*Debt*, Vess had said. *The debt comes due.*

Yara drew the First Ledger's copy from her coat, checked the binding-rite one final time by lantern light, and led them down into the dark.
