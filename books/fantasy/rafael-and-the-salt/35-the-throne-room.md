## 35. The Throne Room

The palace was forgetting itself around us.

We came down through the east wing, and the east wing was already gone—not burned, not broken, but *unwritten*. Corridor after corridor stood in a state of half-argument with itself: walls that had been marble yesterday were rough-hewn stone today, then nothing at all, then something older than both, cyclopean blocks fitted without mortar by hands that had never learned the king's alphabet. Tapestries hung gray as fog. Where a portrait of the old queen-mother should have been, there was only a frame holding a rectangle of pale nothing, like a window onto overcast sky.

"The feeding's accelerated," Corvane said. He had aged thirty years in a single confession, and his body was still catching up to it; he climbed the stairs like a man wading upstream, one hand pressed to the wall for balance, breathing in short, economical pulls. "It's not rationing anymore. It's gorging before the fast."

"Before what fast?" Tam asked. He kept close to Vess, one hand hovering near her shoulder the way you hover near a candleflame in wind—not touching, just ready.

"Before us," I said.

Vess walked in the middle of us, and the palace bent toward her as we passed. Doors that had sealed themselves for centuries sighed open at her approach. A fountain in some forgotten courtyard, dry since before my recruitment, shuddered and coughed up a single thread of water—just enough to show us its own reflection, silver and brief—and then went still again. She did not command any of this. It simply remembered her, the way an old dog remembers the child who left.

She had stopped speaking Root-Tongue in sentences. Now she spoke it the way she'd spoken everything else since we entered the under-palace: in fragments, in single words dropped like stones down wells. *"Deyan."* Down. *"Sar."* Old. And once, looking at the unwritten portrait, a word I now knew well enough to feel it in my teeth: *"Neth."* Forgotten.

"Yes," I told her. "It is."

* * *

We lost the light twice.

The first time was on the Stair of Hours, the great spiral descent from the royal apartments into the foundations—a stair most courtiers believed was a legend, built by the first dynasty to connect the living palace to whatever had come before it. The torches Tam carried guttered all at once, all four of them, though there was no draft. In the dark Vess made a sound I had never heard from her: not fear. Recognition. She hummed three notes, low and round, and the dark answered with a pressure against my ears like the memory of a voice.

Then the torches caught again, and we saw that the stairwell walls were covered in writing.

Not carved. *Written*, in ink that had gone brown with age, in a hundred different hands, layered over centuries—the way pilgrims write their names at shrines, except these weren't names. They were entries. Ledger entries, in the old guild script and in scripts older than the guild, climbing the spiral down into the earth. *One year of strong back, traded for a son's fever broken. One grief, bottled amber, sold twice. One name, surrendered freely—*

I stopped reading. Reading them felt like standing too close to an open mouth.

"They wrote their bargains here," Corvane said quietly. "Every master, on the night they took the seal. Before the First Ledger existed, the wall was the ledger." His hand rose toward the writing and stopped short of touching it, hovering, the way Tam hovered near Vess. "I remember wanting to add mine. I don't remember why I didn't." He looked at his own hand as if it belonged to someone braver. "Perhaps that was the last honest thing I ever did."

"Keep moving," I said.

The second darkness came at the bottom, and it was not darkness at all. It was *absence*. We stepped off the last stair into the under-court antechamber, and the light didn't fail—it was taken. Not snuffed; consumed. I felt it go, felt the warmth peel off my skin like a bandage, and in that blackness I heard Vess say, clearly, in the trade-tongue she almost never used:

"He's hungry."

* * *

The under-court throne room had been described in exactly one document I had ever audited: a sealed folio in the First Ledger, catalogued under a heading that translated roughly as *the room where the mistake was kept*. The guild's founders had written about it the way sailors write about reefs—with precision, and with the understanding that precision would not save anyone.

It was a hall built to shame the palace above it. The ceiling soared into darkness that our torchlight could not find an end to, and the pillars that held it up were not pillars but *columns of salt*—vast translucent spars, honey-colored where the flame touched them, veined with shadow. The whole room smelled of the sea at low tide, of brine and exposed stone, and beneath that, faintly, of something sweet going bad.

The throne stood at the far end on a dais of black glass, and it was not the gilded chair of the court above. It was a slab. A flat stone slab, worn smooth in the shape of a body, with iron rings at the wrists and ankles and throat.

The binding seat. The original one.

And on it sat the king.

He was wearing the crown, and he was wearing the body I had guarded with my tongue for eleven years—that broad, weathered frame, those heavy hands—but he was wearing it the way a man wears clothes borrowed for a funeral: correctly, and all wrong. The posture was too still. The chest rose and fell but the rhythm of it had no source, no tide of breath behind it, just motion, performed. His eyes were open and fixed on the doorway where we stood, and they were the king's grey-green eyes, and they were full of tears.

"There you are," the thing said, in the king's voice.

Tam made a small sound and stepped in front of Vess. Corvane's hand found my arm—not restraining me. Bracing me.

"You brought her," the crowned thing said, and the king's voice cracked on the words, and the tears ran down the king's face, and every part of me that had spent years learning to read poison in the set of a man's mouth read, unmistakably, *distress.* "Thank you. Oh—thank you. I have been so hungry, and so tired, and it hurts, Yara. You know how it hurts. You've felt it at the table every day of your life. Come here. Bring the child. Let me look at her."

"Stop," I said.

"I can't." The king's head turned with terrible smoothness, tracking Vess behind Tam's shoulder. "That's the truth of it, taster. That's the honest bargain no ledger ever recorded. I cannot stop. Do you know what I ate first, when they put me in this body? Not memories. Not at first. I ate the *taste* of things. Every flavor in the palace, drained out of the air. That's why your king doesn't taste his food anymore—he never will again, because I swallowed that sense whole the first night, and I've been starving ever since. Flavor, then songs. Then faces. Then roads." The king's hands opened on the slab, palms up, supplicating. "Do you understand? I am a hunger wearing a mercy. I was bound into a shape too small for me, and I have been eating this kingdom one remembering at a time just to keep the edges of myself from unraveling, and it has never once been enough, and I am *so tired*, and you—you are the only person in this world who knows what it is to swallow poison every day and call it duty."

The words went into me like a blade finding the gap in armor. Because it was true. All of it. I could feel its truth the way I felt arsenic on the tongue: instantly, and below thought.

"That's not him talking," I said, and my voice held, mostly. "That's you reading me. Don't."

"I'm not reading you," said the thing in the king's face, gently. "I'm *in* the same book."

* * *

It rose off the slab, and the salt columns dimmed.

Not all at once—in waves, light pulling away from them like water withdrawing down a beach, so that the hall strobed between honey-gold and gloom. The crowned thing descended the dais steps one at a time, unhurried, and with each step the air grew colder and thinner, as if the room itself were being eaten ahead of its arrival. Tam backed us toward the wall, arm out across Vess like a gate-bar. Corvane drew a knife I hadn't known he carried—an enforcer's blade, guild-marked—and his hand shook around the grip, not from fear. From age. From the thirty years he'd spent out of his own account to buy my escape. He would swing that knife like a man of sixty-eight because that was what he was now, inside.

"Yara Eriksen," the thing said, and used my name, my real name, the one that existed nowhere anymore except in a dead girl's memory and a burning archive—and hearing it in that voice was worse than any threat. "Ledger-keeper. Taster. Orphan of the Salt Row fire. I know your entries better than you do. Shall I recite?"

"No need," I said. "I wrote most of them."

"Some you didn't." It smiled with the king's mouth, and the smile fit badly, like a mask strapped on by someone who had studied smiles but never worn one. "Here is an entry no book holds. Nine days ago I offered you a trade. You refused. Very well—I respect refusal; it's the rarest thing in any ledger. So hear my final terms, freely given, no trick in them, because I am too hungry now for tricks. Deliver me the vessel"—the king's eyes flicked to Vess, and the hunger in that glance was so vast and so old that my knees nearly buckled under the weight of merely witnessing it—"and I will leave this city. This kingdom. I will take the sea road and trouble no one born under your flag again. You will have your king back, waking, whole, remembering his wife. You will have your own name restored to every record the fire erased. And the scribe"—a tilt of the king's head toward Tam, who had gone white—"the scribe lives to be old, in a city that remembers him fondly. Four trades. One price."

"And if I refuse?" I asked, though I knew. Though we all knew. The gray streets above. The mothers forgetting children mid-embrace. The season already stolen.

"If you refuse," the thing said, and now the king's voice frayed, and underneath the fraying I heard something enormous shifting its weight in the dark of it, "then in nine days I finish eating. Not the memories this time. The *rememberers*. I will drink this city down to bedrock, and I will wear whoever survives longest, and I will begin again somewhere else, because that is what I am. I was doing it before your kingdoms had names. I will do it after your sun forgets to burn." The king's shoulders sagged. The tears came again, real-seeming, endless. "I do not want that. Even I do not want that. Feed me the fragment and let me go, and everyone—*everyone*—gets to keep existing. Is that not mercy? Have I not, in my hunger, offered you the gentlest bargain of anything in your ledgers?"

And the horror of it, the true horror, the thing I knew I would carry past my own death if death was coming: it wasn't lying. I had spent eleven years tasting truth and lies in the mouths of nobles, and this creature's offer rang clean. It meant it. Given Vess, it would go. Given nothing, it would end the world. Both statements true. Both statements sincere.

That's what made it unbearable. Monsters lie, and lies can be fought. This was only arithmetic, and the arithmetic was almost kind.

"Yara," Tam whispered. "Your face. Whatever you're thinking—your face is doing something."

"I'm thinking it's a fair price," I said.

"Don't."

"I'm thinking it's a fair price," I repeated, "and that every fair price in that archive was paid by someone who couldn't afford it, and that the guild lasted six hundred years on exactly that mistake." I stepped forward, out from behind Corvane, out from behind the plan, into the open floor between the torchlight and the throne. "You want the vessel. Come and calculate."

* * *

It moved faster than a king's body should.

One moment it stood ten paces away, the next the king's face filled my vision, hands closing toward me—and then Vess was between us. Small, mud-brown hair, salt-white coin clutched in her fist, held up before her like a lamp.

The crowned thing stopped as if it had struck glass.

The sound it made was not a word in any language. It was the sound the stairwell-darkness had made, magnified a thousandfold—a pressure, a groaning, the complaint of something vast meeting the one thing in creation it could not digest. The king's face contorted. Behind the eyes, behind the tears, behind even the hunger, something recoiled from that little raised fist the way a hand recoils from a brand.

"*Neth sar,*" Vess said. Her voice was very small and very steady. *Forgotten old.* Then, lifting the coin higher, in Root-Tongue, a full sentence at last—the first I had ever heard complete from her, each syllable landing in the salt columns like a hammer on a bell:

"What was given to the salt belongs to the salt."

The columns flared. Light ran up them like fire up fuses, and the veins of shadow inside the salt writhed—and I understood, finally, with a lurch, what the pillars were. Not architecture. *Storage*. Six hundred years of the guild's inventory packed into the bones of the binding place, grief and years and names, all of it salt-cured, all of it present, all of it *here*—and all of it resonating to the Root-Tongue like struck crystal.

The crowned thing staggered back toward the slab. The king's hands clawed at empty air.

"You cannot," it rasped, in a voice losing its shape, sliding between the king's baritone and something older, wider, hollow as a sea cave. "Those are *mine*. Every entry fed me. Every trade—"

"Was theft," I said. "And the vault remembers its owner."

Vess turned and looked at me. Not at the thing. At me. And her eyes—brown, ordinary, a child's eyes—held something patient and immense, and she said one more word, and this word she aimed at me alone:

*"Rafael."*

The name went through me like cold water through cloth. Not the orphan-name. Not yet. But the shape of it, the door of it, opening.

"Now," she said, in trade-tongue, urgent, the first command I had ever heard her give. "While the salt is singing. Yara. *Now.*"

Behind her, dragging itself up onto the black-glass dais, the crowned thing gathered the last of its stolen light around the king's failing body and began—to my absolute horror—to *weep*, great racking sobs in a father's voice, begging, pleading, promising, while the salt sang and the countdown I could feel in my own poisoned blood ticked toward nine days or toward none.

Vess held out her free hand to me, palm open.

"Give me the name," she said, "before it finds its feet."
