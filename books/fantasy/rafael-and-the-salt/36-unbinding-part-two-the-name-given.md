## 36. Unbinding, Part Two: The Name Given

The under-court was drowning.

Not in water—in forgetting. It poured down the palace foundations like silt through a cracked cistern, gray and fine and endless, and where it settled, the world went quiet. Yara watched it happen to the wall beside her. The mortar between the ancient stones had been carved with names—she could see the ghost of them, grooves worn by some mason's chisel a thousand years before the first king—and as she stood there breathing the ash-thick air, those names smoothed over one by one, like faces sinking beneath still water. The stones did not crumble. They simply stopped having ever been carved.

"Keep moving." Corvane's voice came from behind her, and it was wrong—old. He had spent thirty years of his recorded life to buy her an escape, and his body had collected the debt all at once: his hands were liver-spotted now, his back bent, his voice a dry rasp that belonged to a man twice his age. He moved like a man carrying sandbags of years on each shoulder. "The thing is starving. Starving things rush."

"It's not rushing," Tam said. He had Vess's hand in his, and the girl's small fingers were white where he gripped them. "It's *savoring*. Look at the walls. It's eating slowly because it wants us to watch."

Yara wanted to tell him he was wrong. She could not. Above them, muffled through forty feet of stone and earth, the capital was going out like lamps. She had felt it begin an hour ago—the Grayout, the tasters' word for it now, passed in whispers through the servants' corridors. First the eastern districts, then the river quarter. A whole neighborhood of people who had stopped mid-stride and stood smiling at nothing, holding their children without knowing whose children they held. The bells had rung for noon and no one remembered why bells existed.

Vess pulled free of Tam's hand and walked ahead.

She did not run. That was the thing about Vess that never stopped astonishing Yara—the girl had been a fragment of a king's soul, cast out and stuffed into a child's body and left to wander half the kingdom mute, and she still walked like someone who expected the world to make room. Her bare feet left prints in the gray silt, and where she stepped, the prints stayed dark. Clean. Unforgotten.

"She knows the way," Yara said.

"Of course she does," Corvane rasped. "She was here when they built it."

* * *

The original throne room had no doors. You arrived at it the way you arrived at grief—suddenly, and after thinking you had been prepared.

One moment they were squeezing through a fissure in the foundation stone, Tam's lantern guttering; the next, the fissure opened into a vaulted dark so vast the lantern-light died before it found the far wall. Pillars rose around them, each one carved floor to ceiling in the Root-Tongue, and the words were still alive. Yara could feel them working, the way you feel a poultice drawing poison—a slow, patient pull, centuries old, drawing something up out of the deep and binding it there.

And there, at the center, on a dais of black salt:

A throne. And on the throne, a man.

He looked, at this distance, almost peaceful. The king sat slumped the way an exhausted father slumps at the end of a feast day, crown tilted, hands open on his knees. But his chest did not rise. His eyes were open and fixed on nothing, and around him the air shimmered faintly, like heat above a road, like the surface tension of something wearing him from inside.

Between them, on the floor of the great chamber, lay the guild.

Not the men—the ledgers. Hundreds of them, thousands, dragged down here over centuries and arranged in concentric rings around the throne like offerings, like scales, like a digestive tract. Yara recognized bindings she had catalogued herself. There was the First Ledger, chained shut, its iron hasps green with age. There were volumes she had burned three nights ago—or thought she had—and here they were, unburnt, because the fire had only freed what was written in them, not destroyed the writing itself. The words had come home to roost.

"Oh," Tam said softly. "Oh, it's been *feeding*."

"The ledgers are its stomach," Corvane said. He sounded like a man describing his own autopsy. "I told you what we were. I didn't understand it until tonight. We weren't jailers. We were farmers. Every trade, every bargain, every bottled grief and borrowed name—we harvested memory and carried it here and laid it at its feet, and it ate, and in exchange it let us keep our little kingdom of salt."

"And when we burned the archive," Yara said, "we starved it."

"You *woke* it hungry," Corvane said.

The king's head turned.

It was not the motion of a neck. It was the motion of something inside the neck deciding to use it, and every hair on Yara's arms stood up beneath her sleeves. The crowned thing looked at them across the rings of ledgers, and it smiled with the king's mouth, and the smile was warm and terrible and *familiar*, because she had seen that exact smile across the tasting table a hundred times—the smile of a sovereign being gracious to his food.

"Little taster," it said.

The voice was the king's voice. That was the cruelty of it. It spoke in the same low, tired warmth she had heard praise a hundred dishes, and it used his throat the way a musician uses a stolen instrument.

"You brought me my vessel," it said. "After all your running. After all your clever little fires. You carried her right to me." The king's body rose from the throne, joint by joint, unhurried. "I want you to know that I appreciate it. Hunger makes everything crude, and I have been so very hungry, and yet here you are, delivering yourself with such *ceremony*. I will remember this. I will remember you especially."

"No," Yara said.

The thing tilted the king's head, curious.

"You won't," she said. "Remember me. That's the whole of it, isn't it? That's what you do. You eat remembering. And I am going to stand here, in front of you, and I am going to say a name you have spent nine days trying to drown, and you are not going to be able to stop it."

Something flickered across the king's face—not fear, exactly. Recognition. The look of a creature that has just realized the trap was not the room but the conversation.

"Girl," it said, and now the warmth drained away, and what remained was older than kingdoms, vast and dry as a salt flat. "Do you know how many names I have eaten? I drank the tongue of the people who built these pillars. I swallowed the god-names of the river valleys before your kings learned to write. I have forgotten more worlds than yours has ever contained. And you bring me *one name*?"

"One name," Yara said, "freely given."

The pillars hummed.

She felt it in her teeth, in the scars along her jaw where twenty years of poison had written their own ledger into her flesh. The Root-Tongue words carved into the stone woke at the phrase, the way coals wake at breath. *Freely given.* The oldest clause. The clause the guild had built its entire trade upon and never once honored, because nothing given under debt is freely given, and everything in the guild's world ran on debt.

Except one entry.

Her hand went to the page tucked against her heart—torn from the First Ledger in the burning, the one record she had saved. The paper was soft as cloth now, handled too often. She did not need to read it. She had read it a hundred times since the fire.

*A debt of bread, entered in the year the plague came north. One loaf, given without price, to a starving child at the Salt Gate. Entered not as debt but as trust. Name of the debtor, unknown. Name given to the debtor: Rafael.*

The king had saved her. Years ago, before she was a taster, before she was a ledger-keeper, before she was anything but a girl starving at a gate in the worst winter of her life—somebody had knelt down in the snow, broken a loaf in half, and put it in her hands. And somebody had written it down honestly, in the one book in the world that kept honest records, and had given her a name while doing it. Not taken a name. Given one.

That was the trust the unbinding required. A name freely given by someone the king's true self trusted. She had searched the ledgers for weeks for a living soul who qualified, and the answer had been in her own hand the whole time, in her own file, in the entry that made her the only person alive the king had ever trusted without knowing it.

"I need you to hold," she said quietly, over her shoulder. To Tam. To Corvane. To Vess most of all.

"We're holding," Tam said. His voice shook. His feet did not move.

* * *

The thing came down off the dais while she unfolded the page.

It moved fast now—hunger had burned the ceremony out of it—and the gray forgetting poured off it like smoke off wet iron. Where it stepped, the nearest ring of ledgers curled and blackened, and the words on their pages lifted into the air like ash made of language, and the thing breathed them in. Yara heard fragments as they passed: a woman's wedding vow. A fisherman's name for a wind. A lullaby in a dialect dead four hundred years. All of it going down the king's throat into the dark below the dark.

"Corvane," she said.

"On it." The old man limped forward, and from inside his coat he drew the conclave's seal—the iron stamp of the Salt Guild, the mark that had ruled her whole life, stamped on the coin Vess had carried to her door. He raised it high, and he spoke the counter-word every ledger-keeper learned first and hoped never to use: the word that opened the books. All of them. At once.

Every ledger in the chamber fell open.

The sound was enormous—not a crash but a sigh, ten thousand covers parting like wings—and the thing staggered. For one heartbeat, the king's body simply stopped, swaying, both hands pressed to its temples, and the forgetting pouring off it faltered, stuttered, reversed. Freed pages everywhere, unbound debts, released trades—bottled grief evaporating into the air, stolen decades flying home to bodies long buried, borrowed names peeling off their wearers like bandages. The chamber filled with returning things. Yara saw a street exist briefly in the empty air, cobbles and lamplight and a baker's boy laughing, restored for a moment before it faded, seeking its place in the world above.

"*You dare*—" the thing began, in a voice that was suddenly not the king's at all.

"Tam!" Yara shouted. "Now!"

Tam swept Vess up onto his shoulders and ran—not toward the throne but along the outermost ring, following the path Yara had drilled into them for two days: the spiral, the pilgrim's road, the way the founders themselves must have walked when they came down here to strike the first bargain. Around and inward, ring by ring, ledger by ledger, closer to the dais. The thing tore itself free of the opening books and lunged, and Corvane stepped into its path—an old, bent, dying man standing between a god of hunger and a child—and he did the only thing left in his arsenal.

He read aloud.

His voice was ruined, a rasp, barely a whisper—but he had spent sixty years keeping accounts, and he knew them all by heart. He recited entries. Names. Debts. The whole honest shame of the guild, poured out syllable by syllable, and every word was a hook in the thing's hide, because it could not pass a recorded debt without stopping to collect. It snarled and reached and *stopped*, again and again, compelled by the oldest law of its own making, while Tam ran and Yara walked the spiral with the torn page in her fist.

At the foot of the dais, Vess slid down from Tam's shoulders.

She looked up at the black salt throne, and then at the king sitting in it, and Yara saw the girl's face do something it had never done in all their weeks together. It broke. Not with fear—with *recognition*. This was home. This was the shape she had been cut from. A fragment of a soul, looking up at the hollow it had been carved out of.

Vess turned back and held out both hands to Yara.

*Come,* said her cupped palms, her tilted head, her whole small eloquent body. *Finish it.*

* * *

Yara climbed the dais.

The thing fought Corvane's recitation and won—it ripped free of the last hooks with a sound like a ship tearing from a dock, and it came up the steps after her, and Tam threw himself onto its back and was shaken off like a coat, and none of it mattered anymore, because Yara was close enough now to see the king's face, truly see it, and beneath the thing's possession she could see *him*: the exhausted father, the man who had once knelt in snow, the good and tired heart that had trusted a starving girl with a loaf and a name.

She put her palm flat on his cold forehead.

"I don't know if you can hear me," she said. "I think maybe the part of you that gave the bread can hear me. So listen. You wrote a name for me once, in a book, in the snow-year. Rafael. You gave it to me free, and I never knew, and I kept it anyway—I kept it my whole life without knowing I was carrying it. That was a gift. Gifts bind tighter than chains. Your own law. Your own book."

The thing's hand closed around her wrist. Its grip was ice and hunger, and it began to squeeze, and somewhere below Tam was screaming her name, and she did not stop.

"So here is mine," she said. "Freely given. In exchange for yours."

She leaned down until her lips nearly touched the king's ear, past the reaching fingers, past the smell of ash and old salt, and she spoke the true name of the king.

It was not a grand name. That was the shock of it—that was the thing she would carry out of this room if she survived to carry anything. It was a short name, a homely name, a name that sounded like a farmer's son, like bread and woodsmoke, like someone's actual child. It had probably not been spoken aloud since the coronation. It had certainly never been spoken by anyone the king trusted.

Until now.

The name left her mouth and the world *inverted*.

Every pillar in the chamber flared white. The Root-Tongue woke all at once, every carved word igniting like a fuse running back through a thousand years, and the binding that the founders had struck—the binding the guild had fed instead of maintained—closed at last, complete, finished, *paid*. Yara felt the debt-entry leave her, felt the paper in her fist go weightless and then weightless-er, dissolving into light between her fingers, the last honest record in the world discharging its final account.

And the thing screamed.

It screamed in the king's voice first, and then in voices that were not the king's, and then in voices that were not anyone's—languages drowned before the first city, tongues of peoples whose bones had gone to dust unnamed, the speech of the river-gods and the salt-witches and the builders of the pillars, all of it pouring out of the king's open mouth in a flood of unraveling sound. Because that was what it was made of, Yara understood, watching it come apart: it was made of everything it had ever eaten. It was a hunger shaped like everything it had consumed, and now, name by name, word by word, the meal was leaving.

It looked at her with the king's eyes as it went. And in the last instant—she would swear this later, to Tam, to the dark, to herself—in the last instant the fury was gone and something else looked out of the fading face. Something old and tired and, impossibly, *relieved*.

"*Finally*," it said, in a voice like the last page of a book closing. "*Someone paid.*"

Then it came apart into gray light and rising dust, and the dust smelled, absurdly, of rain.

* * *

Vess climbed onto the throne.

She did it the way children climb everything—knees and elbows and total commitment—and she knelt astride the king's chest, and she pressed both small hands flat over his silent heart, and she bowed her head.

"Poor Yara," Tam whispered. He had reached the steps, bleeding from the mouth, holding his ribs. "What's she—"

"Watch," Corvane rasped. "For the love of whatever's left, watch."

Light moved. That was the only way Yara could ever describe it afterward. Light moved *from* the girl *into* the man, a slow pour, like honey, like the last measure of something precious being emptied from its jar into the vessel it had always been meant for. Vess grew translucent as she poured. Her edges softened. Her mud-brown hair went bright and then pale and then faint, and she did not cry out, and she did not stop, and near the end she turned her head and found Yara's eyes and smiled—

—and mouthed one word, in the Root-Tongue, in the language older than kingdoms, in the only tongue that had ever known Yara Eriksen's true name.

Then she was gone. All of her, home at last, a fragment returned to the whole, and the king's chest rose.

He breathed.

It was a terrible, ragged, human breath, the breath of a man surfacing from deep water, and then another, and then he coughed and rolled sideways on the black salt and was sick, and groaned, and said, in a voice hoarse and small and entirely his own:

"How long?"

Yara knelt beside him. Her wrist was broken—she could feel it grinding, the thing's last gift—and her vision kept sliding sideways, and somewhere above them the capital was waking up to find nine days missing and a season unwritten, and none of it mattered, none of it would ever matter as much as this: the king was looking at her with his own eyes, and the eyes were confused, and frightened, and *alive*.

"Awhile," she said.

He stared at her. Frowned. The frown of a man grasping at a dream already dissolving. "Have we—" He stopped. Started again. "There was bread. Snow. Someone—" His hand rose, trembling, and touched her cheek, gentle as the touch he had once laid on a starving girl's shoulder. "I know you."

"No," Yara said softly. "But you did."

Above them, muffled by forty feet of stone, a bell began to ring—and this time, all across the city, people remembered why.
