## 37. The King Waking

The scream came first.

It rose out of the under-court like water finding a crack in stone, thin and high and wrong—a sound made of every language the city had ever forgotten, braided together and pulled taut until it snapped. Yara felt it pass through her ribs on the palace stairs, felt it rattle the teeth of the dead ledgers still smoldering behind her, and then—silence. Not the silence of an ending. The silence of a held breath.

Then the light changed.

She was halfway up the servants' stair with Vess's hand locked in hers and Tam a step behind, and the light through the arrow-slit went from gray to gold so suddenly that it seemed the sun itself had remembered its job. Dust motes hung in the beam like seeds waiting to be planted. Somewhere below, a bell began to ring—the dawn bell, though it was past noon—and then a second bell answered it, and a third, bells that had not rung in years because no one remembered what they were for.

"It's done," Tam said. His voice cracked on the second word. He said it again, as if testing whether the world would let him keep it. "It's done."

Vess did not answer. The girl stood very still on the stair, her small face tilted upward toward the light, and tears ran down it without her seeming to notice. She had given away the last of herself to put the king back together, and what remained of her looked lighter than air—as if grief had been the only thing holding her to the ground.

Yara knelt. Her knees screamed; everything screamed; her body was a ledger of debts long overdue. But she knelt, and she took the child's face in her hands, and she looked at her for a long moment before speaking.

"You remember," Yara said. It was not a question. Of all the things the fire had unmade, Vess alone stood outside the burning. "You'll remember me."

Vess nodded once. Then she opened her mouth and spoke—not in the Root-Tongue this time, but in plain words, the first plain words Yara had ever heard from her, rough and small and perfect:

"Go now. Before they look for you."

* * *

They found the king sitting on the floor of the under-court throne room with his back against the true throne, which was not a throne at all but a slab of salt-white stone worn smooth by centuries of kneeling. He was dressed in the rotted finery the thing had kept him in, and he was weeping without any idea why, the way a man weeps at a funeral for someone whose name he cannot recall.

The queen reached him first.

She came down those stairs faster than dignity allowed, skirts gathered in both fists, two guards clattering behind her and losing ground—and when she saw him, when she saw his eyes, something happened to her face that Yara would later try, and fail, to describe to herself. It was not joy. Joy is too simple a word for what happens when a woman gets back a husband she had stopped being able to mourn.

"Marek," she said.

The name fell out of her like a stone out of a clenched fist. And with it, everything else came—all at once, the way floodwater comes when the dam breaks. Later, the physicians would argue about how it was possible, how a mind hollowed by the crowned thing could refill in a single heartbeat. But those who had seen the thing unravel understood: it had not eaten the queen's memories. It had only swallowed them, held them down like a heron holds fish, and when it died, everything it had taken came up and out and home.

So Queen Isolde remembered her wedding day in the space between one breath and the next—the rain on the canopy, the way Marek's hand shook, the joke he whispered during the vows that made her bite her cheek to keep from laughing. She remembered the winter he taught her to skate on the millpond and fell eleven times and blamed the ice each time. She remembered the last morning before it happened, when he had kissed her forehead and said, *If I am ever strange to you, love, look beneath the crown.*

She remembered, and she wept, and she crossed the floor of the under-court and knelt in the dust beside him in her royal silks and took his ruined face in her hands.

"I looked," she told him. "I looked beneath. I couldn't hold it—I tried, every day I tried, and it slipped through me like water, but I never stopped looking."

"I know," the king said. His voice was hoarse from disuse, from years of being borrowed by something else. "I heard you. All that time, I heard you. You were the one thing it couldn't make me forget, so it made me forget that I hadn't."

That made no sense, and she did not care. She held him among the ashes of the First Ledger while the guards stood awkwardly at the stairhead and the bells rang on overhead, and neither of them moved for a long time.

When the king finally rose, he asked the question everyone knew was coming.

"Who did this?"

No one answered. The guards looked at their boots. A chamberlain who had followed them down stammered something about providence and the gods' mercy, and the king looked at him with the flat patience of a man who has spent years wearing a mask and has no tolerance left for anyone else's.

"There was a fire," he said. "There are ashes of books here. Books do not burn themselves, and whatever was in them does not die on its own. Someone came down here. Someone *knew*." He turned, slowly, taking in the scorch marks, the shattered scrying-glass, the salt-white stone of the true throne. "Find them. Find whoever they were. I owe a debt I intend to pay."

* * *

The search began within the hour, and it found nothing, because there was nothing to find.

That is the peculiar mercy of what Yara did in the archive. When she set the ledgers alight, she did not only free the traded lives recorded in them—she erased the records themselves, and the guild's whole architecture of debt and name and collateral came down with the smoke. A person whose name exists nowhere cannot be summoned, cannot be traced, cannot be owed. Yara Eriksen had spent thirty years making herself invisible to poison and politics alike, and in one night of fire she had made herself invisible to history.

The king's men searched anyway. They were thorough; he commanded it personally, and he was, everyone was rediscovering, a king who meant what he said.

They questioned the kitchen staff. They questioned the tasters—all seven of them, lined up in the pantry where Yara had recruited them barely a week before, each of them sworn to silence by a look from Sera, the eldest, who had buried more friends than she could count and was not about to give up the one who had finally fought back. They questioned the scribes, and Tam Voss stood among them with ink still on his fingers and his heart going like a hammer, and answered every question with the bland, bored precision of a man who had spent his career being overlooked and had finally learned to weaponize it.

"The archive?" he said. "I filed writs there sometimes. I never went below the third landing. There are rats below the third landing."

They questioned Corvane, or rather, they tried, and got nowhere, because Corvane sat in the guild-house ruins with his hands folded and his face like a closed door, and when the captain of the guard pressed him, he said only: "The Salt Guild is dissolved. Whatever it kept, it kept for itself. Ask your king what his grandfather's coin bought, if you want answers. Or don't. Some debts are kinder unpaid."

They might have arrested him for that. But the king, hearing of it, waved the captain off. "Leave him," he said quietly. "He spent something enormous to be here. I can see it in his face. Men who spend that much don't lie about small things."

And they questioned the child.

This was the near disaster. Vess was found on the palace steps at dusk, sitting exactly where Yara had left her, small and mud-streaked and mute again—or appearing mute, which amounted to the same thing for a court full of questions. The guards brought her inside wrapped in a captain's cloak. The physicians pronounced her undernourished, traumatized, and possibly simple. The chaplain suggested an exorcism; the queen overruled him.

But the king came himself.

He came down to the guest chamber where they had put her, and he dismissed the attendants, and he sat on the edge of her bed the way a man sits when he has been sitting beside sickbeds all his life. For a long time neither of them spoke. Then Vess looked up at him with those old, old eyes, and the king went very still.

"You," he said softly. "I know you. Not your face. Something deeper than faces." He frowned, pressing his palm flat against his own chest, as if feeling for an echo. "You feel like—" He stopped. Started again. "There was a piece of me gone. I can feel the shape of where it used to be, the way you feel the shape of a missing tooth. And you fit that shape."

Vess studied him for a long moment. Then, deliberately, she shook her head, and pointed at her own chest, and shook her head again: *not anymore.* And after that she lay down and turned to face the wall, and no physician, priest, or king could get another word from her.

The king stayed a while longer anyway. Before he left, he tucked the cloak higher around her shoulders, an oddly gentle gesture from a man the whole court was relearning how to fear and love at once.

"Whoever gave you back to me," he murmured, to the child or to the dark, "I hope they know I'm looking."

* * *

Yara watched none of this. She was three miles downstream by then, and getting farther.

They had gone out through the laundry gate at dusk, the three of them—though *three* was already becoming a lie, because Vess had stopped at the gate and refused to go farther, and no argument, no pleading, no threat moved her. In the end Yara had crouched in the gateway's shadow and held the girl hard enough to hurt them both, and Vess had whispered against her ear in the Root-Tongue, words Yara now understood well enough to translate even as they broke her heart:

*Not goodbye. A name is never goodbye. Go be called.*

Tam had cried. Yara discovered this only later, by the salt tracks on his collar, because he had faced the river the whole time and said nothing, and she loved him a little more for the silence.

Now they walked the towpath in the blue hour, past barge-crews lighting their cookfires, past warehouses where the day's last workers sang a work-song that had come back that morning—everyone agreed it had always existed, though not one of them could say where they'd learned it. The city was stitching itself closed around its wounds. Streets that had grayed out were streets again. Names were returning to doors. In the market square, an old woman stood in front of a fountain weeping and laughing at once, telling anyone who'd listen that she'd just remembered she had a sister, a whole sister, forty years forgotten, alive in the country somewhere, and wasn't that a thing, wasn't that a *thing*—

"She should rest," Tam said, watching Yara sway slightly on the towpath. "We should both rest. There's an inn ahead. The Ferryman's Arms. We can—"

"We can't stop anywhere with my face," Yara said. "The king's men will be at every inn inside the walls by tomorrow."

"So we don't go inside the walls. So we dye your hair, we take the north road, we—" He caught himself, hearing the plan build itself aloud, and stopped, and looked at her sideways. "You're not coming, are you."

It was not quite a question either.

Yara walked a few more paces before answering. The river slid black and patient beside them. Somewhere behind them, faint as memory, the palace bells were still ringing, and she thought of the king waking in his own body for the first time in years, reaching for a stranger who had already become smoke.

"He'll look for me," she said. "He'll look his whole life, maybe. And he'll never find anything, because there's nothing left to find. No ledger entry. No guild record. No debt with my name attached. I burned it all, Tam. Every scrap of paper that ever said *Yara Eriksen* is ash." She laughed, and it came out wrong, ragged at the edges. "Do you know what that's like? To win? To save everything, and stand outside the story of it? Even my scars belong to no one now. Even my crimes. I'm not free. I'm *unwritten*. There's a difference, and I keep waiting for someone to tell me what it is."

Tam stopped walking. He turned and took her by the shoulders, and in the light of the nearest barge-fire his face was very serious and very young and absolutely certain.

"Then I'll write you," he said. "Every day, if I have to. On whatever I've got. I've got ink and I've got hands and I've got—" his voice wobbled, steadied— "I've got however much of you the fire left, and I'm not letting the rest go. That's not a ledger. Ledgers are debts. This is just... remembering. People are allowed to remember people. Nobody has to burn for it."

Yara looked at him for a long moment. Then, because her body was failing and her courage was nearly spent and the truth was the only currency she had never learned to counterfeit, she leaned her forehead against his chest and let him hold her up.

"One condition," she said into his shirt.

"Name it."

"We don't run north. We don't hide at all." She lifted her head. "There's a street off Cooper's Row with an empty shopfront. Big hearth. Good light. It used to be a chandler's. I walked past it a hundred times on guild business and thought: *that's where a person would cook, if a person were allowed to want things.*" Her mouth curved, tired and real. "I want to open a kitchen, Tam. A plain one. Where nothing is traded and nothing is owed and everything is tasted honestly. Where a woman with no name can stand behind a counter and feed people, and if the king himself walks in someday, he can have soup like anybody else."

Tam stared at her. Then he laughed—a startled, broken, wonderful sound—and pulled her close again.

"A kitchen," he said. "We save the kingdom and you want to open a *kitchen*."

"Kingdoms burn," Yara said. "Soup endures."

* * *

In the palace, on that same blue hour, the king stood at his study window with the queen's hand in his, watching the city lights come on one by one along the river.

"Tell me again what the glass showed," he said. "Before it broke. Tell me exactly."

Isolde told him. She had only glimpsed it—one terrible moment, reflected in the shattering—but she described the figures she'd seen: a woman in servant's grays with a child's hand in hers, a young man with ink-stained fingers, an older man standing apart with white spreading through his hair like frost claiming a field. She described how the woman had looked directly into the glass—directly at *her*—in the instant before it burst, and how her expression had not been triumph, exactly. It had been apology. As if she were sorry to be leaving the world better than she found it.

The king listened without moving. When she finished, he was quiet a long time.

"A taster," he said at last. "The kitchen records show a Yara Eriksen, employed nine years. Except—" he tapped the page— "except there's no record of her hiring. No bond posted, no references, no family listed. It's as if she appeared fully formed at my table one day and the paperwork simply... agreed." He looked up. "The guild kept names as collateral. That's what the histories say, now that anyone dares say anything. If she burned the ledgers—"

"Then her name burned with them," Isolde finished softly.

"And she knew it would." He turned back to the window. Down along the river, the Ferryman's Arms was lighting its lamps, and beyond it the dark took the road north, and beyond that, darkness took everything. "She knew exactly what it would cost, and she paid it, and she didn't stay to be thanked." His jaw tightened. "I have spent my life learning what men will do for gold and title. I find I don't know what to do with someone who wants nothing."

"Then learn," the queen said. "You're good at impossible things lately."

He huffed—almost a laugh—and squeezed her hand. But he did not stop watching the river, and long after she had gone to bed he stood there, saying a name under his breath, over and over, quietly, the way a man keeps a coal alive by breathing on it.

"Yara Eriksen. Yara Eriksen."

The name did nothing. It was only sounds, empty as a burnt page. Whoever she had been, she belonged to the fire now.

Or so he believed.

Because three miles downstream, in the doorway of a dark and dusty chandler's shop, a mute child stood waiting in the shadows where she had promised she would wait—patient as salt, older than kingdoms—and when Yara and Tam came up the lane arguing happily about hearthstones and shelf-space, Vess stepped out to meet them, smiling, and opened her mouth, and prepared to speak the only word in all the world that still belonged to Yara Eriksen.

The word that would give her back to herself.
