## 1. The Valley That Forgets

The ink was misbehaving again.

Mirabel Dunmore noticed it the way one notices a draft under a door—not at first, but as a small coldness that gathers until it must be acknowledged. She had been copying the harvest ledgers for three hours, and somewhere in the middle of the barley column, the letters had begun to lean. Not toward her. Never toward her. Toward the window, where the morning light came through the temple's eastern glass in long amber bars, and where—she was quite certain—the ink wanted to go.

She set down her pen.

Around her, the scriptorium of Vellharrow went about its business with the gentle industry of bees. Forty desks, forty scribes, forty pens scratching in unison like rain on a roof. Brother Tamel two rows over had fallen into his usual mid-morning doze, his chin dipping toward his ledger of tannery dues. Young Pell, who still smelled of the farm he'd been given up from, chewed the end of his quill and stared at the ceiling as though the correct spelling of "millstone" might be written there. And at the front of the room, upright as a fence post, Mother Osla presided over everything with the particular silence of a woman who missed nothing and admitted less.

Mirabel glanced at the leaning letters. The letters leaned back, if such a thing could be said of ink, which it could not, which was precisely the problem.

*Stop it,* she told them silently. *You are barley figures. You are dull. You are the dullest thing ever written.*

The letters did not stop, exactly, but they settled—a sulky sort of settling, like a cat denied the window. Mirabel exhaled and bent back to her work, copying the numbers out in her careful, deliberately unremarkable hand: *Barley, north fields, 214 sacks. Barley, south fields, 198 sacks.* Numbers so boring they could put a war to sleep. That was the point. Boring was safe. Boring was invisible.

Invisibility, Mirabel had learned over twenty-three years, was not something you were born with. It was a craft, practiced daily, like penmanship or prayer. You kept your head down but not so far down that it drew notice. You finished your work competently but never brilliantly. You wore grey because grey asked nothing of anyone. When the other scribes gathered at supper to complain about the porridge or gossip about Brother Tamel's snoring, you laughed at the right moments and offered no stories of your own, because stories invited questions, and questions were how people got looked at, and being looked at—in Vellharrow, in the year the usurper took the crown—was a debt accruing interest.

She copied another line. The ink behaved. She copied another. It did not.

This time a single letter—the 4 in *198*, a fat, honest farmer's 4—lifted itself very slightly off the page. Not floating; she would have screamed if it floated. Just... standing. A letter deciding it had legs.

Mirabel's hand moved without her permission, casual as brushing away a fly, and pressed flat over the numeral until it lay down again like a scolded child. From the outside, it looked like nothing. A scribe resting her palm on her work, thinking. Scribes did that constantly. Scribes were famous for it.

Her heart was going like a hammer in a barrel, though.

"You're smudging," said Pell, without looking up from his ceiling study.

"I'm resting."

"Resting looks like smudging when you do it."

"Pell," said Mirabel, "if you can spell millstone, you can mind your own ledger."

He grinned around his ruined quill. He was sixteen and had the untroubled face of someone whose largest secret was that he'd once stolen a goose. Mirabel envied him with her whole heart and would have died before telling him so.

* * *

At midday the bell rang for bread and broth, and the scribes filed down to the refectory in their shuffling line, and Mirabel took her usual place at the end of the bench by the wall, where the shadows pooled and nobody's elbow competed with hers. The broth was barley, naturally—Vellharrow was a valley that had committed to one crop and refused, on principle, to be talked out of it—and the bread was yesterday's, which everyone agreed was an improvement over the day before's.

"So then my da says," Pell was saying, mouth full, "says to the tax man, says, *that goat's been dead since spring, you can't tithe a dead goat*—"

"And the tax man says," supplied Sera from across the table, a broad-shouldered woman who copied shipping manifests and laughed like a door slamming, "*dead or alive, the crown counts what it counts.*"

"That's the whole trouble with the crown," said old Brother Fenwick, who had been copying psalms so long his handwriting had achieved a kind of holiness. "It counts things. Counting is all it knows. You show a king a sunset and he'll ask how many colors it's got and whether any of them are taxable."

There was general laughter, and Mirabel laughed too, at the right moment, with the right weight, and felt the familiar small hollowness of it. She knew these people. She had known them for eleven years, since the debt brought her here at twelve years old with her mother's last letter and her father's unpaid loans folded into her pocket like stones. Eleven years of benches and bells and barley. And yet if any of them had been asked to say three true things about Mirabel Dunmore, they would have managed perhaps two: that she was quiet, and that her copies were clean.

That was the design. She checked on it nightly, the way other people checked locks.

Because here was the thing Mirabel did not say, did not write, did not even think in full sentences most days: the valley of Vellharrow was a place where magic could not follow. Everyone knew it the way everyone knows water runs downhill. Gifted folk passed through sometimes on the high road—you could tell them by the way they looked at the valley walls with an expression like homesickness—but whatever they carried never crossed the threshold. Candles that lit at a word went out at the border. Charms went dumb. Blessings dried up like puddles in summer sun. The valley forgot magic, and magic forgot the valley, and the ordinary folk who lived here counted themselves lucky and built their lives on barley and bookkeeping and never wondered too hard why the mountains on either side stood so tall, or who had raised them, or when.

Mirabel had arrived at twelve with a gift already budding in her hands—ink that listened, letters that leaned toward light, words that wanted to move the way living things move—and she had learned within her first month that Vellharrow starved such things quietly. Her gift didn't die here. It only grew hungry, and slow, and strange, like a plant grown in a cellar. Which meant she couldn't be rid of it, and couldn't feed it, and couldn't let anyone see it. Three impossibilities, balanced daily, for eleven years.

She mopped her bowl with the last of the bread and thought, as she did every day, *one more year, maybe two, and the debt is paid, and I can leave, and somewhere else I'll decide what I am.* She had been thinking *one more year, maybe two* for approximately nine years now. Hope, in Vellharrow, was a crop like any other: planted in poor soil, harvested thin.

* * *

The trouble began, as trouble often does, with a man carrying official paper.

He arrived in the afternoon, while the light had gone long and gold and the scriptorium had entered its drowsiest hour. Mirabel heard him before she saw him: boots with a hard heel, the kind that announced themselves on stone floors, and a voice pitched at the volume of a man accustomed to rooms falling silent for him.

"—the census supplement, yes, the crown registry of persons and obligations, His Majesty's edict of the spring, I'll need desk space and a candle and no interruptions—"

Mother Osla met him at the scriptorium door. Mirabel, head bent over her ledger, watched sideways, in the manner of the truly practiced invisible.

The collector was young for the office, which somehow made it worse. Young men in crown livery hadn't yet learned that authority sits badly on some shoulders; this one wore his like a borrowed coat, straightening it every few minutes. Two soldiers waited in the corridor behind him with the patient blankness of furniture.

"Mother Osla." He produced a document with a flourish. "Aldric, King of Vellmark and the Marchlands, requires a full accounting of persons resident upon temple lands. Names, ages, trades, debts, and—" here he consulted the paper, and read the next word carefully, as though it might bite him, "—*dispositions*."

"The temple keeps its own rolls," said Osla. Her voice was level as a tabletop. "As it has for two hundred years."

"The king has modernized the rolls."

"The king," said Osla, "has been king for eight months."

The collector smiled the smile of a man paid by the hour. "Then his rolls are new, madam, and need filling. Desk space. Candle. No interruptions."

Osla regarded him for a long moment, and Mirabel saw something pass over the old woman's face—there and gone, quick as a fish under ice—that Mirabel had never seen there before. If she'd had to name it, she'd have called it fear. But Mother Osla did not do fear, so it was probably indigestion.

"You may use the alcove by the west window," Osla said. "Brother Tamel will bring you the rolls."

* * *

They worked in parallel for two hours, the crown's counting and the temple's, and the scriptorium pretended very hard that nothing unusual was happening, which is the specialty of scriptoriums. The collector wrote swiftly, transferring names from the temple rolls into his own ledger with a clerk's economy. Occasionally he called out a question—*"This Pell Wicker, age sixteen, given to the temple by his family—debt or donation?"*—and Tamel answered in his sleepy way, and the pen scratched on.

Mirabel copied her barley and did not look up. She was so good at not looking up that she achieved a kind of perfect awareness of everything happening in the room without moving her eyes at all, a skill she privately considered her second gift, and her safer one.

The collector worked alphabetically. She knew this because she heard him reach the D's sometime after the fourth bell, and she felt the approach of her own name the way you feel weather coming.

"Dunmore." A pause. Paper turning. "Mirabel Dunmore. Age twenty-three. Bound to the temple for debt—whose debt?"

"Her father's," said Tamel. "Loans against the farm at Harrow's Edge. The farm failed. The girl came instead."

"Mm." Scratch, scratch. "Trades?"

"Scribe. Copywork, mostly. Ledgers."

"Skill?"

"Clean hand," said Tamel, with the faint pride of a man praising a well-behaved ox. "Never any trouble."

"Disposition?" The collector read the word off his form again, and this time Mirabel heard what lived underneath it, plain as a bone under skin. The usurper's edict had used the same word in its public proclamation, posted at the valley's mouth in spring: all persons of *irregular disposition* to be registered for the crown's review. Nobody in Vellharrow had needed the word explained. Irregular disposition was what they'd called the gifted, in the old stories, before everyone agreed the stories were only stories.

"Ordinary," said Tamel.

"Everyone is ordinary," said the collector. "Until they're not."

Scratch, scratch. Mirabel copied *barley, east terraces, 141 sacks* with a hand that did not shake, because she had trained it not to, because a shaking hand was a loud hand, and loud hands got looked at.

But the ink on her page had gone very still. All of it. Every letter lying flat as dead things, as though the ink itself understood what was being written two rows away, and was playing dead alongside her.

The collector rose near dusk. She heard him thank Osla in his borrowed-coat voice, heard the hard heels cross the floor, and then—she would swear this afterward, to herself, in the dark, though she never said it aloud—he stopped. Beside her desk. For the length of one breath, two.

She kept her eyes on the barley. *East terraces, 141 sacks.* Her pen did not waver. Her shoulders were relaxed. Her face arranged itself into the mild, vacant patience of a woman thinking about absolutely nothing, which is the hardest expression in the world to hold.

"Your copywork," the collector said.

"Sir?"

"It leans." He tapped the edge of her page once, with one finger. "To the left. Most scribes drift right, if anything. Curious."

"Bad habit, sir," said Mirabel. "I fight it daily."

Something hung in the air then—a moment with weight, like the pause before a scale tips. Then he made a small note in his own ledger—she heard the scratch of it, one line, brief—and walked on, and the hard heels receded, and the door closed, and the scriptorium exhaled all together like a congregation after a sermon.

Mirabel looked down at her page. Every letter lay flat. Except, at the bottom margin, where her hand had rested while the collector stood beside her—one word, written without her knowledge, in her own handwriting, small as a secret:

*Run.*

She covered it with her palm and sat very still, listening to the evening bell begin to ring, and thought about the fish-under-ice expression on Mother Osla's face, and the single scratch of the collector's pen, and the fact that her name was now traveling to the capital inside a crown ledger, in ink that did not listen to anyone.

Invisible, she had always believed, was safe. Invisible was a craft, practiced daily.

But sitting there in the fading light with one forbidden word hidden under her hand, Mirabel Dunmore began to understand, with the slow cold certainty of water finding a crack, that invisibility had a price of its own. That the valley's forgetting was not a wall but a debt, accruing. That somewhere in the capital a ledger now held her name beside the word *disposition*, and that ledgers, unlike valleys, never forgot anything at all.

She rubbed the word out with her thumb until the paper was smooth, and went down to supper, and laughed at the right moments, and told no stories.

Three days later, a bleeding stranger would collapse at the temple gate with a stone in his arms that would make every careful year of her life irrelevant.

But that evening, she only knew that the ink had whispered *run*—and that, for the first time in eleven years, she was afraid to find out whether it was wrong.
