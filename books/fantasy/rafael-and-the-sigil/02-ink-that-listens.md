## 2. Ink That Listens

The trouble with ink, Mirabel had decided over eleven years of copying, was that it listened.

Good ink—the temple's own oak-gall brew, thick and black as sin—ought to sit where a pen put it. Obedient. Dead. But hers never quite behaved. She would write *three bushels barley* and the word *barley* would lean toward her like a man eavesdropping at a door. She would set down a date and the numerals would seem to breathe, faintly, the way a sleeping child's chest rises. Nothing dramatic. Nothing anyone could point to and say *there, that's sorcery*. Just a small wrongness in the way the letters settled, as if they were less written than *planted*.

So Mirabel had taught herself tricks. She wrote slowly when she was tired. She shook out her wrist before difficult pages. And most of all, she kept her mind empty while her hand worked—no wishing, no wanting, no stray thought of *I wish this ledger were finished* or *I wish I were anywhere but here*, because wishes were what the ink heard best.

It was a strange kind of discipline, praying to nothing. But it had kept her invisible for eleven years in a valley that forgot things on purpose, and invisibility, she had learned, was its own full-time occupation.

"Mirabel." Mother Osla's voice came from the doorway of the scriptorium like a stone dropped into still water. "You've been on the Harrow tithes since dawn. Come away from them."

"I'm nearly through the—"

"Come away from them."

There was no arguing with that voice. There never had been. Mother Osla was the head scribe of Vellharrow Temple, a woman built like a granary door and about as easy to move, with iron-grey hair scraped back so tightly it seemed to be holding her opinions in. She had run the scriptorium since before Mirabel could hold a pen, and she ran it with two commandments: accuracy above beauty, and silence above all.

Mirabel blotted the page, capped her inkwell, and followed her down the cloister walk, where morning light fell in pale bars through the arches. Outside, Vellharrow went about its forgetting: carters hauling turnips, children chasing a dog with someone's supper in its mouth, the mill wheel turning its slow brown circle. An ordinary valley. The most ordinary valley in the world, if you didn't look too closely at the fact that no one here ever dreamed, and no one ever wondered why.

"You rushed the last column," Osla said.

"The ink was drying unevenly."

"Ink dries evenly if you let it." They walked three more paces. Then, without turning her head, without changing her tone at all, Osla said: "Your letters moved."

Mirabel's stomach performed a slow, deliberate somersault. "Moved."

"Slanted. Toward your left hand. On the word *tallow*. Page forty-one of the tithe ledger." Osla stopped walking and looked out at the yard, at the dog finally being cornered by its victims' owners. "I have been a scribe for fifty-one years, girl. I have seen every way a hand can fail. Tremor. Cramp. Drink. Grief. None of them slant only one word, only once, only when the writer is frightened."

The morning air felt suddenly very thin. Eleven years of careful, patient nothing, and it had come apart over the word *tallow*.

"Mother, I—"

"Hold your tongue until I'm finished, because I will only say this once." Osla's voice stayed level, quiet, the voice she used for dictating inventories. "I am not going to ask you what you are. This valley has laws about questions, and I keep them. What I am going to do is give you work that keeps you alone, where no tax collector with a crown registry can stand behind your shoulder and watch your pen."

Mirabel's mouth had gone dry enough to sand. "The registry," she managed. "You know about—"

"I know everything that comes through my scriptorium, including the copies made for the crown's men. I also know that Collector Vess lingered over your name Tuesday last like a man lingering over a coin he means to bite." Osla resumed walking, briskly, as though they were discussing flour deliveries. "So. The undercroft archive is due for culling. Records older than sixty years are to be burned, per the abbot's instruction, which I have encouraged him to have. You will do the sorting. Alone. You will burn what's marked, and you will touch nothing else, and you will say nothing to anyone, and we will both pretend this conversation was about tallow."

"And if it happens again?" Mirabel asked. "If the ink listens again?"

Osla paused at the head of the stairs that led down into the undercroft. For just a moment—half a heartbeat—something moved behind her face that was not sternness at all. It looked almost like grief, or perhaps like recognition, and it was gone before Mirabel could be sure.

"Then write faster," Osla said, "and wish smaller."

* * *

The undercroft smelled of dust, mouse, and cold stone—the smell, Mirabel thought, of other people's forgotten time. Rows of cedar chests lined the vaulted room, each labeled in Osla's fierce upright hand: *Baptisms 1189–1201. Land Disputes. Grain Pledges. Correspondence, Secular.* A brazier waited in the corner, already laid with kindling, beside a stack of empty sacking for carrying the condemned upstairs to the yard fire.

Sixty years or older. That was the sentence. Anything past that line was ash.

It should have been tedious. It was meant to be tedious—that was the mercy of it, Mirabel understood now. Hours alone with dead paper, no witnesses, no registry men, no ink to embarrass her. She could let her mind wander here. She could even, carefully, in the privacy of the dark, stop wishing small.

She started with the grain pledges, because starting with anything else felt like cowardice. She read names of farmers four generations gone, quarrels over field boundaries, a widow's plea for a second chance on a flooded acre. Ordinary lives, pressed flat between boards. She carried armfuls up to the yard fire and watched the corners curl, and felt the peculiar guilt of the destroyer—these had been someone's whole year, once, someone's desperate autumn—and felt also the strange lightness, because burned paper asked nothing of anyone.

By late afternoon she'd reached the chest marked *Correspondence, Secular, 1140–1160*, and that was where she found the page.

It wasn't correspondence. It had been tucked inside a bundle of merchant letters, folded once, carelessly, the way you'd file something you wanted lost rather than found. The paper itself was wrong for the era—heavier, cream-colored, with a faint sheen like the inside of a shell. And across it, drawn in ink that had not faded so much as *settled*, deep and dark as a well, was a sigil.

A circle. Within it, seven lines radiating from a center point, each line broken midway by a small interlocking knot. Around the rim, characters in no alphabet Mirabel knew—and yet her eyes moved along them with the horrible ease of reading her own name.

She sat down on the cold floor without meaning to.

Because she did know it. Not from any book; the temple library held nothing like it, she'd catalogued every shelf by fifteen. Not from any teacher. She knew it the way you know the shape of your own front door in the dark—by some faculty deeper than learning, some memory with the memory part removed. Her hand rose without her permission, fingers hovering above the page, tracing the circle's curve an inch above the paper, and beneath her hovering fingertips the ink *moved*. Not leaning this time. Not breathing. The seven radiating lines turned, slowly, gently, like the spokes of a wheel, like a lock beginning to consider opening.

Mirabel snatched her hand back and pressed it flat against her chest, over her hammering heart.

The lines stilled.

She knelt there a long while in the dust and the grey light from the ventilation slit, listening to her own breath echo off the vaulting. Somewhere overhead, the temple bell rang the hour, muffled and far away, and she counted the strokes automatically—six—and thought: *I have been down here six hours and I have touched nothing else, and I am going to put this page back exactly where I found it.*

That was the sensible thing. That was the invisible thing.

Her hands did not obey. They unfolded the page instead, smoothing it against her knee, and she read the rim-characters again, and this time she noticed what she'd missed: at the bottom of the sigil, tiny, cramped into the border as if the writer had run out of courage, was a line of ordinary script. Common tongue. Old-fashioned spelling.

*For the reader who comes after — forgive us the cost. M.D.*

*M.D.*

Mirabel Dunmore. M.D.

Her own initials. In a hand sixty years old, in a document hidden in a chest of merchant letters, addressed—to whom? To her? Impossible. She had been born twenty-three years ago in the house by the millrace; her mother had died of fever when she was five; her father had drowned the spring after, leaving debts that bound her to this temple until they were paid. She knew these facts the way she knew the catechism, solid and recitable.

And yet she could not remember her mother's face. Not fever, not the funeral, not the house by the millrace—not one detail of the first five years of her life, which everyone agreed she ought to remember, and which she had learned, long ago, to stop mentioning. When she was small she had told people she'd been too young. When she was older she had stopped telling people anything.

She looked at the sigil, and the sigil's seven spokes looked back, and somewhere below her ribs, in a place that had no words, something ached with a longing so specific and so groundless that tears came before she understood she was crying. It was homesickness, she realized. Homesickness for a home she couldn't name. Grief for a person whose face she'd never been shown. It was the feeling she got sometimes at dusk, standing at the valley's edge, looking at the mountains that ringed Vellharrow like a wall—as if something on the other side of them knew her name and was waiting for her to say it back.

*Forgive us the cost,* the old hand had written. *Cost of what?*

The brazier had gone cold. The light in the ventilation slit had gone from grey to blue to nothing. Mirabel sat on the floor of the undercroft with a forbidden page in her lap and made, slowly, the first dishonest decision of her carefully honest life.

She did not burn it.

She did not return it to the chest, either. Instead she folded it small—smaller than it had been filed, small enough to vanish—and slipped it inside her sleeve, against the skin of her forearm, where it lay warm as a living thing. Then she climbed the stairs, banked the yard fire's embers, and walked back through the cloister with her face arranged into the blankness of a tired copyist, past the refectory smells of barley stew, past the novices chattering about a peddler's rumor from over the pass.

"—soldiers at Thornford, my cousin swears it—"

"—crown's men asking after papers, not taxes this time—"

"—some edict from the new king, about—"

*hush*, said an older novice, sharply, glancing around. "Not in the walkway."

Mirabel kept walking. Against her arm, through her sleeve, the folded page pressed its warmth into her pulse, and the seven spokes of the sigil turned in her mind's eye, slow as a millwheel, patient as a lock that had waited sixty years and could wait a little longer.

In the morning, she told herself, she would put it back. In the morning she would be sensible.

She was halfway to her cell when Mother Osla stepped out of the shadow of the scriptorium arch, lantern in hand, and looked—at Mirabel's sleeve, then at Mirabel's face—with an expression that said she had counted the pages in that chest herself, years ago, and had been waiting a very long time for one of them to go missing.

"Mother," Mirabel began, "I can explain—"

"No," said Osla quietly. "But tomorrow, someone will arrive who can."
