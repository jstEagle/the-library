## 4. What the Crown Hunts

Rafael had been shot at, stabbed, thrown from a horse twice, and once nearly married in the city of Vaskarren, all of which he'd survived with more or less the same amount of dignity. But none of it had prepared him for waking up under a temple roof in a valley where magic could not follow, being stared at by a scribe who looked like she wanted to be anywhere else in the world.

She was sitting on a stool beside his cot, arms crossed, a ledger balanced on her knees as if she intended to take notes on his dying. Her name was Mirabel — the head scribe had said so gruffly while changing his bandages, in the tone of a woman who resented every syllable of hospitality.

"You're awake," Mirabel said. It wasn't a question so much as an accusation.

"Barely," Rafael croaked. His side throbbed where the crossbow bolt had kissed him on the road down from the pass. "Is that disappointment I hear?"

"You bled on the threshold stone. Mother Osla says it will need scrubbing."

"Ah." He considered this. "Well. In my defense, I was trying very hard to bleed somewhere else at the time."

Her mouth did something complicated — the ghost of a twitch that she caught and strangled before it became a smile. She looked away, back to her ledger, and dipped her pen. "The soldiers," she said. "Tell me about them. Why they're coming. And don't lie to me, courier. I copy tax records. I know what lying looks like written down."

Rafael let his head sink back against the cot's thin pillow and stared up at the rafters, where swallows' nests crowded into the eaves like tenants who hadn't paid rent in years. Outside, Vellharrow went about its morning: cart wheels in the lane, a smith's hammer, someone singing badly to a goat. A valley that smelled of barley and river water and nothing else. No hum of ley beneath the stones. No whisper at the edge of hearing. Just silence — the deep, engineered silence of a place where magic had been walled out so long ago that most folk here probably thought silence was simply what the world sounded like.

He envied them. Then he remembered why he'd come, and stopped envying anyone at all.

* * *

"The man who sits the throne now isn't really a king," Rafael said. "Everyone calls him Aldric, King Aldric, first of his name, long may he etcetera. But Aldric was never crowned properly. He took the capital six years ago with three legions and a very good speechwriter, and he's been holding it ever since by making everyone too frightened to remember there's another way to choose a king."

Mirabel's pen scratched. "You're not from here. You talk like the capital."

"I *am* from the capital. Or I was." He flexed his fingers over the blanket, watching the way the bandage pulled across his ribs. "I grew up four streets from the palace gates. My father sold lamps. Good ones — you could read by them without going blind, which in Vaskarren counted as craftsmanship bordering on sorcery."

"And then?"

"And then the edicts started." He kept his voice level, the way you keep your hand level when you're pulling a splinter out of someone you love. "First it was small things. Gifted folk had to register their gifts. Then register their names. Then report their neighbors. Then — " He paused. "Then came the Taking."

The pen stopped scratching.

"They call it the Gathering, officially," Rafael went on. "Aldric's edict, proclaimed last autumn from the palace steps, read aloud in every market square in the crownlands. *All persons possessing the old gift shall present themselves to crown officers for assessment and safekeeping.* Safekeeping." He laughed, and it came out wrong, jagged at the edges. "They're taken to the capital in wagons with barred windows. Families get a receipt. An actual paper receipt, stamped with the royal seal, promising return upon the kingdom's need. Nobody has come back yet."

Mirabel had gone very still. Not the stillness of shock, he thought — or not only that. There was something else underneath it, some private recognition, like a woman reading a list of names and finding her own at the bottom.

"That's why you ran," she said finally. "You're gifted."

"I'm nothing half so interesting." He turned his head to look at her, and the movement made his wound flare white-hot, so he finished the gesture anyway out of spite. "I can't light a candle with a word. I can't mend a bone or charm a lock. What I am is fast, stubborn, and good at carrying things. That's the whole of my gift, and my father would tell you the carrying part is genetic."

"Then why do they hunt *you*?"

"Because of what I'm carrying." He pushed himself upright, gasping, and swung his legs off the cot. Mirabel was on her feet instantly, hands hovering, torn between helping him and maintaining her air of professional indifference. He won the contest by ignoring both. "Where's my pack?"

"Confiscated."

"Where's my confiscated pack?"

Something flickered across her face — guilt, quickly buried. "The reliquary closet. Osla didn't want it loose in the infirmary." She hesitated, then fetched it herself: a battered leather satchel, scorched along one flap, heavier than it looked. She set it on the cot between them like a cat bringing its owner a dead bird and immediately regretting it.

Rafael opened the flap.

Inside, wrapped in oilcloth and wool and one of his own shirts that would never be white again, lay the stone.

It was the size of a loaf of bread, dark as wet slate, and etched across its face with lines that seemed almost to move when you weren't looking directly at them — spirals within spirals, a geometry that hurt to follow, anchored at the center by a single mark like a closed eye. Even here, even in this valley where magic couldn't reach, the thing had weight beyond its weight. He'd carried it for two hundred miles and had grown convinced it was slowly learning to carry *him*.

Beside him, Mirabel made a sound.

It wasn't a gasp. It was quieter than that — a small, involuntary breath, the kind a person makes when they open a door expecting a familiar room and find it changed. When he glanced at her, she was staring at the sigil on the stone's face with an expression he couldn't name, her lips slightly parted, her hand risen halfway toward it and frozen there.

"You've seen it before," he said softly.

"No." Too quick. Too certain. She withdrew her hand and clasped both of them in her lap like a schoolgirl reciting catechism. "No. I haven't."

"Mirabel."

"You don't get to use my name like you've earned it. You've been unconscious since yesterday."

"Fair." He wrapped the stone again, carefully, and felt her attention track every fold of cloth. "Then I'll just tell you what it is, and you can decide whether you've seen it or not. Deal?"

She gave a single nod, which from her, he was beginning to understand, constituted lavish agreement.

* * *

"It's called the sigil-stone," he said. "There were nine of them once. Fragments of something older — the covenant, the old records call it. The working that walled this valley off from magic, among other things. Walled whole realms apart. The founders broke the covenant into pieces and scattered them so no one person could ever hold enough of it to unmake the seal — or remake it. Nine stones, nine keepers' lines, sworn to guard them and say nothing. For six hundred years, that held."

"And now?"

"And now Aldric found one." Rafael's jaw tightened. "Two years ago. Under a granary in the town of Harrowmere, of all the stupid places — they tore down half the market district looking for rebels and cracked open a vault nobody knew was there. One of the keeper families had hidden their fragment under the floorboards of a *granary*. Six hundred years of sacred vigilance, defeated by a grain merchant's renovation."

Despite everything, Mirabel's mouth twitched. "What happened to the keepers?"

"Gone. Long dead, mostly. The last of the line was an old woman who'd forgotten what her family even guarded. Aldric's scholars spent a year trying to read the stone. They brought in linguists, artificers, a man who claimed to have eaten the heart of a wyrm and gained its wisdom — he mostly gained gout." Rafael rubbed his eyes. "Nothing worked. The sigil won't answer to force. It needs a reader. Someone born able to see what's written there. And Aldric decided that if he couldn't read the covenant, he'd build something new instead — a covenant of his own, forged however he pleased, with whatever fuel he needed. Do you understand? The Taking isn't cruelty for its own sake. It's a harvest. Every gifted person his officers gather is another thread for the loom. Another memory. Another life he can feed into a working."

The silence after that was very long. Somewhere outside, the bad singer was still serenading the goat, and Rafael found himself absurdly grateful for it — proof that ordinary mornings still existed somewhere.

"So I stole it," he said simply. "Three weeks ago. From the Tower of Records, where Aldric keeps his curiosities. I'm told it's the first theft from the tower in its history, which I intend to have engraved on my tombstone. *He was terrible at subtlety but excellent at stairs.*"

"Why you?" Mirabel asked. "If you have no gift, how did you get past wards built to stop the gifted?"

"Because the wards are keyed to magic, and I'm the least magical man alive. I got in through the laundry chute." He raised a hand before she could speak. "Before you ask — yes, it was exactly as dignified as it sounds. There was a moment involving a laundress and a basket of the king's personal linens that I will take to my grave."

And there it was again — the strangled smile, fighting free for half a heartbeat before she mastered it. Two smiles in one conversation. By his count, that made him the funniest man in Vellharrow, possibly the only funny man in Vellharrow, which he supposed was a kind of distinction.

But the humor drained out of him as quickly as it came, because the next part wouldn't wait.

"There's a problem," he said. "The reason I came north through the passes instead of the low roads. Aldric's hounds — real hounds, and worse, the men who handle them. Scent-trackers bound to a scrap of the stolen stone. They can smell the fragments. Smell *me*, now, after carrying it this long. Three days behind me, maybe less. I led them over the Serpent's Teeth to lose them, and I thought — " He stopped. Looked down at his hands. "I thought no one would think to search a null valley. Magic doesn't work here. The hounds' binding shouldn't hold inside the ward-line. I thought I'd be invisible."

Mirabel waited.

"But that's the thing about a null valley, isn't it," Rafael said quietly. "Nobody here can defend themselves either. If the hunt follows me to the edge of Vellharrow — and it will, because I've left a trail of blooded bandages from here to the pass like the world's worst festival decorations — then everyone in this valley is standing in front of soldiers with no gift to raise against them. No shield. No warning. Nothing. Your temple, your ledgers, your goats and your terrible singing — all of it safe right up until the moment I limped through your gate."

He heard the words leave him and hated each one individually.

"I didn't bring sanctuary here," he said. "I brought a war. I brought the hunt to the one place in the world where nobody can fight back."

* * *

Mirabel didn't comfort him. That was the first thing he noticed, and later he would understand it was the kindest thing she could have done. She didn't pat his hand or murmur that it wasn't his fault. She sat with her ledger on her knees and looked at him the way she presumably looked at a column of numbers that refused to add — thoroughly, unsparingly, until she understood it.

"The soldiers," she said at last. "When will they reach the valley's edge?"

"A day. Maybe two. The hounds will slow at the ward-line — the binding frays there — but their handlers aren't fools. They'll know what a null valley means. They'll know what's hiding in one." He managed a lopsided shrug that cost him dearly. "You should burn the bandages. Scrub the threshold. Put me in a boat and send me downriver, and with any luck they follow the current instead of the scent."

"We don't have boats."

"You have a *goat*. I've become attached to the goat. Improvise."

"You are," Mirabel said, rising and tucking her ledger under her arm, "an idiot."

"Also true, but I fail to see how it's relevant—"

"If they're coming regardless," she said, already moving toward the door, "then it doesn't matter whose fault it is. Fault is a luxury. I copy debt records, courier. I know exactly what luxuries cost." She paused at the door, and for a moment her shoulders were stiff as a drawn bowstring, and he saw — or thought he saw — a girl bracing against a tide only she could feel. "Rest. Bleed somewhere with a bucket nearby. And keep that stone wrapped."

"Mirabel."

She stopped but didn't turn.

"When they come," Rafael said, "and they ask if anyone here has seen a stranger — you haven't. Whatever they offer, whatever they threaten. You haven't seen me, and you haven't seen the stone, and you especially haven't seen the way you looked at that sigil like you were reading your own handwriting."

Now she turned. Her face had gone pale as fresh vellum, and her voice, when it came, was barely above the scratch of a pen.

"I told you," she said. "I've never seen it before in my life."

She left. The door clicked shut behind her with the tidy finality of a ledger closing.

Rafael lay back against the pillow and listened to the valley sounds drift through the window — hammer, cart, song, goat — and pressed his palm flat against the satchel at his side, feeling the stone's slow, cold pulse like a second heartbeat that wasn't quite his own.

*I'm sorry,* he told it silently, the way he'd been telling it for three weeks, for two hundred miles. *I'm sorry I brought you here. I'm sorry I brought them.*

Far off, past the barley fields and the river bend, at the tree line where the mountains began, something howled.

Not a wolf. Wolves sang; this was a question, asked over and over, patient as arithmetic.

Rafael closed his eyes and began counting days.
