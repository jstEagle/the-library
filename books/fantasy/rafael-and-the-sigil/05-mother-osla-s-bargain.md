## 5. Mother Osla's Bargain

The debt ledger in Vellharrow temple was written in three hands: the abbot's, the tax collector's, and mine. I knew this because I had copied it eleven times over nine years, once for each audit, and each time the numbers grew a little fatter, like a pig being fattened for a market that never came.

Four hundred and twelve crowns, six shillings, and one apology owed to the Crown of Aldric's line. The apology was my father's contribution. He'd died owing it, and debts of the tongue passed down like debts of coin. Somewhere in the capital there was presumably an official whose whole job was to wait for that apology, growing old and bitter about it.

I was copying the ledger for the twelfth time when Mother Osla came into the scriptorium and stood at my shoulder without saying anything, which was how she said most things.

She smelled of lamp oil and dried feverfew, as always. Her shadow fell across my page, and I noticed—too late—that my pen had gone strange again. The ink on the line I'd just finished was curling upward off the vellum in tiny black filaments, like ferns unfurling, spelling out a word I hadn't written: *run*.

I slammed my palm flat over it.

"Interesting choice of marginalia," Osla said.

"It's a—a smudge."

"Mm." She pulled the stool from the next desk and sat down with the groan of a woman who had been sitting down painfully for forty years and intended to complain about every one of them. "Mirabel. How long have you been smudging?"

My heart performed a maneuver I can only describe as falling downstairs in reverse. "Mother, I don't—"

"I have watched you copy ledgers for nine years," she said. "You are the fastest scribe I have ever trained, you never make an error, and your ink occasionally behaves like something alive. The first time was in your second month, when you were so tired you forgot to be careful. I told myself it was lamplight. The second time I told myself it was my eyes. By the fifth time I ran out of things to tell myself." She folded her hands. "I am old, child, not blind. Though I admit blindness would have been more convenient for us both."

The word *us both* landed strangely. I looked at her—at the deep seams around her mouth, the ink stains permanent as birthmarks on her fingers—and felt the floor tilt under a certainty I'd carried since childhood: that nobody knew, that nobody could know, that knowing me meant danger and therefore nobody ever would.

"You've known," I said. "All this time."

"Since you were nineteen." She said it the way she announced feast days. Flatly, and with an air of having survived worse. "And in nine years, how many times have I reported you? How many times has the collector's registry marked you gifted?"

"Never."

"Never," she agreed. "Do you know what that has cost me? Every audit, I stand before those men and swear the temple holds no unregistered talent, and every year the lie gets heavier. It sits in my chest now like a stone I swallowed. And I would swallow it another nine years gladly—" her voice did something complicated, "—if the world had left us alone to keep lying."

Outside the scriptorium window, the valley lay green and ordinary. Smoke rose from the kilns. Someone was arguing with a mule by the millpond, at length and without success. Vellharrow, doing what Vellharrow did: forgetting, gently, everything that didn't concern bread and water and the price of wool.

But Rafael was asleep in the infirmary with a soldier's arrow-scar across his ribs, and somewhere beyond the pass, dogs were being fed and pointed south.

"The courier," Osla said. "Show me the stone."

* * *

We went down through the cloister, past the herb garden where Brother Tam was pretending very hard not to eavesdrop, into the infirmary's small side room where we'd put Rafael. He was awake, propped against pillows, gray-faced but watching the door like a man who expected it to be kicked in. When he saw Osla his whole body tensed; then he registered the habit, the age, the general air of a woman who had buried better men than any soldier, and some of the tension went out of him.

"Mother."

"You brought a crown-registry artifact into my temple," Osla said, without preamble, "along with a hunt, hounds, and whatever portion of the king's army is currently admiring our valley from the ridge. Sit up. Let me see the stone."

He fumbled it from beneath the blanket—the tablet of dark stone, no bigger than a prayer book, etched with that spiral-and-bar sigil that had been living behind my eyes since the moment I first touched it. Up close, in good light, I could see the lines weren't carved at all. They were grown, the way frost grows on glass, or veins grow under skin.

Osla looked at it for a long time. Then she looked at me, and something in her face made my stomach drop.

"You recognize it," she said. Not a question.

"It recognizes *me*," I said, before I could stop myself. "That sounds mad. I know it sounds mad."

"It doesn't sound mad." She lowered herself onto the room's one chair, joints cracking like green kindling. "It sounds like Vellharrow. Nothing in this valley works the way it should. That is rather the point of it." She nodded at the stone. "Hold it, child. I want to see what happens."

"Mother—"

"Hold it. If the king's men are coming regardless, I would like to spend my remaining ignorance wisely."

So I took it. And the sigil woke—not flaring, nothing so dramatic, but warming under my thumb like a held bird, and the lines shifting, rearranging themselves into an order that almost meant something, almost said something, right at the edge of understanding, like a voice calling from another room.

Rafael made a small sound. "It's never done that for me. Three years I carried it. It's been dead weight the whole way."

"Because it wasn't waiting for you." Osla's voice had gone quiet in a new way. "It was waiting for her." She rubbed her eyes with thumb and forefinger, and for a moment she looked every one of her sixty-some years, plus interest. "Nine years I kept you safe by keeping you small. Copying ledgers. Burning records. Invisible work for an invisible girl. And now the world has sent a bleeding courier to my gate to inform me that invisibility is no longer on offer." She stood, wincing. "Come to my office, both of you. There is a document you need to see before I lose my nerve."

* * *

Her office smelled of the same lamp oil and feverfew as the rest of her, plus dust, plus the particular mustiness of paper that has not been disturbed in years. She climbed the ladder to the high shelf where the oldest records lived—the ones even I wasn't allowed to touch—and after some rummaging and one muttered curse, brought down a slim folio tied with ribbon gone brittle as autumn leaves.

She set it on the desk between us and did not untie it.

"Before I show you this," she said, "you should understand what I am, Mirabel. I am not brave. I have never been brave. I am a coward who has gotten very good at arithmetic. For nine years I have done sums: the cost of hiding you against the cost of losing you. And every year the sums said hide. Until three days ago, when a boy fell through my gate with that stone in his arms, and the sums changed." She pushed the folio toward me. "Open it."

Inside was a single sheet of old vellum, covered in a hand I knew intimately, because it was mine—or nearly mine. Younger. Shakier. A child's careful letters, but the loops and slants were unmistakably my own.

It was a debt contract. Dated the spring I arrived at the temple, aged thirteen. Four hundred crowns, six shillings, and one apology—the exact sum, to the farthing, that I had spent nine years copying.

Except the signature at the bottom was not my father's.

It read: *M. Dunmore.*

"That's impossible," Rafael said quietly. "She was a child."

"She was," Osla said, "and she signed it anyway, and neither of you remembers it happening, and I have spent nine years telling myself I dreamed the whole evening." She sat down heavily. "A woman came with you, the night you were left at our gate. Tall, gray-eyed, exhausted past sense. She carried you herself—wouldn't let the carters touch you. She paid the debt in full, in crown coin, on the condition that the contract be drawn in the child's name, signed by the child, and sealed." Her finger hovered over the bottom edge of the vellum, where a mark pressed into the wax caught the lamplight.

A spiral, barred through. The sigil.

"She said the girl would try to leave," Osla went on, and her voice had gone rough. "That she would always want to leave, and always find a reason not to, and that the debt was the kindest chain I could give her. She said—" Osla stopped. Started again. "She said, *when the stone comes for her, let her go without argument, because I bought her nine quiet years and I will not have them wasted on a siege.*"

The room was very still. Somewhere below, Brother Tam had given up on the mule or the mule had given up on him.

"My mother," I said. My voice sounded far away. "That was my mother."

"Was it?" Osla asked gently. "What do you remember of her?"

And I reached, the way I had reached all my life, for the shape of a woman tall and gray-eyed and tired—and found, as always, fog. A smell of road dust. The hem of a cloak. Nothing with a face. Nothing with a name. Thirteen years of childhood with a hole in it exactly the size of a person.

"No," I admitted. "I don't remember anything. I never have. I thought—I assumed she died on the road."

"Perhaps she did." Osla closed the folio. "Or perhaps she gave something else instead of coin. I have wondered, these nine years, why a woman paying four hundred crowns would insist the debt be recorded in a child's name. Debts are chains, Mirabel. But they are also anchors. Something in that woman wanted you bound to this valley—to the one place in the world her kind of power cannot follow." She met my eyes. "She wanted you safe. And safe, for people like us, has always meant small."

Rafael shifted against his pillows. "Then the edict changes the sums," he said. "Doesn't it. Hiding isn't possible anymore. Aldric's registry already has her name—your collector wrote it down two days ago. When the hunt reaches the valley, they'll come for her whether she runs or not."

"They will," Osla said. "Which brings me to the unpleasant part." She opened a drawer, took out a key, unlocked the iron-bound strongbox beneath her desk, and drew out a leather satchel that clinked.

She set it on the desk and pushed it toward me.

"Four hundred and twelve crowns, six shillings, and one apology," she said. "Paid in full. Your debt to the temple and the Crown is discharged as of tonight. You are free, Mirabel Dunmore. Free to go anywhere in the world you like."

I stared at the satchel. Nine years of my life, weighed out in coin. I should have felt lighter. Instead I felt like a rope that had just been cut on the wrong side of the knot.

"In exchange," Osla continued, and here her voice finally cracked, just slightly, like ice on a pond in early spring, "you leave tonight. With him. Through the shepherd's tunnel before the dogs reach the pass. And you never come back. Never write. Never send word. As far as Vellharrow is concerned, the scribe Mirabel died of a fever and was buried in the yard with the other paupers, and I said the words over her myself." She wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand, briskly, angrily, as though tears were a clerical error. "That is my bargain. Freedom, purchased with exile. I'm told most bargains are worse."

"Mother Osla." My throat hurt. "This is my home. You're my—you're the closest thing to family I—"

"Don't." She held up a hand. "If you say it, I will lose what's left of my nerve, and I have a temple full of children to think about, and a valley that cannot fight, and nine years of lies I intend to keep telling on your behalf. So you will be cruel, child. You will take the money and break an old woman's heart efficiently, and someday, if the world turns kind, someone will explain to me why that was mercy." She straightened, and the steel came back into her spine like a blade going back in its sheath. "Well? The tunnel won't stay empty forever."

I looked at Rafael. He was watching me with an expression I couldn't read—guilt and hope tangled together, a man who knew he was the reason for someone else's heartbreak and couldn't fix it and hadn't stopped wishing he could.

"I don't even know what the stone wants from me," I said. "I don't know what reading it costs. I don't know who my mother was, or what she traded, or why I can't remember my own childhood. Everything I know fits in a ledger, and you're asking me to close the book."

"Yes," Osla said. "I am. Because the alternative is that soldiers open it for you, and they use sharper pens."

* * *

Packing took ten minutes, because nine years of invisibility amounts to very little: two spare shifts, a brush, a knife for sharpening pens, the little wooden bird Brother Tam had whittled me when I was fifteen and homesick for a home I couldn't remember. I stood in my cell a last time, looking at the walls I'd known longer than any face, and felt them already becoming a story someone else would tell.

At the tunnel mouth behind the laundry, Osla was waiting with a lantern and a bundle of provisions wrapped in oilcloth, and Brother Tam, who had apparently stopped pretending not to eavesdrop sometime around the mule incident and now simply stood there crying openly into his sleeves.

"Oh, stop it," Osla said. "You'll wake the hounds."

"She's the best scribe I ever—" Tam began.

"She's standing right here, you great soft fool, and she can hear you." But her voice was thick too, and when she handed me the bundle her hands stayed on it a moment longer than necessary, holding mine through the cloth. "Mirabel. Listen to me. Whatever the stone asks of you—and it will ask; stones like that always ask—remember that you were worth four hundred crowns and one apology, and I never once regretted the lie I told to keep you. Not one day. Do you hear me? Not one."

"I hear you," I whispered.

"And—" she hesitated, glanced at Rafael, and lowered her voice. "Your mother said one thing more, that night. I've kept it nine years like a splinter. She said: *tell her the debt was never owed to the Crown.*" Osla's gray eyes searched my face. "I never understood it. Perhaps you will."

The tunnel breathed cold air at us, smelling of stone and old water. Rafael went first, the sigil-stone wrapped against his chest. I paused at the threshold, one foot in the dark, and looked back at the lamplight, at Osla's face, at the valley that had held me the way a wall holds ivy—firmly, and without ever letting it inside.

"Goodbye, Mother," I said.

"Go," she said, which in her language meant a great many other things besides.

Then the dark took us, and behind us I heard the small sound of a door closing on nine years—and ahead, faint but unmistakable, rising up through the mountain's bones, the belling of hounds.

They had crossed the pass early. We had hours, perhaps less.

Rafael's hand found mine in the blackness, and together we ran.
