## 6. Three Days Ahead of the Dogs

### Mirabel

The mountains did not care that Mirabel Dunmore had never climbed anything steeper than the temple stairs.

By midmorning of the first day her calves were screaming, her palms were raw from gripping root and stone, and Rafael—bleeding courier, wanted man, general nuisance—kept glancing back at her with that particular expression people wore when they were deciding whether to say something kind or something useful. He always chose useful.

"You're walking like your feet are arguing with each other," he said.

"My feet have reached a consensus," she panted, "that this was a terrible idea."

"Your feet weren't consulted. I asked you."

"You dragged me."

"I *urged* you with great urgency." He offered his hand to haul her up a shelf of granite. She ignored it and pulled herself up on her own, mostly out of spite, which she had discovered in the last twelve hours was an excellent fuel source. "There's a stream over the ridge. We can break trail there. Hounds hate water."

"Hounds hate water. Wonderful. Do the soldiers hate water too? Should we hope for rain?"

He gave her a look—the one where one corner of his mouth twitched, as if smiling were a debt he couldn't afford. "You're funnier when you're frightened. Did you know that?"

"No one has ever told me that," said Mirabel, "because no one has ever spoken to me before yesterday." It came out sharper than she meant it to. She heard it land on him, saw him flinch slightly, and found, to her surprise, that she didn't want it to hurt. That was new. Wanting things not to hurt other people was, apparently, also a fuel source.

They crested the ridge. Below them Vellharrow lay green and small and utterly indifferent to her leaving, the temple roofs catching light like coins at the bottom of a well. Somewhere down there was Mother Osla, counting out the coin that bought Mirabel's exile, wearing that face she wore that was not quite kindness and not quite cruelty but some third thing Mirabel had never learned the name of.

"She could have just told me to go," Mirabel said quietly.

Rafael didn't answer right away. He was watching the tree line below, the way he'd been watching it all morning, his whole body angled toward it like a compass needle. "People who love you often can't tell you the truth," he said finally. "It gets stuck somewhere between their chest and their mouth."

"Do you speak from experience?"

"I speak from being shot," he said. "Everything since then is experience."

* * *

### Rafael

The wound in his side had opinions, and they were getting louder.

Rafael had been stabbed twice, shot once, thrown from two horses, and briefly employed by the crown spymaster's office, and he could report with confidence that none of those hurts compared to climbing a mountain in the company of a scribe who asked questions the way other people threw knives.

"Why does the stone hum?" she'd asked, an hour ago.

"It doesn't hum."

"It hums. Like a struck bell nobody can hear properly. Why do *I* hear it?"

"Because you're the reader," he'd said, which was true and useless and exactly the sort of answer he'd been trained to give, back when he'd been trained, in another life, under another name.

She'd stopped walking and looked at him with those gray eyes that seemed perpetually braced for disappointment, and he'd felt the strange urge to apologize for every evasive thing he would ever say to her, past and future.

Now, at the stream, while she drank and refilled the waterskin with the careful economy of someone who had grown up rationing everything including hope, he let himself lean against a boulder and take stock of the pain. Bad but not deep. The bandage was holding. He'd dressed worse in worse places.

"The dogs," Mirabel said, without looking up. "How far behind?"

"Half a day. Maybe less if the handler pushes them." He watched her shoulders tighten and hated himself a little for how easily he read fear now, how fluently. "But hounds follow scent, and scent follows ground, and we're about to be very rude to both."

He showed her how to walk the streambed itself, stepping on stone so the water ate their tracks, and she took to it faster than he expected—of course she did, she copied documents for a living, her whole existence was precision—and for perhaps twenty minutes neither of them spoke, and the silence was almost comfortable, and Rafael thought: *this is the part where you remember why you don't travel with anyone.*

Then the rockslide started.

* * *

### Mirabel

Later, Mirabel would try to describe the sound and fail. It wasn't thunder. Thunder came from above. This came from everywhere—a long grinding roar, the mountain clearing its throat, and the slope above the stream simply *decided*, all at once, to become a different shape.

Rafael grabbed her arm. She grabbed the stone.

Not the waterskin, not his hand—the sigil-stone, thrust through her belt, and her hand went to it without asking her permission, the way her hand sometimes moved ink across paper without permission, and she had one wild half-second to think *not now, not now*—

The world became noise and motion. They were running along the streambed with the whole hillside coming apart behind them, and Rafael shoved her left and she obeyed because obedience was easier than thought, and then he shoved her *down*, into the lee of a slab of granite, and covered her with his own body, and the world ended in dust and thunder.

When it stopped, the silence rang worse than the noise.

Mirabel coughed. Tasted grit and blood—her own, she hoped. "Rafael?"

"Present," he wheezed, from directly on top of her. "Barely."

"You're bleeding again."

"I'm aware."

"Through the bandage. Onto my sleeve. This sleeve is temple property."

Something happened to his face that she slowly recognized as a laugh—it looked painful, like a door opening on rusted hinges. "You're joking. You're actually joking. You survived a rockslide and your first concern is laundry."

"My first concern," she said, wriggling out from under him and immediately regretting it, because he'd clearly taken more weight than she'd realized, "is that you are bleeding onto my only sleeve, and my second concern is that you are lying there looking pleased with yourself, and my third—" she pressed her palm flat against his side and he hissed, "—is that you've torn something."

"Third concern noted. Filed. Ignored." He tried to sit up, failed, and let her push him back down, which she suspected cost him more than the injury did. "We need to move. The slide will have cut our trail but it'll also have told every soldier in three valleys exactly where we are. Rockfalls are loud. Loud means *here*."

"So we move."

"So we move. Slowly. You'll carry the pack."

"And what will you carry?"

"Grievances," he said. "I'm told I'm good at those."

* * *

### Rafael

That night they sheltered in a hollow beneath an overhang that some earlier traveler had walled up with stacked stones—a shepherd's bothy, maybe, abandoned years ago. Rafael got a fire going small enough to hide in cupped hands, and Mirabel sat across from it cleaning grit out of her hair with the grim thoroughness of a woman performing surgery on herself.

He rebandaged his side and pretended not to notice her watching, and she pretended not to watch, and the fire crackled, and the stone in its wrap at her belt made that faint sound he still could not hear but could somehow feel, like a word on the tip of everyone's tongue.

"Ask me," he said finally.

She looked up. "What?"

"Whatever it is you've been not-asking since the gate. You've got a list. I can see it behind your eyes, ranked by importance. Ask me one. I'll even answer honestly." He turned his hands toward the fire. "Seems fair. You followed a bleeding stranger into the mountains. You should know what you're following."

Mirabel was quiet for so long he thought she wouldn't take it. Then: "Did you know? Before the temple. Did you know what reading the stone might do to me?"

And there it was—the question he'd been dreading, arriving precisely, like everything else she did.

"Yes," he said.

Her face didn't change, which was somehow worse than anger. "And you came anyway."

"Yes."

"Why?"

"Because the alternative was Aldric using it to hunt every gifted soul in the nine provinces, and because the keepers' lore says the reader exists, has always existed, and because—" He stopped. Started again, slower. "Because when I read the old accounts, they described the reader. Scribe-born. Ink moves for them. Erases themselves to stay safe. And I thought, whoever you are, you've spent your whole life making yourself smaller so the world wouldn't notice you. And I thought—that's not safety. That's just a slower way of disappearing." He looked at her across the fire. "So yes. I knew it might cost you. I'm sorry. I'd do it again. Both of those things are true, and I've stopped pretending they cancel out."

Mirabel fed a twig to the fire. Her hands, he noticed, were steady.

"Ink moved for me when I was six," she said. "Before I could write anything real. Mother Osla found me copying letters I'd never been taught, and she went white as bone, and she taught me—taught me to hold it down. To make the ink behave. Twenty years of holding it down." A pause. "She paid off my debt today to get rid of me. To protect the valley. Or to protect me. I genuinely cannot tell which, and I think neither can she."

"That sounds like love," Rafael said. "The confusing kind."

"Don't," she said—not sharply, but tiredly. "Don't call it love yet. Let it be confusing first. Everything true starts out confusing."

He smiled despite himself, hinges and rust and all. "Where did you learn that?"

"Nowhere," said Mirabel Dunmore. "I invented it four seconds ago. It's the first true thing I've ever said out loud."

* * *

### Mirabel

The second day, they argued about the pass.

"There are two ways over the Spur," Rafael said, crouched over the map he kept folded into a square no bigger than a hymnal. "The low road is fast but open. The high road is slow and hidden."

"We take the high road."

"The high road adds a day. With hounds behind us—"

"With hounds behind us, an open road is a gift to whoever holds the leash." She tapped the map. "You said it yourself. Scent follows ground. Give them nothing to follow."

He looked at her. She braced for the argument, for the male certainty she'd seen tax collectors and senior scribes wear like a badge—but instead he tilted his head, actually considered it, and said, "You're right."

"Oh," said Mirabel.

"'Oh'?"

"I'd prepared rebuttals," she admitted. "Three of them. Quite good ones. One involved an analogy about goats."

"Save it. We may need the goat analogy later."

The high road earned its reputation. It was barely a road at all—a ledge stitched to the mountainside, wide enough for one, dropping away into pine and cloud. They walked it single file in a wind that smelled of snow though the season was wrong for it, and somewhere on the third hour Rafael began, unasked, to talk—about the free cities, about Callowmere's lantern streets, about a tinker named Brannic who fixed pots and broke hearts, about twins who shared one gift between them like a coat. Stories, she realized later. He was telling her stories the way a man throws rope to someone on a ledge. Keeping her mind off the drop.

It worked until it didn't.

The ledge narrowed. Her foot found loose scree, and suddenly the mountain was tilting, the pines wheeling below her, and her hand shot out and caught—nothing, air—and then Rafael's fist closed around her wrist and hauled, and she slammed against the rockface with her heart trying to exit through her ribs.

For a moment they just stood there, his grip on her wrist, her back to the stone, the wind shouting.

"Goat analogy," he said shakily, "wouldn't have helped there."

"No," she agreed. Her voice came out thin. "Thank you."

"Don't thank me. Just—" His thumb was still pressed to her pulse point; she felt him register it, feel her heartbeat hammering, and he didn't let go, quite. "Watch your feet. That's all. Watch your feet and I'll watch the trail, and between us we might manage one competent person."

"That," said Mirabel, "is the most honest division of labor I've ever heard."

They walked on. But something had shifted, some small weight transferred between them, and when he offered his hand at the next bad step, she took it.

* * *

### Rafael

On the evening of the third day, from a shoulder of rock above the treeline, Rafael looked back the way they'd come and saw them: four points of lamplight winding up the low road, patient as ticks. Behind the lights, faint on the wind, a hound's voice rose and fell.

"They crossed the stream," he said. "Someone taught those dogs to swim."

Mirabel came to stand beside him. She'd stopped complaining about her legs sometime that afternoon, which worried him more than the complaining had. "How long?"

"A day. Maybe less." He chewed the inside of his cheek, doing sums he didn't like. The high road had bought them time but spent their strength; Rafael's wound had gone hot and tight, which meant fever was circling him like the hounds circled the trail. "We can't outrun them to Callowmere. Not both of us. Not like this."

"Then what?"

He was quiet a moment. Then he pointed—south and east, where the forest sloped down toward a river valley, and where, if the tinker hadn't changed his route in three years, a string of cart-lanterns should be swinging through the dusk like low-hung stars.

"Down there," Rafael said, "there's a caravan of people the crown already gave up on. Smugglers, tinkers, a pair of twins who can do impossible things when they hold hands. Old Wren, who claims to remember things that haven't happened yet." He allowed himself the twitching smile. "If anyone knows a road the hounds can't smell, it's them."

"And they'll help us? Strangers?"

"They helped me once. Brannic says debt is the only currency that never devalues." He shouldered the pack and winced. "Besides—you'll like them. They argue like you do."

"I don't argue."

"You absolutely argue."

"I *discuss*, with emphasis."

"See? Arguing."

Below them, the first of the cart-lanterns flickered into view through the trees, warm gold against the blue dark, and Mirabel went very still beside him, the way people go still at doors they're afraid to knock on.

"They're expecting trouble," she said. "Caravans like that. They won't want us bringing soldiers to their fires."

"Probably not," Rafael agreed. "Lucky for us, we're very charming."

"Speak for yourself."

"I am. Constantly." He started downslope, then paused, and held out his hand—not for balance, this time. Just held it out. After a moment, she took it.

Behind them, faint and patient, the hounds sang. Ahead, the lanterns swung closer, and Mirabel Dunmore—who had spent twenty years learning to be invisible—walked toward her first firelight that wasn't the temple's.
