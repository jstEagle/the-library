## 7. The Tinker's Caravan

They heard the caravan before they saw it: a clatter and jingle like a kitchen falling down a staircase, punctuated by a man's voice raised in cheerful argument with a mule.

"Come on, then. Come *on*. You've carried worse than a bent axle up worse than this hill, and if you pretend otherwise I'll have you melted down for spoons—"

Rafael's hand went to his knife. Mirabel caught his wrist.

"It's a tinker," she said. "Or the world's least intimidating bandit."

The road bent around a stand of black pines, and there it was: three wagons painted in peeling blues and golds, hung with copper pots that caught the morning sun and threw it around like gossip. A mule stood stubbornly in the traces while a mountain of a man argued with it, gray-bearded, sleeves rolled over forearms scarred by solder burns. Two young women sat atop the lead wagon, so alike they had to be twins, sharing an apple between them with the solemnity of a treaty negotiation.

And on the tailboard of the last wagon, feet swinging like a girl's despite her age, sat an old woman who looked directly at Mirabel across forty yards of mountain road and said, loudly enough to carry:

"There she is."

Mirabel stopped walking.

The old woman hopped down, joints cracking like green firewood, and came toward them at a brisk shuffle. She was small and round and wrapped in a shawl embroidered with symbols Mirabel almost recognized—which was worse than not recognizing them at all.

"You'd be the reader," the old woman said, stopping close enough that Mirabel could see her eyes were the pale washed-out blue of very old glass. "And you'd be the courier who bled all over my good blanket two winters back." This to Rafael. "You still owe me for that blanket."

"Wren?" Rafael said slowly.

"You remember me. Good. One of you should remember something." She turned back to Mirabel and studied her the way Mother Osla studied a page for errors—with a patience that made clear every flaw would eventually be noted. "I dreamed about you three nights running. A girl made of margins. Ink under her fingernails and nowhere in the ledger."

"I'm sorry," Mirabel said, because it was the only response she had ever learned for being seen too closely. "I think you've mistaken me for—"

"Don't apologize," Wren said. "It wastes time, and we haven't got any. Brannic!" she called over her shoulder. "Put the kettle on. We've got guests whether they know it yet or not."

---

Brannic's name, it turned out, was Brannic, and he did put the kettle on, and within twenty minutes of meeting these strangers Mirabel was sitting on an overturned crate drinking tea from a tin cup with a dent in it while the mule—who was named Duchess and held grudges like a bank holds coin—sniffed her hair for anything edible.

She kept waiting for the moment when it would become a mistake.

That was how kindness worked in her experience. It was a door left open just wide enough to see through before someone noticed their error and closed it again. The temple had fed her and housed her and paid her wages against her debt, and every coin of it had been arithmetic, never welcome. So she sat with her cup held carefully in both hands and waited for the tally to begin.

It didn't come.

Instead, one of the twins—Sefa, the one with the ink-dark braid and the quicker smile—dropped down beside her on the next crate and said, "So. You're running from soldiers."

"Sefa," said the other twin warningly. Ilo, quieter, with a faded scar through one eyebrow.

"What? It's obvious. Look at him." Sefa pointed her apple core at Rafael. "Nobody walks that fast unless something's behind them. And she keeps looking over her shoulder like the sky owes her money." She leaned toward Mirabel conspiratorially. "We're running from soldiers too. Well. We're running from *everything*, but mostly soldiers lately. What did yours do?"

"They—" Mirabel glanced at Rafael, who gave a small shake of his head. "They want something he's carrying."

"Mm," said Sefa, in the tone of someone filing a fact away in a drawer she intended to open later. "Well, you can eat with us regardless. Brannic makes enough for six and we're only four, and Duchess can't be trusted with leftovers."

"We couldn't impose," Rafael began.

"You already are," said Brannic comfortably, ladling out stew from a pot the size of a cauldron, which perhaps it was. "Imposition's half of what a caravan is. Sit down before it goes cold."

They sat. They ate. And Mirabel, who had eaten eleven thousand meals alone in a scriptorium corner, discovered that food tasted different when it was passed to you by hand instead of taken from a queue. She could not have explained why. The stew was mutton and barley and turnip, nothing remarkable. But Sefa refilled her bowl without asking, and Ilo quietly moved the salt nearer to her, and Wren watched all of it with those pale eyes and said nothing at all, which somehow felt like a blessing.

---

By evening they had made an arrangement, because travelers on mountain roads make arrangements or they make graves. The caravan was headed for Callowmere; so, it emerged, were Rafael and Mirabel, though Rafael had been vague about why and everyone had been polite enough—or curious enough—to let him stay that way. In exchange for travel in the convoy's company, Rafael would walk watch, and Mirabel...

"Well, what do you do?" Sefa asked, sprawled by the fire with her head in her sister's lap.

"I'm a scribe."

"A scribe!" Brannic lit up like a lamp. "Do you keep accounts?"

"I... yes."

"*Wonderful.* Do you know what my ledgers look like? I'll show you what my ledgers look like." He fetched a book bound in oiled canvas, and Mirabel opened it with the professional dread of her trade. It was worse than she feared. Numbers wandered. Columns merged and divorced. At some point a recipe for pickled onions had colonized the middle of the autumn inventory.

"How," she said faintly, "do you know what you own?"

"I don't! That's the beauty of it. Every year's a surprise." He grinned at her expression. "Go on, then. You fix it, and I'll owe you. That's fair dealing, that is. First honest transaction I've had since spring."

So Mirabel sat by the fire with Brannic's disastrous ledger on her knees and the caravan settling into its night noises around her—Ilo humming tunelessly as she mended harness, Sefa arguing with Duchess about an apple, Wren already asleep sitting upright like a folded letter—and she fixed numbers. Simple work. Safe work. Work that asked nothing of her except the small careful part of herself she'd spent a lifetime sharpening.

Except that halfway down the second page, her pen slowed.

Because it didn't feel like hiding, tonight. It felt like... helping. Like the difference between whispering and singing.

The ink moved a little for her, just a little, nudging a smudged figure legible—and she looked up in sudden panic, but no one was watching. Or no one but Wren, whose eyes were open after all, and who smiled at her across the fire with terrible gentleness, and closed them again.

---

Three days on the road taught Mirabel the shape of them.

Brannic was the heart of the caravan, its furnace and its anchor. He had a word of comfort for every refugee they passed heading the other way down the mountain, and half a loaf for every child, and he never once asked where they were going. Once, when Mirabel thanked him for the third meal in a row, he waved it off and said, "Feed people first. Ask questions never. My wife's rule, gods rest her. I just kept the rule after I lost the reason for it." He said it lightly, and changed the subject heavily, and Mirabel understood that there was a locked room in Brannic the way there was one in everyone, and that his kindness was built on the far side of whatever was inside it.

Sefa and Ilo were a puzzle she solved slowly, and enjoyed solving.

They were twins in the way rivers are twins—same source, utterly different courses. Sefa talked enough for four people; Ilo spoke maybe forty words a day and made each one feel load-bearing. But watch them together and something stranger showed beneath: when Sefa reached for a falling pot, it arrived in Ilo's hands ten feet away, neither of them appearing to notice. When Ilo frowned at a stuck wagon wheel, the wheel rolled free while Sefa, across camp, laughed at something unrelated and wiped her suddenly dirty palms on her skirt.

On the second night, Mirabel finally worked up the courage to ask.

"Your gift," she said, very quietly, by the fire while the others slept. "Is it—shared?"

Ilo looked at her for a long moment. Then, unexpectedly, she smiled—the rare full version, the one that made her face younger. "One gift," she said. "Between two people. Like a coat we take turns wearing. Sefa has the reach, I have the grip. Alone, each of us is half of something. Together—" she shrugged. "Together we're whole. Barely."

"That sounds lonely."

"It sounds lonely until you've tried being alone," Ilo said, and looked at Mirabel with those steady dark eyes, and Mirabel felt the words land somewhere she hadn't invited them to land. "You'll learn. You've got people now."

*You've got people now.* Four words. Mirabel turned them over in her mind all night, like coins of unfamiliar currency, checking them for counterfeit.

---

Wren she did not solve at all.

The old woman traveled in the last wagon among boxes of things she claimed were relics and Brannic claimed were rubbish, and she told stories the way weather happens—suddenly, at scale, indifferent to whether anyone wanted it. She claimed to be ninety-one years old. She claimed to have been sixty-one at the fall of the last covenant. Both claims could not be true unless the last covenant had fallen thirty years ago, which Rafael, when Mirabel mentioned it, said flatly was impossible—it had been broken generations ago, before Aldric, before even old King Marren.

"She exaggerates," Rafael said. "Old people do."

"Does she? About everything?"

He hesitated. "About most things," he admitted. "But here's the thing, Mirabel. She was right about you. Before she ever laid eyes on you. 'A girl made of margins.'" He shook his head. "I don't know where a person stores a thing like that for seventy years. But I know better than to laugh at it."

So Mirabel listened to Wren's stories, and catalogued them the way she catalogued everything, and grew quietly more troubled with each telling.

---

On the fourth evening, camped in a hollow below a ridge with the wind worrying the tent ropes, Wren told the story of the Sundering. She told it to the whole company, because she told everything to the whole company, but Mirabel felt—as she had felt at the temple gate—that parts of it were aimed at her like arrows.

"Once," Wren began, in the voice she used for such things, pitched low and rolling, "magic ran through the world like water through a net. There was no valley without it, no hill, no hearthstone. And the gifted were as common as rain, and as various, and mostly as harmless."

"And mostly as harmless," Brannic echoed skeptically, from behind his pipe.

"Hush, I'm establishing context. Then came the war of the Broken Crowns, when gifted tore kingdoms apart, and the wise of that age gathered at a place called the Sundered Spire, and they wove a covenant—a great sealing—to wall magic away from one valley entire. Vellharrow. A sanctuary, they called it. A place where no gift could follow and no gift could reach, where the powerless could live safe from the powerful forever." Wren's pale eyes found Mirabel's. "They paid a price for it, of course. All great workings cost. Each founder gave something up. Some say years of life. Some say—" she paused, and her brow creased, and for a moment the storyteller vanished and only a very old confused woman remained, "—some say they gave their memories. Their pasts. Poured into the working like wine into earth."

"Which was it?" Mirabel asked softly.

Wren blinked at her. "Which was...?"

"The price. Life, or memory."

"Why, memory, obviously," Wren said. Then she frowned. "No. No, it was years. Years of life, poured out like..." She trailed off. Her hands worried at the fringe of her shawl. "It was memory. I remember the giving of memories. I remember—" She stopped entirely. The fire cracked. Somewhere up the ridge, a stone loosened itself and clicked down the slope.

"Grandmother?" Ilo said gently. "Take your time."

But Wren's confusion curdled into something sharper, and she rounded on Mirabel almost accusingly. "How long were you in Vellharrow, girl?"

"All my life."

"And before?"

"Before what? There is no before. I was born there. I grew up in the temple orphanage—"

"Born there," Wren repeated. She leaned forward, and the firelight moved in her washed-out eyes, and Mirabel's skin prickled with a cold that had nothing to do with the mountain wind. "Born there. Grew up there. And yet I dreamed you before I met you, and I dreamed you standing in a doorway I knew, girl. A doorway in the Spire itself, with light coming through it the wrong way. And I dreamed your mother standing in that light, holding out her hands to you, and her face—" Wren pressed her knuckles to her mouth. "Her face was the founder's face. The one from the vault carvings. The one I saw when I was a girl, before the covenant fell, before..."

She faltered. The sentence fell off a cliff.

"Before the covenant fell," Rafael prompted quietly. "When was that, Wren?"

The old woman stared at him. And Mirabel watched—watched with a scribe's eye for erasure, for the ghost of ink scraped away and written over—as the certainty drained out of Wren's face and left behind smooth blank parchment.

"When was what?" Wren said pleasantly. "Anyone want more tea?"

No one answered. The wind pulled at the tent ropes. And Mirabel sat very still by the fire, holding her tin cup in both hands, feeling the ground shift beneath the one story she had believed unshakeable: her own. Because if Wren's memories contradicted themselves—if gaps yawned where a lifetime should be—

Then somewhere in the world, other people were missing the same years she was.

And Wren, watching her over the teapot, nodded slowly, as if Mirabel had said it aloud.
