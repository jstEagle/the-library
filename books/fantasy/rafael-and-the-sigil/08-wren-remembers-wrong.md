## 8. Wren Remembers Wrong

Wren had told the story of the Sundering so many times that the telling had worn grooves in her mind, the way water wears channels in stone. She knew this, and she told it anyway, because a story worn smooth was easier to carry than one with all its sharp edges intact.

They were camped in a hollow below the tree line, where Brannic's caravan had wedged itself against the wind like a stubborn old mule refusing to be moved. The tinker's wagons formed a rough half-circle, canvas stretched between them, and a cookfire burned low in the center, throwing more warmth than light. Sefa and Ilo were arguing over whose turn it was to mend the mule's harness—a dispute conducted entirely in elbow-jabs and meaningful glances, since the twins could apparently conduct entire conversations without words when they felt like it—and Mirabel sat closest to the fire, her hands wrapped around a tin cup of Wren's nettle tea as though it were the last warm thing in the world.

Which, Wren supposed, it might soon be.

"Tell it again," Rafael said. He was propped against a wagon wheel, his wounded side carefully arranged, his face doing that thing it did when he wanted something badly and was pretending he didn't. "The covenant. From the beginning."

"You've heard it twice already," Wren said.

"I heard it once from you and once from a drunk ferryman who also claimed the valley was haunted by a giant goose. I want to hear them match."

"The ferryman was an idiot."

"So match him anyway."

Wren sighed, which was mostly theater—she would have told it whether they asked or not. An old woman with a story is like a kettle left on the fire; sooner or later she boils over.

"Very well." She settled herself on her folded blanket, arranged her hands on her knees, and let the grooves take her. "Before there was a Vellharrow, before there was a crown worth cursing, there were two realms laid side by side like lovers in one bed—the waking world and the gifted world, the one where magic pools like groundwater. And in places where the ground between them grew thin, magic seeped up into ordinary lives. Children floated crockery. Grandmothers cursed crops with a word. Rivers ran backward out of spite."

"That sounds nice, actually," Sefa said.

"It sounds *chaotic*," Wren corrected. "Nice and chaotic are not the same thing. A village where anyone can set your barn alight by sneezing is not a village at peace. So the founders of the old orders met beneath the Sundered Spire—seven of them, or nine, depending on which account you trust—and they swore a covenant. They walled the two realms apart. They built Vellharrow in the deepest fold of the wall, a valley where magic simply *cannot follow*, and they made it a sanctuary. A place where a gifted child could grow up unremarkable. Where power couldn't find you."

"And the price?" Mirabel asked quietly.

Wren paused.

She hadn't meant to pause. The pause was not part of the groove. But Mirabel was looking at her across the firelight with those ink-stained fingers curled around her cup, and the question landed somewhere it shouldn't have.

"There's always a price," Wren said. "Great workings eat something. The founders paid in—" She stopped. Frowned. "In blood. Yes. Blood oaths, all seven of them. Or nine."

"You said seven," Ilo said.

"I said *or nine*."

"You said 'seven of them, or nine,'" Sefa confirmed, with the merciless precision of someone who shared a head with another person and therefore never got to be vague about anything. "But then just now you said blood oaths, all seven. You picked seven."

Wren waved a hand. "I'm eighty-one years old. Numbers are for tax collectors."

But the itch started behind her eyes, the way it always did. The small scratching feeling of a memory that wouldn't sit still, like a flea in the bedding of her mind.

"Go on," Rafael said gently. "The price."

"The price," Wren said firmly, "was blood, and the wall held for three hundred years, and it would hold for three hundred more if meddling kings would leave it alone. That's the whole of it. Now, who wants more tea?"

"You're leaving bits out," Ilo said—not accusingly, just observing, the way you'd observe weather.

"I'm *summarizing*. There's a difference."

"What happened to the founders after?" Mirabel asked. "The ones who swore it. Did they live long lives? Are there descendants?"

And there it was again, that pause, opening under Wren like a trapdoor.

"They..." She had a picture, suddenly, vivid and wrong: a hall of white stone, seven chairs, and every chair empty. Or was it nine chairs? Was it a hall at all? She had seen the Spire once, long ago, hadn't she? In her traveling days, before her knees filed their complaints. She remembered standing beneath it. She remembered the color of the stone. She did *not* remember the road there, or the year, or the face of a single person she'd traveled with, and when she reached for those things the way you'd reach for a stair in the dark, there was nothing there. Just smooth blankness, and then the next memory, waiting on the far side of the gap like a neighbor across a missing bridge.

"They went home," she said at last. "They lived quiet lives. It was a mercy, what they did. People forgot magic, and good riddance."

"A mercy," Mirabel repeated. Not mocking. Just tasting the word.

* * *

Later, when the twins had lost the harness argument to the mule and gone to their bedrolls, when Rafael slept with his hand near his knife even in his dreams, Wren sat up keeping the fire company, as old women do, and found Mirabel sitting up too, watching her.

"Can't sleep either?" Wren asked.

"I keep thinking about what you said. About the founders going home."

"Mm."

"You hesitated."

"I'm old. Hesitating is most of what I do."

Mirabel turned her cup slowly. "When my mother died—I was told she died, anyway; the temple raised me after—the scribes gave me back my family records. Debts, mostly. My parents owed the temple everything. But there were gaps in the ledgers. Whole years missing. Not torn out—just... absent. As if whoever kept the accounts skipped pages and bound the book anyway." She looked up. "You hesitated the same way I feel when I try to remember being six years old. Like reaching for a stair that isn't there."

Wren should have laughed it off. Eighty-one years of practice at laughing things off, and she'd gotten quite good. Instead she heard herself say: "What years?"

"Five through eight, roughly. Maybe nine. It's foggy at the edges."

Something cold walked down Wren's spine, wearing small boots.

Because she had gaps too. Everyone assumed old age ate memories from the edges inward—first names, then dates, then faces—but Wren's gaps weren't at the edges. They were holes punched in the middle, clean-edged, deliberate-looking, like windows bricked over in a house people still lived in. She remembered her wedding day but not her husband's face. She remembered learning to mend nets but not who taught her. She remembered the Spire and not the journey to it.

"My girl," Wren said slowly, "when you were five, what's the first thing you remember after the fog?"

"A corridor. Stone floors. Mother Osla's voice saying my name for the first time." Mirabel's mouth twisted. "Except she wasn't my mother, and it wasn't the first time anyone said my name. Someone must have named me before that. Someone must have called me in to supper, scolded me, sung to me. There should be *someone*."

The fire popped. Neither of them spoke.

"You know what frightens me," Wren said finally, and her voice came out smaller than she liked. "It's not the gaps. It's that the gaps don't hurt. A wound hurts. These are just... smooth. Like the story I tell about the covenant. Worn smooth. Told so many times the sharp parts are gone, and I can't even tell anymore which parts wore down honestly and which parts were *made* smooth."

"Made by whom?"

"That," said Wren, "is the question, isn't it."

She looked at the girl across the fire—at the ink-stained fingers, the careful invisible posture of someone who had spent a lifetime making herself small—and thought about the sigil-stone wrapped in oilcloth in Rafael's pack, and about how stories, like debts, always come due.

"Worn smooth," Mirabel murmured. "Like something read aloud many times until it takes the shape of the reading."

Wren's skin prickled. "Say that again."

But Mirabel only shook her head, frowning at her own words as if they'd arrived from somewhere else. "I don't know why I said that. Forget it."

*Forget it.* Wren almost laughed. If only it worked that way. If only forgetting were a thing you could choose, rather than a thing that had been chosen for you, stitched into you before you were old enough to object.

"Get some sleep, girl," she said instead. "Tomorrow we cross the pass, and the pass doesn't care what any of us remember."

Mirabel went, obedient as a temple novice. Wren stayed with the fire a while longer, feeding it sticks one at a time, turning the evening over in her mind.

Seven founders or nine. Blood oaths or—what? What was the other answer, the one that lived on the far side of the gap? She reached for it the way she'd reached a thousand times before, and hit the smooth blank wall, and this time—for the first time in sixty years—the wall *ached*, faintly, like an old break in wet weather.

As if something on the other side had heard her knocking.

Above the hollow, far off toward the pass, a hound began to sing. Then another. Then the whole dark chorus of them, threading through the mountains, patient and certain, singing the song that said: *we know where you are, and we are not tired yet.*

Wren put out the fire and went to wake the others.
