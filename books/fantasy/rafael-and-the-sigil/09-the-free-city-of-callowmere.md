## 9. The Free City of Callowmere

Rafael smelled Callowmere before he saw it—woodsmoke and river mud, tar and frying onions, the particular sourness of too many people packed behind walls that had never been meant to hold this many. It was, he thought with something dangerously close to affection, the smell of a city pretending very hard that everything was fine.

"It smells like a stable that's been argued with," Sefa said beside him.

"That's the smell of freedom," Rafael said. "Freedom fries a lot of onions."

From the crest of the last hill, the city spread out below them in the grey-gold light of late afternoon: a crescent of pale stone wrapped around the bend of the River Maren, its rooftops shingled in slate and copper gone green with age, its harbor crowded with barges that should have been three times fewer for this time of year. The walls were old—older than the kingdom, older than Aldric's grandfather—and crowned with watchtowers whose beacon-fires were already lit, though the sun hadn't set. That was new. That was six months new, maybe less.

Mirabel stood a little apart from the rest of them, her scribe's cloak brushed clean, her hands folded in front of her like a woman waiting to be called on in class. She was looking at the city the way she looked at everything: as if she were copying it down somewhere behind her eyes in a hand so small no one would ever find it.

"You've been here before," she said. Not quite a question.

"Twice." Rafael shifted his weight off his healing leg. The rockslide bruise had faded to a yellow-green shadow, but it still spoke up when he stood too long. "Once delivering letters, once hiding from the people the letters were about."

"And your contact. Dessa." Mirabel said the name carefully, tasting it. "She's a friend?"

Rafael considered how to answer that honestly. "Dessa is a woman who once pulled an arrowhead out of my shoulder with a fishhook and a bottle of plum brandy, and then charged me for both. She is not anyone's friend. But she owes me, I owe her, and between those two debts there is a narrow bridge where useful things can happen."

Behind them, Brannic's caravan creaked to a halt at the top of the hill—the tinker's wagon drawn by his two patient mules, Wren's cart hitched behind it, the twins walking alongside because they claimed the wagon made them itch, which Rafael suspected was a lie they told so they could talk privately while appearing to be part of the group. Old habits of hunted people.

Brannic cupped his hands around his mouth. "Are we going to stand here admiring it all evening, or are we going to get inside before someone admires *us*?"

"He's right," Rafael said. "Walls only work if you're on the right side of them."

---

The queue at the Southgate moved with the sluggish resentment of all queues everywhere. A bored gate-sergeant waved through farmers with produce, merchants with manifests, pilgrims with the weary thousand-yard stare of people who had walked a long way to be disappointed by a shrine. When the caravan reached the head of the line, the sergeant's eyes did a slow inventory: one tinker with a wagon full of pots, one ancient woman, two young men who might have been brothers or might have been twins or might have been nothing at all, one courier with a limp, and one woman in temple grey.

"Business in Callowmere?" the sergeant asked.

"Tinkering," Brannic said, patting his wagon with the pride of a man presenting a firstborn child. "Pots, pans, kettles, knives sharpened, umbrellas mended—"

"It's autumn. Who needs an umbrella mended in autumn?"

"The pessimists of this city, sergeant, and they are legion."

The sergeant almost smiled, caught himself, and turned to Mirabel. "And you?"

"Pilgrim," she said smoothly. "Temple of Vellharrow. On retreat."

Something flickered across the sergeant's face—not suspicion exactly, but the careful blankness of a man who has been told to look for certain words and pretend he hasn't heard them. "Vellharrow," he repeated. "Quiet valley, I hear. Nothing ever happens there."

"Nothing ever does," Mirabel agreed.

He stamped their papers without further comment and waved them through, but Rafael watched him lean toward the man at the next post and murmur something, and watched the second man glance after them, and filed it away in the growing drawer of things to worry about after dinner.

Inside the walls, Callowmere was holding its breath and calling it commerce. The market streets were full, but the fullness had a frantic edge—stall-keepers selling off stock rather than storing it, prices chalked high and crossed out higher, a smithy running three forges at once with the shutters open despite the cold. Soldiers of the city watch drilled in the square where, on Rafael's last visit, jugglers had performed. A crier stood on a barrel reading proclamations in a voice like a cracked bell:

"—by order of the Council of Nine, the grain levy is doubled until further notice—all citizens possessing arms are to register them with the ward captains—refugees from the eastern provinces are to report to the Tannery Quarter for processing—"

"*Processing,*" Wren muttered darkly. "In my day we just gave them soup and asked their names."

"In your day," Brannic said, "the day was considerably longer ago than you remember."

"My memories are perfectly intact, you great kettle of a man."

"Your memories contradict each other like feuding siblings."

Rafael let their bickering wash over him, warm and familiar now, the sound of a family arguing its way down a street. He caught Mirabel glancing sideways at the crier, at the drilling soldiers, at a wall where someone had pasted a printed notice over an older, torn notice, and he saw her read both—the new one and the ghost of the old one beneath—with those quick scribe's eyes.

"What does it say?" he asked quietly. "The one underneath."

Her jaw tightened. "'By order of King Osric, the free cities shall enjoy perpetual amity with the crown.'" She paused. "Osric was Aldric's brother. The one who died."

"The one who was *helped* to die," Rafael said, "if you believe half the stories."

"I believe the other half," Mirabel said, "which are worse."

---

Dessa kept her offices above a dye-house in the Weavers' Quarter, which was either a brilliant choice or a terrible one depending on whether you minded spending your days in a building that smelled permanently of fermented urine and mordant salts. Rafael had spent three memorable days there once, recovering from the aforementioned arrowhead, and had come away with both a scar and an enduring inability to smell indigo without flinching.

He left the caravan at the Sign of the Crooked Kettle—an inn run by a former smuggler named Hesk who owed Rafael nothing but charged fair prices and asked fewer questions than most priests—and went up alone. Mirabel had wanted to come. He'd talked her out of it with the truth: Dessa counted strangers the way misers counted coins, and every extra face at the table cost something.

The dye-house stairs groaned under him like a guilty witness. At the top, a door painted the exact blue of expensive grief, and no knocker. He rapped the rhythm he'd been taught four years ago—two, pause, three, pause, one—and waited.

The door opened on a chain. One eye appeared in the gap, brown, unimpressed, framed by a spill of iron-grey curls.

"You're supposed to be dead," the eye said.

"Hello to you too, Dessa."

"People who owe me money aren't allowed to die. It's theft." The chain rattled off. "Get in before the hallway sees you."

Dessa Vail was fifty-some years old and built like a question nobody wanted answered. She wore a dye-stained apron over what Rafael knew was a very good sword, and she poured two cups of chicory-and-sawdust coffee without asking whether he wanted any, because wanting was irrelevant; in Dessa's office you drank what was poured.

She sat, looked at him for a long moment, and her face did something complicated.

"You're limping worse than last time," she said. "And thinner. And you came up the river road, which means you came through Ashfen's edge, which means you're either very brave or running from something specific." She set down her cup. "Start with the thing you're running from."

"Aldric's hunt," Rafael said. "The edict. Gifted taken to the capital—for protection, the proclamation says."

"I know what it says. I also know what the wagons say that leave the capital at night with their wheels greased and their cargo quiet." Dessa's voice stayed flat, but her hands had gone still around her cup, and Rafael had learned long ago that Dessa's hands told truer stories than her mouth. "What I don't know is why a dead courier walks into my office smelling of mountain rain and hound oil."

"Hounds followed us out of Vellharrow. We lost them in the passes." He reached into his shirt and drew out the sigil-stone.

He hadn't meant to show her. He'd meant to keep it hidden, trade information, get out. But the stone seemed to have opinions about secrecy these days—it grew warm against his chest whenever he lied, which was inconvenient, and cold when danger was near, which was worse—and Dessa's eyes went straight to the place where his shirt gapped, sharp as awls.

"That," she said slowly, "is a very old piece of rock for a courier to be carrying."

"It's the reason the hunt exists. Or half of it. The other half is a king who's afraid of anything he can't tax." He laid it on the desk between them, and Dessa leaned back in her chair as if it had hissed at her.

"Rafael." Her voice dropped to the register she used for truths. "Three weeks ago, a man came through here claiming to sell maps of the Sundered Spire. Paid for passage west. Had crown scrip in his purse, fresh-minted, and a tattoo under his collar that my people recognized as a keeper's mark—the old covenant keepers, back when there were such things. Two days later, Aldric's surveyors arrived in Callowmere asking after roads to the Spire. Coincidence is a luxury I stopped believing in around the same time I stopped believing in honest tax assessors."

Rafael's stomach went cold. "How many surveyors?"

"Twelve. Very polite. Very thorough. They bought a lot of ink and paper." Dessa smiled without any humor in it whatsoever. "Kings don't buy ink unless they're planning to write something large."

"How soon? How many soldiers?"

And Dessa told him, and the room got smaller.

Not the border garrisons everyone had been bracing for. Not the slow seasonal campaign the Council of Nine had been debating since midsummer, with committees and counter-petitions and the particular paralysis of nine proud cities each convinced the other eight were cowards. Three columns. Thirty thousand, maybe more, moving now, in autumn, breaking every rule of sane warfare because Aldric didn't care about supply lines—he cared about speed. The vanguard had already crossed the Ashfen. Ten days, perhaps twelve, to Callowmere's walls. The eastern cities wouldn't even know they were at war until the war arrived and knocked.

"They'll call a council session tomorrow," Dessa said. "Emergency session. Half the delegates will propose surrendering 'the gifted elements' to buy peace. It's the oldest bargain in the world—give them someone smaller than you and pray they stop there." She looked at the sigil-stone on her desk, and then at Rafael, and her eyes narrowed. "You didn't come here just to warn me. You came because you need the council to listen to whoever carries that stone. And you need it done fast."

"Dessa—"

"Don't thank me. Thanking me costs extra." She stood, went to a cabinet, and began pulling out papers—passes, seals, letters of introduction written in three different hands. "I can get you in front of the council. Day after tomorrow, morning session. After that, you're on your own, because if this goes wrong, Dessa Vail never heard of any of you, and I will deny you with such conviction that even *you* will doubt you exist."

She paused, quill hovering, and glanced back at him with something that in a softer person might have been called worry.

"The girl," she said. "The pilgrim from Vellharrow. Your people talk in their sleep, Rafael, and my innkeeper talks to me. She's the reader, isn't she? The one the stone wants."

Rafael said nothing, which was answer enough.

"Then understand what you're carrying into that chamber." Dessa stamped a seal into wax with a hard, deliberate press. "Aldric wants that stone because he thinks it's a weapon. The council will want it because they think it's a shield. And the moment either side learns what it actually *costs*—" she slid the papers across the desk, "—they'll want it even more. Kings and councils, they're the same animal. They just shed differently."

---

He found them at the Crooked Kettle in the common room, around the big corner table Hesk saved for people who paid in advance and apologized rarely. Brannic was demonstrating to a fascinated circle of locals how a certain dented pot could be persuaded back into shape with nothing but a wooden mallet and sustained moral judgment. Wren was asleep sitting up, which she insisted was a form of meditation. The twins were playing tiles against two wool merchants and winning in a manner that suggested the merchants would shortly discover their purses lighter and their faith in probability damaged.

Mirabel sat slightly apart, as she always did, with a borrowed book she wasn't reading. When Rafael came in, she looked up, and whatever she saw in his face made her close the book.

"How bad?" she asked.

He sat down across from her. Around them the inn roared on—laughter, clatter, a fiddle tuning up in the corner, ordinary life being ordinary at the top of its lungs—but between the two of them a silence opened like a well.

"Ten days," he said. "Maybe twelve. Three columns, thirty thousand strong, already across the Ashfen. They're not marching on the free cities, Mirabel. They're marching on the Spire. Everything else is on the way."

He watched her take that in. She didn't gasp or go pale; she did what she always did—her fingers curled slightly, as if gripping a pen, as if some part of her were already writing it down.

"The council?" she said.

"Dessa gets us in. Day after tomorrow." He hesitated, and then made himself say the rest, because she deserved the whole weight of it, not the trimmed version. "There's a chance—Dessa says better than a chance—that when they see what you can do, some of them won't want to fight Aldric. They'll want to hand you over. Buy peace with a scribe from nowhere who nobody will miss."

Mirabel was quiet for a moment. Then, to his surprise, she almost smiled.

"Nobody will miss me," she said. "That used to be the entire point of my life. I worked very hard at it." She turned the closed book over in her hands. "It's strange. Two months ago, being handed to a king would have been the worst thing I could imagine. Now it's only the second worst."

"What's the first?"

"Losing this." She nodded, very slightly, at the table—at Brannic's laughter, at the twins cheating the wool merchants with flawless synchrony, at Wren snoring through her meditation. "At all of you. I finally have something to lose, Rafael. It's made me terribly brave and completely insufferable."

Across the room, Brannic's pot rang true under the mallet, and the locals applauded, and for one more hour the city could pretend it wasn't counting days.

But late that night, standing at the window of the room he shared with Brannic, Rafael looked east along the dark ribbon of the river road and saw, far off, at the very edge of seeing, a smudge of orange against the black horizon. Too steady for lightning. Too distant for a farm fire.

Beacon fires, answering each other down the length of the Ashfen, like a message being passed hand to hand in the dark:

*Coming. Coming. Coming.*

He stood there a long time, the sigil-stone cold against his chest, and did not wake the others. There would be time enough for fear tomorrow. Tonight, let them sleep.

Let them have this one night of onions and fiddles and cheated merchants, before the luxury of safety ran out for good.
