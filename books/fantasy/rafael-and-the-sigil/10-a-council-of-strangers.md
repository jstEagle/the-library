## 10. A Council of Strangers

Callowmere smelled of salt and panic.

Mirabel had expected grandeur from a free city—the kind of place that appeared in ballads, all white towers and singing markets—but what she saw from the caravan's last rise was a city cinched tight like a fist. The harbor was crowded with ships riding low under the weight of fleeing families. The walls bristled not with banners but with sharpened stakes, and every third rooftop seemed to host a watchman squinting southward, toward the road Aldric's armies would take.

"Cheerful place," Brannic said, flicking his mule's reins. "Reminds me of a funeral where nobody's brought enough food."

"You've been to cheerful funerals?" Sefa asked from the wagon bed, where she sat fanning Ilo with a scrap of canvas despite her brother's protests that he wasn't an invalid, merely *temporarily perforated*.

"I've *been* to funerals," Brannic said. "The cheerful ones are the ones with enough food."

Mirabel smiled because it was expected of her, and because Brannic's humor had become something like a handrail she could grip when everything else tilted. But beneath the smile her stomach was doing slow, deliberate somersaults. In two hours—less, if the council ran punctually, which Rafael assured her no council in history ever had—she was going to stand before the assembled delegates of five free cities and do the one thing she had spent twenty-six years learning never to do.

She was going to let them see.

* * *

Dessa's safehouse occupied the upper floor of a chandler's shop near the harbor, and it smelled of tallow and secrets. The spymaster herself was a compact woman with iron-gray hair cropped short and eyes that catalogued you the way a customs officer catalogued cargo: weight, value, contraband.

"So," Dessa said, pouring tea for six without asking who wanted any. "Rafael Vane, alive, which I'd have bet against at three-to-one. And he brings me—" she gestured at the sigil-stone, wrapped in oilcloth on her table, "—the single most dangerous object on the continent, plus five strangers, one of whom is leaking."

Ilo looked down at his bandaged side. "That's fair."

"Not you." Dessa's gaze settled on Mirabel with uncomfortable precision. "Her. She's been staring at that bundle since she walked in like it's calling her name across a crowded room. That's how the gifted always look at things they shouldn't touch. I've seen it a hundred times."

"Dessa," Rafael began.

"Don't 'Dessa' me. You want the council to move, they'll need more than a rock and a pretty story. They'll need proof. And proof means someone demonstrates what this thing actually does." She sipped her tea. "So. Which of you is it going to be?"

The silence stretched. Wren, who had been dozing in a chair by the window, opened one eye.

"The girl," Wren said simply. "It was always going to be the girl."

Mirabel's hands were cold. "I don't—I'm not sure I can control it. When I read it before, in the mountains, the letters moved. The light came off it like heat off a forge. If I do that in front of the council—"

"Then they'll know magic is real, the covenant is real, and the thing Aldric wants most in the world is sitting in their city," Dessa said flatly. "Right now half those delegates think Aldric's hunt is a political maneuver and the other half think the gifted are a fairy tale their grandparents invented to explain bad harvests. Fear moves councils, but only specific fear. The fear of something *named*."

"She's right," Rafael said quietly. He crossed the room to stand beside Mirabel—not touching her, but close, the way he'd learned to be over the weeks of the road. Near enough to anchor. Far enough to leave her the choice. "You don't have to. We can find another way."

"There isn't another way," Mirabel said. Her voice surprised her by being steady. "There's a road through Ashfen full of people running, and behind them an army, and behind the army a king who wants to own the sky. There's just this." She looked down at the oilcloth bundle. Through the fabric, faint as a heartbeat under a blanket, she could feel it waiting. It had been waiting for her for a long time, she suspected. Longer than she knew.

"All right," she said. "But if I set fire to the council chamber, I want it noted that I warned everyone."

Brannic snorted into his tea. "Girl after my own heart."

* * *

The Hall of Delegates had been a shipyard warehouse once, and it still smelled faintly of tar. Five thrones—or rather, five chairs that aspired to throneness, each carved and gilded by a different city's prideful carpenters—faced a semicircle of benches packed with merchants, guildmasters, soldiers, and the particular species of civic official whose entire talent is looking skeptical.

Mirabel stood at the center of the floor with the sigil-stone in her hands, and the hall went quiet around her the way water goes quiet before it freezes.

The delegates had argued for an hour first. She had listened to the delegate from Callowmere—a broad, red-faced man named Corvess—insist that provoking Aldric was madness. She had heard the delegate from little Saltmere, a thin woman with a sailor's weathered voice, snap back that Aldric was coming regardless and it was better to meet him holding something than holding nothing. She had watched Rafael present the stone's provenance, Wren recite the covenant's history in her fractured, circling way, and Dessa lay out intelligence maps until the table looked like a game of conquest played by a nervous god.

And still the delegate from Veymar had said, with the weary condescension of a man who has chaired many meetings: *"Proof. Show us this covenant can be reforged, or we're being asked to burn our cities on the strength of a courier's bedtime story."*

Which was how Mirabel came to be standing alone on a warehouse floor, holding a fragment of the oldest working in the world, while several hundred strangers waited to see if she was a fraud or a miracle or—most likely, in her experience—a disappointment.

*Breathe,* she told herself. *You've read harder things. You once copied Mother Osla's entire ledger of grain tariffs without weeping, and that was genuinely heroic.*

She unwrapped the stone.

It drank the lamplight the way it always did, its surface seeming both rougher and smoother than stone had any right to be. Up close, the sigil crawled across it in lines that were almost writing and almost drawing—a circuit, a sentence, a promise made in a language older than sentences. She had felt it the first time in the temple records, that jolt of recognition, like hearing your own name spoken in a crowd by someone who shouldn't know it.

Now she let herself listen.

The hall fell away. Not vanished—she could still hear the shuffling, the coughing, Corvess muttering "*is she even doing anything*" somewhere to her left—but it grew distant, unimportant, a tide going out. What remained was the stone, and the words rising through it.

She didn't read so much as *remember aloud*. That was the only way she could describe it afterward, though afterward was a long time coming. The sigil spoke itself through her, syllables that her mouth shaped without quite consulting her, and light rose off the stone in slow ribbons—gold shot through with green, the colors of the valley she'd been born in, though she couldn't have said how she knew that—and the ribbons climbed, and braided, and hung in the air above her hands like a living knot.

The hall made a sound she had never heard a large group of people make: a single collective intake of breath, sharp enough to stir the dust motes.

"*By root and river, by ink and oath,*" the sigil said through her, and her voice did not sound entirely like her voice; there were echoes in it, older voices layered beneath, founders long dead lending their timbre. "*What was sundered may be bound again. What was given may be given again.*"

The knot of light turned slowly overhead. And in its turning, Mirabel saw—or the stone showed her, or memory that wasn't hers surfaced like a drowned thing—flashes: a circle of robed figures on a mountaintop. A valley wrapped in mist. Hands pressed to stone. A woman's face, tear-streaked and resolute, mouthing words of farewell—

The woman had Mirabel's own eyes.

The vision broke. The light collapsed inward, sank back into the stone, and the silence that followed was total and ringing.

Mirabel stood very still, heart slamming, and became aware of several things at once. First: that every eye in the hall was fixed on her with an intensity that made her skin prickle. Second: that the air around her still shimmered faintly, traces of gold-green clinging to her sleeves and hair like pollen. Third: that there was no possible interpretation of the last thirty seconds under which she was not, publicly and undeniably, gifted.

Invisibility, she thought, with a strange floating calm, dies loudly after all. All these years I assumed it would go out like a candle. Instead it went out like a bonfire.

"Well," said the delegate from Veymar, into the hush. His voice cracked slightly on the word, which Mirabel found deeply satisfying. "That is... that is certainly..."

"That," said the sailor-woman from Saltmere, rising to her feet, "is the first honest thing anyone has shown this council in a year. I move we act."

Chaos, but productive chaos. Voices overlapped; the Saltmere delegate and Corvess shouted past each other; somewhere a guildmaster demanded to know whether the glowing counted as witchcraft or diplomacy. Rafael appeared at Mirabel's elbow, steadying her with a hand at her back, and Sefa pushed through the crowd with the fierce scowl she wore in place of worry.

"You're pale," Sefa said.

"I'm fine."

"You're *pale*, which is different from fine, sit down before you fall down." Sefa steered her to a bench with the unstoppable authority of a small person used to managing a larger brother. "Also, for the record, that was the most terrifying and impressive thing I've ever seen, and I once watched Ilo talk his way out of a marriage proposal from a pirate captain."

"It was mutual," Ilo called from nearby. "The captain was lovely. It just wasn't the right time."

Mirabel laughed—a shaky, disbelieving sound—and pressed her palms against her knees to stop their trembling. Around her the council argued itself toward war, and she sat in the middle of it, marked, exposed, visible to hundreds of strangers.

And yet.

It should have felt like dying. For twenty-six years, being seen had meant danger; being known had meant the collector's pen moving across a registry, a name entered into a column of the condemned. She had built her whole life on the principle that safety was the same thing as smallness.

But sitting here, with Sefa's shoulder warm against hers and Rafael watching her with an expression she couldn't name and Brannic giving her an enormous approving nod from across the aisle, she found that exposure had a strange warmth in it. Like stepping out of shade into sun. Dangerous, yes. But also—light.

*They see me,* she thought. *And the world hasn't ended. Imagine that.*

* * *

The council voted at dusk. Four cities in favor, one abstaining—Veymar, whose delegate departed with the sour dignity of a man planning his memoirs. Callowmere would arm. Saltmere would send its fleet. Messengers would ride tonight to the remaining free cities with word of what had been seen in the hall: the covenant was real, and it could be reforged, and there existed a reader who could do the reading.

The celebration in Dessa's safehouse that night was modest by any reasonable standard—watered wine, hard cheese, Brannic attempting a song about a tinker's daughter that lost its tune twice and gained a new verse each time—but Mirabel found her throat tight through all of it. Found family, she thought, watching Wren doze by the fire and Ilo re-enact the pirate captain story with gestures increasingly libelous to all involved. It isn't made in a moment. It's made in moments. This is one of them.

Rafael found her at the window, looking out at the harbor lights.

"You did it," he said.

"We did it. You carried the stone across half a kingdom. I just read it out loud."

"You read it out loud in front of three hundred skeptics while glowing like a festival lantern. Don't minimize." He hesitated, then, quieter: "When the light came up—you saw something. I saw your face change."

Mirabel's fingers tightened on the windowsill. Should she tell him? A woman's face, wearing her eyes. A founder's farewell. It might mean nothing. It might mean everything. It might connect to the hollow years in her childhood that Mother Osla had gone quiet about whenever asked, the missing seasons between her sixth summer and her ninth that no record explained.

"I saw..." she began.

The door burst open.

One of Dessa's runners stood in it, a boy of perhaps fourteen, chest heaving, harbor spray frozen in his hair. His eyes went around the room and found Dessa first, then Rafael, and whatever he saw in their faces made Mirabel's stomach drop before he said a word.

"Boat came in from the south coast," the boy gasped. "Fisherman. Says he passed an army on the coast road at dawn. Banners of the crowned hand." He swallowed. "Aldric's not marching on the free cities next week. He's marching now. Three days. Maybe four."

The cheese and wine and laughter died in the silence. Brannic set down his cup with a click that sounded, in the quiet, like a door closing.

"Three days," Rafael repeated softly. He looked at Mirabel, and she saw in his face the same arithmetic she was already doing: the Sundered Spire was twelve days' hard travel east. The vault beneath it. The ritual. Everything they'd come this far to attempt.

Three days to reach a place that needed twelve.

Wren opened her eyes by the fire, and the old woman's voice, when it came, was thin and clear as a wire.

"Then we'd best find a shorter road," she said. "And I know one. Though I'll warn you now—it's a road I swore never to walk again."
