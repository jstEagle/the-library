## 11. Hunted in the Lantern Streets

Sefa had always known which half of herself was the loud one.

She and Ilo shared one gift between them — that was how it worked, how it had always worked since they were children in the salt marshes. She carried the seeing: the sense of where a thing *was*, the weight of it, the direction of its going. Ilo carried the moving: the small nudges, the pushes, the way a coin could leap from a pickpocket's palm back into an honest man's pocket if Ilo thought hard enough at it. Between them they made one whole person, split inconveniently across two bodies, and Sefa had spent twenty-two years being the half that noticed trouble coming.

So when she felt it — three somethings heavy with iron and intent sliding through the Lantern District like knives through water — she didn't need to see them to know what they were.

"Ilo." She caught her brother's sleeve as they came out of the tinker's stall, Brannic's mended kettle still warm under her arm. "We're leaving."

Ilo followed her gaze to nothing at all. He couldn't feel them; he never could. That was the bargain of their gift — she saw the river, he moved the water. "Leaving where? We just got here. Brannic said there'd be fried dough."

"Fried dough later. Crown agents now. Three of them, coming up Cooper's Lane, and they're not shopping."

The color went out of Ilo's face all at once, the way it did. He was the gentler half of them, always had been. "Mirabel?"

"Mirabel," Sefa confirmed grimly, because of course it was Mirabel. It had been Mirabel since Callowmere's council hall, since the sigil-stone had flared like a small sun in her hands in front of two hundred strangers and every spy in the free cities had gone home to write letters. Their quiet scribe, their ink-and-paper ghost, who had spent her whole life learning to be invisible and had been made visible in the worst possible room in the world.

They found her exactly where Wren had said she'd be — the scriptorium annex off the Temple of Small Mercies, hunched over Dessa's copied pages with Rafael standing guard beside her and looking, as usual, like a man pretending very hard that he wasn't terrified.

"Sefa?" Mirabel looked up, and something in Sefa's face must have told her everything, because she was on her feet before Sefa said a word. "How close?"

"Cooper's Lane. Ten minutes if they're slow. They're not slow."

Rafael moved like the soldier he'd been before he was a courier — hand to his knife, then away from it, because knives against crown agents in a crowded temple district was a good way to get everyone hanged. "Back ways," he said. "Dessa showed me. Rooftops."

"You want to take the *scribe* up the rooftops," Sefa said.

"I want to take Mirabel somewhere they can't follow," Rafael said, and there was something underneath his voice that Sefa recognized, because she heard it in Ilo's voice every time someone threatened their little ragged family. It was the sound of a person deciding, quietly and completely, that they would rather die than let a thing happen.

Well. Sefa knew that sound. She'd been making it since she was nine years old.

* * *

The rooftops of Callowmere were a country unto themselves — a sloping nation of slate and clay tile, strung with laundry lines and lantern chains, ruled by cats and the occasional ambitious pigeon. The Lantern District earned its name at dusk, when ten thousand paper lamps bloomed along the eaves for the Festival of Small Lights, and tonight the festival was only two days off, so the lamplighters were already testing their strings. The whole city glowed gold and rose above them as they ran.

Mirabel ran better than Sefa expected. Not well — she stumbled twice, caught once by Rafael and once by Sefa herself — but she kept going, breath sawing, skirts hiked and knotted at her knees, the sigil-stone wrapped in oilcloth and strapped flat against her ribs beneath her coat. She'd refused to leave it behind even for speed. Sefa understood. You didn't leave your heart in a drawer just because it was heavy.

"They've split," Sefa gasped, halfway across a courtyard of chimney pots. The seeing washed through her, cold and precise. "Two on our trail, one circling ahead — he knows where we're going, Rafael, he's heading for the south stair—"

"The aqueduct, then." Rafael changed direction without breaking stride. "There's a gap behind the dye-works. We jump it."

"We *what*?"

"It's four feet."

"It's four feet over a *courtyard full of festival crowds*, you absolute—"

"Sefa." Ilo's voice, from behind, winded but steady. He'd taken the rear without being asked, because that was Ilo — he'd give away his half of the bread, his half of the blanket, his half of anything, and call it fairness. "It's fine. I'll be right behind you."

He was always right behind her. That was the whole architecture of their lives.

They jumped the gap. Sefa went first because she was furious, and fury made her stupid and brave in equal measure; she cleared the dye-works roof and landed rolling among the drying racks, and Rafael came after with Mirabel's wrist locked in his grip, and they were both up and running again before Sefa finished counting heartbeats.

Ilo jumped last.

Sefa felt it happen the way you feel a stitch pull loose in a hem — a wrongness, small and then enormous. The third agent wasn't circling anymore. He was below them, in the mouth of the alley behind the dye-works, and he had a crossbow, and he wasn't aiming at Rafael or Mirabel or her.

He was aiming at the easiest target. The one still in the air.

"*Ilo!*"

She saw it all in the half-second the seeing gave her: the bolt's path, bright as a drawn line; Ilo twisting mid-leap, doing the only thing his half of their gift could do — pushing, shoving at the bolt with that desperate invisible strength, knocking it wide —

And the lamp chain.

The bolt missed him. The lamp chain did not. It snapped under the impact of his shoulder as he came down, and the whole glittering string of festival lights — forty paper lanterns and the iron wire that held them — came down across the rooftop edge, and Ilo came down with it, into the dark between the dye-works and the wall.

* * *

Afterward, Sefa would remember it in pieces, the way shattered things are remembered.

Mirabel screaming his name. Rafael already moving, dropping down the drainpipe with a recklessness that would have made Sefa laugh on any other night. Herself standing frozen on the rooftop with her brother's heartbeat flickering in her sight like a guttering candle — because that was the cruel joke of their gift, that she could feel exactly where he was and exactly how faint he was getting, and do precisely nothing about either.

Then she was moving too, down the pipe, into the alley that smelled of dye-vats and hot copper, and Ilo was crumpled against the wall with Rafael kneeling over him, and there was blood, so much blood, dark on the pale cobbles.

"Ilo. Hey. Hey, look at me, you idiot, look at me—" She dropped to her knees and took his face in her hands, and his eyes found hers, unfocused, trying to smile.

"'m fine," he said. "Mostly fine. Some fine."

"You fell off a *building*."

"I pushed the bolt first." A bubble of red at his lip. "Did you see? I pushed it. Told you... told you I'd protect the rear."

"You absolute *donkey*," Sefa said, and her voice cracked straight down the middle, and she pressed her forehead to his and felt, through whatever thread connected their halves, the terrible thinness of him. His half of the gift guttered. Her own half answered, flaring wild and useless — she could see everything, every grain of sand in the alley, every drop of blood, and none of it would lift him, none of it would hold him together, because moving things had never been hers.

That had always been his job. He was the one who moved things.

"Ribs," Rafael was saying, hands quick and sure, peeling back the torn coat. "Two, maybe three, broken. His leg — Sefa, I need you to hold his leg still while I splint it. And we have maybe two minutes before those agents find this alley."

"Then you'd better work fast."

"Sefa." Mirabel's voice. Sefa hadn't even registered her arriving — the quiet scribe, silent as ever, kneeling on Ilo's other side with her sleeves shoved up and the oilcloth-wrapped stone pressed between them like a table around which a family might gather. But her hands were steady, tearing strips from her own petticoat without a second thought, and her eyes when they met Sefa's were wet and absolutely ferocious. "Tell me what to do."

"He can't be moved walking. Not with the leg." Sefa heard her own voice go flat and practical, the voice she used when feeling would kill her. "Rafael, is there a cart, a door, anything—"

"Dessa's safehouse is six streets north. There's a laundry cart route — the guild runs it at dusk." Rafael tied off the splint with his teeth and his hands. "If we can get him to the cart."

"If," said Sefa.

"If," agreed Rafael.

Above them, at the roofline, a silhouette paused against the glow of the lanterns. Then another. The agents had found the broken chain.

* * *

What happened next, Sefa would think about for the rest of her life, though she'd never quite manage to explain it.

They should have been caught. Two armed crown agents, trained killers, against a wounded man, a soldier, a scribe, and a girl kneeling in the blood of her twin. The math was simple. The math said they were dead.

But Mirabel stood up.

She stood up slowly, the way a tide comes in, and she unwrapped the sigil-stone from its oilcloth, and she held it out in front of her in both hands, and she said, in a voice Sefa had never heard from her — low, level, older than her years — "You don't want to do this."

The nearest agent laughed. Sefa saw it coming — saw the knife-hand shift, the weight transfer, the whole bright geometry of violence — and then the stone *flared*.

Not light, exactly. Something older than light. The lanterns along the alley walls guttered all at once, and the shadows bent toward Mirabel like iron filings toward a lodestone, and the agent's knife simply — stopped. Halfway through its arc. As if the air itself had decided against it.

Sefa felt it through her half of the gift: a vast stillness, a pressure like the bottom of the sea. And threaded through it, impossibly, she felt *Ilo* — his faint, guttering half answering the stone, pushing, the way he'd pushed the bolt, except now he was pushing at the agents' courage, at their certainty, shoving it sideways until it broke.

The agents ran. Sefa would never know entirely why, and neither would they. They dropped their weapons and fled up the alley as if the dark itself had teeth, and afterward one of them, when Dessa's people finally pulled his confession out of him, would only say: *it looked at me. The stone looked at me, and it knew everything I'd ever done.*

Mirabel swayed. Rafael caught her. The stone dimmed and went quiet in her hands, and she looked down at it, and then at Ilo, and her face was white as fresh paper.

"That was me," she whispered. "That was — I don't know what that was."

"That," Sefa said, hauling Ilo's arm across her shoulders, "was family business. Now help me carry my brother, and fall apart later. Everyone falls apart later. It's house rules."

* * *

The laundry cart smelled of lye and lavender, and Ilo complained about it the entire six streets, which was how Sefa knew he was going to live. Complaining was his strongest organ.

"Lavender," he muttered, gray-faced under the folded linens, as Sefa rode the cart's tailboard with her hand hidden under the cloth and pressed to his wrist. "Of all the — we're fugitives, Sef. Fugitives should smell of *danger*. Of smoke and horse sweat."

"You smell of lavender and blood. Very romantic. The ballads will love it."

"Will there be ballads?"

"There will be ballads, and in them you will have fallen from a much greater height, and I will have fought off six agents single-handedly."

"Five," said Ilo. "Leave some dignity for the rest of us."

Rafael walked beside the cart with his hood up, and Mirabel sat on the tailboard opposite Sefa, holding Ilo's uninjured hand, and nobody spoke for a while. The lantern streets slid past, gold and rose and utterly indifferent, festival preparations humming in every window. Somewhere a fiddler tuned up for the Festival of Small Lights. Somewhere a mother called a child home to supper.

In the safehouse, Dessa's healer — a brisk woman named Corla with forearms like a blacksmith — set Ilo's ribs and splinted the leg properly and pronounced him "broken but stubborn, which in my experience is survivable." Brannic arrived within the hour, having heard everything from a fishwife who'd seen the lamp chain fall, and filled the doorway of the sickroom with his bulk and his worry, and Wren arrived after him, ancient and sharp-eyed, and stood looking at Ilo's bandages with an expression Sefa couldn't read.

"Chain snapped clean," Wren said. "Festival wire doesn't snap clean. It's braided bronze. Someone cut it ahead of time."

The room went very quiet.

"They knew the route," Rafael said slowly. "They knew the rooftops. They knew the festival wiring." He looked at Mirabel. "This wasn't a patrol that got lucky. This was planned. Someone gave them your habits."

"Someone in Callowmere," Brannic rumbled.

"Someone who watched us since the council," said Wren, and her old eyes went to the sigil-stone where it lay wrapped on the table, and then — briefly, strangely — to Mirabel, with something that looked almost like grief.

Sefa sat on the edge of Ilo's cot and held his hand and felt, through the thin bright thread of their shared gift, her brother's half flickering back toward its old warmth, slow as dawn. Outside, the lanterns of Callowmere bloomed in their thousands, beautiful and blind, and somewhere among them walked the people who had cut the wire.

"They'll come again," she said quietly. Not a question.

"They'll come again," Rafael agreed.

Ilo squeezed her fingers, weak but there. "Next time," he said, "I'm pushing *them* off the roof."

And despite everything — the blood, the fear, the traitor hiding somewhere in the golden city — Sefa laughed, because that was Ilo, and because laughing was the only weapon she had left tonight that didn't weigh anything at all.
