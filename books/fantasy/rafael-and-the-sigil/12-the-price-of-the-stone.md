## 12. The Price of the Stone

The stone did not like being studied at night.

Mirabel had discovered this on the second evening after Callowmere, when she'd unwrapped it from its oilcloth by candlelight and the sigils along its edge had dimmed, sulking, like a cat withdrawing under a bed. By daylight it woke again—slowly, grudgingly—and if she held it in both hands and breathed the way she'd once breathed over ink that listened, the carvings began to move.

Not move exactly. *Unfold.* The grooves deepened and branched, splitting into finer lines than any chisel could have cut, until the whole face of the stone resembled frost climbing a windowpane. And within those lines, faint as breath on glass, words appeared.

She had copied three of them into her notebook before dawn on the first day. Six by the end of the second. It was slow, painstaking work—the sigil-stone gave up its language the way a miser gives up coins, one at a time, each one wrung loose only after hours of patient attention. But Mirabel had spent eleven years copying tax ledgers in a temple where invisibility was survival. Patience was the one weapon she had never been without.

"You're doing the thing again," Sefa said, dropping onto the bench beside her in the caravan's morning camp. "The thing where you forget bread exists."

"I don't forget bread exists."

"You forgot bread existed for two days straight back on the mountain road. Brannic had to put a roll *in your hand*." Sefa tore the loaf in question in half and pressed the larger piece into Mirabel's palm with the imperiousness of someone who had decided, unilaterally, that keeping their new friend fed was now her personal responsibility. "Eat. Ilo says you get a crease right here—" she tapped the space between Mirabel's brows "—when you've been reading too long, and it makes you look like a disapproving owl."

"I do not have a crease."

"You have a crease," Ilo called from across the fire, where he was mending a harness strap with his teeth holding the needle. Both twins said it in unison, which was somehow worse: "*You have a crease.*"

Mirabel ate the bread. It was easier than arguing with twins who shared one gift between them and, apparently, one opinion about her eyebrows.

* * *

It was Wren who changed everything, on the fourth day, though not in the way anyone expected.

The old woman had taken to sitting near Mirabel while she worked—not reading over her shoulder, which would have been impossible anyway since the stone showed its words only to Mirabel, but simply *near*, whittling a piece of ashwood into something that was either a bird or a spoon. Wren's memory was a moth-eaten blanket; everyone knew it, most of all Wren herself. She remembered the last covenant the way you remember a house you lived in as a child: the shape of it, the feeling of it, almost none of the facts.

But that afternoon, as Mirabel coaxed the forty-seventh word free of the stone—a word she had transcribed phonetically because she couldn't yet read it—Wren went very still.

"Say that again," she said.

"It's just a sound. *Vess-ah-reen.* It doesn't mean anything to me."

"*Vessareen.*" Wren set down her knife and the half-bird-half-spoon. Her clouded eyes had gone sharp. "That's a founder's name. That's *Ilyane Vessareen*. She was the youngest of them. The one who—" Wren's brow furrowed, and Mirabel watched, with a familiar sick lurch, as the thought slipped through the holes in the old woman's mind like water through a net. "The one who... gave..."

"Gave what?"

"I don't—" Wren pressed her knuckles against her mouth. "I knew this. I *knew* this. I remember her standing on the Spire steps with her hair undone, and I remember everyone crying, and I remember thinking *she's so young to pay so much*, and I cannot remember what she paid."

The fire crackled. Somewhere behind them, Brannic was arguing cheerfully with his own mule.

"Wren," Mirabel said carefully, "you told us you were alive when the covenant was sealed. You told us you *watched* it happen."

"I did." Wren's voice had gone thin. "Or I dreamed I did. Or someone put the watching inside me like a stone in a shoe. Child, I have been carrying that memory for sixty years and I no longer know if it is mine."

Mirabel looked down at the notebook open on her knee. At the column of half-transcribed words. At the name *Vessareen* sitting there in her own careful hand.

And beneath it, in the stone's unfolding script, the next line had already begun to surface—as if the stone had been waiting for someone to ask the right question before showing what came after the names.

*Each gave what they loved best kept,* it read. *Each gave what they could least spare.*

* * *

She didn't sleep that night. She sat up past the watch rotation, wrapped in Brannic's spare cloak, and she made herself read.

This was the discipline of it, she understood now. The stone wasn't locked. It was *grieving*. Every word had to be coaxed out gently, respectfully, the way you'd speak to someone telling you the worst day of their life. And the worst days were all it had to tell.

By the time the sky went grey at the edges, Mirabel knew how the covenant had been forged.

Seven founders. Seven gifts, pooled and woven into a single working strong enough to wall magic out of Vellharrow forever—to make of one valley a sanctuary where the gifted could not be hunted because their power simply *could not follow them there*. That much, Wren's fractured legend had gotten right.

What the legend had never said—what perhaps no one living had ever known—was the price.

Magic that great could not be built from nothing. It needed material. And the only substance in all the world dense enough, rich enough, *true* enough to anchor a covenant between realms was memory. Lived memory. The accumulated weight of a whole human life, given up willingly, poured into the working like ore into a crucible.

Each founder had surrendered their past. Not their future—their *past*. Everything they had been before the Spire: childhoods, loves, griefs, the faces of their mothers, the names of their first homes. They had walked up those steps whole and walked down them hollow, and the covenant had drunk what they carried, and Vellharrow had become safe.

*Ilyane Vessareen,* Mirabel wrote, *gave her childhood.*

Her pen stopped.

She read the line again. Read the passage around it, the whole terrible paragraph, three times through, because her mind kept sliding off it the way a boot slides off ice.

*Ilyane Vessareen, youngest of the seven, gave her childhood entire—every year of it, every hour, every face. This was the keystone. Without the child's portion freely given, the working fails.*

Freely given. Except—

Except that Mirabel had no childhood. She had woken to herself at eleven years old in the temple infirmary at Vellharrow with a fever, an unexplained debt in her family's name, and a blank where twelve years of living should have been. Mother Osla had told her it was the fever. The other scribes had assumed it was trauma. Mirabel herself had spent years constructing theories and then, exhausted, constructing a personality out of what remained: a girl with no yesterday, learning to be invisible, learning to be useful, learning to press the missing years down flat whenever they ached.

And here, in a dead woman's covenant, written in a language only she could read, was a line that said *the child's portion, freely given.*

"No," she said aloud, to nobody, to the cold grey morning. "No, that's—that's coincidence. That's a pattern my mind is making because I want—"

Because she wanted what? An explanation? A reason? Some grand cosmic machinery to account for the hole inside her, rather than the alternative, which was that she was simply broken in an ordinary, meaningless way?

But the stone wasn't finished.

The words kept coming now, faster, as though her horror had loosened something, as though grief were also a key. She transcribed with shaking hands:

*The covenant holds so long as its anchors hold. What is given may be reclaimed only by the giver or the giver's blood. Should the working fail, that which was surrendered returns to the world—and the valley forgets itself.*

*The reader of this stone shall be of the founders' line, marked by absence. She will know the price in her own body. She will know it as a room in a house she cannot enter.*

*A room in a house she cannot enter.*

Mirabel set down the pen very carefully, because if she didn't set it down carefully she was going to throw it into the fire.

* * *

Brannic found her there at first light, still sitting in the same position, the notebook closed on her lap and the stone in her hands like something she was afraid to hold and afraid to put down.

He didn't ask what was wrong. He was Brannic; he never asked first. He simply lowered his enormous frame onto the log beside her, handed her a tin cup of tea that was mostly milk and sugar in the way he believed tea ought to be, and waited. He was good at waiting. Fifteen years of tinkering taught a man that most things opened eventually, and the ones that didn't weren't worth forcing.

"My childhood," Mirabel said finally. Her voice sounded strange to her, flat and far away. "The years I lost. The fever, the blank from before I was eleven."

"Aye?"

"They weren't lost." She turned the stone over. The sigils caught the sunrise and threw it back in tiny red sparks. "They were *taken*. Deliberately. By the people who founded the covenant. I was—I must have been—I don't know, a vessel? A keeper? Someone's daughter, at minimum, and they emptied me out and used what was inside me to build a wall around a valley, and then everyone let me grow up believing I was ill." She laughed, and hated the sound of it. "My whole life, Brannic. Eleven years of scrubbing ledgers and hiding what I am and being grateful to be overlooked—all of it built on top of a theft. I'm not a person who lost her memories. I'm a *receipt*. I'm proof of payment. Somebody spent my childhood like coin and I've been living in the change."

Brannic drank his tea. He took his time about it, and Mirabel realized he was giving himself time too—time to find the right thing, because Brannic always found the right thing eventually, though he approached words the way he approached stubborn locks: slowly, patiently, with great respect for how easily they broke.

"When the purge came to Corrow Fen," he said at last, "they burned our street on a Tuesday. Ordinary day. I'd kissed my wife that morning and complained about the porridge." His big hands turned the cup. "For a long time after, I wanted to know why. Wanted it like a man wants water. Who ordered it, what my boy's name got written in some ledger, whether anyone was punished. I thought if I could just get the *why*, the wound would close."

"And?"

"And it doesn't." He looked at her, and his eyes were wet and steady. "The why doesn't heal anything, lass. It just gives the pain a shape. But a shape—" he tapped his chest, twice, over the heart "—a shape you can carry. Right now yours hasn't got one. That's the cruelest part of what was done to you. Not even the theft. The theft left no shape."

Mirabel looked down at the stone. "There's a shape now."

"There's a shape now," Brannic agreed. "Ugly one. Heavy one. But it's *yours*, and knowing is yours, and nobody—not Osla, not the founders, not whatever god let this happen—can take the knowing back out of you. You understand? They took your yesterdays. They can't touch your tomorrows. Those are the only ones that were ever really yours anyway."

From across the camp, Ilo's voice floated over: "If you two are done having feelings, the mule has eaten the map."

"He ate the *corner* of the map," Sefa corrected. "The corner with Callowmere on it. So technically we now live in a world without cities."

Despite everything—because of everything—Mirabel felt her mouth twitch. Brannic squeezed her shoulder once, hard, and went off to negotiate with the mule, and Mirabel sat alone with the stone and the sunrise and the shape of her grief, which was heavy, and ugly, and hers.

* * *

Rafael found her at midmorning. He'd been avoiding her, she'd noticed—or not avoiding, exactly, but orbiting. Keeping a careful distance ever since Callowmere, ever since the council hall, ever since the night agents had chased them across the rooftops and Ilo had come home bleeding. He hovered at the edge of her days like a man afraid of stepping on fresh-laid floorboards.

"The twins say you figured something out," he said. "They also say you look like you fought a war with your own head and lost."

"The twins talk too much."

"The twins saved your life three nights ago. They've earned talking rights in perpetuity." He crouched beside her, and up close she could see he hadn't slept either; there were shadows under his eyes that matched her own. "What does the stone say, Mirabel?"

And there it was—the question underneath the question. Because Rafael had carried this stone across half a kingdom. He had bled for it, starved for it, brought it to a stranger in a temple valley because of a prophecy or an instruction or a hope she still didn't fully understand. He had a right to know what it said. He had, possibly, known more about what it would say than he'd ever admitted.

She thought of the line in the founders' script: *the reader shall know the price in her own body.* She thought of the way Rafael watched her sometimes—not with pity, but with *anticipation of grief*, the way you watch someone walk toward a cliff you've already seen the edge of.

She could tell him. All of it. The founders, the memories, the stolen childhood, the room in the house she couldn't enter.

Or she could ask him first the question that had been forming since dawn, quietly, in the place where hurt curdles into suspicion:

"Rafael," she said, "when you gave me this stone—when you begged sanctuary at the temple gate—did you know what reading it would cost me?"

The wind moved through the camp. Somewhere, the mule brayed. Rafael looked at her, and opened his mouth, and for the first time since she'd met him—bleeding, desperate, brave—Rafael had nothing to say.
