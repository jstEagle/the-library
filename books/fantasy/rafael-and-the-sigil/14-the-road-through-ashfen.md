## 14. The Road Through Ashfen

Brannic had a rule about burned country: you didn't look at the horizon too long, and you didn't count the chimneys.

He'd broken both rules once, eleven years ago, walking out of the Kettlelands after the first purge with nothing but his cart, his tools, and a smell of smoke in his beard that took three years to wash out. He'd counted every chimney between his village and the river. Forty-one. He could still name them if he let himself, which was why he never let himself.

Now he sat at the front of his wagon with the reins loose in his fists and watched Ashfen unroll ahead of them like a page someone had held too close to a candle. Black fields. Gray fields. Fields where the ash had settled into the furrows and made the earth look like it had been salted by some enormous, careless hand. And chimneys—no, he corrected himself firmly—*structures*. Standing alone in the ruins of farmhouses, patient as headstones.

"You're doing the face," said Sefa from beside him.

"I don't have a face."

"Everyone has a face. Yours is the one that looks like you're chewing on something that insulted your mother."

Brannic grunted. "Your brother's the one who reads people. You're supposed to be the one who throws things."

"I can do both." She hopped down off the wagon seat before he could stop her, landing light as a cat in the ash, and jogged back along the line to where Ilo walked with Wren. Brannic watched her go and felt the old ache shift into something almost bearable. The twins did that to him. They were proof, he supposed, that the world kept making things worth saving even while it burned the rest.

The caravan had grown used to moving quiet through Ashfen. No singing—Ilo had been the singer, and since Callowmere, since the rooftop and the blood and the long fevered nights, Ilo hadn't sung. He walked now with his arm bound against his chest and his gift banked low, the way you'd bank a fire you couldn't afford to feed. Sefa carried their shared magic for both of them, and she carried it carefully, like a woman balancing a full jug across ice.

Ahead, Mirabel walked at Rafael's shoulder, and behind them Wren muttered her way through another version of the covenant legend that contradicted the version she'd told two nights ago. Brannic had stopped keeping track of which parts were true. What struck him, watching the scribe girl pick her way over a fallen fence rail, was how she'd changed since Callowmere. Before the city, Mirabel Dunmore had moved like a woman trying not to be seen—shoulders drawn in, eyes down, apologizing to the ground for standing on it. Now she walked like someone with somewhere to be. Still quiet. But the quiet had a spine in it.

She'd also, he'd noticed, started sitting near him at the fires. Not next to him—that would have been too much, too soon—but near. Close enough that when the wind turned cold she could lean her shoulder against the wagon wheel he leaned against. He'd spent enough years on the road to know what that meant. It meant *I've decided you're safe*, and it was a decision he intended to be worthy of, or die trying.

"There," Rafael said, pointing. "Smoke."

Everyone stopped pretending they weren't looking at the horizon.

It wasn't the gray smear of an old burn. This was a thin dark thread rising off the road maybe half a mile ahead, and beneath it, small with distance, came the sound that Brannic hated most in all the world: carts. Wheels. Voices. The particular ragged music of people going somewhere because staying had stopped being possible.

"Refugees," Dessa's runner had called them, back in Callowmere, when the reports first came north. A tidy word. It made it sound like weather.

"We should pass wide," Rafael said. "If Aldric's vanguard is pushing this far—"

"If we pass wide," Brannic said, "we'll miss whatever news is on that road, and we'll teach ourselves that other people's trouble is catching." He climbed down and began unhitching the mule. "Besides. I've got stock nobody's bought in three provinces. Time somebody did."

Rafael looked at him. "You're going to sell things. To refugees."

"I'm going to *give* things to refugees and charge them nothing, and if any of them has information about what's burning and what isn't, then yes, I'm going to trade for it like an honest man." Brannic pulled the canvas back off the wagon bed, revealing the orderly chaos of his trade: kettles, nails, needles, lamp oil, dried apples, a crate of tin whistles he'd been meaning to melt down for solder stock. "A tinker who won't stop at a column of refugees is no tinker at all. He's just a man with a heavy cart."

Mirabel had come up quietly, the way she did. "My mother's family came through a purge," she said. "Before Vellharrow. I don't remember it." Her hand went briefly to her sternum, that little gesture she made whenever the missing years brushed against her, like touching a bruise to check if it still hurt. "But Osla told me once that what refugees need most isn't food. It's somebody who treats them like they're still people."

Brannic looked at her a long moment. "Your mother raised you right, whoever she was."

Something flickered across Mirabel's face—grief, or its ghost—and then she nodded and went to wake Wren.

* * *

The refugee column was worse up close than it had been from a distance, which was the way of such things.

They filled the road for a quarter mile: farmers with their goods strapped to their backs because there was nothing left to pull a cart; a grandmother driving two goats and four children; a man carrying a door. An actual door, balanced on his shoulder, and when Brannic caught his eye the man just shrugged, as if to say *it was my mother's door and the house is gone but the door isn't.* Brannic understood completely. He'd left the Kettlelands with a hammer that had belonged to his father, and he'd have carried that hammer through fire itself.

He set up shop right in the middle of the road, which scandalized Rafael until it didn't. Within an hour the wagon was surrounded. Brannic worked the crowd the way he'd worked a hundred market squares—loud, warm, unfair in everyone's favor. Lamp oil for the grandmother, no argument accepted. Needles for a young woman whose hands shook so badly she could barely hold the packet, and who flinched when he pressed it into her palm, and who flinched harder when he said gently, "Soldier's done that to you?"

"Aldric's men," she said. "In Corvane. They took my brother because the well water bent around him when he washed. Bent around him. Like he was a stone in a stream." She laughed, a terrible small laugh. "He was twelve."

Brannic kept his hands busy so his face wouldn't do anything unforgivable. "Take the oil too," he said. "And the apples. Go on, before I change my mind and become a respectable merchant."

Word traveled down the column faster than the column itself moved. By midday he'd given away half his stock and learned more than he wanted to know: Aldric's army had taken Corvane without a fight, then Rillwater, and was moving on the river towns. The purges weren't random anymore—they were systematic, village by village, using lists. Names written down in advance. Someone in each town always seemed to know exactly who was gifted, and the soldiers arrived with those names already in hand.

"Registries," Mirabel said quietly, when Brannic passed the detail along to the others that evening. She'd gone pale. "Temple registries. Crown registries. Every valley keeps them. Debt ledgers, birth rolls—" She stopped. Brannic saw her arrive at the thought the way you watch someone walk into a door. "Every scribe in every temple has been writing the hunt's paperwork for years without knowing it. We built the list ourselves."

"That's not yours to carry," Rafael said.

"Isn't it? I copied ledgers for nine years. My handwriting might be on one of those lists. Might have *made* one of those lists." She wrapped her arms around herself. "You can hide from soldiers. It's harder to hide from ink."

Brannic cleared his throat. "Well," he said, "then it's fortunate we're traveling with the finest ink-fighter in seven provinces," and was rewarded by the sight of Mirabel Dunmore, terror-stricken scribe, snorting with laughter despite herself.

* * *

That night they camped in the shell of a waystation inn, one of the few structures in Ashfen still standing, though why it had been spared was anyone's guess. Its roof was mostly intact. Its hearth drew. Somebody had scratched *GONE TO MARROWFORD, GOD WILLING* into the bar, and under it, in different hands, a dozen replies: *WE PASSED SAFE, BLESSINGS ON YOUR HOUSE.* *MARROWFORD HAS FALLEN, GO EAST.* *STAYED. COULDN'T LEAVE HER GRAVE.*

Brannic read them all twice, then went out to check the picket lines, because reading them a third time would have undone him.

He found Mirabel out there already, sitting on the mounting block with her knees drawn up, staring east toward the black line of the hills. She didn't startle when he sat down beside her, which told him how far she'd come. Three weeks ago she'd have jumped out of her skin.

"My father used to sit like that," Brannic said, before he could stop himself. He almost never talked about the Kettlelands. "On the fence after supper. Watching the road. I asked him once what he was looking for, and he said, 'Nothing, lad. Just making sure it stays empty.'" He turned a nail over in his fingers. "Then one autumn it didn't stay empty."

Mirabel said nothing. That was another thing he'd learned about her—she knew when silence was the kind thing.

"They came for the miller's daughter first. Sweet girl, could make bread rise by humming at it. Nobody thought anything of it, back then—it was before the edicts, before anybody decided a gift was a weapon. Then the king's men came with a list, and her name was on it, and my son—" His voice did something he didn't authorize. He waited it out. "My son stood up in the square and said *she's ten years old, you can't take her,* and they wrote his name down too. For the trouble of it. My wife went with him. Wouldn't be parted." He looked at the eastern dark. "Forty-one chimneys between our village and the river. I counted every one, walking out. Told myself I'd come back when things were better. Things were never better. Things got worse, and then they got different-worse, and here we are."

"I'm sorry," Mirabel whispered.

"Don't be sorry. Be angry. Sorry goes soft. Anger keeps." He glanced sideways at her. "You've got a family in that inn, girl. Did you notice? A tinker, twins, a mad old woman, and a courier who'd walk into a bonfire for you and pretend afterward it was about the stone. That's a family. I know because I've had two kinds now, and they feel the same from the inside."

Mirabel's eyes were shining. "I had a mother once. I think. There's a shape of her, sometimes, when I'm nearly asleep. Warm hands. Smelling of lamp oil." She laughed wetly. "Maybe that's why I liked your wagon from the start."

"Lamp oil attracts moths and tinkers," Brannic said gravely. "It's a known hazard of the trade."

She laughed properly then, and he put his arm around her shoulders, brief and gruff, the way you'd steady a load on a rough road, and she let him, and neither of them said anything else, because nothing needed saying.

* * *

They broke camp before dawn, because a boy from the refugee column had come through at dusk with word that outriders had been seen at the Ashfen ford—crown riders, asking questions, showing a paper with names on it.

Brannic drove the first stretch himself, Mirabel beside him on the bench because the wagon bed was full of sleeping twins and Wren talked in her sleep and Rafael claimed the motion made him ill, which Brannic privately suspected was a lie of great convenience. The road climbed away from the burned flats into foothills where the fire hadn't reached, and the dawn came up gray-gold over the ridges, and for a mile or two it was almost peaceful.

Then Mirabel stiffened. "Brannic. The stone."

He followed her gaze. In the wagon bed, wrapped in its wool, the sigil-stone was glowing—a faint pulse, like a heartbeat seen through a blanket. As he watched, it pulsed again, faster.

"It's never done that," Mirabel said. "Not since the Spire vision. Something's—" She pressed her palm above it without touching, frowning. "It's pulling. East. Toward the Sundered Spire." Her voice dropped. "And Brannic—there's something else. When it pulses, I can almost *hear* it. Like a voice under water. It keeps saying the same thing."

"What thing?"

She looked up at him, and in the gray-gold light her face was the face of someone hearing their own name called from very far away.

"*Come read me. Before the king does.*"

Behind them, far down the valley, a horn sounded—once, twice, a third time. Not a hunting horn. A muster horn. And on the eastern ridge, small as toys, rode a line of horsemen wearing the black and gold of King Aldric.

Brannic snapped the reins. "Hold on to something, lass. Family sticks together—but today, we run."
