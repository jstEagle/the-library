## 15. Aldric's Summons

King Aldric Vane did not sleep in palaces. He slept in war tents, because palaces made men soft and soft men lost kingdoms, and he had not clawed his way to a crown only to lose it to feather beds and flatterers.

He slept badly anyway.

The tent was large enough for a map table, a campaign chest, and the particular silence that followed him everywhere now—the silence of a man who could no longer trust his own senses. He lay awake on a cot that smelled of canvas and horse liniment, listening to the camp settle around him: the groan of picket lines, a sentry coughing, somewhere far off a cook singing tunelessly about a girl from the coast. Ordinary sounds. Human sounds.

And beneath them, faint as a hairline crack in porcelain, the sound of the sigil-stone calling to him across two hundred miles of mountains.

*Come find me,* it whispered. *Come take me.*

Aldric opened his eyes in the dark and let himself feel it—just for a moment, just as a private indulgence, the way other men allowed themselves wine or grief. The pull of the stone was like standing at the edge of a high cliff with the wind at your back. Terrifying. Exhilarating. It promised him everything he had spent thirty years pretending he didn't want.

He had been nine years old when he first knew what he was. He'd been mucking out his father's stables—not yet a lord's son then, merely a lord's bastard by a kitchen maid, which was worse—and a mare had been foaling wrong, and Aldric had put his hands on her sweating flank and *willed* her right, and she had turned inside herself and delivered clean while he stood there shaking, knowing without being told that what he'd done had no name any priest would bless.

His father had found out eventually. Fathers always found out. And his father—a pious, frightened man who loved his legitimate sons and feared God in equal measure—had done the merciful thing, which was to say nothing, and the cruel thing, which was to say nothing *forever*. He had simply stopped looking at Aldric. Stopped speaking to him except through stewards. A boy learns quickly what such silence means. It means: *you are not what I hoped. You are a debt I am quietly paying off.*

So Aldric had learned the second lesson of his life, the one that sat beside the first like a knife beside a whetstone: magic was real, and magic was shame, and the only way to survive possessing a shameful thing was to make certain no one else ever knew you had it.

He had gotten very good at hiding. Better than good. He had hidden so well that when the old king died childless and the succession fractured into three squabbling claimants, nobody thought to look twice at the quiet bastard cousin with the careful manners and the unremarkable face. Nobody noticed that doors seemed to open for him. That dice rolled his way. That men who meant him harm woke with headaches and changed their minds.

He had won a kingdom with a gift he was forced to pretend he didn't have, and the winning had curdled something in him that had never quite uncurdled.

Because here was the joke, the great cosmic jest that Aldric alone was positioned to appreciate: he was now the most powerful man in the realm, and he still had to hide. Still. Every day. In front of his own court, his own generals, his own wife—who was lovely, ambitious, and would have him declared mad or possessed within the week if she ever saw his eyes go silver, as they did sometimes, when he was tired, when the gift pressed too close to the surface.

He had built an edict to hunt people like himself. He told the court it was for order, for safety, for the purity of the realm. All true, in its way. But the deeper truth—the truth he kept locked in the same iron box as his childhood—was simpler and uglier:

If he could not be free, no one would be.

And if he could seize the covenant—the old working that had walled magic out of Vellharrow and bound the gifted's power into leash and ledger—then he would never have to hide again. Neither would anyone. There would be no more hiding because there would be nothing left to hide behind. One king, one covenant, one hand on the throat of every wonder in the world.

His own included. Especially his own. He had thought about that part very carefully, late at night, and decided he could live with it. He had lived with worse.

* * *

In the morning he held council in the map tent, and it went the way councils always went, which was slowly and with too many opinions.

"The pass at Ashfen is clear," reported General Corvane, a broad man with a voice like a landslide. "The rebels burned the granaries ahead of us, but we've forage enough for three weeks. Four if we're sparing."

"We will not be sparing," Aldric said. "Sparing armies arrive late."

Corvane bowed. Someone else raised the matter of supply wagons and someone else the matter of the free cities' navy, and Aldric let them talk, because a king who listened appeared wise, and a wise appearance cost nothing. His attention drifted, as it always drifted now, to the small iron casket at the edge of the table—the one that held the courier's satchel, recovered from the wreck of the man's saddlebags after the hounds ran him down at the valley's edge.

Empty. Of course empty. The stone itself had vanished into the mountains with a bleeding thief and, according to the reports that made Aldric's jaw tighten every time he read them, a temple scribe. A *scribe*. Some gray little ink-stained nobody out of Vellharrow, the kind of person kings stepped over on staircases.

Except the stone didn't call to nobodies. The stone called to *him*, and he was neither a nobody nor, strictly speaking, anybody the world had been permitted to see. Which meant the scribe was something. The keepers' records—what few pages his agents had salvaged from the burning at the capital—spoke of a reader. One born under the covenant's shadow, marked to read what others could only hold.

So. The gray little nobody was the reader. Fate had a sense of humor after all; it had simply waited until Aldric was old enough to appreciate cruelty as craft.

"Your Majesty." Corvane again, patient, because Aldric had clearly gone elsewhere. "Shall I speak to the question of the Spire road?"

"The Spire road is closed," Aldric said. "Snowmelt. We go the long way, through Ashfen proper." He tapped the map, and something in his chest hummed faintly as his finger touched the ink, and he hated that hum, and he leaned into it, the way a man leans into a toothache to prove he can bear it. "And send for the prisoner."

* * *

They brought the messenger in at midday, between two guards, walking on his own feet but barely.

His name, according to the intake ledger, was Tam Wicker, and he had been a courier for the free city of Callowmere for eleven years, which meant he was either brave or stupid, and eleven years on those roads suggested both. They'd taken him three days ago trying to slip a dispatch through the lines. The dispatch itself was unremarkable—troop counts, grain stores, the usual nervous arithmetic of cities expecting war—but the man was useful for a different reason entirely.

Aldric had read his file. Tam Wicker, aged thirty-four. Born in Vellharrow.

"You're a long way from the valley, Master Wicker," Aldric said, when the tent had emptied of everyone but the guards, the prisoner, and himself.

Wicker lifted his chin. He had a split lip and a fine stubborn set to his shoulders. "Am I, Majesty. Hadn't noticed."

"There's no need for bravery. Bravery is for battles, and this isn't one." Aldric gestured to a stool. "Sit. Your legs are shaking."

"They do that when I'm insulted."

"Then sit before you fall down." Aldric waited, and Wicker, after a long mutinous moment, sat. Good. Stubbornness was fuel, and fuel could be redirected. "You were born in Vellharrow. Left at nineteen. Do you remember why?"

"I remember why I left anywhere, Majesty. Work."

"Do you remember your childhood there? Clearly? The streets, the neighbors, the—" Aldric paused, delicately. "The years between six and twelve?"

Something flickered across the courier's face. Not confusion exactly. More like a man reaching for a stair that wasn't where his foot expected it. "I remember enough."

"No," Aldric said softly. "You don't. None of them do. Isn't that the marvel of it? An entire valley of people with smooth places in their memories, and not one of them thinks to ask why." He let that sit. He watched the courier's stubbornness begin, very slightly, to sweat. "I'm going to offer you a bargain, Master Wicker. Before you refuse it—and you will want to refuse it, I can see it gathering in you like weather—I want you to understand that I know precisely what I'm asking, and I've priced it fairly."

"A bargain from a king who burns the gifted out of their homes."

"Yes," Aldric agreed pleasantly. "That king. Listen anyway. Clever men listen to devils; it's how they stay clever."

He rose and walked the length of the tent, unhurried, letting the silence do half his work. "Here is the situation as it stands. My army is ten days from Callowmere. Perhaps eight, if the weather holds and I choose to spend soldiers freely, which I do. The free cities will fight—they always do, beautifully, and then they lose, because courage is not a strategy. Somewhere in the mountains between here and there walks a woman named Mirabel Dunmore, carrying an object that belongs to me. When my forces reach her—and they will reach her, Master Wicker, the hounds have her scent and my patience is longer than her legs—she will be taken, and the object will be taken, and everything that happens afterward will happen to her rather than with her. I trust you follow."

"I follow that you're a tyrant."

"I'm a man who has read the same histories you have and drawn the correct conclusions from them." Aldric turned. "Now. The bargain. You will carry a message to her. Not a summons—an *offer*. She delivers the stone to me at the Sundered Spire, freely, before my army arrives, and in exchange she receives full amnesty. Pardon under seal. No hunt, no registry, no capital. She may live out her life in Vellharrow or Callowmere or the moon, for all I care, with her name struck from every list my clerks possess. And you—" he smiled, thin and genuine, because he did enjoy this part, the architecture of it— "you receive passage home. To Vellharrow. Permanently. With a pardon of your own, and enough coin that the word 'debt' never troubles you again. I understand debts are something of a preoccupation, in the valley."

Wicker had gone very still. "How do you know about—"

"I know a great deal about the valley. Its ledgers. Its habits. Its peculiar relationship with forgetting." Aldric returned to the table and set a letter on it, sealed with the royal crest, blank save for the address. "This is the offer, written in my own hand. No one else has touched it. If she refuses, burn it and walk away, and I'll release you at the border regardless—that promise I'll make aloud, and I keep my promises; ask anyone I've ruined whether I kept my promises to them."

"And if she accepts?"

"Then she saves her own life and the lives of whoever's fool enough to travel with her, and the realms are sealed in balance under a keeper who understands what balance actually costs." He said it smoothly. He almost believed it. Underneath, in the iron box, something older and hungrier turned over in its sleep: *and then I will never hide again.*

"Why her?" Wicker asked suddenly. "Why does a king negotiate with a scribe?"

Ah. There it was—the sharp question, the dangerous one. Aldric considered several answers and discarded them all, and chose instead a sliver of truth, because slivers of truth were the most durable currency he knew.

"Because she can read something I cannot," he said. "And because I have spent my whole life watching clever, powerless people be destroyed by powerful, careless ones, and I find I prefer to make use of the former before the latter finish the job." He slid the letter across the table. "Take it to her. Take my words with it, exactly as I give them to you: tell her the covenant was built on stolen things, and I am offering her the only honest trade she will ever be offered. Tell her—" and here he paused, because the next part mattered, mattered more than he liked to admit even to himself— "tell her that I know what it is to erase yourself to survive. Tell her I am the only man in this war who will never ask her to do it again."

Wicker stared at him. "That last part. Is it true?"

"It doesn't matter if it's true," Aldric said. "It matters that it's persuasive. Go and be persuasive."

The guards took the courier out. Aldric stood alone with the map and the humming in his chest and the letter's absence, which felt oddly like having cut off a finger and sent it into the mountains to do his seeing for him.

* * *

That night the dreams came, as they came every night now, and they were all the same dream.

He was nine years old again, in the stable, and the mare was foaling wrong, and he put his hands on her flank and the power rose in him like water finding a crack—and in the dream, this time, he did not stop it. He let it fill the stable, the yard, the county, the sky, silver and vast and *his*, and every person who had ever looked through him, past him, around him—the father, the brothers, the whole kneeling world—looked at him at last, truly looked, and their faces were not afraid.

They were grateful.

He woke with his hands burning and the sigil-stone's whisper louder than it had ever been, threading through the canvas walls, up from the south and east, from the high cold passes where a scribe and a thief and a caravan of outcasts were climbing toward a vault he had spent years searching for and could never enter, because the vault, like the stone, like the covenant itself, answered only to a reader.

*Let her read it,* Aldric thought, lying in the dark, flexing his burning fingers, watching silver light play along the seams of them like veins of ore. *Let her open every door. I will be standing behind her when she does.*

He smiled at the tent ceiling, and outside, the sentries swore softly at a frost that had come early and come wrong, creeping against the wind, flowing downhill toward the army like something obedient.

Ten days to Callowmere. Eight, if he spent freely.

He intended to spend freely.
