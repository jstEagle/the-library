## 17. Sefa Stands Alone

The gift had always been a shared thing, like a coat worn by two people at once.

When they were children in the fishing village of Marrow's End, Sefa and Ilo had discovered it by accident—Ilo falling through lake ice, Sefa on the shore screaming, and somewhere between them a wall of water rising up and setting her brother gently back on the ice like a mother cat retrieving a kitten. Neither of them could do it alone. Sefa could push and pull at the world's weight; Ilo could shape what she pushed. Together they were something the old women crossed themselves over. Apart, Sefa was half a song hummed without a tune.

Now Ilo lay in the lee of a boulder at the mouth of the Kestrel Pass, gray as old ash, with an Aldric crossbow bolt through him just below the ribs, and Sefa was about to learn exactly how much of the song one voice could carry.

"You're making that face," Ilo said.

"What face?"

"The face you made when you ate the bad mussel. The 'I have made a decision and everyone will hate it' face."

"I don't make faces."

"Sefa." He caught her wrist. His grip was still strong, which she chose to believe meant something hopeful, though the blood soaking into the ground beside him argued otherwise. "Brannic's already gone ahead with Mirabel and Rafael. Wren's with them. Someone has to close this pass behind them, and someone—" his breath hitched, and he smiled anyway, because Ilo had never once in twenty-two years let pain finish a sentence before humor did— "someone has to stay here and hold your hand while you do it."

"That's not funny."

"It's a little funny."

"It's not."

"It's *mildly* funny, which is the best either of us manages under pressure."

She wanted to hit him. She wanted to hold him. She settled for pressing her forehead against his, the way they'd done since they were small, twin to twin, when words were too big for whatever needed saying. His skin was cold. That was the part she couldn't argue with. His skin was cold as the lake ice all those years ago, and no amount of joking warmed it.

"Listen," he said quietly, against her hair. "You can't do both halves alone. Nobody can. But you don't need both halves. You need enough. A door doesn't have to be beautiful, Sef. It just has to be shut."

"And you'll be behind me, pushing."

"Every scrap of me," he said. "That's the whole point of us. There's no version of this where I'm not pushing."

* * *

They had come to the Kestrel Pass two days ago, following Brannic's map and Wren's fractured memories toward the Sundered Spire, and it should have been simple. Nothing past Ashfen had been simple. The refugees on the road had told them what was coming: Aldric's vanguard, three hundred strong, moving fast and light, hunting the sigil-stone the way hounds hunt a bleeding deer. And somewhere along the ridgeline yesterday, Sefa had felt it—a wrongness in the air, a pressure like a storm front, and known without being told that the vanguard had its own gifted in it now. Aldric had stopped pretending. He was using magic to hunt magic.

Ilo had been hit covering their retreat from a scout patrol. One bolt. He hadn't even complained, just folded up mid-stride like a puppet with cut strings, and Sefa had caught him and screamed at Brannic to go, *go*, get the others to the Spire, and Brannic—the great soft mountain of a man who had lost a family to a purge and clearly decided these strays were the replacement—had looked at her with eyes full of terrible understanding and said, "We'll be at the Spire. Both of you. I don't care what you have to do to manage it."

Then he'd gone, and the pass had gone quiet, and it had been just the two of them and the wind and the slow dark stain spreading under her brother.

Now dawn came gray over the eastern peaks, and with it came the sound. Boots. Hundreds of boots, rhythmic as a loom, echoing up the switchbacks below. Banners she couldn't see yet but could imagine: the black boar of Aldric on white fields.

Sefa stood at the narrowest point of the pass, where the trail ran between two walls of stone barely wide enough for four men abreast, and she rolled up her sleeves, and she reached for the gift.

It answered the way it always did—but wrong. Or rather, half-wrong. She could feel the weight of the mountain, the deep patient tonnage of it, everything she might push or pull or drop, but the shaping of it, the fine work, Ilo's half—it sat in her chest like a keyhole with no key. She could bring down the whole cliffside. She could bury the pass and every soldier in it. But she couldn't make the rock fall *only there*, couldn't carve the collapse to spare herself, couldn't do anything except the largest, bluntest, most suicidal version of the thing.

"Well," she said aloud, to the pass, to the morning, to nobody. "That's a problem."

"No it isn't," said Ilo, from behind her, and she turned, and he was sitting up.

He should not have been sitting up. The color in his face said so. But he had braced himself against the boulder with both arms, and his eyes were bright, fever-bright, and he was grinning at her with the grin he'd worn at seven years old standing on the frozen lake.

"Ilo—"

"Here's what we're going to do." He talked fast, the way he did when he was spending strength he didn't have, buying jokes on credit. "You're going to stand right there, and you're going to be my hands. I'll be the head. You push, I steer. Same as always, except I'm doing the steering from over here instead of next to you, which honestly is a nice change, you breathe too loud."

"You can't even stand."

"Don't need to stand. Never needed to stand. You think Ma stood up to steer the boat? She sat. Sat like a queen. Steering is a *sitting* discipline." He patted the ground beside him. "Get over here. Put your back against mine. We've done this a thousand times."

They had. Not this—never this—but the shape of it. Back to back on the dock at Marrow's End, feeling each other's pulse through their shoulder blades, learning where one twin ended and the other began, until the answer was: nowhere, really, nowhere at all.

Sefa went to him. She sat down with her spine against his spine, and the cold of him soaked through her shirt, and she laced her fingers through his fingers, and she reached for the gift again.

This time, when it came, it came whole.

Not entirely—not the way it used to be, easy as breathing, a thing they did without thinking. This cost. Every ounce of shaping Ilo fed her came out of him like thread pulled from a hem, and she could feel him fraying at the edges, and she hated it, and she did it anyway, because the alternative was written in bootbeats below them, getting louder.

"There you are," Ilo murmured. "There we are."

Below them, the first soldiers came around the switchback.

* * *

Aldric's vanguard climbed into the Kestrel Pass the way water climbs a hill: steadily, inevitably, with total confidence that nothing above them could possibly matter. They were good soldiers. Sefa could see that even from here—tight ranks, steady pace, officers moving along the line. Good soldiers who believed in their king, or feared him, or had simply never been given another option. She tried not to look at their faces. It was easier to stop a river than a crowd of faces.

"They're going to start shooting soon," Ilo said. "Ready?"

"No."

"Perfect. On three. One—"

The first crossbow bolts came up the pass, and Ilo said "three" into the middle of it, because he was Ilo, and then they moved the mountain.

It was not elegant. Sefa pushed, and Ilo shaped, and together they took the western cliff face—the one above the second switchback, where the trail narrowed to nothing—and shrugged it loose.

The sound was enormous. It was the sound the world would make if the world cleared its throat. Half a mountainside slid in a long white roar, dust boiling up like a struck match the size of a house, and the switchback below ceased to exist, and thirty men with it. The column stopped. Shouting. Officers' whistles cutting the dust-haze.

"Thirty," Sefa whispered, sick.

"Thirty soldiers coming to kill everyone we love," Ilo said, "and they signed up for it. Feel bad later. Right now, feel the east wall. They're going to try the other side."

He was right. The vanguard split, flowing toward the eastern cliff like water finding a new channel, and Sefa pushed again, and Ilo shaped again, and the east wall shed a curtain of stone across the upper trail. Less this time. Smaller. Cheaper. Ilo was rationing himself, and she could feel him doing it, feel the careful thinning of him, and she squeezed his fingers hard enough to hurt.

"Stop counting," she said.

"I'm not counting."

"You're counting. You've got your counting face."

"I don't have a counting face, I have one face, we literally have the same face—"

"Ilo."

A pause. Below them, the soldiers regrouped, and someone with a commanding voice started organizing ropes and ladders, because good soldiers solve problems.

"Sef," he said finally, softly. "Talk to me about something else. Tell me about the caravan. Tell me about Brannic's cooking."

"Brannic's cooking is a war crime."

"Tell me anyway."

So she did, pushing stone and talking, telling him about Brannic's stew, which contained ingredients Brannic refused to name and which Wren claimed had once been a person. Telling him about Mirabel, who blushed when Rafael looked at her and pretended she didn't, and about Rafael, who looked at Mirabel like she was a book he'd waited his whole life to read. About Wren's stories that changed every telling, and how Sefa had started keeping score. About the night they'd all slept in the tinker's wagon outside Callowmere, packed like fish in a barrel, and Ilo had put his cold feet on Brannic and Brannic had yelped and woken the entire camp and a mule.

Ilo laughed at that, a real laugh, and the laugh cost him, she felt it cost him, a whole thread of him spent on a joke about a mule, and she thought: *this is what he is. He spends everything on jokes. He always has.*

Another volley of bolts came up the pass. One of them went wide and buried itself in the scree a hand's width from Ilo's boot, and Sefa flinched, and Ilo said, "Rude," and shaped the next collapse.

They held the pass through the morning. Three times the vanguard tried the heights, and three times the twins brought stone down on them—not killing blows anymore, Ilo wouldn't spend that much, just walls, barriers, the trail erased and re-erased. The sun climbed. The dust settled and rose again. Sefa's arms shook. Her head pounded like a blacksmith lived in it. And beneath her back, Ilo grew lighter.

That was the horror of it. Not blood, not the wound—lightness. As if he were slowly becoming the kind of person you could carry without noticing.

"Hey," she said, during a lull. "Hey. Stay heavy."

"I'm always heavy. Ask anyone."

"*Stay heavy.*"

A pause. Then, very quietly, without any joke in it at all: "I'm trying, Sef. I'm trying."

* * *

Early afternoon, the vanguard stopped trying the cliffs.

Instead, a single man walked up the pass under a white rag on a spear, and stopped at a respectful distance, and called up: "In the name of King Aldric! The gifted woman on the heights! Your king extends amnesty!"

Sefa laughed. It came out cracked and ugly.

"He means it!" the man called. "Deliver the sigil-stone and the reader, and you'll want for nothing! The king honors his gifts!"

"The king steals memories!" she shouted back, because Mirabel had told them, told them all, in the firelight at Callowmere, what the covenant was made of, what Aldric wanted it for. "Tell your king we know what he is!"

The messenger hesitated. Then, oddly, he bowed—actually bowed, up the length of the pass, to two filthy twins on a ledge—and withdrew. And Sefa felt Ilo go very still against her back.

"Sef."

"What?"

"When he bowed. For a second, I felt—there's a gifted one down there. In the vanguard. I felt them touch the edge of our working." His voice had gone strange. "They know. They know exactly what we are and how much we've got left. That message wasn't for us, Sef. It was a measurement."

She understood then, with a clarity like ice water, what the afternoon would be. They wouldn't waste more men on the cliffs. They would wait. Wait for two half-people to run out of mountain, and then walk up the pass and take what they came for.

"How long?" she asked.

"If we keep spending like this? Till sundown. Maybe less."

"And after sundown?"

Ilo was quiet for a moment. Below, the vanguard made camp, orderly as an anthill, cookfires blooming one by one in the blue dusk. Somewhere down there, a gifted soldier was listening to their heartbeats.

"After sundown," Ilo said, "you run. Up the goat track, over the shoulder of the peak, down to the Spire by morning. Brannic will kill me for making you late, which will be funny, because I'll be dead, and dead men are very hard to kill."

"No."

"Sef—"

"*No.*" She twisted around to look at him, and the sight of him stopped her breath—he was gray to the lips now, propped against the boulder like a man made of driftwood, and still smiling at her, still *smiling*. "We go together or not at all. That was the deal. Before we were born, practically. One gift, two keepers. You don't get to spend yourself down to nothing and then hand me the bill."

"Sefa." He reached up and touched her cheek, and his hand was freezing. "Look at me. Look at what today cost. I've got maybe a thread left. Maybe two. If I come with you, I slow you down, and if I slow you down, Aldric's people catch us both, and then Mirabel reads that stone with an army on her heels, and everything—everyone—we love burns for nothing." His thumb brushed her cheekbone. "But if you run light, and I spend my last thread making sure nobody follows you up that goat track... then we win. Both halves, Sef. Just not the way we planned."

"I hate you."

"I know."

"I hate you so much."

"I know," he said, and his smile broke open into the real one, the one from the lake, the one from the dock, the one that had gotten them through every bad year of their lives. "Do you remember the ice? When we were little? Everybody thought it was a miracle. Old Mother Cass said the lake spirit saved me." He laughed, soft and wet. "It was you. It was always you. You caught me before I ever fell, and you've been catching me ever since. So catch me one more time, yeah? Catch what I'm about to give you."

The gift stirred between them, all that remained of it, small and bright as an ember cupped in two hands.

"Ilo—"

"Take it," he whispered. "And when you get to the Spire—when Mirabel does the reading—tell her the twins of Marrow's End paid the toll. Tell her we were glad to. And Sef—" his voice frayed, and he pulled it straight again by sheer stubbornness, one last joke bought on final credit— "tell Brannic his stew *is* a war crime. Promise me. Dying wish. Legally binding."

"Promise," she said, and her voice broke on it.

She leaned forward and pressed her forehead to his, twin to twin, and he pushed.

Everything he had left—all of it, the last of the shared thing, twenty-two years of one gift worn by two people—came pouring into her like a river into a bottle. She felt him in every part of it, his steadiness, his cleverness, his ridiculous incorrigible warmth, and then she felt the place where he had been, which was not empty, exactly. It was quiet. Quiet the way a house is quiet when someone you love has just stepped out and will be back any minute.

Any minute now.

Sefa stood up alone at the mouth of the Kestrel Pass, wearing the whole coat by herself, and found—to her astonishment—that she could shape. Fine work. Delicate work. Ilo's half, alive in her hands now, a legacy with instructions written in the muscle of it. She looked down at the campfires below, at the three hundred sleeping soldiers and the one gifted listener among them, and she raised her arms, and she sealed the pass behind her with a wall of stone forty feet thick and utterly, perfectly silent—no roar, no warning, just the mountain quietly deciding to be closed.

Then she turned uphill, toward the goat track, toward the Sundered Spire rising black against the stars, and she ran.

She ran light. She ran carrying two people's worth of gift and one person's worth of grief, and she did not let herself think about *any minute now*, because any minute now was a country she could not afford to live in tonight. Behind her the pass stood sealed. Ahead of her, somewhere in the dark, her strange ragged family was waiting—Brannic and Wren, Rafael and Mirabel, and a stone that could remake the world.

She only hoped she'd arrive in time. Because far below and behind her, faint but unmistakable, she felt the gifted soldier begin, patiently, expertly, to unmake her wall.
