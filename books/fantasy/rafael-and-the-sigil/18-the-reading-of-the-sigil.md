## 18. The Reading of the Sigil

The heart of the Sundered Spire was a room shaped like a held breath.

That was the only way Mirabel could think of it. The chamber rose in a perfect column of pale stone, its walls unmarked by chisel or tool, as though the mountain had simply decided, one ancient morning, to be hollow here. Far above, a shaft opened to the sky, and a single thread of dawn light fell all the way down to the floor, where it pooled around a shallow basin of black glass no wider than a washing tub.

Everything else—the vault doors behind them, the ghost-echoes that had shown her her mother's face, the distant thunder of Aldric's army still days out—felt very far away. Here there was only the light, the basin, and the sigil-stone in her hands, warm as a sleeping bird.

"You don't have to do it like this," Rafael said quietly. He stood at the edge of the light, unwilling—or unable—to step into it. "There might be another way. Wren's memories, the founders' echoes. We could spend years looking."

"We have three days," Mirabel said. "Maybe less. And besides." She turned the stone over in her palms, watching the sigil's lines catch the light. "I spent my whole life erasing myself a little at a time so no one would see me. This is just... doing it on purpose. Doing it for something."

"That's a terrible comfort."

"I know." She smiled at him. "It's the best one I have."

He came closer then, boots scuffing on stone worn smooth by ten centuries of silence, and took her hands—both of them, the stone pressed between their fingers like something they were holding together, which, she supposed, they were.

"Tell me the rules again," he said.

"You've heard them four times."

"Humor me. If I'm going to stand here and watch you feed yourself into a rock, I want to feel like I did my homework."

Mirabel laughed—a real laugh, startled out of her—and was grateful for it. Her chest had been tight since the vault, since her mother's face among the founders' echoes, since she'd understood what the covenant actually asked of the people who made it.

"The rules," she said. "One: I read the sigil aloud, and the stone takes memories to complete the working. It takes what I offer. Two: I can't stop once I start, or the half-forged covenant will tear instead of seal, and everything within a hundred miles of this mountain stops existing. So if I look like I'm changing my mind, you're not allowed to let me change my mind."

"Understood."

"Three: you talk. The whole time. You keep telling me our story—the road, the caravan, Callowmere, all of it—because the memories the stone takes first are the ones I'm not holding on to. If I'm hearing them, if I'm gripping them, maybe..." She shrugged. "Maybe I get to choose which ones go."

"And if none of them stay?"

She looked up at him. His face was doing the thing it did when he was frightened and refusing to admit it: jaw set, eyes too steady, a muscle ticking near his temple. She had learned his face the way she'd once learned ledgers, line by line, and she loved every stubborn entry in it.

"Then you'll remember for both of us," she said. "That's rule four. That's the whole point of you."

* * *

They prepared the way Wren remembered wrong and Brannic remembered right, which is to say imperfectly, arguing the whole time.

Brannic insisted on rigging ropes from the vault door down to the chamber, in case the ritual left her collapsed and unable to climb. Sefa, who should have been resting after holding the pass—after *Ilo*, who lay fevered in the caravan two miles back with Wren watching over him—had refused to be anywhere but here, and now crouched by the doorway with her brother's gift humming faintly under her skin, ready to shield the chamber if anything came through it.

"Nothing is coming through it," Rafael told her.

"Something always comes through it," Sefa said. "That's the story of your entire life, courier. I'm staying."

Brannic hugged Mirabel last, before retreating to the doorway. He held her the way he must once have held daughters she would never meet, one broad hand cradling the back of her head, and said into her hair, in his gravel-and-smoke voice:

"You come back with as much of yourself as you can carry. Whatever's missing, we'll help you find. That's not a promise, girl. That's a load-bearing wall."

"I love you too," she managed, and felt him smile against her hair.

Then they were gone, and it was just the two of them and the light and the stone.

Mirabel knelt at the basin and set the sigil-stone in its center. The black glass drank the dawn-light and gave nothing back, and when she looked closely she saw that the surface was not empty—it was crowded, faint as breath on winter glass, with thousands upon thousands of tiny inscribed names. The founders. Every one who had ever paid the covenant's price.

Her finger found the newest carving, still sharp-edged: a name in a script she almost recognized. Her mother's.

"All right," Mirabel whispered. "I'm listening."

She began to read.

* * *

The sigil was not words, exactly. It was instructions written in the grammar of the world itself—she understood it the way she understood ink, the way ink had always understood *her*. As she spoke, the lines of the stone began to glow, and the glow spread outward across the black glass, lighting the carved names one by one until the whole basin burned cold silver, and Mirabel felt the first tug.

It was gentle. That was the horror of it. Like a tide taking a sandcastle grain by grain, so politely you could almost pretend it wasn't happening.

A memory went: her seventh summer, the smell of the temple's tallow candles, Mother Osla's voice correcting her penmanship. She gasped—she could feel the shape of the absence, a tooth-socket of nothing where something warm had been—

"—the road through Ashfen," Rafael said immediately, kneeling beside her, his hands on her shoulders, his voice low and certain. "You hated the dust. You complained about it for eleven miles, which Brannic counted out loud just to spite you. You said, and I quote, 'If I wanted to be buried alive in grit I'd have stayed at Vellharrow and let Osla work me to death.' And Brannic laughed so hard he had to sit down on a burnt fencepost."

She grabbed for it. Ashfen. Dust like gray snow. Brannic laughing, wheezing, slapping his knee—yes—yes, she could hold it, she wrapped the memory around herself like a coat while the tide pulled at the edges of others.

Another memory slipped free: the lantern streets of Callowmere, Sefa's hand in hers on the rooftop—

"Sefa taught you to throw a knife," Rafael said, faster now. "You were terrible. You put a knife through Dessa's favorite hat and Dessa made you buy her dinner as an apology, which was extortion, and you paid it, because you said being extorted by a spymaster was 'practically an honor.' You were *delighted*, Mirabel. You'd never been extorted before."

"I remember," she breathed, and she did—the hat, the hole, Dessa's raised eyebrow, her own ridiculous pride—and she laughed even as another memory tore loose, some childhood afternoon, a garden, a woman's voice singing—

"Don't chase that one," Rafael said softly. "You can't reach it. Stay with me. Stay *here*." He shifted so she could see his face. "The night at the tinker's caravan. Your first night. You couldn't sleep because the wagon wheels creaked in a rhythm like a hymn and it kept making you cry, and you finally climbed down and found Wren awake by the fire, and she told you about the covenant. Wrongly. Enthusiastically. With completely fabricated details about a giant. You didn't correct her. You said it was the nicest lie anyone had ever told you."

"It was," Mirabel whispered. The fire. The giant. Wren's hands folding and unfolding around the tale like a bird settling. Hers. Held.

But the tide kept coming, and she was beginning to understand the shape of the bargain in her body, not just her mind. The stone did not take at random. It took the oldest first. It took the roots. Her childhood was going—Osla's candle-smell had been only the outermost branch; beneath it whole seasons were lifting away like mist off a morning river, her mother's face, her father's—

*Rafael,* she thought, *tell me faster—*

And he did. He talked as she had never heard him talk, pouring out their story like a man bailing water from a sinking boat: the rockslide, the argument about rationing honey, the way she'd bandaged his shoulder and lectured him about infection while he pretended not to whimper, the first time she'd laughed at his jokes, the second time, the fiftieth, the way she looked at dawn, the way she argued with maps, the way she'd held Ilo's head during the worst fever-night and sung—not well, *terribly*, off-key, and Ilo had sworn it saved his life anyway—

"Ilo says your singing violates several treaties," Rafael said, and Mirabel sobbed out a laugh, gripping that memory with both hands, *both* hands, but she could feel it thinning already at the edges like cloth wearing through, and beneath it more memories rising, offering themselves, the tide patient and enormous—

"How many?" she gasped. "How many does it need?"

"I don't know." For the first time his voice cracked. "Mirabel—"

"Keep talking."

* * *

Time lost its hinges. The light in the basin deepened from silver to gold, and the sigil-stone grew hot enough that she could no longer touch it, though her hands stayed cupped around it anyway, because letting go meant stopping, and stopping meant the tear, and the tear meant everyone she loved stopped existing, and that was not a choice, it had never been a choice, it had only ever been a question of *which* memories, and she was choosing, God help her, she was choosing every moment—

The valley. Vellharrow. The temple where she'd hidden for twenty years, going first like smoke.

No—no, she fought for that one, she clawed it back—Osla burning records, Osla paying her debt, Osla's heartbreak disguised as mercy, *belonging offered only through exile*—hers, hers, she would keep Osla if it killed her—

It nearly did. Pain lanced through her skull white and total, and she heard herself scream, and she heard Rafael shouting her name, and somewhere very far away Sefa's gift flared at the doorway like a struck bell.

"*Stay with me.*" Rafael's hands framed her face now. His eyes were wet. When had his eyes gotten wet? "Mirabel. Look at me. One more. Just one more. Tell me—" his voice shook, steadied, shook again— "tell me why you're doing this."

"Because," she panted, "because someone has to—"

"No. Why. *Really.*"

She looked at him. At this stupid, brave, guilty, honest courier who had carried a stone across a kingdom to find a scribe he'd never met, who had lied to her and confessed it and been forgiven, who was kneeling in the ruins of her childhood right now handing her pieces of herself as fast as they burned.

"Because I spent my whole life invisible," she said, "and then you people *saw* me. And seeing is worth any price. Even—" her breath hitched, and she felt something enormous begin to move inside her, some vast final layer of herself rising like a floor giving way— "even *this*."

The basin blazed.

Every name carved in the black glass lit at once, ten centuries of the forgotten, and Mirabel felt the covenant close around the realms like two great hands finding each other across a wound. Somewhere beyond the walls of the world, something that had been tearing knit shut.

And the last thing the tide took, gentle as a thief at the end of a long night, was—

* * *

Rafael caught her as she fell.

The stone in the basin had gone dark and smooth, its work done, its sigil faded to a faint scar. The light above fell ordinary and pale on Mirabel's face. She was breathing. She was warm. But when he touched her cheek and said her name, she didn't stir, and when her eyes finally fluttered open, minutes or hours later, they were clear and calm and utterly, perfectly empty of him.

She looked at Rafael with the polite blankness of a stranger.

"Oh," she said softly. "Hello. Where am I?"

Behind him, at the doorway, Sefa made a small broken sound.
