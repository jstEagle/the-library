## 19. The Usurper at the Gate

*Rafael*

The Spire shook before the horns did.

Rafael felt it through his boots first—a deep, grinding tremor, like the mountain clearing its throat—and then the sound arrived: war-horns from the pass below, three long notes and a fourth cut short, which was Sefa's signal that the vanguard had reached the outer gate and that she was no longer merely delaying them but *engaging* them.

"Mirabel." He turned from the arrow-slit window. "It's time."

She didn't answer at once. She knelt at the heart of the vault with the sigil-stone cradled in both hands, and the light coming off it was wrong for lamplight—too silver, too steady, moving in patterns that made his eyes ache if he looked too long. Half the sigil's lines had already been rewritten in her own hand, ink-bright against the stone, and where they glowed, the air itself seemed to lean toward her.

"Mirabel."

"I heard you." Her voice was thin. "I'm trying to decide whether to be terrified or furious, and I can't do both at once."

"Furious," Rafael said. "Terrified comes later. Terrified is for after." He crouched beside her, close enough to see the fine trembling in her fingers. "Right now, everyone down there is buying you minutes with their lives. The least we can do is spend them well."

Above them, the ghost-echoes of the founders circled the vault's dome like slow birds—seven shapes of pale light, murmuring in a language older than any kingdom. One of them had Mirabel's mother's face. Rafael had seen Mirabel look up at it twice now, and both times she had looked away as if scalded.

"The line will hold," he said, though he had no way of knowing it.

"You keep saying that."

"It keeps being true so far."

"That's not the same thing and you know it."

He grinned at her, because it was either grin or say something true and terrible, and there would be time for true and terrible later. "Brannic is down there with a hammer and forty years of grudges. Wren is telling the soldiers stories until they forget which end of their swords to hold. Ilo is *alive*, Mirabel, which nobody bet on last week, and angry about it, which makes him dangerous. And Sefa—" he paused, because Sefa deserved better than a joke, "Sefa is holding a mountain pass alone, and I have never in my life seen anyone do anything half so brave."

Mirabel closed her eyes. When she opened them, the terror had been folded away somewhere, tucked small, and what remained was the scribe: precise, stubborn, unimpressed by kings.

"Then let's not waste it," she said, and set her hands back on the stone.

* * *

*Aldric*

King Aldric Vane rode through the broken outer gate of the Sundered Spire at the head of his third column, and he did not feel triumphant, which annoyed him.

Triumph was supposed to feel like something. He'd been promised it all his life—in whispers, in the careful flattery of courtiers who never guessed what he was, in the cold arithmetic of his own ambition. Seize the covenant, seize the source. Every gifted soul in the realm gathered under one crown instead of scattered like sparks, hunted like vermin, wasted. Order. Control. *His* order.

Instead he felt the mountain resisting him. That was the only word for it. The deeper his army pushed into the Spire's throat, the heavier the air grew, as though the stone itself disapproved of him.

"Your Majesty." Captain Ferro reined in beside him, ash streaking her cheek. "The pass behind us—the girl. She's still holding it. We can't reinforce the column without going through her."

"One girl."

"Yes, sire."

Aldric looked back down the pass. Far below, tiny as an insect, a figure stood in the smoke where the road narrowed, and the road around her was *wrong*—bent, folded, refusing to deliver his soldiers where they wanted to go. His gift stirred uneasily under his skin, the stolen warmth of it, and for a moment he could have sworn the figure below looked up and met his eyes across half a mile of burning air.

"Sire?" Ferro said.

"Leave her," Aldric said. "She's a door held shut by one hinge. We don't need that door. We need what's at the top." He spurred forward. "The scribe. Find me the scribe."

They found resistance instead, and it was absurd, and it should have been nothing.

Tinkers' wagons chained across the second gate. A huge grey-bearded man swinging a tinker's hammer like it owed him money, roaring something about a daughter while soldiers broke against him like weather. An old woman sitting calmly on a wagon wheel telling a story in a clear carrying voice, and Aldric watched six of his own men stop fighting to listen, faces slack, until an officer shook them. Somewhere above, crossbow bolts fell with uncanny accuracy, always finding hands and shoulders and never throats—as if someone were shooting to wound with a precision no archer alive possessed.

And through it all, cutting down the slope with two swords and no helmet and blood already sheeting down one arm, came a courier in a ruined blue coat.

Rafael. The thief. The heir who didn't want his inheritance.

"There," Aldric said quietly, and his gift rose in him like heat off coals. He had taken this power from a dying storm-caller twenty years ago, and he had never once used it gently. Lightning answered him out of a cloudless sky.

* * *

*Rafael*

The lightning missed him by the width of a prayer.

It took the wagon beside him instead, and the wagon simply ceased—not burned, not splintered, *ceased*, leaving a shape of scorched air and Brannic screaming and Rafael flat on his back with his ears ringing and the taste of copper everywhere.

Get up. Get up get up get—

He got up. The second gate was lost; he could see that in one glance. Brannic was dragging Wren backward by the collar while she shouted the ending of her story over her shoulder like a woman being evicted mid-sentence. Soldiers poured through the gap in ranks now, too many, far too many, and above them on the rampart Ilo loosed bolt after bolt with Sefa's borrowed steadiness in his hands, and Rafael understood with cold clarity that they had perhaps ninety seconds of being alive left.

So he did the only thing he could think of. He ran *at* them.

Not to win—he'd done the arithmetic on winning weeks ago and the sums were ugly. He ran at them because every soldier who turned to face a screaming man with two swords was a soldier not climbing the stairs to the vault. He heard Brannic bellow behind him and join in, hammer high, and heard Wren's voice rise into something that wasn't a story anymore but a keening old battle-song in a language the founders had spoken, and Rafael laughed—actually laughed, wild and helpless—because this was the ragtag worst army in the history of armies and they were *magnificent*.

He took a spear-graze along the ribs. He gave worse than he got. He put a sword through a shield-boss and wrenched it free and thought, absurdly, of Mirabel's face when he'd first collapsed at the temple gate, bleeding and begging, and how she had looked at him like he was a problem she intended to solve personally.

"Hold the line!" he roared, to four people and a storyteller, as if they were ten thousand. "Hold the *line!*"

And then the world went white, and the King's voice rolled down the stairwell like a landslide:

"*STOP.*"

* * *

*Mirabel*

Mirabel heard the fighting stop the way you hear a held breath.

Upstairs, boots on stone, dozens of them, then hundreds, filling the Spire's throat. Down here in the vault, the sigil-stone burned brighter under her hands, half-forged, humming like a struck bell that refused to finish ringing. Seven founder-echoes wheeled overhead. Her mother's face among them, patient, sorrowful, waiting.

*Almost,* the stone said, in the way stones say things, which is to say Mirabel felt the meaning rather than heard it. *Almost. Not yet.*

"Not yet isn't good enough," she whispered. "He's here."

Footsteps on the stairs. Unhurried. A king does not run to his coronation.

The doors at the top of the vault steps—doors that had sealed against armies for three hundred years—swung open without being touched, peeled back like petals, and King Aldric Vane descended into the founders' vault alone, his soldiers held at the threshold by some instinct even they couldn't name.

He was smaller than she'd imagined. That was her first stupid thought. Her second was that his eyes were the eyes of a man looking at a meal after a long famine, and that she was the meal.

"The scribe," he said. His voice was almost gentle. "Do you know what you're holding?"

"A covenant," Mirabel said. "Older than your crown."

"A cage." He came closer, and she saw the gift moving under his skin like light through dirty water—stolen, she realized, remembering Wren's fractured tales, *taken*, not given. No wonder the founders' echoes shrank from him. "I've spent my whole life feeling it leak out of me, piece by piece, into a world that calls people like us abominations and hunts us for it. You've spent yours hiding in a valley that won't even let magic exist. Don't tell me you love the cage, scribe. Help me break it. Hand me the stone, and I'll give you a place at my right hand. Amnesty. Power. Everything they took from us, returned with interest."

For one strange moment, Mirabel almost pitied him. She knew that hunger. She had starved beside it her whole life—starved for belonging, for a past, for a self that hadn't been sold off in pieces before she could walk.

But the founders had taught her the difference, in their wordless circling way, and she spoke it aloud now, steady as a ledger-line:

"They *gave*, Aldric. That's the whole working. That's the secret your crown has been strangling kingdoms to find. The covenant isn't a cage. It's a *gift*—given freely, renewed freely, or it's nothing." She lifted the stone. It blazed. "You can't take it. Taking is the one thing it cannot survive."

"Everything can be taken," Aldric said, "from the right kind of corpse."

Lightning filled the vault.

* * *

*Rafael*

Rafael came up the last flight of stairs on pure spite, Brannic's shoulder under one arm and Wren's grip on his belt, in time to see the vault flash white through the doorway and hear Mirabel scream.

What happened next, he would tell a hundred times in the years after, and never quite manage to describe honestly, because honest words failed him.

The lightning did not strike Mirabel. It struck the sigil-stone—or rather, it struck the half-forged covenant flowing through her, and the covenant *read it*. Read it the way she read ink, the way she read everything: down to its roots, down to its first theft. And the covenant found, wrapped inside the storm-light, the signature of a dying storm-caller murdered in a ditch twenty years ago, whose gift had been torn out of her and stuffed into a young ambitious man who told himself it was destiny.

The covenant called it what it was.

Stolen.

The light went out of Aldric like a snuffed candle. Rafael watched the King stagger, watched him claw at his own chest as if reaching for something that had always been there and was suddenly, horribly gone—twenty years of borrowed power draining out of him in a single silent instant, returning, Rafael somehow knew, to wherever stolen things go when they're finally named. Aldric dropped to his knees on the founders' floor, an ordinary man in a gilded coat, gasping.

"No," the King whispered. "No, it's *mine*, I earned it, I—"

"You stole it," Mirabel said. Her voice echoed strangely, doubled, as if the seven echoes spoke with her. "That's all you ever did. And the covenant doesn't recognize thieves."

Soldiers flooded the doorway—and stopped. Because their king was on his knees, and because the vault was full of light that showed each of them, briefly and terribly, some truth about themselves they'd rather not have seen, and because an old woman walked past them all, unhurried, and stood between the fallen usurper and the kneeling scribe with her arms crossed like a grandmother at a funeral.

"Well," said Wren. "That's settled, then."

It was not settled. Even Rafael could see it wasn't settled—Aldric was still breathing, the army still outnumbered them fifty to one, and Mirabel was swaying on her knees with blood running freely from her nose and the stone still only *half*-forged, its remaining lines dark, waiting, hungry.

"Mira." He shoved past Wren and dropped beside her, catching her before she tipped sideways. Her eyes wouldn't focus. "Hey. Hey, look at me. Talk to me."

"So bright," she murmured. "Rafael, it wants—it wants more, it wants the rest of—"

"I know." His throat closed. He knew exactly what it wanted, and what it would cost, and he had known since Ashfen, since the vault, since the night by the fire when she'd asked him what he was so afraid of and he'd lied to her. "I know. But not yet. First you have to stay *here*. Stay with me. Tell me—" he cast around desperately, "tell me what Brannic calls his worst wagon."

Her mouth twitched, bloody and exhausted. "...The one that squeaks."

"He named it. What's its name?"

"The... the Squeaking Duchess."

"And what did Wren say the covenant's first keeper smelled like?"

"Cedar and... and bad decisions." A breath of a laugh. Then her eyes cleared, fixed on his, and the fear in them was bottomless. "Rafael. If I finish it—"

"Then we'll be there," said Brannic's gravel voice behind him, "when you wake up."

"All of us," Wren said.

From far away, faint through the mountain's bones, came a sound none of them expected: horns—but different horns, higher, sweeter, in triplets. Callowmere's signal. Dessa's signal. The free cities had come after all, late and insufficient and *coming*, banners on the southern road, and somewhere on a smoking pass a girl named Sefa was laughing through her tears because the pressure on her door had suddenly, blessedly eased.

Aldric's soldiers, kingless and suddenly outnumbered, began—slowly, in ones and twos—to lower their weapons.

Mirabel looked at the stone. At the dark half of the sigil, waiting for the last of her. At Rafael, who was holding her hands like he could anchor her to the world by grip alone.

"Tell me our story while I work," she whispered. "All of it. From the temple gate. So I can find my way back."

Rafael bowed his head over their joined hands, and began.

"It was raining," he said, "the day I fell through your gate..."

And beneath them, patient as winter, the covenant waited to be paid.
