## 20. What Mirabel Gave

The last thing she remembered giving was the smell of rain on the caravan canvas.

She had thought she would run out of memories long before the sigil was full. She had braced herself for it like a woman wading into a cold river—first the ankles, then the knees, then the terrible moment when the current takes your feet from under you. But the stone drank and drank, patient as a debt collector, and there was always more. A life, it turned out, held an astonishing quantity of small things.

The taste of Osla's bitter tea. The exact weight of Brannic's hand on her shoulder, warm as a hearthstone. Sefa laughing so hard at Ilo's impression of a crown tax collector that she snorted tea through her nose, and Ilo looking insufferably proud of it. Wren's cracked voice singing a road song with half the words wrong and all of them sung anyway.

Rafael.

She saved him for last. Or perhaps the stone did; she could no longer tell where her choices ended and its hunger began. She fed him in pieces because she could not bear to feed him whole—the way he said her name as though it were a place he'd been looking for, the fever-bright look of him the first night at the gate, the argument about whether hardtack counted as food or merely as punishment, his hand finding hers in the dark beneath the Spire and not letting go.

*Anchor me,* she had begged him. *Tell me what we were.*

And he had, his voice steady while hers unraveled, telling her their story back to her like a scribe reading over a dying woman's shoulder: *You copied ledgers. You hid your gift like a thief hides coin. You burned records you weren't supposed to burn. You found me bleeding at your gate and you were furious about it, you were so furious, and then you bandaged my arm anyway.* He told her about the rockslide, the dogs, Dessa's spy-holes, the lantern streets, Ilo's blood on the rooftops, Aldric's face when his stolen gifts tore out of him like teeth being pulled. He told her everything, fast, urgent, a man bailing water from a sinking boat with his cupped hands.

*Say it again,* she kept whispering. *Say it again.*

And then, at some point, she stopped whispering it, because she no longer knew who *it* referred to.

The covenant sealed with a sound like a great bell heard from underwater—a note she felt in her bones rather than heard—and the light went out of the stone all at once, leaving it gray and ordinary as river pebble. And Mirabel Dunmore stood in the heart of the Sundered Spire holding nothing at all.

* * *

She woke to birdsong.

That was the first wrongness, though she didn't yet know it was wrong. Birdsong meant morning, and morning meant somewhere safe, and the last thing—no. There was no last thing. There was birdsong, and sunlight through a small window, and the scratchy wool of a blanket against her cheek, and a silence inside her head so complete it rang.

She sat up slowly, the way you rise from a fever, testing each part of yourself. Her body ached in a general, honest way, the ache of long roads and little sleep. Her hands were clean. Her hair had been washed—someone else's work; she could feel the careful comb-marks. On the table beside the bed lay a folded shift, a bowl of water gone lukewarm, and a stone.

It was a plain thing, gray as weathered slate, smooth as if a river had worried at it for a hundred years. It should have been nothing. A rock. A paperweight for a scribe's desk.

But when her fingers closed around it, something moved in her chest—not memory, not quite, but the *shape* where memory would go, the way an amputated limb still itches. Her eyes stung. She had no idea why. She sat holding a stranger's grief with no address to send it to.

The room was small and low-ceilinged, plaster walls, a shelf of books whose spines she read without effort—ledgers, annals, a primer of scriptorium hands. She could read every one of them. She tested herself idly, the way you wiggle a bruised finger: yes, letters came easily, arranged themselves into meaning without friction. Words stayed. Everything else had been scooped out like the flesh of a fruit.

She got up. Her legs held. In the tiny polished scrap of mirror by the washstand, a young woman looked back at her—dark-haired, pale from illness or grief or both, with ink stains worked so deep into the cuticles they looked permanent. A scribe's hands. She turned her left wrist over and found a small scar she had no story for.

"Mirabel," she said aloud, testing.

The name fit like a borrowed coat. Correct in its seams, wrong in its weight. She knew it was hers—knew it the way you know your own reflection—but knowing and remembering proved to be different rooms entirely, and the door between them was shut.

There was a knock. Before she could answer, the door opened, and an old woman came in carrying a tray: bread, honey, tea that smelled bitter enough to strip paint. The old woman was straight-backed and sharp-eyed, with the calloused middle finger of a lifelong scribe, and when she saw Mirabel sitting up, her whole face did something complicated—relief and fear and a fierce tenderness all fighting for space.

"You're awake," the old woman said. "Good. That's—good." She set the tray down with slightly too much care, as though her hands needed a job. "I'm Mother Osla. You're in Vellharrow. In the temple. You're—" She stopped. Started again, slower, like someone picking her way across ice. "You've been ill. There was a war. It's over. We won."

"We," Mirabel repeated.

"You." The old woman's jaw tightened. "Mostly you."

Mirabel looked down at the stone in her hand. "Did I win this?"

Osla was quiet a moment. "You might say you paid for it."

"I don't remember paying."

"No," Osla said softly. "I imagine you don't."

Something in the way she said it—not pitying, exactly, but careful, the way you speak near a wound—made Mirabel's throat close. She set the stone on the blanket and pressed her palms flat against her knees. "How bad is it? What's missing?"

Osla pulled the room's single stool over and sat, which struck Mirabel as the gesture of a woman preparing for a long and unpleasant conversation. "What do you remember? Start anywhere."

"Birdsong. This room. My name—I think it's my name. How to read." She hesitated. "I remember how to write three different scriptorium hands, which seems excessive. I remember that tea can be bitter. I don't remember ever drinking any." She looked up. "I remember a word. *Covenant.* I don't know what it means, but when I say it, my chest hurts."

Osla nodded slowly, and Mirabel watched her decide, visibly, not to lie. "Then here is what I'll tell you, and you may believe all, none, or some of it. A year ago—nearly—you walked out of this temple with a wounded stranger, because the world was ending and you were the only person alive who could stop it. You went north. You did something impossible under the Sundered Spire. And whatever it cost, you paid it in full, and the world is still turning, and the valley is still blind to magic, and the free cities are still free." She paused. "You came home four days ago, carried on a tinker's cart, asleep and breathing and empty as a winter granary."

"Four days."

"You woke twice before now. Asked for water both times. Called for someone both times." Osla's voice went very even. "A name. You didn't say it just now, so perhaps that's gone too."

Mirabel's hand drifted toward the stone without her permission. "Was there someone?"

"There was a whole company of someones," Osla said. "A tinker built you that bedframe himself—he measured the door three times to be sure it would fit through. Twin girls who won't leave the courtyard. An old woman who sits with you when she can and tells you stories you've never heard, which must be a strange experience for you both." Something flickered across her face—grief, quickly shuttered. "They've been waiting for you to wake. They're waiting now. They've been camped in my courtyard for four days like a traveling fair, eating my food, repairing my roof unasked—"

She stopped. Not because she'd run out of grievances, Mirabel suspected, but because she'd heard her own voice and recognized what lived underneath it.

"They're your family," Osla finished quietly. "Or they were. Whether they still are—that's yours to decide. That's the one thing nobody took from you. The choosing."

Mirabel looked at the tray. Bread, honey, bitter tea. Her stomach made a decision independent of her heart, and she reached for the bread, and it was only halfway to her mouth that she realized her hands were shaking.

"I don't know them," she whispered. "They crossed mountains for me and I don't know their faces."

"You will," Osla said. "Or you'll make new ones. Memory isn't the only mortar, child. I've known you since you were nine years old, and I'm telling you: you were never a girl who loved people because she remembered them. You loved them because you chose them, stubbornly, against all sense, the way you chose to copy extra pages when the temple fell behind, the way you chose to feed the temple cat you were officially forbidden to feed." Her mouth twitched. "Some things survive the granary fire. The shape of you survives."

"The cat," Mirabel said. "Do I—"

"He's on your bed most nights. His name is Turnip. He has no respect for you whatsoever." Osla rose, brisk again, armor going back on. "Eat. Then, if you're able, come to the courtyard. Or don't. No one will press you. But there's one among them who's been asking for you since the hour you arrived, and I judged it crueler to keep him waiting than to warn you first."

"Him?"

Osla paused at the door. For a moment she looked older than the temple itself.

"A courier," she said. "He says he has a delivery for you. Says he's been carrying it a long way." Her voice roughened. "Says he promised to tell you a story, one evening at a time, until you know it all by heart—even if your heart has to learn it fresh."

* * *

The courtyard was full of late-afternoon gold.

Mirabel stood in the doorway with the stone in her pocket and let the scene arrive in pieces, the way all new things arrived now: a tinker's cart gleaming with fresh paint, a man beside it mending a hinge with the absorbed tenderness of a nurse. Two young women on the well-wall, heads together, so alike they might have been one person reflected—one of them thin and drawn, her arm in a sling, the other leaning against her as if the two of them shared a single supply of strength. An old woman on a bench, mid-story, hands conducting some invisible orchestra, and a large orange cat draped across her lap wearing the expression of a creature who has heard this story many times and tolerated it.

They hadn't seen her yet. She stood in the shadow of the doorway and studied their faces, hunting for recognition the way her tongue hunted for a missing tooth. Nothing. Nothing, and beneath the nothing, that awful itching *shape*—grief with no object, love with no address, homesickness for a home she was standing in.

Then the old woman on the bench looked up. Mid-word, she stopped, and her eyes went wide and wet, and she said—not to Mirabel, but to the courtyard, to the sky, to anyone—"She's awake. Look. *Look.*"

Everything turned at once.

The twins came off the wall like a single creature with two bodies. The tinker dropped his hinge. The cat, offended, poured himself off the bench and stalked away with dignity. And Mirabel stood frozen while strangers embraced her—careful of her frailty, careless of everything else—while voices overlapped: *you're awake, you're awake, we didn't dare hope, do you know us, it doesn't matter, it doesn't matter, we know enough for everyone—*

The thin twin with the sling caught Mirabel's face in her good hand and searched her eyes, and whatever she found there made her smile break and reform. "I'm Sefa," she said. "This is Ilo. We had—we *have*—one gift between us. You once told me that made us lucky, because we could never lose each other the way other people lose each other." Her voice wobbled. "I've been thinking about that for four days."

"I'm sorry," Mirabel said helplessly. "I'm sorry, I don't—"

"Don't apologize." It was the tinker, big and gray-bearded, his voice gentle as his hands. "Brannic," he offered, pressing her palm against his rough cheek a moment, the way you'd greet family. "You don't owe us your past, girl. We brought it with us. All of it. Every road, every meal, every stupid argument. We'll lend it back to you piece by piece, however long it takes." He sniffed, gruffly, and glared at the middle distance. "Blasted dust in this courtyard."

The old woman—Wren, someone said, *Wren, Wren*—took both Mirabel's hands and looked at her with eyes ancient and bright and slightly wrong, and said, "I remember the last covenant, you know. Or I remember pieces of it. Between us, child, we might assemble a whole truth. Like a pot mended with gold." She smiled. "Kintsugi, my mother called it. Broken and more beautiful for the mending."

"That's not kintsugi," said Ilo, from the well wall. "That's just a broken pot."

"It's *metaphorical* kintsugi," Wren snapped, and the twins laughed, and the sound went through Mirabel like sunlight through a window—warming everything it touched, belonging to none of it.

And then the courtyard went quiet, the way a room goes quiet when someone important enters, and Mirabel turned.

He was standing by the gate.

He was lean and travel-worn and healing, with a fresh scar along his jaw she would never remember earning, and he was looking at her the way a man looks at a horizon he'd given up expecting. He didn't rush forward. He stood very still, as if she were a bird that might startle, and when he finally spoke, his voice was rough and low and terribly careful.

"Hello," he said. "My name is Rafael."

"Mirabel," she said. "I think."

A ghost of a smile crossed his face—there and gone, like a fish rising. "I know. I've been practicing saying it for months. I'm told I'm very good at it." He came forward slowly, stopping an arm's length away, and held out his open hand. Resting in the palm was a battered leather notebook, swollen with water damage and re-stitched twice, its cover scarred with burns. "I promised you a story. One evening at a time. Chapter one starts with a courier bleeding at a temple gate, making a spectacular nuisance of himself." His voice caught, steadied. "You were furious with me. You bandaged my arm anyway. It's a good chapter. I think you'll like how it ends."

Mirabel looked at his hand. At the notebook. At the faces ringing the courtyard—strangers wearing the expressions of people who had already grieved her once and refused to do it twice.

Inside her, where a life used to be, the hollow ached. But hollows, she thought—hollows are where the echo lives. And echoes, given time and patience and stubborn love, become songs.

She put her hand in his.

"Start at the beginning," Mirabel said. "I want to know everything."
