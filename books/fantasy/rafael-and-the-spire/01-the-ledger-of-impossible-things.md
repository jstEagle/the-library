## 1. The Ledger of Impossible Things

The Gilded Hollow kept its accounts in three ledgers, and only one of them was legal.

The legal one lived behind the counting-house counter in plain view: a fat, honest book recording the sale of candles, lamp oil, tallow, thread, and other goods so dull that no magistrate had ever felt compelled to look past its cover. Beatrix Ravensworth maintained it faithfully. She rather enjoyed it. There was something soothing about a world where everything cost what it cost.

The second ledger lived under a loose floorboard beneath her chair, and it recorded trades of a different nature. A memory of rain on the harbor at dusk, traded for a memory of a wedding the seller wished to forget. Three years of a fisherman's luck, bottled in green glass, sold to a gambler who did not ask where it came from. The second ledger was written in Beatrix's own shorthand, which looked like the scratches a cat might leave on a door if the cat were literate and in a hurry.

The third ledger lived nowhere at all. It lived in Beatrix.

On this particular evening — a Thursday, grey with drizzle, the kind of night when the city of Vellmarin seemed to fold itself up like wet paper — she sat alone in the archive room with a candle, an inkpot, and the second ledger open before her, doing what she did every Thursday: the audit.

She worked with two pens. One wrote; the other she held between her teeth like a rose stem, because it was habit, and because Corvane said it made her look like a woman who took herself too seriously, and Beatrix considered that a compliment.

"Item forty-one," she murmured around the pen, turning a page. "One first kiss. Seller: unnamed, per standard practice. Buyer: the widow Ansel, who reports she has not been kissed since the war and would like to remember how it goes."

She made a small notation. *Condition: good. Slight smudging at edges, as is natural. Buyer satisfied.*

A first kiss was delicate merchandise. It arrived already worn soft, the way a coin wears soft from passing through many hands — except a kiss passed through no hands at all. It simply sat in a person, glowing faintly, until one day they decided they could live without it, or needed bread more than they needed the remembering.

Beatrix had handled eleven hundred first kisses in her career. She had never once kept one.

That was the rule. Her rule, self-imposed, older than her apprenticeship scars and just as permanent: *Never keep what you steal.* A thief of memories who kept souvenirs was not a thief but a magpie, and magpies ended badly. Magpies woke one morning unable to tell which life was theirs. Beatrix intended to die knowing exactly which life was hers, down to the last dull Thursday.

She turned the next leaf.

"Item forty-two. One mother's face." Her pen hesitated over the line, as it always did. "Seller: a man from the Tanners' Row, age thirty-four. Reason for sale: he says he cannot stop seeing her, and grief that won't sleep deserves wages."

*Buyer:* none yet. Mother's faces were hard to place. You could not sell a stranger's mother to just anyone; the face had to fit some empty shape in the buyer, or it sat in them wrong, like a key ground to the wrong lock. They kept it in the vault on the third shelf, wrapped in blue cloth, waiting. Beatrix checked the wrapping every week. Some things deserved careful keeping even when you were only holding them for someone else.

"Item forty-three." Here she sighed, set down the tooth-pen, and rubbed the bridge of her nose. "One crime, half-remembered."

This one had come in Tuesday, and it was trouble. The seller was a cooper with flour-white knuckles who had walked into the Hollow trembling and laid on the table the memory of something he'd done — or almost done, or watched done; even he couldn't say which, because the memory had been fraying for years, nibbled at the edges by guilt the way mice nibble at grain sacks. He wanted it gone entirely. He wanted, in his words, "to be a man with clean hands," which was the sort of thing people said right before they learned that memories were not stains you could launder out. Take away the memory of a wrong and the wrong remained, sitting in the body like cold weather. The cooper would still flinch at certain doors. He would never know why.

Beatrix had taken the trade anyway, because refusing a frightened man at your counter teaches him nothing except that frightened men should go to worse shops. But she had priced it honestly, which is to say low, and she had told him the truth, which is to say once, quietly, without letting him weep on the ledger.

Now she recorded its condition: *Fragmentary. Guilt has eaten roughly half. What remains is mostly hands, a doorway, and someone breathing wrong.* She paused. *Do not sell to anyone connected to Tanner's Row until provenance is understood.*

Because here was the thing about a half-remembered crime: somewhere out there was the person it happened to, or the person it was done beside, and if the missing half ever surfaced — if another seller walked in carrying the same doorway, the same wrong breathing — then the Hollow would be holding both halves of somebody's worst night. Beatrix liked problems she could solve with arithmetic. This was not one of those.

She blotted the entry, capped the ink, and allowed herself the audit's final ritual: counting the vault shelf-tags against the register. Memory-ware was stored in glass — small vials for moments, bottles for days, and for whole years, jars the size of a man's fist that glowed the color the life inside them had been. A jar of a childhood by the sea glowed green-grey, like water over sand. A jar of someone's wedding day burned gold. The cooper's crime, shelved in quarantine, gave off a dull brown light, the color of tea left too long.

Everything accounted for. Everything precise. Beatrix sat back and permitted herself, as she did at the end of every audit, one full minute of contentment.

It was a controlled contentment. That was important. Uncontrolled anything was how people got caught.

* *

The client arrived at half past nine, dripping, early by ten minutes, which marked him immediately as new. Experienced clients arrived late. Lateness said *I am casual about this illegal errand.* Punctuality said *I have been standing around the corner rehearsing.*

He was young — five-and-twenty, perhaps — with the soft hands of a clerk and the eyes of a man who had not slept in a week and had decided, sometime around the fourth sleepless dawn, that this was now a problem money could fix. He stood in the candle-and-tallow shop proper, pretending very badly to browse the lamp oil.

Beatrix finished shelving a row of honest candles, dusted her palms on her apron, and said pleasantly, "The wick oil on the third shelf will ruin any lamp you own. If you're buying, buy the second shelf."

He jumped. "I — yes. Right. I'm actually—"

"You're here about a trade." She untied her apron, folded it over the counter — the folding mattered, it told clients the shopkeeper portion of the evening had ended and the other portion had begun — and lifted the counter flap. "Through the back, if you please. Mind the step. It minds everyone."

The back room of the Gilded Hollow was deliberately unimpressive: a scarred table, two chairs, a candle, and a teapot that had seen more secrets than a confessor. Beatrix poured two cups without asking. Tea steadied sellers. Steady sellers signed honest prices instead of panicked ones.

"I'm told — that is, a friend said — that you can take things," the young man said. "Out of a person."

"I can take things out of a person, yes." She set his cup down. "I cannot put things back once they're gone, I cannot take things you don't truly wish to give, and I do not work in futures. Memories only. Past tense. Is that going to be a problem?"

"No. No, it's — it's the past that's the problem." He wrapped both hands around the cup like a man holding onto a rail in rough seas. "There's a night. Last autumn. My brother—" He stopped. Started again. Beatrix waited; waiting was most of the craft. "My brother and I quarreled. Badly. Things were said, and I said the worst ones, and three weeks later he took a posting on a ship south, and the ship went down off the Cape with all hands." His voice cracked neatly down the middle. "And now the last thing I remember of him alive is me, being hateful. Every time I close my eyes. Every single time. And I thought — I was told maybe it could be taken out."

Beatrix nodded slowly. She let the silence sit for exactly as long as it needed to, which in her professional judgment was four seconds.

"It can be taken out," she said. "Here is what happens, so there are no surprises. I'll touch your temples — just fingers, nothing frightening. I'll find the memory. Everyone's memories feel different; yours will probably feel like a splinter, given how often you've pressed on it. I lift it out. It goes into glass. And then it is mine, and it is gone from you, and you will wake tomorrow and know that your brother sailed south and died, but you will not carry that last night anymore. You'll grieve him clean. Or cleaner, anyway. Grief doesn't take orders."

"And the — the part of me that remembers? Will I miss it?"

"Of course you'll miss it. You'll miss it like an amputee misses a hand. You'll reach for it out of habit and find it gone, and that will ache, especially at first." She met his eyes steadily. "Anyone who tells you otherwise is selling you a lie at a premium price. What I can promise is that it stops bleeding. Right now that memory is a wound that reopens nightly. I can make it a scar. Scars are quiet."

He stared into his tea. "What does it cost me?"

"The fee is nine crowns, and I'll want something true in trade besides coin — a small memory, your choosing, nothing precious. The trade keeps the scales balanced. Magic that runs only on coin gets greedy, and greedy magic gets sloppy, and sloppy magic gets us all hanged." She said it lightly, the way one recites a familiar prayer, and watched him almost smile.

"And the memory itself. The night. What happens to it?"

"It goes in the vault. Sealed, tagged, and sold only to someone whose emptiness fits it — which, frankly, may be no one. A quarrel with a brother is a specific shape of grief." She tilted her head. "Sometimes, in a year or two, a seller comes back and buys their own memory home again. That happens more than you'd think. People discover that the hateful night is also the last night, and last nights get scarce."

His chin trembled. He mastered it with visible effort, the way young men do, spending enormous strength on a small victory.

"My friend said you had a rule," he said. "About not keeping what you take."

"Never keep what you steal." Beatrix shrugged. "A thief who pockets souvenirs stops being able to tell her own shelves from her own head. I have one life. It's small and it smells of candle wax and I intend to keep it exactly as it is."

"That sounds lonely," the young man said, with the blunt tactlessness of exhaustion.

Beatrix laughed — a real laugh, short and surprised out of her. "It sounds orderly," she corrected. "Lonely is orderly's cousin. We're not close."

He set down the cup. Held out his hand, then pulled it back, then held it out again. "All right," he whispered. "Take it."

"Nine crowns first," said Beatrix, who was precise about sequence, "and pick your trade-memory. Something within the last year, nothing larger than an afternoon. And I warn you — most people choose wrong. They hand me something they think is worthless that turns out to be load-bearing. Choose carefully."

He paid. He chose — an afternoon of watching boats, which she accepted with an internal wince because boat-watching afternoons were frequently load-bearing — and then he closed his eyes and Beatrix placed her fingertips to his temples and went to work.

Inside, the clerk's mind smelled of ink and damp wool, and near the center, wrapped in thorns of its own making, sat the night. She took hold of it gently, the way one lifts a bird with a broken wing, and drew it free. It came reluctantly, tearing a little at the root, and she soothed the tear with the small trick she'd never been able to explain to anyone — smoothing the place where the memory had been into something like smooth water, so that tomorrow he would not keep reaching into an absence and cutting himself on the edges of it.

The vial in her palm filled with a reddish-brown glow, shot through with one bright thread of gold — the gold being, presumably, whatever good had been tangled in that night before the bad swallowed it. There usually was some. Beatrix had learned to look for it. It helped her respect the merchandise.

"There," she said aloud, in the ordinary room again, corking the vial. "Done. Tomorrow you'll test it and panic because you think it didn't work, because you'll still hurt. It worked. The hurt is grief now, not a knife. Give it a month."

The clerk flexed his fingers, blinked, wept briefly and quietly into his sleeve without appearing to notice he was doing it, thanked her twice, and went out into the drizzle looking lighter by several pounds.

Beatrix labeled the vial in her neat, narrow hand — *One quarrel, final. Gold thread present. Vault, shelf six* — and sat for a moment with her hands flat on the table, feeling the familiar settling of a completed trade. Somewhere in the city a mother was walking to a door that no longer had a brother behind it, and did not know why the walk felt easier tonight, and would never know. That was the work. You did invisible mending on invisible tears, and you were invisible too, and on the whole Beatrix Ravensworth found that arrangement satisfactory.

Mostly satisfactory. On Thursdays, after audits, there was sometimes a quarter-hour when the satisfaction developed a hairline crack in it, and she heard herself saying *never keep what you steal* in her own voice and wondered, distantly, why a rule that solid needed repeating so often.

She blew out the candle, locked the second ledger beneath the floorboard, and went upstairs to her rooms above the shop, where she kept exactly one personal memory in glass — not stolen, hers, bought honestly with the first coins she'd ever earned — and did not open it, and went to bed.

* *

Three things happened in quick succession over the following days, each of which Beatrix logged mentally under *trouble*, in ascending order.

First: the price of bottled luck doubled at market, which meant demand was up, which meant fear was up. Luck was what frightened people bought.

Second: Corvane called a guild meeting for Sunday, full attendance, no exceptions — and Corvane's meetings were weather. You learned to read the sky of him.

Third: on Friday morning, while Beatrix was sweeping the tallow-shop floor and the second ledger lay safely under her chair, the bell above the front door rang, and a man stepped in out of the rain wearing white and grey — the colors of the crown's inquisition — with a warrant-case under one arm and rain in his dark hair, and he looked directly at Beatrix across the candle racks with an expression she recognized, and had spent seven years successfully not thinking about.

"Routine inspection," said Rafael, the crown's inquisitor, in a voice carefully stripped of everything it used to hold. "I'll need to see your ledgers."

Beatrix set down her broom, smiled the smile of a perfectly lawful candle merchant, and began calculating which of her lies would need sharpening first.
