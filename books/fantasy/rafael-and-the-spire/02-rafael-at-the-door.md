## 2. Rafael at the Door

The rain had been falling on Corvane's Row since dawn, and by the time Rafael Dain reached the Gilded Hollow, his boots had given up any pretense of dignity and simply squelched.

He stood across the street for a moment under the dripping eaves of a chandler's shop, doing what he always did before an inspection: nothing. Officially, this was called *assessing the premises*. Unofficially, it was called being afraid of a bookkeeper.

The Hollow itself looked exactly as it was meant to look — unremarkable, respectable, forgettable. A narrow storefront of dark green wood between a tailor and a moneylender, its windows displaying the guild's legitimate trade: clocks of dubious provenance, lockets, a taxidermied owl that had been staring at passersby with the same expression of mild disappointment for eleven years. Above the door, the guild sign — a gilded keyhole — caught what little light the morning offered.

Somewhere behind that door were ledgers full of stolen first kisses. Somewhere behind that door was Beatrix Ravensworth.

Rafael adjusted his inquisitor's sash, which insisted on riding up like a child's collar no matter how he arranged it, and crossed the street.

*Routine inspection,* he reminded himself. *Rumors of gifted smugglers moving memory-goods through the harbor district. You knock, you ask questions, you look at their licenses, you leave. Nobody gets hurt. Nobody gets arrested. It is a Tuesday.*

The bell above the door chimed as he entered, a small, clear sound entirely at odds with the way it made his stomach drop.

The front room smelled of beeswax and old paper and, faintly, of something sweeter underneath — the smell he'd learned to recognize in confiscated goods, the smell of bottled memory. Vanilla and rain on hot stone. Every vial of stolen past had its own scent, but they all shared that ghost of sweetness, like a room where someone had once been happy.

"Good morning," said the clerk at the counter, a young man with ink-stained fingers and the carefully blank face of someone who had rehearsed this moment. "Welcome to the Gilded Hollow. Can I help you find something? We have a lovely selection of commemorative lockets."

"I'm sure you do." Rafael set his credentials on the counter. "Inquisitor Rafael Dain, Office of the Crown. Routine inspection."

The clerk looked at the papers. Looked at Rafael. Looked at the papers again, as though hoping they might have changed into something less catastrophic.

"I'll fetch the registrar," he said, and vanished through the back curtain with the urgency of a man fleeing a fire he had personally started.

Rafael waited. He examined the lockets. He examined the owl. The owl examined him back, or seemed to.

And then she came through the curtain, and every careful thought he had arranged like papers on a desk went sliding to the floor.

Beatrix Ravensworth had not changed. That was the unfair part. Three years, and she was still small and precise and composed, still with that way of holding her head slightly tilted, as though the world were a ledger entry she was checking for errors. Her hair was pinned up with what appeared to be an actual pencil. Her sleeves were rolled to the elbow. There was ink on her left wrist — a smudge, accidental, human — and Rafael found himself absurdly fixated on it, because everything else about her was armor, and the ink was not.

"Inquisitor." She inclined her head the exact correct degree. Not too deep — she wasn't afraid. Not too shallow — she wasn't insulted. "You're wet."

"It's raining."

"So it is. We do get reports." She came around the counter, produced a cloth from somewhere — of course she had a cloth ready, she had a cloth ready for every conceivable emergency — and set it on the counter beside a chair, an invitation to sit without quite saying so. "Tea?"

"That won't be necessary."

"It's already brewed. Refusing it would be wasteful, and I know how you feel about waste." She was already pouring. Two cups. She had poured two cups before he'd said a word, which meant she had known he was coming, which meant the clerk hadn't fled to fetch her so much as fled to warn her, which meant Rafael had been watched from the moment he stopped across the street.

*Assessing the premises.* She had assessed him assessing her premises, and made tea accordingly.

He sat. The chair was more comfortable than it had any right to be, which he suspected was deliberate. Everything in the Gilded Hollow was deliberate. "Miss Ravensworth."

"Mistress Ravensworth," she corrected mildly, handing him the cup. "I own the ledgers now. Registrar, keeper, and chief cook of the back-room kettle." She sat opposite him, folded her hands, and gave him the smile she presumably gave auditors. It was a good smile. Warm at the edges, guarded at the center, like a house with all its shutters painted a welcoming color. "Now. What does the crown want with our little shop of clocks and lockets?"

"Rumors," Rafael said. "Gifted smugglers. Memory-goods moving through the harbor. Your guild holds trading rights on... unconventional commodities. I'm required to inspect."

"Required," she repeated, tasting the word. "By whom?"

"The Office of the Crown."

"Which answers to whom?"

"You know how the hierarchy works, Beatrix."

"Do I?" She sipped her tea. "I know the Office used to send old Master Hollen, who drank our tea, complimented our owl, and left within the hour. I know you're not Master Hollen. So either the crown has grown suspicious of us, or the crown has grown suspicious of everyone, and I confess I'm curious which."

It was too perceptive by half, and delivered in that light, almost teasing tone she'd always had, the one that made interrogation feel like conversation and conversation feel like a trap laid with clean linen. Rafael drank his tea because his hands needed something to do. It was good tea. Of course it was good tea. She probably kept a ledger entry rating the teas by which inspector preferred which.

"The rumors name your guild," he said. "That's all. If there's nothing to find, there's nothing to find."

"Mm." She rose, smoothed her apron, and gestured toward the back. "Then let's not keep the crown waiting. Ledgers are through here. Mind the third stair; it lies."

"The stair lies?"

"It creaks on the fourth step instead of the third. People brace for the wrong one and startle themselves. I've been meaning to have it seen to for two years." She glanced back at him, and for half a heartbeat the registrar's mask slipped and there was something else underneath — the old look, the one from the archive-house, from the night of the lantern festival, from every moment he had spent three years trying not to remember. "You always did startle easily. For an inquisitor."

"For a *bookkeeper*, you always did talk too much."

"And yet you're following me anyway."

He was. Down the corridor, past shelves of boxed inventory, past a door labeled *PRIVATE, AND WE MEAN IT* in handwriting he recognized with a lurch — rounder then, younger, the letters still deciding what they wanted to be — and down the lying stair into the archive.

* *

The archive of the Gilded Hollow occupied what had once been a wine cellar, and it was beautiful in the way that only illegal things are permitted to be beautiful. Rows of cedar shelving. Hundreds of ledgers, spines color-coded by commodity. Lamps with green glass shades that turned the light the color of underwater. And everywhere that faint sweet smell, stronger here, layered over years — vanilla and rain and a hundred other ghosts.

Rafael had raided places like this before. Confiscated archives in Vell's Crossing, in the Salt Quarter. They had all felt like crime scenes.

This felt like a library. That was the trouble with Beatrix Ravensworth. She could make anything feel legitimate. She could make *you* feel legitimate, standing in the middle of contraband with a warrant in your pocket, and some traitorous part of you would think: *well, surely if it's catalogued this nicely—*

"Commodity ledgers here." She ran a finger along the shelf edges without touching them. "Trade licenses, framed, on the wall behind you — yes, the ones with the seals, don't squint, they're real. Client consent registers, third case. And the audit trail for every transaction going back six years, cross-referenced, initialed, and available for your review." She pulled a ledger from the shelf and set it on the reading table with a soft thump. "Shall I leave you to it, or would you prefer me hovering? Some inspectors like hovering. Old Hollen liked hovering. He also liked to be told his deductions were clever, but I suspect you're above flattery."

"I'm above most things," Rafael said, opening the ledger. "It's the occupational hazard."

She laughed. It escaped her before she could catch it — a short, startled sound, quickly folded away — and Rafael pretended very hard that he hadn't noticed, and failed completely, and drank tea to cover the failure.

He worked. Or performed working, which in an inspection amounts to the same thing. He checked entries against licenses. He checked seals against registries. He asked questions designed to sound thorough: provenance of the clock stock, chain of custody for consigned goods, the guild's procedure for handling items of uncertain origin.

"Uncertain origin," Beatrix repeated, writing something down as he spoke — notes, she claimed, though he'd have wagered the notes said something like *asked the usual questions, answered adequately, still has terrible taste in sashes.* "Everything has an origin, Inquisitor. The question is whether anyone wants to claim it. Take this—" she lifted a locket from the display tray she'd brought down, ostensibly to demonstrate documentation practices, "—sold to us by a widow on Candle Street. Inside, a portrait of a man who may or may not have been her husband. Do I need to know? Does the crown? Or does the widow simply need forty crowns and the locket needs a home?"

"And if the man in the portrait was someone else's husband?"

"Then somewhere there's another widow, and I've made two people richer in grief than they started, which is more than most trades can say." She set the locket down. Her voice stayed light, but her eyes didn't. "We sell objects, Inquisitor. Objects with histories attached, yes. Histories are sticky. But we're a curiosity broker, not a — " she waved a hand, " — whatever it is the rumors say."

"A smuggler of memories."

"What a phrase." She shivered theatrically. "As though you could put a memory in a bottle."

"As though," Rafael agreed, and held her gaze just long enough.

She held it back. Neither of them blinked. Somewhere overhead, the owl maintained its vigil.

* *

Here is what Rafael Dain knew about Beatrix Ravensworth, in the order he had learned it:

First, that she was a junior archivist at the Hall of Records who alphabetized things that did not require alphabetizing, and who had once corrected a ministerial decree's punctuation in red ink, and who had been, at twenty-two, the only person in the building who knew where anything was.

Second, that she had helped him — a young constable then, out of his depth — untangle a forgery case that had stumped three investigators, working through two nights over cold tea, laughing at his jokes even when they were bad, especially when they were bad.

Third, that she had disappeared. Left no address. The Hall of Records said she'd resigned; the lodging-house said she'd paid ahead; the city, indifferent, said nothing at all.

Fourth — two years later, when he'd made inquisitor and gained access to certain restricted registries — that the Gilded Hollow employed a registrar named B. Ravensworth, and that the guild traded in categories of goods that the registry euphemized as *intangibles, sentimental class.* And that among the intangibles, listed with prices and dates and initials, were things like *a childhood summer, complete* and *the face of a mother, deceased, well-preserved.*

Fifth, and last, and never spoken aloud to any living soul: that he had stood outside the Gilded Hollow on a rainy evening much like this one, three years ago, warrant unsigned in his pocket, and had turned around and walked away, and had told himself it was because the evidence was thin, and had known, in the honest cellar of himself where he kept such things, that it was because it was her.

And here is what Beatrix Ravensworth knew about him — he was nearly certain she knew it, because she was Beatrix — which was all of the above, plus, probably, several things he didn't know about himself yet. She had that kind of mind. She read people the way she read ledgers: thoroughly, quietly, and with small red marks in the margins.

"Your tea's gone cold," she said.

"I've been drinking it slowly. On principle."

"You've been avoiding the fourth case." She nodded toward a shelf near the far wall, apart from the others, locked behind a grate of black iron. "Recent acquisitions. High-value. That's where memory-goods would live, if we kept any. Which we don't."

"No?"

"We keep *heirlooms.* Objects of sentimental significance, consigned and resold according to license." A pause, delicate as a scale finding balance. "The grate is because the pieces are valuable, not because they're forbidden. You're welcome to inspect. I'll fetch the key."

She crossed to the cabinet where the keys hung, and Rafael looked at the iron grate, and understood that he was being offered a choice dressed as a courtesy.

If he inspected the fourth case and found nothing, the inspection ended clean, and the rumor died, and the Office moved on to some other district. If he inspected the fourth case and found something — a vial, a bottle, anything with that sweet ghost-smell — then he would have to arrest her. There would be no turning around in the rain this time. There would be paperwork, and chains, and a tribunal, and Beatrix Ravensworth's clever hands in irons, and the thought arrived in his chest like a swallowed stone.

Or she had nothing to hide, and the offer was genuine, and he was a paranoid man letting an old affection rot his judgment.

Or — the third possibility, the one that fit her best — the case contained exactly enough nothing to satisfy an honest inspector and exactly enough something to ruin a dishonest one, and she had built it that way herself, and she was watching him right now, key in hand, to see which kind of man the crown had sent.

"Actually," Rafael said, closing the ledger, "I've seen what I need."

Her eyebrows rose a fraction. "Have you?"

"The trade records reconcile. The licenses are current. Your documentation is — " he searched for the word, and hated that the true one arrived first, " — exemplary. Whatever the rumors say, Mistress Ravensworth, I find no evidence of gifted smuggling at the Gilded Hollow."

"How disappointing for the rumors." Something in her shoulders loosened, barely visible, like a sail taking in wind. "Will there be a follow-up inspection?"

"There's always a follow-up inspection. That's how bureaucracies show affection." He stood, and she walked him back up the lying stair, past the PRIVATE door, through the front room where the clerk was dusting the same shelf he had been dusting for an hour, and to the green door and the gray day beyond it.

At the threshold, she stopped him.

"Inquisitor. One thing." Her voice had changed — quieter, stripped of the performance. "The rumors. Where did they come from?"

"You know I can't disclose—"

"I'm not asking for names. I'm asking whether they came from *above.*" Her eyes were steady on his, and beneath the question he heard the thing underneath the question, the same thing he'd been hearing for weeks in the corridors of the Spire: whispers about edicts drafted in haste, about registrations and 'treatments,' about a king who signed things no one had seen him read. "Because the guildmasters are frightened, Rafael. Properly frightened, not performative-frightened. And frightened people with ledgers start making copies of things, and hiding things, and I would hate for a routine inspection to become something else because nobody talked to each other like people."

It was the closest she had come, in three years, to saying *I know what you are and what I am and what we were, and I am asking you to be careful with me.*

"I'll write in my report that the premises are orderly," he said. "I'll note that the registrar was cooperative."

"Cooperative," she murmured. "High praise, from the Office of the Crown."

"The highest they let me give." He hesitated on the step, rain beginning again, fine as sifted flour. "Beatrix. If the rumors change. If they stop being rumors. If the Office asks me to come back with something other than a teacup — " he couldn't finish it, so he finished it badly, the way he finished everything with her, " — take care of the ledgers."

Something flickered across her face. Understanding, maybe. Or grief. With her it was often the same expression.

"Mind the stair," she said, and closed the door gently, and the bell chimed its small clear chime, and Rafael Dain stood alone in the rain feeling like a man who had just been handed a message written in invisible ink.

* *

He was three streets away when he remembered the page.

During the inspection — while she'd fetched the key, while his attention had been on the iron grate and the choice inside it — he had turned a page in the acquisition ledger, and there, in the margin, in that round young handwriting he'd have known anywhere, was a note. Not contraband. Not evidence. Just a line in pencil, in her hand, dated eleven days ago:

*Client 114 — payment received in kind. One memory, first-class, unregistered origin. HOLD FOR REVIEW. Do not file. — B.R.*

An unregistered memory. Held off the books. *Do not file.*

Every regulation he had sworn to said he should march straight back and demand the case, the client, the vial. Every regulation, and also Lord Chancellor Veyra's new directive, issued that very week, that irregularities involving intangible goods be escalated directly to the Spire — directly, the directive emphasized, to the Chancellor's own office, bypassing normal channels, a detail that had struck Rafael as odd at the time and struck him as considerably worse than odd now.

He thought of Beatrix's face at the door. *Frightened people with ledgers start making copies of things.*

He thought of the directive. *Bypassing normal channels.*

And he thought — with the particular shame of a man catching himself mid-rationalization — that he was already composing the excuse: *one page, retained for follow-up inquiry, standard practice, nothing unusual.* He was going to steal a page from a smuggler's ledger to protect a smuggler from his own superiors, and he was going to file it in the drawer of his desk where he kept the other things he refused to look at, and he was going to tell himself it meant nothing.

Rafael Dain ducked into the chandler's doorway, took out his notebook, and copied the entry in his own careful hand — date, client number, initials, all of it.

Then he tore the original page free, folded it small, and put it in his breast pocket, over his heart, where he kept the other evidence of his slow treason.

Behind him, the rain went on falling on Corvane's Row, and in the Gilded Hollow, he had no doubt at all, Beatrix Ravensworth was already rewriting the ledger to account for the missing page — and smiling while she did it.
