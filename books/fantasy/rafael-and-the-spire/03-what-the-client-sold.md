## 3. What the Client Sold

The rain came down on the Gilded Hollow like a polite apology—steady, gray, and impossible to argue with—and inside the counting room, Beatrix Ravensworth did what she always did on wet afternoons: she balanced the impossible.

The ledger lay open before her, its pages soft as old cloth from years of handling. Ink in three colors: black for trades completed, red for trades pending, and green for the small category of things that had gone wrong, which Corvane insisted be recorded honestly because, as he liked to say, "a guild that lies to itself about its mistakes will eventually lie to itself about everything." Beatrix had privately amended this over the years to *and some of us lie beautifully*, but she kept the amendment where it belonged—in her head.

Tonight's entries:

*One first kiss, traded by a cooper's widow for the memory of her husband's laugh. She said she couldn't bear to carry both.*

*Half a crime, sold by a man who would not say whose half he kept.*

*A mother's face, bought back at last by a dockhand who had sold it in a bad winter and spent eleven years saving up to reclaim it. He wept on the stairs. Beatrix had pretended not to see, because pretending not to see was one of the services the Hollow provided free of charge.*

She dipped her pen, blotted it against the well's lip, and wrote the date in her small, exact hand—the hand Rafael Voss had once described, in another life, as "the handwriting of someone who has never once been late." She had thrown an inkwell at him for that. She still thought of it sometimes, the way you think of a scar: with irritation, and something warmer underneath that you didn't examine in good light.

A knock at the counting room door. Three taps, then a pause, then two more—the client's knock, the nervous one.

"Enter," Beatrix said, and closed the ledger with the soft finality of a woman putting away knives.

* * *

Lady Seraphine Calloway was dying the way rich people often died: slowly, expensively, and with excellent posture. She was seventy-one, dressed in mourning silk so fine it seemed embarrassed to be worn to a back-street guildhouse, and she moved through the counting room doorway as though it were a state chamber and Beatrix a duchess who simply hadn't been informed yet.

"Madam Ravensworth." She extended a gloved hand.

"Beatrix," said Beatrix, shaking it. "We're informal here. It frightens the clients less."

"It does not frighten me."

"No," Beatrix agreed, studying her. "It doesn't. Sit, please. Tea?"

"I have brought my own." Lady Calloway produced a silver flask from a reticule and poured a thimbleful into a cup Beatrix hadn't seen her take out either. The smell of brandy cut through the candlewax. "I am told you are the best auditor in the city. That you can hold a memory without bruising it."

"I can. Whether I'm the best depends on which of my colleagues you ask, and I'd advise against asking Fenwick, who is a liar."

"And are you?"

Beatrix smiled her shopkeeper's smile. "I'm precise. In this trade, precision is better than honesty. Honesty is a mood. Precision is a craft."

Something flickered across the old woman's face—not quite approval, but the cousin of it. "Then let us be precise. I am dying. The physicians give me until midsummer, perhaps. I have arranged my estate, my letters, my tomb, which is frankly excessive but my late husband designed it and I haven't the heart to simplify it. There remains one item I wish to dispose of before the end. Not to sell, precisely. To *place*."

"We don't store memories, my lady. We trade them. Storage is how you get haunted."

"So I've heard." Lady Calloway set down her cup with a click. "What I want is to trade it away entirely. To forget. And in exchange—" she paused, and for the first time her composure showed its seams, "—in exchange I want to know it goes somewhere kind. Somewhere it will be *kept*. Not sold to some gawker. Kept."

"That's not usually how it works."

"I am not usually how it works."

Beatrix leaned back. Outside, the rain thickened, drumming on the shutters like impatient fingers. Twenty-two years in the Hollow had taught her every species of client: the desperate, the greedy, the curious, the heartbroken. But this one sat differently. This one had rehearsed.

"What memory?" Beatrix asked.

"The king's."

The candleflame leaned in the draft. Beatrix did not move, which took practice, because everything in her had just gone very still and very awake.

"Explain," she said.

* * *

Twenty-three years ago, Lady Seraphine Calloway had been a young woman at court, pretty and unimportant, which in palace terms meant invisible, which meant privileged. She had attended the young king Aldric at a harvest festival in his third year of reign—before the wars, before the edicts, before the crown on his head became the only thing anyone saw when they looked at him.

"I danced with him," she said. "Once. A country dance, nothing grand. And afterward we stood on a balcony while the fireworks went off over the river, and he talked to me for perhaps ten minutes as though I were a person rather than a reflection of his own importance. And in those ten minutes—" her voice caught, smoothed itself, "—he flinched."

"Flinched?"

"At his own crown. He reached up to straighten it—it had slipped, you understand, it was a windy night—and the moment his fingers touched the metal, he went white as milk and snatched his hand away as if it had burned him. Then he laughed and made a joke about clumsy goldsmiths. But I saw his eyes." She looked at Beatrix steadily. "I have carried that memory for fifty years, madam. I have taken it out and looked at it more times than I can count, trying to decide what I saw. And now that I'm dying, I find I cannot take the answer with me. Because I think the answer matters. I think it has *always* mattered, and everyone was too busy looking at the crown to look at the king."

The rain filled the silence. Beatrix found her own hands had gone quiet on the ledger, and she made herself pick up the pen, because a trader's hands should never look idle.

"You want to sell me the king's flinch," she said.

"I want to sell you the whole of it. The dance, the balcony, the fireworks, the flinch. Ten minutes, complete and undamaged. I've protected it carefully—I know how these things degrade when handled roughly. And in exchange I'll take..." she waved a vague hand, "...something dull. A market day. My wedding breakfast. Something beige."

"Lady Calloway. What you're describing is a trade of extraordinary value for almost nothing. Either you don't understand what that memory might be worth—which I doubt—or you want something else and haven't said it."

The old woman smiled then, and for a moment Beatrix saw the girl who had once made a king laugh on a balcony.

"I want it to matter," she said simply. "Fifty years I've been the only person alive who knows the king was afraid of his own crown before there was any reason to be. If I die with it, it dies with me, and then it was just... an old woman's fancy. Give it to someone who will do something with it. That's all. That's the whole of my price."

Beatrix looked at her for a long moment. Then she opened the ledger to a fresh page, and wrote, in her small exact hand: *Received of Lady S. Calloway, one memory, royal, approx. 10 min., condition excellent.* And beneath it, in the column where the payment was always recorded, she hesitated—then wrote: *one promise, kept.*

"Come here," she said. "This part stings."

* * *

Taking a memory was nothing like the stories said. No swirling lights, no dramatic gasp. It felt, from the taker's side, like lifting a thread from a tapestry so fine you could barely see the gap left behind. From the giver's side, Lady Calloway later reported, it felt like setting down a basket she'd forgotten she was carrying.

Beatrix held the thread of it lightly, tasted its edges the way an archivist tastes paper—fireworks, sulfur-sweet; music, a fiddle slightly flat; the king's voice, young and warm and quick with humor—and then, because the trade required it, she pressed the memory into the prepared vessel: a plain glass bottle, stoppered with wax, labeled in her own hand.

And then she did the thing she had never done in twenty-two years.

She kept a copy.

It wasn't even difficult. That was the terrible part. The gift made copying as easy as breathing—a whisper of attention, a second vessel, done before the first was sealed. Every gifted person in the city knew the temptation. Every one of them knew the rule. *Never keep what you steal,* Corvane said, and he said it because the ones who kept things stopped being traders and started being collectors, and collectors ended badly—hoarding other lives until their own wore thin as borrowed cloth.

Beatrix sealed the copy anyway. She told herself it was professional diligence. She told herself a memory concerning the king was exactly the sort of thing the Hollow ought to have on file, given the rumors coming down from the palace lately, the criers practicing proclamations in the square, the way Corvane had gone quiet and watchful for a fortnight.

She told herself several things. She was good at telling herself things. It was, she reflected, not so different a skill from taking memories: you lifted the true version out, and pressed a workable one into the space.

Lady Calloway rose, steadier than she'd arrived, lighter in a way that had nothing to do with her cane. At the door she paused.

"You kept a copy," she said.

Beatrix went cold. "My lady—"

"Don't apologize. I hoped you would." The old woman's eyes were bright and terribly clear. "Someone should remember him as he was. Before whatever is happening to him finishes happening. Good evening, madam Ravensworth. Do something worthy of it."

And she was gone down the stairs, her cane tapping a rhythm like a slow heartbeat, leaving Beatrix alone with two bottles where the ledger said there should be one.

* * *

She should have poured the copy out. There was a sink in the corner of the counting room for exactly that purpose—spoiled goods, failed trades, the occasional thing too dangerous to keep. You tipped the bottle, the memory ran out into the water, and by morning it was gone, dissolved into the city's plumbing along with everything else nobody wanted to remember.

Beatrix stood over the sink for a long time.

Then she wrapped the bottle in cloth, hid it inside the false binding of a book of tax records—no thief in history had ever voluntarily opened a book of tax records—and went home through the rain with her collar up and her heart behaving oddly, like a clock that had developed opinions.

Her rooms above the chandler's shop were small and smelled of tallow and old paper, and she loved them with the fierce, unsentimental love of a woman who had grown up with neither. She lit no fire. She ate bread and cheese standing up. And then, because she was weak, or curious, or both—curiosity being merely weakness with better handwriting—she took out the bottle, unstopped it, and touched the memory with one finger.

Sleep took her between one breath and the next.

* * *

She dreamed the king's dream.

Fireworks first—green and gold bursting over the river, their light broken into coins on the black water. Music behind, a fiddle running slightly flat through a harvest reel. The smell of woodsmoke and spilled cider and crushed grass. And warmth beside her—no, *she* was beside *him*; she was wearing his body the way you wear a coat in a dream, seeing from behind his eyes—

Young. Gods, he had been *young*. Twenty-four, maybe, with a jaw still deciding whether to be regal and hair the color of dark honey falling loose from a crown that sat on him like a joke everyone was too polite to laugh at. His cheeks hurt from smiling. His feet hurt from dancing. He was holding a cup of cider he had no intention of drinking, because kings were watched even at festivals, especially at festivals, and a drunk king was a story that would outlive them all.

Beside him, Lady Seraphine at nineteen, laughing at something he'd said, and the strange luxury of it—that she laughed at *him*, not at his shadow. Do you know, he was thinking, that in six hours no one will look me in the eye? They'll look at the crown and talk slightly above my head, as though I'm a shelf it rests on—

The wind came off the river and the crown slipped, just a little, just enough, and his hand went up to steady it without thinking.

The moment his fingers touched the metal, the dream *changed*.

Cold. Not the wind's cold—another kind, from underneath, from inside the gold itself, a cold like a door opening in a house he hadn't known he owned. And something looked back at him through his own touch. Something patient. Something *hungry* in the way winter is hungry, without malice, without hurry, certain that everything ends up eaten eventually.

He snatched his hand away. His heart slammed. On the balcony, Lady Seraphine saw him go white and said, "Your Majesty?" and he laughed—gods, the discipline of that laugh, Beatrix thought, watching from inside his skin, feeling the terror underneath it like a stone under a rug—and said something about clumsy goldsmiths, and the moment passed, and the fireworks went on, and the crown settled back onto his head warm as a sleeping animal, and he spent the rest of the night very careful not to touch it again.

He did not tell anyone. Of course he did not tell anyone. Who does a king tell? And by morning it had softened into something he could doubt—a trick of the wind, a chill, a young man's imagination overheated by fireworks. He doubted it for a year. He doubted it for five. He married, and governed, and signed what was put in front of him, and learned to let the crown be placed on his head by other hands so that he never had to touch it himself, and if anyone noticed that the king flinched at his own coronation regalia, no one was foolish enough to mention it.

And the last thing in the dream, the thing Lady Calloway could not have known because it happened after she'd gone back to the dance: Aldric alone in his chambers that night, sitting on the edge of his bed, reaching up slowly—so slowly, like a boy testing a stove—and laying one fingertip against the crown where it sat on its stand.

Nothing. Cold gold. Ordinary.

*I imagined it,* he told himself, with relief that tasted almost like grief.

*You didn't,* said the crown, in a voice like embers settling.

Beatrix woke with a gasp, upright in bed, her hand pressed flat against her own forehead as though checking for a fever—or checking that her head was still hers.

Gray light at the window. Rain finished. The chandler's cart below, rattling toward the market. Ordinary sounds. Her own room. Her own skull.

But her fingertip, when she looked at it, remembered cold.

She sat there a long while, doing what she did best: cataloguing. *Item: the king is not mad, or not only mad. Item: the crown is not a crown. Item: Lady Calloway knew, or guessed, or feared, and died lighter for handing it to me. Item:* —and here her pen, in the ledger of herself, hesitated over the entry— *item: I broke the rule, and instead of regretting it, I want to know more. Which is how every disaster in every ledger begins.*

Down in the square, faintly, a crier had begun to practice. She couldn't make out the words yet. But she could hear the rhythm of them, formal and terrible, repeated over and over like a man learning a sentence he'd been ordered to speak.

Beatrix got up, dressed, and went to the window.

Below, in the pale morning, the crier unrolled his proclamation and began, for the third time that hour, to read.
