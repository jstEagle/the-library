## 4. The Proclamation

The bells began at dawn, and Beatrix knew before she opened her eyes that something had gone wrong in the world above.

The Gilded Hollow kept its own hours. Down here, beneath the tannery on Vetch Street, dawn was a rumor and midnight was a suggestion, and the only bells that mattered were the small brass ones Corvane strung over the archive door to catch thieves. The city's church bells did not ring for them. The city did not know they existed. That was the entire point of the Hollow.

But these bells rang wrong—quick, urgent, overlapping, every tower in the capital at once—and by the time Beatrix reached the common room, half-dressed with her hair still braided from the night before, the whole guild was already crowded around the listening-tube.

The listening-tube was Odette's invention: a copper pipe that ran up through three floors of masonry to a vent grate behind a baker's shop on Cordwainer Lane. It brought the street into the cellar, faint and tinny, like the city whispering down a well. Usually it carried nothing but cart wheels and arguments about bread prices. This morning it carried a voice.

"By decree of His Majesty King Aldric the Third," the crier was saying, in the flat practiced bellow of a man who had been awake since before the bells and would rather not discuss it, "all persons bearing the unlicensed gift of mind-shaping are commanded to present themselves for registration within fourteen days. Registration is mandatory. Refusal will be treated as treason. Registered persons will then be summoned for treatment."

"Treatment," repeated Odette, who ran the memory-vaults and had a voice like a drawer full of knives being sorted. "What manner of treatment?"

Nobody answered her. The crier went on: "Let no citizen harbor the gifted. Let no citizen conceal them. Rewards are offered for information leading to discovery. The king loves his people, and moves to protect them."

"The king," said Fenwick, the lock-boy, who was seventeen and had never once managed to be quiet, "loves his people. Right. And I'm the Duchess of Marrowmere."

"Fenwick." Corvane's voice came from the doorway, and the room went still the way water goes still when something large passes under it.

Corvane, master of the Gilded Hollow, was a broad, gray man built like a piece of furniture intended to outlive its owner. He crossed the common room without hurrying, which was somehow worse than hurrying, and put one hand flat against the copper tube as though he could feel the proclamation through it.

"Fourteen days," he said. "Then registration. Then treatment." He turned. "Does anyone in this room believe the crown has invented a cure for what we are?"

Silence. Somewhere upstairs the bakery was already open; Beatrix could smell the day's first loaves seeping down through the stone, warm and ordinary and utterly indifferent.

"Then we have work," Corvane said. "All of you. Now."

* * *

They worked through the morning, and the work was fire.

It fell to Beatrix to sort the loose records—the pages that lived outside the ledgers, in the drawers and pigeonholes of the archive where she had spent eleven years building a system so elegant that even Corvane, who trusted nothing he could not burn, had once called it beautiful. Client intake slips. Trade confirmations. The little annotated cards on which she recorded, in her precise clerk's hand, exactly which memory had been bought, from whom, for what price, and with what cautions.

She had always thought of the archive as a kind of mercy. Every page meant someone had walked into the Hollow carrying something too heavy—a grief, a guilt, a face they could no longer bear to see—and walked out lighter, and somewhere in the vault below, in a bottle or a wax seal or a folded scrap of dream-silk, that weight waited safely in trust until they might want it back.

Now every page was a list of names.

"Burn order goes oldest first," Corvane said, moving along the sorting tables with a lamp in one hand and no expression at all. "Loose papers before ledgers. Ledgers—" he paused, and Beatrix saw him do the arithmetic everyone in the room was doing, the arithmetic of years of trades and trusts and secrets, "—ledgers go last, and only if we must. A ledger can be hidden. Ash cannot be unburned, and neither can a name on a page in an inquisitor's pocket."

He said it lightly. Nobody laughed.

Fenwick was assigned to carry stacks to the furnace. He was white around the mouth and trying very hard to be brave about it, which for Fenwick meant narrating. "This one's a confession of adultery from a bishop," he announced, feeding a page into the flames. "Goodbye, Your Grace. This one is somebody's memory of drowning. Why does anyone *buy* that? Who wakes up and says, you know what I want, the exact sensation of drowning—"

"People buy strange things," said Odette. "I sold a man his own funeral once. He wanted to attend."

"That's the saddest thing I've ever heard."

"It was very well attended. He'd planned it himself."

Beatrix let their voices wash over her and kept sorting. Her hands were steady. Her hands were always steady; it was the thing people knew her for, the thing clients whispered about in the waiting room—*ask for Ravensworth, she never flinches, she'll write your worst day into a ledger like it's a grocery bill.* She took a certain pride in it, or she had, once.

She was three-quarters through the second drawer when she found the page.

She knew it before she read it, the way you know your own handwriting in a crowd. Dated nine days ago. Intake slip, client designated Noblewoman, Elderly, Refused Anonymity. And beneath the standard columns—item traded, price paid, condition of goods—one line in her own careful script:

*Item: memory, visual-auditory, approx. twenty years old. Subject: His Majesty King Aldric, prior to coronation. Note: seller retains copy. Buyer advised: item is distressed. Handle gently.*

Handle gently. She had written that because the old woman's hands had shaken, and because the memory itself had felt fragile even through the trade, like a bird cupped in two palms.

And because Beatrix had broken her rule. Never keep what you steal—that was the law she'd lived by since she was nine years old, since her father had knelt down to her eye level and explained, patiently, why a gift like theirs had to be governed by rules stricter than anyone else's, because nobody would forgive them a single sin. She had copied the king's memory anyway. One copy, private, kept against every rule she had, and she still could not fully explain to herself why.

If this page went into the furnace, the trade would be deniable. A ghost transaction. If it survived—if it turned up in a search, if Rafael or any of his white-coated colleagues spread it under a lamp—it was proof. Proof that the Gilded Hollow had trafficked in memories *of the king*. Proof, specifically, that Beatrix Ravensworth had handled the king's mind and written about it in her tidy little hand.

It was the most dangerous piece of paper in the building.

She looked at the furnace. She looked at Corvane across the room, checking a drawer count. She looked at the page.

Then she slid it into the sleeve of her cardigan, beneath her wrist, and kept sorting, and told herself she would decide later what to do with it, and knew perfectly well she had already decided.

* * *

By noon the loose records were gone. By mid-afternoon the vault inventory had been halved, the most incriminating vessels cracked open and emptied into the furnace one by one—a strange, terrible smell, Beatrix thought, like hot sugar and rain, the smell of a hundred borrowed lives going up the flue into the sky where the criers were still walking their circuits.

The guild gathered in the common room at four. Corvane stood at the head of the long table with his arms folded, and Beatrix watched him, really watched him, for the first time in months. Something had changed in his face overnight. Not fear exactly. Fear she would have known how to read; fear she could almost respect. This was something more calculated, a look like a man doing sums behind his eyes and not showing the totals.

"Here is what happens now," he said. "No one leaves the Hollow except on sanctioned business, in pairs, during daylight, wearing the gray. No one carries anything identifying. No new clients—not for a week, not for a month, however loud they knock. We are closed. We are a tannery cellar full of harmless leather-workers, and we have always been a tannery cellar full of harmless leather-workers, and if anyone asks, we have never heard of the Gilded Hollow and find the rumors distasteful." He paused. "Registration is a trap dressed as paperwork. Do not register. Do not be registered. If the inquisitors come to Vetch Street, there is nothing here but hides and brine, and you will all be able to say that with a straight face because I am telling you now to practice."

"What about the ones who've already been seen using the gift?" asked Marta, who did the luck-bottling. "There's a woman on Candlemaker's Row who saw me charm her kettle. She's friendly, but friendly people crack."

"Then Candlemaker's Row is about to lose a kettle-charmer. People scatter tonight. Odette has safehouse keys; see her before dark." Corvane's gaze moved around the table, unhurried. "We survive this the way we've survived everything. Quietly. Underneath. The crown forgets about cellars. Crowns always do—they're looking up, at thrones and borders and each other. In six months this edict will be a story we tell over drinks."

He believed it. That was the frightening part, Beatrix thought. He actually believed it, or had decided to believe it loudly enough that believing became policy.

Fenwick raised his hand, which he did even indoors, which Beatrix had told him a hundred times made him look like a choirboy infiltrating a smuggling ring.

"Yes, Fenwick."

"Why fourteen days?" The boy's voice was smaller than usual. "If it's real, if they're really going to—why give us warning at all? You don't warn mice before you set the trap."

Nobody spoke. Beatrix found she had stopped breathing.

"Because they want us to run," Corvane said at last. "A hunted man leads you to his den. Fourteen days of panic flushes every burrow in the city. It's not mercy, boy. It's bookkeeping." He unfolded his arms. "Which means the clever move is to be boring. Stay. Work. Be invisible. Panic is what they're harvesting."

Beatrix looked down at her wrists, at the edge of the page pressed against her pulse, and thought: *he's right, and it doesn't matter, because I'm keeping it anyway.*

* * *

That night the Hollow burned its ledgers' shadows—all the copies, the duplicates, the working notes—and Beatrix stood in the archive alone after the others had gone, holding the intake slip over the waste-basket where the burning was supposed to happen.

The paper trembled. Her hands never trembled.

Nine days ago an old woman had sat across from her in the trading room, dying by inches of something the physicians had given seven names for, none of them useful, and had said: *I want to sell him back to them. Someone should remember him as he was, before the crown got its teeth in. Someone other than me—I won't last, and it shouldn't die with me.*

*Someone should remember him as he was.*

Beatrix thought about the dream. She had dreamed the king's dream the night after the trade—she hadn't told anyone, hadn't written it in any log, because how would the entry even read?—the young Aldric standing before a mirror while servants lifted the Emberbound toward his head, and in the dream he had flinched. Just slightly. Just enough. The flinch of a man who knows the thing being put on him is heavier than gold.

She thought about the crier's voice coming down the copper pipe. *The king loves his people.*

Did he? Could he love anything anymore, with that thing drinking him year by year? Or was the hunt—fourteen days, registration, treatment, *treatment*—was it pouring out of a mind that no longer entirely belonged to the man whose name signed it?

She didn't know. That was the truth of it. She had one page and one stolen memory and a question, and Corvane wanted ash, and ash answered nothing.

"You're going to keep it," said a voice from the doorway.

Odette stood there with a candle, watching her with the mild, terrible attention of a woman who had catalogued ten thousand human weaknesses and liked most of them.

"I haven't decided," Beatrix said.

"Mm." Odette came into the room and looked into the wastebasket, at the curled dead edges of yesterday's confessions. "Eleven years, and I've watched you file away things that would make a priest weep, neat as buttons. So either you're about to become the first sentimental fool in Hollow history, or that page is worth something." She held out her free hand. "Show me."

Beatrix showed her. It cost her something to do it, and Odette noticed that too, of course.

The old woman read the slip twice. "The king," she said slowly. "Twenty years ago. From Lady Casselaine, unless I miss my guess—she's been dying politely for a year." She looked up. "Why would she have a memory of the king?"

"She didn't say."

"But you kept a copy."

Beatrix said nothing.

Odette smiled, which on her face was less comfort than weather warning. "You know what Corvane will do if he finds that on you."

"He'll burn it."

"He'll burn *it*, girl. What he does with you depends on the day." Odette tapped the page with one fingernail. "Whatever you think you're doing—you're not a spy, you're not a hero, you're a clerk with good posture—do it carefully. And Beatrix." At the door she paused, candlelight making her face into a skull with kind eyes. "If the answer to your question is what I think it is, don't come to me. Come to Maren. Old debts open old doors, and mine are all nailed shut."

She left. Beatrix stood a long moment in the smell of smoke and hot sugar.

Then she folded the page along its original creases, tucked it into the false spine of her least interesting ledger—the one cataloguing bottled sneezes, a genuinely baffling season of trades—and blew out the lamps.

* * *

In the common room, past midnight, Fenwick couldn't sleep and didn't pretend to try.

He sat on the stairs with his knees up, listening to the building settle, and thought about the woman on Candlemaker's Row and her kettle, and about the word *treatment*, which had been following him around all day like a dog he'd kicked. Treatment. As if what they were was a fever. As if what they could do was a disease and not—well. He was seventeen. He could pick any lock in the capital, and when he put his palm flat on a mechanism and listened, really listened, the lock told him things. Where the wards slept. Which teeth lied. It wasn't power, exactly. It was more like being introduced.

Were they going to treat that out of him? Would he still know how locks worked afterward, or just how they looked?

"You'll wear a groove in those steps."

Marta came down with two cups of chicory and handed him one. She sat beside him without asking, the way family did.

"Do you think Corvane's plan will work?" Fenwick asked. "Being boring?"

"I think Corvane's plans always work for Corvane." She drank. "Ask me again in fourteen days."

Above them, faintly, the listening-tube ticked as the city cooled. Down in the archive Beatrix's lamp still glowed, though Fenwick wouldn't learn until much later what she'd saved from the fire, or what it would cost, or that the page in her cardigan sleeve was already, in its small paper way, the first shot of a war nobody had declared yet.

* * *

Lord Chancellor Veyra received the first day's reports at her desk in the Spire, in a room with no candles because she disliked depending on light she did not control.

Fourteen registrants, voluntarily, in the capital alone. Forty-one tips. Three arrests. The numbers were satisfactory. The numbers were always satisfactory; fear was the most reliable instrument ever devised, requiring no maintenance and producing consistent yields.

She dipped her pen. On a fresh sheet, in a hand indistinguishable from a dozen clerks', she began drafting the schedule of treatments for the second week—because the proclamation had promised registration first and treatment after, and the crown had insisted on the interval, and Veyra understood better than anyone alive why.

The crown liked to watch them run.

She wrote the names of the arrested at the top of the list, and paused over the third one, and smiled at nothing, because the third arrest had been made on a tip that mentioned, in passing, a tannery on Vetch Street.

Everything, sooner or later, came down to bookkeeping.

---

Far below the Spire, in a tannery cellar, Beatrix Ravensworth lay awake in the dark with a dangerous piece of paper sleeping against her wrist, and made herself a promise she would break within the month: *one question answered, then I stop. One look inside his head, then I burn it and go back to being invisible.*

Somewhere across the sleeping city, a man in inquisitor's gray was also awake, also holding a page of her handwriting to the lamplight, also telling himself a comforting lie.

The bells would ring again at dawn. Neither of them heard them yet.
