## 5. Ashes in the Register

The summons came at dawn, as the worst things always did, delivered by a boy who knocked as though he were afraid the door might knock back.

Rafael Vessant had been awake for two hours already. He was always awake before dawn — an inheritance from his father, who had been awake before dawn for thirty years of soldiering and had passed the habit to his son along with his shoulders and his stubbornness. So when the boy arrived breathless with the Chancellor's seal on his letter, Rafael was dressed, shaved, and halfway through a pot of coffee that his sister Lira would have called "a punishment" and which Rafael privately considered the only honest thing in the city.

He read the letter twice.

*By order of His Majesty King Aldric the Just, all persons found to possess unregistered gift-craft shall be taken into custody for assessment and treatment. You are appointed First Inquisitor of the Hunt. Present yourself to Lord Chancellor Veyra within the hour.*

"Assessment and treatment," Rafael said aloud, to no one. The kitchen didn't answer. The coffee steamed. Somewhere outside, the criers were already beginning, their voices cracking on words they clearly hadn't rehearsed: *"By royal decree— by royal decree— by—"*

Lira shuffled in, hair like a crow's nest after a storm, and poured herself coffee without asking, which was her right as the only person alive permitted to do so.

"You've got your face on," she said.

"What face?"

"The one where you look like a man reading his own funeral arrangements and trying to decide if he should bring flowers." She squinted at the letter upside down. She could not read it upside down; she'd never learned properly, and Rafael had offered a dozen times to teach her, and she had refused a dozen times on the grounds that knowing how to read was how you ended up responsible for other people's paperwork. But she could read *him*, and she read him fluently. "It's bad, then."

"It's a hunt."

"For the gifted ones." She said it the way she said most terrible things — flatly, over the rim of her cup, as though discussing the price of mutton. Their mother had been gifted. Small craft, nothing worth hunting: she could make cut flowers last a day longer than they ought, could find lost buttons by holding them to her ear. She had died ordinary and beloved, and Rafael had spent twenty years since quietly furious that anyone should ever be made to hide something as harmless as that. "You'll say no."

"I'll say yes."

Lira set down her cup. "Rafael."

"If I refuse, they give it to Hask." Hask, whose enthusiasm for the hunt had been visible from across the river, whose idea of assessment involved a hot iron and whose idea of treatment began where assessment left off. "If I take it, I decide who gets assessed. I decide what 'treatment' means. I can be slow. Slow is a mercy, Lira. Slow is a whole country of mercy."

She looked at him a long moment. Then she took the letter out of his hand, folded it into quarters, and put it in the bread box, which was where she kept everything she disapproved of — tax notices, a proposal of marriage from the butcher's widower, one truly alarming pamphlet about the healing properties of vinegar.

"You can collect it when you're ready to admit it's a mistake," she said, and went back to bed.

Rafael retrieved the letter, because duty was duty even when your sister filed it under baked goods, and went to present himself to the Chancellor.

* * *

Lord Chancellor Veyra received him in a room with no windows, which Rafael had always assumed was for security and now, standing in it, understood was for effect. Candles everywhere. Maps on every wall — not maps of territory but of the city itself, block by block, street by street, annotated in a small precise hand. Certain districts were marked in red ink. Rafael recognized them without wanting to: Tallowbridge, the Foundry Quarter, the crooked lanes below the Gilded Hollow, where the poor lived pressed together like pages in a wet book.

"Inquisitor Vessant." Veyra did not rise. She never rose. It was one of her signature courtesies, offering everyone the same mild disrespect regardless of rank, so that being snubbed by her felt almost like equality. "Congratulations are in order."

"Am I meant to thank Your Excellency?"

"You're meant to understand the honor. Few men are trusted with this." She gestured at the maps. "The king has charged us with a great cleansing."

"A great cleansing." Rafael let the phrase sit between them like something dead on a table. "Your Excellency, with respect — these people are cobblers and fishwives. My own mother could make flowers live past their season. Are we to drag flower-sellers through the streets?"

Veyra smiled. It was a good smile. Rafael had watched it work on harder men than himself.

"Most of them, no," she said. "That's why I want *you*. A careful man. A man who understands that a register is not a pyre." She slid a ledger across the table toward him — thick, bound in gray leather, its spine already cracked from use. "Names will come to you. Some will be lies told by neighbors settling grudges. Your task is to sort the true from the false, and to treat only what must be treated."

"And what does treatment mean? Precisely. I'd like the word defined, since we're building laws on it."

Something flickered behind Veyra's eyes — not anger. Interest, almost. As though he were a lock she was considering picking.

"Removal of the gift," she said. "Where the gift is dangerous. Where it is bound up with sedition." She laced her fingers. "The crown has determined that unregistered gift-craft constitutes a threat to the realm's stability. The king was very clear."

"The king," Rafael said carefully, "was clear."

"Entirely clear." She tilted her head. "You have doubts about the edict's provenance, Inquisitor?"

"I have doubts about everything, Your Excellency. It's why you hired me."

That earned him a real laugh, brief and dry as a struck match. "Indeed it is." She stood, finally, and came around the table, and for a moment she stood beside him looking at the maps as though they were both admiring a garden. "You wonder where such an edict comes from. A king who has ruled gently for twenty years, and then in one winter — registers, hunts, treatments." Her voice dropped, intimate, conspiratorial, the voice of a woman letting you in on the family secret. "I will tell you what I tell no one else, Inquisitor. The edicts come from above the king."

Rafael turned. "Above the—"

"From the crown itself." She said it plainly, terribly, as though reporting the weather. "A king is a man. A crown is an institution. Institutions outlive men, and institutions have appetites, and lately the appetite has grown. We serve the realm, you and I. The realm wears the crown. Do you see?"

He did not see. He saw a woman explaining treason with the calm of a woman reciting a recipe, and he saw that no one in this windowless room would ever believe him if he reported it, because she was the person one reported things *to*.

"I see my orders," he said.

"Good man." She patted the gray ledger once, twice, like soothing an animal. "Begin with the Hollow district. There are rumors of smugglers there. Gifted smugglers. Memory-workers, if the reports can be believed."

Memory-workers. Something cold walked down Rafael's spine wearing very familiar boots.

"Reports," he said, "can rarely be believed."

"No," Veyra agreed pleasantly. "But they can be followed. Dismissed, Inquisitor. And Rafael—" she used his name deliberately, a hand laid briefly on the shoulder, "—do well at this, and there may be a place for you above inquisitors. Fail, or falter, or develop scruples inconveniently late, and remember that I keep ledgers too."

* * *

The Gilded Hollow district got its name from a canal that ran gold-green with dye waste from the tanneries, which told you everything about the neighborhood's relationship with gilding. Rafael walked it that afternoon with two men he did not trust — Sergeant Pell, who sneezed constantly and blamed the weather in a district where it wasn't raining, and young Corporal Dunmore, who was nineteen and thought the hunt was romantic.

They came to the guildhouse at the end of Candlemaker's Row: a narrow building squeezed between a chandler's and a shop that sold, according to its sign, "Birds, Cages, Advice." Above the door hung a brass plaque polished bright: THE GILDED HOLLOW — APPRAISALS & ACQUISITIONS OF RECORDS. Which was either the dullest business in the city or the least convincing front in it.

"They're licensed," Pell said, sneezing. "Records appraisers. Legitimate trade. Bought old letters off my aunt once, gave fair money."

"Licensed trades get inspected," Rafael said. "That's why we're here. Knock."

The door was opened by a girl of perhaps fifteen with ink to her elbows and the specific expression of someone who had been warned about this exact moment and had decided to meet it with contempt. "We're closing."

"We're the king's inquisitors," Dunmore announced, with the relish of a boy who had practiced the line in a mirror.

"Closing *early*," the girl amended, without moving aside.

"Fetch your master," Rafael said mildly. "Or mistress. Whomever keeps the books."

There was a long pause, during which the girl visibly weighed several insolent replies and discarded them one by one like a gambler folding hands, and then footsteps sounded inside — unhurried, measured — and Corvane emerged.

Rafael knew Corvane by reputation the way one knows a well: deep, dark, and best not fallen into. The guild master of the Hollow was a broad, silver-templed man with the genial menace of a tavern-keeper who has buried three customers and mourned none of them. He greeted the inspection like a host greeting guests who had arrived drunk and were unlikely to remember the evening.

"Inquisitors! An honor. Come in, come in, mind the cat, she bites clerks specifically."

"There's no cat," Pell said.

"Not yet. Give her a moment."

The interior was exactly what a records office pretending to be dull should be: shelves of ledgers, a counter, a lamp, a smell of paper and candlewax and, beneath it, something fainter — beeswax and something herbal, something that made Rafael think inexplicably of a memory he couldn't place, which was itself unsettling, because Rafael placed things for a living.

And behind the counter, cataloguing, stood Beatrix Ravensworth.

She did not look up. That was the first thing. Everyone else in the room had performed some small theater upon the inquisitors' entrance — Corvane's welcome, the girl's defiance, Pell's sneeze — but Beatrix simply continued writing, quill scratching, as though the king's law were a draft she'd shut a window against. Only when Corvane cleared his throat did she lift her head, and her eyes found Rafael's, and held them for exactly one second longer than courtesy required, and in that second Rafael felt twenty years compress into the space between them like a book slammed shut.

"Master Ravensworth keeps our ledgers," Corvane said. "Beatrix, dear, fetch the gentlemen the audit rolls. The *current* audit rolls."

"Of course." Her voice was smooth and bored and perfect. She moved along the shelf, and Rafael watched her hands — quick, precise, the hands of someone who had learned early that fingers could lie better than faces — and hated himself for watching them.

The inspection proceeded. Ledgers were produced. They were immaculate. Every entry dated, initialed, cross-referenced; every transaction boring enough to induce sleep in a bishop. Appraisals of correspondence estates, purchases of private papers from families clearing out deceased relatives' effects, sales of authenticated documents to collectors. Rafael turned page after page and found nothing, and the nothing was so beautifully arranged that it became, itself, suspicious — a garden so tidy you knew the weeds hadn't died, they'd been *transplanted*.

Pell wandered. Dunmore preened. The girl — Tam, someone called her, though Rafael would learn that name later, and learn it in a way that would keep him up nights — sharpened pencils with unnecessary violence.

And Rafael, turning pages, came to one that did not belong.

It was near the back, an older folio, water-stained at the corner. Most of the entries were in Corvane's blunt hand. But a handful — maybe two dozen, scattered through the years like coins dropped in a stairwell — were in another hand entirely. Slanted, economical, with a distinctive habit of crossing the letter *t* with a single decisive stroke that trailed slightly upward, as though the writer were always in a hurry to finish the sentence and begin the next thought.

Rafael knew that handwriting the way you know a voice through a wall.

He had first seen it on a note slipped under his door eleven years ago, when he was a young officer investigating thefts in the archive quarter and the thefts stopped abruptly the same week the notes started, each one helpfully anonymous, each one correct, each one signed with nothing at all. He had seen it since in margins, in pawn tickets, once — memorably — on a get-well card left for Lira during her fever, unsigned, containing a pressed sprig of feverfew that had no business working as well as it did.

Beatrix Ravensworth wrote like a woman fleeing the scene of her own sentence.

He looked at the page. The entries themselves were innocuous — appraisals, valuations, a purchase of "one lot assorted correspondence, condition fair." Nothing seditious. Nothing gifted. Nothing that any inquisitor in the kingdom could call evidence of anything.

But the hunt needed examples. Veyra needed names. A page in a gifted hand, seized from a suspect establishment, would justify warrants, searches, pressure. It would be leverage, exactly the kind of leverage a careful man could use — or a careless one could burn a street with.

Corvane was watching him. Rafael felt the weight of the guild master's attention settle on his neck like a hand.

And Beatrix was watching too, now openly, quill stilled, her face composed into polite professional blankness — but her thumb had gone white where it pressed against the edge of the desk, and Rafael, who had spent years learning to read the small betrayals of liars, read hers perfectly: *take it and I'll know what you are; leave it and I'll know what you are.*

He turned the page.

He kept turning. Past the slanted hand, past the water stain, through forty more pages of magnificent tedium, until he closed the folio and said, "Your records are in order."

"They usually are," Corvane said warmly.

"One thing." Rafael held up a finger. "This older volume — it stays in the building? Doesn't leave premises?"

"Never leaves. House policy."

"Good. Keep it that way. Loose paper causes accidents." He straightened, and let his official voice fall into place like a visor. "The Hollow will be registered in the coming weeks. All residents. Notices will be posted. Cooperation will be noted favorably in the record." He paused, and heard his own next words as if spoken by someone else, someone braver or more foolish: "Cooperation means answering questions honestly. It does not mean volunteering neighbors' names. The register is not a pyre. Make sure your people know the difference."

Corvane's smile widened by a degree. "I'll convey it exactly so, Inquisitor."

At the door, Rafael paused. Beatrix stood at the counter behind him, reshelving volumes, her back half-turned.

"Master Ravensworth," he said. "Your penmanship is very distinctive. In my trade, distinctive handwriting is a liability. You might consider learning to write like everyone else."

For the first time all afternoon, her composure cracked — just a hairline fracture, just enough for something to show through that was neither fear nor guilt. It was, he realized with a sensation like stepping off a stair that wasn't there, *amusement*.

"I'll bear it in mind, Inquisitor," she said, "next time I write anything worth forging."

Outside, the light was going amber over the rooftops, and Pell sneezed, and Dunmore asked whether they'd be raiding the place soon, and Rafael said no, the records were in order, and walked the length of Candlemaker's Row feeling something shift inside his chest with each step — a slow structural groan, like ice on a river beginning to move.

Duty was a load-bearing wall. He had built his life against it. And now, walking away from the Hollow with the memory of a slanted, hurried hand burning in his mind like a brand, Rafael Vessant understood that something had gotten into the wall. Water, maybe. Rot, maybe. Something patient, working outward from a crack he'd ignored for eleven years.

That night he sat at his desk with the gray ledger open before him and the Chancellor's instructions, and he wrote the first entry of the hunt in his own tidy, blameless hand:

*Gilded Hollow, appraisers of records. Inspected this day. No irregularities found.*

He read it back. It was true. Every word. He had found no irregularities, because he had chosen not to look at the one he'd found.

Somewhere across the city, in a district that smelled of tanneries and candlewax, a woman was shelving ledgers and keeping secrets, and one of those secrets was now sitting in Rafael's chest like swallowed coals.

He dipped his pen again. Below his clean entry, small, in the margin, in handwriting he made a point of disguising — slanted, hurried, trailing slightly upward — he wrote four words:

*Ask me about the crown.*

Then he crossed them out. Then he burned the blotting paper. Then he sat in the dark for a long time, listening to the criers in the distance calling the king's name over streets full of frightened people, wondering which of them was more damned: the hunted, or the man holding the leash who had just discovered he didn't want to pull it.

In the morning, there would be lists to draw up. Names to sort. A machine to feed.

But tonight, in the margin of a gray ledger, four crossed-out words waited like a lit fuse — and Rafael, duty rotting sweetly in his mouth, found that he did not have the strength to scrape the page clean.
