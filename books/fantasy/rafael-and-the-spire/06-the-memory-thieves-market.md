## 6. The Memory Thieves' Market

There is a rule among those of us who trade in the impossible: never go to market hungry.

I broke it on the third night after the proclamation, which tells you something about how well I was holding together.

The Gilded Hollow had gone quiet in the way a house goes quiet when someone in it has died. Corvane had ordered the ledgers sealed, the lamps dimmed, and every gifted soul in our employ moved below street level into the old wine cellars, where we slept in rows like spoons in a drawer. I had spent two days pretending this was fine. I had spent two days auditing accounts that no longer mattered — who owed us for a forgotten grudge, who had paid in advance for a summer they'd never lived — because numbers were the only language I trusted not to lie to me.

But the proclamation had changed the arithmetic. Registered by month's end. *Treated* thereafter. Nobody in the Hollow used the word *treated* without flinching, the way you don't say a dead man's name too loudly. And registration required papers proving residence, trade, and — this was the elegant cruelty of it — proof that you were *ordinary*. A certificate of mundanity, stamped by a crown clerk, attesting that you were nothing special at all.

For most people, that was a formality. For us, it was a death warrant written in the passive voice.

So on the third night I put on my second-best coat — the one that said *poor but honest* rather than *suspiciously well-dressed bookkeeper* — and I went down.

* * *

The Memory Thieves' Market does not have an address. It has a mood.

You find it by walking down Cordwainer Street until the lamplight gives up, then through a doorway that is only a doorway if you already know what you're looking for — a bricked-up arch behind a chandler's shop, breathing cold air like a sleeping animal. Then stairs. Always stairs. The city sits on top of itself like sediment, layer upon layer of older cities, and somewhere around the fourth stratum down, past a cistern the size of a cathedral and a tunnel where somebody once tried to raise pigs (don't ask; the smell outlived the pigs), you come to the Vault.

The Vault had been a grain exchange two hundred years ago, back when this part of the undercity belonged to merchants instead of mushrooms. Its ceiling still arched overhead like the ribs of some enormous patient whale, and its floor was crowded now with stalls, blankets, lanterns hung from rusted chains — dozens of them, swaying slightly in the air current, so that the whole market seemed to breathe light.

And the sound. That was the thing nobody warned you about. The Vault hummed. Not with voices, though there were plenty of those, but with the memories themselves — bottled, jarred, stoppered in glass and wax, each one faintly alive. Stand in the middle of it and you could feel them the way you feel heat off a bakery wall: first kisses going warm and pink, childhood summers humming gold, griefs sitting heavy and blue at the bottom of their jars. A thousand small lives, shelved and priced.

I had been coming here since I was nineteen, first as Corvane's errand girl, later as his auditor. I knew the vendors by their wares. Old Sef, who sold nothing but other people's regrets and swore they kept better than joy. The twins at the far stall who dealt in skills — languages, swordwork, the ability to hold your breath for four minutes — decanted straight from the heads of sailors and duelists who'd needed coin more than talent. And at the center of everything, on a raised dais like a pulpit, the Assessment Table, where a woman named Quill sat with a jeweler's loupe screwed into her eye and decided what your memory was worth.

Quill had hands like a concert pianist and the moral flexibility of wet rope. She was also, tonight, the busiest person in the undercity.

That was the difference. That was what stopped me three steps inside the arch with my heart doing something unprofessional in my chest.

The market was full of families.

Not thieves. Not traders. Families — mothers with children asleep against their shoulders, fathers holding jars up to the lanternlight with the desperate care of men appraising the last of the family silver. Because that was exactly what they were doing. The forged papers everyone needed cost money, and money was scarce, and there was one commodity every gifted person carried that the ordinary world would always pay for.

Themselves.

A boy of maybe ten stood at Sef's stall while his mother gripped his shoulder. On the counter between them sat a single small jar, glowing a soft, uncomplicated green, like June seen through bottle glass.

"That's a good one," Sef was saying, gently, the way you talk to someone at a graveside. "First day of school? I can see the chalk dust. Chalk dust keeps beautifully."

"He won't miss it," the mother said. Her voice had the flatness of someone who had rehearsed being calm until the rehearsal wore through. "He's got four more years of school. He won't miss one day."

"Mama," the boy said, "will I remember being brave?"

Sef looked away. The mother didn't.

"You'll be brave anyway, love. It isn't the remembering that makes it."

I turned and walked very fast toward the Assessment Table, because my eyes had begun doing something I refused to permit in public.

* * *

"Beatrix Ravensworth." Quill didn't look up. She was holding a jar of something amber to her loupe, tilting it, watching the light move inside like honey with weather in it. "Corvane's ledger-keeper. Come to tell me my interest rates are immoral again?"

"Your interest rates are a form of violence," I said. "Tonight I'm a customer."

That got her attention. She set down the jar and looked at me over the loupe, one eye huge and brown and unsettlingly kind, the other normal-sized and entirely merciless. "A customer. During *this*." She gestured at the market with its shivering lanterns, its queue of hollow-eyed parents. "Everyone's a seller tonight, Beatrix. Nobody's buying except the brokers and the crown's little procurement friends." She spat, delicately, into a spittoon that had seen worse. "What do you need?"

"Papers. Full set. Residence, trade, mundanity certificate. Good enough to survive a clerk with a suspicious squint."

"Veyra's clerks all squint. It's how they're trained." Quill pulled a ledger toward herself — everyone in my line of work kept ledgers; we were a profession of compulsive list-makers — and licked her thumb. "Full set runs forty crowns. From anyone else. From me, thirty-five, because I like you and because you once talked Corvane out of repossessing my stall, which was the single decent act I've witnessed in the Hollow in eleven years."

"I don't have thirty-five crowns."

"No," Quill said. "I know. You've got a coat that says poor-but-honest, a guild salary that Corvane pays in promises, and—" she tapped the counter twice, "—whatever you brought to sell. So. What did you bring?"

And here is the thing about the rule. *Never keep what you steal.* I had built my whole careful life on it. Steal clean, store clean, sell clean, keep nothing. What you keep, keeps *you*. Every memory I had ever taken from a client had passed through my hands like water through a sieve, leaving me precise and empty and safe.

Except one.

My father died when I was nine, and he died wrong — suddenly, stupidly, in a cart accident on a road he shouldn't have been on, carrying goods he shouldn't have been carrying for a man whose name I never learned. What the accident left me was a hole shaped like him. But before the Hollow found me, before Corvane taught me that memory was inventory and feeling was overhead, there had been one evening. One whole, entire evening. He'd taken me up onto the warehouse roof where he worked, because I'd asked him what the harbor looked like when the lamps came on, and instead of answering he'd carried me up four ladders to show me. We'd counted ships. He'd named every one of them wrong on purpose — *that's the Dread Ferret, that's the Queen of Soup* — until I laughed hard enough to scare the gulls. And when it got dark he'd pointed at the lighthouse and told me, with complete seriousness, that it was the city's own star and we were lucky enough to live in its sky.

I hadn't stolen that memory. You can't steal what's given. But I had copied it once, years ago, into a jar, the way you press a flower into a book — just to know I could keep something safe. Just once. Just that.

It was in my coat pocket, and it had been burning a hole through the wool for three days, ever since I'd read the proclamation and understood what the arithmetic demanded.

"Show me," Quill said quietly.

I set the jar on the counter. The green-gold glow of it lit her face, and for a moment the great merciless eye went soft.

"Oh," she said. "Oh, Beatrix."

"It's mine. Given, not taken. Don't—"

"I know what given looks like." She picked it up with both hands, the way you hold a bird. "This will fetch sixty. Easy. Roof-harbors are rare, and father-memories—" she stopped herself. "It'll fetch sixty."

"I need papers and ten crowns change."

"Take twenty."

"Ten."

"*Twenty*, you stubborn—"

"Ten," I said, "and you tell no one what you bought, and when this is over — if there is an over — you give me first refusal to buy it back."

Quill looked at me for a long moment. Down in the Vault, the lanterns swayed, and somewhere a child asked her mother whether forgetting hurt, and the mother lied, because that was the currency we were all trading in now.

"Done," Quill said. "But I'm writing it in the ledger. Witnessed terms. I may be wet rope, Ravensworth, but I honor my knots."

She uncorked a fresh page and wrote, and I watched my father's evening become a line item: *One (1) memory, harbor-rooftop, provenance: gift. Purchased from B.R. Terms: buy-back priority.* My handwriting had audited ten thousand such entries. It was different, seeing your own heart in a fair hand, priced and dated.

"There," Quill said, softer than I'd ever heard her. "Now it's not gone. Now it's *owed* to you. There's a difference, and the difference is hope, and hope is the only thing in this market I don't charge interest on."

* * *

The forger worked two stalls down, behind a curtain of hanging keys that clinked like nervous rain. His name was Doss, and he was not gifted himself, which made him either the bravest or the most sensible man in the undercity, depending on whom you asked. He took Quill's paper and my ten crowns and studied me across his lamp.

"Ravensworth. The auditor." He began pulling type from little wooden drawers, and the room filled with the soft iron smell of ink. "Funny thing. I've forged papers for half the Hollow by now. Corvane sent his people down in batches. Cooks, couriers, the lock-boy, even old Maren's name came through — well, a name *like* Maren's." He set the type with quick fingers. "Yours didn't."

"I can do my own paperwork."

"Can you?" He glanced up. "Because here's my trouble, see. I make documents that say a person is ordinary. And you —" he nodded at my pocket, where the empty space where the jar had been seemed to ache — "you just paid for ordinary with the least ordinary thing I've seen all week. So which is true? The paper or the price?"

"The paper," I said. "The paper better be true, or we both hang."

He grinned, and it was a good grin, tired at the edges. "Fair. Hold still, I need your hand for the seal impression."

While I pressed my palm into the warm wax, he talked, because Doss always talked, and what he said was this: that business had tripled since the proclamation. That he'd forged papers for a fishwife who sold her wedding day to pay for them, and for a choir-master who sold the music itself — *the music, Beatrix, he can't hear a hymn now without it sounding like arithmetic* — and that every one of them said the same thing. *They won't miss it. They won't miss it. They won't miss it.*

"And do they miss it?" I asked.

Doss pressed the seal down. "Come back in a year and ask them," he said. "If any of them remember why they came."

He slid the papers across: residence certificate, trade license, and the mundanity certificate with its fat crown stamp, declaring Beatrix Ravensworth of Cordwainer Ward to be a person of no particular gifts whatsoever, useful only for sums. I read it twice. It was beautiful work. It was also, I thought, the saddest document I had ever held: my entire legal existence now rested on an official declaration that I was nothing.

"Word of advice," Doss said as I tucked them away. "The clerk who processes these — Ferren, gray whiskers, drinks on duty — he's honest, which is rare, but he's thorough, which is worse. If he asks you anything odd, answer boring. Boring is armor down here."

"Boring is armor everywhere," I said. "It's how I've survived the Hollow."

* * *

I was nearly at the arch, nearly up the stairs, nearly back to being a person who had merely sold a piece of her childhood rather than a person standing in the middle of having done it, when I heard the argument.

It came from the shadowed end of the Vault, near the cistern, where the stalls thinned out and the lantern-light gave up altogether. Two voices, low and urgent. One I didn't know. The other —

The other was Corvane's.

I should have walked on. I want that recorded somewhere, in whatever ledger heaven keeps for people like me: *she should have walked on.* But Corvane had no reason to be in the Vault tonight — he'd forbidden the rest of us from coming alone — and Corvane's voice, when it dropped that low, was the voice he used for transactions he'd deny later. I had audited this man for six years. I knew his registers the way musicians know keys.

So I stopped in the dark behind a pillar of black stone, and I listened.

"—the names, or the arrangement ends," Corvane was saying. "I'm not unreasonable. Give me the Kell family and the tinker, and the rest of your vendors keep their stalls and their heads."

"And if I don't?"

"Then next week the inquisitors find this place anyway, because I will have stopped being able to protect it, and everyone down here learns what 'treated' means. I'm offering you a scalpel, Sef. Refuse me and you get the axe."

Old Sef, the regret-broker, the gentlest haggler in the undercity, the man who'd spoken to that boy about chalk dust like a priest administering a sacrament — Sef was silent for a long moment. When he spoke, his voice had aged ten years.

"You've done this before," he said. "Haven't you. Names for safety. How many?"

"That," Corvane said pleasantly, "is not a question you can afford to have answered."

I stood in the dark with my forged ordinariness against my ribs and my father's evening weighing nothing in the empty pocket where it used to be, and I understood, with the terrible clarity that comes only to people who read numbers for a living, that the hunt had not started at the palace gates.

It had started at home. It had possibly *only* ever started at home.

Corvane's footsteps came toward the arch — toward me. I pressed myself flat against the pillar, heart hammering out a rhythm no mundane bookkeeper's heart should manage, and he passed within arm's reach, smelling of candle-wax and expensive lies, and climbed the stairs out of the Vault without ever knowing I was there.

I stayed in the dark a long while after.

Then I went up, and out, and home through streets that felt newly made of eyes — and the first thing I did, before lighting a lamp, before checking the seals on the cellar door, was pull Quill's ledger-entry receipt from my coat and read it again. *Buy-back priority. Witnessed terms.*

Hope, the only thing she didn't charge interest on.

I needed it. Because tomorrow morning, Corvane would be waiting in the Hollow with his ledgers balanced and his smile oiled, and he was going to offer me a bargain — I could feel it coming the way you feel weather in an old scar — and I would have to decide, with my father's laughter freshly sold off my own roof, exactly what kind of thief I intended to be.
