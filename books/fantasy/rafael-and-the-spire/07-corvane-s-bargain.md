## 7. Corvane's Bargain

Corvane Ash kept his office above the counting room because he liked to hear money being handled while he thought. Not spent — the Hollow rarely spent anything — but *handled*: the soft slide of coin across felt, the click of a scale's arm finding its balance, the particular sigh a client made when they realized what their memory was actually worth. It was the sound of an honest business, more or less, and after thirty years in the trade Corvane had learned that honesty was mostly a matter of acoustics.

He was listening to it now, and it was not comforting him.

Below, the counting room had gone quiet in the wrong way. Since the criers had come through three days ago — *all persons bearing the gift shall present themselves for registration; unregistered bearers shall be treated* — the sounds of the Hollow had changed. The scales still balanced. The ledgers still opened. But clients came at odd hours now, hooded, and left without haggling, and twice Corvane had heard someone weep quietly over a memory of no great market value at all: a kitchen, a mother humming, rain on a window. Memories people used to sell to pay debts were now being sold to buy the appearance of having nothing worth taking.

Corvane poured himself a measure of plum brandy and did not drink it. He was a large man built like a door that had been left slightly open for years and had settled that way, with a beard he trimmed himself badly and a reputation for fairness that he had cultivated the way other men cultivated orchards — patiently, with an eye on the harvest.

The reputation was not false, exactly. That was the thing nobody understood about him, and the thing he could never explain without sounding like a monster: Corvane Ash had never once lied to anyone who mattered.

He simply decided, privately, quietly, who mattered.

There was a knock. Two knocks, then one — Tess, his floor manager, which meant trouble, because Tess knocked once for business and twice for gossip and only combined them when the gossip *was* the business.

"Enter."

Tess came in smelling of lamp oil and nerves. She was a narrow woman with ink-stained fingers and the gift of a minor sort — she could hold a number in her head for years without losing it, which in the Hollow counted as magic the way a good knife counts as a sword. "Master. The clerk from the Chancery is back."

"He was here Tuesday."

"He's here again. He says he'll wait." Tess wrung her hands. "He brought a second chair this time."

Corvane looked at her. "A second chair."

"For himself," Tess said. "He means to stay until you see him."

"Then show him up, and bring the good brandy, and stand outside my door looking ominous. You're very good at ominous. You've had practice all week."

Tess almost smiled, which was the point. A frightened guild was a dead guild; fear needed somewhere to go, and Corvane had spent three days making sure it went into work rather than into flight. He'd doubled the night shifts on the archive. He'd ordered the loose records burned — that order had cost him something, though not what Beatrix Ravensworth probably imagined it cost him. And he'd begun, each evening, doing a thing he had done every year for eleven years, which was to write a letter.

The clerk from the Chancery arrived looking like a man who had been dressed by an enemy: robes a half-size too small, collar too high, a face that kept trying to be stern and settling for sweaty. Corvane gestured him into the chair across the desk and noted, with professional interest, that the man sat down like someone who expected the chair to be a trap. Veyra trained her people well in some respects.

"Master Corvane." The clerk produced a document wallet and did not open it. "Lord Chancellor Veyra extends her regards."

"Her regards are always welcome. Her clerks are welcome less often. Brandy?"

"No, thank you." A pause. "Yes. Please."

Corvane poured. Small mercies; a man who drank was a man who talked. "You're the third one she's sent this month. The first two asked about smuggled luck-charms. This one—" he nodded at the wallet, "—will ask about people. So let's skip to it, and I'll save us both the theater. What does the Chancellor want?"

The clerk took a swallow of brandy that was meant to be casual and wasn't. "She wants to know if the Gilded Hollow intends to comply with the proclamation."

"The proclamation requires gifted persons to register for treatment." Corvane laced his fingers over his belly. "I am fifty-eight years old and I have never been treated for anything in my life except gout, which I earned honestly. My staff are tradesmen. We handle goods. Some of those goods are memories. Handling is not bearing, clerk. A fishmonger is not a fish."

"That distinction may have carried weight last month," the clerk said, and there it was — the flicker of a decent man trapped in a bad job, the same flicker Corvane had seen in every Chancery emissary for a decade. "It doesn't carry now. There are lists, Master Corvane. Names. And the names come from somewhere."

"Yes," said Corvane pleasantly. "They do."

The clerk blinked. He had prepared, clearly, for denial, outrage, stonewalling — the whole repertoire of a guild master protecting his own. He had not prepared for agreement, and Corvane watched him flounder in it like a man handed the wrong end of a rope.

"You... admit—"

"I admit nothing. I observe that lists come from somewhere, and I observe that you've drunk your brandy, and I observe that Lord Veyra sent you with a second chair, which tells me she expects this conversation to be long, and I'm telling you it needn't be." He leaned forward. "Go back to her. Tell her the Hollow's position is unchanged from last year. Tell her the arrangement stands. She'll know what it means."

"The arrangement—" The clerk set down the glass. "I don't know any arrangement."

"No," said Corvane kindly. "You wouldn't. That's rather the point of arrangements. Good afternoon."

* * *

Afterward he sat alone for a long time with the brandy he hadn't drunk earlier and finally drank now.

Eleven years. Eleven years since the plague winter, when the old king's men had come sniffing down Copperlane and the Hollow had faced a choice: burn and scatter, or find a patron. Corvane had found one. It had taken him four meetings and the surrender of exactly one thing — a list of names, updated quarterly, of every gifted person who traded through the Hollow. Not his staff. Never his staff; that line he had held through every negotiation, and Veyra, to her credit, had accepted it, because a guild master who sold his own people was a guild master who would eventually sell *her*, and Veyra understood arithmetic better than morality.

Clients, though. Clients were fair game. That was the ledger-entry way of saying it, and Corvane had written it that way once, in his private book, in his own shorthand, and had slept fine ever after. *Clients are fair game.* They came to the Hollow to trade away pieces of themselves; they knew, when they walked in, that memory was currency and currency moved. If a name moved along with it — well. Every trade has a fee.

He had told himself versions of this for eleven years, and the versions had held up well under daily use, the way a good boot holds up, right up until tonight, when he caught himself thinking about the boy.

Pell. Sixteen, quick-fingered, taught half the younger guild kids to pick locks using a bread loaf and a borrowed key. Gifted in a small, sweet way — he could take the sting out of a memory, smooth its edges, make a bad night merely a dull one. Clients loved him. Children loved him. He had registered, of course, the moment the criers went through, because Pell was obedient the way clever boys sometimes are, believing rules will protect them if they follow them precisely enough.

His name was already on Corvane's list. Had been for two years, entered the day he'd first traded through the Hollow, before anyone dreamed of proclamations. Quarterly updates went to the Chancery. The spring update had gone out nine days ago.

Corvane looked at his glass and found it empty and did not remember drinking it.

*Fair game,* he told himself, and the boot finally split.

* * *

He found Beatrix in the archive at the hour when the candles were changed, which was the only hour she tolerated interruption, because it was the hour when the ledgers were closed and the numbers stopped speaking to her.

She was standing on a ladder with a rag, wiping dust off a shelf of bottled recollections — the physical stock, the actual goods, little stoppered vials that glowed faintly different colors depending on what they held. Grief was blue-white. First loves ran warm amber. She worked with her sleeves rolled and her hair pinned carelessly, and she did not look down when he came in, which was her way of telling him she'd heard his footsteps at the door and had opinions about the visit already.

"You burned the loose records," she said to the shelf.

"I did."

"There was a page I needed. The Aldric transaction. Twenty years back, the noblewoman's deposit — the provenance chain runs through it."

"Provenance chains run through ash just as well," Corvane said. "Come down, Beatrix. I want to talk to you where I can see your face. You cheat at conversations from up there."

She came down the ladder slowly, which was her way of conceding nothing. Up close she had the look she always had after a week of bad news: composed, precise, and running on something colder than sleep. She set the rag down and folded her arms. "Talk, then."

He had rehearsed this. He was a man who rehearsed everything — speeches, negotiations, the annual letter. But standing in front of her now, in the candle-smell and the dust, he found the rehearsal had rotted somewhere between his office and the stairs, and what came out instead was quieter than planned.

"How much do you think I've sold?" he asked.

Beatrix went very still. It was a gift-keeper's stillness, total and instant, the stillness of someone whose livelihood depended on never being surprised visibly. "Sold. As in memories?"

"As in names."

The candle nearest them guttered and steadied. Somewhere below, faintly, the scales clicked. Corvane watched her arrive at it — he could see the arrival happen, watch the arithmetic move behind her eyes the way it moved when she audited: one fact laid against another, the proclamation, the burned records, the Chancery clerks who always seemed to know which doors to knock on first.

"Eleven years," she said at last. Her voice didn't rise. That was the frightening thing about Beatrix; everything important happened in the flatness. "The raids on Tallow Row. The arrest of the Vess family. The way the Chancery always knew about the luck-smugglers before we did. I audited this guild for six years, Corvane. I reconciled every column. And the whole time there was a second ledger."

"There's always a second ledger," Corvane said. "In every house in this city. In the palace most of all. The only question is who keeps it and what it buys." He pulled out the chair at the reading table and sat, because he wanted his hands occupied and because looming over Beatrix Ravensworth was a mistake no one made twice. "Sit down."

"I'll stand."

"Suit yourself." He turned his glass a quarter-turn on the table, a fidget he despised in others. "Here is the truth, since you'll dig it up anyway — you're the best auditor I've ever had and the worst liar, which is a strange pairing, and I've never understood how you live with it. Yes. For eleven years I have sent Lord Chancellor Veyra a list. Names of gifted clients who trade through the Hollow. Quarterly. In exchange, the Hollow is invisible. No inspections that stick. No seizures. No questions about what moves through our cellar. This guild has survived every purge, every panic, every change of crown, because I made sure the people who make lists owed us quiet."

"And the people on your lists?"

"Some were questioned. Some fled. Some—" He stopped. Started again, because he had promised himself he would not soften it; softened truths were just lies wearing better clothes. "Some were treated. I don't know how many. Veyra doesn't send receipts."

Beatrix looked at him for a long moment, and Corvane realized, with a sensation like a stair missing in the dark, that she wasn't shocked. She was *checking*. Running it against things she already suspected, fitting it into gaps she'd noticed months ago and filed away. Of course she was. He had trained her himself.

"Pell," she said. "He's on the list."

"He's been on the list two years."

"He registered. He did what the proclamation said. He stood in line like a good boy and gave his name to the criers, and he was safe either way, wasn't he? Because you'd already given it." Something moved behind her flatness then, and he wished it hadn't, because it was worse than anger. It was grief, arriving early, the kind you feel for someone who is still standing in front of you. "Does he know?"

"No."

"Will you tell him?"

"What would it protect him from?" Corvane spread his hands. "Beatrix. Look at the board. The king has declared a hunt. Veyra has lists whether I feed them or not — she has the census, the parish rolls, the tithe records. The Hollow cannot stop the hunt. All I can decide is whether *we* are standing in front of it or behind it. Eleven years ago I decided. I decide it again every quarter. That's the job. Somebody keeps the roof on."

"Somebody keeps the roof on," she repeated softly. "And underneath the roof, you sell the people who trusted it."

"They're *clients*—"

"They're people who came to us with the most fragile thing they owned and paid us to be careful with it." Now her voice did rise, just barely, one degree, which from Beatrix was a shout. "I sit downstairs every day and I watch a woman trade her mother's face so she can eat, and I write it in the ledger, and the ledger says the trade is complete and fair and final. And you're telling me there's a footnote. That the fee was never just coin."

"The fee was survival," Corvane said. "Mine, yours, everyone under this roof. You think I like it? You think I sleep—" He stopped, because the honest answer was *yes, I sleep fine, I have always slept fine,* and he found, sitting here, that he no longer knew if it was true, and the not-knowing was new and unwelcome, like finding water where the cellar floor should be. He pressed on. "I didn't come up here to confess. Confession is for people with a priest and a plan. I came up here to make you an offer."

And there it was — the thing he had actually climbed the stairs for, the real business, and he watched her recognize it too, watched her face close like a ledger snapping shut.

"Veyra knows there's a leak in my operation," Corvane said. "Someone's been asking palace questions. Old-debt questions. Crown questions. She doesn't know who. I don't know who. But I keep this guild alive by knowing things, Beatrix, and I will know, because I always know, and when I know, I'll have a choice." He met her eyes. "Bring me what you find. Whatever you're digging at — the crown, the king, Maren's old routes, all of it, I'm not a fool, I've watched you for six years — bring it to me first. Let me shape it. Let me decide what reaches the Chancery and in what form. Do that, and you're behind the roof with the rest of us. Protected. Fed. Untouched."

"And if I don't?"

"Then you're a name on a list I don't control the timing of." He said it gently, which somehow made it worse, and he knew it made it worse, and he said it gently anyway, because cruelty would at least have been clean. "I'm offering you the same deal I gave myself eleven years ago. Inform, or be informed upon. There's no third door. I've looked. I've looked for thirty years, and there's no third door, there's just the roof and the rain and choosing which side of the wall you're standing on when it comes down."

The silence stretched. Below them the scales clicked; somewhere a client laughed at something Tess said, a bright startled laugh, the laugh of someone who had briefly forgotten to be afraid. Beatrix picked up her rag from the table, folded it once, twice, precise as everything she did.

"You're wrong about the third door," she said.

"Beatrix—"

"You're wrong, and worse, you've *stopped looking*, and that's the difference between you and me. You decided the world is a roof and a wall, and you've been collecting rent on that decision for eleven years." She slung the rag over her shoulder. "I'll consider your offer. That's not a yes. You of all people should appreciate the distinction — it's the same one you've been hiding behind since before I could write."

She went to the ladder, paused, and looked back at him, and for one moment Corvane saw not his best auditor, not his most troublesome employee, but the girl he'd taken in off the Copperlane steps with ink on her chin and nothing in the world but a head full of other people's memories — the girl he had, in whatever way a man like him could love anything, loved.

"When Pell's wagon leaves," she said quietly, "I'll know whose signature is on the order. And so will you. Every time you look in a mirror."

Then she climbed, and took up her rag, and wiped dust off a bottle of somebody's first love, and did not look down again.

Corvane sat alone at the reading table for a long while. Then he went down to his office, unlocked the bottom drawer, and took out the private book — the second ledger, the true one — and turned to the current page, where the autumn list waited in his neat shorthand. Fourteen names. He read them twice. His pen hovered over the page for a long time, longer than it had ever hovered, and the scales clicked below, and the candles burned, and in the end he wrote nothing at all — which was itself a decision, though he was too careful a man to admit what kind.

He sealed the book, put it away, and rang for Tess.

"Send word to the Chancery clerk," he said. "Tell him the master of the Gilded Hollow requests an audience with Lord Veyra herself. Not a letter. An audience."

Tess frowned. "About what, Master?"

Corvane looked at the drawer where the book lay shut, and thought of a boy with a bread loaf and a borrowed key, and a woman on a ladder who believed in third doors.

"About terms," he said.
