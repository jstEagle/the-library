## 8. The King Who Flinches

Aldric Vane woke in his own bed, which was the first wrong thing.

He had gone to sleep—had he gone to sleep?—in the map room, he was almost certain, bent over the eastern levies with a cup of wine going cold beside him. Now he was in the royal chamber, under the heavy coverlet embroidered with the stag-and-spire, and the light through the shutters said late afternoon, and the last thing he could find in his memory was the map room at dusk. Between dusk and now lay a gap, smooth-edged and featureless, like a page torn cleanly from a ledger.

He lay still and did the arithmetic the way his father had taught him to do it before battles: count what you have, count what you've lost, don't panic until the second number is bigger than the first.

What he had: a body that ached as though it had been sitting for many hours. A mouth dry as old parchment. A candle burned down to a puddle on the nightstand, which meant someone had been in the room—or no one had, and the candle had simply been left, and hours had poured through the room like water through an open window while he sat somewhere else entirely.

What he'd lost: the evening. Possibly more than the evening.

"Hello?" he said, to the room, because there are times when a king must be willing to look foolish in front of furniture.

The room said nothing back. The fire had been tended. His dressing gown hung ready on its hook. Everything was correct, and everything was wrong, and beneath it all—faint, patient, at the very floor of his hearing—came the whisper.

It was not a sound. He had learned that much over the years. It was closer to the feeling of a word you can't quite recall, sitting on the tongue, except the word never came and the not-coming went on forever. *You are tired,* the whisper suggested, without words. *You were always tired. Rest is only sensible.*

"I'm getting up," Aldric told it, and swung his legs out of bed, and noticed his hands.

There was ink on them. That was ordinary; kings signed things all day, and half the chancery's pens leaked like wounded birds. But the ink was smudged in long strokes, the way it looks when a hand has been dragged across wet writing again and again, and beneath the ink, packed into the crescents under his fingernails, was something darker.

Dried blood.

He held his hands up to the shuttered light and turned them over, slowly, the way one turns over a strange coin found on the road. There wasn't much. A crust in two nails of the right hand, a smear along the edge of the left thumb where skin meets nail—the place a man would press if he were gripping something. Or holding someone's wrist. Or—

*Stop,* said the whisper, gently, fondly. *You'll make yourself ill. Wash. Eat. You are hungry, aren't you? You're always hungry after.*

"After what?" Aldric asked aloud. His voice came out steady, which surprised him. Somewhere in twenty years of wearing the crown he had developed this: a court voice for his own skull. "After *what*?"

The whisper did not answer questions. It never answered questions. It made suggestions, and the suggestions arrived already wearing his own voice, already dressed in his own reasons, so that by the time he noticed he was agreeing with something, agreement felt like memory.

He washed. He scrubbed at his nails with the little brush the chamberlain provided and watched the water in the basin blush pink, then clear, and he thought, with a calm that frightened him more than screaming would have: *I have done this before. I know exactly how hard to scrub. I have done this many, many times.*

That was the thing about being a passenger in your own body. The horror wasn't in any single discovery. It was in the competence. The blood under his nails was familiar to him the way a stair is familiar—a body remembers its habits even when the mind has been locked out of the house.

* * *

The edict was on the desk in his private study, weighted under the obsidian seal, waiting for him as though it had been set there by a considerate servant.

*By order of the King,* it began, in his own hand—his own hand, unmistakably, the slight leftward lean of it, the way he crossed his t's too high—and it declared a hunt. All persons bearing the gift of unlicensed memory-work were to present themselves for registration within thirty days. Those who failed would be sought. Those found would be *treated*, and the word *treated* had been written with a care that made his stomach turn, the letters neat and deliberate, a physician's word doing a butcher's work.

Below it, his signature. Below that, the date. Yesterday.

Aldric read it three times. On the third reading he became aware that he was standing very still, the way one stands still when a dog one loves is growling at nothing, and that part of him—the part that lived below thought, the part that flinched—was trying to tell him something.

He flinched. That was the truth of it, the joke the gods had played on Aldric Vane: he had been flinching from his own crown for twenty years, some animal part of him jerking away every time the gold came near, and he had spent those same twenty years telling himself it was dignity. Gravity. The weight of office. Kings are supposed to feel the weight.

But a weight doesn't whisper. A weight doesn't leave you waking at midnight with your cheeks wet and no memory of weeping. A weight doesn't sign things.

"You didn't write this," he said to the edict. Then, because the study was empty and pride was a luxury he could no longer afford: "I didn't write this."

*Of course you did,* said the whisper, warm as mulled wine. *Who else? You saw the danger. You have always seen it clearly. These gifted ones—they reach into minds, they steal what isn't theirs, they twist love into obedience. You are protecting your people. You should be proud.*

And here was the trap, the beautiful, well-oiled trap: every word of it was reasonable. That was how he knew. Twenty years of listening had taught him the crown's one tell. Its arguments were always *slightly* too smooth, like a floor polished by a thousand feet, and always, underneath, they tasted faintly of someone else's mouth.

"No," Aldric said. He sat down heavily in the chair behind the desk—the chair his father had worn hollow, the chair that fit him like a borrowed coat—and pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes. "No. I remember the debate about the border tolls. I remember my daughter's name-day. I remember—" He stopped. He took stock, the way his father had taught him. Count what you have.

He could not remember deciding anything in three years.

Decrees came to him already argued, already won, and he signed them in bright good humor and could not afterward reconstruct why. Councilors spoke and their voices slid off him like rain off slate. He had begun, lately, to find notes in his own handwriting that he had no memory of writing—not just edicts but small domestic things, a reminder to order candles, a complaint about the soup—and each one was a little door in a wall, and behind each door was darkness, and the darkness was growing.

"Treated," he said aloud, tasting the word. "What does treated mean?"

The whisper offered him a picture, unbidden: a white room, a table, straps. It offered it the way one offers a child a sweet—as comfort. *Order,* the picture said. *Safety. You are a good king.*

Aldric stood up so fast the chair skidded, and he crossed to the window and threw open the shutters and let the cold late air hit his face, and for a moment—just a moment—the whisper receded, the way a tide draws back, and he could hear the city. Carts. Gulls. Someone in the lower town laughing at something, the laugh rising up between the roofs like a bird.

They were real, those sounds. They were outside the crown's jurisdiction, and he clung to them the way a drowning man clings to driftwood, and he understood, with terrible clarity, that this was his life now: a man surfacing for breath in his own head, gasping down whatever reality he could reach before the water closed over him again.

* * *

He tried, that evening, to test the edges of the gap.

This was another thing his father had taught him, though his father had meant it for sieges: when you cannot hold the wall, learn the shape of the breach. So Aldric sat in the map room with a decanter of water instead of wine—he had stopped drinking wine alone years ago, having woken once with a broken vase and no explanation and decided that a king who cannot account for his evenings should not drink alone—and he interrogated the servants, gently, one at a time.

The under-chamberlain remembered delivering papers at midday. The guard at the study door remembered the king taking no meals after noon. The maid who tended the fires remembered His Majesty speaking—this she reported with the careful blankness servants perfect for reporting madness to its face—remembered His Majesty speaking to himself in the study, "in a different voice, sire, if it please you."

"It doesn't please me," Aldric said, "but thank you. Different how?"

She looked at the floor. "Steadier, sire."

Steadier. He turned that over all night. His own voice, but steadier—the way a puppet is steadier than a dancer, the way a signature is steadier than a hand. Something wore him the way he wore the crown, and it wore him *well*, and that was somehow worse than if it wore him badly. If it stumbled, people would notice. It did not stumble.

Near dawn he found the second thing.

He had gone down to the council antechamber to fetch his cloak—he slept poorly and walked the corridors like a ghost auditing its own haunting—and there, on the side table where clerks left documents awaiting review, lay a list. A plain list, in a clerk's fair hand, of names. Names and districts. A lamplighter from Copperlane. A midwife from the Weft. A boy, aged fourteen, apprentice to a locksmith.

Against each name, in red ink, a single notation: *confirmed.*

Confirmed. Confirmed by whom? By what process? He read down the column and the column went on and on, forty names, sixty, and at the bottom, in his own hand, the instruction he did not remember giving: *Begin with the Hollow.*

The Gilded Hollow. He knew the name the way one knows a word heard through a wall. Some guild of traders—curiosities, he'd always assumed, relics and rarities, the sort of shop that sold dukes' grandmothers their conversation. Why would a curiosity-shop lead a list of the gifted?

Unless it wasn't a curiosity-shop. Unless the crown knew things he didn't, learned them through his own eyes during the missing hours, and was using his hand to write them down.

Aldric stood in the cold antechamber holding the list, and the whisper stirred, sleepy and satisfied, and murmured something that was almost a lullaby.

He did the arithmetic again. What he had: a body, a title, a few hours a day in which he was, as far as he could prove, himself. What he'd lost: nearly everything else, by slow degrees, the way ice leaves a pond—not all at once but from the edges inward, so that the middle stays blue and lovely and lies about the depth.

His father's voice, in memory: *When the second number is bigger, boy, stop counting and start fighting.*

But how did a man fight the thing inside his own head? You could not arrest it. You could not banish it. Every plan he formed, he formed *with* it listening; every ally he chose, it chose with him; every secret he kept, he kept in a house whose walls had ears. He had tried, once, three years ago—he was almost sure it was three years ago—to write a warning, a full confession, everything, sealed and sent to someone outside the palace. He could not now remember who he had chosen, or whether the letter had ever been written, or whether the hand that wrote it had been his.

Perhaps it was still in a drawer somewhere. Perhaps it had been burned. Perhaps it had been written and delivered and the person who received it had looked into his honest, kingly face and believed the crown instead.

There was one thing he could do that the whisper could not easily undo, because it required no decision, only a habit. Small things. Hidden things. Things a body could do on its own, below the level where the thief kept watch.

So that morning, in the grey hour before the household woke, King Aldric Vane sat at his desk and, with his left hand—his clumsy, honest, unpracticed left hand, the hand the crown never bothered to steer because it signed nothing official—wrote a note. It took him four attempts. The letters wandered like drunken soldiers. He did not care.

*I am still here,* it said. *Most days, some hours. The crown writes with my right hand. If you find this, I am sorry for whatever the right hand has done. Find the fire. Burn it. Do not mourn me—mourn the men I might have been, there were several, they were better company.*

He read it over twice. Then he looked around the study, at the desk with its false bottom, at the ledgers, at the clever hiding places any searcher would check, and rejected all of them, and began instead to laugh—quietly, helplessly, the first true laugh he'd had in months—at the only hiding place in the palace that was genuinely, perfectly safe.

The throne.

No one searched the throne. No one touched the throne. The throne was sacred, ceremonial, dusted by priests twice a year and otherwise ignored, and its armrests were old, and old oak shrinks, and shrunk oak leaves gaps, and gaps hold paper. He had noticed the gap years ago, in another life, idly, with a fingernail—the left-hand fingernail, as it happened.

He folded the note small, and he waited for the hour when the throne room stood empty, and he knelt before his own seat of power like a penitent, and he slid the paper into the hollow arm of the kingdom.

Then he rose, and straightened his robe, and put his face back on—the mild, agreeable, slightly tired face of a good king—and walked out into the corridor, where a clerk was already waiting with fresh papers for his signature.

"Good morning, sire," the clerk said.

"Is it?" said Aldric pleasantly, and held out his right hand for the pen.

Far below the city, in places he did not know existed, other hands were also beginning to move—an archivist lighting a candle over forbidden ledgers, an old thief-archivist waking from a dream of wings—and the gap in the king's day was about to become the most important blank page in the realm.
