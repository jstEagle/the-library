## 9. Old Debts

The address Maren had given me—three years ago, over a glass of plum wine and a debt neither of us ever named aloud—was *the blue door under the tannery, knock twice, then once, then wait until you hear the kettle.*

I had assumed the kettle was a joke. Maren did not joke about doors. Maren had once spent four months learning the specific rhythm of a palace guard's cough so she could walk past him during his sneezing fit, so no, the kettle was not a joke.

The tannery district smelled the way it always had: like money being made from something unpleasant. I kept my hood up and my hands in my pockets, where they could reach the three forged protection papers sewn into my lining. The criers had been at it since dawn. *By order of His Majesty King Aldric, all persons bearing the gift of unmaking memory shall present themselves for registration.* Registration first. Then "treatment." Nobody had explained what treatment meant, which was its own kind of answer.

Twice, then once. I waited on the step of a door painted a blue that had faded toward apology, and listened.

From inside: the clank of iron, a pause, and then—a kettle. Not boiling. Just lifted, and set down again, deliberately, like a hand knocking back.

I went in.

* * *

Maren had been beautiful the way a knife is beautiful, and forty years later she was still both, just duller at the edges. She sat in a chair by a stove that heated nothing but her feet, wrapped in three shawls, and she looked at me the way archivists look at misfiled documents: fondly, and with an immediate desire to correct me.

"You're thinner," she said.

"You're alive," I said. "I'd counted on it."

"Flatterer." She jerked her chin at the second chair. "Sit. You've got the look."

"What look?"

"The look of someone who's found something in a ledger that she wishes she hadn't." Maren poured tea from a pot that had seen more decades than either of us. "I taught your master Corvane everything he knows about filing, and filing is just lying with good posture. So don't lie to me with good posture, girl. What did you find?"

I held the cup and let the warmth soak through my palms while I decided how much to say. That was the trouble with old thieves. They could smell a withheld thing the way cats smell weather.

"A memory," I said finally. "A client sold it. A noblewoman, dying, clearing out her inventory before the end. It was twenty years old. The king—young, riding without escort through the Lantern Quarter because he liked to see the lamps lit himself, not have them lit *for* him. And every time he passed under the palace walls, Beatrix—" I stopped. Corrected myself. "*He* flinched. He flinched at his own crown. Like a man flinching from a dog he's fed all its life."

Maren drank her tea. Her eyes, over the rim, were very still.

"And you kept a copy," she said.

It wasn't a question, but I answered it anyway, because lying to Maren with bad posture was pointless and lying with good posture was worse. "Yes."

"Breaking your rule."

"Yes."

"Mm." She set down the cup. "And now you've come to the retired thief-archivist who broke into the palace vaults twice before you were weaned, because you want to know what makes a king flinch at his own crown."

"I want to know what's *in* it," I said. "The crown. There's something wrong with it, Maren. Wrong in the way our trade knows wrong. I've handled enough stolen memories to know what a mind sounds like when something else is wearing it. And the edicts—the hunt—it doesn't read like a king frightened of magic. It reads like a king *being used*. I need to know if I'm imagining it."

Maren was quiet long enough that the stove ticked twice.

Then she said, "Help me up. If we're doing this, we're doing it properly, and properly means the strongbox, and the strongbox means my knees are going to file a complaint."

* * *

Her strongbox was under the floorboards beneath the loom she never used, and it took both of us and one muttered curse to lift. Inside, wrapped in oilcloth against damp and time, lay the only archive of its kind outside the Hollow: Maren's private collection of things that should not exist. A pressed flower that bloomed whenever someone lied near it. A coin from a country that had never existed. And a ledger—hand-bound, spine cracked, pages soft as cloth—that she opened with the reverence other people reserved for scripture.

"This," she said, "is the ledger of debts I couldn't collect. Every job I ever walked away from because walking away was smarter than finishing. There are eleven entries. I'm going to show you number seven, and then you're going to wish I hadn't, and then you're going to ask me to keep going anyway, because you're Corvane's girl and curiosity is bred into your bones like gray eyes."

She turned to a page marked with a small inked flame.

"Thirty-one years ago," she said, "I stole from the palace. Not the treasury—any fool can steal gold, gold doesn't remember being stolen. I stole from the *vault beneath the vault*, the one that isn't on any map because the maps were burned by the man who drew them. I was young and I had a patron who paid in secrets, and what I brought out was this."

From the bottom of the strongbox she lifted a bundle of papers. Most were fragments—charred edges, water-stained corners, the wreckage of whatever bonfire the mapmaker had made. But one sheet was whole, and old, and written in a hand so formal it looked like architecture.

I read it. Then I read it again, because the first time, my mind had refused to hold it.

*"…the vessel shall be named Emberbound, and it shall be fed with living fire, and it shall be bound to the brow of kings, and it shall keep their counsel, and their counsel shall be kept…"*

"It's a forging contract," I said slowly. "Like a guild indenture. But the apprentice is—"

"The crown," Maren said. "Yes. Read the feeding clause."

I found it halfway down the page, in language so dry it took a moment to understand how horrifying it was. *"…and the fire shall be renewed each solstice, for the vessel consumes even that which feeds it, as the wearer's will is consumed also, that the vessel may hold what the wearer cannot…"*

"That's why he flinches," I whispered. "It's not fear. It's—"

"Appetite," said Maren. "You know how it feels when you take a memory from someone? That little pull, like a thread coming loose?" I nodded. Everyone with the gift knew it; some of us loved it, which was a truth we didn't say at dinner parties. "Now imagine a thing that does that not to memories but to *will*. To the part of a man that decides. It eats him a spoonful at a time, and it's been eating Aldric for twenty years, and everyone in that court calls it wisdom."

I sat with that. The tea had gone cold in my hands and I hadn't noticed.

"The contract says it's fed with living fire," I said. "Dragon-fire?"

"There were dragons once. Before the Spire, before the city, before anyone you've ever met was born. Men and dragons made bargains—the old stories aren't wrong, they're just badly edited. And when the last bargains broke, the dragons went into hiding or into death, and the ones who forged the Emberbound caught a dragon's fire while it was still burning and shut it inside the crown, and they've been feeding it fresh fire every solstice for centuries to keep it alive. Because here's the thing, girl, the thing that kept me up nights for a year after I read that page." Maren leaned forward, and her voice dropped as though the stove might repeat it. "The Emberbound isn't a tool. It's a *creditor*. Everything it gives—counsel, certainty, the appearance of a wise and steady king—it collects back with interest. And the interest is the man."

"Can it be removed?"

"Unbound, you mean? Taken off without killing him?" She shrugged, and the shrug was a masterpiece of studied ignorance. "Perhaps. There would be a rite. There's always a rite; men who forge monsters love ceremony. But removal isn't destruction, and destruction—" she tapped the contract, right on the word *living* —"destruction requires fire that's alive. Fire that chooses. The fire inside the crown is dying, has been dying for thirty years if the feeding schedule slipping is anything to go by, and when it dies the crown dies with it—but not before it takes the king all the way down. Unless someone feeds it. Or unless someone burns it with fire that's stronger than it is."

"How much living dragon-fire is left in the world?"

Maren looked at me for a long moment, and in that moment she wasn't an old woman in three shawls. She was the thief who had walked out of the palace vaults with a monster's birth certificate folded against her heart.

"One," she said. "Possibly. There's a story—only a story, mind, and stories in our trade are usually invoices in disguise—of a dragon sleeping under the old foundry, down in the drowned vaults, in ember-sleep. Has been for longer than the city's had a name. The fire gutters, they say. Slowly. Like a candle left in an empty house." She closed the ledger. "But Beatrix—and don't think I haven't noticed you flinched when I used your name, which means you gave the client a false one, clever girl—if that story is true, and if the fire is guttering, then there is a clock running. And clocks in stories like this do not run slowly at the end."

* * *

I stayed for a second pot of tea, because leaving too quickly after learning that the king of the realm was a hollowed man wearing a hungry ring of gold felt rude, somehow, to both of us.

"Why give me this?" I asked, nodding at the contract, which she had insisted I take. "You sat on it for thirty years. Why hand it to me now?"

Maren refilled my cup with the unhurried precision of a woman who had once picked a lock inside a moving carriage.

"Because thirty years ago, taking it anywhere would have gotten people killed, and I was tired, and cowardice fits nicely into retirement if you furnish it well." She said it without self-pity, which somehow made it worse. "And because this morning a crier stood under my window and told the whole street that people like us are going to be registered and treated, and I am too old to run, and too proud to be treated. If the crown is behind it—and you and I both know it is—then somebody ought to know what it is they're serving." She fixed me with a look. "Somebody with steady hands and a bad habit of keeping things she shouldn't."

"I'm going to fix that habit," I said.

"No, you're not. It's the only interesting thing about you." She waved me toward the door. "Go on. And girl—one more thing, free of charge, which should worry you, because nothing I know is ever free."

I paused at the threshold.

"The palace keeps records too. Better ones than mine, and crueler. And the crown's inquisitors have been pulling files all week—I hear things, even retired, especially retired. If a man with white cuffs and tired eyes starts asking questions about old vault-thefts, you tell him the thief died in the plague year and I'll confirm it myself." She smiled, thin as a blade's edge. "Because whoever he is, he'll come asking eventually. They always do."

* * *

I walked home the long way, along the canal, because the direct way passed the proclamation board and I couldn't bear to read my own name's future posted in letters two feet high.

The contract rode against my ribs inside my coat, and I kept touching it through the wool like a bruise. *Emberbound.* All those years auditing trades—a mother's face here, a first kiss there, a crime half-remembered—and I had thought I understood appetite. I catalogued hunger for a living. But every theft I'd ever brokered had ended somewhere. The memory changed hands, settled into a new skull, grew quiet. Even grief, bought and sold, came to rest.

The Emberbound's hunger had no ledger. No entry, no close of day, no line marked *paid in full*. It had been eating one specific man for twenty years, spoonful by spoonful, and the court called the result wisdom, and the criers called his edicts law, and somewhere above the king—or *through* the king, which was worse—someone was signing orders in a hand that used to be kind.

I thought of the dream. The lamps going out one by one. The weight settling onto his brow like a hand.

At the corner by the lamplighter's stand, I stopped, because a thought had arrived that I didn't invite and couldn't dismiss:

Destroying it required living dragon-fire. Living dragon-fire required a dragon. And a dragon, if Maren's story was more than an invoice in disguise, slept beneath the drowned vaults with a candle-flame for a heartbeat—which meant that if I meant to do anything at all, I needed to move soon, quietly, and alone.

Which was exactly the sort of plan I was good at.

Which was exactly the problem, said a smaller voice, because the last time I moved quietly and alone, I kept a copy of a king's memory, and now I was carrying a monster's contract through the streets like a woman carrying water in both hands, unable to set it down.

Behind me, somewhere across the rooftops, a bell rang the hour. Ahead, in the direction of the Spire, the evening light caught the palace windows and turned them briefly, horribly, the color of fire.

I turned up my collar and went to find a map of the drowned vaults.

I did not yet know that someone else was already looking for one—someone with tired eyes and white cuffs, holding a salvaged page in my own handwriting, saying nothing to anyone at all.
