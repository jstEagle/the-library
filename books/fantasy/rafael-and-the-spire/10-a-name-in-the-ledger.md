## 10. A Name in the Ledger

The records room of the Inquisitor's Hall smelled of tallow, dust, and the particular despair of paper that had been filed by frightened clerks. Rafael had spent eleven years learning to read that smell. It meant someone had been here before him, moving fast, and had not cared whether the story stayed straight.

He sat alone at the long table beneath the one high window, a lamp pulled close, and spread the seized documents across the scarred oak like a man laying out cards he already suspected were marked.

Three days since the proclamation. Three days since the criers had stood on every corner of the city, their voices cracking with official joy, announcing His Majesty's great hunt for the gifted — register now, be treated kindly, hide and be hunted. Three days since Rafael had watched Beatrix's face across a crowded guild hall and seen her do the arithmetic everyone gifted was doing: *how long do I have?*

And three days since he had taken a single page from the Hollow's ledger, folded it into his coat, and told himself it was leverage. He still told himself that. The telling was getting less convincing.

Tonight's work was supposed to be simple. Routine. Veyra wanted the arrest lists reconciled against the census rolls before the first wagons rolled out at dawn, and Rafael, as senior inquisitor, was the natural hand for it. Cross-reference, confirm, initial. A night of clerk-work. He had almost been grateful for it — an evening too busy to think.

Then he noticed the dates.

It began as a small wrongness, the kind he'd been trained to catch: a ledger entry dated the day *after* an event it described. Clerks made such mistakes constantly. But this wasn't one mistake. It was a pattern, and patterns in dates are like patterns in footprints — once you see them, you cannot stop seeing them.

He pulled the master list toward him. Forty-one names in the first wave. Each name had a dossier: known associates, known haunts, known gifts where the gift was known. Meticulous work. Admirable work, even. The kind of work that takes weeks.

The proclamation had been issued nine days ago.

Rafael set down his pen. He looked at the dossier in front of him — a woman named Sera Ilvane, gift of finding lost things, last seen in the Tanners' Quarter — and read the date at its head with the care of a man reading his own death warrant.

*Prepared the 3rd of Emberfall.*

The proclamation was issued the 21st of Emberfall.

Eighteen days. Eighteen days before the king told his people there would be a hunt, someone had already written out who would be caught in it.

He told himself there was an explanation. There was always an explanation; he had built a career on believing them. Drafts prepared in advance, perhaps. Contingency planning. A prudent chancellery preparing options for a sovereign famous for changing his mind. Kings announced things they had long decided to do; any competent government worked ahead of its own announcements.

But contingency drafts were rough things. These were not rough. These dossiers had been *corrected*. Someone had gone back through them after the proclamation and updated addresses, added recent sightings, noted which families had fled and which had foolishly stayed. The original lists sat underneath like bones under skin, and the bones were eighteen days older than the flesh.

Rafael did the only thing he could think to do, which was to check more names. He worked until the candle burned down two fingers' width, pulling dossiers, checking heads against the proclamation date, and forty-one times out of forty-one the answer was the same. Every name on the first arrest list had been written down before anyone in the city knew there would be arrests.

Except one thing troubled him more than the dates, and it took him another hour to understand why.

There was no name for the person who had compiled them.

Every document in the Hall bore a compiler's mark — tradition, accountability, vanity, call it what you liked. Even the most routine seizure list carried the initials of some junior clerk proud or resigned enough to sign his work. These dossiers carried nothing. No initials, no seal, no hand he recognized. Just clean, confident, anonymous script, as though the lists had written themselves.

Or as though whoever wrote them had never needed to fear an audit.

Rafael sat back in the dark and listened to the building settle around him. Somewhere below, a door closed. Somewhere above, wind worried at the shutters of the high window. And somewhere in this city, a man wore a crown and signed edicts, and Rafael had always assumed — had *needed* to assume — that the edicts began with the man.

*From above the king.* Veyra's words came back to him, spoken over tea five days ago with the mildness of a woman discussing weather. He had taken it for courtly cynicism then, the old chancellor's habit of reminding ambitious young men that power flowed through channels they couldn't see. He had laughed, politely.

He was not laughing now.

Because there was one more thing, and it was the thing that had actually brought him here tonight, though he'd dressed it up as duty. Folded inside his coat, wrapped in oilcloth, was the page he'd taken from the Gilded Hollow. He unwrapped it now and laid it flat under the lamp.

A ledger page. Columns of entries in a fine, disciplined hand — trades recorded, prices noted, conditions appended in that meticulous way of hers, *memory intact, donor wept, handle gently*. He had read it a dozen times. He could have recited it. What held him was not the content but the hand itself: the small hooked crossbar on the numeral seven, the way the descenders on g and y curled just slightly leftward, like handwriting leaning away from something.

Beatrix Ravensworth's hand. He'd know it anywhere. He had, in another life, watched it form across a whole summer of letters she'd never sent him, back when they were both too young and too proud to say anything that mattered.

An inquisitor should have logged it as evidence. Should have entered it into the record, tied it to the Hollow, started the machinery that would end with Beatrix in a wagon bound for 'treatment.'

Instead he had stolen it. From a crime scene. From his own investigation. Like a love letter.

He looked at the page for a long time. Then he turned it over, and that was when he saw it — faint, pressed through from the sheet beneath in whatever stack it had been copied from. Ghost-writing. A mirror-image impression of a line from another document entirely.

He held it up to the lamp, tilted it, squinted. The impression was shallow, but the lamp was good and his eyes were better. Word by word, letter by reversed letter, he reconstructed it:

*...the Emberbound requires sustenance proportionate to...*

Nothing else survived. No second line, no signature. Just that fragment, pressed into the back of a guild ledger by some heavier document that had lain atop it — a document about something called the Emberbound, written in the same anonymous hand as the dossiers.

Rafael wrapped the page up again slowly.

He did not know what the Emberbound was. But he knew this: the same hand that had drafted the arrest lists eighteen days early had touched the guild's records. The hunt and the Hollow, connected at the wrist. And a fragment of a sentence about *sustenance*, as if the thing being discussed ate.

He thought of the king. Old stories said Aldric had been merry once, before the crown. Recent stories said the king rarely appeared in public anymore, and when he did, he stood very still, like a man listening to something.

Rafael blew out the lamp and sat in the dark with all of it, and understood that he had crossed a line he could not uncross. Not when he took the page — that had been weakness, forgivable, human. This was different. He had *noticed*. And in the Inquisitor's Hall, noticing was the treason. You could commit any sin so long as you didn't see it.

* * *

Lord Chancellor Veyra received him at the seventh hour of the morning, which was itself a message. Chancellors did not see junior officers at the seventh hour unless they wished to remind the junior officer exactly how far beneath them he stood.

Her office occupied the top of the Chancellery tower, all pale stone and tall windows, arranged with a precision that made Rafael's teeth ache. Everything faced outward. Nothing in the room suggested a private life: no portraits, no keepsakes, no clutter of humanity. Just maps, ledgers, and a woman of sixty-odd years with iron-grey hair and eyes the color of a winter sky, who greeted him with genuine warmth.

"Inquisitor Vale. Sit. You look like a man who has been reading all night."

"I have, my lord."

"Good. That's what I pay you for." She poured tea herself, which was also a message — Veyra performed humility the way other women wore perfume, lightly and with intent. "You've finished the reconciliation?"

"Mostly." He accepted the cup. His hands were steady. He'd have bet his commission on them being steady. "I found some irregularities."

"Tell me."

"The arrest dossiers. Their preparation predates the proclamation. By eighteen days, in every case I checked."

He laid the master list on her desk, open to the dates, and watched her face with everything he had. Veyra was not a woman whose face gave much away, but she was a woman who enjoyed competence, and for half a heartbeat something moved behind those winter eyes that might have been approval.

"Yes," she said. "I wondered when you'd find that."

Not denial. Not anger. *Yes.*

"You knew."

"Of course I knew. I've known for years." She settled back, folding her hands, and spoke with the patience of a tutor addressing a promising student. "Do you know how government actually works, Inquisitor? Not the civics they teach magistrates' sons. The real mechanism. Government is anticipation. The announcement is theater. The work is done long before, quietly, by people who understand that order is not declared — it is *prepared*." She tapped the master list. "His Majesty resolved upon the hunt some time ago. We merely ensured that when he spoke, the machinery was ready. Would you prefer chaos instead? Unprepared officers, mobs, gifted children burned in alleys by frightened neighbors? Because that is the alternative to preparation."

"It would explain the preparation," Rafael said carefully. "It doesn't explain why the dossiers carry no compiler's mark."

Something cooled in the room. Not the temperature — the attention.

"That," Veyra said, "is above your station."

"With respect, my lord, unsigned state documents are precisely my station. Accountability is—"

"Do you know what I admire about you, Rafael?" She rose and walked to the window, hands clasped behind her back, silhouetted against the grey morning. "You're honest. Genuinely, stubbornly honest. It's rare. It's valuable. It's also, I'm sorry to say, a liability, because honesty insists on seeing patterns, and some patterns are load-bearing. Pull at them and things come down on people." She turned. "Your sister, for instance."

The word landed like a dropped glass.

"Liss," Veyra continued, pleasantly. "Twenty-two now, isn't she? Teaching at the charity school off Cooper's Lane. Bright girl. Devoted. Writes you long letters every week — I confess I've read several. She has your honesty." A pause, delicate as a knife laid on silk. "And your habit of asking questions. Last month she wrote to a friend in the capital asking why the palace physician visits the Chancellery. Curious girl."

Rafael did not move. Every instinct screamed at him to move — to stand, to deny, to do something — and he did none of it, because he understood suddenly and completely that this conversation had been designed, that every step of it, perhaps every step of his last month, had been designed, and that the only ground left to him was stillness.

"My sister," he said, "has committed no crime."

"No. She's committed curiosity, which in the current season is nearly the same thing." Veyra returned to her desk and sat, and the warmth came back into her voice like sun through parting cloud. "Which brings me to why I asked you here. The hunt will need strong hands. Honest ones. I want you leading the second wave — a promotion, with the salary and standing that implies. Liss could attend a proper school then. The Meridian Academy, perhaps. Boarding. South, where the air is cleaner for a growing girl." She smiled. "All I ask is what you already do best, Inquisitor. Read the records. Follow the orders. And leave the patterns that are load-bearing alone."

The tea sat untouched between them, going cold.

"And if I decline?"

Veyra's smile deepened by a degree, and for the first time Rafael saw what lived underneath the warmth, and it was not cruelty exactly. It was certainty — vast, patient, architectural certainty, the kind that builds gallows and calls them scaffolding.

"Then nothing happens to you," she said. "Nothing ever happens to anyone, Inquisitor. Wagons simply arrive at schools. Registrations develop errors. Honest men find themselves reassigned to duties where their particular talents are wasted, and curious sisters find themselves in districts where the hunting is... less regulated." She lifted his untouched cup and set it in his hands, closing his fingers around the warmth. "Drink your tea. Consider the offer. You have until the wagons roll at dawn."

He drank it. He would remember that afterward with a particular shame — that he stood in her office, holding proof that the law had been forged before the lawgiver spoke, and he drank her tea because his hands needed something to do.

At the door he paused.

"The Emberbound," he said. "What is it?"

For one instant — one — the certainty flickered. Something moved behind Veyra's eyes that was not surprise but recognition, the look of a card-player watching an opponent turn a card she'd forgotten she'd dealt.

"Where did you hear that word?"

"A fragment. Pressed into a ledger page I confiscated from the Hollow."

Silence stretched. Then Veyra laughed softly, shaking her head, and it was almost fond.

"You took a page from a crime scene and didn't log it." She clicked her tongue. "That's theft from evidence, Inquisitor. Treason, in wartime, if I cared to press it. So you see how it goes — we each hold a knife, and neither of us can afford to use theirs." She waved a hand, dismissal and blessing in one gesture. "Go home. Sleep. Lead your wave at dawn. And Rafael — burn that page. Whatever the Emberbound is, it isn't yours to carry."

He walked out through the marble corridors, past clerks and guards and the great seal of the crown, past all the beautiful machinery of a lawful state, and the truth rang in him like a cracked bell:

The law was not broken. That was the horror of it. The law was *working perfectly* — precise, prepared, punctual — and it had been aimed, like a weapon, at everyone he loved, by hands that had never once needed to sign their work.

Dawn was four hours off. The wagons rolled at first light, and among the names on his list — he had checked, of course he had checked, the moment he'd read the master list, heart in his throat — among the forty-one names of the second wave, scheduled for the Tanners' Quarter by week's end, was one he knew better than his own.

*Ravensworth, B. — Gilded Hollow — memory-gift — priority.*

Priority. Her name, written eighteen days before the king spoke, in a hand that feared no audit.

Rafael stood in the corridor a long moment. Then he went back to the records room, sat down at the long table, and drew a fresh sheet of paper toward him.

If the lists had been written in advance, then they could be rewritten. If the machinery ran on paper, then paper could lie. He had spent eleven years learning to read falsified records.

It was time he learned to write them.

He dipped his pen, and began.
