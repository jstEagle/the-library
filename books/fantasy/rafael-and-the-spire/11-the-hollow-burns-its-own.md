## 11. The Hollow Burns Its Own

The summons came at dawn, folded into the hand of a messenger who wouldn't look at me.

*Corvane requests the full guild in the Long Room. Attendance is not optional.*

I had been awake anyway. I was always awake now, it seemed — lying in my narrow room above the archive, listening to the city's criers rehearse the king's proclamation in their sleep-deprived voices: *all who carry the gift shall present themselves for registration, for their own protection and the protection of the realm.* The phrase *for their own protection* had a particular ring to it, the way a trap has a particular smell. Rats know. That's why they avoid the cheese that smells like kindness.

I dressed, braided my hair back tight the way I did when I expected bad news — loose hair is a liability when someone grabs you from behind, a lesson learned at fourteen and never forgotten — and went down into the Hollow.

The Gilded Hollow at dawn was a different creature than the Hollow at midnight. By candlelight, with the ledgers open and the clients nervous and hopeful, it felt like a church of small mercies. In the grey morning light, with the lamps unlit and the dust showing plainly on the shelves of bottled luck, it looked like what it was: a cellar full of stolen things, run by tired people who told themselves stories about necessity.

We gathered in the Long Room, thirty-odd of us, standing among the trestle tables where we usually ate. Corvane stood at the head, and beside him — this was the first wrong note, struck before he even spoke — stood Master Quillon, the Hollow's advocate, a soft-spoken man whose entire profession was making ugly bargains sound like weather.

Corvane looked well. That was the second wrong note. A man delivering terrible news should look like he'd slept badly. Corvane's coat was brushed, his beard trimmed, his hands folded with the calm of a merchant who has already counted the profit.

"You all know why we're here," he said. "The proclamation. The hunt." He let the words sit, the way he did when he wanted us to feel the weight before he lifted it off us. "Veyra's inquisitors have been asking questions. Names. They came to me three nights ago."

A murmur went through the room. Someone near me — Hesta, the luck-broker with the crooked teeth — whispered, "What did you tell them?"

Corvane's eyes found her without moving his head. It was a gift of his, that stillness. "I told them," he said, "that the Gilded Hollow keeps its ledgers clean."

Which was true, in the sense that a riverbed is clean after the flood has taken everything loose.

"The Crown does not want the Hollow," Corvane continued. "The Crown wants *gifted*. Registered ones, treated ones, gone ones — it doesn't much care which, so long as the count is satisfied. And so long as the count is satisfied elsewhere, no one comes knocking on our door." He unfolded his hands. "Three names have been given up. Voluntarily offered by me, as proof of our cooperation. Those three will be escorted out this morning. The rest of you will be invisible. That is the arithmetic, and I won't pretend it's anything gentler than arithmetic."

The silence afterward had texture. You could feel everyone doing the same calculation at once — *not me, not me, not me* — and hating themselves for it, and doing it anyway. I did it too. I am not going to lie to you, whoever you are, reading this in some kinder year: my first thought was *not me*, and my second thought was shame about the first thought, and only somewhere third or fourth did I get around to asking the question that mattered.

"Who?" I said.

My voice came out flat. Corvane heard it anyway. He had known me since I was nine years old and caught picking his lock with a bent nail; he knew every tone I owned.

"Names stay between me and the Crown," he said.

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only answer that keeps thirty people safe instead of three."

Quillon cleared his throat softly, the advocate preparing to make the ugly bargain sound like weather. "Beatrix. No one is being *given to* anything. Registration is not execution. These are interviews, assessments—"

"I've read Veyra's assessment protocols," I said. "They're written in the margins of the seized records. 'Treatment' has a definition, and it isn't a kind one."

"Then those three are heroes," Corvane said, and there it was — the mask slipping just enough to show the machinery underneath, the pragmatism wearing its Sunday face. "They save the many by standing for the few. I'll see their families provided for. Generously."

Generously. As if grief were a line item. As if you could balance a ledger against a person.

I looked around the room. Hesta had gone grey. Old Fenwick, who bottled dreams and watered them down like cheap wine, was staring at his shoes. And near the back, half-hidden behind a shelf of unluck —

Wick.

He was nineteen. He had been eleven when I met him, a ferret-thin boy from the tannery rows with quick fingers and no fear, and it was Wick — not Maren, not Corvane, Wick — who taught me to pick locks properly. Not the bent-nail fumbling. The real craft. *Feel it, don't force it,* he used to say, guiding my wrist with his small sure hands. *A lock is just a door that hasn't been persuaded yet.* He could open anything. He could also, which mattered more, remember everything — faces, numbers, the exact grain of a stranger's voice — because Wick's gift wasn't theft at all. His gift was perfect memory. He couldn't help keeping things. Every face he'd ever seen lived in him like pressed flowers in a book.

And now his name was in a ledger I hadn't written, sold by the man who kept them all.

He caught me looking. He didn't cry. He gave me the ghost of the grin he'd given me at twelve, hanging upside down from a drainpipe, holding up a lock he'd opened in four seconds flat. *Feel it, don't force it, Bea.*

I looked away.

That is the sentence I have carried longest. Not *they took him.* Not even *I did nothing.* Just: *I looked away.* Four syllables, and they weigh more than any ledger I ever balanced.

* * *

They came for the three of them within the hour.

I watched from the loading bay, where the wagons came in with crates of bottled memory and left with nothing anyone could prove. Two inquisitors in grey, polite as tax assessors, with the registration papers already made out — which meant the names had been prepared days ago, which meant Corvane hadn't surrendered them last night in a crisis but had been curating this list the way he curated stock. The other two were a dream-bottler named Sef and a quiet woman called Ilse who traded in forgotten languages. I barely knew them. That should have made it easier. It didn't; it made it worse, in the way that strangers' funerals are worse than family ones, because there's no ritual to hold you up.

Wick came down the stairs between the two greys with his wrists bound in front of him — bound, though he'd offered no resistance, because procedure is its own kind of violence — and he stopped at the bottom step and looked at me.

Not accusingly. That would have been bearable. Accusation gives you something to push against. He looked at me the way he used to look at a lock he'd finally understood: with recognition. Like we were both mechanisms, and he'd worked out how I turned.

"Bea," he said. "It's all right."

"It is not all right."

"No," he agreed, after a moment, honest to the bone the way he'd always been. "But it's not your fault either. Feel it, don't force it." And then, because he was nineteen and brave in the specific way of people who keep everything and regret nothing: "You'll write it down, won't you? What happened. Somewhere. So it stays."

Wick, who could not forget, asking to be remembered by someone who could choose.

"Yes," I said. "Page one."

He smiled then, the whole grin, the drainpipe grin, and let them walk him to the wagon.

The wagon rolled out through the bay doors into the grey morning. I stood in the doorway with my hands very still at my sides — stillness being the thing I do instead of screaming, the trade I've practiced longest — and I watched until it turned the corner by the chandler's, and I did nothing.

Nothing. The word looks so small written down. It fit inside the space of one heartbeat. A whole human life can be abandoned in the time it takes blood to move once through your heart, if you're disciplined enough.

Corvane appeared at my shoulder. He smelled of cedar oil and clean linen, and I hated him with a completeness that frightened me, because hatred that total is just love that's been robbed.

"You'll understand someday," he said quietly. "When you're the one keeping people alive. Survival isn't a virtue, Beatrix. It's a budget."

"Whose budget?"

"Exactly," he said, as if I'd agreed with him, and walked away.

* * *

I don't remember most of that day. This is the truth of it, and I record it precisely because I'm a woman who deals in memory and knows how unreliable the inventory gets under strain: I have gaps. I know I entered figures in the ledger, because the figures are there in my handwriting, correct to the copper. I know I ate something at midday, because the plate was empty after. But the hours themselves are missing, the way hours go missing for people who have decided not to be present in their own lives.

What I remember is the alley.

Behind the Hollow there's an alley that runs down to the drainage grate, where we used to dump the dregs of failed bottlings — memories too degraded to sell, luck gone sour, dreams that curdled. It smells of tallow and wet stone. In the old days Wick used to crouch by that grate and fish out interesting failures, holding them up to the light like a magpie with stolen silver. *Look, Bea, someone's whole wedding day, ruined, going to waste.*

I made it as far as the grate before my body filed its objection.

I was sick into the alley, violently, gracelessly, against the wall where the mortar has crumbled into a gap exactly the size of a hiding child's eye — the gap I used to spy through at nine years old, watching Corvane's meetings, while Wick held my ankles steady from below and whispered *breathe, breathe, you're doing beautifully.*

I vomited until there was nothing left, and then I stayed crouched there, cheek nearly against the cold stone, looking through that little gap at the grey light beyond, and I thought: *this is what survival costs. Not death. This. The alley. The taste. The looking away.*

There's a lie the gifted tell each other, and I told it to clients for years with a straight face: *what's traded away is gone, and good riddance.* Clean transactions. No residue. But here is what I actually know, kneeling in the tallow-smell with my arms shaking: everything leaves a residue. Every trade. Every silence. Wick's face at the bottom of the stairs, saying *it's not your fault,* which was the single cruelest thing anyone has ever done for me, because he spent his last free breath absolving the person who stood still.

I could have spoken. One name, spoken loudly, in front of thirty witnesses — *take me instead, take me too* — and the arithmetic changes. Maybe it collapses entirely. Maybe Corvane loses his neat little budget and the inquisitors take the whole cellar. Or maybe — and this is the thought I turned over and over like a coin worn smooth — maybe one more name on the wagon saves no one, and all it buys is my absence from the story before the story needs telling. Maren's voice in my head, dry as old paper: *A dead archivist protects no one, girl. Live ugly. Write it down.*

Live ugly. Write it down.

I wiped my mouth with my sleeve, and I sat back against the wall, and I made myself a promise in the exact grammar of a ledger entry, because promises in plain language break too easily:

*One page, owed: W. To be paid in full. Interest accruing.*

Then I got up, and I washed my face at the pump, and I went back inside, and I was precise, and I was useful, and I was invisible — the three virtues of the Hollow, the three virtues of the grave.

But underneath the precision, something had shifted its weight, the way a floorboard shifts before it gives. I had spent my whole life believing that neutrality was safety, that the person who keeps the books stands outside the story. Corvane had proven otherwise in a single morning: the bookkeeper is always in the story. The only question is whether she's holding the pen or being written.

And there was a page in the archive I needed. I'd saved it from Corvane's fire weeks ago — the noblewoman's sale, the young king flinching from his own crown — and I had told myself I kept it out of professional curiosity. Kneeling in that alley, tasting bile and honesty, I admitted what it actually was. It was the beginning of a debt of my own. The king on that throne was a man in a wagon, bound and polite, being carried somewhere by forces that wore his own face.

Someone had to feel that lock. Not force it. Feel it.

I climbed back up to the archive as the light went amber, and I took out the hidden page, and I spread it flat beneath the lamp — and that was when I heard the knock at the street door. Three slow raps. Unhurried. Official.

Through the spy-slit in the door, white as a wound in the dark: an inquisitor's coat.

Rafael.
