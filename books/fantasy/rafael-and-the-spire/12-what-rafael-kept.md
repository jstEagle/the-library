## 12. What Rafael Kept

The forgery took Rafael three hours, and he was ashamed of how much he enjoyed it.

He sat in his office above the Ninth Precinct with the door bolted, a lamp turned low, and the boy's registration form spread before him like a patient on a table. Tam. Fourteen years old. Gifted—weakly, the way a candle is fire—and now property of the hunt, scheduled for transport to the registration houses by week's end.

Rafael dipped his pen and began to lie.

It was not difficult work. The forms were designed by clerks who assumed no one would bother reading them closely, and Rafael had spent eleven years learning exactly which details inquisitors skimmed and which they lingered over. He adjusted the boy's age upward by two years—the threshold for mandatory treatment was sixteen, and children under it went to "assessment," a slower, softer machine. He shifted the date of manifestation back so that it fell before the proclamation, into the gray territory of cases pending review. He miswrote the district of residence, sending the file to an archive in Lowmarsh that was famously three years behind on its intake.

Then, because he could not help himself, he improved the handwriting.

That was the part that shamed him. There was something almost pleasant about it, the small craft of it, the way a lockpick must feel about a stubborn tumbler. He had spent his career building a reputation for incorruptibility, and here he was at midnight, whistling through his teeth—actually whistling, quietly, appalled at himself—as he committed his first act of documented treason.

*Documented,* he thought, capping the ink. *Everything else was just cowardice.*

He thought of Veyra's face when she spoke of the king's edicts coming from above the king. He thought of the arrest lists drafted before the proclamation existed. He thought of his sister, Lira, asleep in her room two streets away, and of how many steps there were between forging one form and losing everything, and whether he had already taken the first without noticing.

The boy would buy time. Weeks, perhaps a month, before some clerk in Lowmarsh noticed the discrepancy. It was not rescue. It was a candle held against a flood.

But candles were what he had.

He blotted the page, sealed it into the morning's stack where it would look suitably dull, and sat for a long moment with his hands flat on the desk, listening to the precinct settle around him—the groan of floorboards, a drunk singing three cells down, the rain starting against the window.

There was one more thing he needed tonight, and it was not in any file.

---

Beatrix Ravensworth kept rooms above a chandler's shop on Wick Lane, which Rafael considered either the best or worst possible cover for a woman who traded in stolen light of another kind entirely. He had known the address for six years and never once used it. Tonight he stood across the street in the rain for the better part of an hour, telling himself he was waiting for the chandler's dog—a fat, judgmental creature named Bishop—to settle, when in fact he was simply failing to climb the stairs.

He had rehearsed this. That was the humiliating part. In his office, over the forgery, he had composed opening lines the way other men composed letters to women they admired, discarding each one as too blunt, too soft, too obviously rehearsed. *I'm not here to arrest you.* Too defensive; only guilty people said that first. *We need to talk about the crown.* Too abrupt; she'd have the window open and him falling into the yard before he finished the sentence. *Do you remember the night at the harbor—* No. Absolutely not. That memory belonged to neither of them anymore, or to both, and dragging it out now would be like using a knife at dinner.

In the end he climbed the stairs without deciding anything, which was, he reflected, how he made most of his important decisions.

The knock he gave was the old one. Three quick, one slow. He hadn't used it since he was twenty-two, and his hand performed it without consulting him, the way hands do with prayers.

Silence. Then, from within, the sound of a chair pushed back, careful footsteps, and Beatrix's voice, level as a ledger line: "If you're selling candles, the shop is downstairs."

"It's me."

Another silence, longer. When the door opened, she stood in the gap with a candlestick raised—not lit, just held, the way you'd hold a club you were too polite to swing. She was dressed for sleep but clearly hadn't been anywhere near it: boots on, hair pinned up with what appeared to be a letter opener, ink on her fingers to the second knuckle.

"You used the harbor knock," she said.

"I did not."

"Three quick, one slow. You absolutely did."

"That was an accident." He stepped past her before she could decide otherwise, out of the rain and into a room that smelled of beeswax, paper, and the particular clean nothing of a woman who owned very little on purpose. "I need to talk to you."

"Then talk fast, because I'm expecting someone."

"At midnight?"

"She's very punctual." Beatrix set down the candlestick and folded her arms. "Talk."

He talked. He told her about the lists drafted before the proclamation, and watched her go very still in the way of people who had already suspected and hated being right. He told her about Veyra—*from above the king*—and about the threat wrapped inside the Chancellor's courtesy, though he left Lira out of it; that wound was his own. And then he told her the thing he had come to tell, the thing that had been sitting in his chest since the wagon left with the guild's three handed over like bad debts:

"I falsified Tam's registration tonight. The boy Corvane sold. He has weeks now instead of days. It isn't enough. It isn't nearly enough, and I don't know what *is* enough, and I've come to ask you what you know about the crown, because I think you know something, and I think you've known it longer than I have."

Beatrix looked at him for a long time.

"You're not here to arrest me," she said slowly, testing the words like ice on a pond.

"No."

"Say it plainly. I want it said."

"I am not here to arrest you, Beatrix." He heard his own voice drop. "I'm here because I don't know who else to be afraid with."

Something moved behind her eyes—something old, quickly filed away. She turned to the shelf, poured two fingers of plum brandy into mismatched cups, and handed him one.

"Sit down," she said. "This will take a while, and you'll want to be sitting for the parts that sound insane."

---

She told him everything. Maren, the retired archivist. The Emberbound. Dragon-fire, and its dying. A last dragon sleeping beneath the foundry, guarded by a blind woman who measured weeks the way other people measured years. Rafael listened with his cup untouched, and the horror of it was not the dragon or the crown but how completely it explained the shape of the last month—every edict, every list, every wrongness he had felt and filed under *politics.*

"The crown eats him," Beatrix said. "Slowly. And it signs things with his hand. And Veyra feeds it." She laughed, short and bitter. "And we're going to stop it, apparently, you and I, with a forged form and a stolen ledger page."

"We?" Rafael set down the cup. "You said *we.*"

"I misspoke."

"You didn't."

"Rafael." She rubbed her eyes with the heel of her hand, and for a moment she looked less like the most dangerous woman in the Gilded Hollow and more like a tired clerk at the end of a long audit. "Do you understand what you're asking? If you're caught near me, Veyra doesn't ruin you. She ruins your sister first, and makes you watch, and *then* ruins you. I've seen how she works from the outside. You'd be handing her the map."

"I know how she works from the inside," he said. "That's rather my point."

They stared at each other across the little table, and the rain filled the silence, and Rafael understood that they were both doing the same arithmetic—weighing trust against survival and finding the sums refused to balance—and that neither of them was going to say any of it aloud, because saying it aloud would make it a bargain, and bargains could be broken, and what they needed was something that couldn't.

"There's one more thing," he said.

He reached into his coat and brought out a flat packet wrapped in oilcloth, and set it on the table between them.

Beatrix went white.

He had imagined this moment all evening—her anger, her fear, her recriminations—and none of it arrived. Instead she simply looked at the packet the way people look at graves they thought had been moved. Her hand came out and stopped an inch above it, not touching.

"Where did you get this."

"Corvane's burned records. The salvage pile before the inquisitors sorted it." He kept his voice even. "A memory, stored in wax and thread. I recognized the preservation style. Guild work. I recognized—" he hesitated— "the label. Your father's name. In your handwriting."

"You read the label."

"I read the label."

"So you know what it is."

"A memory you stole and kept. Which breaks your own rule, according to every rumor I've ever heard about you." He leaned forward. "Which means it matters more than any rule."

For a long moment she didn't move. Then she unwrapped the oilcloth with the care of a surgeon, and there it was: a small cylinder of pale wax, threaded with silver wire, holding inside it a dead man's afternoon. Rafael knew almost nothing of her father—she had never spoken of him, not once in six years—but he saw what the object was to her, plain as a wound.

"Why do you have it?" she asked quietly. "Why bring it here? You could have used it. Leverage. Blackmail. It's worth more than my life to certain people."

"Yes."

"*Rafael.*"

"Because I'm tired," he said, and heard the truth arrive before he could dress it up. "I'm tired of keeping things. Everyone in this city keeps things now—Veyra keeps minds, the crown keeps kings, Corvane keeps names, I keep files on people I love. I wanted, just once, to give something back." He pushed it an inch toward her. "It was never mine. Take it."

She picked it up. Held it to the candlelight. And then, to his astonishment, she laughed—a real laugh, wet at the edges, startled out of her.

"Do you know what's in it?" she said. "Do you know what memory this is?"

"No."

"Neither do I. I stole it from a client eight years ago—a copy, off the books, the one rule I ever broke—and I've never dared look inside. Because if I take it into my head, it becomes mine, truly mine, and if it's something terrible—" She stopped. Pressed her lips together. "I've been carrying a locked box for eight years because I was afraid of what my father put in it."

"And now?"

"Now I'm going to open it," she said, "because a man who commits treason with excellent penmanship just handed it back to me for free, and if that's not a sign, nothing is."

She held out her hand across the table, palm up. Not to shake. Open, the way you offer a hand to show it holds nothing.

Rafael looked at it. Six years of circling, of almosts, of a history neither of them had ever put into words because words would make it accountable—and here was her hand, asking for nothing except to be believed.

He put his hand in hers.

No oath. No terms. They shook once, briefly, like merchants closing a deal whose goods were invisible, and let go, and that was the whole of the pact: two liars, choosing trust anyway, because every alternative had rotted.

"Now," Beatrix said, standing, tucking the wax cylinder into her shirt like a heartbeat. "Get out. My punctual visitor will be here soon, and she's extremely judgmental about men who smell of government ink."

"What is it with you and animals that judge me?"

"It's a gift."

At the door he paused, rain-wet and lighter than he'd been in months, and made the mistake of looking back. She stood in the candlelight with her father's memory against her chest and her chin up, and she looked—there was no other word for it—*ready.*

"One more thing," she said. "The dragon's keeper. Odile. If the fire gutters in weeks, then whatever we're going to do, we do it soon. I'm going back under the city tomorrow."

"Alone?"

"Obviously."

"Beatrix." He smiled despite himself. "I'll meet you at the foundry at dusk. Bring the map. I'll bring the lies—we'll need a lot of lies."

She grinned, quick and bright, and shut the door on him, and Rafael went down the stairs into the rain feeling like a man who had just signed something enormous in invisible ink.

Behind him, faintly, he heard her voice through the door, talking to someone small and punctual:

"Well, Bishop. I think I've just done something incredibly stupid."

The dog, judging, said nothing.
