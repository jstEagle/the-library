## 13. Into the Undercity

Maren's map was drawn on the back of a debt note, which told Beatrix everything she needed to know about the woman's priorities.

*Old foundry. Third stair past the cistern that isn't a cistern. Count your heartbeats on the drowned steps — if you reach two hundred before the water touches your knees, you've gone too far and should apologize to whatever is following you.*

That last line was very Maren. Twenty years retired from the trade and still unable to write a single instruction without a joke folded into it like a knife in a bread loaf.

Beatrix stood at the mouth of the old foundry district at dusk, hood up, forged papers against her ribs — the ones she had traded her father's memory for, and which now felt less like protection than like a receipt for something she could never buy back. The proclamation had been up for nine days. The city wore its fear the way it wore soot: everywhere, ground in, mostly ignored until you looked closely.

She counted her heartbeats.

The cistern-that-was-not-a-cistern turned out to be exactly what Maren promised: a round stone mouth that looked for all the world like an ordinary well except that it breathed. Warm air rose out of it in slow pulses, smelling of iron and hot stone and something older underneath, something like a forest fire remembered by the rocks themselves. The third stair was hidden behind a slab of masonry that swung when she pressed her palm flat against a carved mark — a spiral, worn nearly smooth by centuries of hands.

"Count," she whispered to herself, and went down into the dark.

* * *

The stairs were cut by no guild she knew. They were too wide for smugglers and too smooth for miners, each step curving slightly inward as though the whole staircase had been grown rather than built. Water ran somewhere below, patient as a bookkeeper. By heartbeat forty her boots found shallow water; by heartbeat ninety it lapped at her ankles, warm as bathwater, and she stopped counting because Maren's arithmetic had clearly been designed to frighten rather than inform.

The dark down there was not empty. It hummed.

At first Beatrix thought the sound was water moving through old pipes. Then she thought it was machinery, some vast abandoned engine still turning in its sleep. Then, standing ankle-deep in a flooded gallery where the ceiling opened into vaults tall as cathedral naves, she understood what she was hearing, and every hair on her arms lifted.

It was breathing. Slow, tidal, enormous — one breath lasting perhaps a minute, drawing the warm air past her face toward the deep places and then exhaling it back again with a faint smell of cinders and rain.

Something alive slept beneath her city. Something the size of the city itself.

"Maren," she said aloud, to nobody, "you absolute horror. You never thought to mention this."

The map, damp and curling in her pocket, offered no reply.

* * *

The keeper found her before she found anything worth finding. Beatrix heard the small sounds first — a tin cup set down on stone, the tap of a stick — and turned to see a lantern approaching through the flooded hall, held low, lighting nothing above the knee of the woman who carried it.

"You count loud," the woman said. Her voice was dry and amused, like paper being folded. "In your head, I mean. Most people who count their steps down here do it out loud the first time. You did it inside, all tight and careful, like balancing a column of numbers." She smiled. "A ledger-keeper. How nice. We don't get many."

"How could you possibly—"

"The echo, dear. Everything echoes down here. Even thoughts, if you carry them loudly enough." She raised the lantern a little, revealing a broad, weathered face and eyes gone white as milk. She was perhaps sixty, wrapped in layered wool despite the warmth, and she moved over the flooded stones without hesitation, her stick reading the water like a finger running down a page. "I'm Odile. You're lost, frightened, and carrying something you're ashamed of. In that order or none."

Beatrix's hand went instinctively to the satchel where Maren's map lay — and where, folded behind it, the salvaged ledger page lay with its half-burned record of the Emberbound. Odile's blind gaze tracked the movement anyway.

"I'm here about the dragon," Beatrix said.

"Yes." Odile turned and began walking, stick tapping, lantern swaying. "They usually are, eventually. Everyone comes down here eventually. Come along. The wet part gets deeper, so hold my elbow and try not to narrate your anxiety. It echoes."

* * *

The drowned vaults opened, gallery by gallery, into spaces that made no architectural sense — ceilings supported by pillars carved in the likeness of wings, floors of black stone veined with dull red light that pulsed in time with that vast breathing. Beatrix walked through a hall where the walls were scored by claw-marks taller than three men, and another where something long ago had melted the stone into smooth glassy rivers. Warm water rose to her shins, then her knees. Fish — pale, eyeless, unbothered — drifted around her legs.

"What is this place?" she asked.

"His bed." Odile said it the way you'd say *his study*. "The foundry was built on top of him, back when men and dragons still spoke. The founders dug their furnaces down close to his warmth — free heat, they thought themselves clever — and he let them, because in those days he liked the company. Smithsong, he called it. He said human hammering sounded like birdsong, only more ambitious." Her mouth twisted. "Then the crown happened. And after the crown, the wars. And after the wars, everyone who remembered how to talk to him was dead or fled or hunted, and he came down here and pulled the dark over himself like a blanket and has not properly woken since."

"The Emberbound," Beatrix said quietly. "The crown is fed on dragon-fire."

Odile stopped walking.

The breathing filled the silence — in, slow, out, slower — and in the red-veined darkness Beatrix felt suddenly very small and very observed, as though the entire sleeping mountain of the world had shifted its attention by a fraction.

"Who told you that name?" Odile asked, and all the fold-of-paper amusement was gone from her voice.

"A page. Half-burned. From the palace archives, before the Hollow burned its records." Beatrix hesitated, then took the page out and held it toward the blind woman, remembering too late that blindness didn't work that way. But Odile took it anyway, and read it — Beatrix would swear afterward that she read it — passing her fingertips across the charred edges while the words seemed to arrive in her some other way.

"Vaskarath's fire, bound in gold and oath-breaking," Odile murmured. "Yes. That's the truth of it, or near enough. Do you know what the Emberbound actually is, girl? Not what the histories say — a symbol of kingship, a relic of the founding. What it *is*?"

"A vessel. A cage for a will."

"It's a broken promise," Odile said, "that learned to eat." She handed the page back. "When the first pact between dragons and men was struck, Vaskarath gave a portion of his own fire freely — a gift, sealed in oath. Men took that gift and hammered it into a circlet, and swore the oath again over it, and then broke the oath within a generation. Fire given freely does not die when it is betrayed. It *hungers*. It has been hungry for four hundred years, and it feeds the only way betrayed things can: it takes. Minds. Wills. Kings. Your King Aldric is merely the latest meal. He is also, I am sorry to say, nearly finished."

The words landed in Beatrix's chest like cold coins. She thought of the dying noblewoman's memory — young Aldric flinching from his own crown — and of the dream she had stolen and kept, against her own oldest rule. The king's dream, with its corridors that lengthened and a voice like banked coals saying *stay, stay, stay.*

"He signs edicts he doesn't remember signing," she said.

"He signs them in his sleep. Or rather, the crown signs them wearing his hand. The hunt for the gifted—" Odile's lip curled, "—that is the crown feeding itself. Every gifted mind taken is a rich meal. You people are *seasoning*, dear. The rest of the court is bread."

Beatrix stood in the warm dark, water rising around her calves, and felt the shape of the thing she had come to ask press against her teeth. She almost couldn't say it. Saying it would make it real, and real things required decisions, and decisions were the one trade she had never been good at.

"Destroying it," she managed. "Is there a way?"

Odile was quiet for a long moment. Above them, far away, the city went on with its evening — carts, bells, someone arguing cheerfully about the price of eels — all of it resting on the back of this breathing dark like passengers asleep on a ship.

"There is exactly one way," Odile said at last. "Living dragon-fire. Fire freely given can unmake fire wrongly taken. That is the old law, and laws like that do not bend." She began walking again, slower now, and Beatrix followed. "And there is exactly one living source left."

The gallery ended at a wall of black glass, and beyond it, seen through the translucent stone as through smoked ice, was the dragon.

* * *

No description Beatrix had ever written prepared her for the act of simply standing in front of one.

Vaskarath filled a cavern the size of the Gilded Hollow's grand hall the way a hand fills a glove. He lay curled, wings folded like a cathedral deciding to be patient, scales the color of cooling embers — and they *were*, literally, cooling embers, dull orange light guttering in the seams between them, brightening faintly with each breath and dimming again. He was so large that Beatrix's eyes refused at first to assemble him into a single creature; she saw a landscape, a hillside, a range of smoldering hills, before her mind reluctantly conceded it was all one sleeping animal, older than the Spire, older than the kingdom, older probably than the idea of kingdoms.

He was dimming. She could see it the way you can see a candle nearing its end — not yet dark, but *shorter*. Each pulse of ember-light came a little weaker than the last.

"He's dying?" she whispered.

"He's *starving*," Odile corrected. "There's a difference, and it matters. A starving thing can be fed. A dying thing can only be mourned." The keeper settled onto a dry ledge with the ease of long habit, setting her lantern down and her stick across her knees. "Dragon-fire renews itself, given time and food. His food is wonder, if you want the honest answer. Stories. Songs. Pilgrims coming to stand where you're standing with their mouths open. For four hundred years there's been no one to feed him but me, and I'm a poor meal — I've known him too long to wonder at him." She patted the ledge beside her. "Sit. You'll want to sit for what comes next."

Beatrix sat. The black glass was warm under her palms, faintly pulsing.

"Weeks," Odile said. "Not years. I've kept his ledgers, if you like — a fellow bookkeeper ought to appreciate the figures. Forty years ago, his fire burned steady as a hearth. Ten years ago it began to gutter. This spring, the intervals between breaths started stretching. When the last ember goes out, there will be no force in this world that can unmake the Emberbound. The crown will go on eating kings until it has eaten the kingdom, and then it will start on the neighbors." She tilted her head. "So. You came all this way, spent whatever it cost you to get here, and asked your question standing up like a fool. Ask it sitting down."

Beatrix watched the ember-light breathe. Brighten. Dim.

"How much fire does it take?" she asked. "To destroy it."

"All of it." Odile said it gently, the way you deliver a diagnosis. "Every spark he has left. It is not a candle applied to a fuse, dear. It is the last dragon giving the last of himself to burn away the first betrayal. There is no rationing. There is no *some*. There is only everything." She paused. "And there is a second cost, which I suspect is why you really came, and why you're holding that satchel like a shield."

Beatrix's fingers had gone white on the strap.

"Fire freely given requires a bearer willing to receive the burden," Odile continued. "Someone must hold the crown while it burns. Take its hunger into themselves, even for a moment — a moment is enough to kill most people, and the ones it doesn't kill, it empties. Whatever gift they carried, whatever stolen things they hoarded, whatever private magics made them *them* — the fire takes those too, as payment. The crown dies. And the one who holds it wakes up ordinary."

The word landed strangely. *Ordinary.* Beatrix had spent her whole life being ordinary on purpose — invisible, precise, a woman whose talent was pretending she had none. And yet the prospect of having it *taken*, rather than hidden, sat in her chest like a swallowed stone.

"And if no one volunteers?" she asked.

"Then in a few weeks the ember goes out on its own, and the question resolves itself," Odile said. "Badly."

Above them the city bells rang the hour, thin and distant, a sound from another century. Vaskarath breathed. The ember-light rose and fell, rose and fell, and Beatrix found herself counting the interval without meaning to — an old habit, a ledger-keeper's tic — and hating herself a little for converting wonder into arithmetic even now.

Forty seconds between breaths. Maren would have made a joke about it. Beatrix couldn't find one.

"Why tell me all this?" she asked finally. "You don't know me. I could be Veyra's creature. I could be anyone."

"I know exactly who you are." Odile picked up her lantern and smiled, and for a moment, in the ember-light, her blind eyes seemed almost to have a color again. "You keep other people's memories for money and you hate yourself for it. You broke your own rule recently and kept something that wasn't yours, and it's been burning a hole in you ever since — I can hear it echoing. And you love someone you haven't told, because telling him would make you responsible for what happens to him." She rose, stick tapping. "Which means you're exactly the kind of fool who comes back. They always come back. Go home, ledger-keeper. Count your days as carefully as you count your steps."

Beatrix climbed the long wet stairs alone, the map useless in her pocket now, the way forward and the way back both lit only by what she'd learned.

At the top, emerging into the sooty dusk of the foundry district, she stopped. Nine days since the proclamation. Weeks, Odile had said. Perhaps fewer. Somewhere overhead, a crier was already calling the evening news — registrations, seizures, a name list posted at Tallowbridge.

She started walking, fast, toward the Hollow — toward Corvane, toward Rafael, toward every impossible conversation she'd been postponing — doing sums in her head the whole way, and for once the columns would not balance.
