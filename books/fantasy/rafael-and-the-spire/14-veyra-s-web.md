## 14. Veyra's Web

Lord Chancellor Veyra had a theory about dust.

Most people believed dust was an enemy — something to be swept, banished, warred against with scented oils and servants' brooms. Veyra knew better. Dust was simply the world forgetting itself, one grain at a time. It settled on the ledgers of the Chancellery the way it settled on everything: patiently, indifferently, proof that nothing stayed remembered unless someone made it so.

She was the someone.

She sat in her office above the Hall of Records as evening came on, and she did not light the lamps. She liked the dusk. It reminded her of the work. In the dark, the city below resolved into its true shape: not a sprawl of rooftops and harbor lights but a great disordered mind, full of quarrels and grudges and griefs nobody had filed properly. Forty years ago she had looked at that mind and asked the question no one else had been brave enough to ask.

*Who is keeping track?*

The answer, for most of her life, had been *no one*. And so the city forgot its debts and repeated its famines and crowned its kings and buried its gifted children in unmarked graves, and every generation swore things would be different, and every generation forgot to remember that they'd sworn it.

Veyra touched the edge of her desk, where a single sheet of paper lay under a paperweight of black glass. On it, in her own small, immaculate hand, were eleven names. Eleven gifted persons currently unaccounted for since the proclamation. She had written the list herself because she trusted no clerk with it, and because writing things down was a kind of prayer, and prayer was the only religion she had ever practiced.

One of the names was Rafael Dain.

* * *

"You're working late again, my lady." Her steward, Pellam, stood in the doorway with a tray: bread, cheese, a small pot of honey, tea going cold in its pot because he always brought it too early and she always drank it too late. He had been bringing her supper for nineteen years and had never once learned her timing, which she privately considered a small miracle of human stubbornness. A machine would have corrected itself by now. Pellam never did.

"I'm always working late, Pellam."

"Yes, my lady. The honey's from the south market. The beekeeper says it's a good year. Whatever that means."

"It means the bees found enough flowers and no one burned their meadow. That is what a good year means, Pellam. It has always meant that."

He nodded as though this were wisdom rather than arithmetic, set down the tray, and paused. He was a round man with the face of a friendly ox and the eyes of someone who had seen more than he let on, which was why she kept him. "There's a young woman downstairs," he said. "Inquisitor's office. Says she has papers for you. Wouldn't give them to anyone but you personally."

"Name?"

"Wouldn't say that either. Pretty thing. Nervous. Kept looking at the doors like they might bite her."

"Show her up. Then go home, Pellam. Eat something that isn't standing over a stove."

"You first," he said, and went to fetch the girl, and Veyra allowed herself the ghost of a smile. Everyone assumed that a woman who could do what she did must be surrounded by fear. In truth she was surrounded mostly by people who brought her honey and told her to eat.

The girl who came up the stairs was not pretty so much as *arranged* to look pretty — hair pinned just so, cloak brushed clean, hands folded like a good daughter's. Veyra knew the arrangement when she saw it. She had invented half its patterns.

"My lady Chancellor." The girl bowed. "I was sent to deliver these." She produced a slim folio sealed with the inquisitors' white wax. "From the Third Office. Regarding the registration lists."

Veyra took it without hurry. "And your name?"

"Lissa, my lady."

"And who sent you, Lissa?"

A flicker. Barely a heartbeat of hesitation — the pause of someone checking a rehearsed answer against a real one. "Under-Secretary Ombre, my lady."

Ombre had been dead for two years. Veyra set the folio down gently, the way one sets down a sleeping cat that may yet wake cross. "Lissa," she said kindly, "when you are next given a cover story, ask your handler whether the person named in it still draws breath. Dead men make poor alibis. They never confirm anything."

The color left the girl's face in a slow tide. To her credit, she didn't run. She stood very straight and said, in a voice that shook only a little, "Then I'll tell them the story failed."

"Do that. And tell whoever sent you—" Veyra opened the folio, glanced at its contents, and felt the evening tilt, very slightly, on its axis. "—tell them I already know about the sister."

* * *

The folio contained a page torn from a salvaged guild register. Beatrix Ravensworth's handwriting — Veyra recognized it from twenty years of files, that precise, upright script, each ledger line ruled as though drawn against a conscience. The page listed three trades: a memory of a wedding, a memory of a debt forgiven, and a memory catalogued only as *"the king, c. 20 yrs past — provenance: House Maren."*

The king. Twenty years past.

Someone in the Hollow had sold — or stolen — a piece of Aldric's younger mind, and a gifted archivist had held it long enough to write it in a ledger, and that ledger had survived Corvane's bonfires by exactly one page, and that page had come to her through channels she hadn't built, which meant someone else was building channels of their own.

And beneath the register page, in a different hand — a clerk's hand, careful, slightly left-slanted, a hand she had read on a thousand reports — someone had noted the archive's location and the seal used to store it.

Rafael's hand.

Veyra sat for a long time in the darkening room, turning the page in the lamplight she finally permitted herself, and thought about Rafael Dain the way a jeweler thinks about a stone with a flaw in it: not with anger, but with appraisal.

She had picked him himself, years ago. Orphaned, brilliant, incorruptible in all the ordinary ways — he couldn't be bought, flattered, frightened, or promoted into compliance. She had valued him precisely for that. An honest blade cuts cleanest. She had never imagined she would need to worry about honesty itself becoming the corruption — the moment when a man's integrity turned in his grip like a knife and pointed back up the chain of command.

But the signs were there, if one read them like ledgers. His reports on the Hollow inspections had grown thinner, more careful, the prose of a man choosing his words the way a man walks on ice. He had requested transfers twice and withdrawn both requests within a day, which meant someone had talked him out of it or he had talked himself out of it, and either way, something anchored him in this city that he would not name. He had asked, once, casually, terribly casually, what the procedure was for a registered citizen whose family petitioned on their behalf.

And now his handwriting sat on a page about the king's stolen memory, next to the name of a guild archivist whose file Veyra had opened and re-opened for twenty years without ever finding enough to close.

She poured her tea, cold, and drank it anyway. Pellam would have approved of the thrift and despaired of everything else.

*You love her,* she thought, with the mild wonder of a naturalist observing a rare and doomed bird. *Of course you do. You always did fall for the ones who could take things out of your head. You've spent your whole life carrying other people's secrets; along comes a woman whose entire trade is carrying them better.*

It was almost touching. It was certainly useful.

Because here was the truth that Veyra had carried alone for forty years, the truth that made her lie awake some nights not with guilt but with a terrible, clarifying loneliness: the crown had chosen her. Not the king. *Her.*

* * *

It had happened in her thirty-first year, when she was a junior records-keeper with ink-stained cuffs and a head full of other people's forgotten debts. She had been summoned to the Spire to audit the royal confessional ledgers — dull work, tithes and pardons — and she had been left alone for one hour in the antechamber of the throne room, and the crown had spoken to her.

Not aloud. Nothing so vulgar. It had simply been *there*, in her mind, the way a temperature is there, a pressure, a certainty. It had shown her what it saw: centuries of kings, each one a little less than the last, each one eaten a little further, and behind them the same endless churn — a kingdom that forgot its own cruelties and therefore committed them again, and again, and again. It had shown her the harvests. The minds of the gifted, taken and stored, whole libraries of human feeling locked in vaults while the city above starved for exactly what those vaults held.

And it had said — not in words, in the way a key says *turn* — *you could keep the accounts. All of them. No more forgetting. No more repeating. Order, if someone is willing to do the bookkeeping.*

Every soul who had ever worn the crown had heard that offer and answered it by putting the crown on. That was the trap, Veyra understood now: the artifact did not want a wearer. It wanted a *regent*. Someone who would rule in its name without needing to sit under its weight. Someone who could feed it steadily, quietly, year after year, and believe — truly believe — that she was saving the world.

She had said yes. She had been thirty-one and she had said yes, and she had never once regretted it, and she wanted that recorded somewhere, in whatever ledger exists for such things: *she never once regretted it.*

Because she had seen the alternative. She had spent her career reading the alternative. The alternative was famine ledgers and witch-burnings and gifted children drowned in wells by neighbors they'd healed. The alternative was a hundred little cruelties compounding like unpaid interest until the whole kingdom went bankrupt in blood. The harvest was not cruelty. The harvest was *consolidation*. Every mind Veyra fed to the Emberbound was a mind removed from the lottery of mob fear and placed in trust — held safe, ordered, accounted for — until such time as the kingdom was stable enough to deserve them back.

That day would come. She believed it the way farmers believe in spring. The crown had promised her: order first, then restoration. A kingdom that remembered itself could afford mercy. A kingdom that forgot could only afford panic, and panic was what had ruled this city for four hundred years.

So when Aldric had begun, decades ago, to fray — when the crown's hunger had grown past what even a willing king could channel — Veyra had done the arithmetic no one else could bear to do. The hunt was necessary. Registration, then treatment, then harvest: not vengeance, but triage. The gifted were not being punished. They were being *filed*. And someday — she genuinely believed this, she clung to it the way other people cling to gods — someday the vaults would open and everything taken would be returned, with interest, to a city finally wise enough to hold it.

That the plan required her to feed frightened families into the fire like coal into a furnace — yes. She knew. She had written every name herself. She kept the lists in her own hand because someone ought to know, really know, what each entry cost, and she refused to let clerks carry that weight for her. It was the closest thing she had to penance, and she performed it nightly, and it did not slow her down at all.

*Order,* the crown murmured, from wherever crowns murmur from — the vault beneath the Spire, the binding-ceremony chamber, the deep places where harvested minds hummed like a hive. *Soon. The solstice. One hundred minds and the fire renewed, and then the long quiet, and then the return.*

"Soon," Veyra agreed aloud, to an empty room, and did not feel foolish. She had been talking to the crown for forty years. It was the longest conversation of her life, and the only one that had never lied to her — though she was honest enough, in her loneliest hour, to admit that this might be because she had never dared ask it a question it would have to answer badly.

* * *

The trap assembled itself in her mind with the satisfying click of a well-made lock.

She did not want Rafael dead. That would be wasteful, and worse, it would be loud — an inquisitor's treason trial would raise questions she preferred unanswered, and martyrs were so untidy. What she wanted was simpler: she wanted him *tested*, and she wanted the test administered before the solstice, when there would still be time to remove him from the board if he failed.

And she had, courtesy of Lissa and her dead man's alibi, just learned two useful things. First, that someone in the underworld was moving information about the Hollow's surviving archives — which meant the Ravensworth woman was active, and hunting the same truths Veyra was guarding. Second, that Rafael's sister, Sera, was the loose thread hanging off his sleeve, visible to anyone who cared to pull.

Sera Dain lived in Tallowbridge, in a narrow house with blue shutters, and taught children their letters, and had once — this was in the old files, from a lifetime ago — been briefly suspected of a furtive gift herself, before the suspicion died for lack of evidence. Whether the gift was real hardly mattered. Suspicion was a currency, and Veyra had never in her life been short of funds.

The design was elegant. A report would reach the Third Office — anonymous, credible, detailed — placing a gathering of unregistered gifted persons at a safehouse near Tallowbridge on a particular night. The raid order would route, by pure administrative chance, to Rafael. If he warned them — and a man in love finds ways to warn, a coded ledger line, a delayed dispatch, a conveniently broken axle — the warning would be traceable. If he did not warn them, and the raid caught innocents, his conscience would begin to rot where Veyra needed it to rot: in doubt, in guilt, in the slow realization that the law he served required him to break the people he loved. Either way, by solstice she would know exactly what she had. A loyal blade, or a compromised one with a leash already fitted.

And if Sera were taken in the net — well. Collateral. The word had a clean sound to it, like a coin dropped on marble. Veyra had learned long ago that the difference between monsters and stewards was only a matter of bookkeeping. Monsters spent lives. Stewards *allocated* them.

She told herself this, and it was nearly true, and the part that wasn't true she filed away in the deepest drawer of herself, the drawer she did not open, the drawer where a thirty-one-year-old girl with ink-stained cuffs still sometimes knocked and asked, very quietly, *are we the good ones, Lyn?*

Veyra did not answer that knock. She had stopped answering it twenty years ago. The work was too large and the hour too late and the honey, in any case, was going to waste.

* * *

Near midnight, she wrote three documents.

The first was the false report, in a clerk's hand she had practiced until it fooled the Third Office's own archivists. The second was the raid order, routing the operation to Inquisitor Rafael Dain with a note in her own script: *Handle personally. Discretion expected.* The third was a letter she did not send — she never sent it — addressed to no one, beginning *If I am wrong about all of this* and ending, after four pages, unfinished, as it always ended, mid-sentence, mid-thought, mid-prayer. She locked it in the black-glass paperweight's drawer with its forty siblings. Someday, perhaps, someone would find them and understand her entirely, or condemn her entirely, and she honestly did not know which she would prefer, which was, she supposed, the only honest sentence she had ever written.

Then she stood at her window and looked down at the city, at the harbor lights and the dark bulk of the Spire, and she spoke to the crown the way one speaks to an old colleague across a long table.

"He loves her," she said. "Which makes him either our greatest liability or our finest instrument. We will know soon. And she — the Ravensworth woman — she's close to something. The king's memory. Perhaps the binding itself. Let her run, for now. Let her lead us to whatever's left of the dragon-fire, because if there is any of it left anywhere in this world, I want it found and counted and *filed* before she can put a match to forty years of work."

Far below, in the deep places, something vast and patient turned over in its sleep of stored minds, and the pressure in the room warmed by a degree, the way a house warms when the banked fire breathes.

Veyra took that for agreement. She had always taken it for agreement.

On her desk, the list of eleven names waited. She dipped her pen and added a twelfth — *S. Dain (probable)* — in her small, immaculate hand, and blotted it, and felt the familiar settling in her chest that she had long ago decided was conscience doing its work and had never once examined closely enough to learn its real name.

Below her window, in the dark, a courier was already saddling a horse toward Tallowbridge, carrying a lie wrapped in a duty wrapped in a love, and every layer of it was hers.

*Let them come to the solstice,* she thought. *Let them bring their dragon-fire and their clever thief and their honest inquisitor. I have been keeping the accounts for forty years. Let us see what their rebellion costs — and who, in the end, can pay.*
