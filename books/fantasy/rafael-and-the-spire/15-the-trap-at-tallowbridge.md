## 15. The Trap at Tallowbridge

The order came at dawn, written in Veyra's own hand, which meant it could not be misread and could not be forgiven.

*Safehouse at Tallowbridge. Three gifted in residence, one of them believed to be the Ravensworth woman. Take them alive. The Chancellor wants them talking by nightfall.*

Rafael read the letter three times while his tea went cold, because reading it a fourth time would not change what it said, and because his hands needed something to do that wasn't shaking.

She was meant to be there. He had told her—no. He had *not* told her. That was the whole architecture of the last two weeks: he had never told her anything, not once, not in words. He had left a ledger entry in the seized Hollow records where a clerk's error might plausibly have occurred, an entry for "B. Ravenhill, milliner, Tallowbridge ward," dated three days past, marked *pending collection.* Anyone reading it would see nothing. Beatrix would see everything, because she had taught him—years ago, in another life, over stolen wine and her terrible laughter—the guild's oldest cipher, the one hidden in dates and names of trades. A milliner in Tallowbridge. *A hat-maker near the bridge.* She would know.

He had to believe she would know.

He dressed in full white. The uniform felt heavier every morning, as though someone were embroidering it fresh each night with lead thread. In the yard, twelve inquisitors waited with their grey cloaks folded over their arms, and among them stood Sergeant Hale, who was young, earnest, and had once asked Rafael whether the gifted felt pain like ordinary people. Rafael had answered, *more so,* and Hale had laughed, thinking it a joke.

"Bridge district," Rafael said. "Quiet approach. We want them calm when we come through the door."

"And if they run?" Hale asked.

"They won't." He heard his own voice go flat and official, the voice of a man he did not recognize in the mirror some mornings. "They'll be mid-morning. Tea, probably."

It was, God help him, nearly true.

* * *

Tallowbridge earned its name from the chandlers who'd worked its banks for two hundred years; the whole street smelled of tallow and honey and old smoke, a smell that on any other morning Rafael might have found homely. Today it smelled like a trap he was walking into from both ends.

The safehouse was a candle-shop above a candle-shop, which struck him as either very clever or very lazy. Its front window displayed beeswax tapers arranged in a fan. Through the glass, a woman moved behind the counter—a broad-shouldered woman with grey in her braid, hands like a dockworker's.

Not Beatrix. His chest did something complicated with that—not relief, exactly, because relief would mean he'd wanted her caught, and something worse than relief too, because if she wasn't here then either his warning had worked or she had never been here at all, and he couldn't bear to learn which.

"Positions," he murmured. "Front door, back stair, the alley. I take the shop."

Hale frowned. "Sir, protocol says—"

"Protocol says the ranking officer takes first contact, Sergeant, so that if our quarry is dangerous, we lose the most expensive man instead of you." He smiled, and hated how easily the smile came. Years of practice. "Go."

They went. Rafael counted to thirty, adjusted his collar, and pushed open the bell-door of the candle shop.

The woman looked up. Her eyes went to his white coat, and Rafael watched her do the arithmetic—watched her arrive at the sum, watched her decide not to scream. That took courage of a specific, quiet kind, and he made a note to remember her face for later, for whatever later turned out to be.

"Inquisitor," she said. "We're out of memorial candles, if that's what you're after."

"I'm after nothing you're selling." He set both hands flat on the counter, palms down, an old signal from another life. *I am not armed in intent.* Whether she read it, he couldn't tell. "How many upstairs?"

Her jaw tightened. "That's none of—"

"How many," he said softly, "because I am about to be obliged to search this premises thoroughly, and I would very much prefer to find fewer people than my orders expect."

Something flickered in her face. Understanding, maybe, or just the animal cunning of the hunted. "Two," she said. "An old man sleeping off a fever, and a boy. Sixteen. He's not—he hasn't done anything, he just *knows things* before they happen, small things, whether rain's coming, whether the bread's burnt—"

"Hale," Rafael called toward the door without turning his head, "the back stair. Now." And then, low, to the woman: "When they come down, no one runs. Running turns a registration into a funeral. Do you understand me?"

She understood him. He saw it land, the awful transactional mercy of it, and he wanted to be sick, and instead he straightened his cuffs.

The old man came down coughing, wrapped in a blanket, furious and terrified in equal measure. The boy came down last, and Rafael's stomach dropped, because the boy was *Tam*—he knew the name from the seized records, Odile's apprentice, the keeper's blind mistress's blind apprentice, a child who fed embers to a sleeping dragon under the city—and Tam looked at Rafael with the flat, ancient calm of someone who had already seen this morning coming.

"You're the one," Tam said. Not a question.

"The one what?" Hale asked.

"The one who writes in ledgers wrong on purpose." The boy shrugged. "I know things. Small things."

Rafael kept his face still through sheer professional discipline. Inside, he was screaming. Of all the houses, all the wards, all the mornings—Veyra's list had named Tallowbridge, and Veyra's lists were never wrong, which meant Veyra had known, which meant—

Which meant the trap had never been for the people in the house.

It had been for whoever warned them.

* * *

They took the three of them in a closed wagon, and Rafael rode on the bench beside the driver with his hands clenched around his knees and his mind moving fast and cold.

Veyra had let him lead the raid. She had given him the address herself, in her own hand. If Beatrix had been inside, he would have been obliged to arrest her in front of twelve witnesses—or die trying not to, and either outcome served the Chancellor. If Beatrix escaped, then his warning had reached her, and his warning was itself evidence, and Veyra would simply wait for him to incriminate himself further. Every branch of the tree bore fruit for her. She hadn't built a trap. She'd built an orchard.

And the boy—she'd taken the boy because the boy belonged to the vaults, to the dragon, to whatever thread connected Beatrix to the drowned places under the city. Veyra didn't want the gifted registered. She wanted the map of everything they loved.

At the Spire gates, the wagon rolled through, and Rafael dismounted, and gave the orders he was required to give, and walked back toward his lodgings with his legs feeling borrowed.

There was one thing left to do, and it was the thing he'd sworn an oath against, and the thing he'd do anyway.

* * *

The seized Hollow records were held in the inquisitors' annex, in a room with iron shelves and a bored clerk named Petch who owed Rafael money from a card game and therefore asked no questions about anything, ever, as a matter of policy.

"I need to verify an entry," Rafael said. "Cross-reference. Give me an hour and your discretion."

"Discretion's extra," Petch said.

"Then consider your debt doubled."

Petch considered, found the arithmetic agreeable, and vanished.

Rafael sat down with the ledger of the Tallowbridge ward and read the entries until his eyes ached, looking for the line he'd planted. *B. Ravenhill, milliner.* Still there. Still pending collection. He dipped his pen.

This was the delicate part. Anything he wrote could be read by Veyra's clerks; anything erased could be reconstructed. But a *correction*—a correction was the most natural act in bureaucracy. Clerks corrected things constantly, compulsively, the way birds preened.

He drew a single line through *milliner* and wrote, in the margin, in his own neat hand: *Erratum — trade misrecorded. Subject is a glover. Premises vacated prior to collection. No further action.*

*A glover.* Glove-maker. In the guild cipher, gloves meant *hands empty, go now, trust no route you've used before.* Vacated premises meant *they know the house is burned.* No further action meant *do not write back.*

It was thin. It was paper-thin. But she had taught him the cipher over a bottle of plum wine with her feet on his chair, laughing at how slow he was, saying, *you think in sentences, Rafe, the ledger thinks in mistakes—you have to learn to make beautiful mistakes*—and he had spent four years since making beautiful mistakes in official documents, and if anyone alive could read this one, it was her.

He blotted the page, shelved the book, and went home to stare at his ceiling and count the ways this could kill them both.

* * *

She came at the hour when even cats give up on the night.

He woke—not to a sound, but to the absence of one, the particular silence of a room that has acquired a second heartbeat—and did not reach for the knife under his pillow, because only one person in the world entered a room like a debt being paid quietly.

"Candle shop," Beatrix said from the corner by the wardrobe, her voice low and rough with exhaustion. "Very funny. A hat-maker who lives above a candle shop. Your cipher's getting sloppy, inquisitor."

"Your escape was sloppy," he whispered back, sitting up. "You're supposed to be in the wind, not standing in my bedchamber where eleven men could hang us both before breakfast."

"Eleven men are asleep. You're awake. I checked." A pause, and then, softer: "Tam."

He heard it in her voice before she finished the word—the crack in it, hairline but deep. "He's in the Spire cells. Registration hold. They haven't begun questioning; Veyra wants him intact until—" He stopped.

"Until she harvests him." Beatrix stepped out of the dark. In the grey light from the window her face was pale and set, and there was blood on her sleeve—not hers, he'd have bet the deed to his soul it wasn't hers—and her eyes had the terrible brightness of someone running on grief and spite in equal measure. "He's sixteen. He knows small things. Rain, and burnt bread, and which doors to knock on. And she's going to pour him out like a bottle because he knows the way to a dragon."

"Yes."

"She took him because of me. Because I went to the vaults, because I asked questions, because I—" Her breath hitched, and she mastered it, the way she mastered everything, visibly, expensively. "I keep a rule, Rafael. Never keep what you steal. Never spend what isn't yours. And I have spent that boy like coin."

"No." He got up, crossed the cold floor, and did the reckless thing, the thing he'd been not-doing for weeks: he took her hands. They were freezing. "You didn't build Veyra's web. You didn't write the proclamation. You've been running since the day the criers opened their mouths, same as everyone else with a gift and a pulse." He squeezed. "But we get him back. Both of us. That's the sentence. We get him back."

For a long moment she didn't move. Then her fingers curled around his, and she laughed—one short, broken, real laugh, the kind she'd never once let him hear across a desk or a doorway in all the years of circling.

"Do you know what you are, Rafael?" she said. "You're an officer of the hunt, falsifying records, harboring a fugitive in your nightshirt, holding hands with the most wanted thief in the city. If Veyra could see you."

"If Veyra could see me, I'd hope she'd note the nightshirt is very dignified."

"It's got a hole in the elbow."

"It's a *dignified* hole."

She laughed again, wetter this time, and pressed her forehead briefly against his shoulder—an anchor dropped, a debt acknowledged—and when she pulled back her eyes were dry and decided, which frightened him more than tears would have.

"Tallowbridge," she said. "Your erratum. Gloves. Empty hands. I read it within the hour; I was already two roofs away when the wagon came." She looked up at him. "You saved my life with a punctuation mark."

"I learned from a very good teacher. Terrible at hats, though. Said so myself, in ink."

"Rafe." Her voice changed, went quiet and level, and he braced, because he knew that voice; it was the voice she used for prices. "What happens to you, when this ends? Whatever way it ends. There's no version of this where you walk back into the Spire and keep the white coat. You know that."

He did know that. He'd known it since the first forged entry, since the first lie of omission, since the morning duty began to rot in his mouth like a tooth. "I know it."

"And you did it anyway."

"I did it anyway."

"Why?"

The question hung there, plain and enormous. He could have deflected—made a joke about her holey influence, cited strategy, cited the boy. Instead he heard himself say, "Because I've spent ten years enforcing laws I watched get written by something that eats kings. And because there's a woman who breaks my rules more elegantly than I ever managed to keep them, and I would rather be ruined alongside her than promoted without her." A breath. "That's the whole answer. It's not even clever. I'm sorry. I know you prefer clever."

Beatrix stared at him. And then she kissed him—quickly, fiercely, like stealing something in a crowded room, like the practiced art she was—except her hand shook against his jaw, which meant it wasn't practiced at all, and that undid him more than the kiss did.

"That," she said, stepping back, composed again except for her eyes, "was also not clever."

"No."

"We should probably never do it again."

"Almost certainly."

They stood in the grey light, grinning at each other like idiots, two liars who had finally run out of lies worth telling, and Rafael thought: *whatever comes, this hour was real, and they can't burn it out of me.*

"There's a cellar," she said, business snapping back over her like a cloak. "Under the ropewalk on Corser Street. Safehouse, unused. Tomorrow after dusk, I'll bring what Maren gave me—the crown's true history, the vaults, all of it. If we're going to get Tam out, we need to know what Veyra wants from him, and I think I already do." Her jaw set. "She doesn't want his gift. She wants what he's seen. The dragon sleeps under the city, Rafael, and dragons don't dream quietly."

"Tomorrow, then." He hesitated. "Beatrix. The cells under the Spire—Veyra's questioners don't use ropes. They use memories. If they start on him before tomorrow—"

"Then he'll hold." She said it like a fact, but he saw the cost of saying it cross her face. "He sent Odile a message once, from three streets away, through a locked door. Small things, remember." A pause. "Pray he sends one more."

She went out the window the way she'd come, silent as a returned debt, and Rafael stood alone in his room with the taste of plum wine that neither of them had drunk, counting the hours until dusk.

He did not sleep. Somewhere beneath the city, a boy who knew small things was sitting in a cell above something vast, and somewhere above the city, a Chancellor was sharpening her patience, and between those two weights hung a single sheet of paper in an iron-shelved room, bearing one beautiful mistake.

Rafael lit a candle, opened his private notebook, and began, in cipher, to plan a burglary of the Spire itself.

It was, he reflected, the least legal thing he'd ever done before breakfast.

He found he didn't mind at all.
