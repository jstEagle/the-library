## 16. Interrogation of the Apprentice

The cellar beneath the Tallowbridge safehouse smelled of wet stone and old tallow, which was appropriate, since it was under a chandler's shop and had been for two hundred years. It was also the safest place in the city, according to Maren's map, and the only place in the city where Beatrix could sit very still without anyone asking why she wasn't doing something useful.

She had been sitting still for three days.

"You're going to wear a groove in that chair," Odile said from her corner. She had come up from the vaults to wait with them — an act of stubbornness she described as "keeping an eye on my investment," though everyone knew better. Her blind eyes were fixed on some middle distance that belonged to no one but herself. "And then I'll have to buy you another chair, and chairs cost money, and money is how Veyra finds people."

"I'm not wearing a groove. I'm thinking."

"Thinking looks different on you. Thinking has more pacing."

Rafael, who had been standing at the top of the stairs listening to the street above through the door's seams, came down two steps and stopped. He'd been doing that all day too — going up, listening, coming back down. The rhythm of it was starting to feel like a heartbeat with someone else's pulse.

"Nothing," he said. "No riders, no criers, no white cloaks."

"That's worse," Beatrix said.

"It's not worse. It's just quiet."

"Quiet is worse. Loud means they've found someone and they're busy. Quiet means they've found someone and they're *not* busy." She heard her own voice climb half an octave and made herself stop. Tam had been in Veyra's custody for three days. Three days of whatever happened in the rooms below the Chancellery, where they kept the harvested minds in their jars like pickled plums. Three days of a sixteen-year-old boy alone with a woman who believed, sincerely and completely, that eating people was a form of salvation.

"He's smart," Rafael said. He said it every few hours, the way other people said prayers. "He taught himself the ledger-codes. You told me that yourself. Smart boys are slow to break."

"Smart boys are also smart enough to know exactly what's coming," Beatrix said, "which is not a mercy, Rafael. That's just arithmetic."

Odile snorted softly. "The two of you argue like an old married couple. It would be charming if it weren't so grim."

Neither of them answered that. There had been no time yet, in the middle of all this running and hiding and treason, to decide what they were to each other beyond allies, and there might never be time, and both of them were pretending this didn't ache. It was one of the things Beatrix liked about him, actually — his talent for pretending. An inquisitor who lied as beautifully as Rafael should have been terrifying. Instead it felt like watching a bird sing in a language only she knew.

She went back to the ledger she was supposed to be studying — Maren's old notes on the palace wards, copied out in her own hand because Maren's handwriting looked like a spider drowning in ink. She read the same line four times. *The throne room is warded against entry, not egress; the crown fears nothing coming out.*

*The crown fears nothing coming out.* Because nothing had ever come out. Because everything that went into it stayed there, forever, eaten.

Somewhere across the city, in a room she couldn't picture because she refused to, Tam was being asked questions. And somewhere inside Tam was the thing he'd seen when he'd been taken — because the apprentice had been taken from Odile's outer chamber, from the anteroom of the vaults themselves, and he had spent weeks carrying water down to a sleeping dragon and listening to a blind woman talk about the Emberbound. If Veyra got all of that out of him—

"They'll have the vault," she said aloud. "If he breaks, they'll have the dragon."

"If he breaks," Odile said, "they'll have a great deal more than that."

* *

It came on the fourth night, at the hour when the chandlers upstairs doused their lamps and the whole street seemed to hold its breath.

Beatrix felt it before she understood it — a pressure behind her eyes like the moment before a sneeze or a storm, a wrongness in the air that made the candle flames lean though there was no draft. She sat up straight in her groove-worn chair. Rafael noticed immediately; he always noticed, and she had stopped finding that unsettling and started finding it necessary.

"What is it?"

"I don't—" She put her fingers to her temples. "Someone's knocking."

"At this hour?"

"Not at the door." She closed her eyes. "In here."

Every gifted person learned the shapes of memory-touch early. A theft felt like a hand slipping into your pocket. A shared memory, freely sent, felt like a letter — a folded, deliberate thing, addressed. This was a letter. It was arriving the way letters arrive in wartime: torn, smudged, carried by whoever could still walk.

*Beatrix.* The thought had a shape she knew, a lopsided, hurried shape, a boy who chewed his words before saying them. *Beatrix, can you hear me. I'm sorry about the mess. I'm holding the door shut as long as I can but she's good, she's so much better than Corvane ever said, she doesn't push, she just waits, and waiting is worse. Listen. Listen fast.*

Beatrix gripped the arms of her chair. Rafael knelt in front of her at once, hands hovering near hers, not touching — he couldn't help, and he knew it, and he stayed anyway, which was its own kind of help.

*I'm sending it now,* Tam said. *I stole it off her. Don't tell Odile I said "off her," she'll be insufferable. While she was questioning me I reached into HER, the way you taught me — remember, the winter in the archive, the exercise with the candle? You said a mind under strain forgets to lock its own doors. Hers is strained. She wants the crown renewed so badly she leaks like a bucket. So I took what she knows. All of it. And now I'm giving it to you, because if I give it away there'll be nothing left in me worth taking.*

"No," Beatrix whispered. "Tam, no, don't—"

*You can't say no to me. You're not actually here.* And there it was, the ghost of his grin, crooked, gap-toothed, entirely unafraid of her. *I'm going to let go now. I'm sorry. Tell my mum the truth. Not magic. The truth. She always liked you better than me anyway, which was fair, because you never set her kitchen on fire.*

Then the door opened, and the memory poured in.

* *

There was no throne room, not at first.

First there was darkness, and cold, and the particular quality of silence that exists underneath a city — stone upon stone upon stone, the weight of a thousand years of building pressing down like a hand on the chest. Beatrix hung inside the memory the way she hung inside all stolen memories: bodiless, invisible, unable to change anything, able only to witness.

She saw torchlight. She saw a younger Veyra — thirty years younger, sharp-faced, dark-haired, walking these deep halls with a lantern in one hand and a document in the other, and behind her walked a king.

Aldric. Young Aldric, twenty-five perhaps, tall and broad-shouldered, with the kind of face that painters loved and poets pretended not to. But even in this memory, even thirty years ago, he flinched. Every few steps his head twitched slightly to the left, toward his own crown, the way a man flinches from a wasp he cannot see. And the crown — the Emberbound, the vessel, the thing Maren had named and Odile had confirmed — sat on his head like a spider on a web, black metal veined with dull red light, drinking.

*Your Majesty needn't come down here,* young Veyra said. *The renewal requires only my presence and the tithe.*

*I want to see them,* the king said. His voice was careful. Flat. A man reading lines written by someone else. *I want to see what I am signing for.*

They came into the chamber, and Beatrix understood why the memory had been worth stealing, why Veyra kept it locked deep and revisited it often — because it was the founding. The first harvest. In the center of the vault stood a basin of black iron, and in the basin lay minds. Not jars, not yet — that refinement came later, along with the ledgers and the clerks and the word *tithe*, which made it sound like taxes instead of theft. Just minds, raw, taken from the first gifted families the crown had registered, and they were screaming. Silently. The way memories scream.

*How many?* the king asked.

*Forty,* Veyra said. *Enough to feed the Emberbound for a decade. Enough to keep the realm orderly, Your Majesty. You've seen what happens when the gifted go unregulated. Memory-thieves in every market. Children selling their mothers' faces for bread. Order requires sacrifice.*

*Whose order?*

Veyra smiled, and even young, even beautiful in the torchlight, the smile had a hinge in it, something mechanical. *Yours, Majesty. Always yours.*

And the crown drank, and the king flinched, and somewhere far away in the dark of the vault a voice that was not Veyra's and not the king's spoke a single word — and the word was the name.

*Emberbound* was what men called it. What it called itself was older, guttural, a name like a tooth breaking: **Vael-karesh**, which meant, in the tongue dragons used before men taught them shame, *the debt that eats the debtor*. It had been forged from a broken promise — Beatrix felt that truth arrive in her chest like a swallowed coal — a promise between dragons and the first kings of men, sealed in fire, broken within a generation. The crown was the broken promise given form. It did not hunger because it was evil. It hungered because it had been *owed*, and no one had ever paid.

The ritual of unbinding unfolded around it like a diagram drawn in firelight:

*A binding-memory anchors the crown to its wearer — one memory, held in the crown itself, of the moment of binding. Steal it, and the bond loosens. But loosened, the crown will feed on anything warm and nearby; therefore the thief must hold the artifact bare-handed while it dies, taking its hunger into her own body, so that the dying fire has nowhere to go but through her. Dragon-fire must burn the vessel while a willing hand holds it. Willing. The crown cannot take what is given; that is its one law, older than its hunger. Given freely, it must accept the hand that offers.*

*Given freely, it must accept.* Beatrix turned the phrase over inside the memory, feeling its weight. It sounded like mercy. It was a trap dressed as mercy — because the only people who would offer such a hand were people who loved something enough to die for it, and the crown had eaten every one of those, for centuries.

The memory shuddered. Veyra's mind was closing around it, reclaiming it, and at the edges Beatrix felt Tam — felt him holding the doors open with both small hands, felt his grip failing, felt him *choosing* to fail, choosing to let the rest of himself go dark so the message would get through clean.

*Tell my mum the truth,* he said again, fainter. *Not magic—*

And then, silence. The kind of silence that has a person missing from the middle of it.

* *

Beatrix opened her eyes. She was on the floor of the cellar. Rafael was holding her, one arm braced behind her shoulders, and Odile stood over them both with a candle, and her face — her blind, unerring face — was already wet.

"She's gone quiet," Rafael said. "The Chancellery. Half the lamps went out an hour ago. I thought it was nothing."

"It wasn't nothing." Beatrix sat up. Her voice came out scraped. "She finished. Whatever she wanted from him, she has it. Or she has nothing left to take." She pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes. "He gave it away first. Everything she needed. He emptied himself out and mailed the contents to me like a — like a parcel."

"Beatrix." Rafael's voice was very gentle, which was how she knew it was bad. "What did he send?"

So she told them. All of it, in order, the way she catalogued everything: the vault beneath the Spire, the first harvest of forty minds, the crown's true name spoken in the dark by a voice that was neither man nor woman, the meaning of that name — *the debt that eats the debtor* — the binding-memory, the unbinding, the willing hand.

When she finished, nobody spoke for a long moment. Upstairs, a chandler's dog barked once at nothing, decided against it, and stopped.

"The willing hand," Rafael said finally. "That's the part you haven't said out loud properly. Say it properly."

"The crown cannot take what is given. Someone has to hold it while it burns, and take its hunger into themselves, and the fire will eat the gift out of them on the way through — and possibly everything else besides." She looked at her own hands, pale in the candlelight, the hands that had picked ten thousand locks and lifted ten thousand memories and never once kept what she stole until the day she kept the king. "Possibly everything else besides."

"No," Rafael said.

"You don't get to say no. It's not your—"

"*No.*" Louder now, cracking. "We'll find another way. We have weeks. Odile, there's another way, tell her there's another way."

Odile lowered herself slowly onto the crate beside Beatrix. For a while the old woman said nothing at all, and the candle flame stood perfectly straight between them.

"There isn't," she said at last. "There's only the question of who. And child—" her sightless face turned toward Beatrix with terrible accuracy, "—don't answer that question tonight. Answer it at dawn. Answers given at midnight are worth half of answers given at sunrise, and this one has to be worth all of it. Freely given means freely given. Even to yourself."

Beatrix laughed — a short, broken sound with no humor in it, though it surprised all three of them. "You know what he said? At the end? He said his mother always liked me better than him, and that it was fair, because he set her kitchen on fire." Her throat closed. "He was sixteen. He taught me to pick locks by breaking into my own strongbox to prove my locks were bad, and I was furious, and he grinned at me like it was a festival day. And I watched a wagon take him and I did nothing, and now he's spent himself like a coin to buy me a map into hell, and the last thing he asked—" 

She stopped. Breathed. Made herself finish, because Tam deserved to have it finished.

"—was for the truth. Not magic. The truth."

Rafael took her hands. His were steady and warm and shaking, somehow, both at once.

"Then we'll spend it well," he said. "Everything he paid. We won't waste a copper of it."

Outside, far to the north, over the district where the Chancellery kept its quiet rooms, a single bell began to toll — not the hour, not any hour, but the slow mourning peal that the city rang for servants of the state who died in service.

Tam had never served the state. He had served a sleeping dragon and a blind woman and a guild that hadn't earned him. But the bell rang anyway, as if the city itself had made a mistake and mourned the right person for the wrong reason, and Beatrix listened to it, and let it hurt, and did not look away.

At dawn, she would answer the question.

But tonight she opened Maren's notes to a fresh page, wrote at the top in her precise, invisible hand — *Vael-karesh. The debt that eats the debtor.* — and beneath it began drafting the plan for the worst theft of her life: the stealing of a king.
