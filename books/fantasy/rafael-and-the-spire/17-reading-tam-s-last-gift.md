## 17. Reading Tam's Last Gift

The safehouse cellar smelled of tallow, damp stone, and old onions, which Beatrix felt was an undignified atmosphere in which to receive a dying boy's final gift.

She said as much, because saying things out loud kept her hands from shaking.

"The onions are not my doing," Rafael said. He had his coat off and folded over a barrel with the particular care of a man who needed something orderly in the world, even if it was only a coat. "The onions belong to Mrs. Halloway, who owns this house and believes I am a customs inspector."

"Are you going to tell her you're an inquisitor?"

"I'm going to tell her whatever keeps her from feeding me to the Crown's men when they come asking." He lit the last of three candles and set it on an upturned crate between them. "Which, given current events, may be soon."

Between the candles, on a square of clean linen Beatrix had laid down like an altar cloth, sat Tam's memory.

It did not look like much. A sliver of amber no longer than her thumb, clouded at its heart, still faintly warm — though that might have been her imagination, or her guilt, which ran hotter than most people's hearths. She had carried it from Tallowbridge wrapped against her skin, because some part of her had decided that if Veyra's hounds came for it, they would have to take it out of her ribs first.

"You're certain it will work?" Rafael asked. "A memory without its owner?"

"Memories keep," she said. "That's the whole horror of the trade. A face can rot, a letter can burn, but a memory properly bottled sits patient as a debt." She turned the sliver over. The light moved inside it, slow as honey. "Tam sent it hard and fast, the way you'd throw a rope to someone drowning. It'll be rough. Unedited. He didn't have time to be polite about what he saw."

Rafael sat down across the crate from her, close enough that their knees nearly touched, and did not quite look at the amber. "And we'll both be inside it? At once?"

"That's the idea."

"Have you ever done that before? Two people in one memory?"

"No."

"Wonderful," he said, in the tone of a man discovering the bridge he is standing on is made of rope and optimism. "I've stormed buildings with less planning."

"Yes, but storming buildings only requires courage. This requires me." She smiled, and found it came out crooked. "Courage is common. Precision is rare."

He looked at her then — really looked, the way he had been carefully not looking since the night he'd returned her father's memory and neither of them had named what passed between them over that small stolen thing. Candlelight did him no favors; it showed every hour he hadn't slept since Tallowbridge, since the raid he'd warned her away from, since Odile's apprentice had been taken in her place.

"He was fourteen," Rafael said quietly. It wasn't a question. They had established this already, twice, each of them needing to say it aloud as though repetition might make it untrue.

"Fifteen in spring. He told everyone sixteen because he wanted to be taken seriously by the lockpickers." Beatrix's voice held steady through sheer professional discipline. "He taught me a trick for tumblers with a false gate. You listen for the click that lies."

"And now he's lying in a cell under the Spire while Veyra picks *him*."

The words dropped into the cellar like a stone into a well. Beatrix reached for the amber rather than answer, because there was nothing honest to say, and she had spent her whole life being excellent at dishonesty and was newly, painfully bad at it with this man.

"Rules," she said instead, briskly, because rules were a kind of armor too. "Whatever you see in there, you stay behind your own eyes. Don't reach for anything. Don't touch anything. Memories are houses — you're a guest, not a burglar. If it starts pulling at you, if you feel yourself wanting to *stay*, you say my name. Out loud. Your mouth will still work."

"And if it doesn't?"

"Then I'll drag you out by the collar, and you'll owe me an apology and a drink."

"A drink," he repeated. "You drive a hard bargain, Ravensworth."

"I'm told it's why the guild fears me."

She set her palms flat on either side of the amber sliver. Rafael hesitated a moment longer — she watched him gather himself the way he gathered himself before doors he expected to be locked — and then he placed his hands beside hers on the linen, close enough that his smallest finger rested against hers, whether by accident or design she chose not to audit.

"On three," she said. "One. Two—"

She went on one, because precision included knowing that Rafael would hesitate on three, and hesitation cost seconds, and somewhere beneath the city a fifteen-year-old boy was spending his.

* * *

The memory took them like cold water.

First: darkness, and the smell of iron and wet straw. Then light, arriving all wrong — too high, too white, the light of the throne room antechamber where tithe-clerks processed harvested minds, except seen from below, from a cage-cart, from the height of someone kneeling.

*They put me on my knees,* Tam's voice said, everywhere and nowhere, young and trying very hard to be steady. *I want you to know I didn't tell them anything. I want you to know that first, before everything else.*

Beatrix felt Rafael flinch beside her — a presence at the edge of her awareness, a second heartbeat in the dark, honoring her rule and staying behind his own eyes even as the memory closed around them both.

*I bit him,* Tam continued, with a flicker of fierce pride. *The big one. He'll have a scar. Good.*

The scene assembled itself in pieces, the way raw memories do — gaps where fear had chewed the record thin, moments burned too bright to see past. The antechamber. The great door to the throne room, banded in black iron, breathing. Yes — *breathing*. The door rose and fell, subtly, slowly, like the flank of a sleeping animal, and Tam's memory knew why, and offered the knowledge up like a child offering a wounded bird.

*The crown has a name.* The memory leaned closer, urgent, straining. *Veyra said it when she thought I was already half-gone. She stood over me with her hands full of my head and she said it like a prayer. The Emberbound. That's what it calls itself. That's what it made her call it.*

*Emberbound,* Beatrix thought, and the word rang through Maren's old stories like a bell through fog — the vessel fed on dragon-fire, the hunger wearing gold.

Then the memory turned, and Beatrix wished it hadn't, and made herself watch anyway.

Veyra, seen from below, from a boy's eye level. Not the composed Chancellor of public audiences but something older and more devout, her silver hair loose, her sleeves rolled as though for surgery. Her hands in Tam's hair. Her voice gentle, which was the worst part, gentler than cruelty.

*You have such tidy little memories,* the memory-Veyra murmured. *Such well-kept shelves. You'll forgive the mess.*

And then — because Tam had been brave in exactly the way Beatrix had taught her guild children to be brave, quietly, in the space between one heartbeat and the next — he had reached back through Veyra's harvest and *taken something*. Stolen, in the heart of the enemy's house, with her fingers inside his mind. A page torn from the book she thought she was reading.

*Here,* Tam's voice said, and the memory split open like a door kicked inward. *This is what you actually need. I heard it. I stole it. Steal it from me.*

The throne room. Full daylight pouring through windows of colored glass, and King Aldric on the throne — no. Not on it. *In* it, the way a hand is in a glove, slumped and distant, his eyes fixed on nothing while the crown upon his head drank. And around the throne, chalked on the floor in lines that hurt to look at, a diagram: the binding-ceremony, the renewal rite, and at its center a truth written in Veyra's own ceremonial hand, read aloud by her own recorded voice to a court of bowed heads:

*The Emberbound holds the king as the king holds it. One will anchors the other. To free the king, the anchor must be drawn — and the anchor must be held by a living hand, a gifted hand, willing, while the fire that made the Emberbound unmakes it. The holder takes into herself all the crown has eaten. All of it. Every king. Every year. Every hunger.*

*A gift freely given cannot be refused. A burden freely taken cannot be set down.*

And then, quieter, in Veyra's dry administrative voice, the footnote that made Beatrix's blood go cold:

*No holder has survived sane. The Emberbound was designed so.*

The memory shuddered. Time in it was failing — Tam was failing, spending his last strength holding this door open for her. *Do you see?* his voice said, thinner now, fraying at the edges. *Someone has to hold it. Someone gifted. Someone who chooses. And Beatrix — I know you're listening, I know how you listen —*

A pause. In it, absurdly, terribly, she could feel him deciding whether to say the next thing, could feel the ghost of his grin, the one that meant trouble.

*Don't let the inquisitor talk you out of it. And don't let him do it either. He'd be terrible at it. He alphabetizes things.*

Laughter — his, brief and real, echoing in a cell under the world —

—and the memory ended, the way a candle ends, all at once and then smoking.

* * *

Beatrix surfaced gasping, hands flat on the crate, candlelight swimming. Beside her Rafael came up harder, a man breaking water, and for a moment neither of them spoke. The onions smelled ridiculous. Somewhere above them Mrs. Halloway's floorboards creaked, ordinary as bread.

"He stole it," Rafael said at last. His voice was wrecked. "In the middle of — she was *in his mind*, and he—"

"He listened for the click that lies." Beatrix stared at the dead amber sliver, empty now, spent. "I taught him that. Eight months ago. I thought he wasn't paying attention."

"He was paying attention." Rafael pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes. "God help us all, he was paying attention better than any of us."

She should have cried. Some part of her observed, distantly, that a normal person would cry, and that the failure to do so was itself a symptom of everything the guild had ever built into her. But what rose in her instead was colder and more useful and she recognized it with shame: the ledger-mind, opening columns, balancing sums.

One gifted hand. Willing. Holding the Emberbound bare while dragon-fire unmade it. Survival of sanity: unlikely, per the manufacturer.

"Well," she said. "Now we know."

"Now we know." Rafael lowered his hands. He was looking at her, and she watched the exact moment he arrived at the same column-sums she had, watched him do the arithmetic of *who*, watched him arrive at the answer he'd been dreading since the cellar began.

"No," he said.

"You don't get to—"

"*No.*" He stood, and the crate rocked, and a candle guttered. "Not you. That is not — Beatrix, you heard it. *No holder has survived sane.* It eats kings. It ate Aldric in twenty years and he was only wearing it. You'd be holding it *bare-handed* while it dies and it will take everything it can reach on the way down, and what it can reach is *you* —"

"And who else?" She stood too. The cellar was small; suddenly it was smaller. "Name them. Go on. Name one other person in this city who can do it. Who can walk into that binding-memory and tear the anchor free without fumbling. Who can hold a thing like that and keep their own name in their mouth. Name one."

"Maren—"

"Is seventy and hasn't stolen so much as a purse in a decade. Odile is blind and bound to the vaults. Every other gifted soul worth the name is registered, hunted, or sold out by Corvane." Her voice cracked on the last word and she hated it. "It's me, Rafael. It was always going to be me. I think maybe it's been me since the night I kept a copy of the king's memory and broke my own rule."

"Then we find another way. There's always another way. We have weeks — Odile said weeks —"

"Weeks," she agreed. "To invent a new form of magic that twenty centuries of dragons and kings never managed. Or—" she spread her hands, "—one girl does the job she was apparently born for."

"It's not a *job*, it's a pyre—"

"Don't you dare." Something in her voice stopped him mid-stride. "Don't you dare make it noble. It's not noble. It's arithmetic. I'm good at arithmetic."

For a long moment they stood in the candle-smelling dark, two people who had spent their lives lying professionally, unable to find a single lie adequate to the moment.

Then Rafael crossed the space between them in two steps and took her by the shoulders — not gently, exactly, but carefully, the way one handles a thing that is precious and also armed.

"I forbid it," he said.

"You *forbid* it." She laughed, and it came out wet, damn it, finally, at the worst possible time. "With what authority, inquisitor? Your commission is a costume. Your chancellor wants your head. You have no warrant, no law, no—"

"I have this." His hands tightened fractionally. "I have the fact that I love you, which I have been carrying around like contraband for years because I thought the timing was wrong, and the timing is *still* wrong, it will always be wrong, and I am telling you now specifically so that you cannot later claim you didn't know what it would cost me. If you go into that throne room planning to hold that thing, you are stealing something from me, Beatrix Ravensworth, and I am asking you — as the finest thief I have ever known — not to."

The cellar was very quiet. Above them, Mrs. Halloway's floorboards creaked again, and a kettle whined toward boiling, and the whole ordinary world went on, ignorant, upstairs.

"That's not fair," Beatrix whispered.

"No," he agreed. "It isn't. Almost nothing left is."

She put her forehead against his chest, just for a moment, just to borrow his steadiness, and felt his heart going like a trapped bird, and understood that he had told her the truth precisely because he believed — correctly — that she would steal it and use it against him.

"I promise you nothing," she said into his shirtfront.

"I know."

"But I'll plan it properly. No heroics. Arithmetic."

"That's somehow worse," he said, and she felt him smile against her hair, broken and unwilling. "Fine. Then we plan it together, and you'll let me stand between you and the court, and when this is over you'll owe me that drink."

"When this is over," she echoed, and did not say *if*, because he had asked her not to lie to him, and she was trying, newly and clumsily, to be a woman of her word.

Above them, sharp and sudden, Mrs. Halloway's knocker sounded — three strikes, paused, two more. The signal Rafael had arranged and hoped never to hear.

Rafael went still. "That's Odile's runner," he said. "From the vaults." He looked at Beatrix, and all the tenderness in his face hardened into dread. "Something's happened at the foundry."
