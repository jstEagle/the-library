## 18. The Guild Turns

Corvane found the page on a Tuesday, which he would later consider the last insult of a long and disappointing week.

Tuesdays were audit days. For thirty years he had kept the Hollow's books in perfect order — every memory logged, every trade weighed, every debt recorded in his small, vicious, immaculate hand. He liked Tuesdays the way other men liked feast days. There was a rightness to them. A column that balanced was the closest thing to prayer Corvane had ever needed.

So when the columns did not balance, he took it personally.

The discrepancy was small. One vial of archive-space unaccounted for, one candle burned past closing hours, one drawer in Beatrix Ravensworth's section opened and not properly re-warded. Sloppy. She was never sloppy. Which meant she had been in a hurry, and Beatrix Ravensworth was only ever in a hurry about two things: her ledgers, and her secrets.

He went down to her alcove himself.

She kept it neat as a chapel — quills aligned, ink pots stoppered, the little brass scale she used to weigh memories polished bright. But beneath the false bottom of her record-case, where a sensible archivist hid nothing because hiding things invited looking, Corvane found paper. Real paper. Not guild stock. Palace stock, cream-colored, watermarked with the Spire's tower.

Pages of it. Dates. Names. The binding-ceremony schedule going back eleven years. Notes in her precise hand: *the king signs edicts at hours he cannot account for.* *Veyra's seal appears before Aldric's.* *the crown is not decoration.*

And beneath it all, copied out over and over until she had it by heart, a single line lifted from Maren's old theft-records: *the Emberbound. Fed on dragon-fire. It eats its wearer.*

Corvane sat down slowly on her stool, which creaked under him in a way he chose to interpret as agreement.

"Well," he said to the empty alcove. "You clever, stupid girl."

He was not a sentimental man. People said this about him often, usually with relief, occasionally while signing away their childhoods. But he had pulled Beatrix out of a gutter when she was nine years old and biting, and he had taught her numbers the way you teach a knife to a child who will need it — carefully, and with respect. He had watched her become the finest archivist the Hollow had ever produced, and he had told himself, in the rare honest hours, that she was the closest thing he had to a daughter, if daughters were things a man like him could have.

Which is why what he felt, reading her notes, was not betrayal.

It was arithmetic.

The hunt was closing. Three guild members already gone in a wagon. Inquisitors walking the streets in white like a fresh fall of snow that everyone knew was poison. And here, in his own house, was proof that his best archivist had been researching the crown itself — the crown, whose Chancellor would pay in gold and immunity for exactly this kind of treason, gift-wrapped and tied with ribbon.

Corvane was a pragmatist. He had sold names before. He had learned long ago that survival was simply bookkeeping conducted at scale: some entries you keep, some entries you burn, and some entries you sell to the man holding the fire.

He closed the case gently, the way you close a coffin.

* * *

Beatrix knew something was wrong because Wren made her tea.

Wren never made anyone tea. Wren was the Hollow's lockbreaker, a wiry woman of fifty-odd with hands like a concert pianist and the conversational warmth of a dropped anvil, and in fifteen years Beatrix had watched her express affection exclusively through the medium of insults and the occasional stolen pastry left pointedly on a desk. Tea meant either catastrophe or condolence, and Beatrix couldn't decide which was worse.

"You're being strange," Beatrix said, accepting the cup anyway. It was good tea. That was somehow more alarming.

"I'm always strange."

"You're being *strange* strange."

Wren looked at her then — really looked, the way she looked at a lock before deciding whether to pick it or break it — and something in her face made Beatrix's stomach drop through three floors of the Hollow and into the cellar.

"Corvane wants you in the counting room," Wren said quietly. "Now. And Beatrix—" she hesitated, and lowered her voice to almost nothing. "Don't tell him anything you'd mind losing."

* * *

The counting room smelled of tallow and old money. Corvane sat behind his desk with the palace papers spread out before him, squared into a neat pile, and Beatrix understood immediately that there was no version of this conversation in which she talked her way out.

She considered running. She had a knife in her boot, a sense of direction worth stealing from any pursuer, and a window. But Wren had said *don't tell him anything you'd mind losing*, which meant Wren already knew, which meant others might too, which meant the exits were probably already spoken for.

She stood instead, hands folded, and waited.

"Eleven years," Corvane said. "Eleven years I have kept this house safe. Do you know how? Not by being brave. By being *boring*. We are boring, Beatrix. We are the most boring den of thieves in the history of crime. Nobody remembers us because we make sure there is nothing to remember." He tapped the papers once, lightly, like a man touching a bruise to confirm it still hurt. "And you. You've been writing a diary about the crown."

"It's not a diary—"

"It's a confession," Corvane said. "With footnotes."

"The hunt isn't going to stop, Corvane. You know that. You've seen the lists. Selling three of ours bought us a month, maybe. The crown doesn't want our names. It wants *us*. Every gifted mind in this city, harvested and shelved, and Veyra is building it a library—"

"I know what Veyra is building." Something moved behind his eyes, quick and unreadable. "I've been corresponding with her office for six years."

The words landed so flatly that for a moment Beatrix thought she'd misheard. Six years. Six years of quiet disappearances she'd blamed on bad luck. Six years of clients who traded their memories and were never seen again, and she had entered them in the ledger as *relocated*.

"You," she said. Her voice came out very small. "The wagon. Pip. Pip who taught me locks. You gave them Pip."

"Pip was careless. Pip was loud. Pip was going to get us all killed, and now Pip has bought the rest of us another season." Corvane rose, and to his credit — or whatever passed for credit in a man like him — he didn't look away. "I'm offering you the same arithmetic I offered myself. Veyra wants your head. I can give it to her, and the Hollow survives. Or you can run tonight, and I tell her I discovered nothing, and we both pretend this conversation was about candle inventory."

"And if I stay?"

"Then tomorrow morning the inquisitors come, and I open the door, and I watch them take you, and I sleep fine." He said it without heat, without cruelty, the way he read out a balance. "That's the offer, Beatrix. It's the only kind I make. I don't do tragedy. Tragedy doesn't balance."

Beatrix looked at him for a long moment — at the man who had fed her soup through a fever when she was ten, who had docked her wages for every mistake and doubled them, secretly, every time she got sick — and felt something in her chest close up like a ledger at year's end.

"You know what the worst part is?" she said. "You're right. Every word. That's what makes people like you dangerous. Not the cruelty. The *correctness*."

"Run, then," Corvane said, and sat back down, and picked up his pen. "And Beatrix. Whatever you're planning with that dragon-fire nonsense — don't come back here after. There's no version of the books where you're in them again."

* * *

She made it as far as the archive hall.

She hadn't planned to go there. Her feet decided, some stubborn animal part of her needing to walk the length of it one last time — past the shelves of bottled luck glinting like a hoard of tiny suns, past the memory-vaults humming softly behind their wards, past the reading alcove where she'd spent twenty years becoming invisible. The lamps were lit. Someone had left a half-eaten apple on the cataloguing desk. Ordinary. Home. The only home she had ever had, built out of other people's stolen yesterdays.

They were waiting for her.

Not guards. Worse. Friends.

Wren stood by the far door with her arms crossed. Beside her, Hesper the bottler, who had held Beatrix's hair back the night her father died and said nothing, because Hesper understood that some services required no words. Old Tamlin from acquisitions. The twins, Sela and Sorrel, who between them had taught her every card trick she'd ever used to cheat at guild holidays. A dozen faces, maybe more, gathered in the lamplight with the particular stillness of people who have already argued about you and lost.

"We heard," Wren said.

"Wren—"

"Don't." Wren's voice cracked on the word, which frightened Beatrix more than shouting would have. "Don't explain. Don't apologize. If you explain, I'll believe you, and then I'll help you, and then Corvane sells *me*, and I'm not brave, Beatrix. I've met brave. Brave gets wagons."

"I wasn't asking you to be brave."

"I know." Wren laughed, wet and ugly. "That's the whole problem. You never ask anybody for anything. You just go off and carry it alone like an idiot, and now look at you."

Hesper stepped forward and pressed something into Beatrix's hand — a small vial, cold as river stone. Inside, a curl of silver light turned lazily, like smoke in water.

"What is this?"

"Your father," Hesper said. "The copy you traded me, years ago. You thought I'd forgotten. I bottle things, Beatrix. Forgetting is sort of my whole profession." She pushed Beatrix's fingers closed around it. "Take it. Take it and go, and don't ever let anyone put you in a jar."

Sorrel was crying. Sela was glaring at Sorrel for crying, which was how the twins loved people. Tamlin wouldn't look at her at all, and Beatrix understood that too — that his silence was the loudest kindness in the room.

"Walk," Wren said finally, jerking her chin toward the far door. "Before I change my mind and pick the locks on your legs."

Beatrix walked. Down the length of the archive hall, past everything she had catalogued and guarded and loved, toward the door that led up to the street and the night and the rest of her life. Behind her, she heard the great doors begin to swing — heavy oak, guild-made, warded against thieves and fire and time.

At the threshold she stopped, just once, and looked back.

They stood in a line among the shelves, her family, watching her go. No one waved. No one spoke. Wren's jaw was set like a man defending a bridge, and Hesper had her hands pressed flat together as if in prayer, and the twins were holding each other up.

Then the doors closed behind her with a sound like a full stop, and the wards sang faintly along the frame — the sound the Hollow made when it locked out something it had decided was no longer welcome.

Beatrix stood in the dark street for a long moment, one hand around a vial of her father, the other pressed flat against the cold oak.

Then she pulled her hood up, turned south toward the harbor, and began, very carefully, to plan a burglary of the crown of the realm.

* * *

In the counting room, Corvane wrote a letter.

It took him four drafts, which was three more than usual. The final version was short, factual, and monstrous in its tidiness:

*To the Office of the Lord Chancellor: The Gilded Hollow confirms possession of materials concerning the Emberbound, currently held by one Beatrix Ravensworth, archivist. The guild disclaims all knowledge of her activities and offers its full cooperation in her apprehension. In exchange, we request formal assurance of immunity, in writing, per prior arrangement.*

He read it twice. He did not feel nothing. He noted the feeling, assigned it a value of approximately nothing, and filed it accordingly.

Then he sealed the letter with the Hollow's signet — a key and a closed eye — and rang for a courier.

Outside his window, the city was lighting its evening lamps, thousands of small warm flames in windows where people lived ordinary lives with their own memories in their own heads. Somewhere out there, a girl he had raised was walking into the dark with a dragon's name in her pocket.

Corvane drew the curtain.

Some entries, he told himself, you burn. Some you sell.

He was still telling himself that, much later, when the inquisitors came for him instead — but that is another chapter, and its columns do not balance either.
