## 19. Flight Through the Rooftops

The archive doors locked behind me with a sound I will hear for the rest of my life: three bolts, one after another, like a sentence being counted out.

Then the shouting started.

I did what any sensible thief does when her family decides she is a traitor. I went up. In the Gilded Hollow you learn early that the ground floor belongs to whoever has the most knives, the cellars belong to whoever holds the ledgers, and the roofs belong to no one—which means they belong to whoever is willing to be very cold and very alone.

I had been willing my whole life. It turned out I was out of practice.

* * *

The guild quarter's rooftops are a country unto themselves: slate hills and clay valleys, chimney forests, the great green spine of the tannery roof where rain has grown actual moss. As a girl I'd crossed them for sport, racing Pip—no, that was Tam's name for me later; as a girl I'd raced Wren and lost, always, because Wren was part cat and knew it. Fifteen years since I'd run them seriously. The body remembers, but it remembers with complaints.

Behind me, three shapes came over the Hollow's ridgeline in the grey pre-dawn light. I knew all of them by silhouette, which was somehow worse than not knowing them at all.

Brannick first—heavy, deliberate, moving across slate like a man walking on a floor he owned. He'd taught half the guild how to fall without breaking anything. Behind him, the Ferro twins, quick and silent, fanning wide to cut off my line toward the river. And behind them, far back, a fourth figure I couldn't name, hanging at the edge of the ridge like he wasn't sure he wanted to be part of this.

Corvane hadn't come himself. Corvane never dirtied his hands when a ledger entry would do. *Beatrix Ravensworth, traitor, reward for return unharmed.* Unharmed. How generous. How very much like him to specify the merchandise condition.

"Beatrix!" Brannick's voice rolled across the roofs, more sorrow than threat. "Stop making this hard!"

"You're chasing me across a rooftop before sunrise," I called back, already running. "Define *hard.*"

It's a gift of mine, the talking. People think memory-thieves work in silence, fingers to temples, eyes rolling back like fortune-tellers. Mostly we work in conversation. A memory wants to be given. You just have to make someone want to give it—and then take slightly more than they offered.

The problem with Brannick was that he'd known me since I was nine years old. You cannot charm a man who remembers teaching you to hold a lockpick like a spoon. Every trick I had, he'd watched me learn it.

So I ran.

* * *

Slate is honest. Tile lies. I dropped from the tannery roof onto the cooper's yard tiles and felt one shift under my heel with the particular soft click that precedes either death or embarrassment, and I chose forward motion over dignity, which is the only choice that ever matters on a roof.

Below me, the city was still asleep. Lamplighters snuffing the last wicks. A baker's boy hauling shutters. Somewhere a dog had opinions about the dawn. Ordinary life going on underneath me while I scrambled over it like a spider on a ceiling, and I thought—not for the first time—that this was what the gifted were to the ungifted. Always above them, technically. Never once welcome.

The Ferro twins took the low route through the bell tower of Saint Vessa's, meaning to pop out ahead of me on the nave roof. I heard the hatch bang and changed my line, cutting left along the gutter, and that was when the world informed me that I'd been bleeding for some minutes without noticing.

My left forearm. When? The archive doors—I'd caught them on the frame when Wren shoved through last, or maybe earlier, maybe when I went through the scullery window. Adrenaline is a wonderful bookkeeper; it writes everything down except your own injuries. The sleeve was soaked dark and the cold made it sing.

Fine. Bleeding is a problem for a future Beatrix. Present Beatrix had a tower to climb.

Saint Vessa's spire is the tallest thing in the guild quarter, a needle of grey stone with a wooden belfry cap and a weathervane shaped like a fish, because the saint who funded it had been a fishmonger and saw no reason to pretend otherwise. The belfry ladder runs up the outside. I'd climbed it at fourteen on a dare and thrown up at the top, and Wren had held my hair back over a two-hundred-foot drop, laughing, and told me the view was worth it.

The view was not worth it. The view was the Ferro twins coming up behind me with the patience of people who were being paid by the hour.

I went up anyway. Habit, spite, and the fact that down was occupied.

* * *

At the top of the belfry there is a maintenance platform barely wider than a coffin, a rope-and-pulley rig for hoisting repair materials, and a great deal of sky. The bells themselves hang below the platform, in the open arches, big bronze throats waiting for seven o'clock.

Seven o'clock. I looked east. The sky had gone the color of a bruise healing—yellow at the seam. I had perhaps twenty minutes.

"Beatrix." One of the twins—Lark, the elder by four minutes and insufferable about it—came over the platform edge, crouched, hands empty and open. The professional courtesy of guild hunters: we don't draw steel on family until the third refusal. "Corvane says come down. Nobody gets hurt."

"Corvane sold Doss and Merrit and little Fen Halloway to the inquisitors yesterday," I said. "You know that. You watched the wagon."

Lark's jaw tightened. His brother, Finch, came up beside him and said nothing, which from Finch was an essay.

"So when Corvane says nobody gets hurt," I went on, backing toward the pulley rig, "what he means is nobody whose name is currently useful to him gets hurt. I've read his ledgers, Lark. Have you? There's a column in the back with names in it. Yours is spelled wrong, by the way. It's been spelled wrong for six years. That should tell you something about how long you've been in that column."

"This isn't—" Lark started.

"The moment to have talked me down was before I found out," I said. "Now move."

They moved. Not away—toward, both of them, spreading along the platform edges the way they'd been trained, and I understood with a lurch that the talking was over, that I had spent my refusals, and that two people I'd eaten supper with every night for a decade were about to do their jobs.

Here is what nobody tells you about stealing memories: it costs the thief too. Every theft leaves a hollow in you shaped like what you took. Steal a man's mother's face and you carry his grief home like a stone in your shoe. Steal a child's sense of direction and—

Well. Let me tell you about that.

Finch lunged first, low, going for my legs, textbook, and I gave him a textbook answer—a knee he wasn't expecting and a shove that sent him into the pulley ropes. Lark grabbed my bad arm and the pain went through me like a struck bell, white and enormous, and I did the thing I had sworn on my father's grave I would never do to family.

I reached into Lark Ferro's mind and stole his sense of direction.

Not roughly. I want that recorded somewhere, in whatever ledger exists for sins of this kind. I took it gently, the way you lift a coin off a sleeping man, and I left him everything else—his name, his love for his brother, his hatred of boiled mutton, his memory of teaching me to juggle with oranges in the Hollow kitchen when I was eleven and terrible at it. Just the one small thing. The inner compass. The quiet knowledge every body carries of where down is and where home is and which way the door is.

He let go of me immediately. Not because he was beaten. Because suddenly there was no *toward* or *away*, no platform edge versus safety, no up. He sat down on the coffin-wide planks with the careful deliberateness of a drunk man and said, in a small voice entirely unlike his own:

"Finch. Finch, where are we."

"We're on the—" Finch stopped. Because Finch, holding his brother's hand, catching the confusion like a fever, felt the edges of the same absence. Proximity does that, sometimes, with a fresh theft. The hole in Lark gaped, and Finch leaned against it.

They huddled together in the middle of the platform, two grown men who could not have found the ladder if it had bitten them, and I backed away across the planks with my heart going like the bells would in— I glanced east— twelve minutes.

"I'm sorry," I said, and meant it so hard it hurt worse than the arm. "It grows back. Weeks, maybe. Hold hands and don't stand up."

And then, because I am who I am, I added: "There's a column in Corvane's ledger with your names in it. Check the spelling."

* * *

Getting down was its own humiliation. My arm had stopped bleeding and started throbbing in time with my pulse, my palms were shredded from the ladder, and somewhere over the cooper's yard my knee announced that it intended to file a formal complaint. I dropped the last eight feet into a hay cart, which is the kind of thing that sounds graceful in stories and is, in fact, mostly a lot of flailing and a mouthful of straw.

The fourth figure—the one who'd hung back on the ridge—was waiting at the bottom of the lane.

I knew him. Odo. Sixty years old, keeper of the Hollow's storeroom, a man who moved like furniture and had once, famously, slept through a raid. He stood under the eaves with his hands folded, and when I came around the corner he looked at me for a long moment with those mild, watery eyes.

"They'll circle round to the square," he said. "The twins will be a while yet." A pause. "What did you take from Lark?"

"His way home."

Odo nodded slowly, as though this were a reasonable item to have stolen. "Corvane's gone to Veyra. This morning. With your description and the palace research you hid in the false binding of *Meditations on Humility*—which, incidentally, was a poor choice of hiding place, everyone reads the bindings first." He sighed. "You have until midmorning before the inquisitors have your face."

"Why are you telling me this?"

"Because you returned my wife's laugh to me," Odo said simply. "Thirty-one years ago. Before you could read. I've been waiting since then to repay it, and this—" he gestured at the lane, the dawn, the whole ruined morning— "this is all I've got. Go left at the fountain. Left, Beatrix. Not right. You're bleeding and you're stupid and you always go right."

I went left.

* * *

Rafael's lodgings are in Candlewick Row, which is a joke the city plays on itself: the street where the crown's inquisitors keep their modest apartments is named for the chandlers' guild that went bankrupt forty years ago. His building is narrow and grey and has a drainpipe on the north side that I had climbed exactly once before, at night, for reasons that had nothing to do with treason and everything to do with a bottle of wine and a window latch he'd never fixed because he knew I'd keep using it.

I stood in the alley below that window as the sun came up proper, and I hesitated.

This was the arithmetic, and it was ugly. If I climbed, I was asking an officer of the hunt to harbor the hunt's most wanted thief—an act that would end his career, possibly his sister's safety, certainly his freedom. If I didn't climb, I had nowhere. The Hollow was closed to me. Maren's attic was the second place Corvane would look. Odile's vaults were sacred and also underwater. Every door in this city now had my name on the other side of it, locked.

Adrenaline curdles. Nobody warns you about that. For hours it keeps you brilliant—cold, fast, precise, a creature made entirely of next steps—and then it spoils all at once, and what's left underneath is just a woman with a ruined arm standing in an alley smelling of hay and blood, realizing that she has been alone for days and that every person she ran *from* this morning was also, in a sense, every person she had.

I climbed the drainpipe. Badly. The knee filed its complaint; the arm screamed its objection; a shutter bracket gave way under my boot with a crack like a starting pistol. But grief and fear make excellent climbers, and I came over the sill into Rafael's rooms the way I always had—uninvited, bleeding slightly, insufferably pleased with myself.

He was asleep in the chair by the cold fireplace, still dressed, a report open facedown on his chest. Even asleep he looked like a man doing his duty. The candle had burned to a coin of wax. On the table, his sister's letter, read and reread, creased soft as cloth.

I stood there dripping dawn light and hay and decided—because I am, as established, stupid—to wake him gently.

"Rafael," I whispered. "Inquisitor. Your window security is disgraceful."

His eyes opened and for one full second he was twenty-two again, and so was I, and neither of us was hunted by anyone. Then the present arrived, as it does, wearing its boots.

"Beatrix." He was up, steadying me before I'd finished swaying, and his hands found the torn arm and the split palm and catalogued them with the flat efficiency of a man who has seen wounds and hates each one personally. "Sit. Don't argue. What happened to your arm?"

"A door. A ladder. A twin. In ascending order of regret."

"That's descending order."

"It's been a long night." I sat, because my legs had unionized. "Rafael. Corvane went to Veyra. This morning. He's sold me—my face, my methods, probably my favorite breakfast. By midmorning every inquisitor in the city will have my description, and by afternoon they'll have something better than a description." I looked up at him and made myself say it plainly, because he deserved plain. "They'll have my handwriting. They'll know to look for ledger entries that are *too* neat. You can't be associated with me anymore. I shouldn't even be here. I know that. I'm here anyway, and I need you to decide, right now, whether you're going to arrest me, because if you are, I'd rather it happened before the tea."

He didn't answer at once. He knelt instead and began cleaning the arm with water and that rough spirit he kept for disinfecting things and occasionally drinking, and the sting made my eyes water, and I chose to blame the spirit.

"Veyra summoned me yesterday," he said finally, winding the bandage with maddening competence. "She showed me documents. Arrest lists. Drafted weeks before the proclamation—before the king ever spoke the words. Someone wrote the hunt before the hunt existed." His jaw worked. "She threatened my sister, Beatrix. To my face, with tea and biscuits, like discussing weather. And then she assigned me to the solstice ceremony."

"The binding ceremony." I went cold in a way the morning sun couldn't touch. "Renewing the crown's fire. With the harvested minds."

"With a hundred of them." He tied off the bandage and looked at me, and there it was between us, the thing we'd been circling since the first night he'd walked into the Hollow with his inspection warrant and his careful, hungry eyes: two liars who had chosen trust anyway, now discovering what the trust was *for*. "She's putting me inside the Spire, at the king's side, on the one night the crown is vulnerable. Either she doesn't know what I am now, or—"

"Or it's exactly what she wants," I finished. "A trap with good posture."

Outside, the bells of Saint Vessa's began to ring seven. I thought of Lark and Finch sitting hand in hand on a belfry platform, waiting patiently for the world to grow directions again, and I laughed—a short, cracked sound with nothing funny in it.

"What?" Rafael asked.

"I robbed two of my oldest friends this morning to get away from the rest of them," I said. "And the only person in this city I haven't stolen from is you." I held out my good hand. "Let's keep it that way. Help me steal a crown instead."

He took my hand. And that was when we both heard, from the street below, the measured tread of boots, and a knock at his door—polite, official, and far too early for anyone selling anything.

Rafael looked at me. I looked at the window.

"Tea first," I whispered, "or arrests?"

The knock came again.
