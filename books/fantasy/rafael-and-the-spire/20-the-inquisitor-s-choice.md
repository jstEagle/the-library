## 20. The Inquisitor's Choice

The summons came at dawn, which was how Rafael knew it was not a summons at all. Summons arrived by courier, on cream paper, with a wax seal and a polite fiction of choice attached. This one came as a knock on his own door — three soft raps, the rhythm of a man who had already been inside his rooms once that morning to confirm he slept alone.

He opened it to find a boy of perhaps fourteen in Chancellor's gray, holding out a folded card like it might bite him.

"Lord Veyra requests your presence," the boy recited. "At your earliest convenience."

"And if my earliest convenience is next spring?"

The boy's eyes flicked past Rafael's shoulder, to the two soldiers waiting at the end of the corridor, pretending very badly to examine a wall sconce. "She said you'd say something like that," he said, with the weary sympathy of the young for the doomed. "She said to tell you: *bring your good boots.*"

Rafael looked down at his feet. He was wearing his good boots. He had put them on without quite knowing why, the way a man puts on armor before a battle he has told himself isn't coming.

"Tell her I'm delighted," he said.

* * *

Veyra kept her office at the top of the Chancellery, in a round room with windows on every side, so that no matter where you stood she could see the whole sky behind her and you could see nothing behind her at all. She had arranged it, Rafael suspected, the way a spider arranges a web — not for comfort but for geometry. Everything that entered would be seen against the light.

She stood at the eastern window when he came in, hands clasped behind her back, watching the city wake below. Smoke from ten thousand chimneys rose in straight gray columns into a windless morning. Somewhere down there, Beatrix was awake too. He had to stop himself from thinking about where.

"Inquisitor Vale." She didn't turn. "You look tired."

"I sleep poorly in winter."

"Mm. You've slept poorly for eleven winters, by my count." Now she turned, and smiled, and the smile was the worst part of her — warm as a hearth, patient as rot. "Sit, please. Would you like tea? It's from the southern terraces. My own import. The court thinks me extravagant about it."

"No, thank you."

"More for me." She poured anyway, two cups, and set one within his reach, and left it there like a small test he was expected to fail or pass and would be graded either way. He did not touch it. She sipped hers and made a small sound of pleasure, entirely genuine, which somehow made everything worse. Veyra enjoyed things. That was what no one warned you about her. She enjoyed tea and sunsets and clever conversation, and she enjoyed ruin, and she enjoyed all of them in exactly the same unhurried way.

"Do you know," she said, "what I admire most about you, Rafael? You are the only honest man in this building. Everyone else lies to me beautifully. You lie to me terribly, and only when you absolutely must, and it pains you visibly. It's restful. Like a clock that chimes slightly off — you always know something is wrong because you can hear it."

"You flatter me, Chancellor."

"I inventory you, Inquisitor. Flattery is for people whose use I haven't determined yet." She set down her cup and crossed to her desk, unhurried, and lifted a leather folder from its center with both hands, the way a priest lifts a relic. "Now. Let us be honest together, since we are both so gifted at it."

She opened the folder and turned it toward him.

There were papers inside. Of course there were papers; there were always papers. But Rafael had spent eleven years reading other people's documents for a living, and he knew how to read a trap, and this one was written in his own hand.

His report on the Tallowbridge raid. His original draft — the one he had burned in his own hearth, or believed he had. Beside it, a fair copy in a clerk's careful script, and beside *that*, the ledger page itself: the coded entry, the false heading, the little arithmetic error he had planted in column four because Beatrix would know what it meant. *Third shelf, wet cellar, count the candles twice.* And beneath all of it, in red ink, in Veyra's own narrow hand, a single annotation:

*Confirmed. Safehouse empty at time of raid. Occupant departed approximately forty minutes prior.*

Forty minutes. His warning had worked. She had let it work. She had watched him do it, weighed it, measured it, and filed it away like a coin she wasn't ready to spend.

"The interesting question," Veyra said pleasantly, "is not whether you betrayed your post. We settled that some weeks ago. The interesting question is why I have not yet hanged you for it."

Rafael kept his face still. Eleven years of interrogations had taught him that the first thing a trapped animal does is move, and the second thing it does is die. "I assumed you were saving me for a special occasion."

"Oh, I am." Her smile deepened. "But occasions require preparation, and preparations require leverage, and speaking of leverage—"

She rang a small silver bell on her desk. Once. Not twice.

The side door opened, and Rafael's whole body went cold before his mind caught up, because he knew that door led to the antechamber, and he knew who was being brought through it, and when they came — two guards and a small figure between them, wrists bound in front, chin up, dark hair escaping its braid in furious wisps — the sound that came out of him was not words at all. It was low and involuntary, an animal noise, and he hated himself for it even as he made it, because he saw Veyra cataloguing it, filing it, spending nothing and gaining everything.

"Sera," he said.

"Rafi." His sister looked at him across the room, and her eyes were bright and dry and furious, and she said, with the particular contempt only a younger sister can produce under any circumstances whatsoever, including chains: "You look terrible. Have you been sleeping?"

"Sera—"

"She's been comfortable," Veyra said smoothly. "I want to be clear about that. Good food, a warm room, books — she's read four, she has opinions about the third one, I'm told. I do not mistreat children, Inquisitor. Mistreatment is inefficient. Fear makes people stupid, and your sister is many things, but stupid is not among them." She paused. "Unlike certain members of her family."

Sera's bound hands twitched, and Rafael realized she was trying to sign to him — the old childhood code, *thumb to knuckle, stay calm* — and the guards were watching her hands, and she stopped, and lifted her chin higher instead.

"What do you want?" Rafael asked. His voice came out level. He was almost proud of it.

"What I have always wanted." Veyra returned to her window, hands clasped, silhouetted against the waking city. "Order. The hunt proceeds. The solstice approaches. On solstice night, the binding-ceremony renews the crown's fire, and a hundred minds will be given to it, and the realm will have another generation of peace — real peace, not the paper kind your edicts are printed on. Every piece must be in place. Every guard at every door must be a guard I can trust absolutely." She turned her head, just enough that he caught her profile against the light. "Or a guard I can predict absolutely. Those are nearly the same thing, and in your case, Inquisitor, they may be identical."

"You're asking me to serve you."

"I'm asking you to serve the crown. There is a difference, though I understand why you'd struggle to see it." She came back to the desk, sat, folded her hands. "Here is the shape of it. You kneel. You swear the oath again, properly this time, before witnesses. You accept reinstatement with full honors — the story will be that you were cleared after investigation, that your lapse at Tallowbridge was a ruse to penetrate the thieves' network. Which, incidentally, will now be true, because I will write it, and what I write becomes true; that is rather the point of me."

"And Sera?"

"Goes south. Safely. With funds, with letters of introduction, with a new name if she wants one. She'll never see the inside of a cell again. She'll never see *me* again. She'll write you letters from somewhere warm, and you'll read them, and occasionally you'll wonder if I'm reading over your shoulder, and the answer will usually be no." She said it lightly, almost kindly, and that was the cruelest thing in the room besides the chains. "In exchange, you stand post at the solstice ceremony. The inner door. The honor assignment. The one position in the entire Spire that matters most."

Silence. The tea steamed gently between them, going cold, unclaimed by anyone.

"Why the inner door?" Rafael asked. "You don't trust me. You just told me you don't trust me. Why give me the one post that—"

"Because you're honest," Veyra said, and for a moment — half a moment — something moved behind her eyes that was not warmth and was not cruelty but was perhaps loneliness, ancient and well-fed, the kind that has eaten everything else on the plate. "Because everyone else in that room will be watching the crowd, and the doors, and each other. Only you will be watching *me*. Because a man who has already committed treason, and been forgiven for it, and knows that his sister's freedom hangs on his perfect behavior — that man is the most reliable instrument I possess. Betrayal is a debt, Inquisitor. I've simply refinanced yours." She slid the folder aside and produced, from beneath it, a single sheet of heavy white paper bearing the crown's seal. "Kneel."

He looked at Sera. She was shaking her head, slowly, minutely — *don't* — and her eyes were saying something else entirely, something twelve years old and terrified and brave, and he loved her so much it felt like a wound reopening.

He thought about Beatrix. He thought about the cellar, and the candle stubs, and Tam's memory unspooling between them in the dark while they held hands over a dying boy's last gift, and the way she had looked at him afterward and said, *you know what has to happen,* and he had said *no,* and she had smiled the way people smile at funerals.

He thought: *she needs someone inside.*

He thought: *Veyra has just handed me the door.*

It was, he reflected later, the strangest moment of his life — the instant where surrender and gambit occupy the same body, wear the same face, bend the same knee. He could not have told you, kneeling there on the cold stone of the Chancellery floor with the seal-paper in his hands and his sister's breath audible across the room, whether he was giving up or beginning. Perhaps there was no difference. Perhaps that was the lesson Veyra had never learned, the one the crown had eaten out of her decades ago: that a man's surrender, freely chosen, is just another word for a plan.

He knelt.

"Repeat after me," Veyra said, and her voice had gone soft and liturgical, and outside the round windows the sun climbed another degree and the smoke columns leaned eastward, toward the Spire. "I swear my hand, my eye, and my silence—"

"I swear my hand, my eye, and my silence."

"—to the crown above the king, and the king beneath the crown—"

"To the crown above the king, and the king beneath the crown."

"—until the fire is renewed."

"*Until the fire is renewed.*"

The words went into him like swallowed stones. He felt each one settle.

Veyra exhaled, satisfied, and rang the bell again, and the guards stepped forward to unlock Sera's wrists, and Rafael stood, knees aching, wearing his treason like a fresh uniform that fit worse than the old one.

"Sera," he said. "You'll go south. Today. There's a carter named Doss who owes me — gray mule, bad temper, honest man. Tell him the inquisitor sent you and show him your left boot."

"Why my left boot?"

"Because there's a letter in it and Doss can't read, which is the safest kind of messenger." He managed a smile. It cost him. "And Sera — the third book. When you write to me. Tell me what you thought of the third book."

Her eyes narrowed. Twelve years of sibling warfare had taught her that when her brother got sentimental, he was also getting tactical. "It was dreadful," she said carefully. "Everyone dies in chapter nine and then it turns out they didn't. The author cheats."

"Does she," Rafael said gravely. "Imagine that. Someone surviving something they were supposed to die of." He kissed the top of her head, quickly, before she could squirm away, and whispered into her hair, so quietly that only she could possibly have heard it: "Whatever happens at solstice — whatever you hear — burn my letters and believe the third book."

* * *

They gave him his orders that afternoon, sealed in white wax, delivered by the same gray boy who had fetched him at dawn.

*By appointment of the Lord Chancellor: Inquisitor Rafael Vale to stand honor-guard at the inner door of the throne room, solstice night, from the first bell until the renewal of the flame. No relief. No substitution. No communication with persons outside the ceremony.*

He read it twice in his lodgings, then fed it to the candle and watched it curl.

Then he took out a clean sheet of the Hollow's ledger paper — he had kept one page, all these months, the way a man keeps a key to a house he's no longer sure exists — and sat for a long time with the pen uncapped, thinking.

A coded entry was impossible; Veyra read his hand like scripture now. A message was impossible; every word he wrote would pass through her clerks. He had, by accepting the post, walked into a cell with gilded bars and a view of exactly one door — and the only person who needed to reach that door was the one person he could no longer safely speak to.

So he did the only thing left. He did what she had taught him, years ago, without ever meaning to: he hid the message where only a thief would think to look.

He wrote a routine requisition — lamp oil, ceremonial tallow, forty pounds, for the solstice lighting — and submitted it through channels, dull as dishwater, and in the margin, in the tiny cramped notation clerks used for weight variances, he added a figure that was wrong. Deliberately wrong. Forty pounds of tallow for a hall that required sixty-two. Any auditor would correct it. Any auditor except the one woman in the city who had spent her life making numbers lie beautifully, and who had once, in a cellar, taught him that the Gilded Hollow priced its contraband memories by weight — and that *forty* was the old guild-code for *the vaults*, and *sixty-two* meant *midnight*.

She would find it. Or she wouldn't, and he would stand at that door alone, and one of them would hang, and probably both.

He blotted the page. Outside his window, the Spire stood black against the reddening sky, and somewhere inside it a king he had never met sat flinching from his own crown, and somewhere beneath the city a dragon guttered like a candle in a draft, and somewhere — he refused to wonder where, he absolutely refused — Beatrix was doing whatever Beatrix did, which was to say: the impossible, quietly, on a schedule.

"Forty pounds of tallow," he said aloud to the empty room, and laughed, briefly and without much humor, at the size of the wager he had just placed. "Gods help us both."

The candle burned down. He did not sleep. At dawn he put on his good boots, and his white cloak, and began to wait for the longest night of the year.
