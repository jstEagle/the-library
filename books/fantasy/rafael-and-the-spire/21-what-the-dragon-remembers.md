## 21. What the Dragon Remembers

Odile heard her coming down the foundry stairs three days before she actually arrived.

That was the trouble with the drowned vaults: sound traveled strangely in them, bouncing off wet stone and still water until footsteps became rumors and rumors became ghosts. A drip in the east gallery might be a drip, or it might be someone's boot on the far stair, or it might be nothing at all. Odile had spent eleven years learning to tell the difference, and she was very good at it now, which was fortunate, because she was blind as a mole and twice as stubborn.

The girl's steps were wrong for a courier and too careful for a rat-catcher. They paused at the third landing — everyone paused at the third landing, where the heat began — and then came on again, slower, with that particular hesitation Odile had learned to read as *I have made a terrible mistake and I am going to keep walking anyway.*

"Tea's cold," Odile called out, before the girl could announce herself. "And I only have two cups, so if you've brought friends, they can drink from the condensation."

A pause. Then, wryly: "Just me."

"Then you're in luck." Odile turned toward the brazier and felt for the kettle by its handle-wrapping of old leather. "Sit anywhere. Mind the third crate from the wall; it's mostly teeth."

"*Mostly* teeth?"

"The rest is buttons. Don't ask." She poured by sound — the kettle's pitch changed when the cup was half full, a trick her mother had taught her, though her mother had used it for soup. "You're the ledger-keeper. Ravensworth. Maren sent you."

"How did you—"

"You walk like someone who counts things," Odile said. "Left foot always a little shorter than the right, like you're measuring the floor. Maren does it too. All you archivists do it. It's very annoying and I want you to know I noticed immediately."

Beatrix Ravensworth laughed — a short, startled laugh, the kind people made when they'd forgotten they could. Odile liked her already, which was inconvenient. She preferred not to like the ones who came asking about Vaskarath. Liking them made the disappointment worse.

* * *

The vaults beneath the old foundry were not, strictly speaking, a vault. They had been one once — a counting-house cellar, then a smuggler's cache, then a smuggler's flooded cache after the river changed its mind about where it wanted to go — but the maps called them the Drowned Vaults and maps are stubborn things. Now they were something older than any of their names. Something with a heartbeat.

Odile led Beatrix down past the tea things, past the crates of teeth (dragon teeth, shed over centuries, each one taller than a man; Odile kept them because they were hers to keep, and because the hollow ones made excellent wine racks), and out onto the long gallery where the water lay black and mirror-still except at its center.

There, the water glowed.

It was not firelight. Firelight flickered; this breathed. A slow red-gold pulse under the surface, like a coal wrapped in wool, like a heart deciding whether to bother beating again. The whole gallery smelled of hot iron and rain and something sweeter underneath — ozone, maybe, or memory.

"He's sleeping," Odile said quietly. "Ember-sleep. Has been for sixty years, more or less. He'll wake properly twice more in his life, maybe three times if he's lucky, and then—" She shrugged, an old gesture, worn smooth. "Well. You know what nearly extinct means. It means the word 'nearly' is doing all the work."

Beatrix stood at the gallery rail. Odile heard her breathing change — the quick shallow pull of someone seeing something too large for their eyes to file away.

"He's beautiful," Beatrix whispered.

"He's a grumpy old lizard with opinions about architecture," Odile said. "But yes. Also that."

* * *

They sat through the night watch together, because that was how it worked. You didn't wake a dragon. You waited near one, and you talked, and eventually — if the dragon felt like it, and if your questions were worth the effort of waking for — the ember-light would rise, and the voice would come.

Odile explained this while sharing bread and hard cheese and the last of the tea, reheated, which improved it not at all.

"So I just... talk?" Beatrix said. "To the water?"

"To him. The water's just where he keeps most of himself these days. Body's too heavy to move much; joints ache; you know how it is." Odile broke off a piece of cheese and ate it with dignity. "Talk about why you came. Dragons can smell a lie the way I can smell rain coming. Better, actually. I smell rain in my knees. He smells lies in his scales, or so he claims, and he's never given me reason to doubt it."

"And if he doesn't answer?"

"Then you come back next week and try again, and I charge you for the tea both times."

Beatrix was quiet a moment. When she spoke again, her voice had changed — stripped of the archivist's neatness, down to something rawer.

"I need his fire. Or — the last of it. There's a crown. The Emberbound. It's eating the king alive, and it's eating everyone the king touches, and there's a hunt on for my people, and I think—" Her breath caught. "I think it has to burn. And I think ordinary fire won't do it. And I think I'm the one who has to carry it into the burning, and I'm terrified, and I don't know if that's courage or just a different kind of theft, taking a burden that isn't mine to take."

Silence. The drip in the east gallery. The slow breath of the water.

Then the light rose.

* * *

Odile had heard Vaskarath speak perhaps forty times in eleven years, and it never stopped being like standing inside a bell. The voice didn't come through the ears so much as through the ribs — a resonance in the stone, in the water, in the small bones behind the eyes. Words arrived already glowing, like coals rolled across a hearth.

**THE LEDGER-KEEPER.**

Beside her, Beatrix went rigid. Odile reached over and patted her knee, the way you'd steady a spooked horse.

"That's you," she said helpfully. "He does introductions. He thinks it's polite."

**YOU SMELL OF INK AND STOLEN THINGS,** the dragon said. **MOSTLY THE FIRST. THAT IS RARE, DOWN HERE.**

"I'm trying to return some," Beatrix said. Her voice shook only a little. "Stolen things. Starting with a king."

A long rumble moved through the water — amusement or displeasure, impossible to say. With Vaskarath, Odile had learned, the two lived close together, like neighbors in a thin-walled house.

**THE CROWN,** the dragon said, and the word landed heavy enough to make ripples. **YES. I REMEMBER THE CROWN.**

"I hoped you might."

**I FORGED ITS FIRST FIRE.** The light pulsed, dimmed, pulsed again. **NO. That is not true, and I will not let even my own memory lie. I did not forge it. I was *present* at its forging, which is worse. There is a difference between the hand that makes a wound and the friend who stands by holding the needle.**

Odile poured herself more tea she didn't want. This part she knew. This part she had heard before, on the two other occasions someone had come asking about dragon-fire, and both times the telling had cost the old dragon something, and both times he had told it anyway, because that was the kind of creature he was — vain, ancient, tired, and scrupulous to the bone.

**Men came to us in the year your histories call the Quiet Spring,** Vaskarath said. **Before your walls were tall. We were many, then. The sky had shapes in it.**

He told it slowly, the way old creatures tell things, folding each sentence twice before setting it down.

**There was a famine. Not of food — of trust. Men and dragons had made promises to one another since the first fires, and every generation broke a few, and the breaking accumulated like silt. So we made a final promise. A binding promise. My fire would anchor it. A covenant between dragons and men, sealed in flame, that neither kind would ever harvest the minds of the other. No theft of memory between species. Ever. It sounds small to you. It was everything to us. Your kind dreams. We remember. To steal a dragon's remembering is to unmake it entire.**

"What happened?" Beatrix asked softly.

**Your king's grandfather happened. Or his grandfather's grandfather — the line blurs; crowns are handed down like debts. He came to the forging with a gift in his hands: a circlet of iron and gold, meant to hold the covenant-fire, a vessel worthy of the oath. We breathed our promise into it. And he wore it home.**

The water shivered.

**He was not supposed to wear it. It was to sit upon the altar at the border-stone, a treaty-object, touched by no head. But a crown on an altar is a symbol, and a crown on a head is power, and your kind has never once in its history confused the two. He wore it to his coronation. Just for the ceremony, he said. Just to honor the oath.**

**But the fire remembered what it was for. Fire is honest that way. It had been made to bind — to hold a promise fast against all breaking — and so, finding itself on a living mind instead of a dead altar, it did the only thing it knew. It bound. It held the king to his own oath, and then to his own thoughts, and then to itself. Every year a little deeper. Every king who inherited it a little more consumed than the last.**

**We discovered it within a decade. We came to take our fire back.**

"And?" Beatrix whispered, though Odile could have told her. Everyone who knew the story could have told her.

**And men had learned, in that decade, how to raise armies between sunrise and sunset. How to write edicts. How to make the word "dragon" mean "monster" in a hundred languages before we finished knocking on the gate.** The ember-light flared, guttered. **We fought. Of course we fought. There were nine hundred of us. There are — **

He stopped. The pause lasted so long that the drip in the east gallery counted eleven falls.

**There are four,** Vaskarath said finally. **One sleeps under ice in the far north and will not wake for anyone. One went into the sea and does not answer. One is you, little thief, standing at my rail asking me for the last of my fire. And one is me.**

Nobody said anything. The water breathed its slow red breath.

* * *

Later — much later, when Beatrix had cried the quiet, furious tears of someone who hates being seen crying, and Odile had pretended very hard to be interested in the ceiling — the dragon spoke again.

**You want my fire to destroy it.**

"Yes."

**Understand what you ask. My fire is not wood-flame. It does not simply burn. It *unmakes bindings*. It returns things to what they were before the promise was spoken. If it takes the crown, it will take everything the crown holds — every harvested mind, every stolen year, every binding-thread running back six generations. All of it, released at once, into whatever vessel is nearest.**

"Which is why someone has to hold it," Beatrix said. "Carry the burden while it burns. Take the crown's hunger into themselves so it doesn't scatter into the court."

**Yes.** A pause. **And understand further: the vessel must be gifted. Only a mind that knows how to hold memories can hold *that* memory. And the vessel must be willing. Truly willing, in the place where willingness lives — the fire will taste it, and it cannot be lied to, and it cannot be stolen from. Do you see? For one night, at the end of all things, the theft must stop. The giving must begin. That is the only payment my fire will accept.**

"Why?" Odile asked, though she suspected she knew.

**Because that is what was broken,** Vaskarath said simply. **Six hundred years ago, men took what was not given. The wound closes only the same way it opened. Freely. Or not at all.**

The light sank lower. The great shape beneath the water shifted — Odile felt it in the floor, a tectonic sigh — and the voice grew quieter, the embers banked.

**I am dying, ledger-keeper. Slowly, the way mountains die, but dying. My fire has perhaps weeks. When it goes, it goes into the dark with me, and the world loses its last key.** A long breath. **So I will tell you what I told the last one who asked, sixty years ago, and what I should have shouted from the mountaintops six hundred years ago instead of brooding in holes:**

**Come back when you are ready to give. Not to trade. Not to bargain. Not to spend yourself like coin because you hate yourself a little. Come ready to give the way my fire gives — wholly, knowing the cost, loving something enough to pay it. Bring the one who loves you, if there is one, because no one should stand alone at a thing like this. And bring the truth about the crown, all of it, because the fire will want to know exactly what it is dying to kill.**

**Do this, and on the solstice, when the veil between kept and broken things wears thinnest — I will rise.**

The ember-light dimmed to a coal, then a spark, then the faintest blush beneath the black water, like a sunset remembered rather than seen.

* * *

They climbed back up to the tea things in silence. Odile put the kettle on. Some rituals held the world together, and hers were few enough to guard jealously.

Beatrix sat with her hands around a cup she wasn't drinking, staring at nothing, which for a blind woman's guest was a considerable discourtesy, but Odile forgave it.

"He remembers everything," Beatrix said at last. "Every broken promise. Six hundred years of watching men do exactly what he watched them do. And he's still willing to give the fire."

"Mm."

"Why? After all of it — why?"

Odile considered the question honestly, turning it over the way she turned over the dragon's shed teeth, looking for the hollow side.

"Because he's the only one left who *can*," she said. "When you're the last of anything, keeping score stops mattering so much. What matters is what you leave behind." She sipped her tea and grimaced at it. "Also he's sentimental, though he'd eat me for saying so. He kept a lock of hair from the man who betrayed the covenant. First king. Kept it sixty years in a matchbox. Asked me once if I thought it meant he'd forgiven him."

"What did you tell him?"

"That it meant he hadn't finished grieving. Those aren't the same thing." Odile set down her cup. "Go home, Ravensworth. Sleep. Eat something that isn't vault-bread. And then go find whoever it is you're afraid to bring here, because I heard you hesitate when he said *the one who loves you*, and dragons hear better than I do."

Beatrix's silence had a color to it. Odile had spent a lifetime learning colors without eyes, and that particular shade was *yes, there is someone, and I haven't told him, and the telling terrifies me more than the burning does.*

"There's a ceremony," Beatrix said finally, voice steadying into something harder, something with edges. "At the solstice. Veyra's renewing the crown's fire with a hundred harvested minds. That's when I have to do it — steal the binding-memory, free the king, get the crown to the fire. Weeks from now. Maybe less."

"Then you'd best hurry," Odile said. "Both errands."

She listened to the girl stand, count her steps to the stair — left foot always shorter — and pause at the top of the gallery.

"Odile. Thank you. For the tea. And the teeth warning."

"Come back soon," Odile said. "Bring news. Good or bad, he'll want it. He pretends he doesn't care what happens up there." She smiled at the dark. "He asks me every single morning."

Footsteps faded up the stair. The drip resumed in the east gallery. And beneath the black water, so faint that only a keeper would catch it, the ember-light pulsed once — twice — like a heart making up its mind, like a question already asked, waiting in the dark for the answer to climb back down the stairs carrying someone else's hand in hers.
