## 22. Rehearsal for Treason

The safehouse cellar smelled of lamp oil, damp stone, and Rafael's terrible attempt at tea.

Beatrix held the cup at arm's length and studied it the way she studied forged ledgers. "What is this?"

"Tea."

"This is what tea dreams of becoming when it grows up. This is tea's ambition." She set it down on a crate between them, next to Maren's map of the Spire underlevels and Tam's memory, which sat in its little glass vial like a held breath. "We're going to commit treason in four days. The least you could do is provide drinkable beverages."

"I'm an inquisitor," Rafael said. "We're trained in interrogation, scripture, and swordwork. Nobody thought to teach us tea."

"Clearly. Your whole order is a scandal." She pulled the map toward her and smoothed it flat with both palms, and just like that the humor drained out of the room, the way it always did when they bent over this particular table. Four days to solstice. One chance.

He came around the crate and stood beside her, close enough that she could feel the warmth of him through her sleeve, and they looked down at the palace together the way generals look at a country they intend to break.

"Walk it again," he said. "From the top."

"From the top." She tapped the foundry district at the map's southern edge. "Solstice morning, the tithe wagons come up from the harvest houses — a hundred harvested minds for the binding rite. Veyra's clerks escort them through the Service Gate because the ceremonial doors are reserved for nobility and nobody searches a clerk carrying grief." She moved her finger up through the underlevels, past the old wine vaults, past the cisterns, to the block that was the throne room floor. "I go in with the wagons. Gray coat, gray ledger, gray face. I have papers."

"Forged papers."

"Beautifully forged papers. I did them myself; I'm allowed to compliment my own work, it's not vanity if it's true."

"And the gate clerks?"

"Will check the seal, count the vials, and wave me through, because the seal is real — I stole it off a genuine tithe clerk two nights ago while he was eating a pie. He never felt a thing. He'll wake tomorrow thinking he lost it in the wash, and he'll be slightly more careful, and that small improvement in his life is my gift to the crown's bureaucracy."

Rafael made the sound he made when he was trying not to laugh and losing — a low exhale through the nose, like a horse deciding to tolerate something. "You stole a man's seal while he ate a pie."

"He had a whole pie, Rafael. He wasn't going to notice anything but the pie."

* * *

They rehearsed it the way actors rehearse, except every line was a lie and every prop was stolen.

Rafael played the gate clerk. Beatrix handed him her papers with the bored slouch she'd practiced in front of a cracked mirror, and he examined them with his inquisitor's frown, which was genuinely unnerving even in a cellar, even playing a part.

"You're nervous," he said.

"I'm *acting* nervous. There's a difference. A real tithe clerk is nervous because clerks get searched too. Give me your worst."

"My worst." He looked up slowly, and something shifted behind his eyes, and suddenly he wasn't Rafael playing a clerk anymore; he was the White Order's finest, and the temperature of the cellar dropped. "State your name and house of assignment."

"Hessa Vole, Harvest House Nine." Her voice didn't waver. She was proud of that later.

"Open the case."

She opened the case. Inside were forty vials of harvested memory, or rather thirty-nine empty vials and one containing water and a drop of tincture that would read as memory to any testing-stone short of a cathedral's. Rafael lifted one, turned it against the lamplight, and held it there a beat too long.

"Longer," Beatrix murmured. "You'd hold it longer. Suspicion is patient."

"Suspicion is patient," he agreed, and put it back, and stamped the paper with an imaginary seal. "Pass."

She let out the breath she'd been holding since the pie story. "Again."

They ran it eleven times. By the seventh, he stopped going easy on her. By the ninth, she caught him trying to trip her with a question about harvest quotas and she answered it correctly, having memorized Harvest House Nine's actual ledgers, which she had also stolen, because thoroughness was the only religion she'd ever kept. By the eleventh, they were both grinning like idiots over a crate of lamp oil, and she had to look away first, because his grin did something to her chest that no amount of rehearsal could fix.

Then the grin faded, because the next part of the plan required looking at.

"The antechamber," she said quietly, and touched the map. "You."

"I open the ceremonial door." His voice went flat and official, the voice he used for confessions he hated giving. "Solstice midnight, I hold the honor watch outside the throne room. It's written into my reinstatement orders — Veyra assigned it herself, which means either she trusts me completely or she wants me standing exactly where she can see me when everything goes wrong. Possibly both. Veyra doesn't do anything with only one meaning."

"When you open the door—"

"I open it, and then I close it behind you, and I bar it from the inside. Which is treason. Openly, in front of the court, in white." He flexed his right hand, the one that had sworn oaths on. "There's no version where I keep my commission after that. I've made peace with it."

"Have you?"

"No," he said honestly. "But I've made an arrangement with it. We coexist."

She wanted to say something kind and couldn't find one that wasn't also a lie, so instead she said, "And the binding-memory. My part."

His jaw tightened. That was the part he hated — worse than the treason, worse than the door. "You step out of the line of clerks. In front of the entire court. And you steal the binding out of the crown while Veyra watches."

"While Veyra *realizes*, eventually, what she's watching. There's a delay. Theft looks like nothing, from the outside. It looks like a woman standing very still." She traced the throne room floor on the map, the great circle where the rite would happen. "The binding-memory anchors the crown to Aldric. It's the knot. Twenty years of knots, actually — renewed at every solstice ceremony, layered and layered. I don't untie it. Untying takes hours and cooperation. I take it. All of it, in one pull, the way you'd pull a thread and unravel a sweater."

"And what happens to the person holding twenty years of a crown's hunger?"

She didn't answer immediately, and he watched her not answer, and she watched him watch her, and the cellar got very quiet.

"We've been over this," she said finally.

"We've been over this badly. You keep saying 'I take it' as though taking is the end of the sentence. What happens *after* you take it? While the fire comes? Because the fire has to come through the foundry shaft, which means Vaskarath wakes, which means minutes — maybe less — and during those minutes you are standing in front of the court holding the entire appetite of the Emberbound in your bare hands."

"Yes."

"*Beatrix.*"

"It's the plan, Rafael. There isn't another plan. Believe me, I've audited the alternatives. They all balance to zero."

He turned away from the map and stood with his hands braced on the wall, shoulders rigid, and for a long moment the only sound was the lamp hissing. When he spoke again his voice was rough. "When I was twelve, my sister fell through the ice on the Corren millpond. I went in after her. The cold was — it wasn't pain, it was *instruction*. The water teaching my body how little it mattered. And the whole time, the only thing in my head was: I should have grabbed her hand before she fell. Everything after that is just arithmetic." He turned around. "I know how this ends for someone. I've done the arithmetic. I hate every sum."

"So do I." She crossed the cellar to him. "So here's my honest ledger, since we're apparently trading in honesty now, which I want noted is entirely your fault. Yes, holding the crown might unmake me. Might. Odile says willing hands change the equation — the burden only devours what resists it. Tam bought us the ritual with his mind because he resisted. I won't resist. I'll carry it the way you carry a hot coal — quickly, and to where it needs to burn."

"That's not comforting."

"It's not meant to comfort you. It's meant to be true. Comfort is what people trade when truth is too expensive." She took his hand — the oath hand — and turned it over, and traced the callus where his sword sat. "And here's the other entry. If I fall, Aldric still goes free, the crown still burns, and you still walk out of that room. That's not a cost. That's a *trade*, and it's a good one, and I've never once in my life made a bad trade."

"You made a bad trade the day you met me."

"Debatable. The returns have been unusual, but substantial."

* * *

They worked until the lamp burned low, and then they worked by memory, and then there was nothing left to rehearse and neither of them moved toward the stairs.

It was Rafael who finally said it, or started to. He was repacking the vial of Tam's memory into its padded box with the excessive care of a man avoiding eye contact, and he said, "At the door. Tomorrow night, when I open it. Before you go through."

"Mm?"

"I'll have about four seconds. Maybe five, if the court is slow. And I've been trying to work out what to do with them, and every option is either treasonous or insufficient, and I've decided—" He closed the box. Looked up. The lamplight caught the gray in his temple that hadn't been there a year ago. "I've decided to spend them on the truth. Since we're apparently rich in it tonight."

"Rafael."

"I love you." It came out steady, which surprised them both; she saw the surprise flicker across his face like a man who'd braced for a wave and found calm water. "I think I have since the second time we met, and the first time doesn't count because you were robbing me. I loved you when I took the ledger page. I loved you when I falsified a boy's registration and knew I'd hang for it someday. I loved you at Tallowbridge when you ran across the roofs and I stood in the smoke and felt *proud*, which is not a feeling inquisitors are issued. I have conducted myself for years like a man guarding a secret from his own arrest, and I'm done. Not because it's safe. Because if the solstice goes wrong, I refuse — I *refuse* — to have spent my four seconds on protocol."

The cellar was very quiet. Somewhere above them, the city settled and sighed, ten thousand people who didn't know that their whole rotten architecture was four days from a dragon.

Beatrix discovered that her hands, which had never once shaken over a lock or a ledger or a lie, were shaking now.

"You should know," she said, "that I've run the audit. On this. On you. Repeatedly. For years." She stepped closer. "And every time I try to write you down as a liability, the numbers come back wrong. Too high. And I'm very good with numbers, Rafael, so when the numbers are wrong, it's not the numbers." She reached up and laid her palm against his cheek, and he leaned into it with his eyes closing, like a man setting down a weight carried too long. "I love you. I love you in defiance of every rule I ever wrote for myself, and I've broken rules before, but never happily. You're the first theft I ever committed that I'd commit again."

"That's the least romantic declaration of love ever spoken."

"I'm a ledger-keeper. Take it or leave it."

He laughed — really laughed, helpless and bright, the sound of a man briefly forgetting he was a traitor — and then he kissed her, and it was not brief, and it was not careful, and it tasted like terrible tea and lamp oil and the end of pretending.

When they broke apart, her forehead against his, she whispered, "Four seconds. What will you spend them on?"

"Three words. I just spent the rehearsal. At the door, I'll spend the rest."

"Greedy."

"Treason makes a man bold."

* * *

He left first, as always — separate streets, separate shadows, the choreography of two people whose meeting was itself a crime. Beatrix stayed to bank the lamp and fold the map, and she was blowing out the flame when she heard it: a soft scrape at the cellar's outer hatch, deliberate, unhurried. Not the sound of a guild hunter. Guild hunters knocked things over.

She went cold, and put her hand on the knife she'd started carrying since the rooftops.

The hatch opened a hand's width. In the gap, pale in the last lamplight, was a face she knew from Rafael's locket — younger, sharper, with his same stubborn jaw.

"Are you Beatrix Ravensworth?" the girl whispered. "Rafael's sister? Because I've ridden nine days without stopping, and the men following me are half a day behind, and he told me — he *swore* — that if it ever went wrong, I should find you." Her voice cracked. "It went wrong."
