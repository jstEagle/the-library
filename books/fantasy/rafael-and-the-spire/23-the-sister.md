## 23. The Sister

The letter arrived at dawn, folded into thirds and sealed with the Chancellor's own wax—the black wax, not the red of ordinary correspondence. Red meant business. Black meant *be careful whom you let see you reading this*.

Rafael stood in his lodgings with the window cracked to the gray morning air and read it twice before his hands stopped shaking enough to hold it flat.

*Bring the girl to the Tallowbridge checkpoint by noon. Bring the confession-letter you have been so carefully not writing. She goes south. You stay. These are not negotiable, Inquisitor, but I am told gratitude is possible even in chains.*

Below the formal hand, in smaller script, almost an afterthought: *She asked for you by name this morning. It was the first thing she said all week.*

He burned the letter in the candle flame and watched the Chancellor's name curl into ash.

Then he sat down at his desk, took out a fresh sheet of paper, and began—at last—to write the letter he had been carrying in his chest for three years like a stone swallowed whole.

* * *

His sister's name was Liora, and she had been born arguing.

Not crying—arguing. Their mother swore that Liora had come into the world mid-sentence, already objecting to something, and she had never once stopped. When they were children she had argued with him about whose turn it was to pump the well water (hers, always hers, because Rafael was four years older and therefore "a gentleman," a title she deployed exclusively as a weapon). She had argued with the schoolmistress about the shape of history. She had argued, at fourteen, with a recruiting officer about the moral character of the Crown's service while Rafael stood behind her wishing the ground would open up and swallow them both, because the officer was his commanding officer and Liora did not care.

She was twenty-six now, and she had spent the last eleven days in Veyra's custody in a room with good light and better food and a lock on the outside of the door, and Rafael knew without asking that she had spent all eleven days arguing.

"You're late," she said, when the door opened.

"I'm exactly on time."

"You're eleven days late." She was sitting on the narrow bed with her back straight and her hands folded, and she looked—this was the part that hurt worse than anything—she looked *composed*. They had given her a plain gray dress and she had somehow made it look deliberate. Only her eyes gave her away: they went over him quickly, thoroughly, the way she checked a room for exits, and then softened for half a heartbeat before hardening again. "You look terrible."

"Thank you. You look like you've been holding court."

"I have. The guards love me. One of them is going to name his daughter after me." She stood. "Well? Are you here to get me out or to explain why I shouldn't be angry with you? Because only one of those is worth my time."

Rafael held up the papers. Travel writs, stamped twice, valid for passage to Merrow's Landing and the southern road beyond. Real ones—he had traded three favors and a debt of honor to a customs clerk with expensive taste in brandy to get them real. "South," he said. "Past the salt marshes. There's a widow in Merrow's Landing who owes me her son's life; she has a house with two rooms and a fig tree, and she has agreed to pretend you're her niece from the coast. Your name is Marta. You're quiet, you're dull, and you have no opinions whatsoever."

Liora stared at him. "No opinions."

"None. You find the weather agreeable and the fishing adequate."

"This is the cruelest thing you've ever said to me."

"It's the kindest lie anyone will tell you this year. Take it."

* * *

They walked out through the checkpoint together under a sky the color of old pewter, and Rafael kept waiting for the moment it would go wrong. He had planned for six different ways it could go wrong. Guards who wanted to search the luggage. A clerk whose stamp-hand didn't match. Veyra herself, appearing out of the gray like a spider descending on silk, wanting to watch him do it.

But the checkpoint was nearly empty. Word of the solstice ceremony had emptied half the Spire's outposts; every soldier with ambition was angling for duty in the capital, where the promotions would be handed out like bread. The guard who checked their papers barely looked up from his tea. The gate swung open onto the causeway and the marsh beyond, flat and silver and smelling of cold salt, and Liora walked through it, and nothing went wrong at all.

That, Rafael thought, was the most frightening thing that had happened all week.

They walked a quarter mile along the causeway before either of them spoke. The wind came off the water and pulled at Liora's hood. Somewhere out in the reeds a marsh bird made its idiotic honking call.

"Marta," Liora said finally. "Adequate fishing. No opinions."

"It's a role. You're capable of playing roles. I've seen you pretend to enjoy our aunt's cooking."

"Our aunt's cooking *was* enjoyable. That wasn't pretense, that was survival, there's a difference." She stopped walking. He heard her stop, and made himself keep walking three more paces before he turned around, because if he turned too fast she would see his face and Liora could read his face the way scholars read dead languages—fluently, and with annotations.

"Rafael." His name in her mouth had always had a particular weight, the weight of someone who had known him since before he learned to lie. "Look at me."

He looked at her.

"I'm not getting on that boat," she said.

"Yes, you are."

"I'm not getting on that boat until you tell me what it cost. Don't do the thing where you smile and say 'nothing.' You're doing the thing where you're about to smile and say 'nothing.' Stop it."

"I'm not smiling."

"You're preparing to smile. I can see the muscles gathering." She came closer, and now her voice dropped, lost its edge, became the voice she used maybe once a year, usually when he was sick or bleeding or both. "Eleven days in that room, brother mine. Do you know what I did with them? I thought. It turns out I hate thinking. And here is what I worked out: Veyra does not release hostages. Veyra *trades* them. So either you gave her something terrible, or you promised her something worse, and you are going to stand there in the wind looking noble and tall until you tell me which."

The marsh bird honked again, idiotically, encouragingly.

"Liora—"

"And don't call me Liora. My name is Marta. I find the weather agreeable."

Despite everything—a crack of laughter got out of him, brief and startled, like a bird itself escaping. "You'd have hated the south anyway. Nothing to argue with. The reeds just stand there taking it."

"The reeds are cowards," she agreed. Then, quietly: "Tell me."

So he told her. Not all of it—he would carry the rest himself, the way he carried everything—but enough. He told her that he had written a letter. That in the letter he confessed, in full and in his own hand, to falsifying a registration record during the hunt; to warning a target at Tallowbridge; to consorting with a known gifted operative of the Gilded Hollow. He told her that the letter was dated, signed, and sealed, and that it now sat in Veyra's strongbox, and that the day she chose to open it, Rafael Vale would go from the youngest commissioned inquisitor in the Spire's history to a traitor with a cell and a short future.

He did not mention Beatrix by name. He noticed himself not mentioning Beatrix by name, and understood that Liora noticed it too.

"So that's it," she said. "You bought me with yourself."

"I had a surplus of myself lying around. Barely using it."

"Rafael."

"They were going to move you into the harvest wing next month." He hadn't meant to say it. It came out low and flat, like a stone dropped down a well. "I saw the transfer order. I couldn't—" He stopped, recalibrated, found the inquisitor's voice, the one that reported facts. "The harvest wing is where they take people whose minds Veyra wants. What comes out the other side isn't—you wouldn't—" He gave up on the sentence entirely. "I wasn't going to let that happen. Whatever it cost. That's the whole of it."

Liora looked at him for a long time. Out past her shoulder the marsh stretched away to the sea, and the tide was turning, and somewhere in the reeds that ridiculous bird was still going, and Rafael thought: *if she cries, I will come apart entirely, and one of us has to hold together.*

Liora did not cry. Liora had never once done the expected thing.

Instead she hit him. Open-handed, on the arm, hard enough to sting through the coat.

"Ow—"

"That's for trading yourself like a sack of turnips." Another blow. "That's for eleven days of thinking." She grabbed the front of his coat with both fists then, and pressed her forehead against his chest, and stood there shaking—not crying, she would deny it forever, shaking with fury, she would insist to her dying day—and Rafael put his arms around his sister and held on and looked out over the silver marsh and let himself, for exactly ten seconds, be twenty-two years old again and powerless in a different way, when their parents died and he signed the enlistment papers because someone had to feed her, and she had stood in the doorway then too, arguing, telling him not to go, telling him the army would eat him alive, telling him—

"You have to get on the boat," he said into her hair.

"No."

"Liora."

"*Marta.* And no. We both go. We'll go east instead, over the mountains, nobody hunts anyone in the mountains, we can—"

"We can what? Live in a cave? Argue with goats?"

"Goats deserve arguing with. Someone has to hold them accountable." But her voice had gone thick, and she pulled back and wiped her face briskly with her sleeve as though clearing condensation from a window, an administrative act, nothing more. "You can't stay. You know you can't stay. She has your confession. The second you're inconvenient she'll use it."

"She'll use it whether I'm here or in the mountains. At least here I can control what she uses it *for*." He took her shoulders. Her shoulders were narrower than he remembered. Everything about her was slightly less than his memory of her, as if custody had been slowly sanding her down, and the thought made something in his chest go hot and dangerous. "Listen to me. There is something happening here—something bigger than Veyra, bigger than the hunt. By midsummer it will be finished, one way or the other. If it goes well, I burn that letter's usefulness to ash and walk away clean. If it goes badly—" He shrugged. "Then you'll want to be far away with a fig tree and adequate fishing, and you'll want to have left *before*, not after, because after is much harder to travel."

"What's happening at midsummer?"

"A ceremony."

"Rafael."

"A boring ceremony. Lots of incense. You'd hate it." He smiled at her, and this time he let the smile be real, and he watched her decide—watched the argument drain out of her posture by degrees, reluctantly, like water finding its level. She had never lost an argument in her life. But she knew, the way he knew, that some arguments are won only by conceding them, and this was hers to concede.

"If you die," she said, "I will be so angry with you."

"I know."

"Not grief. Grief is fine, grief is respectable. *Angry.* I will spend the rest of my life furious with you, and I will tell everyone, and I will ruin your posthumous reputation so thoroughly that historians will write disclaimers about you."

"That seems fair."

"And you'll write to me. Real letters, not those three-line things with the formal closing. Letters with gossip in them. If you can't produce gossip you are to invent it. I want to hear about people I've never met having problems I don't understand."

"Deal."

"And Rafael?" She reached up and straightened his collar, briskly, mothering him in the way she reserved exclusively for moments of maximum emotional danger. "Whoever it is—the one from the Hollow, the one you didn't mention—tell her that if she lets you die, I'll come back north, and I'm *much* harder to trade than you are. Nobody sensible trades me twice."

He laughed, and it hurt, laughing, the way deep cold hurts when feeling returns to frozen hands. "I'll pass that along."

"You won't. You'll be embarrassed. But you'll think of it at some crucial moment, and it'll make you braver, and that's what family is for." She stepped back, picked up her single bag—she had owned more than a single bag, eleven days ago; custody had reduced her to this—and squared those narrow shoulders. "Well. Adequate fishing awaits. Walk me to the boat, Inquisitor, and try to look like a man escorting his dull cousin Marta, who has no opinions."

"You have never once in your life looked like a woman with no opinions."

"For you? For freedom? I can act." She set off down the causeway, then paused. "The weather had better be agreeable, is all I'm saying. If the south has disagreeable weather I'm writing a letter of complaint to the Chancellor personally."

* * *

The boat was a coastal trader called the *Patient Wren*, bound for Merrow's Landing with a hold full of salt fish and dye-stuffs. Its captain was a broad, weathered woman named Osk who owed Rafael nothing and liked him less, which made her ideal—no loyalties to exploit, no debts to trace. She took Liora's papers, glanced at them, glanced at Liora, and grunted.

"Cousin?" she said.

"Niece," said Rafael.

"She argues?"

"Constantly," said Liora.

Osk nodded with what appeared to be approval. "Good. Quiet passengers make me nervous." She jerked her thumb toward the deck. "Go on, then. We sail with the tide."

There was the gangplank. Narrow, wet, sloping gently down toward everything he was choosing for her. Liora stood at its foot and did not move, and Rafael felt the moment gather itself like a wave.

"Walk me aboard," she said. "Cousins do that. Dull ones especially."

So he walked her up the plank, across the deck strewn with rope and salt-crusted crates, to the rail at the stern, where the harbor fell away below them in green-gray layers and the gulls wheeled screaming their eternal complaints. He put his hands on the rail beside hers. Their hands did not touch. Some things were easier for not touching.

"When I was seven," Liora said, to the water, "you told me Mother hadn't died, she'd just gone to live at the lighthouse. Because I kept asking. And I believed you for a whole year, and every night I waved at the lighthouse light, and you never once told me the truth, you just started waving too." She turned to look at him. Her eyes were dry and fierce and bottomless. "That's the best lie anyone ever told me. Whatever you're about to say now, make it that good."

Rafael looked at his little sister, who had been born arguing and would die objecting to something, and he lied beautifully, because it was the last gift he had to give her.

"I'll see you at Midwinter," he said. "Merrow's Landing. There's a festival—fires on the headland, everyone comes out. I'll be standing by the biggest one. You'll find me because I'll be the only person on the whole coast wearing a coat this ugly." He plucked at his gray inquisitor's wool. "And I'll bring gossip. Whole sacks of it. People you've never met, problems you won't understand. And you can argue with me about the quality of the figs, and I'll lose, because I always lose, and it will be an ordinary day in an ordinary winter, and none of this—" his voice caught, once, briefly, and he rode through it like a man fording a stream— "none of any of this will ever have mattered."

Liora studied him for a long moment. She was not fooled. She had never been fooled in her life; she had waved at a lighthouse for a year knowing, somewhere beneath believing, that her brother lied when the truth was unbearable, and loving him for the architecture of the lie.

"All right," she said softly. "Midwinter. Biggest fire. Ugly coat." She rose on her toes and kissed his cheek, quick and fierce as a strike. Then she stepped back, lifted her chin, and became Marta of the coast—shoulders slackened, opinions packed away, dullness assumed like a cloak. It was the finest performance Rafael had ever witnessed, and he included the entire court in that assessment.

"Fair winds, cousin," said Marta, in a voice like unsalted porridge.

"Safe roads, Marta."

He stood at the rail until the *Patient Wren* cleared the breakwater. He stood there as the sails filled and the hull leaned and the green water opened ahead of her. He stood there long past the point where standing there accomplished anything, one hand raised in a gesture that could have been waving and could have been hailing a coachman, until the ship shrank to a white sliver and the sliver to a suggestion and the suggestion to nothing at all, and the gulls wheeled, and the harbor resumed its indifferent business.

Then Rafael Vale, youngest commissioned inquisitor in the history of the Spire, holder of secrets, seller of his own confession, liar of lighthouse-quality lies, wiped his face once, efficiently, with his sleeve—an administrative act, nothing more—turned his collar up against the wind, and walked back toward the city where a war was waiting for him.

He was halfway up the causeway when he saw the boy.

A child, really—thin, ragged, crouched in the lee of the checkpoint wall with the hollow-eyed stillness of someone who had learned that stillness equals safety. As Rafael passed, the boy flinched and looked up, and for one unguarded instant his eyes flickered with a pale, shifting light, like coals breathed upon—the unmistakable shine of the gift, frightened and leaking, the boy too scared to even know he was showing it.

Two checkpoint guards were ten paces away, laughing over their tea, and one of them had a registration list folded in his pocket.

Rafael kept walking. Then stopped. Then, cursing silently, comprehensively, turned around and sat down on the cold stones beside the boy, and began, in his gentlest and most dangerous voice, to talk about the weather.

"Agreeable," he said. "Wouldn't you say? Very agreeable weather."
