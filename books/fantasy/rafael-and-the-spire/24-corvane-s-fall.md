## 24. Corvane's Fall

I had always imagined that if the Gilded Hollow ever fell, it would fall loudly — doors kicked in, glass singing across the counting floor, Corvane roaring some final grandiose nonsense about the guild's honor while inquisitors dragged him out by his beautiful coat.

Instead it fell on a Tuesday, at half past nine in the morning, while I was buying eggs.

I knew it first by the pigeons. The market square outside the tannery kept a permanent parliament of them, fat and insolent, and they went up all at once in a grey sheet, wheeling east away from Copperlane Street. Then came the sound underneath: boots. Not two pairs, not six. Dozens, moving in step, which no city watch had ever managed and which only one kind of soldier bothered to learn.

The egg-seller, a round woman named Pella who had sold me eggs for eleven years without once asking why I always paid in exact small coin, looked past my shoulder and went pale.

"Those are the white cloaks," she said.

"I see that," I said.

"You'd best be going, love."

"I'm buying eggs," I said, because panic is a luxury and I have never been able to afford it gracefully. "Two dozen."

"Beatrix." She pressed the basket into my hands and refused the coin, which frightened me more than the boots did. Pella had never once given me anything for free. Kindness from a stranger is how you know your face has changed.

* * *

I did not go toward Copperlane. That is the first thing they teach you in the Hollow — not lock-picking, not ledger-keeping, though Corvane liked to pretend those were the sacred arts. The first lesson is *never walk toward the fire*. So I walked away, unhurried, a woman with eggs and somewhere dull to be, and I took the long way around through the dye-works district where the air stinks of mordant enough to make any inquisitor's eyes water, and I climbed.

There is a roof above the tanner's drying yard from which you can see the whole frontage of the Gilded Hollow: the green door with its brass knocker shaped like a closed hand, the three tall windows of the archive floor, the chimney that vented nothing because we burned nothing we wanted to keep. I sat down behind the ridge of slate with my basket of eggs like the world's most domestic gargoyle, and I watched my home being taken apart with paperwork.

That was the worst of it. Not the white cloaks — there were perhaps forty, ringing the building in a neat perimeter, and a wagon with barred windows waiting at the curb. It was the clerks. Veyra had sent clerks. Grey-robed, ink-stained, methodical men and women who carried the archives out shelf by shelf and logged each box against a manifest before loading it, as though memory were grain and they were merely tallying the harvest. One of them wore spectacles and paused on the steps to adjust them, and I thought, absurdly, *he has a smudge on the left lens*, and then I thought about what was in those boxes, and the smudge stopped mattering.

Every box was a person. That was the truth of our trade that Corvane had spent thirty years dressing up in velvet language — *deposits*, *holdings*, *the client portfolio*. A widow's last afternoon with her husband. A sailor's reason for coming home. A boy's mother's face, traded once for a winter of bread and never redeemed, because he couldn't afford the buy-back and then he grew up and forgot he'd ever had it to lose. All of it stacked on a handcart now, wheeled past the closed-hand knocker by strangers with manifests.

And among it, filed under R under a false name in a series I had built myself, page forty-one of the deep register: my copy of the king's memory. Twenty years old. Aldric young and laughing at something a gardener said, and then flinching — flinching — when a servant lifted the crown-box too near his head.

I had broken my own rule for that memory. Never keep what you steal. I had told myself it was evidence. I had told myself a great many tidy things, and now every one of them was sitting in a government cart.

* * *

Corvane did not roar. That surprised me most of all.

They brought him out at midday, and I had a clean sightline from the roof, close enough to see the state of him. They had let him dress. He wore the dove-grey coat with the silver thread at the cuffs, the one he saved for clients he intended to ruin gently, and he walked down the steps of his own guild between two white cloaks with his hands unbound and his chin up, and he looked — there is no other word for it — *satisfied*.

I understood it a moment later, when the second wagon arrived.

It was not an arrest wagon. It was a carriage, black lacquer, the Chancellor's seal on the door, and out of it stepped a woman I recognized from Tam's stolen glimpse and from twenty years of whispered rumor: Lady Veyra's under-secretary, Mistress Hallowell, a narrow person with a voice like a filing drawer. She met Corvane at the bottom of the steps. They spoke for perhaps a minute. I could not hear a word of it, but I could read the shape of it the way I read ledgers, and the shape was: *terms honored, balance settled, thank you for your years of service.*

He had sold us. He had been selling us for years — I had learned that much in his study with the lamp turned low, the offer he'd made me, inform or be informed upon — and now the account was closed, and he was being paid out. The carriage would take him somewhere comfortable. The boxes on the cart would take everyone else somewhere else entirely.

He glanced up, just before they put him in the carriage. Straight at the roofline. At me, or at the place where a careful observer might have been.

Corvane had taught me the roof routes himself, back when I was fifteen and all elbows. He used to say I had the best head for heights of anyone he'd trained, and then he'd say it was wasted on a girl who insisted on keeping honest books. He looked at me for two seconds, maybe three. And then he inclined his head — slightly, ironically, the little bow he gave when a negotiation concluded exactly as he'd intended — and got into the carriage, and was gone.

I sat behind the slate until my legs went numb, doing arithmetic.

Forty white cloaks. Two wagons of archives. One carriage south. And somewhere in that inventory, catalogued by a clerk with a smudged lens, a memory with the King of Caelmor's face in it, entered under a name that Veyra's people would unpick in an afternoon, because I had built the false name myself and I knew its seams, and so would anyone who tortured the right archivist, and the Hollow's junior archivists were currently being loaded into the barred wagon.

By nightfall, Veyra would know I held the king's memory.

By midnight, she would know what it meant: that I had seen Aldric young and afraid, that I had dreamed his dream, that I was the one person in Caelmor outside the palace walls who had proof the crown had been eating him for twenty years.

She would not come for me with forty soldiers. She would come for me with something much worse.

She would come for me with an invitation.

* * *

I went to ground at Maren's, because Maren was retired, which meant paranoid, which meant her attic had three exits and none of them were doors.

She listened to my report lying flat on her back on the floorboards, staring at the rafters, hands folded on her stomach like a woman at her own funeral taking notes. When I finished she was quiet long enough that I counted the drips in the eaves. Eleven.

"They'll have the deep register," she said at last. "Series R?"

"Filed under Rosethorn, Merla."

"Your cipher or the house cipher?"

"Mine. House ciphers are compromised the moment anyone's arrested. Mine's layered — it'll take them a day, two at most, once they lean on Pell or young Oskar."

"Pell cracked inside an hour last time," Maren said. "Oskar will cry before they finish the introductions." She rolled onto her side and propped her head on her hand and looked at me with her milky, all-seeing stare. "So. By tomorrow the Chancellor holds twenty years of your trades, every client you ever touched, and one very particular deposit. What does she do with it?"

"She uses it," I said. "That's the whole point of seizing an archive. You don't burn a weapon."

"No," Maren agreed. "You aim it." She tapped the floorboard twice, thinking. "Here's what I can't decide, girl. Are you the blade or the hostage? Because a memory of the king, young and flinching — that's dangerous to the crown's story. But a thief who can prove she's held it, whose whole guild just folded, who's alone—" she spread her hands, "—that's not a target. That's bait. Or a lever. Depends what she wants moved."

"And what would she want moved?"

Maren smiled, and it was not a warm smile, but Maren's smiles never were; they were weather instruments. "What does she always want moved? Whoever's standing between her and the throne room." She sat up suddenly, joints cracking like green wood. "Rafael. Your inquisitor. If Veyra suspects him — and she'd be a fool not to, he's been rotting from the inside for weeks, anyone can smell it — then the fastest way to break him is you. And the fastest way to move *you* is everything you've ever stored coming back to bite you. Every client who ever sold you anything, summoned, questioned, shown their own signatures in your ledger. 'Did you know what the Ravensworth woman kept? Did you know she kept a piece of the king?'"

The eggs. I had completely forgotten the eggs. They were still in the basket by the trapdoor, two dozen of them, bought in another life that morning. I got up and put them in Maren's cold larder because my hands needed work and my head needed silence, and neither was getting either.

"Maren," I said, through the larder door. "If she summons them — the clients — some of them will talk. Some of them will say things I actually did. Things that are true and ugly."

"Of course they will."

"The boy with his mother's face. I held that deposit for six years and never told him I still had the copy I made for verification. House policy. He thinks it burned in a ledger fire in the spring. If Veyra shows it to him—"

"Then he gets his mother's face back," Maren said flatly, "in the worst possible room, at the worst possible price, and he will hate you for it, and he will be right to." She came and stood in the larder doorway, filling it, an old woman shaped like a barrel with a spyglass where the heart should be. "You want absolution, go to a temple. You want to survive the week, tell me the plan."

"There isn't one yet."

"Liar," she said fondly. "There's always a plan. Yours is just the kind you don't say aloud because saying it makes it treason instead of theft."

I closed the larder. The eggs sat in their straw, patient and blameless.

The plan, unsaid, was simple and impossible and had been assembling itself in me since the wagon left: the binding ceremony at solstice. Rafael inside the Spire. Tam's memory of the rite of unbinding folded into the lining of my coat. And now, added to the column like a figure I couldn't strike out: Veyra holding my entire past, ready to spend it wherever it cut deepest.

I had spent my life believing that a well-kept ledger was a kind of armor. Every entry true, every name coded, every debt balanced — and no one could hurt you, because no one knew where you lived.

Veyra had just bought the ledger.

* * *

The summons came the next evening, and it came exactly as Maren predicted: not with boots, but with cream paper.

A street child brought it to Maren's door — one of the runner network the Hollow kept for exactly this purpose, a sharp-faced girl of about ten named Wren who took payment in candied ginger and asked no questions, mostly because she couldn't read. She handed me the envelope and a ginger twist and was gone over the wall before I'd finished breaking the seal.

Cream paper. Real cream paper, wove, with the Chancellor's seal in green wax and no threat anywhere on the page. That was the thing about Veyra that Tam's stolen memory had taught me and that the letter confirmed: she never threatened. Threats admitted doubt. She simply described the future as though it were already furniture.

*Madam Ravensworth,* it began, *the Crown acknowledges with regret the regrettable dissolution of certain commercial arrangements in Copperlane. Certain items formerly in your professional custody have passed into the Crown's inventory. Among them, an item of particular historical interest, catalogued under the name Rosethorn.*

*You will appreciate that provenance matters in matters of state. The Crown invites you to present yourself at the Office of Records, Spire Court, on the day after tomorrow, at the tenth bell, to assist in verifying the authenticity of this item. Your cooperation will be noted favorably. Your absence will also be noted.*

*In service of order,*
*A. Veyra, Lord Chancellor*

I read it three times. Then I laughed — actually laughed, a short ugly sound that startled me out of my own chest — because of course. Of course. She wasn't going to arrest me. Arrest was for people whose guilt needed proving. She was inviting me to authenticate my own crime, in her own office, under her own seal, two days before the solstice — close enough to the ceremony that if I ran, she'd hunt me with the whole machinery of the hunt, and if I came, she'd have me inside the Spire, inside the web, exactly where she wanted me.

Or — and here was the thought that turned my laugh cold — exactly where *I* wanted to be. No infiltration required. No forged tithe-clerk's badge, no wagon of harvested minds to hide among. An invitation, cream paper, the door opened by her own hand.

Either she was handing me the keys to the throne room because she believed I was already beaten.

Or she knew precisely what I meant to do at the solstice, and the invitation was the trap, and the trap was the whole point.

Maren read the letter over my shoulder, lips moving. Then she took it, held it to the lamp, and tilted it, and there it was — faint as breath, a watermark in the weave that only showed at an angle: the sigil of the Emberbound, the crown's own mark, worked into the paper itself.

"She's not writing to you as Chancellor," Maren said quietly. "Look again. This letter came from *above* the Chancellor. From whatever wears the crown these days." She set it down on the table between us, and the lamplight made the watermark swim, and for a moment I swear the little woven shape seemed to turn its head. "It knows you have its king's memory, girl. And it wants you to bring it closer."

Outside, far off toward the harbor, a bell rang the ninth hour. Two days until the solstice. Somewhere beneath the old foundry, an ember-sleeping dragon's fire guttered lower by the hour. And on Maren's table, in cream paper and green wax, the crown of Caelmor had just asked me — politely, formally, in triplicate prose — to come home.

I reached out and turned the letter face-down.

"Well," I said. "I suppose I should pick out something to wear."
