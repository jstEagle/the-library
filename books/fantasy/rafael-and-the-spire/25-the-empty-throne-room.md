## 25. The Empty Throne Room

Aldric woke the way a man surfaces from deep water—slowly, painfully, with his ears ringing and no certainty of which way was up.

He was standing.

That was the first wrong thing. He had gone to sleep—he was almost sure he had gone to sleep—in his bed, in the royal apartments, beneath the tapestry of the Founding Hunt that his grandmother had woven and that he had never much liked because every figure in it looked constipated. He had blown out the candle himself. He remembered that. It was one of perhaps forty things he still owned outright, unopposed: blowing out his own candle.

And now it was morning—or something like morning, grey light seeping through the high windows of the throne room—and he was standing at the foot of the dais in his nightshirt, barefoot on cold stone, holding a document.

The document was heavy paper, sealed with the black wax of state edicts. His own signature sat at the bottom of it, neat and confident, in ink that was not yet entirely dry.

*By order of the King,* it began, *on the morrow of the solstice, all persons found to bear the gift shall be conveyed to the Spire for the Rite of Renewal, there to be given gladly to the—*

Aldric stopped reading. Not because he could not bear it, though he could not bear it, but because a sound had started behind his eyes: a low, patient hum, like a hive waking, like a wheel beginning to turn. The crown knew he was reading. The crown did not approve of him reading.

"Good morning to you too," Aldric said aloud.

His voice came out rusty. When did I last speak? he wondered. Truly speak, to someone, as myself? He cast back through the fog of the last months and found—a steward, nodding. A council meeting, during which he had agreed to things. A banquet where he had laughed at the Chancellor's jokes, and the laughter had felt like it belonged to a man wearing his face as a coat.

The hum deepened. Somewhere behind his thoughts, something vast and old shifted its weight, the way a dragon shifts in a cave, and Aldric felt the edges of himself go soft and grey at the borders.

*No,* he told it. *Not yet. Give me an hour. You always give me an hour after you've spent me.*

It was not true that it always gave him an hour. But bargaining with the crown was like bargaining with weather; you did it because the alternative was simply standing in the rain.

He looked down at the edict again. *Given gladly.* Whoever drafted this had a poet's cruelty. He thought of the word *treated*, which the criers used in the streets below—the city's polite word, the word that let mothers sleep. Treated. As if the gift were a fever. As if the people who carried it were ill rather than miraculous.

He folded the edict once, precisely, and slid it into the sleeve of his nightshirt. Evidence. He had begun collecting evidence the way other men collected sins: secretly, guiltily, without any clear plan for what to do with the hoard.

Then he noticed his hands.

There was blood under his fingernails. Dried, brown, worked into the quick of three fingers on his right hand. He raised them to his nose and smelled iron and something else—something sweetish and wrong, like fruit going bad in a closed room.

He stood very still for a long moment while the throne room held its breath around him.

*I don't know whose that is,* he thought, and the thought arrived with the terrible calm of a man reporting a fire in a house he has already stopped living in. *I don't know whose blood is under my nails, and I am the King of Vellamare, and I cannot even tell you what day it is.*

The hum behind his eyes purred, almost affectionately. *You are tired,* it said, in the voice that was not a voice, the voice like warm honey poured over cold stone. *Rest. Let me carry the morning.*

"That," said Aldric, "is how we got here."

But his knees were already bending, and the grey was already coming in from the edges of him like tide over sand, and the last thing he saw clearly was his own reflection in the polished floor: a tall man in a nightshirt, barefoot, bleeding slightly from one heel he had cut on the stairs and not noticed, looking less like a king than like a ghost rehearsing to be a king.

Then Aldric went down into the dark, and the crown put him on like a coat, and the King of Vellamare smiled at the empty room and rang for his steward.

* * *

He surfaced twice more before evening.

The first time was brief and humiliating: he came up inside his own body to find himself mid-audience, listening to a delegation of wool merchants complain about tariffs, and discovered that his mouth had been promising them a reduction he had no memory of agreeing to and no power to deliver. The crown, apparently, had opinions about tariffs. The crown, apparently, had opinions about everything now.

*Revoke it,* Aldric tried to say, from somewhere far beneath his own skull. *I beg you, revoke it, those men will bankrupt themselves—*

The crown soothed him the way one soothes a child at a funeral. *Later. Rest.*

The second surfacing came at dusk, and it was different.

It came slowly, gently, the way a fisherman sometimes draws a line in hopes the catch is asleep—and Aldric rose through the layers of himself cautiously, testing each one, keeping his breathing even, keeping his face slack, because he had learned—if learned was the right word for knowledge that arrived in fragments, between drownings—that the crown watched most closely when he struggled and grew bored when he was quiet.

He was alone in the throne room. That was unusual. Evening ceremonies had been suspended—no, not suspended, *prepared*. Tomorrow was the solstice. Tomorrow was the Rite of Renewal. He could feel it in the building itself: the Spire humming like a struck bell, every corridor tuned toward tomorrow, servants moving with the hushed urgency of people preparing a temple for a god.

Or a slaughterhouse, said a small stubborn voice in him that the crown had never quite managed to digest. Some part of Aldric had survived eleven years of this the way a stone survives a river: by being hard, and small, and patient.

He opened his eyes.

The throne room at dusk was the most beautiful lie in the kingdom. The architects had built it to make a man feel small in the presence of majesty, and it worked even on him, even now: the pillars rising like trees, the light falling in long amber bars, the throne itself on its dais of white stone, flanked by the banners of nine generations.

Eleven years ago, he had loved this room. He had been crowned in it at twenty-six, young and kind and terrified, and he had sworn oaths on it, and for the first few years he had come here in the evenings just to sit and feel the weight of the thing he'd inherited—not the crown on his head but the crown as an idea, the duty of it, the honor.

That was before he understood that the crown was not an idea.

He walked toward the dais. His legs obeyed him. That was another thing he had learned to check early, the way a sailor checks the rigging: hands, feet, voice. All his. For now.

The throne was gold and ironwood, ancient, ugly in the way that only very old beautiful things can be ugly—it had been repaired so many times across so many centuries that it was less a chair than a biography. And its right arm, if you knew, if someone had told you a lifetime ago when you were a boy exploring the palace with your cousin and gotten lost and hidden from a servant you feared—

Its right arm was hollow.

Aldric knelt beside the throne, and the gesture cost him something. A king does not kneel to his own seat. But then, he thought, wryly, I have done worse than kneel in this room. I have signed things kneeling, in every way but the visible one.

The panel in the arm's underside was loose. He worked it with a fingernail—the same hand, he noted distantly, that had borne the blood, though the nails were clean now; someone had washed them, or he had, or the crown had, and he chose not to follow that thought to wherever it led.

Inside the arm's hollow: darkness, and dust, and the faint smell of ironwood and old oil.

And paper.

His heart stumbled. He reached in carefully—there were things in there already. Three, four scraps, folded tight. With trembling fingers he drew them out into the amber light.

The oldest was his own handwriting, younger, firmer, from—gods, from six years ago? Seven? *Day 40 since I understood. I tried to refuse the edict on the northern levies. I do not remember signing it. If anyone finds these: I am in here. I am in here. I am in here.* Written three times, the way a drowning man writes.

Another scrap, newer, shakier: *Count the days yourself. Do not trust my count. I lose hours. Yesterday I lost a whole afternoon and woke with wax on my fingers and a name in my mouth I did not know. Find out whose name.*

And a third, barely legible, written in what might have been charcoal, in a hand that shook like a leaf: *If I ever stop leaving notes, assume the worst and act anyway.*

Aldric sat back on his heels in the empty throne room and laughed.

It was not a healthy laugh. It was the laugh of a man who has just discovered he has been writing letters to himself for seven years and hiding them in furniture, and who recognizes, with the clarity available only to the very exhausted, that this is either madness or the sanest thing he has ever done, and that the two categories have quietly merged sometime in the past decade and become indistinguishable.

"I am still here," he said to the empty room, testing the words. They sounded strange in his mouth. True words often did; lies fit so much more comfortably.

The crown stirred. He felt it turning its attention toward him like a great eye opening—not alarmed yet, merely curious, the way a cat is curious about a mouse that has stopped running. *What are you doing?* the hum asked, honey-warm. *Come. Rest. Tomorrow is a great day. Tomorrow we are renewed.*

Tomorrow we are fed, he translated silently. Tomorrow a hundred minds—gifted minds, stolen from their homes and carted here like grain—will be poured into you to keep your fire burning, and I will sit on this throne and smile while it happens, and I will not remember smiling.

Unless.

The thought came to him whole, sudden, the way hope always came to him—never gradually, never reasonably, but all at once, like a window thrown open in a sealed room.

*Unless someone burns you first.*

He had heard the whispers. Even drowned, even buried, some part of him heard everything: servants' gossip, guards' grumbling, the market rumors that drifted up the Spire like smoke. There was talk of a thief. Talk of a woman with clever hands and a ledger full of impossible things. Talk—this was newer, this had come in the last weeks, carried on the breath of frightened clerks—that someone was asking questions about the crown. About its name. About dragon-fire.

The crown despised those rumors. When they surfaced in council, Aldric could feel it bristle against the inside of his skull, could feel the hot animal anger of a thing that had been comfortable for eleven years and had just heard the word *knife*.

Which meant the rumors were true.

Aldric looked down at the scraps of paper in his hand—seven years of messages in bottles, thrown into the sea of furniture, none of them ever answered—and understood, with a calm that surprised him, that tonight he would write one more.

Not for himself. He was long past saving himself; whatever happened tomorrow, whether the thief succeeded or failed, the man who had been crowned at twenty-six was mostly gone, worn away like a coin passed through too many hands. He had made his peace with that in the small hours of some night he could no longer locate. Grief, he had found, was a country you could live in, if you built small houses.

This note was for whoever came after. For the thief, if she read it. For the historians, if there were historians. For his niece, gods willing, if she ever inherited a throne worth inheriting.

He needed ink. There was a writing desk behind the arras in the antechamber—the crown kept him from it usually, steered his feet elsewhere whenever he approached, but tonight the crown was drowsy with anticipation, drunk on tomorrow, its attention fixed on the great hall below where the harvested minds were being counted and crated like vintage wine.

He moved quietly. A king learns to move quietly in his own palace or he does not survive it; that was a lesson no tutor had taught him and the palace itself had provided free of charge.

The desk yielded ink, a pen, and a strip of good paper. He carried them back to the dais and sat on the steps of it—sat, actually sat, on the stone like a servant waiting for wages, because he would be damned if he wrote his last testament enthroned. The throne had taken enough from him. It would not get this.

He thought for a long time. Outside, the dusk deepened; the amber bars of light climbed the pillars and thinned to grey; somewhere below, the Spire went on humming its hungry hymn.

Then he wrote:

*I am still here. Burn it. Do not mourn me.*

He looked at the eight words. They seemed very small for what they cost. He considered adding more—explanations, apologies, the names of the edicts he had signed and the faces of the people they had broken—but his hand was beginning to shake, and the hum behind his eyes was sharpening from drowsy to attentive, and he had learned the hard arithmetic of his survivals: a short note finished was worth ten pages abandoned.

Besides, he thought, what else is there to say? Everything else is in the ledgers, if she finds them. Everything else is in the archives, in the witnesses, in the city itself. A king's confession is written in what he permitted. Mine is written in ash, if there is any justice, and I have stopped expecting justice and started hoping for thieves instead, which is either wisdom or surrender, and at my age I find I no longer care which.

One thing more, though. His hand hovered. Then, beneath the eight words, smaller, hurried:

*If you burn it, and I do not wake as myself—let them say I tried. Check the arm of the throne. Tell my story truly, not kindly. Truthfully. The truth is kinder than what they'll write.*

He folded the note small, the way he had folded the first one seven years ago, and knelt again at the throne's side, and slid it into the hollow arm with its brothers, and pressed the panel home until it clicked.

"There," he whispered. "Now I exist."

The crown woke fully.

He felt it happen—felt the attention swing around like a searchlight, felt the honey-voice turn to iron. *What did you hide?*

"Nothing," said Aldric, and was proud, distantly, of how steady his voice came out. "A prayer."

*Pray, then,* said the crown, and the grey came flooding in, and the last thing he felt with his own mind was his own hand—his hand, his choice—closing briefly around the arm of the throne, the hollow arm, the arm that held the only honest record of his reign.

*Hold on,* he told whoever might be listening—God, the thief, the future, the small stubborn undigested stone of himself. *Hold on. Help is coming. I can hear her footsteps in the rumors.*

Then the King of Vellamare stood, and straightened his nightshirt, and rang for his steward, and asked—in a voice smooth and pleasant and entirely foreign—to be dressed for the evening's rehearsal, as tomorrow he would be renewed.

Deep in the hollow arm of the throne, in the dark, eight words waited for morning like a coal that refused to go out.
