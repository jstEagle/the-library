## 26. Infiltration

The wagon smelled of onions.

That was the first thing Beatrix noticed, and she clung to it the way a drowning woman clings to driftwood—because if she thought about what else the wagon might smell of, beneath the onions, she would have to step out of it and walk home and let the crown keep eating kings until there were no kings left worth saving.

"Oi." The driver, a squat man with a lantern hooked to his belt and a face like a closed ledger, jerked his chin at her. "You're the new tithe clerk?"

"I'm the new tithe clerk," Beatrix said.

"You're late."

"The roads are long and my legs are short."

He squinted at her, decided this was either insolence or honesty, and apparently couldn't be bothered to determine which. "Papers."

She handed them over—the forged documents she'd traded her father's memory for, now bearing a second layer of forgery on top of the first, which felt rather like wearing a disguise over a disguise. Maren had done the final touches herself, hunched over the work by candlelight, muttering that Veyra's clerks checked seals but never ink-flow, because chancellors always assumed no one else understood quality.

The driver read slowly, lips moving. Beatrix waited, hands folded in her lap, heart performing what she could only describe as an aggressive percussion solo against her ribs.

*You have walked into burning buildings,* she told herself. *You have talked Corvane out of killing a client twice. You once convinced a customs officer that a crate of bottled luck was pickled eels.*

*And you vomited in an alley after the wagons came for Tam's friends,* said another voice, quieter. *Terror is not new to you. It's just usually better dressed.*

"Papers check," the driver said at last, handing them back. "Name?"

"Clerk Wren. Of the Tallowbridge office."

"Wren." He snorted. "They do love their bird names up top. Right then. Rules." He held up fingers as he counted. "One: don't touch the jars. Two: don't look at the jars. Three: if a jar starts glowing, you tell me, and we both pretend it isn't happening until someone whose job it is arrives to make it not happen."

"And what happens if I touch a jar?"

"Then you'll know things you didn't want to know, and so will everyone within ten feet of you, and the Spire has very strong opinions about unauthorized knowing." He flicked the reins. "Sit still, Clerk Wren. We've a queue ahead of us."

The wagon lurched forward. Around them, the night was full of other wagons—dozens of them, converging on the Spire from every road like iron filings drawn to a lodestone. Each one carried barrels, crates, and jars packed in straw, and each jar carried something that had once lived inside a person. A childhood. A wedding day. The taste of a particular bread on a particular morning. Veyra had spent twenty years harvesting minds, and tonight, on the longest night of the year, she meant to pour all of it into the crown at once.

Beatrix sat among the cargo with her hands folded and did not touch anything, and tried very hard not to think about how many people were in these jars, and failed, and made herself useful instead by being furious. Fury, she had learned, was a serviceable substitute for courage. It kept the knees from buckling. It gave the hands something to do besides shake.

*Hold on,* she thought at the jars, absurdly, uselessly. *I'm coming. Most of you won't even know I passed.*

It occurred to her, somewhere in the third mile, that this was the first time in her life she had entered a place intending to give something back.

Her whole career had been built on taking. Carefully, precisely, with ledgers and receipts and rules—but taking nonetheless. She had told herself for years that the Hollow was honest in its way, that memory traded freely was fair coin, that her rule—*never keep what you steal*—kept her clean. And then she had kept a copy of a king's memory, and one page had become two, and two had become a cellar full of research, and here she was, disguised as a functionary of the thing she hated, carrying nothing but herself and a plan with more holes than a moth-eaten coat.

Maren would have called it growth. Corvane would have called it treason. Both, she suspected, would have been right.

* * *

The Spire's outer gate was a mouth, and the queue of wagons fed into it slowly, one by one, while clerks in grey livery went barrel to barrel with brass instruments that clicked and hummed.

Beatrix had expected guards. What she found instead was worse: *bureaucracy*. Three checkpoints, two signatures, and a woman with a voice like wet gravel who asked, "Purpose of delivery?" in a tone suggesting that any answer other than the correct one would result in forms.

"Solstice tithe," Beatrix said. "Clerk Wren, Tallowbridge office."

"Tallowbridge." The gravel-voice woman consulted a list. "Your office sent forty-one jars last quarter. This manifest says sixty-three."

"We had a good year," Beatrix said, before her brain could stop her mouth.

A pause. Somewhere behind them, a jar glowed faintly amber, and its handler pretended very hard that it wasn't happening, per rule three.

"A good year," the woman repeated, in the tone of someone filing the phrase away for later examination. She stamped the manifest. "Antechamber nine. Unload, verify, and *wait*. Nobody wanders on ceremony night. If you get lost, stand still and a warden will find you. Wardens do not enjoy finding people."

"No one enjoys finding me," Beatrix agreed solemnly, and walked her handcart through the gate before the woman could decide whether that had been a joke.

Inside, the Spire was warm.

Not comfortably warm—*hungry* warm, the warmth of a beast's gullet, radiating down through stone that had been drinking heat for centuries. The palace hummed. That was the only word for it. Not sound exactly; it lived below hearing, in the teeth and the breastbone, a vibration like a struck bell that never quite finished ringing. Beatrix had felt the crown's hunger once before, secondhand, through a stolen memory. Feeling it firsthand was different. Feeling it firsthand was like standing next to an open furnace and realizing the furnace was *paying attention*.

*It knows I'm here,* she thought, and then corrected herself. No. It didn't know. It was vast and blind and ravenous, and she was one small mind among hundreds being carted toward it. To the crown she was not an intruder. She was *provisions*.

There was a horrible comfort in that. Things that see you as food rarely suspect you of ambition.

She followed the flow of grey-livered clerks down corridors lit by lamps that burned without flame—a gift-tithe of light, someone had told her once, harvested from a woman who could kindle fire with a word and who had sold the trick to pay a debt. The memory-trade was everywhere in this building, soaked into the walls. Every beautiful thing in the Spire had been paid for by someone's forgetting.

Antechamber nine was a vaulted room stacked with crates, where a bored supervisor pointed Beatrix at a manifest and told her to verify counts against labels. She did, moving methodically, buying time, mapping exits with her eyes while her hands checked numbers. Two doors: the main one back toward the delivery court, and a smaller one behind a hanging tapestry, guarded by a single soldier who was, blessedly, fighting sleep.

Through the smaller door, she could hear music. Distant, ceremonial, wrong—too many instruments playing too perfectly, the sound of a rehearsal conducted by something that did not understand joy but had studied it extensively.

Midnight was four hours off. The binding ceremony began at moonset. Rafael would open the ceremonial door on the east side when the bells rang the first hour past midnight, and she would need to be at the antechamber beside it—not this antechamber, the *other* one, the one reserved for the court—with a clerk's livery and a stolen binding-memory between her and discovery.

All she had to do was cross half the Spire without being seen, in a building where nobody was permitted to wander, on the one night of the year when the crown was awake enough to notice.

Beatrix checked a jar against its label, wrote a small tick on her manifest, and allowed herself exactly five seconds of despair.

Then she got back to work.

* * *

The problem, as it turned out, solved itself in the worst possible way.

An hour before midnight, one of the jars in antechamber nine began to glow. Properly glow—amber light leaking through the glass seams, pulsing gently, like a heartbeat made visible. Rule three activated. The handler announced it loudly. The supervisor arrived, frowned at it, and summoned a warden, and the warden arrived with two assistants and a wheeled frame, and the order went down the corridor:

"All tithe stock to the procession floor. Now. All clerks accompany your manifests. *Now.*"

Which meant that instead of sneaking anywhere, Beatrix simply walked, in the middle of a river of grey livery, pushing a handcart with sixty-three jars on it, directly toward the throne room's antechambers, and no one looked at her twice because everyone was too busy looking frightened.

The crown's hunger thickened as they went. By the time they reached the procession corridor, the hum had climbed out of her bones and into her ears, and she could feel the jars around her answering it—faint warmth blooming through the straw, dozens of stolen memories stirring like sleepers smelling breakfast. One of them, she realized with a lurch, might be Tam's. Might hold some scrap of the boy who had died sending her a map of this very building through a hole in his own mind.

*I'll carry it out,* she promised the straw. *Whatever's left. I'll carry it out or die trying, and I'd really prefer not to die trying, so let's all be brave and quiet.*

The procession floor opened around them like the inside of a bell.

It was a vast circular hall beneath the throne room itself, ringed with pillars, floored in pale stone shot through with veins of something dark that pulsed in time with the hum. Clerks arranged jars in concentric rings around a central dais where a mechanism waited—brass and black iron, shaped rather like a crown's shadow. Above, separated by a floor she could not see through, the court was gathering. She could hear them: silk, laughter, the tuned music again, closer now.

And there, along the eastern wall, stood a door.

She knew it instantly, though she'd only ever seen it in Tam's dying memory—tall, banded in white bronze, carved with dragons whose faces had been worn smooth by centuries of hands. The ceremonial door. Rafael's door. Beside it, flanked by honor guards, waiting in full inquisitor white—

Rafael.

Even across the hall, even in the wrong-colored light, she knew him the way you know your own handwriting. He stood very straight, hands clasped behind him, white cloak falling in perfect folds, face composed into the serene blankness the inquisitor corps drilled into its officers. He looked, from thirty feet away, like exactly what he was supposed to be: the crown's man, the hunt's blade, a wall between the court and everything it feared.

He did not look at her. He did not look at anyone. His eyes rested on the middle distance with the fixed calm of a man who has decided that if he lets himself see too much, he will not be able to do what comes next.

But as the clerks filed past, as Beatrix's handcart rattled over the pale stone, as she passed within fifteen feet of him with her heart hammering so hard she was certain the whole hall must hear it—he spoke. Quietly. To no one. To the air.

"The bells ring the first hour past midnight," he said, in the flat cadence of a guard announcing schedule to himself. "The door opens for the recessional hymn. Ninety seconds. Not one more."

Beatrix kept walking. Kept her eyes forward. Kept her face as blank as his.

"Ninety seconds," she murmured, barely a breath, as she passed. "I only need forty."

Something happened at the corner of his mouth. It was not quite a smile. It was the ghost of a smile, the memory of one, worn like a forged seal—and it was, she thought wildly, the most beautiful thing she had ever seen, because it meant he had heard, and he had not turned his head, and they had just committed treason together in front of forty witnesses and neither of them had broken stride.

Then the moment folded itself away, and she was Clerk Wren again, wheeling her jars toward her assigned ring, and he was the Inquisitor again, watching nothing, and between them stretched thirty feet of pale stone and the entire weight of the law.

* * *

They left her alone after that, which was the trouble with being invisible: eventually you had to *be* somewhere, alone, with nothing but your own thoughts for company, and Beatrix's thoughts on solstice night were poor company indeed.

She knelt beside her ring of jars under the pretext of checking seals, and let herself feel it—all of it. The fear, cold and specific, itemized: fear of the warden's rounds, fear of the humming floor, fear of the thing above them that ate kings. Fear of ninety seconds. Fear that forty wouldn't be enough. Fear, sharpest of all, of the part that came after the door—the theft itself, reaching into a binding-memory centuries deep, into a hunger older than the guild, older than the hunt, and tearing loose the one thread that held a king to his own ruin.

Maren had told her once, over cheap wine: *"Every thief thinks she's ready until the night she isn't. The good ones aren't the ones who aren't afraid. They're the ones who go anyway, and are afraid beautifully."*

"Well," Beatrix whispered to the jars, to the humming floor, to the dragon-carved door across the hall. "Let's find out how beautiful I can be."

Above them, muffled by stone, the music changed. Slower now. Richer. The sound of a court settling into its seats, of candles being lit, of a ceremony beginning to arrange itself around a hunger it did not understand it was feeding.

The bells would ring soon. The door would open for ninety seconds. And somewhere overhead, a king who did not remember his own name was being dressed in white and gold and led, gently, like a lamb, toward the thing that was eating him.

Beatrix straightened her clerk's collar, smoothed her forged papers one last time, and waited in the dark beneath the throne of Veyra's making—terrified, furious, certain, and ready—as the first bell began to swing.
