## 27. The Binding Ceremony

### Veyra

The throne room had been built to make people feel small, and Lord Chancellor Veyra considered this its finest feature.

She stood at the foot of the dais in robes of white and gold, watching the hundred chairs fill. They were plain chairs, deliberately so—oak, uncarved, arranged in a great crescent before the throne—and into each one the tithe-clerks were wheeling a harvested mind.

Not bodies. Bodies would have been simpler. Veyra had learned long ago that a body in a chair made courtiers look away, made them whisper about cruelty, made them think of themselves. But a mind in a jar—a glass vessel the size of a wine bottle, filled with pale silver light that drifted like smoke in still water—that was merely *administration*. The courtiers looked at the jars the way they looked at tax ledgers: with respect, mild boredom, and no guilt at all.

"Chancellor." A young clerk bowed, quill trembling. "Ninety-six vessels accounted for. Four remain in transit from the Spire vaults."

"Ninety-six will do," Veyra said, "if the last four are late."

"They're never late, my lady."

"No," she agreed. "They aren't. That is rather the point of them."

Above her, on the throne, King Aldric sat motionless beneath the Emberbound Crown. He had been motionless for three days now—the crown conserving him, the way a miser conserves coin, spending only what was needed to keep his chest rising. His eyes were open. They were fixed on the middle distance, on nothing, and if you watched very carefully you could see them tracking something that was not in the room at all.

*Soon,* Veyra thought at him, with something she almost permitted herself to call tenderness. *Soon you can rest.*

She had served four chancellors before serving the crown directly, and every one of them had governed by reacting—by patching leaks, by bribing feuds, by holding back a flood with their own shoulders until they drowned or retired. She had been twenty-three when she first heard it speak through Aldric's grandfather's mouth, and it had said to her, in a voice like banked coals: *Order is not found. Order is made. Give me minds, and I will give you peace.*

Thirty years later, there was peace. There were ledgers that balanced. There were no famines, no border wars, no riots in the capital for a generation. And all it cost was a hundred minds a year, poured like oil into an old, hungry thing, and the occasional hunt for the gifted whose loose, unregistered magic muddied the harvest.

The doors at the far end of the throne room boomed open, and the last four vessels arrived on a wheeled cart, escorted by six inquisitors in white.

And at the head of them, tall and straight in his ceremonial whites, walked Inquisitor Rafael Corvane-adjunct, sword at his hip, face composed into the particular blankness that Veyra had spent months teaching him to wear.

She smiled at him across the length of the room. He did not smile back. Good. She had never trusted men who smiled easily; they gave away too much for free.

"Position your escort along the eastern wall," she called to him. "When the rite begins, no one leaves."

"Yes, Chancellor," Rafael said, and his voice carried perfectly, and it told her nothing at all.

Veyra turned back to her jars, and did not let herself wonder why that felt like a door closing somewhere far away.

### Beatrix

The memory-tithe clerk's uniform itched.

Beatrix stood in the third row of clerks, gray robe, gray hood, brass badge stamped with the seal of the Office of Harvests, and she kept her eyes down and her breathing slow while a hundred harvested minds glowed softly around her like a field of cold stars.

She had counted the exits twice. Three: the great western doors, the small northern door behind the dais, and the floor-grate near the eastern wall that Maren's map swore opened onto the foundry shaft—if the grate still moved after two hundred years, and if what slept below it could be woken gently enough not to bring the whole Spire down on top of them. Two ifs. She'd survived worse odds. She'd also died in dreams over worse odds, which was perhaps not the comforting comparison she wanted.

*Rafael is here.* She could feel him without looking, the way you feel weather coming. Somewhere along the eastern wall, in white, playing a part so well that even she couldn't find the seams. Last night he had held her hands in a cellar that smelled of turnips and said, "When I seal the doors, don't look at me. If you look at me, I'll lose the thread." And she had said, "Then I won't look," and they had both known she was lying, because looking at him was the only thing keeping her spine straight.

A bell rang once, high and silver.

The court hushed. Veyra raised her arms, and the light in the hundred jars leaned toward her like grass toward sun.

"People of the realm," the Chancellor began, and her voice was warm, warm as bread, warm as a hearth, and Beatrix felt the old professional admiration rise in her despite everything—*she's good*, some buried guild-girl whispered, *she's better than Corvane ever was*—"we gather on the longest night to renew the oldest covenant. The crown has kept us. It has given us order where our fathers knew only chaos. Tonight we give back."

She lifted the first jar.

Beatrix watched the silver light inside it press against the glass, swirl, and thin—and understood, with a lurch, that whatever mind lived in that light knew exactly what was happening. Was watching the Chancellor lift it like a candle. Had maybe been someone's mother, someone's lock-picking teacher, someone's—

*Tam*, she thought, and her hands went cold under the gray sleeves. *Is Tam in one of these?*

She scanned the jars. She couldn't tell. Light was light. That was the horror of harvesting, the thing the guild had always refused to say aloud: once you took a memory whole, once you took the *person* out of the remembering, there was no label on the outside. Just shine.

"On this solstice," Veyra intoned, "I bind these offerings to the Emberbound, as my predecessors bound, as the covenant requires—"

*Now or never*, Beatrix thought, and began, very quietly, to work.

It was the hardest theft of her life, and she hadn't touched anything yet. The binding-memory wasn't in a jar. It wasn't anywhere. It was *everywhere*—woven through the room itself, through the stones and the banners and the very air, a great anchoring knot of ritual and repetition thirty years deep, renewed every solstice, tied between the crown and the man wearing it. To steal it she had to find the knot's loose end in a room full of a hundred screaming lights and a Chancellor who could feel gifted magic the way a spider feels a fly land on its web.

So she didn't reach for the knot. Not yet.

Instead she reached for the nearest jar—the one Veyra had set down on its stand, closest to the clerks' rows—and she stole, delicately, a single thread of its silver light. Just a taste. Just enough to see.

The world went wide.

She saw the throne room from above, from below, from inside the walls. She saw the binding-memory the way a fish sees water: a vast golden lattice, anchored at the crown, running down through the throne, through the floor, through the mountain itself, humming with every renewal it had ever drunk. And at its heart, wrapped around King Aldric like wire around a tree, the knot.

There he was, too. Inside the lattice. A thin, worn figure curled at the center of all that gold, arms over his head, enduring.

*I'm coming*, Beatrix thought at him, absurdly, knowing he couldn't hear. *Hold on.*

At the eastern wall, unremarked, a man in white shifted his weight half an inch toward the great western doors.

### Rafael

Rafael had imagined this moment perhaps four hundred times, and in none of those imaginings had his hands been steady. In the actual moment, to his surprise, they were.

That frightened him more than shaking would have.

He stood at parade rest against the eastern wall, six inquisitors flanking him, and watched Veyra pour the second jar into the channel cut into the dais steps. The silver light ran along the groove like water finding an old riverbed, up the steps, across the platform, and into the base of the throne, where it vanished with a sound he felt in his teeth rather than heard—a soft, satisfied *hum*, like a belly filling.

*You fed it*, he thought. *Every year. You signed the requisitions. You told yourself it was administration.* He had told himself the same things for eleven years, in smaller quantities. He and Veyra were not different animals. They were the same animal at different sizes, and today he intended to bite the hand that fed them both.

His sister was south by now. He had put her on the coastal road himself, three days ago, with a lie so beautiful he almost believed it: *I'll follow at week's end. Save me a seat by the window.* She had hugged him hard enough to crack a rib and believed him completely, and he intended to live long enough to be ashamed of it properly.

Across the room, in the third row of gray-hooded clerks, a head tilted a fraction to the left.

He didn't look. He had promised himself he wouldn't look. He looked anyway—he was only human, and besides, he had decided somewhere around midnight that promises to himself about Beatrix Ravensworth had a historical failure rate of one hundred percent—and found her hood tipped down, her posture slack, her lips barely moving.

Working. Already working. Of course she was. He had sealed the plan: *on my signal*. She had agreed. She had also, clearly, decided that signals were for people with time to spare.

*Fine*, he thought, and something in his chest that had been clenched since dawn loosened, just slightly. *Do it your way. You always were faster than the paperwork.*

Veyra lifted the tenth jar. The court murmured approval. Somewhere a child—some lordling's heir, brought to witness tradition—yawned.

Rafael moved his hand to his sword hilt. Not to draw. The pommel held the key to the western doors' locking mechanism—inquisitors' swords were keys; it was how you locked a riot *in*—and the mechanism worked both ways.

He thought of the boy from the wagon, the one who taught Beatrix locks, and the sound the wheels had made on cobblestones. He thought of his sister's laugh. He thought of a ledger page in handwriting he'd recognized a year ago and said nothing about, and how saying nothing had been the first betrayal, and how this—whatever happened in the next five minutes—would be the last one, the one that paid for all the others or drowned beside them.

Veyra raised the twentieth jar.

"Now," Rafael said—to himself, to the room, to no one—and turned the key.

The great western doors slammed shut with a boom that shook dust from the rafters. The iron bolts threw themselves home, one, two, three, a sound like the knuckles of a giant cracking. Every head in the throne room turned. Six inquisitors along the wall reached for their swords and found, thanks to a small modification Rafael had made at four in the morning, that their scabbards had welded themselves shut.

"What," said Lord Chancellor Veyra, in a voice of perfect, arctic calm, "is this?"

Rafael stepped away from the wall, out of line, alone in the open floor of the throne room, and drew his sword—not to fight, but because it was the only honest thing left in his hands.

"Treason, Chancellor," he said. "Mine. All mine. I'd advise everyone else to stay seated."

### Beatrix

The doors' boom rolled through the lattice like a stone dropped in a pond, and in the golden web Beatrix felt the knot *flinch*—felt thirty years of ritual tighten in sudden alarm around the king's throat.

No more delicacy, then.

She pulled back her hood, and the gray clerk's robe with it, and stepped out of the third row into the open space before the dais, and the entire court of the realm turned to look at her: a small woman in a stolen uniform with ink stains on her fingers and absolutely no weapon at all.

"Apologies for the interruption," she announced, in her best ledger-auditing voice, the one that made grown merchants apologize to *her*. "There appears to be an error in tonight's accounts."

Veyra stared at her. Around the room, a hundred jars flickered, sensing—Beatrix could feel them sensing—the gift unfolding off her like heat off a forge.

"You," Veyra said slowly. "The archivist. Corvane's little keeper of books." Something moved behind the Chancellor's eyes, fast and cold: recalculation. "You're the one who has the king's memory. Twenty years old. My agents catalogued it in the Hollow's seizure."

"You really should hire better cataloguers," Beatrix said. "It's a copy. The original's been in my head for months. Along with quite a lot of yours, actually—you'd be amazed what the Hollow's clients sell when they think no one's listening." This was a lie. Veyra's secrets were the one shelf in the city Beatrix had never managed to reach. But lies, her father had taught her, were just memories you installed in other people, and she planted this one deep and watched it take root in the Chancellor's suddenly careful expression.

Then she stopped talking, closed her eyes, and *took hold of the knot*.

The binding-memory came apart under her hands like a woven rope picked strand from strand. It fought. Saints, it fought—it was thirty years of ceremony, centuries of covenant, the accumulated weight of every oath ever sworn over that throne, and it did not want to be untied, it wanted to *tighten*, and it reached for her the way the crown's hunger reached for everything, offering, offering—

*Rule beside it*, something whispered in a voice like banked coals. *Remember everything. Keep every ledger ever written. Never lose a name, a face, a debt—*

"My father's face," Beatrix said out loud, though no one could hear her, though her mouth barely moved, "is worth more than your whole archive."

And she tore the first strand free.

The effect on the room was immediate and terrible. The golden lattice shuddered. On the throne, King Aldric's body jerked upright like a puppet yanked by its strings, and a sound came out of him—not words, not yet, just a raw, rusty *sound*, the noise of a door opening in a house long shut—and every jar in the crescent of chairs blazed silver at once, a hundred harvested minds crying out together.

"Stop her!" Veyra's composure cracked clean down the middle. "Inquisitors! *Stop her!*"

"I'm afraid they're indisposed," Rafael called pleasantly, standing alone amid six welded scabbards, sword bare and level. "Anyone else? No? Wonderful."

Two palace guards broke ranks anyway, because guards are paid to break ranks on command, and Rafael met them in the open floor, and Beatrix lost sight of him because she was elbow-deep in thirty years of stolen covenant, pulling strand after strand, and the knot was screaming now, a high thin note only she could hear, and the crown on Aldric's head was turning from ember-orange to a furious white—

And beneath the floor, faint but unmistakable, something vast turned over in its sleep.

Beatrix froze, one strand from freedom, and looked down at the stones.

The floor was warm. The floor was getting warmer. And through the grate by the eastern wall, deep down in the dark of the foundry shaft, an eye the size of a cart wheel opened, and a voice older than the Spire spoke upward through two hundred feet of rock, into her bones:

***Little thief. I smell fire that isn't mine. Shall we finish what was promised?***

"Oh," Beatrix breathed, gripping the last strand of the binding-memory with both hands as the crown shrieked and the court screamed and Rafael's blade flashed somewhere behind her. "Oh, *yes*."
