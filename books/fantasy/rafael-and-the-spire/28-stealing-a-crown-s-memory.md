## 28. Stealing a Crown's Memory

There is a moment—every thief knows it—when the lock stops being a lock and becomes a question you have already answered. The tumblers fall into place not because you forced them but because they were always going to. Beatrix had lived her whole life inside that moment.

She was living in it now, except the lock was a memory the size of a cathedral, and she was falling into it in front of three hundred courtiers, one traitor-inquisitor, and a king who had just remembered his own name.

The binding-memory opened around her like water closing over her head.

* * *

She fell through centuries.

Not walked—fell, the way you fall in dreams, except this dream had been built by something that did not sleep and did not forgive and had all the time in the world.

The first thing she saw was fire. Not dragon-fire—she knew that now, knew its color the way you know your mother's voice—but forge-fire, human fire, greedy and orange and impatient. Hands that were not hands shaped metal that was not quite metal. She felt the shape of a promise pass through the making: *we will keep your kings safe, and your kings will keep us.* A bargain struck between dragons and men, hammered into a circlet while it was still hot enough to scream.

It screamed. She heard it. The crown had been screaming for nine hundred years, and no one had ever once thought to listen.

*Oh,* Beatrix thought, in the small cold room of herself she kept for exactly this kind of horror. *Oh, you poor, terrible thing.*

Then the first king put it on, and she stopped feeling sorry for anyone, including herself.

* * *

His name was Aldous. Not *the* Aldric—Aldric came later, much later—but Aldous the Unifier, whose statues stood in four city squares with their noses chiseled off by history. Through his eyes she wore the crown for the first time, and it fit like a key fits a lock, and she understood with sickening clarity that a key does not care what it opens.

He had been a good man. That was the worst of it. He had wanted roads and granaries and an end to the border wars, and the crown had given him all of it, eagerly, the way a dog brings you things you never asked for. And when he hesitated over the burning of a rebellious province—the crown had offered, gently, helpfully, to carry that decision for him. Just this one. Just so he wouldn't have to feel it.

He let it.

And the crown took the feeling, folded it up, and ate it. And Aldous the Unifier went to bed lighter than he'd been in years, and woke up a little less himself, and by the time he died—old, celebrated, adored—there was perhaps a third of him left behind the eyes, watching from a great distance as his hands signed things and his mouth said things and none of it was his.

Beatrix had stolen memories for twenty years. She had seen grief, guilt, love gone rotten, whole childhoods traded away for luck. She had thought she understood hunger.

She had understood nothing. This was not hunger. This was *appetite*—patient, refined, almost loving. The crown did not devour its kings the way a wolf devours a lamb. It devoured them the way a devoted servant devours a master's life: gradually, gratefully, always insisting it was for their own good.

*They are so heavy,* the crown murmured, in a voice like warm ash. *Kings. So heavy. I carry them.*

*You eat them,* Beatrix answered, because apparently she could answer, because she was inside its memory now and memories talk back if you're gifted enough and foolish enough.

*I carry them,* it repeated, with the serene certainty of a thing that has never once been contradicted by anything it couldn't consume.

* * *

King after king after king.

She fell through them like pages. Marrec the Pious, who prayed for forgiveness every night for sins he could no longer remember committing, because the crown had eaten the sins and left only the guilt, distilled, like brandy. Sera the Bold, who fought it—actually fought it, for eleven years, waking each morning and saying her own name aloud before the crown could take it, and Beatrix loved her instantly and completely, and wept inside her own skull when the crown finally grew tired of the game and simply closed Sera's eyes one afternoon during council and never fully opened them again.

Each king, the same bargain. Each king, the same slow vanishing. And threaded through all of it, promises—hundreds of them, thousands, oaths sworn on the crown itself by lords and lovers and priests, and every single one broken, because a promise made to a thing that eats minds is just food wearing a ribbon.

*Do you see?* the crown asked her, somewhere around the fourth century, and its voice had grown almost tender. *I am the only one who remembers. They forget themselves. I keep them. Every king I have ever carried is still here, still mine, still safe. Would you like to meet them?*

And it opened its doors.

* * *

They were all there.

Every king, every queen consort who'd worn it at coronations out of tradition and lost a decade apiece, every regent and puppet and child-monarch. They stood in rows in a country made of ember-light, whole and smiling and hollow, preserved like insects in amber. Aldous waved at her. Sera did not wave; Sera's eyes tracked her, and in them was a flicker of something that had survived nine hundred years of being digested, and it looked at Beatrix with an expression she recognized from mirrors:

*Get out while you can.*

*This is mercy,* the crown said. *This is what I offer everyone I love.*

"You don't love," Beatrix said. "You collect."

*Is there a difference?* it asked, genuinely curious, and that—that was the moment she understood she was in real danger. Not because it was cruel. Because it wasn't. Cruelty you can fight. You cannot fight sincerity this large.

* * *

Then it turned to her, and the ember-country folded away, and it was just the two of them in the dark, and it made its offer.

*You are the finest thief it has ever been my fortune to host,* it said. *Do you know what I felt when you stepped into my binding? Recognition. We are colleagues, you and I. I take what people cannot bear to keep. You take what people cannot bear to lose. Between us we could empty the world of pain entirely.*

"Or fill it," Beatrix said. Her voice sounded far away, thin, a thread. "Mostly fill it."

*A matter of technique.* It laughed—a sound like a banked fire settling—and then, softly, the way a lover leans close: *Rule beside me.*

The words went through her like wine through water.

*Think of it. Veyra wants order—I can give her order, and she will be my hands for a decade or two until I grow bored, and you—* it paused, savoring— *you would be my memory. My true keeper. Every mind I harvest, stored safe in you instead of rotting in her vaults. Every stolen childhood returned to its owner whenever you please. You could give back everything, Beatrix Ravensworth. Everything anyone has ever lost. Your father's face—you sold that, didn't you? I have felt it in your thoughts since you entered. I could make it yours again. Perfectly. Forever. You would never lose another thing as long as you lived.*

*Remember everything,* it whispered. *That is all power is. That is all power has ever been.*

And here is the truth Silas Reyes will tell you, if you're reading this account, and I'll tell you now because someone should: the crown nearly had her.

Not with threats. Threats are easy; any thug with a cudgel can threaten. It nearly had her with *competence*. Nine hundred years of perfect record-keeping. Every grief catalogued, every loss reversible, every wound filed under a name and a date and waiting to be un-done. Beatrix had spent her whole life believing that precision was a virtue, that a well-kept ledger was a form of love, and here was the oldest, largest, most meticulous ledger in the world, opening its arms and saying *you belong with me.*

Her father's face. It held it up like a lantern. Young, laughing, flour on his knuckles from the morning bread, the exact face she'd traded in the undercity for forged papers and had told herself she'd remember anyway, and hadn't, not really, not the way it deserved—

She reached for it.

She actually reached. Somewhere outside—in the throne room, in the world where bodies mattered—her hand lifted toward the crown on the cushioned air before her, fingers spreading, and three hundred courtiers drew breath as one, and Rafael made a sound like a man being unmade.

And in the last inch before her fingertips touched gold, her father's face smiled at her.

Not the crown's copy. The copy was perfect, flawless, better than memory. But it smiled the way a portrait smiles, and her father had never smiled like a portrait. Her father had smiled crooked, late, always half a second after the joke landed, because he liked to pretend he hadn't found it funny and then fail. The crown's version smiled on time.

"That's not him," Beatrix said.

*It is better than him.*

"It's *tidier* than him." Her voice cracked and she let it. "He burned the bread twice a week. He sang wrong. He forgot my birthday one year and cried about it for a month, and I wouldn't trade that month for your entire collection, you great gilded *maggot*, because the crying was *his*, and the forgetting was *his*, and he was mine—not perfect, not filed, not *safe*—mine."

The lantern-face flickered.

*You would refuse permanence?*

"I refuse *you*." And she reached past the offered face, past the ember-country with its rows of hollow kings, past nine hundred years of patient appetite, and took hold of the binding-memory the way you take hold of a live wire—with both hands and no plan—and pulled.

* * *

Tearing free of a memory is nothing like tearing cloth. Cloth tears clean. This tore like a birth, like a tooth, like a root system ripped out of ground it had spent centuries deepening. The crown screamed—not the forge-scream of its making but a new sound, furious, betrayed, the scream of a collector watching its rarest piece walk out the door.

*YOU CANNOT KEEP WHAT IS MINE—*

"It was never yours!" She was falling upward, or rising downward, the geometry of memory collapsing around her. "None of them were! That's the whole lie, you understand? You never carried a single king in your existence. You *swallowed* them and called it carrying, and every one of them is still screaming in there, and I heard them, and I am going to burn this whole library down—"

*THEN BURN WITH IT.*

The binding-memory convulsed. And Beatrix, in the final instant before she tore loose, did the one thing no thief is supposed to do, the thing Corvane had beaten out of every apprentice, the rule above all rules:

She looked back.

She saw all of it, one last time, compressed into a heartbeat: the forge, the promise, Aldous waving, Sera's defiant flickering eyes, four centuries of swallowed men, and beneath it all—so faint she might have invented it—a small, exhausted voice that was not the crown's, saying *help us*, and she carved that voice into herself deeper than any scar, because she was going to need it for whatever came next—

—and the memory tore free.

* * *

The throne room slammed back into her like cold water.

She was on her knees on the marble, gasping, and in her hands—actual hands, actual marble, actual air—the binding-memory blazed like a coal torn from a hearth, a knot of golden light writhing between her palms, trying to crawl home to the crown.

Above her, on Aldric's head, the Emberbound shrieked.

Not whispered. Not murmured. *Shrieked*—a sound that was not entirely sound, that went through the teeth and the marrow, and every candle in the Spire guttered sideways at once, and the hundred harvested minds stacked in Veyra's tithe-wagons began to rattle against their glass like moths against a window.

Veyra was shouting something. Guards were moving. Somewhere to her left Rafael had drawn steel and planted himself between her and the room, white cloak snapping, and later—much later, in a shop near the harbor, over tea—he would tell her she had looked up at him in that moment and grinned like a woman who'd just picked the hardest lock of her career, which was true, and which he said was the single most terrifying thing he had ever seen.

But that was later.

Now, the binding-memory bucked in her grip. Now, the crown loosed from a king for the first time in nine hundred years turned its attention—all of it, the whole vast starving weight of it—away from Aldric and toward the room. Toward the courtiers frozen in their finery. Toward the nearest row of faces, mouths beginning to open in a scream that hadn't started yet.

Toward anything it could reach, now that its meal had been taken off the table.

"Oh no you don't," Beatrix breathed.

She got one knee under her. Then the other. The binding-memory burned between her palms like a heart that refused to stop beating, and the crown leaned out over the court, hungry, enormous, and Beatrix Ravensworth—thief, liar, keeper of impossible ledgers—rose to her feet between them.

The last dragon-fire was coming. She could feel it below the floor, a warmth rising through the stone like dawn through a cellar grate.

She only had to hold the line until it arrived.
