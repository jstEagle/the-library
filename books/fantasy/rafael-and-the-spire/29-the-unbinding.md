## 29. The Unbinding

The binding-memory came out of the crown like a tooth from a rotten jaw.

Beatrix felt it go—felt the whole centuries-long weight of it tear loose from Aldric's brow and pour through her hands, her arms, her chest, a river of other people's hungers trying to become hers. She had stolen memories before. She had stolen first kisses and deathbeds and one truly remarkable memory of a pie contest gone wrong. She had never stolen something that stole back.

She let it fall.

The memory hit the marble floor of the throne room and shattered into light—not clean light, but the greasy amber of old candleflame, of banked coals, of every fire that had ever been fed something it shouldn't have been fed. The court screamed. Half of them didn't know why they were screaming; they only knew that something had been holding their minds in a polite, velvet grip for years, and now the grip was opening.

And Aldric—

Aldric fell.

Not gracefully. Not like a king. He dropped off the throne steps like a man fainting at his own wedding, caught himself on the arm of a horrified duchess, and slid down to the marble on his knees. The crown sat crooked on his head, half-loosed, still gripping, its ember-light guttering like a lamp in a draft.

"Aldric," Beatrix said. She had crossed the room among the screaming clerks, her tithe-clerk disguise already shed somewhere around the moment she'd begun committing high treason in front of four hundred witnesses. "Aldric. Your name. Say your name."

He looked up at her, and she saw what twenty years of the Emberbound had left: a face like a house whose owner had been away so long the furniture was covered in sheets. But behind the sheets, something moved.

"My name," he whispered. His voice cracked on it, rusted through. "My name is—" He stopped. Frowned. Beatrix watched him reach for it the way a man reaches for a word on the tip of his tongue, except the word was *himself*.

"Take your time," she said, which was an absurd thing to say, because they did not have time. The crown was shrieking now—a sound less heard than felt, a frequency that lived in the teeth—and the amber light was spreading along the floor toward the assembled court like spilled honey with intentions.

"Aldric," he said at last. Just that. Then again, louder, wondering, like a man tasting water after years of drinking sand: "*Aldric.* I'm Aldric. I'm—" He laughed, a small broken huff of a laugh. "I'm hungry. Why am I hungry? I ate. When did I eat?"

"You're not hungry," Beatrix said. "That's not yours. That's the crown's."

He looked at the thing on his head as if noticing it for the first time. Perhaps he was. His hand came up, trembling, and touched the metal, and he flinched—the flinch she had seen in a dying woman's twenty-year-old memory, the young king flinching from his own coronation, and her heart broke cleanly in two, right down the ledger line.

"It's still on," he said. "Why is it still on? You were supposed to—"

"I know." Beatrix knelt beside him. The binding-memory was out; the anchor was cut. But a crown that has been drinking a king for twenty years does not simply accept unemployment. It had loosed one hand and clenched the other. "I need you to hold very still."

"That," said Aldric, with the ghost of dry humor surfacing through the ruin of him, "is what everyone says before something terrible happens to me."

* * *

Across the throne room, Rafael was discovering that treason is mostly paperwork done very fast.

He had sealed the doors. All six of them, iron-banded, ceremonial, designed to keep assassins out and courts in, and now keeping everyone exactly where the disaster was. Veyra stood at the center of the rite-circle with her arms raised, mid-incantation, watching her hundred harvested minds gutter in their vessels as the crown's hunger turned from ceremony to panic.

"Inquisitor!" she had shouted, when the doors boomed shut. Her voice had been perfectly calm. That was the worst part. Even now, even with everything burning, Veyra's voice sounded like a well-run office. "You will open those doors or you will die a traitor."

"Both, possibly," Rafael had said, drawing his sword, "but not quickly."

Now he stood between the panicking court and the throne, white cloak swirling, doing the one thing inquisitors were actually trained for: being a wall. Courtiers pressed against him, weeping, clawing. Somewhere a bishop was praying in what Rafael sincerely hoped was High Liturgical and not, as it sounded, a grocery list.

"Hold the line," he told the two palace guards who had, after visible soul-searching, decided that the man with the sword and the plan beat the woman with the incantation. "Nobody touches the queen's—nobody touches *anything* glowing."

Because the glow was the problem. The crown's amber light had reached the front rows of the court, and where it touched, people went quiet. Not dead—worse. Their eyes went soft and empty and grateful, mouths slightly open, leaning toward the throne like sunflowers toward a sun that ate them. An elderly countess was humming. Rafael recognized the tune with a lurch: it was a harvest-hymn, the one Veyra's clerks sang in the tithe-wagons.

It was feeding. Loosed from its single long meal, the Emberbound had discovered buffet service.

* * *

"It's eating them," Aldric said. He was on his feet now, swaying, both hands pressed to his temples as if he could hold his own skull together by willpower. "I can feel them. I could always feel them, I thought it was conscience—I thought I was *conscientious*—"

"You were," Beatrix said. She was circling him, reading the crown the way she read ledgers: line by line, looking for the entry that didn't balance. The binding-memory was torn free, yes. But the crown had grown roots. Twenty years of roots, threaded through a mind like ivy through a wall. Pull it now and the wall came down too. "Aldric, listen to me. I can't pull it off. If I pull it off, it takes you with it—all of you, everything, the whole house."

"Then leave it," he said. "Leave it and run. You've done enough. More than—" He stopped. Looked at her properly for the first time, this stranger in clerk's gray who had walked into his throne room and committed the largest theft in the history of thefts. "Who are you?"

"Beatrix Ravensworth," she said. "I audit things."

"Ah." A flicker of the young king from the memory, the kind one, the one who had flinched. "Then you know exactly how much trouble I'm in."

"To the copper," Beatrix said. "Which is why I'm not leaving. And neither are you. We're going to do this differently." She rolled up her sleeves. Her hands were steady. She noted, distantly, that they were steady, and filed that away as either courage or shock, to be reviewed later. "I can't pull the crown off without taking you apart. So I'm not going to pull it off. I'm going to take it *onto myself*. All of it. Every root, every hook, every year it spent inside your head. I'll carry it bare-handed until the fire comes, and then we burn the whole wretched thing and you get to be a man who owns exactly one head."

Aldric stared at her.

"That sounds," he said carefully, "like a terrible plan."

"It's my best one."

"How long have you had it?"

"Since about ten seconds ago."

"Ten seconds," the King of the Realm repeated. "You've been planning this for ten seconds."

"Nine of which were spent ruling out worse plans," Beatrix said. "There weren't any. Kneel down, Your Majesty. This next part requires trust, and I understand if that's difficult, given recent history."

To his eternal credit, Aldric knelt.

* * *

Rafael saw it happen across the length of the throne room: the thief kneeling before the king, the king bowing his crowned head to her like a penitent, her bare hands rising toward the ember-light. And he understood, in one cold drop falling through his stomach, exactly what she was about to do, because of course she was, because she had been planning it since the cellar, since Tam's last gift, since the words *whoever holds it may not survive sane*, and he had forbidden her, and she had kissed him and said *yes, dear* in the specific tone that meant the matter was closed.

"*Beatrix!*"

She didn't look up. Of course she didn't look up.

The crown screamed.

Not the tooth-ache frequency of before—this was a true scream, the sound of a thing that had been fed kings for three hundred years hearing the word *no*. The amber light flared white. Along the walls, the harvested minds in their glass vessels burst one after another like soap bubbles, and each released mind flew—not scattered, not freed, but *drawn*, streaming across the throne room in ribbons of pale light toward the crown, toward the vacancy it had just been offered, toward—

Toward Beatrix.

"No," Rafael said, and started running.

* * *

It was like catching a waterfall in cupped hands.

Beatrix had braced for pain. Pain would have been fine. Pain was familiar; pain had a shape; pain kept its own ledger. What actually happened was *hunger*—a hunger so vast it had architecture, corridors, a throne room of its own inside the metal, and she fell into it the moment her skin touched the crown's edge.

*Yes,* said the Emberbound, in a voice made of every king it had eaten, layered like sediment. *Yes. This. We have waited so long for a gifted hand. They gave us dull ones. They gave us obedient ones. But you—you could hold everything. Every memory ever traded in your little guild. Every secret in the city. We would show you your father's face. We would show you the day you lost it. We would give it back, and more, and more—*

Her father's face bloomed in her mind, warm and whole, laughing at some joke she couldn't quite hear, and it cost her everything she had not to reach for it.

"That's not him," she said through her teeth. "I know what he looks like. I carried him honestly. You're offering me a forgery."

*All memories are forgeries,* said the crown reasonably. *Yours included. Take the real thing. Take everything.*

"The real thing," Beatrix managed, hauling another ribbon of fleeing mind out of the air and into herself, feeling it settle into her chest like a swallowed coal, "is that you are a broken promise wearing jewelry. And I don't keep what I steal."

She pulled.

* * *

From the outside, it did not look like pulling. From the outside, it looked like a small woman kneeling in a lake of white fire, hair lifting, eyes wide and fixed, while the crown on the kneeling king's head began—slowly, joint by joint, like a crab leaving a shell—to turn toward her.

Aldric felt it go. Root by root. It hurt in ways he had no vocabulary for, because the crown had been so deep for so long that its extraction took pieces of him with it—twenty years of edicts signed by a hand that wasn't entirely his, mornings he couldn't account for, the taste of blood under his fingernails—but underneath the hurt was something so simple he nearly wept.

Quiet.

His own mind, making its own weather, for the first time in two decades. Small thoughts. Petty ones. He was cold. The marble was hard. He wanted tea. *Tea,* thought the King of the Realm, with tears running freely down his face, *I want tea with too much honey, the way my mother made it,* and the thought was his, only his, unmonitored, unwritten-upon, and it was the finest thought he had ever had.

The crown lifted free.

For one heartbeat it hung in the air between them, dripping light, and the whole court held its breath, and Beatrix—kneeling, shaking, her arms full of other people's minds and the coal of her own gift burning low—reached up and took it in her bare hands.

* * *

The silence lasted three seconds. Beatrix counted.

Then the Emberbound, finding itself held instead of holding, did what any cornered predator does.

It began to feed.

Not on her—she was full, she was a locked door, she was carrying it the way Maren had described, willingly, and willingness was the one lock it couldn't pick. So it went outward. The amber light poured off her hands and across the floor and up into the stands of the court, and people began to fall—not asleep, not dead, *emptied*, their faces going smooth as fresh paper. A young guard dropped his spear and smiled at nothing. The bishop stopped mid-grocery-list and sat down abruptly, folding his hands, patient as furniture.

"It's drinking them," Rafael shouted, cutting through the crowd toward her, shoulder-first. "Beatrix! How long can you hold it?"

"How long can you hold a flood with your body?" she called back. Her voice was steady. Her hands were not. The crown was heavy in a way weight had nothing to do with. "Ask me something easier!"

"Where's the dragon?"

"Under us! Odile promised—Vaskarath promised—" She gritted her teeth. Another ribbon of mind came flying at the crown and she caught it, barely, and stuffed it down into the furnace of herself. "It has to rise. It *said* it would rise. Dragons keep promises. That's the whole tragedy, apparently."

"Then we keep it alive until it gets here!" Rafael reached her side, planted himself between her and the court, sword out, back to her back—the same position they'd rehearsed in the cellar, except the cellar hadn't come with four hundred hostages and a chancellor. Where was Veyra?

Veyra was standing at the edge of the rite-circle, and she was not screaming, and she was not running. She was watching the crown in Beatrix's hands with an expression Beatrix would remember for the rest of her life, however much of that life remained: naked, grieving longing, the look of a woman watching her life's work walk out the door on someone else's arm.

"You don't understand what you're holding," Veyra said. Her voice carried over the chaos, calm as a ledger closing. "Without it, there is no order. There is only every grudge and grief and appetite in this city, loose at once. I built peace. You're unbuilding it."

"You built a cage," Beatrix said, "and called the bars *peace*, and fed the jailer on children. I've read your books, Chancellor. I audit things, remember?" She heaved against another surge of hunger; her vision whited at the edges. "Your arithmetic never balanced. It never once balanced."

Something moved beneath the floor.

At first Beatrix thought it was the crown's light playing tricks. Then the marble *ticked*—a sound like a giant knuckle cracking—and dust sifted down from the rafters, and every candle in the throne room leaned sideways at once, all in the same direction, as if the whole room had turned to look at something below.

The tick came again. Closer. Rhythmic.

A heartbeat.

"Oh," said Aldric, still on his knees, still weeping, still gloriously, ordinarily himself, staring at the floor with the wonder of a child. "Oh, I think your friend is coming."

* * *

Rafael felt it through the soles of his boots before anyone spoke: a heat rising through the stone, ancient and enormous, the warmth of a forge the size of a cathedral. The court felt it too. The emptied ones lifted their heads. The screaming ones stopped screaming. Four hundred people turned, as one, toward the great seal in the floor beneath the throne—the old foundry shaft, bricked over three hundred years ago, the one the histories said had been closed forever.

The bricks began to glow.

"Not yet," Beatrix whispered. The crown bucked in her hands like a landed fish, ravenous, dragging at her, and she could feel herself fraying at the edges—memories she hadn't offered starting to smolder, her mother's kitchen, the smell of the Hollow's candle-wax, Rafael's face in the cellar saying *I forbid you*—she held on. She held on the way she held ledgers: precisely, stubbornly, line by line. "Come on, old one. Come on. I'm holding the door. The least you can do is knock it down."

The floor exploded.

* * *

Light came first—not amber, not greedy, but gold, true gold, the color of the first fire anyone ever made on purpose—and with it a wind that smelled of hot stone and rain on far mountains, and through the smoke and the raining masonry rose the head of Vaskarath the Last, shedding three centuries of brick like a snake shedding a skin too small for it.

The dragon's eyes found Beatrix immediately. Found the crown in her scorched hands. Found her, kneeling, emptied almost to the wick, still holding.

*Little thief,* said a voice like a landslide deciding to be gentle. *You are smaller than you were in the vaults.*

"You're later than you were in the vaults," Beatrix gasped.

*I am old,* said Vaskarath. *Old things take the stairs slowly.* And the dragon drew breath—the last breath of the last fire in the world—and the light of it filled the throne room, and the crown in Beatrix's hands began, at long last, to be afraid.
