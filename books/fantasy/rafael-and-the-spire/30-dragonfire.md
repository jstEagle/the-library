## 30. Dragonfire

### Vaskarath

The dragon had been dreaming for a hundred years.

Not the dreams of men—men dream in pictures, small and greedy. Vaskarath dreamed in heat. It dreamed of the first morning of the world, when the mountains were still soft and the rivers ran bright as hammered copper. It dreamed of its brothers and sisters, whose names had been spoken so long ago that even the stone had forgotten them, and it dreamed—this was the newest dream, the one it liked least—of a promise.

*We will not make weapons of each other,* the promise had said. *Fire is for warming. Fire is for lighting. Fire is not for ruling.*

And then men had come with chisels and flattery and a crown-shaped hunger, and the promise had broken like an egg.

Now something knocked on the bottom of the world.

It was not a sound, exactly. It was a *tug*. Somewhere far above, through forty feet of drowned vault and old foundry stone and the marble bones of the Spire itself, a girl with clever hands was holding a burning thing bare-handed, and she was holding it *willingly*, and willingness—Vaskarath had learned this over centuries of bitter observation—was the only key that fit any lock worth opening.

The dragon opened one eye. The eye was the size of a cart wheel and the color of a banked hearth, and when it opened, the water in the flooded vault around it began, very politely, to boil.

*Well,* thought Vaskarath, in the way that dragons think, which is to say slowly, enormously, and with a great deal of judgment. *I did say I would.*

It had said it to Odile, who could not see but heard everything, and to Beatrix Ravensworth, who had brought it a memory freely given—the first honest gift it had received from a human since the promise broke—and to no one else, because there was no one else left to tell.

Vaskarath gathered its fire.

Understand what that means. A dragon's fire is not breath. It is not fuel. It is the dragon's *self*, pressed down into light, every year of its long life folded into a single flame. To give the last fire is to give everything: the first morning, the soft mountains, the names of the forgotten brothers and sisters. There would be nothing after. Not death—dragons do not die so much as they *finish*—but a kind of ending that made even Vaskarath, who had watched empires crumble with the mild interest of a cat watching rain, pause.

Then it remembered the crown. The Emberbound. The thing made from its stolen fire, wearing kings like coats, eating them from the inside out, century after century, while men called it order.

The pause ended.

Vaskarath rose.

Stone is very strong against almost everything except a dragon deciding to stand up. The foundry floor above the vault cracked along seams that had been waiting two hundred years for exactly this excuse. Forty feet of masonry lifted, groaned, and began to shed itself downward in slabs, the way a snake sheds skin if the snake were the size of a cathedral and extremely annoyed.

Above, in the throne room of the Spire, on the longest night of the year, three hundred courtiers felt the floor tremble beneath their silk shoes and assumed, being courtiers, that someone else was responsible.

They were about to be corrected.

### Rafael

Rafael had held a great many lines in his life. Lines at inspections, lines at executions he'd hated, the thin blue line of law that his instructors at the Inquisitors' College had described as though it were a physical object you could grip. He had believed in it once, the way you believe in a ladder without ever looking up to see what it's leaning against.

Tonight he gripped an actual line, and it was fire.

When the throne room floor split, the court screamed in the particular harmonized way that only people who have never been genuinely afraid can manage—a high, collective sound, like a choir discovering terror mid-hymn. Courtiers scattered. Guards scattered. Lord Chancellor Veyra, standing at the altar-stone with her hands full of harvested minds, did not scatter; she turned toward the crack in the floor with an expression Rafael would remember for the rest of his life, which was an expression of *betrayal*, as though the geology itself had failed to file the proper paperwork.

And through the crack came light.

Not the gold of the crown's hunger—that light pulled, it drank, it made the air feel borrowed. This light *gave*. It came up through the ruined floor in a column the width of a wagon and the color of dawn having an argument with noon, and in the middle of it, rising, shedding the Spire's foundations off its back like a man shrugging out of a wet coat, was a dragon.

Rafael had read about dragons. Everyone had. They occupied the same shelf of the mind as flood and famine—real in principle, unimaginable in practice. Nothing in the archives had prepared him for the *size*, or for the eyes, or for the way the great head swung across the panicked court with an expression that was not rage.

It was grief. Enormous, ancient, patient grief, the kind that has been keeping receipts for two hundred years.

"STAND," the dragon said, and the word arrived not through the ears but somewhere behind the sternum, and everyone stood, because some words are simply older than disobedience.

Rafael did not stand. Rafael ran—not away, but sideways, placing himself between the dragonfire's spreading edge and the figure kneeling at the center of the shattered dais with her arms wrapped around a screaming crown.

Beatrix looked up at him. Her hair had come loose. Her clerk's disguise was scorched at the sleeves. She was bleeding from both palms, and she was smiling, which frightened him more than the dragon.

"You're supposed to be guarding the king," she shouted over the roar.

"I resigned," he shouted back. "Verbally. Just now. To a dragon."

"That counts."

"It absolutely does not count, and I'll be hearing about it later." He planted himself in front of her, arms spread, white inquisitor's coat already smoking at the hem. Behind him the fire rolled forward in a slow tide, and here was the thing Rafael understood in his marrow, the thing no one had told him and he had somehow always known: the fire was not blind. It was *choosing*. It flowed around Aldric, who lay freed and gasping on the steps of his own throne. It curled away from Odile's apprentice's memory, still echoing faintly in the stones. It parted around Rafael like water around a stone it had decided, for reasons of its own, to respect.

But it would not part around the crown. And Beatrix was holding the crown.

So Rafael held the line. Between the court and the thief. Between the fire and the woman. Between everything that had been and everything that was about to be, with nothing but his body and a coat that was now officially ruined, and he discovered that this—this absurd, doomed, voluntary standing-in-the-way—was the only duty he had ever performed without a single doubt attached.

"Hurry," he said.

"I'm working," said Beatrix, "as fast as I can."

"You're always slow when it matters."

"And you're always *late*—" She gritted her teeth, and the crown shrieked, and the light came down like judgment, and whatever else either of them meant got said anyway, wordlessly, in the space between two people who had finally stopped lying to themselves if not yet to each other.

### Veyra

Regent-designate Ilse Veyra had planned for contingencies. She had planned for assassination, for coup, for the council turning on her, for the gifted rising, for the king dying inconveniently early or inconveniently late. She had a document for every disaster, filed and cross-referenced, because order was not a philosophy to her; it was a *religion*, and she was its most devout and most exhausted priest.

There was no document for a dragon.

She stood in the ruin of her ceremony—the rite half-spoken, the hundred harvested minds she had fed into the altar-stone scattering like startled birds, decades of careful work going up in a light that did not ask her permission—and she felt something she had not felt since childhood, when a fire had taken her family's home and she had resolved, standing in the ashes at age nine, that nothing would ever again happen to her unarranged.

It was happening to her unarranged.

"The crown is mine to renew," she said, to the dragon, to the room, to the universe, in the tone of a woman reading terms aloud before signing. "The hunt was necessary. The harvest was necessary. You cannot—do you understand what happens without it? Without *order*? People are memories, and memories are *chaos*, they lie and rot and contradict—"

The dragon looked at her.

That was all. It looked at her, with two hundred years of broken promises behind its gaze, and Veyra felt every harvested mind she had ever stored turn over in its sleep, and for one moment—one long, unbearable moment—she *felt them*, all of them, the poets and pickpockets and grandmothers whose minds she had filed away as raw material, and they were not chaos. They were people. They had been people the entire time.

"No," she whispered.

The fire took the altar-stone, and the ledgers bound inside it, and the ritual she had spent thirty years perfecting, and it did not take her, because the dragon judged—correctly, in the opinion of history—that living with what she had seen in that one moment would be punishment enough.

She sat down in the wreckage of her religion and did not speak again that night.

### Aldric

Aldric had been a passenger in his own body for eleven years. He knew the sensation intimately: the view from behind glass, the hands that moved without him, the edicts signed in his name by fingers that were technically his. He had learned to live in the small rooms of himself the crown had left unfurnished—a corner of a memory here, a stubborn scrap of stubbornness there.

When Beatrix tore the binding-memory free, the glass shattered.

He came back into himself the way a man surfaces from deep water—all at once, painfully, every joint of his soul aching—and the first thing he felt was the floor shaking, and the second thing he felt was *joy*, pure and stupid and undignified, because the shaking was not the crown. The shaking was the world, and the world was allowed to shake. He was allowed to shake.

He saw the dragon and laughed. Actually laughed, a cracked and rusty sound, his first laugh in years, while fire the color of forgiveness poured through his throne room and burned away the thing that had worn him like a glove.

He saw the inquisitor standing between the flames and the thief, arms wide, coat on fire at the edges, refusing with his whole stubborn body to move.

He saw Beatrix Ravensworth lift the burning crown above her head and *pull it into herself*—

—and Aldric, who had spent eleven years learning exactly what that hunger felt like from the inside, understood in one flash of terrible clarity what she was about to carry, and opened his mouth to shout *no, take it back, I'll hold it, I've held it before, I know how*—

—but the fire came down, and the crown ignited, and the world went white.

His last sight before the light took everything was the dragon's eye, vast and amber and unbearably kind, closing like a door at the end of a very long day.

*Thank you,* Aldric tried to say, to all of them, to the thief and the traitor-inquisitor and the ancient grieving fire. *Thank you. I am still here.*

Whether anyone heard it, he would never know. But he was still here. That, at least, the fire had guaranteed.

### Beatrix

Later—much later, in another life, in a shop near the harbor that sold ink and quiet—people would ask Beatrix what the last fire of the last dragon looked like, and she would struggle, because the honest answer was that it looked like everything.

It looked like her father's workshop, lamplit, the night he taught her that a lock is just a question asked impolitely. It looked like the Gilded Hollow's ledger-room, candle-gold, every impossible thing in its proper column. It looked like Rafael's face the first time he'd lied to protect her, badly, with visible effort, like a man carrying furniture. It looked like Tam's message arriving in her mind like a swallow through a window. It looked like morning. All mornings. The general concept of morning, distilled.

The fire came up through the floor and met the crown in her hands, and the crown—centuries of hunger, every king it ate, every promise it broke, the whole long cold ledger of it—*ignited*, and for one heartbeat the throne room of the Spire contained more light than had ever existed in one place at one time, and every person present would spend the rest of their life failing to describe it, and most of them would try anyway, at dinners, forever.

And Beatrix held on.

Because that was the bargain, and she had made it quietly, in cellars and rooftops and the privacy of her own stubborn heart, without telling Rafael, without telling anyone: the burden goes into a willing hand. The fire destroys the vessel. And whatever the vessel was holding when it dies goes *into the hand that holds it*.

The crown's hunger poured into her like a tide into a bottle.

She felt every king. She felt the first one, proud and terrified, feeling the crown settle onto his brow like a hand. She felt the promises break, one by one, each with its own small sound, like knuckles cracking. She felt the hunger itself—vast, cold, patient, *reasonable*, oh, it was reasonable, it made such good arguments—it offered her everything, it offered her memory without end, it offered her Rafael's every thought laid open like a book, it offered her the guild's secrets and the city's future and a throne beside it, and it said, in a voice like her own voice grown older and wiser and crueler:

*You have spent your whole life taking things. Take me. Keep me. You'll never lose anything again.*

And Beatrix Ravensworth, kneeling in dragonfire with her hands full of the end of an age, thought of her father's face—which she had traded away, and mourned, and mourned properly, and survived mourning—and she said:

"No. I keep my own."

And she let it burn.

The crown died screaming. The gift died with it—she felt it go, the old familiar sense of other minds as open doors, gone, closed, locked from the outside, leaving her standing in a house with only her own room in it—and the fire roared once, joyously, like a laugh at the end of a joke two hundred years in the telling, and went out.

All of it. Everywhere. Forever.

In the sudden dark, the only sounds were settling stone, weeping courtiers, and Rafael coughing smoke off his ruined coat.

"Well," said Beatrix, into the blackness. Her voice came out strange. Lighter. Emptied. "That's done, then."

She reached out in the dark for his hand, and for the first time in her life, she couldn't hear what he was thinking.

She found she didn't mind.

She found, in fact, that she minded so little that she started to laugh—helpless, scorched, ordinary laughter—and then the emptied dark tilted, and the last thing she knew was Rafael catching her, and the last thing she thought was:

*Oh. So this is what it feels like to be only myself.*

Then the floor of the world rose up gently to meet her, and Beatrix Ravensworth, keeper of the ledgers of the Gilded Hollow, thief of nothing anymore, fell asleep in the ruins of the Spire with a dead crown cooling in her lap and no idea at all what she had lost until she woke—

—if she woke—

—in the dark of the drowned vaults, days later, to a blind woman's hand on her forehead and a question she could not yet answer.
