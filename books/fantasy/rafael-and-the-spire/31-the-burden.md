## 31. The Burden

The crown was screaming.

Not with a mouth—it had no mouth—but with everything it had ever eaten, every king's stolen voice layered over every harvested mind, and the sound went through Beatrix's hands like cold water through cloth. She held it above her head as Vaskarath's fire roared up through the broken throne room floor, and she understood, in the last clear moment she would have for a long time, that the fire alone would not be enough.

The Emberbound had been made to survive fire. It had been *forged* in fire. It had drunk dragon-flame for six hundred years and called it nourishment.

What it could not survive—what nothing had ever taught it to survive—was being *held*. Willingly. Openly. By someone who did not flinch.

*Take me,* it whispered, and its whisper was honey poured over broken glass. *You have always wanted more. You have wanted it since you were nine years old and hungry in a doorway. I can give you your father back. I can give you every memory you ever sold, every one you ever stole, all of them, forever, arranged like jewels—*

"I know," Beatrix said aloud.

The court was screaming too, or some of it was; Rafael stood between her and the nobles who had not yet fled, white cloak singed at the hem, sword drawn against nothing he could cut. Aldric lay on the dais steps breathing like a man who had just remembered how. And the crown burned in her hands without burning her, because it was saving all its heat for the argument.

"You lie beautifully," she told it. "I know. It's what I do."

She had rehearsed this. Not here, not exactly—no one rehearses *this*—but in cellars and safehouses and the long sleepless hours before solstice, she had turned the problem over the way she turned lock-picks, feeling for the tumbler that would give. Tam's stolen memory had shown her the ritual of unbinding: *a gifted hand willing to take the crown's burden into itself while the fire destroys it.* Everyone had assumed the burden was weight. A thing you carried, like a sack of grain, until the fire finished its work.

It was not weight. It was hunger. The crown was not an object that made kings cruel; it was an appetite that wore gold so it could be fed by hand. To unbind it, someone had to open themselves wide enough to hold the whole appetite at once—to let it pour into them the way it had poured into Aldric for twenty years—and keep their own name while it tried on theirs.

Rafael had forbidden her. She had kissed him and lied about agreeing, which under the circumstances felt almost traditional.

So now, with the fire climbing the dais and the crown shrieking its offer into her bones, Beatrix Ravensworth did the only thing she had ever truly been good at.

She opened herself, and she took it.

---

There are no words for what came next, but she would spend years afterward trying to find some, so let us record the attempt.

It was like catching rain in cupped hands during a flood. It was like swallowing the sea. It was every ledger she had ever kept, every entry, every impossible thing—the first kiss traded for a debt forgiven, the mother's face sold to pay a gambling loss, the crime half-remembered and half-forgotten—all of it at once, unfiltered, unsorted, screaming to be catalogued. The crown's whole hoard came pouring through the door she opened, centuries of harvested minds, kings and clerks and children, and beneath all of it, older than any of it, the original wound: a promise between dragons and men, broken, hammered into gold, and left to starve.

*Hungry,* the crown said, in the voice of every king it had ever worn. *We are so hungry.*

"I know," Beatrix said again, and meant it differently this time. "I've been hungry too."

And that—that single, stupid, human sentence—was the tumbler that gave.

Because the crown had never once been answered with understanding. It had been fed, feared, worshipped, wielded. No one had ever looked into six hundred years of appetite and said *I recognize you.* For one flickering instant, the Emberbound hesitated, the way a wolf hesitates when the lamb does not run.

In that instant, the fire arrived.

Vaskarath's last flame came up through the floor like dawn coming through a keyhole—impossible, total, gentle somehow even in its violence—and Beatrix held the burning crown against her chest and let it happen. She felt the artifact die in stages. First the whispering stopped, which was worse than the screaming had been; then the weight changed, from the weight of a thing to the weight of a memory of a thing; then the gold itself softened, ran, became light, became heat, became nothing at all except warmth spreading through her ribs like tea on a winter morning.

And the gift—

She felt it go the way you feel a tooth come loose. Not pain, exactly. A strange loosening. All her life there had been a second sense running alongside the first, a quiet awareness of other people's memories like sounds through a wall—the shape of them, the texture, the little handles by which they could be lifted away. She had hated it and relied on it and built an entire self around managing it.

Now the wall simply stopped transmitting. Silence, where the neighbors had always been.

"No," she said, and was surprised to find she wasn't sure what she was refusing. The gift? The crown? The whole long story?

The warmth spread. The throne room tilted. The last thing she saw was Rafael turning, seeing her fall, and crossing the burning floor faster than any inquisitor should be able to run.

The last thing she heard was her own name in his voice—not Ravensworth, not clerk, not thief. Just *Beatrix*, said the way you say a thing you're afraid you'll never get to say again.

Then the dark took the ledger, and closed the book.

---

She dreamed, or something adjacent to dreaming.

She stood in the Gilded Hollow's archive, candlelit, shelves rising past sight, and every jar and bottle and sealed page bore her own handwriting. She began, out of habit, to catalogue. *One first kiss. One mother's face. One king, young and kind, already flinching.* But when she reached for the entries they crumbled into ash that smelled of cinnamon and hot metal, and the shelves receded from her fingers no matter how she stretched, and somewhere far off a voice that might have been Odile's said, kindly, *Let them go, girl. You've done your counting.*

"But I don't know who I am if I'm not counting," she told the dream, and the dream had the grace not to answer.

---

She woke to dripping water and the smell of stone and ember, and for a long, luxurious moment she did not open her eyes, because she was busy being astonished at how much it hurt to be alive. Everything hurt. Her hands especially, wrapped in linen bandages that someone had changed recently enough to smell of clean soap. Her hands, which had held a dying god of appetite, hurt like hands that had held a dying god of appetite.

"She's faking," said a voice near her feet. "Nobody sleeps that peacefully with burns like hers. Look at her ears. She's listening."

"Tam," said Odile's voice, further off, dry as old paper, "the last time you declared someone was faking, it was a corpse, and it ruined your boots."

"The corpse was faking," Tam said with dignity. "Right up until it wasn't."

Beatrix opened her eyes.

She was in the drowned vaults beneath the old foundry, in the warm antechamber beside the great sleeping dark where Vaskarath had settled after giving away its fire—a vast stillness now rather than a presence, like a banked hearth the size of a cathedral. Candlelight. Rough blankets. A basin of water. And Odile, blind eyes turned toward her with unnerving accuracy, sitting in a chair that had clearly been carried down here specifically for the purpose of sitting vigil.

"How long?" Beatrix rasped.

"Nine days." Odile poured water from a jug without looking, handed it toward her with the same unerring precision. "You slept like the dead and twice as smug about it."

"Nine—" Beatrix pushed herself up, and the room performed a slow, sickening rotation. "The court. Rafael. The king—"

"Alive, alive, and alive," Odile said. "Though the third one keeps sending letters down here addressed to 'the woman who saved me,' as though we're running a postal service in a cave. Your inquisitor has been down every day. He sits right where I'm sitting and asks questions of me that are obviously meant for you, and I answer them honestly, which drives him mad."

"Is he—"

"He's fine. Singed. Suspended, last I heard, pending inquiry, which he treats as a vacation." Odile's mouth curved. "He brought bread yesterday. Good bread. He's learning."

Beatrix drank the water. It was the best thing she had ever tasted, possibly because she couldn't remember tasting anything better, and the thought landed in her stomach like a dropped stone.

"Odile." Her voice came out very small. "My gift."

The old woman was quiet a moment. In the silence, Beatrix reached—out of habit, out of ninety years of guild instinct compressed into one lifetime—for the wall of other minds, the sound-through-stone, the handles.

Nothing.

She reached harder. Nothing. She reached the way she'd reached the night Corvane locked the archive doors, desperate and undignified, scrabbling at a wall that was no longer a wall but simply air, open air, ordinary air, air with nobody behind it.

"It's gone," she said. Not a question. An inventory.

"Gone with the crown," Odile said. "Fire takes what feeds it. The Emberbound grew fat on your kind's magic for six hundred years; when it died, it seems it took the local supply with it. Or perhaps just yours. Perhaps the fire needed a price and yours was the fairest one on offer." She shrugged, a rustling of old wool. "I'm a keeper, not a philosopher. What I know is this: you woke up. That was not guaranteed."

"Tam," Beatrix said suddenly, turning. "Tam, I saw—I thought I saw you, in the—"

"Alive," Tam said, and grinned, and his grin had a new scar over one eyebrow and a hollow look underneath it that hadn't been there before the Spire. "Mostly. Veyra got pieces of me before—" He stopped. Started again. "I sent you what mattered. Did it arrive?"

"It arrived." Beatrix's throat tightened. "It saved everything. Tam—"

"Don't." He held up a hand. "Don't do the guilt speech. I've had nine days to draft mine for letting myself get caught, and mine's better, so we'll call it even and neither of us delivers."

Despite everything—despite the emptiness where her gift had been, despite the ache in her hands—Beatrix laughed. It came out cracked and rusty, like a gate opening after a long winter, but it was real.

"There it is," Tam said, satisfied. "Knew it was in there."

* * *

They let her walk, on the fourth day awake, down to see the dragon.

Or what remained of the dragon, which was not a corpse—dragons of Vaskarath's age did not leave corpses, Odile explained, they left *monuments*. The great body lay coiled in the flooded dark, and it had gone still and dense and mineral, scales dulling slowly to something like basalt, wings folded into architecture. Already the vault stones seemed to lean toward it. In a century, Odile said, people would call it a rock formation and invent legends about why it was shaped like grief.

But when Beatrix stood at the edge of the ember-light, something moved in the deep of the monument. One eye, or the memory of an eye, opened a slit, and a voice like the last coal in a dead furnace spoke—not aloud, not in words, but in the way warmth speaks to cold skin.

*Little thief.*

"I'm not," Beatrix started, and stopped. There didn't seem to be much point lying to a creature that had watched her take a crown apart with her bare hands. "I was. I'm not anymore. I can't be. It's gone."

*Yes,* the ember-voice said, and there was something in it that she was slow to identify because she had never heard it directed at her before: tenderness. *You gave the fire a door, and the door was yourself. We do not forget such arithmetic. Neither should you.*

"Was it worth it?" she asked. "The fire. The last of it. For one crown, one kingdom, one—me."

The eye-slit regarded her for a long time. Water dripped somewhere in the dark, patient as a metronome.

*We broke a promise once,* Vaskarath said. *Men promised us remembrance, and we promised them restraint, and both sides cheated, and the cheating was hammered into gold and worn on the head of every liar's heir. Six hundred years I have slept beneath your city holding my anger like a coal. Tonight I am holding nothing. Do you understand? Nothing. It is the richest I have felt since the world was young.* The warmth pulsed, faintly amused. *You ask if it was worth it. Ask the stone whether it was worth becoming a mountain.*

Beatrix bowed—awkwardly, bandaged hands pressed together, the deepest gesture she knew—and the eye closed, and the monument breathed once, slow as geology, and was still.

* * *

Rafael came that evening.

She heard him before she saw him—boots on wet stone, the particular careful tread of a man who has learned that sudden movement frightens people who have been hunted—and then he was standing in the candlelight in plain clothes, no white, no insignia, looking at her like a man checking whether a house is still standing after a storm.

"Hello," he said.

"Hello," she said.

"You look terrible."

"You look worse. They suspended you?"

"Suspended, investigated, and formally thanked by a council that doesn't know what else to do with me." He sat down heavily on the chair Odile had vacated with suspicious tactfulness. "I refused the pardon, incidentally. The new one. Aldric offered."

"Why would you refuse a pardon?"

"Because I don't want to be pardoned for the things I actually did wrong." He said it lightly, but his hands didn't. "I falsified records. I ignored evidence. I fell in love with a suspect and let it compromise an investigation that ended with a hundred harvested minds scattered beyond recovery and a chancellor catatonic in a tower. A pardon says those things didn't happen. They happened. I'd rather carry them."

Beatrix looked at him for a long moment. "That may be the most honest thing anyone has ever said to me," she said. "Which is unfortunate timing, because I've lost the ability to check whether you mean it."

He went still. "How bad is it?"

"Bad. Good. Both." She flexed her bandaged hands, watching the candlelight move on the linen. "Do you know what I miss most? It's not the stealing. It's not even the—the hearing, the sense of everyone's memories humming behind their eyes. It's *certainty*. When someone lied to me, I knew. When a client was hiding something, I knew. I spent my whole life being certain, and I built myself out of it, and now—" She gestured at the empty air, at all that open, unguarded, ordinary air. "Now I'm just a woman in a cave who has to take people at their word."

"That sounds," Rafael said carefully, "like how the rest of us live."

"Yes," she said. "It's horrifying. I recommend against it."

He laughed—really laughed, the sound startled out of him—and then reached into his coat and set something on the blanket between them.

Her father's memory. The copy she'd traded in the undercity market, the one he'd returned to her once already, the small sealed vial with its faded guild-mark. Except the seal was broken now, and the vial was empty, and had been empty since the night in the cellar when they'd opened it together and watched a dead man teach his daughter to whittle, one last time.

"I kept the glass," Rafael said. "I don't know why. Seemed wrong to throw it away."

Beatrix picked it up. Turned it in the light. Inside, faintly, a ghost of lavender still clung—the scent of her father's workbench, which lived now nowhere in the world except her own ordinary, unenhanced, imperfect memory, already going soft at the edges the way all memories do, the way memories are supposed to.

"I can't feel it anymore," she said quietly. "When I held this before, I could feel the memory inside it like a bird in a cage. Now it's just—it's just a bottle."

"Yes."

"And I remember him anyway. Without the vial. Without the gift. Just... remembering. Like a person does."

"Yes," Rafael said again, softer.

She set the empty vial on the shelf beside Odile's candles, where it caught the light and threw a small bright fleck onto the stone ceiling, and found, to her considerable surprise, that her eyes were full and her chest was aching and none of it was grief, or none of it was *only* grief. It was the strange peace Odile had probably seen coming weeks ago, the peace of a woman who has put down something heavy and hasn't yet learned what her arms are for.

"I keep waiting to panic," she admitted. "About the emptiness. It hasn't come. I keep thinking it must be shock, it must be delayed, any minute now I'll understand what I've lost and come apart entirely. But instead I woke up this morning and ate porridge and listened to Tam complain about his boots, and I was—" She searched for the word and found it embarrassing and said it anyway. "*Content.* Is that allowed? After everything? Am I permitted to be content?"

"I think," Rafael said, taking her bandaged hand with a gentleness that would have shocked every inquisitor who ever trained him, "that you paid for it in advance."

They sat like that while the candles burned down, saying nothing, which was its own kind of conversation, and outside the vault the city went on with its enormous unfinished business of healing.

It was Tam who finally broke the quiet, poking his head around the curtain with the expression of a boy delivering excellent gossip.

"There's a crowd forming at the foundry gates," he reported. "Above ground. Hundreds of them. And—" he paused, savoring it, "—they're asking for you. By name. Beatrix Ravensworth. Something about the ledgers."

Beatrix frowned. "What ledgers?"

"The Hollow's," Tam said. "The ones that didn't burn. Someone's been telling people that the stored memories survived the raid—that everything Veyra seized, everything the guild ever bottled and sold and stole, is sitting in the archive vaults right now. And every person in this city who ever traded away a face or a first kiss or a childhood is standing outside the ruins of the Gilded Hollow," he spread his hands, grinning, "asking the famous memory-thief to give them back."

Beatrix looked at Rafael. Rafael looked at Beatrix. Somewhere in the dark behind them, the dragon-monument breathed its slow geological breath, and the empty vial threw its small bright fleck of light against the ceiling, like a point marked on a map.

"Well," she said, and stood up, and her legs held. "I suppose I'd better learn to keep promises the ordinary way."
