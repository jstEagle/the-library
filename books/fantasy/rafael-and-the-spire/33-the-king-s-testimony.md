## 33. The King's Testimony

The night before the council convened, I could not sleep.

This was not unusual. What was unusual was that I knew *why*.

For twenty years, insomnia had been a country I lived in without a map — waking at odd hours with my hands aching and no memory of gripping anything, standing in corridors whose purposes I had to guess at like a servant in his own house. But now the nights were simply nights. The dark was only dark. When I lay awake in it, it was because of ordinary human things: guilt, dread, and the peculiar terror of a man about to tell the truth for the first time since his coronation.

I got up. I lit no candles — old habit, though there was nothing left in me now that needed hiding from — and I walked the long gallery above the great hall where the council would assemble at dawn. My footsteps sounded strange to me. Unaccompanied. For two decades I had never once walked anywhere alone; the crown had been with me even when I could not feel its weight, whispering through my skull in a voice I had mistaken, God forgive me, for my own conscience.

Now the gallery held only me, the portraits of my ancestors, and the morning light just beginning to find the high windows.

I stopped before the portrait of my father. He looked stern and well-fed and entirely certain of himself, which is how kings are painted, and I found myself talking to him as I had not talked to anyone yet — not Rafael Voss, who had resigned his commission with the stiff dignity of a man cutting off his own hand; not the healers who came each week to ask gentle questions; not even the empty air, which had heard more of my confessions than any priest.

"You signed things too," I told him. "Everyone signs things. That is the whole disease of crowns. Someone hands you a document and tells you it is mercy, and your hand moves, and afterward everyone calls it law."

My father said nothing. He was better at silence than I had ever been.

Below me, in the hall, servants were setting out chairs — dozens of them, in a wide semicircle, because this would be no ordinary council. I had insisted on that. A king confesses differently when he is surrounded by faces instead of a table he can hide behind.

* * *

By the time the sun cleared the eastern towers, every seat was filled, and more than seats: the gallery above was crowded too, because word had traveled, as word does, and I had given orders that no one seeking entry was to be turned away. Merchants stood beside clerks. An old fishwife sat directly behind Lord Hallowmere, who kept glancing back at her basket with an expression suggesting he suspected it contained something subversive.

At the center of the semicircle, facing me, sat the delegates of the gifted — the ones the hunt had scattered and starved and registered like livestock. They had come because I asked. Some of them had come, I suspect, only to look at me, and I did not blame them. If a wolf invited the flock to watch him account for his teeth, the flock would want to count them itself.

In the front row sat a woman with grey-streaked hair and hands folded very still in her lap. Her name was Odile Vane. She had once served in this palace; she had been blinded, the records said, "in the course of her duties," which was a phrase I had signed off on seventeen years ago without ever asking what the duties were or why they required someone's eyes.

She could not see me. She had come anyway. I understood that better than anyone in the room.

"Let it be recorded," I began, and my voice cracked on the third word, so I stopped, and drank water, and began again, and this time the crack came later, which I counted as progress.

"Let it be recorded that Aldric, son of Maric, fourth of that name to sit this throne, stands before you today neither as sovereign nor as supplicant, but as a witness. For twenty-two years, an artifact called the Emberbound has been fastened to my brow. It ate my will by degrees, the way rot eats a beam — slowly enough that the house keeps standing while it hollows. Every edict I read to you now, I signed. Understand that first. My hand moved. My seal pressed the wax. And none of it — *none of it* — was mine."

I had rehearsed this part forty times. In rehearsal it had sounded noble. Standing up, with three hundred people watching me shake, it sounded like what it was: a man reading out his own crimes and pointing, feebly, at the thing on his head.

So I set the scroll aside.

"No," I said. "You didn't come for the formal version. Neither did I."

And I told it plainly. The edicts. All of them, in order, from memory — because I had spent the last weeks doing what the crown had forbidden me for twenty years, which is to say, *remembering*. The registration decree. The treatment decrees, with their soft clinical language about "restoring civic wholeness." The requisitions that emptied villages of people whose only crime was remembering too well. The warrant that had sent soldiers into a schoolhouse in Tallowbridge — I named the teacher. Elsbeth Corrin. I had never known her name until three days ago; the crown had seen to it that I learned names only when they were useful, and hers had become useful to me only now, when the least I could do was make three hundred people carry it home.

When I finished the list, the hall was so quiet I could hear the fishwife's basket settle.

"I am not going to stand here and tell you that the man who signed those papers was innocent," I said. "I don't know how much of him was left. That is a question I have asked myself every morning for five months, sitting in a chair by a window, and I have never gotten an answer I trust. Perhaps there wasn't enough of me left to resist. Perhaps there was, and I simply didn't. You are entitled to believe whichever answer lets you sleep. So am I, and some mornings I can't manage either one."

An elderly man in the second row of delegates raised his hand — actually raised it, as if we were all in a schoolroom, and something in the absurdity of that gesture broke the tension in the room like ice cracking off a pond. Several people laughed, briefly, kindly. I gestured for him to speak.

"Your Majesty," he said, "my daughter was taken in the second year of the hunt. They gave her family a receipt." He held up a yellowed paper. "A *receipt*. For her memories, itemized. Eleven items. I have read it every day since. I want to know — not whether you are sorry. Sorry is cheap. I want to know if you can give them back."

I had been waiting for exactly this question, and I was grateful to him for asking it so soon, because I had feared I would have to wait through hours of ceremony before anyone had the courage to cut straight to the bone.

"Some of them," I said. "Not all. Not most. I won't lie to you in a room full of people who have been lied to by their king for twenty years."

I nodded to the clerks along the wall, and they brought forward the crates.

Veyra's vaults beneath the Spire had yielded eleven thousand four hundred vessels — glass and lead and lacquered wood, each holding a harvested mind, each catalogued in her immaculate hand with the serial precision of a woman who believed she was building salvation and had merely built a larder. The healers and archivists had spent months learning to open them safely. It was slow work. Some vessels had degraded; some minds had frayed past mending; some, like Tam's — though I did not know his name yet, then — had been consumed entirely in the crown's final hunger, and no craft in the world would call them back.

But eleven thousand four hundred had been stored, and of those, the archivists judged that perhaps seven thousand could be returned intact.

Seven thousand. Out of everything she took.

"We begin today," I said. "Not by decree. A decree is a signature, and you have all had quite enough of my signatures. We begin by hand. Every vessel will be carried by a courier to the town it came from, matched against the receipt, and returned to the person who sold it or to their kin. The couriers will be paid from the treasury. They will answer to a commission of delegates chosen from among the gifted themselves, because I have learned — late, at ruinous cost — that nothing should be done *for* people by the institution that harmed them. The receipts will be burned publicly, one by one, as each return is made. I would burn them all at once, but—" I spread my hands. "It turns out I am no longer permitted to simply command things to be so. It is remarkably instructive."

That got a laugh — small, surprised, but real, and it loosened something in my chest that had been clenched since dawn. Gentle humor, I have found, is the only kind worth having after horror. The other kind curdles.

"The hunt is dissolved," I went on. "Not suspended — dissolved. Every registry is voided. Every record of who carries the gift is to be destroyed, publicly, in each district square, and I will attend the burning in the capital myself. No one will be 'treated.' No one will be registered. No one will be made to feel that their own mind is a crime requiring management. The gift is not a sickness. It never was. It was a door, and the crown was afraid of doors."

Odile Vane spoke then, without raising her hand, in a voice like dry leaves. "And the crown itself, Majesty? Where is it now?"

"Its ashes are in the flooded vaults beneath the old foundry," I said. "What is left of it sleeps with what is left of the dragon that unmade it. I have ordered the site sealed — not hidden, sealed, with a marker that tells the truth of what happened there, so that no chancellor a hundred years from now goes digging for it thinking herself cleverer than Veyra. Arrogance repeats itself unless you label the grave."

"And your pardon?" asked the man with the receipt. "You said pardons aren't enough. What are they, then?"

"Pardons are a beginning, not a repair," I said. "A pardon says: we will not punish you further for what we did to you. It is an apology shaped like paperwork. But I issue it nonetheless, formally, today, for every soul the hunt touched, living or dead — and for the dead, restitution to their families. And then I issue the harder thing." I drew a breath. "I dissolve the office of the Inquisition entire. Not reformed. Dissolved. Its archives open to public review. Its methods published. If a state is going to do terrible things, it should at least be obliged to display them, so that the next generation knows what to watch for."

Lord Hallowmere rose, purple-faced, and began, "Majesty, the security implications—"

"Sit down, Hallowmere."

He sat down. Twenty years of the crown had taught me exactly one royal skill that survived its removal, and it was the tone.

* * *

We worked through the afternoon. It was, I am told, the longest council session in the history of the kingdom, and certainly the strangest, because at some point past midday I realized that no one in the hall was pretending anymore — not the lords, not the delegates, not me. A fishwife corrected a magistrate's arithmetic about courier routes. A delegate from the river towns explained, patiently, why returning memories by wagon would take years, and proposed the relay system they eventually adopted. Hallowmere sulked, contributed nothing, and was twice caught taking notes that the clerk later confiscated as "unhelpful," which became a running joke in the galleries and, I am reliably informed, in the taverns by nightfall.

Toward evening, a girl of maybe twelve came up from the crowd — she had slipped past the stewards, who were too tired to stop her — and stood in front of me holding a small clay jar with both hands, the way children hold things that matter.

"This came back yesterday," she said. "From the vaults. It's my grandmother's laugh. She died in the third year of the hunt. My mother says we can't open it without the right person, and the commission says next month, and I wanted—" She stopped, gathered herself with tremendous dignity. "I wanted to know if it's really her. In there."

The hall had gone quiet again. Everyone was watching, including half the council, who pretended not to.

I crouched down to her height. My knees complained. Everything complains now; the crown took my certainty and left me with an old man's joints at fifty-one, and I have decided to consider this fair exchange.

"I don't know," I told her honestly. "The archivists say the good vessels keep what's inside them perfectly — every note of it. Whether it feels like her when it comes home, I can't promise. But here is what I can promise, and I have staked what's left of my name on it: no one will ever take it away again. Whatever happens next month, when that jar opens — that laugh belongs to your family. Forever. Not to the registry. Not to the vaults. To you."

She considered this with the gravity of a magistrate, then nodded once, sharply, satisfied with the terms of the treaty, and marched back to her mother carrying the jar like a standard.

Afterward, one of the clerks told me the girl's grandmother had been a singer. I wrote that down in my private book, because I have started keeping one — a plain ledger, my own hand, my own thoughts, nothing in it I would be ashamed to have read aloud. It holds small things. The girl's grandmother sang. The fishwife's name is Marta. Hallowmere's confiscated notes contained, in their entirety, the sentence *"this is madness"* written nine times with increasing pressure, the ninth nearly through the page.

History remembers edicts. I am beginning to think it should remember Marta's basket and a child holding a jar.

* * *

Late, when the hall had emptied and the light had gone amber and low, Odile Vane remained in her seat, hands still folded, face turned toward the dais with the terrible accuracy of the blind.

"Aldric," she said. Just the name, no title. It startled me and then, strangely, steadied me. "Come here where I can hear you properly."

I descended the steps like a schoolboy summoned, and stood before her.

"You said the crown ate your will," she said. "I believe you. But hear an old woman's arithmetic. For twenty-two years you wore that thing, and in twenty-two years you never once — not once — sent soldiers to the drowned vaults. Veyra knew about Vaskarath for thirty years. She never moved on it either, because the dragon terrified even her. But you — the crown whispered at you to hoard, to harvest, to hunt, and it never thought to send you below. Do you know why?"

"Tell me."

"Because somewhere under all that rot, some scrap of you already knew the fire was down there, and some scrap of you was keeping it safe without knowing you were keeping it safe." She smiled, thin and dry. "I watched that dragon sleep for forty years, Majesty. I know what guardians look like. They usually don't know what they are."

I knelt then — knelt, on the marble, in front of a blind washerwoman-turned-keeper, in an emptying hall — because there was no other posture that seemed adequate, and because my knees were going to complain regardless.

"What do I do now?" I asked her. It was not the question a king asks. It was the question I had been wanting to ask someone for five months.

"Now," she said, "you get up, you go back to that window chair of yours, and tomorrow you start signing the *other* kind of documents. Restitution orders. Land returns. School commissions. The boring paper, Aldric. Repair is ninety percent boredom. Miracles are the fun part, and the fun part's over." She patted the air vaguely near my shoulder and connected, to both our surprise. "And one more thing. There's a young woman who used to keep ledgers for the Hollow. Beatrix. She and the Voss boy opened a shop by the harbor. Ink and paper, they say. They refused your council's offer of a seat — good instinct, that — but they'll take visitors. Go see them sometime. Not as king. As a man who owes his mind to strangers."

"I intend to," I said, and found that I meant it with a completeness that surprised me.

* * *

That night I wrote in my plain book by candlelight, and this is what I wrote, and I set it down here because it belongs to the record as much as any edict:

*Today I confessed. Tomorrow the wagons roll. Seven thousand jars against eleven thousand four hundred taken — the arithmetic is shameful and will remain so for the rest of my life, and I decline to soften it. A kingdom is not repaired in a season. It is repaired the way a broken arm heals: splinted, ached over, useless for a while, then slowly, stubbornly, useful again — though the weather always knows where the break was.*

*I signed things for twenty years that were not mine. From tonight, everything under my hand is mine, and I will read every line.*

Then, because the book is mine and no one else's, I added the truest thing I know:

*The crown is ash. The fire is sleeping. Somewhere across the city, in a shop that smells of ink, two people who saved me are learning how to be ordinary, and I envy them, and I am grateful to them, and someday — when the wagons have rolled and the receipts are burned — I will walk down to the harbor without my guard, knock on their door like a neighbor, and thank them in person.*

*But that is a chapter for another season. Tonight there are seven thousand jars waiting, and a commission to appoint, and Hallowmere to endure, and somewhere in the southern provinces a courier is loading her first crate by lantern light.*

Tomorrow the wagons roll. Let it be recorded that I intend to be awake for every mile of it.

* * *

The first wagon left at dawn, as promised — and it had barely cleared the palace gates when a rider caught it at the bridge: a breathless clerk from the archive vaults, carrying a message that would reach me within the hour and change the shape of everything I had planned.

Among the eleven thousand four hundred vessels, deep in Veyra's most guarded cabinet, the archivists had found one that matched no receipt in existence. It was older than the hunt. Older than Veyra's cataloguing. And inside it, impossibly preserved, was a memory of the Emberbound's forging — a memory that predated the crown itself, and bore the mark of the guild that had supposedly kept no records of such things.

The Gilded Hollow had known all along. And someone had hidden the proof where only the honest would ever find it.
