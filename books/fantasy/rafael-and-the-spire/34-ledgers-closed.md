## 34. Ledgers Closed

The Gilded Hollow had always smelled of candlewax and old paper and, faintly, of the tangerine oil Corvane used on his boots—a smell I had once thought of as home. Now it smelled of ash and wet char and something underneath that I could not name until I stood in the doorway and did name it: absence. The Hollow smelled like a place where people had stopped being.

Three weeks since the Spire burned. Three weeks since the crown died in my hands and took my gift with it, the way a storm takes the roof off a house and leaves you staring at sky you'd forgotten was there.

I had come back twice before. The first time I'd stood outside and couldn't make my feet cross the threshold. The second time Rafael had come with me and stood beside me while I looked at the collapsed archive wing and said nothing, which was its own kind of gift—he had learned from me that silence can be carried into a room like an offering.

This time I came alone, with a hand-cart, three lanterns, a fire-poker, and every scrap of courage I had left, which by my honest accounting amounted to about enough for a Tuesday.

The front doors still hung on their hinges, one of them askew, its brass handle—the one shaped like a coiled serpent, polished by forty years of nervous clients gripping it—tarnished green. Inside, the great receiving hall was a ruin of fallen plaster and scorched paneling. The chandelier lay in the middle of the floor like the skeleton of some enormous, foolish bird. But the archive vault below had been built by paranoid men with deep pockets, and though Veyra's soldiers had emptied it—had taken every stored memory, every bottled recollection, every ledger they could carry—the ledgers themselves were too many, too heavy, too tedious to move all at once. Some remained, stacked in the flooded lower gallery, swollen with water, their ink bleeding but legible.

Legible meant useful. Useful meant dangerous. Every page recorded a trade: what memory, whose, sold by whom, held where. Even with Veyra catatonic and her harvest scattered, those pages were a map to every stolen thing in this city—and there were people who would burn the world down for such a map, and other people who would burn the world down to keep it buried.

I had spent two nights deciding what to do, and the answer had arrived not as a decision but as a feeling, the way true answers usually do: like a key turning in a lock I hadn't known was mine.

We would give them back.

* * *

Maren came at dawn, because I had asked her to, and because Maren had never once in thirty years refused a request that involved either arson or bookkeeping, preferably both.

"Ruined," she pronounced, standing in the receiving hall with her hands on her hips, surveying the wreckage like a woman inspecting a disappointing pie. "And to think Corvane made us dust the serpent handle daily. 'First impressions, Beatrix.' That's what he'd say. First impressions are everything." She spat, with feeling, onto the fallen chandelier. "Well. His last impression is soot."

"Maren."

"I'm allowed. He handed Tam's name to Veyra's people and he'd have handed yours too if you'd been worth more than his own skin." She said it plainly, without heat, the way you state that water is wet or that kings flinch. Then she softened—not much, Maren's softening was measured in millimeters—and bumped her shoulder against mine. "You're allowed to grieve the building, though. Buildings didn't do anything wrong."

"That's why I'm here." I set the hand-cart down. "The ledgers in the lower gallery. We're going to burn them. Page by page."

She looked at me for a long moment. Maren had been a thief-archivist before I was born; she understood ledgers the way priests understand scripture, and better, because at least scripture admits it's a story.

"You want to free them," she said slowly. "The stored ones."

"Veyra's people took most of the bottles. What's left in the vault flooded—the vials cracked, and whatever they held seeped out months ago. Gone, or scattered, or—" I shook my head. "But the ledgers aren't just records, Maren. You know that better than anyone. A ledger written in guild-ink holds an echo of the trade. It's how we authenticated. How we verified provenance. Each page is anchored to the memory it describes. Burn the page properly—with intent, with release—and the anchor breaks, and whatever fragment still clings to the ink goes home."

"Guild theory," Maren said. "Old guild theory. Nobody's tested it in decades, because nobody ever wanted to *give anything back.*"

"Somebody's testing it today."

"And if it doesn't work? If we burn sixty years of records and all we get is smoke?"

"Then we get smoke," I said. "Smoke is honest. Smoke doesn't sell anybody's mother."

Maren grinned then, sudden and crooked, and I saw the girl she must have been, the one who'd stolen a queen's ring off a sleeping duchess and replaced it with a radish just to see if anyone would notice. "I'll get the lamp-oil," she said. "You get the pages dry. And Beatrix—if any of Corvane's private ledgers are down there, I get to burn those myself. Personally. With ceremony."

"There are four of them."

"Oh," she breathed, "oh, this is going to be the best day of my retirement."

* * *

It took us two days.

We worked by lantern-light in the flooded gallery, laying the swollen pages out on racks Maren improvised from broken shelving, drying them over braziers until the ink steadied, sorting them into piles. Trade-ledgers. Client-registers. Storage-manifests. And Corvane's private books—slim, black, bound in leather soft as skin—which I could not bring myself to open again and did not have to, because Maren read each entry aloud in a flat, terrible voice, cataloguing his betrayals the way a physician catalogs wounds: *name, price, date delivered to Veyra's office.*

There were ninety-one names.

I wrote them all down on a fresh sheet of plain paper—not in guild-ink, never in guild-ink again—in ordinary iron-gall, the ink of grocers and schoolteachers. Ninety-one names of people who had trusted the Hollow with the most fragile things they owned. When the ledgers burned, I would take that list to every door in the city, and I would tell the truth, and I would let them hate me if hate was what they needed. It was the least of the least I owed.

On the second evening, Rafael came.

He didn't announce himself; he simply appeared at the top of the gallery stairs, out of his inquisitor white forever, dressed instead in a dockworker's coat that fit him badly, carrying a bundle under one arm and a look on his face that I had learned to translate. The look said: *I argued with myself the whole way here and lost.*

"You're burning them," he said. Not a question. Word travels fast when half the city watched a dragon rise through the palace floor.

"We're freeing them. Burning is just the method."

He came down the stairs and crouched beside our drying racks, and studied a page—entry 4,417, *one first kiss, aged seventeen, traded for debt-forgiveness, quality: pristine*—and his jaw did the thing it did when he was swallowing words he'd decided weren't useful.

"My sister wrote," he said finally. "From the south. She says the roads are full of strange weather. People stopping mid-stride and laughing, or crying, or both. A baker in Marrowfield who suddenly remembered his dead wife's voice and had to sit down in the flour." He glanced up at me. "That was before you started. That was just the crown dying. Everything Veyra fed it—it didn't digest. It's coming back on its own, in fragments, like—"

"Like a tide going out and leaving things on the sand," I said.

"Exactly like that." He set his bundle on the rack. Bread, cheese, a bottle of the cheap red wine he pretended not to like. "Then I won't keep you from your work. But I'll help. If helping means feeding a fire, I should warn you I'm extremely qualified."

"You were an inquisitor."

"I burned evidence weekly," he said solemnly. "It was in the job description. Page three."

Despite everything—despite the ashes and the ninety-one names and the hollow where my gift used to be—I laughed. It startled me. I think it startled him more.

* * *

We began at midnight, because guild tradition held that trades happened at midnight and un-trades ought to as well, and because Maren insisted on drama and none of us were strong enough to deny her.

The first page was entry number one in the oldest surviving ledger: a memory of a garden, traded fifty-two years ago by a woman who had needed money for a funeral and could not bear to attend it grieving. I read the entry aloud, as the old rite required—or as Maren claimed the old rite required, and honestly at that point she was the closest thing to authority remaining—and then I touched the page to the flame and spoke the words I had decided on, the only words that felt true:

"This was never ours. Go home."

The page caught. And I will not pretend I saw anything grand—not at first. It burned like paper burns, orange and ordinary, and I felt the disappointment settle in my chest like cold rain.

Then the smoke moved.

Not drifted—*moved*, deliberately, curling upward past the broken ceiling, through the shattered skylight, out into the night air above the Hollow, and as it went I felt something leave too. Something in the room lightened, the way a room lightens when a weeping guest finally stops apologizing and lets themselves be walked home. My gift was gone—I couldn't touch it, couldn't taste another mind if my life depended on it—but I felt the leaving anyway, in the ordinary animal way you feel a door open somewhere in a large house.

Maren grabbed my arm hard enough to bruise. "Did you feel—"

"Yes."

"It *went* somewhere."

"It went home," I said.

We burned all night. Entry after entry. A mother's face. A crime half-remembered. A soldier's last morning of peace. A lullaby in a language the seller no longer spoke. A man's certainty that his brother loved him—traded away drunk, mourned sober for twenty years. Rafael read the entries in his careful court voice; Maren fed the fire with the reverence of a woman who had waited her whole career for permission to commit beautiful arson; and I spoke the words, page after page, until my throat was raw.

*This was never ours. Go home.*

Somewhere past the third hour I realized I was crying, and that I had been crying for some time, and that for once the tears weren't grief wearing a disguise. They were just tears. I let them fall into the fire-warmth and kept reading.

* * *

By dawn the word had spread. Of course it had. The Hollow's chimney—the great stone stack that had vented the smell of candlewax for sixty years—was pouring silver-grey smoke in spirals that no wind could explain, and smoke like that draws a crowd the way honey draws flies.

They gathered in the street outside. Quietly at first. A fishwife I recognized from ledger entries. An old schoolmaster. Two sisters who had once traded a quarrel between them—one keeping the apology, one keeping the anger—and who now stood holding hands like children. A young man in a courier's uniform who pressed against the ruined doors and whispered, "Is it true? Is it true things are coming back?"

And then, around the hour when the sun cleared the rooftops, it began.

The fishwife sat down abruptly on a mounting-block and put both hands over her mouth. "Her face," she said through her fingers. "Oh—oh, I sold her face and I'd forgotten I'd even done it, I thought I'd simply *forgotten*—she's there, she's right there—" She was laughing and sobbing in equal measure, and the crowd drew back to give her room, and then, one by one, they stopped drawing back, because it was happening to all of them.

A man dropped his basket. A woman cried out a name—just a name, over and over, like a bell. The schoolmaster stood very straight with his eyes shut and his lips moving, counting, perhaps, or praying, or reciting something he'd sold decades ago and gotten back whole. The two sisters turned and looked at each other and burst out laughing at exactly the same moment, at exactly the same joke, a joke that had been missing between them for eleven years.

Rafael stood beside me in the doorway, and I felt him go still.

"The baker's wife," he murmured. "The flour. It wasn't fragments. She was right, and I was wrong—it's not a tide going out. It's a tide coming *in*."

"The crown didn't digest," I said. "It hoarded. And now the hoard has no keeper." I watched the street fill with weeping strangers embracing, and something in my chest ached in a way I hadn't known an emptied chest could ache. "Some of it will be too late. Some of them will get back pieces of people who've died waiting. Some will wish they hadn't remembered." I thought of Tam, and of my father, whose face lived now only in the copy I had traded away to save our lives, and I made myself say it aloud, because saying it was practice for all the doors I still had to knock on. "Not everyone gets everything back. But today, everyone gets *something*. Today the scales tip the right way, even if only a little."

Maren appeared at my elbow, sooty to the elbows, holding the last of Corvane's black books with both hands like an offering.

"Last one," she said. "His personal register. Every deal he cut, every favor he sold, starting nineteen years ago." Her eyes glittered. "I've been saving it."

"Read it out," I said. "Every entry. Let the street hear."

So she did. She stood on the mounting-block the fishwife had vacated, in front of a crowd of several hundred returned souls, and read Corvane's betrayals into the morning air in a voice built for cellars and back rooms and somehow perfectly suited to a public square. The crowd went silent, then angry, then—strangely—quiet again, because most of them had suspected, and suspicion confirmed is its own grim comfort.

Then Maren touched flame to the final black page and said, in the ritual words, with immense satisfaction:

"This was never ours. Go home—and *stay* there."

The smoke rose straight as a plumb-line into a clear blue sky, and somewhere across the city, I liked to imagine, ninety-one burdens grew lighter.

* * *

We burned until noon. Then past noon. The last ledger was the newest one—mine. My own hand, my own neat entries, six years of trades I had conducted with perfect precision and perfect detachment, or what I had told myself was detachment.

Rafael offered to read this one. I let him. His voice was gentler than mine would have been.

Halfway through, he paused. "Entry 6,203. One memory of a father, kept in violation of rule one, holder: B.R." He looked at me. "Do you want—"

"Burn it," I said. My father's face was already gone from me, traded at the market beneath the city to buy forged papers, and the copy had gone to Veyra's soldiers along with everything else, and no fire and no rite would bring it back. But the *record* of it—the cold line of ink saying I had once held it and kept it and loved having it—that record I could free. "It was never ours. Go home."

The page curled, blackened, and flew apart into sparks, and the smoke went up through the broken roof into the wide morning, and I stood in the ruins of the only home I'd known for fifteen years and felt—for the first time since the dragonfire—took my gift—completely, ordinarily empty. No echoes. No anchors. Nothing in me that belonged to anyone else.

Just me. Beatrix Ravensworth, who had once been able to steal anything from anyone, and who now possessed exactly one mind, one history, one small stock of memories, all of them honestly hers, several of them painful, one of them a dragon made of ember-light saying *willingly, willingly* in a voice like cooling iron.

It was enough. Goddess forgive me, it was actually enough.

* * *

By evening the crowd had thinned to stragglers, and the stragglers had begun doing something I hadn't expected. They came to the doorway, one at a time, and thanked us. Not all of them. A few spat—fair enough; a guild that steals memories owes some spitting. A few asked, hesitantly, whether more might yet return. But a good number simply stood there, awkward as boys at a dance, and said thank you, and one old woman pressed a heel of bread into Maren's hands and said, "For the fire-tenders," and Maren, who had robbed three archdukes and never once blushed, went pink as a sunset.

"They're grateful," she said, bewildered, after the woman left. "We *ran the place*, Beatrix. We took their kisses and their mothers and charged them for the privilege."

"We gave them back," I said.

"After taking them."

"Restitution isn't purity, Maren. It's just restitution. Take it."

She considered this, chewing bread, and then nodded once, sharply, the nod of a woman filing a lesson in the drawer where she kept lessons learned too late to be comfortable.

Rafael found me at dusk sitting on the loading dock out back, my feet dangling over the canal, watching the last spiral of smoke dissolve. He sat down beside me. For a while neither of us spoke, because we had gotten good at that, at letting silence do the work of ten conversations.

"Ninety-one names," he said at last. "You're really going to every door."

"Every one."

"Some of them will slam."

"Some already have, in my imagination. I've rehearsed it extensively. In the best version I deliver a moving speech. In the realistic version a fishmonger throws a mackerel at me."

"I've seen your reflexes," he said. "You'd catch the mackerel."

"I would catch the mackerel, yes. And then I'd owe him a conversation, because you can't throw a fish back at a man whose childhood you helped catalogue." I leaned my head briefly against his shoulder—briefly, because grief was still doing its work and tenderness had to be rationed like lamp-oil. "Tomorrow it starts. Door one. The widow Anselm, Candlemaker's Row. Her husband sold the sound of her voice, did you know that? To stop himself hearing it after she died. And it worked, and he regretted it for nine years, and now she's gone and he's gone and the voice came back to a house with no one in it."

Rafael was quiet a moment. "And you'll tell her son the truth."

"I'll tell whoever answers the door the truth. That we held it. That we profited. That we burned it too late to matter." I looked out at the darkening water. "Magic got us into this, and magic mostly died on the solstice, and what's left is just people telling each other true things. It's harder. Maren would say it's worth more, and for once I'd agree with her."

He took my hand. His fingers were warm, ink-stained—he'd been helping with the registers all day—and I noticed, with the small sharp pleasure of noticing, that my hands were ink-stained too, matched now.

"One more thing," he said. "Before tomorrow." He reached into his coat and produced a folded piece of paper, creased and re-creased, soft at the edges from handling. "When Veyra's people seized the archives, a clerk misfiled this into the inquisitorial intake. It surfaced during the inventory. I pulled it before anyone else read it." He unfolded it carefully, the way you handle a moth. "It's in your handwriting."

I took it. Guild-ink, my precise slanted script, dated eight years ago. An intake note. A description of a trade.

*A first kiss, aged sixteen, harbor-side, salt air and lamplight. Seller: male, apprentice cooper, destitute. Quality: extraordinary. Disposition: shelf nine, jar forty-four. Appraised by B. Ravensworth.*

Shelf nine had been in the flooded vault. Jar forty-four had cracked, months ago, in the rising water. Whatever fragment of that boy's kiss still existed had seeped into the stones of the Hollow and drowned.

Except.

Except that the tide was coming in. Except that the crown's hoard had no keeper, and the vaults were connected to the old foundry shafts, and the foundry shafts ran down, and down, to a drowned dark where something enormous slept in ember-sleep, dreaming in embers of promises and fire and the long patience of dragons.

And Odile had told me once—had said it lightly, the way blind women say profound things—*everything the city loses runs downhill eventually, dear. Water remembers where things go.*

I looked at the note. I looked at Rafael. Down at the far end of the canal, past the rotting pilings of the old foundry, a single light was burning in Odile's window—the same window where, three nights ago, I had seen it flicker in a pattern I'd told myself was imagination. Flicker, flicker, flicker-steady. Like a signal. Like someone who could not see, signaling anyway, to someone she knew would understand.

"Beatrix?" Rafael said.

I folded the note closed and stood up, and my heart was doing something complicated and hopeful and terrified, the exact rhythm it had kept on the night I first descended into the vaults.

"She's trying to tell me something," I said. "And I think—I think something of mine is down there."
