## 35. What Cannot Be Returned

The ledgers burned in three days.

Not all at once—Beatrix had thought it would be like that, a great bonfire, a cleansing, something with music in it. Instead it was slow and unglamorous work: hauling crates of water-damaged registers out of the ruined Hollow, prying them open one by one, and feeding each page to a small iron brazier she'd carried up from the shop herself because Rafael insisted on carrying everything heavier than a teacup and she'd had to draw a line somewhere.

Some pages went up easily. A first kiss, returned to the wind over Copperlane; somewhere across the city, a baker's wife stopped mid-knead and pressed floury hands to her mouth, laughing and crying into the dough. Some pages fought her. Memories that had been traded under duress clung to their paper like drowned men to wreckage, and Beatrix had to sit with them, read the entries aloud, say *you are free, you are free, you are free* until they let go.

And some pages were simply empty.

She found the first one on the second day—a ledger entry in Tam's careful apprentice hand, recording a memory of his mother's kitchen, sold to pay for his guild dues. The entry was there. The memory was not. It had been in Veyra's archive when the Spire burned, and whatever fire had unmade the crown had unmade the harvested minds with it, scattered so thoroughly that even Odile's dragon could find no ember of them.

Beatrix sat on the floor of the gutted archive with that page in her lap for a long time, listening to the rain come through the hole where the roof used to be.

"You can't fix this one," she said to the empty room.

The empty room agreed.

* * *

Rafael found her there an hour later, which was not difficult, since he always found her, a skill she suspected he'd learned during his inquisitor years and now applied exclusively to bringing her tea.

"You've been sitting in a ruin for an hour," he said, handing her a cup that steamed in the cold.

"I'm auditing."

"The archive is ash."

"Then I'm auditing the ash." She drank. He'd made it too sweet, the way she liked it and would never admit to liking it. "There are gaps, Rafe. Whole shelves of them. Everything Veyra harvested is gone. Everything I stored here that was already traded twice—the copies of copies—they're gone too. And Tam—" Her voice did something she didn't authorize. "Tam sent me his last memory before they took him. I lived it. The throne room, the crown's true name, the ritual. And then I used it, and it tore free in the burning, the way stolen things do. So even my copy of his courage is gone. All that's left of what he gave is what we did with it."

Rafael sat down beside her on the wet floor without checking whether it was wet first, which told her more about how far he'd fallen from inquisitor propriety than any resignation speech could have.

"What do you want to do?" he asked.

"There's a woman in Tallowbridge," Beatrix said. "Marta. Tam's mother. She thinks her son was arrested in the raids and is being held for registration processing. That's what the notice said. *Held for processing.* Like he was wool." She turned the cup in her hands. "I've been drafting what to tell her for two days. Every version I write has magic in it. *I can find a trace of him. I can borrow someone else's memory of his face. I can give you his laugh back.* And every version is a lie, because there's nothing left to give. The gift that could have done those things—" she flexed her fingers, still not quite used to their ordinary silence, "—is gone anyway. But even if it weren't. Even if I could conjure him whole out of borrowed light. I don't think I should."

Rafael was quiet for a moment. "Because it wouldn't be true."

"Because it would be Veyra's trick with kinder packaging. A comfort that eats the person you're comforting." She looked at him. "I spent ten years selling people beautiful lies and calling it mercy. I'm not doing it anymore. Not even once. Not even to be kind."

He took her hand. His palm was warm; hers was always cold now, some small tax the crown's death had levied on her body along with everything else.

"Then tell her the truth," he said. "And let me come with you."

"No," Beatrix said, and watched his face do the thing it did when he was about to argue, and added, "because if you're there, I'll lean on you, and this isn't a thing I get to lean through. Sit by the door if you must. Bring the tea. But the hard part is mine."

He argued anyway, gently, for another quarter hour, and lost, and came anyway, and sat by the door.

* * *

Tallowbridge in autumn smelled of tallow, unsurprisingly, and woodsmoke, and the river mud the bridge was named for and never actually touched. Marta Quill lived above a chandler's shop in a room that held one bed, one chair, one table, and an unreasonable number of candles—every misshapen, unsellable stub the chandler below couldn't move, which Marta collected the way other people collected grievances.

Tam had grown up in candlelight. Beatrix remembered that from the boy himself, from the years he'd been hers to teach—lockpicking by rushlight, his tongue between his teeth, laughing when the pick slipped. *My mum says waste not,* he'd said. *Candles burn down to nothing and she keeps every end.*

Marta opened the door before Beatrix knocked twice. She was a broad, weathered woman with Tam's exact brown eyes, and she looked at Beatrix the way people had been looking at her all month—with recognition, and hope, and the terrible arithmetic of hope, which is that it divides itself by every stranger who might possibly be a messenger.

"You're the Ravensworth girl," Marta said. "From the Hollow. Tam talked about you. Come in before the cold does."

The room was warm. There were two cups already on the table, which meant Marta had been expecting someone, anyone, for days. Beatrix sat. Rafael stayed by the door with the tea he wasn't going to be offered, radiating the particular helplessness of a large man in a small room.

"Is it news?" Marta asked. She didn't sit. "You've got news. You've got the face people get. The king's council posted the lists—names of the dead from the Spire—and Tam's not on them, and the clerk told me unlisted meant unaccounted, and unaccounted meant maybe alive, maybe transferred, maybe—" She stopped herself with visible effort, the way one stops a cart on a hill. "Sit there looking sorry at me all night if you like, but say the word first. Is he alive?"

And here was the moment Beatrix had rehearsed forty ways, and every rehearsal evaporated, and what remained was just a woman in a warm room asking a plain question.

"No," Beatrix said. "I'm so sorry. Tam is dead."

Marta didn't cry. Beatrix had expected crying, had braced for it, had brought a handkerchief for it. Instead Marta stood very still, and then very carefully sat down in her chair, as though her legs had given notice.

"How," she said. Not *how dare you*. Just *how*.

"He was taken in the raid at Tallowbridge safehouse. The Chancellor's people interrogated him. He resisted them longer than anyone could have—he protected people, dozens of us, right up to the end. He died resisting. There was no—" Beatrix made herself keep going. "There was no body released. What Veyra's harvesters did... they took minds, not lives, mostly. But Tam fought them past the point where anything of him was left to return. When the Spire burned, what remained went with it."

"Gone," Marta said. "All of him."

"All of him," Beatrix said. "Everywhere. I wish I had softer words. I've looked for softer words for three days and they're all lies, so I'm giving you the true ones instead."

Marta looked down at her hands. They were chandler's hands, wax-spotted, steady. "You were his teacher."

"For four years. Locks, mostly. He was better than me within a year and insufferable about it."

That almost—*almost*—got a sound out of her that wasn't grief. "He wrote that. In his letters. *Mistress Ravensworth says I show off, which proves I'm showing off, which means I win.*" Her jaw tightened. "He sent money home every month. Guild dues, he said, and then a little extra. Was that true? The dues?"

"It was true."

"And the rest?"

Beatrix had gone down this road in her drafts, too. Every honest answer forked into another honest answer, and all of them led somewhere painful, and she had decided in the ash of the archive that she would walk every road rather than skip one. "The rest was from trades. Tam sold memories to make ends meet. Small ones, at first. Then—" she hesitated, and made herself finish, "—then one of yours. His record shows he sold a memory of your kitchen. Your hands making candles. He was sixteen."

Marta was quiet for a long time. The candles around the room guttered and steadied, guttered and steadied, as though the room itself were breathing through it.

"I wondered," she said finally. "There was a stretch—I couldn't tell you what year—that I'd stand at the table and my hands would know the work but I'd feel like I was watching someone else's hands. I thought it was my heart going soft in the head. Grief does that, I'd heard." She looked up. "It was him, wasn't it. He took the remembering out of me and sold it, and I felt the hole without knowing there was a hole."

"Yes. I'm sorry. We told ourselves it was kind. Sell the sweet memories, keep the sharp ones, nobody misses what they don't remember missing." Beatrix shook her head. "It wasn't kind. It was theft with good manners. I taught him the manners."

"And the memories?" Marta leaned forward. "When the Hollow burned its books—you're the one did that, everyone says so, the Ravensworth girl freed them all—did his come back? Did mine?"

Here it was. The fork in the road. The place where a lesser honesty would flinch.

"Some came back," Beatrix said. "Most, even. The ones stored in our own ledgers went free. But Tam's trade of your kitchen—it passed through the Chancellor's archive before the end. Everything Veyra harvested was destroyed in the burning. Scattered past finding. Your kitchen memory is gone, Marta. Not misplaced. Gone. No dragon-fire, no gift, no miracle brings it back. If I could give it to you, I would kneel on this floor and give it. I can't. Nobody can."

She had expected anger. She had, in her drafts, budgeted a full page for anger. Instead Marta reached out and took one of the misshapen candles from the sill, turned it over in her wax-spotted fingers, and said:

"My mother did that. Sold a memory, back when trading was legal-ish and everyone pretended it was fine. Sold the day I was born. Said she wanted me to have winter boots more than she wanted to remember the pain of me." A short, rough laugh. "I used to think: how could she? And then I grew up and got hungry enough to understand, and I forgave her, but I never got the day back, and there was always this little door in me that rattled. Empty room behind it." She set the candle down. "So I know what you're telling me. There'll be a rattling door in me forever, and nobody's coming to fill it."

"Yes," Beatrix said. "I won't pretend otherwise."

"And you came all this way to tell me that. To tell me the worst thing, plainly, with no ribbon on it." Marta studied her. "The other families. The ones who got their memories back. They wept in the streets, they kissed your hands, the criers are calling it the Great Returning. And you come to me, whose boy is ash and whose kitchen is a hole, and you give me nothing but truth." Her eyes filled at last—not dramatically, just filling, like a cup left under a slow tap. "Do you know how rare that is? Everyone else who's knocked on this door since the lists posted has had a story. A séance-woman swears he speaks through her. A man in Copperlane says Tam's alive in the south and for fifty crowns he'll send word. Fifty crowns to lie to a grieving woman. And you walk in with nothing to sell."

"That's the whole of it," Beatrix said. "I'm done selling."

Marta wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand, briskly, the way women do when they intend to continue functioning. "Tell me about him. Not the hero version—the criers have done the hero version, died protecting the gifted, martyr of the Spire, very fine. Tell me the boy version. The one who showed off at lockpicks."

So Beatrix told her. About the time Tam picked the training locks blindfolded and then couldn't get his blindfold off because he'd knotted it showing off. About the afternoon he liberated an entire tray of honey buns from the Hollow kitchens and distributed them to the dock kids and blamed the cat, and there was no cat, and Corvane knew there was no cat, and let it go because even Corvane had a heart the size of a lentil on his best days. About how Tam hummed when he worked, always the same three notes, never a whole song, and how Beatrix had caught herself humming those three notes for years afterward and only just realized where she'd gotten them.

Marta laughed twice. Real laughs, cracked down the middle, the kind that cost something. At the third story she got up, without appearing to decide to, and put the kettle on, and that was its own kind of answer.

They drank tea. Rafael, by the door, was offered a cup after all, and accepted it with the gravity of a man receiving a medal. Marta asked questions—when had he been taken, had he suffered, had he said anything—and Beatrix answered each one straight, including the hardest one: *did he suffer?*

"Yes," she said. "For a while. And then he chose to spend what he had left getting a message out, and choosing is its own kind of ease. He wasn't afraid at the end. He was busy."

"*Busy,*" Marta repeated, and huffed, and something in her shoulders came down half an inch. "That's him. That's my Tam. Never scared, just occupied."

Near dark, Beatrix rose to go, and Marta walked her to the door, and there paused, holding the latch, in the doorway's last warmth.

"You said nobody can bring back what's gone," Marta said. "I believe you. I've had a lifetime of practice believing the worst, it turns out. But I'll tell you what I can't get out of my head." She looked past Beatrix, at the lamplit street, at the city full of returned memories and emptied ones. "Veyra fed minds to that crown for decades. Decades of harvested people. And it's all scattered—but scattered isn't nothing, is it? Ash isn't nothing. My candles burn down to nothing and I keep every end." Her brown eyes—Tam's eyes—came back to Beatrix's face. "Somewhere in this city there are others like me. Mothers and fathers with rattling doors. Hundreds. Maybe thousands. And somebody ought to count us. Somebody ought to write down, honestly, in a book, what was taken and what can't come back, so that in fifty years nobody sells some poor widow a séance and calls it mercy."

Beatrix felt the words land somewhere under her ribs and stay there.

"A book," she said.

"A ledger," said Marta Quill, chandler's daughter, keeper of candle-ends. "But an honest one. The kind that only ever records what's true, even when the truth is *gone*." She squeezed Beatrix's hand, wax-spot to ink-spot. "You'd be good at that. You're the only person I've met who tells the truth like it costs her and pays anyway."

* * *

Rafael walked her home through the Tallowbridge evening, and neither of them spoke for six streets, which was its own kind of conversation between them by now.

Finally, near the harbor, where the shop's windows glowed with the particular gold of a place where someone is waiting up: "You're going to do it," he said. "The ledger of what can't be returned."

"I'm going to think about doing it."

"You've been thinking about it since the doorway. You've got the face."

"I have one face, Rafe, and it's tired."

"It's the face you get when someone hands you a problem shaped exactly like the thing you swore off." He unlocked the door. Inside, the shop smelled of ink and paper and the stew he'd left simmering, and there was a fresh sheet on the writing desk, squared up, waiting, because he was Rafael and he had known before she had. "Institutions are how crowns happen. Your words. Last month. Very eloquent."

"This wouldn't be an institution. It's a book."

"Ledgers become archives. Archives become offices. Offices become—" he waggled his fingers portentously, "—*institutions*."

"Then I'll keep it in a drawer."

"You kept the king's memory in a drawer and it started a revolution."

Beatrix sat down at the desk. The blank page waited with the patience of all blank pages, which is to say smugly. She thought of Marta's rattling door. Of Tam's three-note hum, which she was humming now, and would probably hum for the rest of her life, honestly earned. Of all the doors in all the rooms in this city that would rattle forever, and the comfort-sellers already circling them like gulls.

She dipped the pen.

At the top of the page, in her precise, unapologetic hand, she wrote: *Register of What Cannot Be Returned.* Underneath, the first entry: *Quill, Tam. Son of Marta. Gave everything. Left a message that saved a kingdom and no copy of himself. Worth more than he was owed.*

The pen hovered. There was a knock at the shop door.

Rafael glanced at her; she glanced back. It was late for clients, late for friends, and the knock had the particular measured politeness of someone official who has been trained to knock rather than enter. Through the frosted glass: a silhouette, upright, patient, wearing what was unmistakably the cut of a council cloak.

Rafael opened the door on a courier in council livery, holding a sealed letter, who looked between the two of them and said, with the apologetic tone of a man delivering something heavy:

"Beg pardon. From the Council of Reconciliation, for the Widow Quill's visitor and the former Lord Inquisitor both. They're forming the new Wardenry of Records." He held out the letter. "They want an answer by week's end."
