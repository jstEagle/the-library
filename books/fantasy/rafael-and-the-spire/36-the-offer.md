## 36. The Offer

The council summoned Beatrix on a morning so aggressively pleasant that she suspected the weather of having an agenda.

Spring had come late to the city that year, as if it too had been waiting to see whether the world would hold together after the solstice. But it had held—scorched Spire and all—and now sunlight lay across the harbor in long gold bars, and the gulls were conducting their usual extortion racket over the fish stalls, and a courier in council blue stood at the door of the ink shop holding an envelope sealed with wax the color of authority.

"He won't take it," Rafael called from inside, not looking up from the ledger he was balancing.

The courier blinked. "It's addressed to Mistress Ravensworth."

"She's right here." Beatrix leaned in the doorway, flour on her hands because she had been attempting bread again, an endeavor that continued to humble her more thoroughly than any guild hunter ever had. She wiped them on her apron and took the envelope. "And he's right, you know. I never accept deliveries before noon. It's a rule."

"Is it," said Rafael.

"It's one of mine. I'm allowed to have those now."

The courier waited, clearly under instructions not to leave without an answer, which meant someone on the new council had already learned the first lesson of dealing with Beatrix Ravensworth: she would agree to nothing simply to make you go away.

She opened the envelope anyway. Inside was a single sheet, written in a careful clerk's hand, and beneath it a second page bearing six signatures and one very large seal.

*The Council of Restoration,* it read, *requests the honor of Mistress Beatrix Ravensworth's service as first Warden of Records, custodian of all recovered memories, overseer of the Archive of Return, guardian against—*

She stopped reading there, mostly because she'd begun to laugh.

"What?" Rafael set down his pen.

"They want me to guard against exactly what Veyra built." She handed him the letter. "Me. Officially. With a title."

He read it twice, the way he read everything, his jaw doing the thing it did when he was keeping his face orderly while his thoughts ran riot underneath. "There's a salary."

"There's a *salary.*"

"A generous one."

"I know what a generous salary is, Rafael, I used to price people's childhoods for a living." She took the letter back and looked at it properly this time, because she was trying—she had been trying for weeks—to be a person who considered things instead of merely surviving them.

That was the strange arithmetic of the months since the burning. She had expected grief for her gift to be a wound. Instead it was more like a room in her house she kept walking into out of habit, finding it empty, and standing there a moment wondering what she'd come in for. The gift had been theft dressed up as wonder. Its absence left her honest, and honesty turned out to be heavier than she'd imagined, but it fit her hands better.

And yet.

"Warden of Records," she said aloud, tasting it.

"You'd be good at it," Rafael said quietly. That was the trouble with him; he refused even now to argue cheaply. "You'd be better than anyone they could find. You know every trick Veyra knew, and half the ones she didn't. You know how archives rot from the inside. You know how ledgers lie."

"Flatterer."

"Fact-teller. There's a difference and it's most of my personality."

She laughed despite herself and carried the letter inside, past the shelf of ink pots, past the little sign Rafael's sister had sent from the south—*QUIET ROOMS FOR QUIET PEOPLE, LOUD PEOPLE ALSO WELCOME IF SINCERE*—and sat down at the worktable where they taught returned-memory folk to write their own names again.

Because that was the thing about the shop. People came. Not many, at first, then more. Men and women who had sold pieces of themselves to the Hollow years ago, or had them harvested by Veyra's clerks, and who had gotten those pieces back when Aldric ordered the stores unlocked and Beatrix spent nine days burning trade-ledgers in the wind. Getting a memory back, it turned out, was like getting a limb back after years in a sling. It ached. It didn't fit right. You had to learn to use it.

So they came to the shop, and Beatrix sat with them, and Rafael made tea that tasted faintly of the cedar he insisted on storing beside the leaves, and nobody stole anything from anybody. It was, she thought some mornings, the only honest business in the city.

Which was precisely why the letter felt like a hook through the gill.

* * *

They gave her a week to answer, which she suspected was a week longer than Chancellor Halloway—the council's spine and its most stubborn vertebra—had wanted to give her.

She spent four of those days genuinely considering it. That was the part Rafael found hardest to watch. He'd have preferred her to refuse instantly; refusing was easy. Considering meant walking down the road a ways to see where it went.

On the fifth day she walked that road in person, all the way to the restored Council Hall in the old Customs House, because Halloway had asked—not summoned, *asked*, which was itself novel—and because a person who had spent her life behind counters ought, at least once, to see what power looked like from the front of the desk.

The chamber smelled of fresh plaster and old ambition. Six seats, five occupied, a sixth draped in black crepe for a member lost to the crown's final convulsion. Maps on the walls. A list of districts still awaiting their returned memories, written in chalk that someone had lovingly underlined.

"Mistress Ravensworth." Halloway rose to greet her personally, which cost him something visible around the eyes. He was a broad, gray man with the manner of a ship's captain permanently braced for weather. "Thank you for coming."

"Thank you for writing instead of sending soldiers. The improvement hasn't gone unnoticed."

A younger councillor laughed and then stopped himself, uncertain whether laughter at the Warden-to-be was permitted yet. Beatrix decided she liked him.

"We'll be direct," Halloway said. "Veyra built her machine out of three things. A crown that ate kings. An archive that ate memories. And a system that made ordinary people believe both were necessary." He tapped the chalk list. "We can return what was taken. We cannot yet prevent it being taken again. The council needs someone inside the machinery—someone with your particular knowledge—to build the safeguards. Registration of gifted persons, yes, but voluntary. Oversight of memory-craft. Standards." He spread his hands. "An institution that cannot become what the last one became."

"An institution that cannot become what the last one became," Beatrix repeated.

"Yes."

"Has anyone told you that's what the last one said about itself?"

The chamber went quiet in the specific way rooms do when someone has said the true thing too early.

"Every institution begins as a fence against its predecessor," Beatrix went on, gentler now, because these were not villains; that was the whole difficulty. They were tired, decent people staring at wreckage and wanting, desperately, to be the ones who finally fixed it. "The Hollow began as a sanctuary. Did you know that? Corvane's predecessor founded it to keep gifted children off the streets. Within thirty years it was pricing first kisses by weight and selling names to the Chancery. No one decided to betray it. It just grew teeth, the way institutions do, quietly, one reasonable compromise at a time. And every tooth was justified by the threat of the day."

"Then help us build one with better teeth," said the young councillor, leaning forward. "Or none at all. You could design it clean—"

"Clean things stay clean for exactly as long as no one wants anything." Beatrix shook her head. "Forgive me. I've watched power from the underside my whole life. The view from up here is lovely. That's the trap. Down below, corruption looks like rot. Up here it looks like prudence. Like *necessity*. Veyra believed, to her last waking hour, that she was saving us. She wasn't lying to the world. She was lying to herself in an office very much like this one, with maps very much like those." She nodded at the walls. "I don't want a map."

"Then what do you want?" Halloway asked. It wasn't hostile. It was the question of a man who had run out of arguments and arrived, at last, at curiosity.

Beatrix thought about it honestly, because he deserved that.

"I want the records public," she said. "Not guarded—*public*. Copy everything Veyra hoarded and give it to the districts, to the temples, to the street criers if need be. Let ten thousand people know what happened instead of one trustworthy woman knowing it for them. Trustworthy women are how we got here. I want the gifted registered nowhere, because registration is a list, and lists are fuel, and someday someone cold will find the fuel. I want—" she smiled, rueful— "I want fewer wardens and more neighbors."

"That's chaos," someone murmured.

"It's mess," Beatrix agreed. "Chaos has ambitions. Mess just gets everywhere and makes tyranny inconvenient. Give me mess."

Halloway studied her for a long moment. Then, unexpectedly, he almost smiled—a crack in the captain's bearing, a glimpse of the man beneath. "You realize," he said, "that we will ask you again. When something goes wrong—and something will—we will come to your shop and ask again."

"I know." She stood. "And I'll make you bread. My bread is terrible. It will remind you why some things are better left unattempted."

At the door, the young councillor caught her. "Mistress Ravensworth—if not you, then who keeps watch?"

"Everyone, briefly," she said. "No one, permanently. That's rather the point."

* * *

Rafael's letter came three days later, delivered to the shop by the same courier, who had apparently drawn the short straw for life.

He was at the market when it arrived, so Beatrix read the envelope's seal without him and felt her stomach do something complicated. Council white wax. The same request, surely. *Warden of Records, Deputy Custodian, Keeper of Safeguards*—they'd want him, of course they'd want him. An ex-inquisitor who had held the burning line at the solstice, who had publicly resigned his commission and refused the pardon Aldric had offered with his own scarred hands. Integrity like that was a natural resource. Governments mined it.

When he came home carrying a basket and arguing cheerfully with a gull about jurisdiction, she had the letter waiting on the table beside two cups.

He read it standing up. Then he sat down, which was unlike him, and read it again sitting, which was more so.

"They've offered you deputy warden," Beatrix said.

"They've offered me *chief* warden." He turned the page toward her. "You declined. So they moved up the list."

"To me."

"And then, apparently, past you, to me." He rubbed his eyes. "Halloway spoke to me at the harbor last week. I thought he was making conversation. He's very good at making conversation that is actually an ambush."

"What did you tell him?"

"The truth. That I spent eleven years believing the law was a wall between the wolves and everyone else, and then I watched the wall turn around and start choosing which sheep looked tasty." He set the letter down. "I don't hate the law, Bea. That's the miserable part. I understand why they're building this. If I were twenty-six and hadn't seen what I've seen, I'd say yes with both hands."

"And now?"

"Now I know that the best people make the worst institutions, because the best people can't imagine becoming the thing, and so they never build the locks against themselves." He looked at her across the table, and the lamplight caught the pale scar along his forearm where the fire had passed close at the solstice. "Your argument. I heard it secondhand from the young councillor, who quotes you like scripture. Institutions are how crowns happen."

"Crowns don't arrive wearing crowns," Beatrix said softly. "They arrive wearing job titles."

"So I wrote back today." He slid his reply across the table. Short, of course. Rafael's refusals were always shorter than other men's acceptances. *With respect: no. Guard the records by giving them away. —R.V.*

"You didn't even hesitate."

"I hesitated beautifully. For four days. You just didn't see it." He reached across and took her hand, flour-roughened, ink-stained, entirely her own. "Besides. If we'd accepted, who would teach the Tuesday class? Old Marta finally wrote her husband's name without shaking yesterday. I'm not abandoning that for a chair in a plaster room."

"Sentimental."

"Deeply." He grinned, and then the grin settled into something quieter. "Do you ever miss it? The gift. Truly."

Beatrix considered lying, out of habit, and then did the newer, harder thing.

"Some evenings. When someone comes in grieving something that can't be given back, and I think—one theft, one small one, and I could hand them their dead mother's laugh for an hour. Just an hour." She turned her cup slowly. "And then I remember what it cost to stop, and I think the missing is probably the healthiest part of me. Grief that can't be bought off. It keeps me honest."

"Terrible business model."

"The best one I've had."

He laughed, and the gull outside laughed too, or screamed, which for gulls amounted to agreement, and for a while they simply sat in the warm clutter of the shop while the harbor light went amber and someone's returned memory—music, faintly, from the flat above—drifted down through the floorboards like weather.

* * *

The council, to its credit, took the double refusal gracefully. Mostly. Halloway published both letters in the civic register alongside the plan they inspired: the great unsealing, every recovered record copied and distributed, nothing held in trust by any single office. The young councillor reportedly called it *government by inconvenience* and meant it as praise.

Beatrix read the announcement in the square, surrounded by strangers, and felt something loosen in her chest that she hadn't known was tight.

But that evening, as she banked the shop's fire, there came a knock—soft, apologetic, familiar in a way that raised the hairs on her arms.

Odile stood in the doorway, blind eyes turned toward the lamplight, leaning on her stick, smelling faintly of deep water and hot stone.

"Begging pardon for the hour," the old keeper said. "But the dragon woke tonight. Properly woke. And the first word out of it, clear as a bell across the water—" her voice dropped, awed and troubled at once— "was a name, child. It asked for *you*. And it says the sleeping isn't sleep anymore. Something's calling it home."

Beatrix looked back at Rafael, who had already reached for his coat.
