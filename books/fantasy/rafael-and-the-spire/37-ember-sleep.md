## 37. Ember-Sleep

The vaults breathed differently in the weeks after the solstice.

Odile had kept the drowned dark beneath the old foundry for thirty-one years, and she knew its moods the way a shepherd knows weather — by pressure, by smell, by the small dishonesties of water against stone. Before the burning of the crown, the air down here had carried a constant low ache, like a tooth that never quite broke: the ember-sleep of a dragon dreaming against its own dying fire. Now the ache was gone. In its place was something she had not felt since she was a girl and her eyes still worked well enough to watch lanterns gutter.

Silence. Real silence, with weight to it.

"Vaskarath," she said, tapping her staff twice on the flooded stair, the way she always did. "I've brought bread. Well. I've brought the idea of bread. Tam's mother sent loaves again, and I've eaten four, which is either grief or gratitude, and at my age they taste the same."

The water ahead of her glowed faintly, ember-orange bleeding up from the deep pool where the dragon lay coiled around the roots of the Spire itself. The glow had changed too. It used to flicker — anxious, guttering, a candle cupped against wind. Now it pulsed slow and even, like the chest of a sleeper finally warm enough.

"You're showing off," Odile told it. "A month ago you were a dying coal. Now look at you, glowing like a festival lamp. Vanity. I respect it."

She settled onto her usual stone, the one worn smooth by decades of keeper's hips, and ate her bread while the dragon's breathing moved the water in long, patient swells. It would sleep now for a very long time. Perhaps centuries. Perhaps forever. Dragon-fire spent in the way Vaskarath had spent it — all of it, gladly, through the bones of a mountain — did not kindle twice. Odile understood this the way she understood tides: not because anyone had explained it lately, but because some truths settle into a body and refuse to leave.

The city above still didn't know what slept under its feet. Let them read about it someday, if Beatrix ever finished that book she'd started muttering about. *Rafael and the Spire*, she'd called it, and then gone scarlet when Odile had asked who Rafael was, as though the whole city hadn't watched an inquisitor hold a line of fire with his bare conviction while a woman burned a crown above him.

Odile smiled at the water and let herself remember — carefully, the way she did everything now, hoarding each image like coins she could no longer earn more of.

* * *

Beatrix came down on the ninth day after the burning, and Odile heard her before she saw her, because Beatrix still walked like a thief even though she no longer was one. Soft heels, careful weight, a body that had learned the grammar of quiet so deeply it couldn't unlearn it.

"You're limping," Odile said.

"I'm walking thoughtfully."

"You're limping thoughtfully, then. Come sit. The stone's cold but honest."

Beatrix laughed — a real laugh, rusty at the hinges — and felt her way along the wall to the keeper's stone. Odile heard her lower herself with a hiss, heard the wet bandage smell of salve and the copper ghost of blood that hadn't fully washed out of skin.

"How bad?" Odile asked.

"The healers say the burns will fade. They're being polite. They mean I'll have a hand that aches when it rains for the rest of my life." A pause. "It rains a lot here."

"It does. You'll fit right in." Odile passed her the last of the bread. "And the rest? The part they don't put in reports?"

Beatrix was quiet long enough that the dragon's breathing filled the space between them, swell and hush, swell and hush.

"I tried to steal something yesterday," she said finally. "Out of habit. A memory off a man selling chestnuts — he was remembering his daughter's wedding, and I reached for it without thinking, the way you reach for a banister that isn't there anymore." Her voice went strange. "There was nothing there, Odile. Not a locked door. Not an empty room. Just — me. Only me, in my own head, like everyone else."

"That sounds terrible."

"It is. And it isn't." Another pause. "I cried into the chestnuts. He gave me extra for free. I think he assumed I was moved by the wedding."

Odile cackled, and the sound rolled down the vaults, and somewhere in the deep water something vast shifted its coils with a groan like a ship settling.

"He heard you," Odile said. "Careful. Dragons judge laughter. Harshly. Vaskarath once sulked for six years over a joke I made about lizards."

"What happened to the water?" Beatrix asked, and Odile heard her lean forward, peering into the glow. "It's brighter. Warmer. Before the ceremony it felt like standing at the edge of a grave."

"You gave it back," Odile said simply. "That's what you did. Everyone keeps saying you destroyed the crown, and yes, fine, the fire did that. But the fire was a gift, and gifts given freely burn differently than fires taken by force. Veyra fed that thing stolen minds for forty years. You fed the dragon one honest memory, willingly held, and the whole ledger balanced. Old arithmetic. Older than kings." She tapped her staff. "The Emberbound was made from a broken promise. You ended it with a kept one. The universe is sentimental like that. Embarrassingly so."

Beatrix was silent again. When she spoke, her voice was careful, the voice of someone setting down something fragile.

"I brought something. For Vaskarath. If — I mean, if it wants it. I don't know the etiquette for giving dragons things."

"There is no etiquette. There has only ever been one dragon and three keepers, and I made most of the etiquette up myself and immediately forgot it." Odile held out her palm. "What have you got?"

Something small pressed into her hand. Paper. Folded many times, softened at the creases, carrying the faint oil-and-ink smell she'd come to associate with the girl.

"A memory?" Odile asked.

"My own. Kept honestly — written down, not stolen. From the first night I came down here." A breath. "You told me to go home, and I didn't, and I saw the vaults for the first time. The water. The light. You, telling me I was an idiot. I wrote it all down that same night, every detail, because I was afraid I'd lose it." A small, wry sound. "I've lost plenty since. But not that. That one stayed mine the whole time."

Odile turned the paper over in her blind fingers. She couldn't read it, but she could feel how much writing there was — dense, front and back, the handwriting of someone who had been afraid to stop.

"And you want to give it to a sleeping dragon."

"I want to give it to the dragon that saved everything I love and got nothing back but a shorter life. Yes."

"Child," Odile said, "dragons don't need our pity. They barely tolerate our gratitude. But—" she turned the paper once more, "—a gift freely given, that's different. That's the only currency down here that was ever real." She raised her voice toward the water, pitched it the way she'd learned to pitch things across great distances and greater silences. "Vaskarath! Wake your leftmost dream. We have a transaction."

The glow deepened. The water swelled once, twice — and then the heat of a hearth bloomed through the vault, gentle as morning sun through glass, and Odile felt rather than saw the dragon's attention turn upon them, vast and slow and ancient, an eye the size of a doorway opening behind the world.

*Take care,* rumbled a voice that was not sound but warmth, pressing against their ribs like sunlight through a wall. *I am mostly sleep now. What remains of me is listening.*

Beatrix's breath caught. Odile grinned into the dark. First time meeting the dragon properly, then. Everyone remembered their first time. Hers had involved screaming, though she'd never admitted that to anyone.

"She brings a gift," Odile translated. "Her own memory. Freely held, freely given. No theft in it anywhere."

*A rare coin,* the dragon said. *In all my long burning, mortals have brought me gold, meat, prayers, and children's teeth. Never this.* A pause, and the warmth shifted, curious. *Read it to her aloud, keeper. Memory given should be witnessed. Otherwise it is only hiding in a new shape.*

"Oh, I can't read—"

"I'll read it," Beatrix said quickly. "If — if that's wanted. It's just — it's embarrassing. I wrote it when I was frightened and full of myself."

*All true memories are both,* said Vaskarath. *Begin.*

* * *

So Beatrix read.

She read by the dragon's own light, holding the pages close to the glow, her voice rough at first and then steadying, and Odile listened with her whole body the way the blind learn to listen — every word arriving twice, once in the ear and once in the chest.

*"The stairs went down farther than anything should,"* Beatrix read. *"Forty-one steps, and I counted, because counting is what I do instead of praying. The air smelled like iron and rain that had nowhere to be. And then the ceiling opened up and there was water below me, black water, except it wasn't black — there was light underneath it, orange, moving, alive. I thought it was a fire at first. Some foundry furnace that never died. Then it blinked.*

*"An eye. Under the water. An eye bigger than my entire apartment, and I stood there on the stair like a fool with my heart going like a hammer on a nail, and an old woman's voice came out of the dark and said, 'Well? Are you going to stand there dripping on my stairs, or are you going to tell me who sent you?'"*

Odile snorted. "I was gentler than that."

"You were not. Keep listening."

*"'Nobody sent me,' I said. Which was true, and also the bravest sentence I owned. And she laughed — this dry, crackling laugh, like a fire eating paper — and said, 'Good. The ones who get sent always want something. The ones who come alone only ever want to know.'*

*"I wanted to know. That was the whole truth of me, then and now. I wanted to know what slept under my city, and whether the stories were true, and why a dragon would let a blind woman scold it, and why the scolding sounded so much like love.*

*"She fed me fish stew that tasted of smoke and patience. She told me nothing and everything. She said, 'He's dying, girl, and dying slowly, and there's no fixing it, so don't you go getting ideas.' And I sat there in the ember-light thinking: everything I have ever been told not to fix, I have fixed. Or stolen. Usually both.*

*"But that night — the first night — I didn't take anything. I just sat on the cold stone next to a stranger and watched a dying dragon breathe under black water, and for the first time in my life, having the gift felt quiet. Like a tool set down. Like hands empty on purpose.*

*"I didn't know yet about crowns or kings or hunts. I didn't know I'd betray my guild or love an inquisitor or burn my own magic out holding a monster while the sky caught fire. All I knew was that the world was bigger and stranger and kinder than the ledgers said, and that a blind keeper had fed me stew, and that the dragon's light looked, from certain angles, exactly like forgiveness.*

*"I wrote it down so I wouldn't lose it. I'm reading it aloud so it belongs to more than me now. Take it. It was always yours — you made the light I was describing."*

Silence, then. The good kind. The kind with weight and warmth and no demand in it.

Then the heat rose — not the fierce blaze of the solstice but something softer, a hearth-kind of gladness — and the water before them shimmered, and Odile felt the paper lifted gently from Beatrix's fingers by a current of warm air, unfolding, unrolling, drinking the words in letter by letter the way dry ground drinks rain.

*Received,* said Vaskarath, and the word resonated so deep in the stone that dust sifted down from the vaulted ceiling like golden snow. *Freely given, freely kept. Little thief — no. Forgive me. Little keeper-of-honest-things. You ask nothing in return, which among your kind is either madness or grace. So I return the only thing I have left to give: witness. What you did above, I saw. What it cost you, I carry. When your hands ache in the rain, know that a dragon remembers why.*

"That's—" Beatrix's voice cracked. She cleared her throat. "That's probably worth more than the stew."

*Considerably,* said the dragon, and there was — Odile would swear it on her staff — humor in it. *Now I am tired in the way mountains are tired. I will sleep. Perhaps I will wake when your grandchildren's grandchildren forget us. Perhaps I will simply become part of the water and the warmth, and the foundry will be comfortable in winter forever, and that will be my second gift.*

"And the third?" Odile asked, because there was always a third with dragons, always a triad, always a shape.

*The third I give now,* said Vaskarath, and the glow gathered itself, coiling upward through the water in a slow bright spiral until it hung in the air before Beatrix like a small contained sunrise, no bigger than a lantern. *A single ember. It cannot burn houses or forge swords — it is the last of my fire that remembers how to be small. It will keep a lamp lit for as long as your shop stands. Every hearth you warm, every page you write beside it, will be written in dragon-light. Consider it rent paid, in reverse, for the use of my dark all these years.*

Beatrix made a sound Odile had never heard from her before — half sob, half laugh, entirely undone. "I don't have a shop."

*You will,* said the dragon, with the infuriating certainty of creatures who measure time in eons. *There is a harbor street where the ink-sellers died in the plague years and the buildings sit empty and cheap and smelling of salt. You will find it. You always find things. It is the only gift you never lost.*

* * *

The dragon sank afterward, coil by coil, deeper into its pool and its dreams, and the glow dimmed to a steady banked warmth that would, Odile suspected, outlast her own bones by a hundred years. She sat with Beatrix on the keeper's stone a while longer, two women in the dark listening to a god breathe.

"He knew about the harbor," Beatrix said eventually, wonder in her voice. "How does a dragon know about real estate?"

"Dragons know everything said near water," Odile said. "Water carries gossip. Why do you think I do my best scheming while doing the laundry?"

Beatrix laughed, wiped her face with the heel of her unburned hand, and stood. Odile heard her wince, heard her stretch, heard the particular pause of a person working up to a question.

"Come with me," Beatrix said. "When I open it. The shop. There's a room in back, or there will be — dry, warm, close to the stairs down. A keeper's rooms. You'd still keep him. I'd just... keep you."

Odile was quiet for a moment, letting the offer sit in her chest, testing its weight the way she tested everything now.

"Thirty-one years I've slept down here," she said. "Listening to him die. I'd rather listen to him rest from somewhere with a window." She reached out and gripped Beatrix's wrist, feeling the pulse there, quick and alive. "Yes, girl. Obviously yes. But I'm bringing the stew pot, and I'm cooking, and you're going to eat proper meals if I have to steal the vegetables myself."

"You can't steal anymore either, you know," Beatrix said gently. "None of us can. That's rather the point."

"Child," Odile said, rising with a groan and taking up her staff, "I have run a smuggling route for thirty years using nothing but charm, laundry-day gossip, and a staff I trip people with. Watch me."

They climbed the forty-one steps together, counting them aloud out of habit, out of something like prayer, while behind and below them the last dragon in the world settled into a sleep that might outlast empires — warm, witnessed, and, for the first time in an age, owed nothing by anyone.

At the top of the stairs, pausing at the foundry's broken doors, Beatrix stopped.

"Odile," she said. "The dragon mentioned an ink-seller's street. By the harbor. Do you actually know it?"

Odile smiled, and tapped her staff, and began, happily, to scheme.
