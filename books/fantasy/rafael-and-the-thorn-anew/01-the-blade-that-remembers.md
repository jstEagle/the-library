## 1. The Blade That Remembers

There are three rules to moving contraband salt through the Thornfell passes in the dead of winter, and I have broken all three at least once, which is how I know they're real.

Rule one: never travel the high road after the first hard freeze, because the ice lies about its thickness the way a drunk lies about his coin. Rule two: never trust a mule that stops walking without reason — the animal knows things about the mountain that you don't, and unlike you, it has no pride to lose by admitting fear. Rule three, and this is the one that matters: never speak through your sword where anyone can hear.

I broke rule one on purpose this morning, because the salt had to move tonight or not at all, and because I am, despite considerable evidence to the contrary, an optimist. The pass above Grayford had frozen solid for six days running, and the smuggler's road — the one that doesn't exist, the one no crown cartographer ever drew because drawing it would mean admitting there was anything worth drawing here — was packed snow over old stone, and old stone holds cold like a grudge.

The mules were doing their job, which is to say they were miserable and slow and honest about both. Four of them, laden with salt bricks wrapped in oiled canvas, each brick stamped with no mark at all, because salt out of the low country carries a tax and salt out of nowhere carries nothing but weight. I'd run this route eleven times. I knew every switchback the way you know the lines of your own palm, which is to say intimately and with occasional resentment.

It was on the twelfth switchback that I found him.

He was sitting against the rock face like a man who'd stopped for a rest and simply never started again. Young. Maybe twenty. Courier's leathers, good ones, the kind with reinforced shoulders for carrying a document case across rough country. Frost had gotten into his beard and made lacework of it, and his eyes were open, fixed on something in the middle distance that only the dead can see clearly.

I crouched beside him and did what couriers do for each other, which is check the body first and grieve second, sentiment being a luxury that gets people killed on mountains. No wounds. No blood. His hands were tucked into his armpits, which meant he'd known he was dying and tried to hold onto his own warmth like a miser counting coins down to the last copper. Cold took him sitting up. That takes a certain stubbornness, or a certain exhaustion, and from the state of his boots — worn through at the heel, patched twice with mismatched leather — I'd guess exhaustion.

His satchel was still slung across his chest. Whatever he'd been carrying, he hadn't dropped it, hadn't abandoned it, hadn't traded it for a faster death somewhere warmer. I unbuckled it with fingers gone clumsy inside my gloves.

Papers. Sealed with gray wax, unmarked. A letter, folded small. And a name tag — courier's custom, stitched into the collar of his coat so that if the mountain kept him, someone would eventually know who to mourn.

*Ostwick, Tam.*

I read it twice, because the name did something to me that names do, and I needed to be sure I hadn't imagined it.

Here is the thing about my gift, the thing I have spent nine years burying under false papers and false names and a carefully cultivated reputation as the most boring woman on the mountain roads: my sword remembers. Not me — the sword. It's a swordspeaker's blade, Thornfell steel, older than any kingdom currently claiming these peaks, and when I speak through it, it speaks back. It holds the names of the dead I've avenged, and it holds them the way a riverbed holds water: not gently, not optionally, and long after everyone else has moved on to drier ground.

Most speakers' blades are given their names in ceremony. Mine collects them in the field. Every person whose death I've answered for — every bandit cut down over stolen goods, every hunter who got too close, every poor fool the mountains killed while I stood close enough to hear the last breath — if I speak their name into the steel, the sword keeps them. Keeps *all* of them. Their voices, their last words, the shape of their lives as far as I could see it. They live in the metal, murmuring, and some nights, when the fire is low and I'm half asleep, I swear I can hear them talking among themselves, trading memories that were never mine to begin with.

That's the ache of it, if you want the truth nobody asks for. I carry griefs that aren't mine. I know how Tam Ostwick's mother used to sing off-key before I ever learned her name. I know a shepherd named Brannic who died four years ago worried about a ewe. I know the taste of a fish stew a dead woman described to her sister with her final breath, and I have craved that stew for years, and I will never taste it, because the woman who could have made it is a murmur in my scabbard.

Nine names. That's what the sword holds now. Nine, because I am careful, and careful means most deaths go unmourned by me, which is its own kind of sin, though I've stopped keeping track of which kind.

Tam Ostwick would make ten.

I sat down next to him, because the living owe the dead at least the courtesy of sitting, and unwrapped my sword from its oilcloth just enough to bare the grip. The wind came down the pass in long white breaths. The mules waited, patient as furniture.

"Tam," I said quietly. "Tam Ostwick."

And I spoke it into the steel.

I won't describe exactly how swordspeech works, partly because I don't fully understand it myself and partly because describing it has gotten better people than me burned. But it goes something like this: you take the name and you press it into the blade the way you'd press a seal into wax, and the blade takes it, and then the blade gives something back. This time it gave me Tam. Three days of him, compressed — the road from wherever he'd started, the storm he'd walked through two nights ago, the moment on this exact switchback when his legs stopped answering and he'd sat down knowing, *knowing*, and chosen to protect the papers in his satchel with the last of his body heat rather than himself.

He'd been carrying word north. I felt that much of him, a direction and a purpose and a desperate hope that whoever found the satchel would carry it on.

I looked at the sealed papers in my lap and thought, with the particular weariness of a woman whose conscience never lets her alone: *of course you were.*

Because here's the other thing, the thing I haven't said aloud in nine years: the name Ostwick isn't a common one. Or rather, it was, once, in a country that the maps no longer show. Thornfell. My country. The one that fell when I was nineteen, the one the crown erased so thoroughly that children born in Grayford these days learn the mountains were always empty, always lawless, always *nothing*. The crown didn't just conquer us. It unmade us. Burned the records, struck the names, redrew the borders, and called the silence peace.

And now here was a dead boy named Ostwick carrying sealed papers north through the bones of that erased country, and the crown's wax on the seals, and no explanation anywhere in the world for why those facts should sit together so badly.

I'm getting ahead of myself. That morning, I didn't open the papers. That morning, I did what I always do, which is the safe thing dressed up as the decent thing.

I buried him. There was scree enough and time enough, barely, and I stacked stones over him in the old way — the Thornfell way, the way you mark a grave so the wind knows a person lived there and doesn't treat the spot carelessly. I set his name-tag face-up under the top stone, because even if no one ever reads it, the stone should know.

Then I took the satchel, because papers are cargo and cargo is my trade, and told myself I'd decide what to do with them at the bottom of the pass. This is the lie I tell myself on a regular basis: that I will decide later. Later is where I keep all my hardest decisions, and the shelf is getting crowded.

* * *

The descent into Grayford took until dusk, and dusk in the Thornfells in early winter arrives like a debt collector — suddenly, officially, and with no interest in your excuses. I came down the smuggler's track behind the tannery, paid the tanner's boy a copper to look the other way, which he did with the professionalism of a child raised on smuggling wages, and put the mules in Old Hesta's barn.

Hesta runs the safehouse, though she'd hex you for using the word. She calls herself a widow who boards livestock, and technically she is, and technically she does, and between those technicalities she has moved more contraband through Grayford than the crown has moved honest grain. She's seventy if she's a day, built like a root vegetable, and she looks at me the way a chess player looks at a piece that keeps refusing to move where it's told.

"You're late," she said, which is how she says hello.

"Dead courier on the twelve-switch," I said, which is how I say it back.

Her face did something complicated. "Name?"

"Ostwick. Tam Ostwick."

She went still in a way that seventy-year-old women shouldn't be able to go still, all that momentum stopping at once. "Ostwick," she repeated, tasting it. Then she looked at me, really looked, the way she almost never does, because looking at people is a habit that gets smugglers caught. "You opened his satchel?"

"I looked. I didn't read past the seals."

"Vesper." And there it was, my real name, spoken in a barn where no real names are supposed to exist. Hesta only used it when she was frightened, which was rare enough that it landed like a dropped anvil. "There's criers in the square since yesterday. Crown criers. You want to hear what they're saying?"

"No," I said honestly.

"They're calling for speakers." She said it fast, like pulling a splinter. "Gifted folk. Swordspeakers, wordspeakers, any kind of speaker. Warrant to detain, authority to burn. Signed by the witch-hunters' office out of the capital." She wiped her hands on her apron, though they weren't dirty. Her hands do that when she's scared; I've watched it for years. "They posted a list, Vesper. Names wanted for questioning. And the third name down—"

"Don't," I said.

"—is a description," she finished anyway, because Hesta has never once in her life respected a request to stop, "that fits you like your own coat."

We stood in the barn with the mules breathing steam around us, and I thought about Tam Ostwick on his switchback, choosing his papers over his warmth, and I thought about sealed gray wax, and I thought about a crown that had spent nine years pretending my country never existed suddenly very interested in anyone who could speak to steel.

"The satchel," Hesta said. "Where is it?"

"Inside my coat."

"And the papers?"

"Sealed."

She held out her hand, palm up, in the universal gesture of *give it here before you get us both killed.* And I want to be clear: I trust Hesta with my life. I have trusted her with my life, repeatedly, on evidence. But my hand went to the satchel strap and stopped there, and I heard — not with my ears, this is important, not with my ears — I heard the sword murmur.

Ten names now, murmuring in the steel, and the newest one saying, in a voice like frost forming: *carry it north. Please. Carry it north.*

"Tomorrow," I said to Hesta. "I'll decide tomorrow."

She gave me the look that means *later is where good smugglers go to die,* and I gave her the look back that means *I know,* and neither of us said anything else, because we're professionals.

But I slept that night in her loft with the satchel under my head like a pillow full of knives, and the sword whispering ten names against my hip, and somewhere below us in the dark town of Grayford, a crier's bell rang the hour, and I lay awake doing arithmetic I didn't like: how many hours until dawn, how many miles to the capital, how many days since a boy named Ostwick froze on my switchback carrying a message he'd died to protect.

The seals were gray wax. Unmarked.

I knew gray wax. Everyone from before knew gray wax. It was the seal of the Thornfell chancery, the last chancery, the one that was supposed to have burned with everything else nine years ago.

Which meant either someone was forging the seals of a dead country — a cruel joke, a trap, a hunter's lure — or the chancery had never entirely burned, and somewhere in these mountains that officially contained nothing, something of Thornfell was still writing letters.

And it had written one to the north.

And a boy had carried it until his legs gave out.

And now it was under my head, humming against my skull, waiting for me to decide whether I was still the crown's woman with false papers and a buried name, or something older, something with a different oath in my mouth and a country in my chest that the maps say was never there at all.

Dawn was three hours off. The criers would start at seven.

I sat up in the hay, drew the sword halfway from its sheath, and listened to ten dead voices argue about what I should do.

Every single one of them said the same thing.

Go north.
