## 2. Proclamation at Grayford

Grayford smelled the way it always did in autumn: woodsmoke, wet wool, and the particular sourness of a town that had learned to mind its own business and was proud of it. I came down off the pass road with salt dust on my coat and a dead man's name still lodged behind my teeth like a plum stone, and I told myself the day would be ordinary.

I have been wrong before. I will be wrong again. But rarely so expensively.

The market square was doing its morning arithmetic — geese arguing with geese, a fishwife shouting down the price of eels because apparently even eels were subject to inflation now — and above all of it, on the steps of the tollhouse, stood three men in crown grey with a drum between them.

I stopped at a cheese stall. You learn things standing at cheese stalls. Nobody watches a woman buying cheese.

The drum went *rat-a-tat*, and the crier unrolled his paper with the ceremony of a man who believed he was delivering scripture.

"By order of His Majesty's Grace," he called, "and by writ of the Office of Purity and Record: be it known that the crime of *speechcraft* is hereby elevated to high treason against the crown and crown's memory."

I paid for my cheese. Slowly.

"Any person found to possess the gift of spoken steel, whispered stone, or named flame is to be taken to the assizes at Carrowmere. Any person *harboring* such a one shall share their judgment." He paused for effect. Criers love effect. "Any person who has *heard* such speech and does not report it within three days shall be fined, branded, or both, according to the magistrate's pleasure."

A murmur went through the square, the low, wet sound of fear finding its level. A woman near me pulled her child closer. An old man spat, thought better of it, and pretended he'd been clearing his throat.

"And further," the crier said, and here his voice took on the oily relish of a man reading the good part, "let it be known that the gift may hide in any face. In the miller. In the midwife. In your neighbor of twenty years. The crown asks you to look at those beside you and ask yourselves: *what do they know that they should not?*"

There it was. Not a proclamation — an invitation. The crown wasn't hunting speakers anymore. It was deputizing everyone else.

I tucked my cheese into my coat and walked toward the far side of the square at the pace of a woman with nowhere urgent to be, which is the only pace worth having when you want to disappear.

* * *

Her name was Odile Brann. She kept the mending stall two doors down from the cheese woman, and she had darned my left glove twice last winter without charging me for the second time, because she said a glove that failed twice was her fault, not mine, and she'd stand by her work or die ashamed.

I use that phrasing deliberately. Hold onto it.

She was at her stall when I passed, sorting buttons by color with the fierce concentration of a woman who had opinions about buttons. She looked up, saw me, and did something small and terrible: she smiled, and then she *stopped* smiling, and her eyes flicked — just once, just briefly — toward the tollhouse steps.

She knew what the criers meant. And she knew what I was.

I had never told her. I want that on the record, wherever records go when countries are erased. I had never told Odile Brann anything except that yes, the pass road was flooded at the second ford, and no, I didn't know when the salt caravans would run again. But I'd lived in Grayford two winters under the name Marta Fell, and Marta Fell had a way of looking at knives that isn't natural, and Odile Brann was not a stupid woman. She'd never asked. That was the arrangement. That was the whole fragile architecture of a life built on false papers: nobody asks, nobody tells, everybody pretends the roof will hold.

At noon, it didn't.

I heard them before I saw them — boots, too many boots, moving through the market with the unhurried confidence of men who had paperwork behind them. Four greys and a clerk with a ledger, and they came straight down the row of stalls like a plow through a field, and they stopped at the mending stall.

"Odile Brann?" the clerk said, though he was reading it off his own page, so the question was purely decorative.

"That's me." Her voice was steady. I'll give her that forever; her voice was steady.

"You are accused of harboring knowledge of speechcraft. A witness states that on the fourteenth of Frostmonth last, you received from a traveler certain words spoken to steel, and did not report them."

"I mend gloves," Odile said. "I don't listen to travelers' knives."

"Nevertheless."

They took her arm. She didn't struggle — a woman of sixty doesn't struggle with four greys and win, and she knew the arithmetic as well as anyone — but as they turned her toward the tollhouse she did one thing. Just one. She looked back over the crowd, over the geese and the eels and the cowering cheese, and her eyes swept past me the way a hand sweeps past the one thing in a room you must not touch.

And then she looked away. Deliberately. She spent her last free act in Grayford making sure no one saw her see me.

I stood there with a wheel of cheese under my arm and learned exactly how heavy a favor can be. Two winters of unasked questions, two gloves' worth of kindness, and she paid for it with whatever happens at the assizes at Carrowmere, and she paid it *quietly*, and the worst part — the part I keep turning over like a coin with a bad edge — is that if she'd pointed at me, screamed *there, that's the one*, she might have bought herself leniency. The criers had practically written the script for her.

She wouldn't read it.

I bought nothing else. I walked out of the square with my spine loose and my face bored, and behind me the drum started up again, and the crier's voice followed me down the lane like weather:

"The crown thanks its loyal subjects. The crown *sees* its loyal subjects."

* * *

My lodging was a rented room above the cooper's shop, and I gave myself until dark to clear it, because leaving in daylight marks you and leaving after full dark marks you worse. Dusk is the smuggler's hour. Everything true about moving unseen lives in that thin grey seam.

While the light died, I did inventory.

False papers first. Marta Fell's whole existence fit in a oilcloth packet: a travel writ signed by a magistrate who'd taken my silver and my silence in equal measure, a bill of lading for salt I'd already delivered, a letter of character from a priest in Lowmarsh who owed me for a delivery he never wanted to know the contents of. Good papers. Expensive papers. Papers that had gotten me through eleven checkpoints and two inspections and one very thorough sergeant with a nose like a ferret.

I fed them to the hearth one page at a time.

You'd think burning your own identity would feel like freedom. It doesn't. It feels like amputation — clean cuts, done quickly, while watching your own hands do it. The writ went first, curling blue at the edges. Then the bill of lading. Then the priest's letter, and I confess I held that one a moment longer, because Father Aldous had written *Marta Fell is honest, sober, and known to me these three years*, and whoever Marta Fell had been, she'd been the closest thing to a person I'd managed to build out of the wreckage, and it seemed rude not to give her a proper funeral.

"Marta," I said to the fire, "you were honest, sober, and known to me these three years. Rest easy."

The fire crackled. Somewhere below, the cooper's dog barked at nothing, then thought better of it.

Then there was the sword.

It lay across my bedroll where it always lies, plain scabbard, worn grip, the kind of blade you could set down in any barracks in the nine provinces and no one would look twice. Which is the point. Which has always been the point. The crown took everything else from Thornfell — the name, the maps, the graves, the language — but they never figured out how to take the swordspeech, because you can't confiscate a thing that lives in the throat.

Ten names in this steel. Ten dead I'd avenged or buried or simply witnessed, each one spoken into the metal so they wouldn't vanish the way Thornfell vanished. The sword remembers. That's the gift and that's the curse and that's the whole of it: the blade holds what the world refuses to.

I drew it an inch and listened, the way I had in the hayloft, and the voices murmured up at me like water over stones. And underneath the ten, faint as a heartbeat under a floorboard, something new. Something that hadn't been there before Grayford.

Not a dead voice. A living one. Mine — the shape of my own name, the one I hadn't spoken aloud since the fall, since the oath, since I'd folded it up small and buried it under *Marta Fell* and seven other names in seven other towns. It pressed against the steel like a face against a window.

"Not yet," I told it. "You don't get out yet."

The blade hummed, unconvinced.

I wrapped it in sacking, slung it across my back under my coat where it made me look hunchbacked and poor — the two most invisible conditions a woman can have — and went down the stairs past the cooper's door, past the dog who gave me one dignified nod, out into the street where the first real cold of the season was coming down off the mountains like a debt collector.

* * *

Here is what I decided at the edge of town, standing where the north road forks away from the river road, with Grayford's lamps guttering behind me and Odile Brann somewhere in a cell learning what loyalty costs.

I could run west. Everyone runs west. West is the coast, the ferries, the free ports where papers are a suggestion and half the population is lying about something. West was sensible. West was what Marta Fell would do.

But the sword had voted in the hayloft, ten dead voices strong, unanimous for once in its chatty existence. *Go north.* North is the Thornfells. North is smuggler roads and passes that close by Frostmonth and valleys the crown struck from every map and record forty years ago and then pretended had never existed. North is a country that officially isn't there, which makes it the one place in the nine provinces where a woman with no country can stop pretending.

And north, if the roads gossip true, is where the crown's wagons have been going. Hunters' convoys, moving prisoners up-country instead of to Carrowmere. Odd, that. You don't haul traitors toward the mountains unless the mountains matter to you. You don't close the passes — and the ferryman at the last crossing swore the passes were closing — unless there's something up there you're afraid of.

The crown fears something in my mountains. Well. Symmetry is a comfort. I fear the crown right back.

So: north. Not because it's wise. Because Odile Brann mended my glove twice and never asked why a salt courier needed hands like a duelist, and because the least I owe her is to become the thing she apparently believed I was worth protecting. Because ten dead people pulled me north like a tide. Because somewhere in the high country there are others like me — speakers, survivors, exiles of a nation that exists now only in stolen steel and stubborn memory — and the crown is hunting them with criers and clerks and neighbors turned informer, and someone has to be ahead of the hunt, walking the roads that no longer exist, telling people to run.

Fear is a fine fuel. I ran on it for years — fear of the oath, fear of the hunters, fear of the small daily death of being no one. But fear only carries you *away* from things. Somewhere between the cheese stall and the fork in the road, it had changed chemistry on me, the way grief does if you let it sit long enough. It had sharpened into something with an edge and a direction.

Resolve, I think the priests call it. We had another word for it in Thornfell. I haven't spoken that word in years either. Maybe the sword remembers it. The sword remembers everything.

I took the north road.

For six days I moved like smoke — byways, sheep tracks, the smugglers' cuts that don't appear on any surveyor's chart because the surveyors' grandfathers helped erase them. I avoided the toll bridges. I slept in culverts and bothies and once, memorably, in a shrine to a saint whose name had also been struck from the records, which felt fitting; the saint and I split the floor and neither of us complained.

On the sixth day, in the high country where the pines go black and the wind comes down the valleys with old words caught in it, I heard wheels.

Wagon wheels, many, and the jingle of harness, and under it — carried on the wind with terrible clarity — a sound I knew the way you know your own heartbeat. The sound of a voice speaking to something that wasn't air.

Someone ahead of me on the high road was using the gift. Someone young. Someone frightened.

And around that someone, from the sound of the escort — twenty men, maybe more, greys, spears, a cage wagon rattling over the stones —

the crown had gotten there first.

I pulled the sacking off my sword and started running.
