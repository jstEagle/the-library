## 3. The Boy in the Cage Wagon

I had planned to be a coward about it. That was the whole strategy, if I'm honest: keep to the treeline, count the escort, decide it was too many, and walk away with my conscience filed neatly under *not my problem*. I'd had a great deal of practice at that particular filing system. It was one of the few skills Thornfell ever taught me that the crown found useful.

The high road above Grayford ran white and empty in the early light, and the convoy crawled along it like something wounded — six riders, a wagon, and a cage bolted to the wagon's bed. Iron bars, iron roof, and inside it, small enough that my first thought was *they've caught a child*, a boy.

I crouched in the pines and did the arithmetic every smuggler learns before they learn their letters. Six armed riders against one woman with a sword she never let speak. The sum came out the same way it always did: stay still, stay silent, stay nothing.

Then the wind shifted, and I heard him.

Not crying. Crying I could have ignored; crying is common as rain on this road. No — he was whispering. A thin, cracked thread of sound through the bars, words in no language I knew, and where they fell, the frost on the cage floor *bloomed*. Little white flowers of ice, opening petal by petal along the iron seams, spreading up the bars like ivy made of winter. The nearest rider laughed and knocked them off with his gauntlet, casual as a man brushing crumbs from his sleeve, and the boy flinched so hard he pressed himself against the far wall of the cage as though he could dissolve into it.

Stone bloomed too, I noticed. Where the wagon wheel had ground against a boulder at the roadside, pale lichen-like flowers had opened in the rock itself. Living stone, flowering at a frightened child's murmur.

My hand was on my sword hilt before I'd decided anything, which is how my worst decisions always begin.

*Not your problem,* said the sensible voice, the one that had kept me alive through nine years of salt runs and false papers. *He's crown property. He's bait or evidence or leverage, and in every one of those cases, the smart move is to be elsewhere.*

But I had watched him flinch. I had watched a grown man in a king's coat knock the flowers off a child's cage like he was tidying up, and something old and unburied turned over in my chest — something with a Thornfell accent.

"Six," I murmured to myself. "It's six against one, Vesper. Even you can't make that sum lie."

The sword, of course, disagreed. It hung at my hip the way it always did, quiet as a held breath, but I felt it the way you feel a dog straining at a leash when it scents something. It wanted to speak. It had been wanting to speak for three days, ever since Grayford, ever since the criers, and I had been clamping my hand over its silence like a woman muffling a scream into a pillow.

Here is what nobody tells you about swordspeech: the blade remembers everyone whose name has been spoken through it. Every death I had avenged lived in that steel, and sometimes, in the dark, it recited them back to me. I carried more names than memories. Some nights I couldn't recall my mother's face, but I could recite the courier Joss Merren's last words word for word, because I'd buried his name in the blade two winters past, alone on a frozen pass, and the sword does not forget what it is given.

Six riders. One of me. And a boy who made stone bloom.

I told myself I was only going to get closer. Count again from the rocks instead of the trees. Get a better look at the cage, confirm he was crown-registered, confirm there was nothing to be done. This is the story I tell myself even now, and I have learned to let myself have it, because the alternative is admitting that some part of me had already unsheathed the sword before my mind caught up.

* * *

The road bent around a shoulder of granite called the Shepherd's Elbow, where the cliff leaned out over the drop and the snow lay deep and blue in the shadow. It was the only place on the high road where six riders would have to go single file, and it was the only place I'd have cover within thirty feet of them, and I am not so much a fatalist as all that — I took it as an omen in my favor, which shows how far desperation will bend a smuggler's theology.

I cut ahead of them through the pines, moving the way you move when noise means hanging: slow, deliberate, each footfall placed like a coin on a merchant's scale. The convoy's lead rider was singing under his breath, some barracks song about a miller's daughter, and I hated him a little for it. Men who sing on duty are men who have never once expected the world to bite them.

The plan, such as it was, formed itself out of the terrain rather than any wisdom. Take the last rider first — the straggler, the one every column has, the one thinking about his cold feet. Then the near side of the column before the far side could turn. Then the driver, then whoever held the cage key. In that order, fast, and pray the arithmetic stayed wrong long enough for me to finish it.

I drew the sword.

You have to understand what drawing it costs me. Unsheathed, it begins to listen. And when I speak to it — not at it, *to* it, the way you'd speak to something alive — it answers, and what it says goes into the world and cannot be taken back. Every name I give it becomes part of it forever. So I ration it. I starve it. Nine years of starving it, and now I stood in the snow behind a granite elbow with a naked blade in my fist and a debt coming due.

The straggler came around the Elbow humming his miller's daughter, and I put the point of my sword against the back of his neck below the helm line and whispered to the steel:

*"Sleep."*

That's the other thing about swordspeech. It isn't shouting. It's the opposite of shouting. The blade takes whatever I say and makes it true in the smallest possible way — a word spoken soft enough to fit inside a heartbeat. The rider folded sideways off his horse without a sound, and I caught his reins before they could slap loose, because a riderless horse galloping ahead is worth ten alarms.

One.

The second rider rounded the bend into a man slumped in his saddle and had just enough time to frown before I stepped out of the shadow of the cliff. He went for his sword and got half a foot of it drawn, and I spoke low and quick — *"Fall"* — and the steel sang it into his bones, and he fell. They always fall. That's the mercy of it, if you want to call it that: my kills are quiet, and the dead don't suffer long, and I tell myself that matters.

Two.

The third and fourth were close enough together that they heard *something* — a sound like a struck bell heard underwater, which is what swordspeech sounds like to anyone standing outside it. They wheeled their horses, and one of them shouted, and the shout died in his throat because I had already spoken *"Break"* to the blade and his sword arm broke like a green branch. His companion got a crossbow up, actually got it leveled at me, and I did the thing I hate most in the world, which is to spend a name I don't know. I spoke *"Stop"* and meant his heart, and the blade understood, and he stopped.

Three. Four.

The fifth rider was smarter. He didn't fight. He put spurs to his horse and ran for Grayford with the alarm in his mouth, and I stood in the road with blood steaming off the steel and let him go, because chasing him meant leaving the cage unguarded, and I had already stopped pretending to myself about which of those mattered.

The driver hauled the wagon around in a wide panicked arc, and the horses screamed, and the cage lurched, and from inside it came a sound I will hear until I die: not screaming. Whispering. Faster and faster, that flower-language pouring out of him, and the iron bars of the cage began to bloom — thick frost-flowers, then buds of living stone pushing up between the bars like the cage was trying to become a garden, like the boy inside it was trying to grow himself a door.

And the driver drew a knife and reached back toward the cage, toward the blossoming, and I understood with a clarity that needed no gift at all: orders. If the cargo blooms, burn the cargo. That's what the criers had meant, underneath the official language. Not arrest. Erasure.

I crossed the distance between us faster than I should have been able to, given the snow, given everything, and I didn't use the sword at all. I used the flat of it, both hands, across the side of his head, and he dropped the knife and dropped after it, and the wagon rolled to a stop crooked across the road with its wheels ticking.

Five. All five accounted for, one running, none dead by my hand except the archer, and I stood there in the ringing quiet breathing like a bellows and feeling the sword pull at my grip, hungry, asking *who else, who else, say someone else's name* — and I sheathed it. Hard. Like slamming a door on a conversation I couldn't afford to have.

* * *

The boy was pressed into the corner of the cage with his knees to his chest, watching me with the particular stillness of prey animals and children who have learned what adults are for. Frost-flowers covered the bars around him. Up close I could see they weren't frost at all — they were stone gone soft and shaped, petals of granite fine as paper, and they were warm. I touched one and snatched my fingers back.

"You're hurt?" I asked. Stupid question. Of course he was hurt. Everyone in a cage is hurt; that's what cages are for.

He didn't answer. His eyes went to my sword, and whatever he saw there made him press harder into the corner.

"I'm not with them." I held up my hands, empty-ish, the sword pointed at the ground. "I'm — look, I'm nobody. Smuggler. Salt, mostly. Very boring."

Nothing. But the blooming had slowed, the stone-buds frozen mid-open, listening the way the sword listened. Everything in these mountains listens, I thought. That's the trouble with them.

"The key," I said, mostly to myself, and found it on the driver's belt — a black iron thing, crown-stamped, heavier than it needed to be. Locks always are. I turned it in the cage door and the mechanism fought me, rusted spiteful, and then gave, and the door swung open onto a boy who did not move.

"Can you walk?"

A nod, barely.

"Good. Because we're going to need to be a very long way from here by the time that rider reaches Grayford, and I don't intend to carry you, and I don't intend to be caught, and those are the only two things I'm sure of right now."

He unfolded himself slowly, joint by joint, like something that had been folded a long time. Fourteen, maybe fifteen. Dark-haired, hollow-cheeked, wrists ringed with healing chafe-marks. He wore a plain gray smock with no mark of house or hold, which was its own kind of answer — the crown doesn't put sigils on things it plans to burn.

He stepped down out of the cage and stood in the road, swaying, and looked at the fallen riders, and looked at me, and said, in an accent that went through me like a fishhook:

"Are you going to kill me too?"

Thornfell. He was Thornfell-born. I knew it the way you know a song from your childhood — not the words anymore, the crown made sure of that, but the shape of the melody, the lift at the end of the sentence that no southern tongue could counterfeit.

"No," I said. And then, because lying to children is a habit I've mostly broken: "I don't know what I'm going to do with you. But I'm not going to kill you, and neither is anyone else today. Can you run?"

Another nod.

"Then run when I run, stop when I stop, and don't talk to me until we're off this road, because I'm currently having an argument with myself and I'm losing."

Something flickered across his face that might, in a different life, have been the ghost of a smile. He glanced once at the blooming cage — the stone flowers already graying, dying back to ordinary rock as their maker moved away from them — and then at me.

"What's your name?" he asked.

And there it was, the question, the one I'd spent nine years making sure no one had reason to ask. Names are currency in these mountains, and mine was counterfeit twice over: Vesper Eastwick, salt-smuggler, born nowhere in particular, loyal to the crown in the way of a woman who has signed papers saying so.

"Later," I said. "Names later. Walk."

We went up off the high road into the pines as the sun cleared the ridge, and behind us the wagon sat crooked in the snow with its dead garden of stone, and somewhere south a rider was carrying the alarm toward Grayford at a gallop. I told myself I'd find a shepherd's bothy, leave the boy with food and directions, and melt back into my small gray life by week's end.

I want it noted, for the record, that I almost believed myself.

Because as we climbed, the boy stumbled, and without thinking — without *anything*, just reflex, the way you catch a stranger's child falling — I caught his arm. And he looked up at me with those hollow, watchful eyes and said, softly, in that accent like a hook behind my ribs:

"They said someone from the old roads would come. They said to keep walking until she found me."

The snow made a sound like the world holding its breath.

"And I've been walking," he said, "for eleven days."
