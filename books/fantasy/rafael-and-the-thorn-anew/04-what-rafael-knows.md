## 4. What Rafael Knows

The bothy smelled like sheep and old smoke, which I want to say was an improvement over the cage wagon, and I want to say it sincerely, but the truth is the wagon at least had a lock on it, and locks mean someone has decided what to do with you. The bothy just had a hole in the roof and a woman with a sword who kept looking at me like I was a letter she'd opened by mistake.

"You're staring," she said.

"You're staring harder."

"I'm armed. My staring counts for more."

She had a point. She'd had several points since the road — the kind made of steel and spoken syllables that had turned four grown men into very quiet piles of equipment. I was trying not to think about that. I was also failing, because every time I closed my eyes I heard her blade say *Corvin* in a voice like a bell dropped down a well, and then the man named Corvin stopped being a problem forever.

I pulled my knees up on the straw pallet and tried to be small. I'd gotten good at being small. Eleven years of practice, give or take the parts I couldn't remember.

Vesper — she hadn't given me a name yet, actually, but I'd heard one of the guards use it before she unmade him, so let's call her Vesper — Vesper crouched by the hearth and coaxed a fire out of damp peat with the patience of someone who had done it a thousand times in places she wasn't supposed to be.

"Food," she said, not turning around. "Then talking. In that order. You look like a scarecrow that lost an argument with winter."

"I feel like one too."

"Good. Honest assessment is the beginning of survival."

She produced bread from somewhere in her coat — smugglers' coats are bottomless, I would learn; they contain bread, rope, lies, and other people's secrets in roughly equal measure — and tore it in half. Not the bigger half for herself. The bigger half came across the room to me, and she did it without ceremony, without watching to see if I noticed, which is how I knew it wasn't kindness performed. It was just arithmetic. I was smaller. I needed more.

I ate like something feral. I'm not proud of the sounds I made. Somewhere in the middle of it I started crying, which was embarrassing, and she pretended not to see, which was the kindest thing anyone had done for me in a year.

* * *

When the bread was gone and my hands had stopped shaking — mostly — she sat back against the wall opposite me, sword across her knees, and said, "Now. Talk."

"About what?"

"Let's start with why the crown is hauling you through the Thornfell passes in a cage wagon with six guards and a witch-hunter's writ." Her eyes were gray, the color of the sky over the pass, the color of a thing that has decided not to have an opinion yet. "And don't tell me you stole a pie. Nobody burns pie-thieves."

"They might if the pie talked."

Something moved at the corner of her mouth. Not quite a smile. A smile considering an application.

"The stones," she said. "On the road. When you were frightened."

I looked at my hands. There was still grit under the nails from when I'd grabbed the wall of the cutting, and where I'd grabbed it, moss had come up through the cracks like green fire, fast as breath, wrong as breath. "It's not — I don't do it on purpose. It happens when I'm scared. Words get into things. Living things mostly. Stones if they've got moss on them, or lichen, or if they're old enough. Old stones remember being part of a mountain, and mountains are alive in a way nobody wants to admit."

"And the guards heard you do it."

"One of them did. He laughed. He said, *well, well, a little speaker*, like he'd found a coin in the mud. And then everything got much worse and much more official." I wrapped my arms around my shins. "They put me in the wagon and read me a list of crimes. Most of them I didn't understand. One of them was 'possession of unsanctioned speech.' I asked what speech was sanctioned and the witch-hunter hit me."

Vesper was quiet for a while. The fire popped. Outside, wind worried at the loose shutter like a dog with a bone.

"Where are your parents?" she asked finally.

"That's the strange part." I took a breath, because I'd been circling this since the wagon, since before the wagon, since the morning a stranger pressed a coin into my hand and told me to walk. "I don't know. I don't remember them. I remember a kitchen. I remember a song about a river that goes uphill. I remember someone saying *run north and don't stop until the roads stop having names*. But faces—" I shook my head. "There's nothing where the faces should be. Just the shape of where they used to be."

Something crossed her face then, quick as a fish under ice. I thought it was pity. Later I would learn it was recognition, which is worse.

"How old are you?" she asked.

"Eleven. Maybe twelve. I counted winters until I ran out of winters to count."

"And who told you to walk the Thornfell roads?"

Here is the thing I had been holding in my chest like a swallowed stone: I didn't know. And I had known I didn't know, and I had walked anyway, because the alternative was standing still, and standing still felt like dying politely.

"A man," I said. "In Grayford. Or near it. He found me sleeping in a tithe-barn. He was old, and his accent was funny — soft, like all his consonants had been worn down by weather. He gave me bread and a copper coin with a tree stamped on it, and he said—" I closed my eyes and made my mouth do the memory, because some things you keep exact or you lose them. "He said: *Walk the high roads into the Thornfells. Keep walking until someone finds you. They'll know what you are. Don't tell anyone your name except them.*"

"Them who?"

"I asked him that." I shrugged. "He smiled like I'd asked him the funniest question in the world and said, *the ones who remember.* Then he was gone. I don't even know which way he went. It was like he'd never been there at all, except for the coin." I dug in my pocket and held it out. The copper was worn nearly smooth, but the tree was still there — a tree with its roots drawn above the ground, which I had always assumed was bad minting.

Vesper did not take it. She looked at it the way you'd look at a snake that had learned to hold still.

"Say the man's words again," she said quietly. "*The ones who remember.*"

"*The ones who remember.* That's what I said."

"And he told you to walk the high roads. Through the passes. In autumn. Alone. A child whose gift flares when he's afraid."

"When you put it like that it sounds stupid."

"It isn't stupid." She stood and went to the window slit, and the gray light cut her face into halves. "It's clever. That's what's wrong with it."

I didn't understand. I want to claim that I did, that I saw it all unfolding the way she did, but I was eleven and hungry and the bothy was warm and for the first time in weeks nobody was hitting me. Understanding came later, the way most understanding does: too late to help, just early enough to hurt.

"Nobody finds a gifted child by accident," she said to the window. "Not out here. The high roads are smuggler roads. Crown patrols avoid them because the maps lie and the bridges argue. If someone wanted a speaker found — a specific kind of speaker, young enough to be shaped, loud enough to be traced — they wouldn't hunt him. Hunting makes noise. They'd *seed* him. Put him on the road like bait on a hook and wait for the fish to come to him."

"What fish?"

She turned around. For a moment she looked older than the mountains, and tired in a way that had nothing to do with walking.

"Me," she said. "Or people like me. People the crown can't find any other way."

* * *

I want to record, honestly, what happened inside me when she said that, because it wasn't what should have happened. What should have happened is fear — the cold drop of realizing you've been used, that your whole lonely walk was someone else's plan moving through you like water through a pipe. And there was some of that. But underneath it, shamefully, there was relief. Enormous, ridiculous relief.

Because if I was bait, then I was *for* something. Someone had wanted me found. Somebody, somewhere, had set me walking toward people who would know what I was — and whether that ended in a cage or a hearth, it meant I hadn't been walking toward nothing. An eleven-year-old can live on almost nothing, but not on nothing.

"Do you know who remembers?" I asked her. "The ones the man meant?"

The silence went on long enough that the fire had to be fed. She fed it. When she spoke, she spoke to the flames.

"There was a country here once," she said. "Before the crown. Before the maps got rewritten. It had a name, and the name was taken out of every ledger, every church bell inscription, every grandmother's mouth that the king's men could reach. People were moved. Records were burned. Roads were renamed so many times they forgot themselves." Her hand rested on the sword at her knee, lightly, the way you'd touch a sleeping animal. "Some of us still remember pieces of it. Some of us remember too much."

"Thornfell," I said.

Her head came up fast. "Where did you hear that word?"

"Nowhere. I— " And this was true, and it frightened me more than anything the guards had done. "I've always known it. Like the song about the uphill river. It's just *in* me. Is that — is that where I'm from? Is that why I can't remember my parents, because they were from somewhere that got erased?"

Vesper crouched in front of me, and up close her eyes weren't hard at all. They were exhausted, and careful, and full of a grief she was holding very still, the way you hold a full cup across a crowded room.

"Yes," she said. "That's my guess. Your people hid you so deep even you couldn't find yourself. And then somebody dug you up and pointed you at the mountains like an arrow." She exhaled slowly. "The question is whether the arrow was aimed by a friend or by the crown."

"How do we find out?"

"We don't. We survive first. Finding out is a luxury." She rose, went to the door, and listened to the wind for a while. "We move at first light. There's a valley east of here where the crown's maps show solid rock, and there isn't rock, there's a road, and at the end of the road there may be people. Real ones. Ones who remember."

"The ones who remember," I said.

"Don't hope yet. Hope is heavy and we're climbing." But she said it gently, and she glanced back at me, and for one second her whole face did something complicated that I was too young and too tired to read.

* * *

We shared the watch, sort of. She took the real watch. I took the pretend watch, which meant I lay on the pallet listening to the wind and the fire and the sound of her breathing, and thinking.

Here's what I decided, lying there in the dark with sheep-smell in my nose: I didn't care if I was bait. Let them use me. Bait gets carried to the hook; bait doesn't have to find its own way. And if the hook was a country that had been stolen out of the world — a country that lived on in worn coins and uphill rivers and a word I'd known before I knew my own name — then I would rather be bait for that than treasure for anything else.

Besides. She'd given me the bigger half.

Somewhere past midnight I heard her speaking very low, and I thought at first she was praying, and then I realized she was talking to the sword, and then I realized the sword was answering — a sound like a word spoken underwater, one syllable, over and over, patient as tide. I didn't catch what it said. I only caught the way her voice cracked around whatever she said back, quiet as an apology.

I fell asleep before I could wonder too hard about what a woman talks to her sword about in the dark. That was lucky. There were things in that blade I would spend chapters wishing I'd never heard.

I woke to her boot nudging mine and the sky outside still black.

"Up," she said. "Bait walks best before dawn."

Outside, the first flakes of the season were coming down, fat and slow, like the mountains deciding something.
