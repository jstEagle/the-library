## 5. Oathbound

There is a particular kind of cold that lives in oath-magic. Not the cold of the passes — I know that one like a cousin who visits too often and eats all the cheese. This is colder. It sits behind the sternum, in the exact place where a person keeps the thing they swore, and it does not thaw. Ever. I have carried mine for nine years now, and some mornings I wake up and think, *Vesper, old girl, you've finally gone numb*, and then I move wrong and feel it turn over inside me like a stone at the bottom of a well, and I remember: no. Not numb. Just paid for.

Let me tell you how you buy a debt like that.

* * *

The year Thornfell fell, I was twenty-two years old and already tired, which tells you something about my judgment. The war — if you can call eleven days of burning *a war* — had ended with royal clerks riding through what remained of the mountain country with ledgers and lamps, striking names from records the way a housewife strikes lice from a seam. Village by village. River by river. There is an art to unmaking a place, and the crown had mastered it. You do not raze everything. Razing leaves ruins, and ruins leave questions. You simply stop writing it down. You let the roads grow over. You pay the mapmakers extra.

I know this because I held the lamp.

Not the decision. I was nobody's councilor; I was a swordswoman with a gift that made people nervous even before they understood it, and the crown had learned there were people like me scattered through Thornfell like seeds in flour. Speakers. Our blades remembered the dead we avenged, spoke their names back into the world when the world tried to forget them. In a country being erased name by name, a sword that insisted on remembering was not a curiosity. It was evidence.

So they gathered us — those they caught, and those who surrendered rather than be caught — into the courtyard of a garrison whose name I will not repeat here, because repeating it feels like picking at a scab that has been holding my whole self together. And they offered us a trade. Your gift, sworn to the crown. Your silence about what you've seen. In exchange: your life, and a paper saying you never lived anywhere called Thornfell, because nowhere called Thornfell ever existed, and isn't it strange how you thought it did? Grief does funny things to the memory. Here, sign.

There was a man in front of me in that line. Older than me by thirty years, with hands like tree roots and a voice that shook. He asked the clerk, very politely, whether his wife's name would still exist somewhere. Anywhere. A ledger, a gravestone, a prayer. The clerk said the question was irrelevant, as the woman in question had never existed, and would he please state his name for the record.

He stated it. And then he drew a knife — not at the clerk, not at anyone, just out, the way a man draws breath — and the crossbowmen did what crossbowmen do, and the clerk wiped his pen on his sleeve and said, "Next," and I stepped forward because stepping backward wasn't among my options.

Here is the thing nobody warns you about oaths: they are spoken aloud, but they are *written* somewhere else. When the oath-priest put his hand on my blade and had me swear — loyalty to the crown above blood, silence above grief, service above self — the words didn't go into the air. They went into the steel. My sword took them the way it takes the names of the dead, and I felt it happen, a cold settling along the fuller like water finding its level. From that day forward, my blade knew two kinds of words: the ones I gave it in mourning, and the one I gave it in surrender.

And the magic keeps books. That's the part they don't tell you in the pretty ceremony with the ribbons. Oath-magic is a lender. It gives you your life up front, interest-free for exactly as long as you keep the terms, and then — the moment you don't — it begins collection. I have seen a breaker of oaths. Once. A smuggler named Corvin Hale, out of the lowlands, who'd sworn crown service in his youth and then run. What came for him didn't come fast. It came like winter comes: first the dreams, then the forgetting of small things — the taste of bread, the color of his mother's hair — then the forgetting of large ones, until the day he sat down in the middle of the road outside Grayford and could not remember which direction walking went. The oath hadn't killed him. It had *unwritten* him, politely, in order of importance, and left a body that breathed and blinked and owned nothing, not even a past.

They found him a place in a charity house. Sometimes I pass such houses and wonder how many of the smiling vacant souls inside once signed their names on the wrong line.

So understand: when I say I broke my oath, I am not describing a decision. Decisions are things you make once, at a table, with wine. Breaking my oath is something I have been doing slowly, daily, since the morning I turned off the king's road with a caged boy behind me and pointed my boots north instead of south. Every mile is another coin against the loan. And the lender knows. Oh, the lender knows.

* * *

Which brings me to three nights ago, or four — the counting gets slippery up here — in a shepherd's bothy with rain coming through the roof like a tax collector through a poor man's door.

Rafael slept by the peat fire with his knees drawn up, and I sat across from him sharpening a blade that didn't need it, because the alternative was sleeping, and sleeping meant dreaming, and dreaming had started meaning something worse than dreams.

It began as a pull. I want to be precise about this, because precision is all I have left that's mine. It was not a voice. The oath doesn't speak; speaking is for swords and priests. It was more like standing on a hillside with the wind at your back — except the wind was behind my ribs, and the hillside sloped south, toward Grayford, toward the capital, toward the garrison courtyard where a clerk had once wiped his pen and said *next*. My body wanted to walk. Not urgently. Patiently. The way water wants downhill. The way debts want paying.

I stood up. Walked to the door. Put my hand on the latch.

"Vesper?" Rafael's voice, thick with sleep. "Where's the fire?"

"Outside," I said, which was true, in the way that most cowardly answers are technically true. "Go back to sleep."

But I stood there with my hand on the latch for a long time, feeling the pull lean on me like a drunk leaning on a fence, and I understood two things with perfect clarity. One: the oath had noticed my treason, and it was beginning collection, gently at first, the way all creditors begin. Two: I had perhaps weeks. Maybe less. Corvin Hale lasted a season after his first dream of the capital road. I intended to last longer, mostly out of spite.

Because here is the arithmetic I did in that doorway, with the rain ticking the thatch and the boy breathing slow behind me: every step I take toward the Door of Anew is a step away from the capital, and the oath pulls the other way, so each mile costs double. But the alternative was delivering Rafael to whatever waited for him in the hunters' wagons, and I had looked into that boy's eyes while stone bloomed under his whisper, and I had already spent something there that I couldn't get back. Call it a down payment on a different kind of oath. Unwritten. Unwitnessed. Possibly stupid.

I seem to specialize in those.

* * *

By dawn the pull had teeth.

I woke — if you can call it waking, I mostly surf the top of sleep these nights like a man on thin ice — and my feet were cold, which was odd, because I'd banked the fire and wrapped my blanket to my chin. Then I realized my boots were on. Laced. And I was standing at the door again with the latch in my hand, and outside the light was the gray that comes before the gray that comes before dawn, and I had no memory of getting up.

That's how it starts. Not the forgetting — the *doing*. The oath doesn't argue with you. It simply borrows your legs while you're not looking, the way a child borrows scissors.

I sat down hard on the bench and unlaced the boots and retied them facing the wall, which is a nonsense gesture and made me feel better anyway, and then Rafael stirred and said, "You talk in your sleep."

"Wonderful. What do I say?"

"Names." He rubbed his eyes. "Lots of them. And then one word over and over, near morning." He hesitated, and I watched him decide whether to say it, and lost the bet either way. "*South.* You kept saying *south, south, south,* like you were arguing with somebody."

"I was," I said. "I'm losing."

He looked at me the way boys look at you when they've decided you're a puzzle worth solving and also possibly dangerous, which is, to be fair, accurate. "Is it the oath? Maren used to—" He stopped. Something crossed his face, quick and painful, the ghost of a name he maybe shouldn't have said aloud in front of me. "People talked. Back home. About what happens to speakers who break crown word."

"Back home," I repeated, carefully. "Which is where?"

He pulled his blanket up to his chin. "A place that doesn't exist," he said, and there was a bitterness in it far too old for his face, and I thought: *bait or not, someone taught this boy to guard his mouth, and whoever it was loved him.*

"The oath-magic," I told him, because lying to him suddenly seemed like carrying a second sword, heavier than the first, "is a debt. I borrowed my life from the crown nine years ago, and I've stopped making payments. So now the crown collects. First it walks you south in your sleep. Then it takes your dreams. Then—" I flexed my hand, watching the knuckles whiten. "Then it takes everything else, in reverse order of how much it hurts."

"What comes after the dreams?"

"You don't want the inventory."

"Yes I do."

Of course he did. He was twelve, or nearly; the age when children demand the whole corpse laid out on the table. "Tastes. Faces. Your own name, last, so you have nothing left to lose when it finishes." I shrugged, as though I were describing the weather in some country I'd never visit. "Corvin Hale sat down in a road once and couldn't remember what legs were for. That's the end state. A body, walking wherever the wind pushes it, owning nothing."

"That's why the hunters don't chase deserters sometimes," Rafael said quietly. "They just wait. The oath hunts for them."

"Now you're learning economics." I stood, tested my weight on my traitorous boots, and found the pull waiting, patient as tide. South tugged at me like a fishhook set behind the breastbone. North, the mountains stood black against the graying sky, and somewhere up in that blackness was a door older than the country it exited, and somewhere between here and there was a colony of people who should not exist, and a woman named Maren Ostwick whom Rafael had almost mentioned and clearly feared, and a winter that was already sharpening its knives in the high valleys.

"How long do you have?" Rafael asked. "Before it — before you're like the man in the road?"

"Hale lasted from first frost to midwinter." I slung my pack. "I plan to beat his record."

"And if the Door needs—"

"It doesn't need anything from me yet." A lie so smooth I almost believed it myself, and hated how good I'd gotten at those. Because I did know — or guessed, which in the mountains amounts to the same thing — that doors like the Door of Anew do not open for free any more than oaths come without interest. Everything in this world that matters is bought with a name. I've known that since the courtyard. I've been saving up.

We walked out into the morning, and the bothy door swung shut behind us, and I turned my back on south with the full weight of the crown's oldest magic leaning between my shoulder blades, pushing.

Here is what nobody tells you about breaking an oath: it doesn't feel like freedom. It feels like swimming upstream in a river that knows your name. Every step north cost me, and I could feel the cost accruing somewhere behind my sternum, itemized, patient. By noon my shadow fell the wrong way — stretched south, tugging, a leash of darkness. By evening I caught myself humming a marching cadence from the garrison, one I hadn't thought of in nine years, my mouth doing the crown's work while my mind screamed at it.

And by full dark, camped under a leaning crag with Rafael asleep and the stars wheeling indifferent overhead, I drew my sword — partly to check the edge, partly because a woman is allowed one conversation per night that isn't with her own dread — and I laid the flat of it against my knee and whispered, "How much do I owe?"

The blade was silent. Blades usually are, unless they're speaking a name.

But the cold behind my sternum shifted, settled, deepened — and I swear on nothing, because nothing will have me, that I felt it *count.*

Three days later we reached Hollowmere, and that is where the road stopped being merely expensive and became something else entirely.
