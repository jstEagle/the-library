## 6. Hollowmere

The village appeared the way a bruise appears — slowly, and only when you were already hurting.

We'd been walking since before dawn, following a river that Vesper insisted was called the Liss even though no map I'd ever seen had a line for it. My feet had passed through *aching* some hours ago and into a numbness I'd decided not to think about, and my stomach had moved on from growling to something more like sulking. So when the rooftops came up out of the mist — low, turf-roofed, smoke rising straight in the windless cold — I actually laughed.

Vesper didn't laugh. She stopped, put one hand on my shoulder to keep me behind her, and studied the place like it might be a trap with chimneys.

"What's wrong?" I whispered.

"Nothing's wrong," she said, which from Vesper meant that several things were wrong but none of them had teeth yet. "It's just that this village isn't supposed to be here."

"It looks like it's here."

"That's what worries me." She lowered her hand. "Come on. But you let me do the talking."

"You always do the talking."

"Yes," she said. "Because I'm good at it, and because every time *you* open your mouth, flowers come out of the walls."

That wasn't fair. It had happened twice. Three times, if you counted the turnip.

* * *

Hollowmere had one street, a well with a roof over it like the water needed sheltering too, and perhaps forty people who all turned to look at us at once, the way sheep do. Not hostile. Just — attentive. A man splitting firewood set down his axe without being told to. A woman pulling a goat on a rope simply stood still until the goat dragged her a few confused steps sideways.

"They're waiting," Vesper murmured.

"For what?"

"For someone to tell them who they are." She said it lightly, but I felt it land somewhere under my ribs and stay there.

An old woman came out of the nearest house before we could knock on anything. She was small and round and wrapped in so many shawls she looked less dressed than upholstered, and her face was the kind of face that has decided, permanently, to be pleased about things.

"Travelers!" she said. "In winter! You'll be wanting soup."

"We'll be wanting nothing," Vesper said, "but thank—"

"Soup," the old woman repeated, with the gentle immovability of a landslide, and took Vesper by the elbow. Vesper, who had killed four men in the time it takes to sneeze, allowed herself to be steered indoors by a grandmother. I followed, mostly because the smell coming out of the house was barley and onions and I would have followed it off a cliff.

Inside it was warm and dim and crowded with objects — dried herbs hanging from beams, a loom, a cat asleep in a basket as though the basket were its whole personality. The old woman sat us at a table and put bowls in front of us and bread beside the bowls and did not accept refusal of either.

"I'm Tansy," she said. "You can eat while you answer questions. That's how we do it here. Questions go down easier with barley."

Vesper gave our names — hers false, mine true, though I noticed she hesitated half a heartbeat before saying mine, like she was setting something fragile on a table. Then Tansy asked where we'd come from, and Vesper said "the south roads," and Tansy nodded and asked where we were going, and Vesper said "north," and Tansy nodded again, and then she looked at me — really looked, the way you look at a word you almost remember — and everything changed.

"Say something," she said to me.

"Tansy—" Vesper started.

"Not you, dear. Him. Say anything."

I glanced at Vesper. Vesper's jaw was tight, but she gave the smallest nod, because refusing would have been worse.

"The soup's very good," I said.

Tansy made a sound. It wasn't quite a sob and wasn't quite a laugh; it was the sound a door makes when it hasn't been opened in years and finally gives. She pressed both hands flat against the table.

"Thornfell," she whispered. "That's Thornfell speech. The way you say *very* — you swallow the end of it, like the word's too heavy to carry all the way out. My mother talked like that. I think. I think she did." Her eyes went bright and lost. "I don't remember her face. But I remember how she said *very*."

The room had gone quiet around us. The other villagers had drifted close without seeming to move — the woodcutter in the doorway, the woman with the goat abandoned outside, two children peering around a bedpost. They weren't staring at me like a curiosity. They were staring at me like a window.

"I'm sorry," I said. "I don't—"

"Don't be sorry." Tansy patted my hand with fingers like little warm stones. "Do you know what country this is? This village?"

I looked around. "I assumed... the crown's. The Ninefold Kingdoms, or whatever they're calling it now."

"So does everyone." She smiled, and the smile was the saddest thing I'd seen in weeks, and I had recently watched a boy make stone bloom while soldiers screamed. "Ask anyone here where they were born. Go on. Ask."

I asked the woodcutter. He frowned with real effort, like a man doing sums in the dark. "Here," he said at last. "I was born here. In... Hollowmere." But he said the name of his own village the way you say a word you've written on your hand to memorize.

I asked the woman with the goat. She thought about it for a long time. "There was a valley," she said slowly. "With terraces. Apple trees on the south side. I can see it — I can *see* it — but I can't tell you its name or whose kingdom held it, and when I try, it's like reaching into a drawer that's been nailed shut." She smiled at me, embarrassed and unbothered at once. "It doesn't hurt, mind. That's the strange part. It's just a hole shaped like an answer."

"And nobody finds that terrifying?" I asked.

"Oh, we did. At first." Tansy ladled more soup into my bowl unasked. "People wept for weeks. Some left to go find their birthplaces and never came back, and I expect what they found was worse than nothing. And then one day old Bram — he was the miller — said, 'Well. If we can't remember where we're from, we'd best be from somewhere worth being,' and he planted an apple tree in the square, and that was more or less that." She shrugged inside her shawls. "Grief is heavy, dear. Most folk put it down eventually. Not because they want to. Because their arms get tired."

I understood, sitting there, why Vesper hadn't wanted to stop. This place was kind the way deep water is calm.

* * *

They gave us the loft above the meeting-house, which was really just a barn that had ambitions. Vesper checked the windows twice, sat with her sword across her knees for an hour, and finally slept — truly slept, deeply, in a way I hadn't seen her do since the cage wagon. I lay awake listening to the wind work at the thatch and thinking about drawers nailed shut.

Near midnight I heard voices below and crept to the top of the ladder.

Tansy and three others sat around the banked hearth, and they were doing something I didn't understand at first. Each of them held a small object — a button, a carved bird, a strip of ribbon — and they were passing them around and telling each other things.

"This belonged to my sister," the goat-woman was saying. "I don't have a sister anymore. I mean, I don't remember having one. But the button is real, so she was real."

"My father sang," said the woodcutter. "Low, in the mornings. I don't know the song. But I know there was one."

And Tansy, holding a wooden spoon worn smooth as sea glass: "Someone taught me to cook with this. Someone patient. Someone who hummed." She turned it over. "That's all I've got of them. A spoon and a humming."

None of them cried. They said these things the way you'd report weather — factual, unhurried — and passed the objects along, and each person touched each object, and somehow that seemed to be the point. Proof by committee. Memory kept alive by people who had none, like a fire passed hand to hand in the dark, everyone burning themselves a little so it wouldn't go out.

I went back to my blanket and lay staring at the rafters with my throat tight.

Because here was the thing I couldn't say to any of them: I knew what country their holes were shaped like. I'd grown up hearing about it in whispers — a country struck from maps, its name taken out of records, its people scattered or made to forget. Thornfell. The word lived in me whole, in my accent, in the lullabies my mother claimed she'd invented herself, in the way I swallowed the ends of words because they were too heavy to carry all the way out.

These people were walking around with a Thornfell-shaped absence and didn't even know the shape's name. And I could give it to them. One sentence. *Your valley was called Thornfell. Your apples grew there.*

But Vesper's voice was already in my head, cool and dry: *Every person who says that name out loud becomes a person the crown wants dead.* And I'd seen enough of the crown's work lately to believe her.

So I lay there holding the name behind my teeth like a hot coal, and hated how much it weighed, and slept badly.

* * *

Morning came gray and smelling of woodsmoke. Tansy fed us porridge with honey — actual honey, which in my experience of hiding in shepherd's bothies counted as luxury — and pressed a sack of bread and dried apples on Vesper, who tried twice to pay and was refused with such firmness that she gave up, looking faintly shaken, as though kindness were a fighting style she hadn't trained against.

"You'll come back through, won't you?" Tansy asked me at the door. She held my wrist, not my hand, the way you hold someone you're afraid will be gone when you look away. "In spring. When the passes open. Come tell me more about how I talk — no. Come tell me about the apples. The terraces. You'll remember them better than I do."

"I don't actually—" I began, and stopped, because her face was so openly hopeful that lying to it felt like kicking a dog. "Yes," I said. "Spring. I'll come back."

She beamed and pressed something into my palm: the wooden spoon. Worn smooth, humming-shaped.

"I've got another," she lied cheerfully, and shut the door before I could argue.

Vesper was already ten paces up the street. When I caught up, she glanced at the spoon in my fist and said nothing, which from Vesper was a paragraph.

We walked. The mist was lifting off the fields, and behind us Hollowmere sat in its hollow with its chimney smoke going up straight, forty people tending fires they couldn't name the warmth of.

"She knew," I said finally. "Tansy. She knew what my accent meant. How? If the country's erased — if even the exiles' children forgot — how does a random village grandmother recognize it?"

Vesper walked a few steps before answering. "Because forgetting isn't clean," she said. "The crown burned the records and rewrote the maps, but they couldn't reach into every skull. Some memories survived in odd corners. A way of swallowing a word. A lullaby somebody's mother swore she made up." She looked at me sidelong. "Sound familiar?"

My stomach dropped. "You think Hollowmere was—"

"I think nobody plants a village in a valley that isn't on any map unless they've got nowhere else to plant it. I think those people are what's left of something, and they don't know what, and the crown likes it that way." She adjusted the strap of her pack. "And I think we should be well north of here by nightfall, because places like that are exactly where the witch-hunters look. Erased countries leave echoes, and Serane Vole hunts echoes for a living."

"Who's Serane Vole?"

"A problem with a ledger," Vesper said, and lengthened her stride.

But we didn't get well north of anywhere. We'd barely cleared the last field when we saw the rider on the far road — one figure, dark-cloaked, moving at an unhurried trot toward Hollowmere with the patience of someone who has all winter and knows exactly where the echoes sleep.

Vesper stopped. Watched. Swore softly in a language I suspected was also erased.

"He's alone," I said. "One hunter. Maybe he's just passing through."

"Nobody passes through Hollowmere," Vesper said. "That's rather the point of it."
