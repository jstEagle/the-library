## 7. The Witch-Hunter's Ledger

There is an art to interrogation that no one teaches properly, and I have spent eleven years learning it anyway. The manuals will tell you about instruments. The instruments are the least of it. Anyone can make a man scream; children manage it with each other on playgrounds. What I want is not screaming. Screaming is noise, and noise is a man telling you whatever he thinks you want, which is worse than useless because now you own two lies instead of one.

What I want is the pause before he answers.

The smuggler's name was Doss Halloway, which was almost certainly not his name, which suited me fine. A man who has already surrendered his name surrenders everything else at a discount. He sat in my tent at Fort Bracken with his wrists bound to the chair arms with soft cord — never iron; iron makes them brave, gives them something to hate — and he watched me write in my ledger for a long time before either of us spoke.

I let him watch. The ledger is part of the work. It is a heavy book, bound in grey calfskin, and every page is ruled into columns: name, trade, road, gift, disposition. *Disposition* is the column men fear, though most of them don't know why until they've read their own entry. There is something about seeing your life reduced to four lines of another person's handwriting that breaks people faster than fire ever could. Fire says you matter enough to burn. The ledger says you were never worth more than ink.

"Captain Vole," Halloway said at last, because they always speak first. "I've got nothing you want."

"That's remarkable," I said, without looking up. "You're the first one."

"I'm just a salt-runner."

"Mm." I turned a page. "Salt-runners have names. Salt-runners have roads. Salt-runners have partners who wait for them at way-stations and get very anxious when they don't arrive." I dipped my pen. "You have all three, and I have time, and the passes are closed by snow for five months out of the year, which means I have nothing else to do but talk to salt-runners. So let's begin with your name, and you can decide whether we spend tonight or next week getting to the interesting parts."

He was smarter than most. I'll grant him that. He gave me a name — Tam Wicker, he said, out of Lowmarsh — and I wrote it down without comment, because the ledger doesn't judge, it only records, and there is a particular horror in watching someone write down your lie as if it were simply one more fact among facts.

* * *

Here is what the crown sent me into these mountains to do, in the language of my commission: *locate and neutralize persons exhibiting unlicensed speech-gifts within the Thornfell territories.* In the language everyone actually uses: find the speakers, take them alive if convenient, burn them if not.

I say *everyone uses* loosely. Nobody uses any language about it. That is the trick of the whole enterprise. Thornfell was struck from the maps nine years ago — not conquered, mind you, *struck*, which is a different thing entirely and took three times the paperwork. A country that has been conquered still exists; it exists as a province, a territory, a sore spot on the body politic. A country that has been struck from the record does not exist at all, and so there can be no Thornfell speakers, because there is no Thornfell, and therefore anyone claiming otherwise is not a political prisoner but a madman, and madmen are burned for hygiene rather than policy.

It is elegant. I will say that for the clerks who designed it. It is the most elegant cruelty I have ever been asked to administer, and I include in that category the incident at Marrow Ford, which I do not discuss.

My commission came with a secondary instruction, verbal only, delivered by a undersecretary with sweat on his upper lip even in the cold: *the Door is the priority. Speakers are incidental.* He did not say what the Door was. He assumed I knew. I let him assume it, because admitting ignorance to an undersecretary is a debt you pay for years.

So: I hunt speakers, and I look for a door, and I keep a ledger, and the mountains keep their secrets, and winter comes down like a lid.

Until three days ago, when a patrol brought me Doss Halloway, alias Tam Wicker, alias possibly nothing at all, caught running contraband salt through a pass that had been officially closed since the first frost — which meant he was running it through a road that does not appear on any map I possess, which meant he knew roads that do not exist, which meant, finally, that he was worth the ink.

* * *

"Tell me about the pass," I said, somewhere past midnight. The candle had burned down to a thumb's width. I had offered him tea twice and food once, and he had refused all three, which told me he'd been coached, and coaching meant an organization, and organizations mean lists, and lists mean pages in my ledger.

"There's nothing to tell. I walk where the snow lets me."

"You walked through the Gray Saddle. My patrols hold both approaches to the Gray Saddle. They have held them since autumn." I turned the ledger toward him, briefly, so he could see the neat columns, the ruled lines, the terrible tidiness of it. "And yet here you sit, having crossed it, carrying forty pounds of white salt that sells for triple its weight in silver on the far side. So either you flew, Master Wicker, or you know a road I don't."

His eyes flicked. Left, then down. Fear looks different in different men — some go pale, some sweat, some laugh — but the flick is universal. The flick is the body trying to look at the thing it must not think about.

"There's old ways," he said. "Smuggler roads. Cut before the war."

"The war," I said pleasantly, "against a country that does not exist."

Silence. Good silence. The kind where the man is recalculating everything.

"Roads," he said carefully, "that nobody remembers."

"Nobody remembers them. But somebody walks them." I set down the pen. This was the moment I had been waiting for since sundown, and I made sure he saw me wait — folded my hands, gave him my full attention, the whole weight of it. Men can endure being ignored. Being *seen* is what cracks them. "Describe the person who taught you those roads."

"I run alone."

"Everyone runs alone. It's the first rule of the trade, isn't it? And yet salt moves through these mountains in quantities that would require a convoy of loners, all mysteriously synchronized, all mysteriously alive." I leaned back. "Someone organizes them. Someone knows the roads well enough to move strangers along them without losing them to the ice. Describe that person."

"They wouldn't like me talking."

"*They*," I repeated, and wrote the word down. Just the word. *They.* I watched him watch me write it. "Who is *they*, Master Wicker?"

"Ain't a who. It's how it works. You get a message, you walk a stretch, you hand off. You never see the same face twice."

"But you've seen one face twice."

The flick again. Left, down. God, but it was easy. He was a decent man doing a criminal job, and decent men are transparent, because decency assumes the world operates on fairness and hasn't yet absorbed the news.

"There's a woman," he said.

I opened the ledger to a fresh page. I remember the small pleasure of the blank columns, the way the pen felt in my hand, the candlelight lying flat and yellow across the rules. Interrogation is tedious work ninety percent of the time, and then there are moments like this one, when the book opens and a life begins to pour into it, and you understand why the old interrogators called it *harvesting.*

"Go on," I said.

* * *

She was tall, he said. Not big — tall, the way a blade is tall, all the height going up and none of it going wide. Dark hair cut short for the hood. Moved quiet on ice, which he emphasized, moving quiet on ice being a skill he clearly considered near-miraculous, having personally broken an ankle learning it. Carried two blades but used one. A long knife or short sword, worn low on the left hip for a cross-draw.

"She talks to it," he said, and then stopped, and looked at his hands.

"Talks to her sword," I said.

"Not talking like — like madmen talk. Like it answers." His voice had dropped. In eleven years I have heard that drop from a hundred mouths, and it never stops being the truest sound in the room. Mockery is cheap and fear is common, but that drop — that hush — is awe, and awe cannot be faked by a man who wants something. "I seen it once. Down in the Hollows, years back. A courier died in the freeze — young lad, took a fall. She knelt by him and she said a word to her sword, low, like a secret, and the sword said it back. Said his *name*. And the snow went — I don't know. Still. Like the mountain was listening."

"What was the courier's name?"

"I never knew it. That's the point, ain't it? She knew it. Out of nowhere she knew it, and she put it in the steel so it wouldn't be lost." He looked up at me, and his eyes were wet, and I understood suddenly that I was not harvesting a smuggler. I was harvesting a witness. "They call her the Gravemaker. On the roads. She buries people twice — once in the ground and once in the blade — so nobody's forgot. You don't cross the Gravemaker, Captain. Not because she'd kill you. Because she'd *remember* you, and there's things walking these mountains that ought to stay forgot."

I wrote it all down. Every word. Tall. Dark hair, cropped. Cross-draw, left hip. Speaks to the blade; the blade speaks back. Known on the smuggler roads as the Gravemaker. Moves salt, moves people, moves — I paused my pen over that last one — *moves people.*

"Does she move people often?" I asked.

Halloway's mouth shut like a drawer.

"Master Wicker."

"I've said too much already."

"You've said exactly enough to hang yourself and not nearly enough to save yourself, and the difference between those two amounts is the space in which I can be generous." I turned the ledger around again and let him read his own entry. I watched him find the *Disposition* column. I watched him understand it. "A woman with a speaking sword is worth a great deal to me alive. A woman with a speaking sword *and a passenger* is worth a great deal to me alive and comfortable, with a pardon, with coin, with a cottage in a warm country where no one has ever heard the word Thornfell. Help me find her, and that cottage is yours. Refuse, and—" I tapped the column once, lightly, with one finger. "Well. You've read it."

He read it. Then he did the thing they sometimes do, the thing the manuals never predict: he laughed. Quietly, hopelessly, at his own wrists.

"You don't get it," he said. "You're hunting her with paper. She's out there with a sword that remembers everything the crown burned. You think she's the one should be scared?"

"Everyone is scared of something," I said. "It's simply a question of finding the right something. For you, tonight, it's that column. For her—" I thought of the undersecretary's sweating lip, of the verbal instruction, of the door that outranked every speaker in the mountains. "For her, it will turn out to be a boy. It usually is. Save her the trouble, Master Wicker. Was there a boy?"

And there — I saw it. Not the flick this time. Something slower and sadder, a settling, like snow coming off a branch.

"I never saw no boy," said Doss Halloway, who was not Doss Halloway, and who had just told me everything.

* * *

I released him at dawn.

Not mercy — arithmetic. A man who vanishes into my cells becomes a rumor, and rumors travel faster than patrols. A man who walks free becomes a thread, and threads can be followed. I had two men assigned to him before he cleared the gate, good ones, patient ones, with instructions to let him run and mark every door he knocked on. By nightfall I would have the shape of the network. By week's end, the roads. By month's end—

By month's end, the Gravemaker.

I stood on the wall of Fort Bracken with the ledger under my arm and watched the sun come up over the Gray Saddle, and I gave the order I had been holding in reserve since the day I arrived.

"Close the passes," I told Captain Erris. "All of them. Official reason: avalanche risk. Unofficial reason: I want every soul in these mountains funneling south through Grayford, where I can count them. Post the proclamation in every market town between here and the coast. Anyone sheltering a speaker reports or shares the pyre. Anyone with knowledge of unregistered roads comes to me directly, and I pay in silver, and I ask no questions about how they know."

Erris hesitated. He is a solid man, Erris, honest the way masonry is honest, and honesty always hesitates before it obeys. "Closing the passes strands travelers. Some of them will die up there."

"Some of them will die regardless," I said. "That is what mountains are for. The difference is that dead travelers leave no witnesses, and I am currently in the business of witnesses." I handed him the sealed order. "Snow closes the passes every year, Captain. All I'm doing is taking the credit."

He went. I stayed on the wall a while longer.

Below me, the fort was waking — cook-smoke, harness-jingle, the drill yard coughing out its first recruits. Ordinary sounds. Crown sounds. Nine years ago an entire country made sounds like these, and now it made none, and the silence was considered a triumph of administration. Somewhere north of me, past the saddle, past the frozen hollows, a tall woman with a cross-drawn blade was kneeling in the snow saying names to a piece of steel, keeping alive, syllable by syllable, a nation that the crown had declared out of existence.

The Gravemaker. Burying people twice.

I opened the ledger against the parapet and read back through the morning's harvest, checking my own hand. Tall. Dark hair, cropped. Blade speaks. *Moves people.* And beneath it, in the column reserved for assessment, I wrote the line I had been composing since midnight:

*Not a fugitive. A keeper. Keepers do not run when hunted — they reroute. Find what she keeps.*

Because that was the flaw in her, the crack I would widen until the whole edifice came down. Smugglers run for profit and fugitives run for their lives, and both can be bought or broken. But a woman who spends her gift burying the names of strangers — a woman who carries other people's dead in her sword like coins in a purse — such a woman is not running *from* anything. She is keeping *something*. A road. A network. A grave-list. Or, if the undersecretary's sweating instruction meant what I suspected it meant:

A door.

The wind came off the saddle, sharp enough to make the candle-flame of the sun gutter behind the peaks, and I closed the ledger, and I felt the particular cold satisfaction that is the only reward this work ever pays: the feeling of a hunt becoming a shape.

Somewhere in the white silence of the Thornfells, a woman was guiding a passenger along roads that did not exist.

I intended to introduce myself shortly.
