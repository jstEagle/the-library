## 8. Roads That No Longer Exist

The crown would have you believe that a country can be unwritten the way a scribe scrapes vellum — that if you strike a name from enough ledgers, burn enough deeds, forbid enough tongues, the land itself will forget what it was. I used to think so too. I had sworn an oath to a crown that insisted Thornfell had never existed, and oaths are heavy things; they press you flat into whatever shape the oath-giver prefers.

But here is what the crown's clerks never learned, sitting warm in their capital with their ink and their fires: you cannot unmake a road by decree. You can only make it *secret*. And secrets, in the Thornfells, have very long memories.

I woke Rafael before dawn on our fourth day out of Hollowmere, and he came up out of his blankets like a startled cat, all elbows and accusation.

"You said we'd sleep until the sun cleared the ridge."

"I lied," I said. "It's a hobby. Up."

He grumbled, and he shivered, and he ate the last of the oatcake without complaint once I told him there'd be no more after it, which is the fastest way I know to make a boy appreciate an oatcake. Then I led him off the shepherd's track we'd been following, north along a streambed frozen into a braid of white glass, until we reached a wall of grey stone that looked, to any honest eye, like the end of the world.

"Where's the road?" he asked.

"Behind the rock."

"There's no behind the rock. There's rock, and then there's sky."

"Mm." I set my palm against the stone. "That's what the crown paid good money for everyone to believe. Now watch, and try not to be impressed, because I'll never hear the end of it."

The smuggler roads of Thornfell don't hide behind illusions. Illusions can be dispelled; hunters carry charms for exactly that. No — these roads hide behind *boredom*. The old road-wardens of Thornfell, back when Thornfell was allowed to be a thing with roads, had worked a quiet craft: they'd folded the passes into the landscape so thoroughly that a traveler looking right at them would see nothing worth a second glance. A scree slope. A dead-end gorge. A wall of rock. Your eye slides off it the way water slides off waxed canvas. The mind simply declines to be interested.

There was one key to the folding, and it wasn't a charm or a word. It was a name. Each road answered to the name of the village or valley it had served, and when the crown erased those names — struck them from maps, forbade them in market squares, made it treason for a grandmother to sing her children the old place-songs — the roads went dark. Locked doors with the keys thrown into the sea.

Except.

Except that my sword remembers.

* * *

I drew it there at the foot of the wall, and the blade came out singing low, the way it does when it has something to say. If you've never heard a swordspeaker's blade, I can't describe it properly — it isn't sound exactly, more like hearing someone talk through a closed door, words arriving already half-understood. This time the murmur rose and settled into something almost tender.

"*Cael Harrow,*" it said. "*Cael Harrow, Cael Harrow.*"

Rafael went still beside me. He'd heard the sword speak before — it had named the courier I buried back in the pass, weeks ago now, another lifetime — but he still flinched at it, the way you flinch at a voice from an empty room.

"What's Cael Harrow?"

"A village," I said. My throat had gone tight, which annoyed me. "Or it was. Population of maybe two hundred. Sheep farmers, mostly, and slate-cutters. Famous for nothing at all." I paused. "Famous for being erased, now. That's the whole of its fame."

The sword hummed the name again, patient as a nursemaid, and the wall of rock *agreed*. There's no better way to put it. The stone stopped pretending to be stone-that-meant-nothing and became instead a gatehouse with the door left unlatched, and a passage opened where a moment before there had been nothing worth seeing. Not a tunnel — a road, cut clean through the shoulder of the mountain, wide enough for two mules abreast, walled with fitted slate that some long-dead mason had been quietly proud of.

Rafael stared. "How long has that been there?"

"Longer than your grandfather's grandfather. Longer than the crown, probably. The Thornfell roads were old when the kingdom was still arguing about its own borders." I sheathed the sword and the road stayed open — it remembered being asked. "Come on. And stay close. These roads have opinions about strangers."

We walked in, and the mountain swallowed us, and this is the part I want to tell you about, because it's the part I've never managed to say aloud to anyone.

Inside the fold, the world was wrong in the gentlest possible way. The light came from nowhere in particular, grey-gold and even, like weather indoors. The air smelled of woodsmoke and wet wool and late summer, though outside it was deep autumn edging toward winter. And along both sides of the road, every hundred paces or so, stood little shrines — waist-high stone cairns with niches carved into them, and in the niches, small offerings gone grey with years: a clay whistle. A child's shoe. A cup with a chip in the lip. Travelers' gifts to the road, left by people who believed roads had souls, or near enough.

And over each shrine, worn into the lintel-stone, a name.

Rafael read them as we passed, running his fingers along the letters the way you'd touch a sleeping animal. "These aren't in any language I know."

"They're in yours," I said. "You just don't know you know it. It's Low Thornic — the common speech of the valleys. The crown banned teaching it twenty-two years ago. Anyone caught using it got fined; anyone teaching it to children got worse." I shrugged, though the shrug cost me. "So nobody speaks it. So nobody reads it. So the names might as well be scratches."

"But you read them."

"I read them," I said, and didn't explain, because explaining would have meant telling him things I wasn't ready to tell him. The truth is uglier than reading. The truth is that I was there, in the years after the fall, wearing a crown officer's coat, carrying a ledger, striking names. The truth is that I know Low Thornic because I helped bury it, and I know these roads because I helped map their closing, and the sword knows everything I know and holds it against me gently, the way a friend holds a debt they'll never call in.

The sword murmured the shrine-names as we passed. "*Brannoc Fell. Brannoc Fell.*" "*Mistvale. Mistvale, Mistvale.*" Each one opened a little further up the road — a side-passage here, a sheltered overhang there, a spring flowing from a wall into a stone basin some thoughtful hand had positioned centuries ago. The road was alive with its dead, and it introduced them to us one by one, and I could not decide whether it was the most wonderful thing I had ever walked through or the saddest.

Both, probably. Wonder and mourning come from the same well. That's the whole trouble with memory.

* * *

Near midday we came to the bridge.

I felt it before I saw it — a change in the road's footing, a widening, a sense of approach, like standing in a house near a window. Then the fold thinned around us and we stepped out onto open rock, and there it was: a bridge of grey slate arching out over nothing.

Not over a gorge. Over *nothing*. Below us, where a valley should have been, there was a pale haze, like breath on glass, and beyond the haze — nothing. Not darkness, not distance. An absence with edges. The mountains on the far side stood complete and unbothered, and between them and us hung a gap in the world roughly half a mile wide, and across that gap ran a bridge, because the bridge refused to be informed of developments.

Rafael walked to the edge of the road and looked down, and I watched his face do several things in quick succession.

"It's not there," he whispered. "The valley's not there."

"No."

"But the bridge is."

"The bridge is."

"That doesn't—" He pressed both hands flat against his own skull, which I've noticed he does when the world misbehaves. "How can the bridge be somewhere the valley isn't? What's it *standing on*?"

"Memory," I said. "Same as everything else out here. The crown unmade the valley — pulled it out of the record, out of the maps, out of the law. But the bridge was built by people who loved the valley, and love turns out to be a kind of record the clerks can't reach." I stepped onto the bridge, because I wanted him to follow and he wouldn't unless I did, and because some stubborn part of me wanted to feel it hold. It held. Slate under my boots, solid as Sunday. "The bridge remembers being needed. So it stays."

He followed me out, slowly, arms out like a man on ice, and halfway across he stopped and looked down again and his voice came out very small. "What happened to the people? Of the valley. When it was — when it went."

I kept walking. It was easier to answer if I kept walking.

"Some fled before it finished. Some were moved — resetted, the decrees called it, as if they were furniture. And some..." I let the wind take a breath of mine. "Some were in their houses. And the houses were in the valley. And the valley was unmade. The crown's clerks wrote that the valley had 'never been inhabited.' Which is a lie so large it circles round to being almost poetry."

Below us, in the haze where the valley should have been, I saw — or thought I saw, or chose to see, I honestly couldn't tell you — lights. Faint ones. Hearth-colored. The sword at my hip murmured a name I didn't catch, soft as a lullaby, and the lights seemed to settle, the way a dog settles when someone says its name.

Rafael saw them too. His hand found my sleeve. "Vesper."

"Don't look too hard," I said. "They're not for us. They're just... remaining. Like heat in a stone after the fire's out." I said it firmly, and I believed about sixty percent of it, and I kept us moving.

Because here is the other thing I haven't told him, and may never: I recognized that valley. I had stood on its far rim eleven years ago with a ledger under my arm and a quill in my hand, and I had written, in my best clerk's hand, the words *no settlement recorded*, while below me the valley filled with evening smoke from forty hearths. I had told myself the order came from above and oaths were oaths and a swordspeaker's gift was a soldier's tool, not a conscience. I had been very good at telling myself things.

The sword, walking beside me across a bridge that stood on love, said nothing. It didn't need to. It remembered enough for both of us.

* * *

We made camp that night in one of the road's sheltered folds, a hollowed chamber with a fire-ring and a vent-hole some ancient engineer had cut with real care, and Rafael fell asleep almost before he finished chewing, the way children do and soldiers pretend to. I sat up with the blade across my knees and let it talk.

This is my private sacrament, if you want the truth of it. Everyone thinks the gift works one way — the speaker commands, the sword obeys, names of the avenged dead rise up when blood is spilled. But there's a quieter traffic. In the dark, alone, I ask, and it answers, and what it gives back are the names the crown tried to drown. Every erased village. Every forbidden song-title. Every person resetted into a life with no past. The sword keeps a ledger of its own, and its ledger runs the other direction.

"*Tell me about the valley,*" I whispered, because I'm a glutton for punishment.

The blade warmed against my palms, and it gave me the name — the true one, the Low Thornic one, the one I had personally scraped from three separate registries. *Dunmarrow.* Dunmarrow, the wool-fair every autumn, the bell in its chapel tower cast from melted-down raiders' swords, the recipe for honey-bread that three grandmothers guarded jealously and all agreed was inferior to their own. Small things. Human things. The exact weight of a place, which is never its acreage.

And then, because the sword is thorough and sometimes cruel in its kindness, it gave me the names of the families. All of them. One by one, like a bell tolling, and I sat in the dark of a road that officially did not exist and I listened to every single one, because someone had to, and I was the only someone left who could.

When it finished, I did what I always do. I spoke the names back to it, softly, in order, and the blade took them up and carried them, and somewhere in the haze beneath a bridge over nothing, I choose to believe, forty hearths burned a little brighter for a moment.

Rafael stirred in his sleep and murmured something in Low Thornic. Fluent, easy, unconscious — the accent of a boy born to it, the accent the old woman in Hollowmere had caught in two sentences. I looked at him for a long time. Bait, I'd thought. Crown bait, seeded onto the roads to draw out survivors. I still thought so. But watching him sleep-talk in a murdered tongue, I began to suspect the trap had grown a will of its own — that whoever set him walking hadn't counted on what else might wake up out here, in the folds, among the names.

The sword murmured, low, and for the first time all day it said something new.

"*Dawnward,*" it said. "*Dawnward, and the road remembers more.*"

Which was either guidance or warning, and with this blade it was usually both. I banked the fire, and lay down, and did not sleep much.

In the morning we walked on, deeper into the country that wasn't, toward whatever the road remembered next — and behind us, faint but unmistakable, from the direction of the south passes, came a sound I knew far too well.

Horns. Many horns, and hunting-cadenced.
