## 9. The Exiles of Thornfell

I want it on the record that I did not want to follow the singing rock.

The map-stone — Maren's word for it, though at that point we didn't have Maren yet, we just had a fist-sized lump of gray granite that Vesper had fished out of her coat like a woman producing a dead rat — had started to hum on the third morning past Hollowmere. Not a tune, exactly. More like a throat clearing. Over and over. The sound of a country trying to remember its own name and getting most of the way there.

"It's a rock," I said.

"It's a compass," Vesper said.

"It's a humming rock."

"Then it's a humming compass, and you're walking behind it." She tucked it into her breast pocket, where it sat against her heart like a second, more opinionated heartbeat. "Keep your voice down. Sound carries strange up here."

Everything carried strange up here. We had climbed out of the unmade valleys two days before, onto a shelf of high ground where the wind came off the glaciers with an edge like a honed lie, and where the road — if you could call it a road — was really just a habit the mountains had gotten into centuries ago and never broken. Vesper walked it like she'd been born on it. Maybe she had. There were a lot of things about Vesper Eastwick I still didn't know, and after what I'd overheard the sword whispering in the night — no. That comes later. I'm getting ahead of myself, and if there's one thing this story has taught me, it's that getting ahead of yourself is how you end up in a cage wagon.

We climbed through the morning. The stone hummed. Somewhere past midday, the humming changed key, and Vesper stopped so suddenly I walked into her back, which is like walking into a wall that has opinions about your posture.

"What is it?" I whispered.

She didn't answer right away. Her hand went to her sword hilt — not drawing, just resting, the way you'd rest a hand on a friend's shoulder. "Smoke," she said finally. "Hearth smoke. Old hearth smoke, banked and rebuilt a hundred times." She looked at me, and something moved behind her eyes that I hadn't seen before. Fear, maybe. Or its cousin, hope. "Rafael. If I'm wrong about what's up there—"

"You're not wrong," I said. "The stone knows."

"The stone doesn't know anything. It's a humming rock."

"You said it was a compass."

"I say a lot of things." But she kept climbing, and I kept following, because that was the shape our days had taken: she decided, I followed, and somewhere along the way the following had turned into something I'd have called trust if I'd been brave enough to name it.

* * *

The colony didn't appear so much as admit us.

One moment we were scrambling up a slope of scree and stunted pine; the next, there were walls — dry-stone walls, low and lichened, built to look like nothing from below and everything from above, a whole village folded into a fold of the mountain like a letter hidden in a book. Roofs of turf. Chimneys smoking thin. A byre with two goats who regarded us with the flat contempt goats reserve for all newcomers. And people. Dozens of them. Coming out of doorways and down paths and up from the terraced gardens with the unhurried alarm of folk who have been found before and expect to be found again.

They all stopped when they saw Vesper's sword.

I felt the change go through the crowd like cold water through cloth. Conversations died mid-word. A woman pulled a child behind her. An old man set down his load of peat very carefully, the way you do when your hands are shaking and you don't want anyone to know.

Vesper raised both hands, sword arm included, empty-palmed. "I'm not here for you," she said. And then, in a language I didn't know but understood anyway — the way you understand a song your mother sang before you could talk — she said something else. Two words. Short ones.

The effect was worse than silence. People flinched. One woman sat down right where she stood, in the mud, as if her legs had been unplugged. The old man with the peat made a sound like a hinge.

Because those two words were Thornfell words. And I knew them. God help me, I knew them, the way I apparently knew the accent the old woman in Hollowmere had heard in my voice — knew them somewhere below knowing, in the cellar of me, where the crown's memory-eaters had never thought to look because they didn't think a boy of six could keep anything worth stealing.

"They can't be," I said. My voice cracked on *can't*. "Thornfell fell. Everyone says — there's nothing left, there's no one left, they burned the records, they burned the—"

"They burned the paper," Vesper said quietly. "Paper burns easy. People are harder."

A path opened through the crowd, and down it came a woman I would come to know as Maren Ostwick, though at the time I only saw: small, broad, sixty-odd, with iron-gray hair cut short as a soldier's and hands like tree roots, and eyes that went straight past me and fixed on Vesper with the particular weight of someone counting the cost of a thing before buying it.

"So," Maren Ostwick said, in the common tongue, flat and clear. "The crown sends us a swordspeaker wearing a crown oath like a collar, leading a lost cub by the hand. Which of you is the trap?"

* * *

They took our weapons, obviously. All except the sword, which Vesper wouldn't surrender, and which they wouldn't press, because — I learned later — there wasn't a soul in the colony strong enough or foolish enough to try taking it off her. They gave us a hut with a turf roof and a fire, and soup that tasted mostly of barley and faintly of being grateful for barley, and then Maren came, alone, and sat across the fire from Vesper, and began what I now understand was not a conversation but an examination.

I was supposed to be asleep. I was lying on the bench by the wall with my eyes shut, listening the way hungry, frightened boys listen: with every bone I owned.

"You'll tell me where you got that oath," Maren said. No greeting. No warmth. The fire popped.

"On the steps of the Hall of Judgment in Carrowgate," Vesper said. "Eight years ago. I swore it with my hand on the Lawstone and my blade across my knees."

"And what does the oath cost, swordspeaker? You know. You've felt it pull."

Silence. Long enough that I peeked. Vesper was looking into the fire, and her face had gone to that place she kept it — smooth, closed, a shuttered window.

"It pulls toward the capital," she said at last. "Like a tide. Every day since I broke it, a little stronger. By midwinter I'll be fighting it every step."

"And you broke it for him." Maren's chin tipped toward me. I kept my breathing slow and boyish. "For a stranger. Why?"

"He asked me to."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the truest one I have."

Maren leaned forward. Firelight caught the deep lines around her mouth, and I saw that they weren't laugh lines. "Then let me ask truer questions. Where were you when Thornfell fell?"

"Maren—" Vesper started.

"Where. Were. You." Each word laid down like a stone in a wall. "You wear a Thornfell name, girl. Eastwick. My mother's mother was an Eastwick out of the Corrie lands. So either you're kin to me, or you're a ghost wearing a stolen name, and I have burned enough ghosts in my time to know the difference. Where were you?"

The fire crackled. Outside, wind worried the turf roof. I lay with my heart going like a drum and watched Vesper Eastwick decide whether to be honest, and I remember thinking, with a clarity that frightened me: *she lies well, but she's tired of lying.*

"In Carrowgate," Vesper said. "In uniform. Under oath."

"And what did the oath make you do?"

Another silence. This one had weight, like a body.

"There was a registry," Vesper said. "Every speaker in Thornfell, every family with the gift, every village where the speech ran strong. Names, bloodlines, holdings. It was to be archived." A pause. "Archived meant burned. I carried ledgers to the fires. I stood guard while they burned. Eleven years old, the youngest name in the first ledger. I remember it because the ink was fresh. Someone had just written her in."

I forgot to breathe boyishly. I lay there feeling the floor tilt under the hut, because I had heard this before — not from her lips, but from the sword's whisper through the tent canvas weeks ago, and I had told myself I'd dreamed it. Told myself a lot of things.

Maren didn't move. "And after?"

"After, they sent me north to hunt survivors. I found some." Her jaw worked. "I reported finding none."

"Why?"

"Because I was good at my oath," Vesper said, "and my oath was evil, and it took me eight years to say that sentence out loud." She looked up, straight into Maren's eyes, and I saw her put down something heavy she'd been carrying so long it had grown into her shoulders. "I can't give you back what I burned. I can't give you the names. But I can give you the boy, and the road, and the Door, and whatever's left of my life, and I'm offering all four. Test me however you like. I've earned worse than your suspicion."

Maren studied her for a long, long moment. Then she said, in Thornfell speech, three words — and I felt the room change again, because this time nobody flinched. This time Vesper answered, in the same tongue, four words back, and Maren closed her eyes like a woman setting down a child she'd carried too far.

When she opened them, she said, in the common tongue, "That was the gate-challenge of House Ostwick. Last spoken aloud the night the capital fell on us. There are perhaps nine people alive who know the answer." She rose, joints cracking like green wood. "You're kin, or near enough. Kin get soup and a bed and my patience until morning. In the morning, I'll show you why we're still here, and what I know about the Door of Anew — and you'll wish I didn't."

She paused at the door, hand on the latch, and looked back at me. At me, specifically, lying there doing my best impression of a sleeping boy, which was apparently bad.

"And the cub can stop pretending," she said. "Nobody sleeps through that kind of truth. Come to the longhouse at dawn, both of you. Bring your humming rock." The ghost of something crossed her face — not quite a smile. "It's mine. I dropped it running south eleven years ago. It always did know the way home better than I did."

* * *

Dawn came gray and hard, the way dawn does at altitude, arriving less like morning than like a verdict. The colony was already awake — of course it was; farmers and exiles don't get to sleep late — and I walked with Vesper through lanes of turf-roofed houses toward the longhouse at the center, and I watched the people watch us.

It's a strange thing, being looked at with hope. I'd been looked at with hunger (by wolves), with greed (by smugglers), with professional interest (by witch-hunters), but never with hope, and hope is heavier than all of them. Because these people — these ragged, stubborn, impossible people who were supposed to be ash and archive-smoke — looked at me, a gangly fourteen-year-old with chapped lips and no gift he could control, and their faces said *maybe*, and their faces said *finally*, and their faces said *don't you dare be bait.*

Which, in fairness, was exactly what I was. I just hadn't been bait for them.

Inside the longhouse, a fire burned in a central pit beneath a smoke-hole, and around the walls hung things that made my chest hurt without my knowing why: a carved door-lintel with spirals worn nearly smooth. A loom with half a weaving in colors I'd never seen anywhere but in dreams. A child's shoes, tiny, thirty years too small for anyone living. And above the lintel, mounted on blackened wood, a bell — a small bronze bell, green with age, silent.

Maren stood beneath it. Behind her, arranged on a cloth, lay objects I recognized with a jolt: stones. Map-stones. A dozen of them, cracked, chipped, one split clean in half.

"Eleven years ago," Maren said, without preamble, "the crown erased our country. Not conquered — erased. New maps, new names, roads rerouted, records burned. They even paid singers to forget songs. You can buy a lot with bread, in a famine year." She touched the split stone gently. "But a country isn't paper. A country is a agreement between a land and its people, and agreements leave seams. The Door of Anew is such a seam — the oldest one, the one our own speakers made back when making doors was a thing speakers did. It opens out of Thornfell. The trouble is, Thornfell officially doesn't exist, so the Door has nothing to open *from.*"

"Meaning?" Vesper said.

"Meaning it needs remembering into being. Meaning it takes a speaker standing before it, giving it what the crown stole." Maren's eyes came to me, and I swear the temperature of the room dropped. "A true name, spoken whole. Everything bound to it. That's the price of any Door, and this one's price hasn't gotten cheaper with age."

Vesper went very still. I mean *still* — the stillness of a drawn bow. "No," she said.

"You don't even know what I was going to—"

"You were going to say the Door needs a full speaking, and you were going to look at Rafael while you said it." Vesper stepped slightly in front of me, casual as anything, the way a mountain is casual. "He's a child. He barely controls the gift. He is not paying that price."

Maren didn't flinch. "Then find another speaker with a whole name and a reason to spend it. I'll wait." She smiled, and it was the least comforting smile I have ever received, warm as a knife left out in snow. "Take today. Rest. Eat. The pass holds maybe three more weeks, and Serane Vole's hunters are already in the low valleys — yes, we know about her, we know about everything, we've survived eleven years by knowing about everything." She turned to go, then stopped, and her voice softened by perhaps one degree, which from her was a heat wave. "The boy eats first. Growing bones. That's not negotiable, swordspeaker, kin or no."

After she left, Vesper and I stood under the silent bell, and neither of us spoke, because what was there to say? Somewhere south, hunters were closing the passes. Somewhere ahead, a door waited that ate names. And between us and both lay soup, and barley bread, and a colony of ghosts who had decided I might be their way home.

Vesper touched the bell once, lightly, with one finger. It didn't ring. It hummed — the same note, I realized, as the map-stone.

"One day," she said quietly, to the bell or to herself or possibly to me, "someone's going to ring you again."

I believed her. That was the terrible thing. Standing in a house of the erased, under a bell that couldn't speak, I believed her completely.

I just didn't yet know what it would cost to make it true.
