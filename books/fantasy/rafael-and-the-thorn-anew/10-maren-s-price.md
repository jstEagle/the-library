## 10. Maren's Price

Maren Ostwick made her offer the way a butcher makes change — flatly, without apology, and with the clear expectation that you wouldn't haggle over the quality of the meat.

"The sword stays here," she said. "Or you stay with it. Those are your choices."

We were in the longhouse again, the one with the roof beams carved into running stags that no one under sixty could name the meaning of. Outside, the colony was pretending very hard not to listen. I could hear them doing it — forty-odd people being extremely casual about their breathing.

"You understand what you're asking," I said.

"I understand exactly what I'm asking." Maren sat behind the trestle table like a woman who had been sitting behind tables since before I was born, which, given Thornfell arithmetic, might have been literally true. "I'm asking you to leave behind the one object in these mountains that screams our country's name every time you draw it."

"It doesn't scream."

"Child." She folded her hands. "I heard it from two ridges away when you cut down those hunters on the high road. So did everything else with ears. You spoke three names that day. Three dead men's names, spoken through steel, in a voice that carries like a church bell across snow. The crown has listening posts on four of those ridges. What do you imagine they wrote in their ledgers?"

I imagined Serane Vole writing my description for the third time, adding *sword, loud* to the list, and felt my stomach do something unhelpful.

"The speech is the point of the sword," I said. "It's not an ornament. It remembers. That's how we found you — that's how we'll find the Door. You want me to hand you a blade that knows roads this kingdom erased, and then what? Hang it on a wall? Bury it? Let it go dumb in the dark?"

"A blade that knows roads can be made to tell them," Maren said. "Once. By whoever holds it, at whatever moment they choose. And I am telling you, Vesper Eastwick, that if the hunters come up that ridge while it speaks, they will not find a colony here. They will find ash. My people have been erased once already. We are not eager to rehearse."

The fire cracked. Someone outside dropped an armload of wood and swore, then went silent as if swearing were also contraband.

I looked at the sword where it leaned against the bench beside me. Plain scabbard, plain crossguard, nothing about it that would turn a head in a market. It had been quiet all morning, the way it gets quiet when it's paying attention.

"You're asking me to give up the only proof I ever belonged to anything," I said.

"No," Maren said. "I'm asking you to prove it differently."

* * *

They gave us a hut to ourselves for the night, which was either hospitality or house arrest depending on how you liked your doors. This one had no bar on the outside, but it did have two guards who were terrible at pretending to warm themselves by a fire twenty feet away.

Rafael sat on the bunk with his knees drawn up, working at a heel of black bread like it had personally wronged him.

"They're right, you know," he said.

"Don't start."

"I'm not starting. I'm finishing. They've been right since the minute we walked in and your sword told everyone within earshot that the last oath-bound speaker of Thornfell just came home." He tore the bread with more force than the bread deserved. "You know what I keep thinking about? The cage wagon. How quiet they kept me. Gagged half the time, not because I talked — because they were afraid I'd *say something*. One word out loud and stone grows. They understood what a voice costs better than anyone I've met since. Better than you do, sometimes."

"That's unfair."

"Most true things are." He looked up. His face was still thin from the convoy, still bruised along the jaw where the manacle had sat, but his eyes had gone steady in a way that kept catching me off guard. When I'd pulled him out of that wagon he'd been a trembling bundle of terror wearing a boy's coat. Somewhere between the bothy and Hollowmere and the frozen pass, the trembling had burned off and left someone else. Someone who noticed things.

"Maren's not wrong about the risk," he said. "And you're not wrong about the sword. So stop trying to decide whose wrongness wins and think about what nobody's said yet."

"Which is?"

He set the bread down. "Give them me instead."

* * *

I want it noted, for the record, that I laughed. Out loud. In a hut with paper-thin walls and two eavesdropping guards.

"No," I said.

"Yes."

"Absolutely not."

"Vesper—"

"You are sixteen years old and worth more to the crown alive than this entire colony put together, and you want to volunteer yourself as a *hostage*—"

"Bargaining piece," he corrected, with the pedantic precision of someone who had recently learned the word from people who used it about him. "There's a difference. A hostage is taken. A bargaining piece is *offered*. It changes the whole shape of the thing. If I stand up in front of Maren tomorrow and say: keep the boy until the Door is found, until the exiles are through it, and if Vesper's sword brings hunters down on us, hand me over — then Maren has insurance that isn't the sword. Then she doesn't need to take it. Then nobody has to choose."

"Then the hunters get exactly what they wanted," I said, "which was always you walking the Thornfell roads until somebody found you. You'd be completing the assignment."

"And you'd be failing yours," he said quietly. "Which is the whole point, isn't it? Somebody set me out as bait to catch survivors of Thornfell. Well. Here they are. All I have to do is sit still and be caught, slowly, on purpose, and meanwhile everyone else gets out." He shrugged, a small terrible shrug. "It's not even a sacrifice, really. It's just refusing to let a trap work for free."

I crossed the hut and crouched so we were eye to eye, because there are conversations you cannot have standing over someone.

"Listen to me," I said. "I have broken one oath in my life, and it cost me more than you know, and I would break it again before breakfast if it kept you out of a cage. I am not going to hand you back to them in installments to make a deal more palatable. That is not how this works."

"How does it work?"

"I don't know yet," I admitted. "But not that."

He held my gaze for a long moment. Then, maddeningly, he smiled — a thin, tired, entirely Rafael smile. "You know what Maren's going to say?"

"She's going to say yes."

"She's going to say yes *gratefully*," he said. "That's worse."

* * *

Morning came gray and windless, the kind of cold that doesn't bite so much as lean its full weight on you. The colony gathered in the space between the longhouse and the smithy — not all of them, but enough. Forty faces I didn't know, wearing clothes I half-remembered from a country I'd helped erase, watching me the way people watch weather.

Maren stood at the front with the map-stone cradled against her chest. Behind her, the mountain wall rose white and indifferent, hiding whatever seam the Door of Anew waited in.

"Well?" she said. "Have you decided, swordspeaker?"

I opened my mouth to begin the argument I'd been sharpening all night — and Rafael stepped forward.

"I have," he said.

Every instinct I owned screamed at once. I grabbed for his sleeve and got air; the boy had learned to move, somewhere along the way.

"Maren Ostwick," Rafael said, and his voice carried the way voices do in cold still air, clean and far, "you don't need her sword. Take me instead. Keep me here, under your guard, until the exiles are through the Door. If hunters come because of her blade, give me to them — I'm what they're hunting anyway, I'm the bait they set out, and I'll walk back into their hands willingly. But let her keep the sword, because without it we will never find the Door at all, and then none of this matters and we all freeze politely in place."

Silence. Even the wind seemed to lean in.

Then the murmur started, moving through the crowd like water finding cracks. I saw it land on faces: relief, mostly, and shame about the relief. A woman near the front — the one who'd sung the threshing song two nights ago — closed her eyes. An old man nodded twice, slow, like a door settling.

Maren studied Rafael for a long moment. Then me. Then, horribly, she smiled.

"The boy understands collateral," she said. "Where did you learn that word, child?"

"In a cage," Rafael said. "They use it a lot."

Something flickered across Maren's face — old, fast, quickly buried. She turned back to me.

"Here is my price, then, and it is final. The boy stays in Hollowmere's keeping — not as hostage, mind—" she raised a hand against the word, though everyone present knew exactly what it was, "—as guest. Honored guest, watched guest, same thing with better manners. Your sword comes with us but stays sheathed and silent unless I say otherwise. You speak no names into it between here and the Door. Not one. If the blade cries out on its own, if it drags hunters up our backsides, the agreement breaks, and I will not stand in the way of anyone who chooses to give the boy to the crown to save the rest. Do you accept?"

Sheathed and silent. A swordspeaker asked to carry a mute blade through the teeth of the mountains, forbidden the only thing that made her gift worth having. It was like asking a singer to walk past a burning house humming.

And it was better than the alternative by exactly the width of Rafael's skinny shoulders.

"I accept," I said.

"Good." Maren pressed the map-stone into my hands. It was smaller than I expected, chipped through the middle, warm as a held bird. "We leave within the hour, before the weather decides for us. Ostwick — pack light. Everyone says goodbye to what needs saying goodbye to."

The crowd began to break apart, and the sound of it was strange: not grief, exactly, but the particular hush of people who had practiced leaving for thirty years and gotten good at traveling light.

Rafael came and stood beside me. He didn't look at me, which I appreciated, because I was busy not looking at him too.

"You hate this," he said.

"Fiercely."

"Good. Hold onto that." He finally turned, and there was the steady look again, the one that made him seem older than his coat. "Hate it all the way to the Door. But Vesper — thank you. For not letting them take the sword, even though I offered. Nobody's ever refused something on my behalf before. It was usually the other arrangement."

I put a hand on his shoulder, briefly, because I am bad at tenderness and he is bad at receiving it, and between us we manage roughly one gesture per week.

"One condition of mine," I said. "You stay within arm's reach on the road. Not Maren's arm. Mine."

"Wasn't planning otherwise," he said. "Someone has to keep you honest."

Behind us, at the edge of the crowd, one of the night guards was no longer pretending to warm his hands. He was looking north, up the ridge, at something small and dark that hadn't been there yesterday — a speck against the snowfield, patient as punctuation.

A crow, probably.

Probably.
