## 12. First Snow, First Blood

The first snow came on a morning that smelled like woodsmoke and iron, and I knew it was going to be a bad day because Maren Ostwick smiled at me over breakfast.

Maren didn't smile. Maren had a face like a door left open in winter — technically possible, deeply inadvisable. In the two weeks since Vesper and I had stumbled into the colony of Thornfell exiles, I'd catalogued exactly three expressions from her: suspicion, deeper suspicion, and the look she gave the stew pot when someone put turnips in it again. So when she set down her bowl across from mine and said, "You've got good hands, boy," I checked my porridge for poison.

"Thank you," I said carefully.

"They're shaking."

"They're always shaking. It's a feature. Vesper says if my hands ever stop shaking, I should sit down immediately and prepare for the end of the world."

"Mm." Maren stirred her tea with a splinter of pine, which was apparently a whole personality trait among people who'd spent nine years hiding from a country that insisted they'd never existed. "When it starts, don't fight it. Fighting it is how you burn out your own ears. You'll need them where you're going."

"Where am I going?"

She looked at me for a long moment, and there was something underneath the suspicion — something old and tired and terribly kind. "Somewhere with a door in it," she said, and drank her tea like she hadn't just said a terrifying thing, and went to check the goat fence like the world was an ordinary place.

I want it noted, for whatever record survives us, that I did not understand what she meant until much too late.

* * *

Here's what nobody tells you about being bait: you start to feel guilty about everything. Every bird that startled from the pines, every stranger's footprint on the ridge trail, every time Vesper went still as a drawn blade at some sound only she could hear — some animal part of me whispered, *that's your fault, that's you, they followed you here.* The men who'd taken me in the cage wagon never told me who set me walking the Thornfell roads or why. They just told me to walk, and I walked, because when you're fourteen and alone and someone with a crown badge tells you to do a thing, you do the thing, and then you spend the rest of your life doing arithmetic about how many people died because of it.

Vesper caught me counting once. We were splitting firewood, or rather she was splitting it with the efficiency of a woman who considered axes a form of conversation, and I was stacking, and my lips were moving.

"Stop that," she said.

"Stop what?"

"The sums. I can hear them from here. You're adding up everyone who's ever been hurt within a mile of you and assigning yourself the debt." She split another log without looking at me. "Bad arithmetic."

"It's not arithmetic, it's just — true."

"It's bad arithmetic," she said again, in the tone she used when she was right and knew it and wasn't going to waste words proving it. "A debt implies someone owed. Nobody chose you for this. That's not a debt, that's weather." Then, quieter, almost to the axe: "Believe me. I know the difference."

I didn't believe her. But I liked hearing it, the way you like a fire in a room where snow is coming through the walls. And then the snow actually came, and the hunters came behind it, and I learned that Maren had been right about my hands after all.

* * *

It started, as catastrophes in cold country often do, with a dog.

One of the exiles — Hesk, a broad silent man who mended nets even though we were a hundred miles from any water worth fishing — kept a brindled hound named Bracken. Bracken started barking at the treeline around midmorning, and Hesk went to quiet him, and Hesk didn't come back. What came back instead, rolling down into the hollow where the colony hid its eleven houses and its smoke-shed and its children, was the sound of horns. Two notes, flat and deliberate. Crown signals. I'd heard them through cage-wagon bars for six days; they live in my spine now like shrapnel.

"Down," Vesper said. She was beside me before the second note finished, one hand flattening me against the wall of the smoke-shed, the other already drawing her sword partway — just enough that I could see the faint grey shimmer along the steel, like breath on a mirror. When her blade woke, it whispered. It always whispered. Names, mostly. Dead names. On bad nights I heard it through the wall of wherever we slept, murmuring to itself like an old woman remembering a village that no longer answered.

"Stay behind me," she said. "Whatever happens. Whatever you hear. Stay *behind* me."

"And if you fall?"

She paused. For half a heartbeat she looked seventeen years old herself, and exhausted beyond counting, and then the shutters came down. "Then run downhill and scream your head off. Stone listens to you, doesn't it? Make it listen hard."

Then the arrows came out of the trees, and the world turned into noise.

* * *

I have tried, since, to remember the raid in order, and I can't. Memory broke along the same fault lines the colony did. Here are the pieces I have:

Hunters in grey-and-crimson coming down the slope in a wedge, maybe twenty of them, crossbows levelled, moving with the horrible calm of men who had done this before and would be paid either way.

Old Sefa standing in her doorway with a bread peel in her hands, shouting at them in Thornfell speech — the language the crown had outlawed so thoroughly that saying *bread* in it was legally an act of treason — and a bolt taking her through the chest before she finished the word.

Children running. There were seven children in the colony. Seven. I counted them the way I count everything now, helplessly, compulsively. They scattered toward the root cellars below the big house, which was where everyone had agreed to go, and the hunters' line angled to cut them off, because of course it did, because burning villages means nothing if the children get away — the crown had learned that lesson about Thornfell once already, and this time it intended to learn better.

And me, standing in the open with Vesper thirty feet away carving through the wedge's edge like a sentence being edited, and the children between me and the crossbows, and my hands shaking so hard I could barely hold them up.

Don't fight it, Maren had said. So I didn't.

I reached down into the ground the way you reach down into cold water for something dropped — blind, gasping, certain — and I spoke to the stone under the colony. Not words, exactly. Not at first. More like a name I'd been carrying since I was born and had never had occasion to use: the name of the earth itself, the deep slow granite mind of the mountains, older than any kingdom's maps. And the stone, which had waited nine years under a country that officially didn't exist, heard me.

The hillside opened.

Not dramatically — no earthquake, no Hollywood chasm. The ground simply *decided*, all at once, to have a trench in it where no trench had been, a seam of dark earth yawning between the hunters' line and the running children, wide enough that the front rank skidded to a halt with their boots crumbling at the lip. One man went down into it screaming. The others fired wildly, bolts hissing over the heads of children who tumbled into the root cellar one after another after another, and I stood there holding the trench open with both hands and my whole soul, and blood started coming out of my nose, and I thought, distantly, *so this is why Maren said don't fight it,* and then I thought nothing at all for a while.

What brought me back was Vesper's voice, right against my ear, saying my actual name — Rafael, my whole name, the one nobody used because names draw attention — and my knees folding, and her catching me before I hit the frozen mud.

"You magnificent idiot," she said, and she was crying, which I had never seen and did not know she could do. "You absolute *idiot.*"

"The kids—"

"In the cellar. All seven. All seven, Rafael." She pressed her forehead to mine, briefly, hard. "All seven."

That's when I saw Maren.

* * *

She'd gone up toward the smoke-shed to cut loose the goats — I found this out later, the absurd, human detail of it, that she died for goats — and a hunter officer had reached her first. Not Serane Vole. Someone smaller, more frightened, the kind of man who does terrible things quickly so he doesn't have to think about them. By the time Vesper and I got there he was running, and Vesper let him go, because Maren was on the ground with a sword through her, and some choices make themselves.

We knelt in the bloody trampled snow. Around us the colony was screaming and singing and dragging wounded into houses; somewhere Bracken the dog was howling at the treeline; somewhere the trench I'd made was slowly closing itself, sullen, like a mouth done talking. Maren looked up at me and her face had finally settled on an expression I hadn't catalogued: relief.

"There he is," she said. "Stone-speaker. Knew it the day you arrived. Felt it in my teeth." Her hand found my wrist and gripped it with shocking strength. "Listen to me. Both of you. We haven't long and I'll not say this twice."

"Maren—" Vesper started.

"*Quiet.* You want to argue, argue with the dead; they've got more patience." She coughed, and the cough was bad, wet and final. "You asked me about the Door of Anew. I told you I knew the way. I lied. I know half the way, and the other half is a stone, and I'm about to give you the stone, and you're going to think it's broken, and it *is* broken, and that's the point of it."

From inside her coat she drew a thing the size of a fist: a shard of pale rock wrapped in waxed cloth, cracked clean through the middle, the halves held together by string that had gone black with age. When she pushed it into Vesper's hands, I felt it — felt it in my teeth, same as she'd described — a hum, low and patient, like something sleeping under ice.

"A map-stone," Maren said. "Cut from the Door's own threshold, back when the Door was ours. It points home. Always has. Nine years I kept it and never once followed it, because following it meant leaving, and somebody had to stay and keep the rest of them alive." She laughed, and it cost her. "Now look at me. Kept them alive straight into a crown raid. Bad arithmetic, Vesper. You'd have hated my sums."

"I don't hate anything of yours," Vesper said, and her voice cracked clean down the middle.

"It's cracked," Maren went on, tapping the stone, "because when the crown erased Thornfell from the records, the stone forgot half of itself too. It remembers the way, but it remembers it *wrong* — it points to where Thornfell was believed, not where it was built. That's why the Door opens for memory, girl. Why do you think they burned every ledger, every register, every christening book? Not to kill us. Killing us they could do anywhere. They burned the *memory*, because the Door eats memory, feeds on it, and a door fed on nothing stays shut forever." Her grip tightened. "To open it, someone has to pay. A name. A whole name, spoken whole, with everything tied to it. You understand me?"

Vesper had gone very still. I watched her face do something complicated and terrible, like a person reading their own death warrant and finding a familiar signature on it.

"No," Vesper said. "No, there's another way, there has to be—"

"There isn't, and we both know there isn't, and you knew it before I ever met you, which is why you flinched every time I said the word 'door.'" Maren's eyes slid to me. "Boy. Come here. Closer." I leaned down. Her breath was failing; each word came out rationed, like coins counted out of a nearly empty purse. "You're the inheritance. Not the stone. *You.* The stone just shows the road. The one who pays the toll — that's the gift, and the curse, and it's hers, and you will not take it from her, and you will not let her die thinking she wasn't loved. Do you hear me? Whatever she did before you knew her. Whatever's in that blade of hers. She was a child of Thornfell too, once, and Thornfell does not abandon its own."

"I hear you," I said, and discovered I was crying.

"Good." She smiled — the second smile in recorded history, and her last — and her eyes found Vesper's above me. "Take them through the pass before the snow seals it. Take them *home*. And Ostwick—" her hand rose, trembling, and touched Vesper's cheek, leaving a smear of red like a war-paint mark, "—you tell them at the Door that Maren Ostwick paid the toll of staying. Let the stone carry that, at least."

And then she was gone, in the middle of the noise, in the middle of the snow, with the smell of bread still on her coat from breakfast.

* * *

They buried her at dusk, fast and shallow, because the hunters would regroup and come back and there was no time for the old rites. Vesper spoke her name into the sword — I heard it, the steel humming it back, adding it to the long grey litany of the avenged and mourned — and the colony's survivors filed past the grave in silence, each dropping a handful of frozen earth. Seven children, red-eyed and uncomprehending. Old men with faces like cracked leather. A woman who sang four bars of something in the outlawed speech, barely a whisper, before her voice gave out.

I stood apart, because that seemed correct. Because the trench was mine, and Sefa's death was mine, and Maren's last morning had been spent worrying about *my hands*, and guilt is a country too, and I am apparently its most loyal citizen.

Vesper came and stood next to me. She held the map-stone in her palm, turning it over, watching the crack catch the last light.

"It hums," she said. "Did you feel it?"

"Yes."

"Northwest. Through the high pass." She closed her fingers around it. "Three days' climb, maybe four, and then the glacier field. After the next snowfall, none of it will be passable until spring. Which means we leave tomorrow at dawn, or we stay here and wait for Serane Vole to finish what her officer started."

"We?" I said. "There's twenty-three survivors down there, Vesper. Are they all—"

"Everyone who wants to walk," she said quietly. "Maren said take *them* through. Them. All of them. She didn't say take you, and she didn't say take the strong ones." She looked at me then, and her eyes were dry now, and harder than the stone in her hand. "So we go at dawn. All of us. Up the spine of the world, ahead of the snow, ahead of the hunters, with a broken rock that remembers wrong and a sword that remembers everything." She almost smiled — and gods, it looked strange on her, like flowers growing through a floorboard. "Get some sleep, Rafael. Tomorrow we commit treason against an entire geography."

Behind us the settlement lights flickered on, one by one, small and stubborn against the coming dark. And north, where the pass waited, the first real storm of winter was already gathering like a debt come due.

I shook out my hands, took a breath, and went to pack.
