## 13. The Long Winter Falls

The snow came down like a verdict.

Not the polite, decorative snow of lowland winters — the kind that makes children and poets stupid with joy — but Thornfell snow, which falls sideways, which fills your boots and your collar and eventually your opinions about the divine. It had been falling for two days when we lost the road entirely, and for half a day after that when I admitted to myself we had ever found it in the first place.

"Is this a road?" Rafael asked, squinting into the white.

"It was," I said. "Somewhere under there is a smuggler's track my father used to run salt on. Or it's a goat path. Or it's a cliff. The mountain doesn't distinguish, this time of year."

"That's very reassuring."

"I wasn't hired for reassurance."

"You weren't hired at all," he pointed out. "You kidnapped me. Then un-kidnapped me. Then got adopted by a colony of exiles who made you their guide against your will."

"And yet somehow I'm still the one carrying the rope." I shook the coil for emphasis. Snow fell off it onto his head. Small victories.

Behind us came the others: eleven souls from Maren's colony, what remained of it after Serane's raid. Eleven, out of a settlement that had hidden itself for twenty years. We'd buried four before we started climbing. I did not let myself do that arithmetic often, because arithmetic like that has a way of becoming prophecy.

Maren we carried. She'd asked to be left behind twice — once at the burned colony, once at the tree line, both times in the flat, reasonable voice of someone who has already done the sums. Both times I'd said no, and both times she'd looked at me with something between gratitude and pity, as if she knew something about winter that I didn't.

She probably did. She'd known everything else about these mountains.

* * *

We climbed the spine of the Thornfells the way you'd climb a sleeping animal — slowly, respectfully, and with a constant low-grade fear that it might wake up.

The pass would close soon. Everyone knew it. Old Doss, who had crossed these mountains as a boy before the erasure, kept repeating it like a prayer: "When the Long Winter falls, the high passes go shut. Not blocked — shut. Like a door with no handle on this side." He was seventy if he was a day, all knuckle and grin, with a laugh that sounded like a cart going over gravel. He'd appointed himself morale officer somewhere around the second day, a position nobody had voted on and nobody dared contest, because the alternative to his terrible jokes was thinking.

The Door of Anew sat — if Maren's broken map-stone could be trusted, and so far it had been trustworthy in the way of old things, meaning it worked but complained — three days up the spine, past a glacier that didn't appear on any map because maps were part of what the crown had burned. The map-stone rode in my breast pocket, cracked down the middle, warm in a way stones shouldn't be. Sometimes at night I thought I felt it ticking. Stones don't tick. I'd decided not to have opinions about that either.

Rafael walked beside me most days. The boy had grown quieter since Hollowmere, quieter even than the raid warranted, and I kept catching him looking at my sword the way you look at a locked door in your own house.

"It talks more when it's cold," I told him on the third morning, mostly to break the silence.

"What?"

"The blade. Cold makes the steel remember harder. Something about the metal contracting around the names. My grandmother called it the winter-hum." I patted the scabbard. "It's been humming for two days. Mostly complaints. It never liked this pass."

"Your sword complains."

"All swords complain. Mine just does it audibly." I glanced at him. "What does yours do?"

He went still in that particular way of his, the way that meant I'd stepped on something without seeing it. His gift made stone bloom — flowers out of granite, green shoots through slate — and every time it happened, something in his face said *that isn't mine, that belongs to someone I miss.*

"She sang," he said finally. "My mother. When she worked stone. That's how they knew. That's why—" He stopped. Kicked snow. "It doesn't matter."

"Rafael."

"It doesn't *matter*, Vesper." And he lengthened his stride until he was walking with Doss instead, absorbing terrible jokes like a good soldier.

I let him go. There are conversations you can't force uphill, in weather, with grief still wet. But I marked it. Marked it the way the sword marks things — a name held in steel, waiting for its moment.

* * *

The Long Winter fell on the fourth night, and it fell all at once, the way a judge drops a gavel.

One hour the wind was merely hostile. The next it was architectural — walls of white moving through the dark, erasing the difference between ground and sky, between up and the other thing. We roped ourselves together and huddled in the lee of a granite shoulder that Doss swore was called the Shepherd's Tooth, back when it had a name anyone could say aloud.

"We can't stop long," I shouted over the wind. "If we're not moving by dawn, we freeze standing up."

"If we move in this, we walk off the spine!" Doss shouted back, delighted, as though this were a fine philosophical point rather than a death sentence with a punchline attached.

He was right. I was right. The mountain, as usual, was righter than both of us.

We survived the night the way climbers survive nights — badly, in shifts, pressed together for warmth while the wind tried to audit us one soul at a time. Maren spent it shivering in Rafael's arms, her wound gone bad beneath the bandages, her breath coming in little torn pieces. Around midnight she gripped my wrist with surprising strength and pulled me close.

"The stone," she rasped. "When it ticks louder — that's the door waking. Don't wait for dawn light. Wait for the tick."

"Noted," I said. "Any other cryptic instructions? I have room for at least six more."

Her laugh turned into a cough. "You remind me of my sister," she said. "She also thought jokes were load-bearing."

"Were they?"

"No." Her eyes closed. "But they kept the roof on longer than it should have stood."

* * *

Dawn came gray and windless, which in the Thornfells is not mercy but ambush. The clear air after a storm shows you exactly how far you are from anywhere, and the answer was: too far, in every direction, forever.

We moved. Slowly, roped, single file along the spine with drop-offs on either side that the fresh snow had kindly rendered invisible. I led. Behind me came Rafael, then the exiles in a rough order of strength, then Doss at the rear because he insisted, saying someone cheerful should guard against despair and he was the only volunteer.

By midday the sun came out properly, and for an hour it was almost beautiful — the whole world blinding white, the peaks around us like teeth, our shadows stretched blue across the snow. I remember thinking, with the superstitious dread of anyone raised in these mountains, that beautiful weather up here is just the mountain taking a breath.

Then Doss stopped joking.

I heard it first as silence — the absence of the gravel-cart laugh from the rear of the line. I turned, and saw him leaning against the rope, and saw the way his legs had stopped participating in the project of holding him upright.

"Frostbite?" I called.

"Nap," he said, grinning. "Just a nap. You go on—"

"Doss." I was already moving down the line, hand over hand on the rope. "Doss, look at me."

His lips were the color of old ash. His hands, bare because he'd given his gloves to one of the children two days ago — of course he had, of course he'd given them away, that was the whole man in a single gesture — his hands were white as the snow he stood in. And his eyes had gone somewhere past me, past the mountain, to some warmer country that only the freezing get to see.

"Old fool," I said, and my voice cracked on it. "Why didn't you say something?"

"Didn't want," he said, each word arriving separately, like coins counted out, "to slow the line."

We got him moving again. We rubbed his hands, forced broth into him, wrapped him in every spare layer we had, and for two hours I believed — the way you believe things at altitude, desperately and against evidence — that we'd caught it in time.

We hadn't. The cold doesn't catch. It waits. It had been inside him since the night at the Shepherd's Tooth, patient as a debt, and around the middle of the afternoon it simply collected him. One step he was walking. The next step he wasn't. He folded down into the snow like a coat slipping off a hook, and when we gathered around him his breathing had gone thin and rattling, and his eyes couldn't find our faces anymore.

"There's a hollow," Rafael said urgently, scanning the slope. "There's rock below the ridge — we could shelter, warm him—"

"He wouldn't make it fifty yards," I said. "And neither would whoever carried him."

"Then we carry him anyway!"

"Rafael—"

"*We don't leave people.*" His voice broke on it, and I understood — everyone on this ridge understood — that he wasn't talking about Doss. He was talking about a colony. About four graves already dug in frozen ground behind us. About a mother whose name he wouldn't say.

I looked at Doss. His chest barely moved. His lips were trying to shape something — words, a joke, a prayer, I couldn't tell. Seventy years of a life, and it was going to end here on an unnamed ridge in a country that officially did not exist, unmourned, unrecorded, unwritten, because the crown had burned the books and the snow would cover the rest.

No.

That, I decided, was the one thing I would not allow. Let the crown take the maps. Let it take the records, the borders, the name of the country. It would not take this man's name. Not while I carried the one object in the world built to refuse such theft.

I drew the sword.

Steel rang thin and cold in the thin cold air, and the winter-hum rose immediately, eager, the blade remembering harder as my grandmother promised. I knelt in the snow beside Doss and took his frostbitten hand in mine.

"Doss," I said. "Look at me. Can you hear me?"

His eyes wandered, found me, held — barely.

"I'm going to speak your name now," I told him. "Into the steel. Do you know what that means?"

A ghost of the grin. "Heard stories," he whispered. "Before. Before the burning. They said the last swordspeaker died at the fall."

"Stories exaggerate." I swallowed. "So do I, apparently. Listen — when I speak it, your name goes into the blade. Everything you were. Every joke, every glove you gave away, every mile you walked to keep other people laughing. It will be kept. Not written down where it can be burned. Kept. In steel, in me, for as long as the steel lasts and longer. You will be mourned, old man. Properly. Out loud. Forever."

Tears moved in the corners of his eyes, freezing as they formed. "That's," he breathed, "the nicest thing anyone's ever threatened me with."

"I know. Ready?"

He nodded. Just once.

I lifted the blade so it caught the sun, and I spoke his name — his true name, the one he'd been born with in a country that no longer existed, the name his mother had used when he was small and skinned-kneed, the name the crown's fires had never gotten because it had lived nowhere but in the memory of exiles. I spoke it slowly, syllable by syllable, and the sword drank it the way dry ground drinks rain, humming deeper with each one, and the air around us changed — everyone on that ridge felt it, the sense of a presence arriving, of a ledger being opened, of something vast and careful paying attention.

Doss heard it. Whatever the dying hear when a name is spoken whole, he heard it, and his face — God help me, his face *lit*. The grin came back full, the gravel-cart laugh rattled up out of a chest that barely had breath left for it, and he said, clear as a bell:

"Well. There I am."

And then he wasn't. The breath went out of him gently, like a guest leaving early to beat the weather, and the wind took the warmth, and the snow began, softly, to fill the shape he'd left in the world.

* * *

We buried him as well as the mountain allowed — cairned under stones, which is the Thornfell way, so the wind can't dig and the crown can't find. Nobody spoke during it except the sword, which hummed low and continuous, a note like a held breath, like a name being kept.

Afterward I stood apart, cleaning a blade that didn't need cleaning, because my hands needed occupation or they were going to do something embarrassing.

Grief is fuel. That's the oldest lesson the mountains teach, older than the smuggling routes, older than the erased villages. You don't get to set it down. You don't get to wait for it to pass, because up here nothing passes — it accumulates. Four graves behind us. Five now. A colony burned. A country erased. A boy walking ten feet away who flinched when I spoke a kindness, because kindness has cost him before. You take all of it, every ounce, and you pack it down into your legs and your lungs and you *walk*, because the alternative is to sit down in the snow the way Doss did, and the mountain always has room for one more.

"Vesper."

Rafael stood beside me. He was looking at the sword at my hip with an expression I couldn't read — hunger and fear braided together, the exact expression I'd worn at fifteen, the first time I understood what I'd inherited.

"When you spoke his name," he said carefully. "The sword. Afterward. It was — did you hear it?"

"Hear what?"

"It hummed. But underneath the hum, there were—" He stopped. Started again. "Words. Under the words you spoke. Other words. Older ones."

The wind picked up. Somewhere in my breast pocket, faint as a beetle in a wall, the map-stone ticked.

"Blades echo," I said. "Cold steel carries sound strangely. It's nothing."

He looked at me a moment too long. "Right," he said. "Nothing."

He turned back toward the line, toward the exiles waiting in the wind, and I stood there telling myself the lie I'd just handed him, and hating how easily it had come, and wondering — for the first time, honestly wondering — what exactly my grandmother's blade had been saying about me, all those nights, in the dark, in the cold, when it thought no one was listening.

Higher up the spine, past the next ridge, the snow glowed faintly gold in the late light. Not sunlight. Fires. Someone was camped between us and the Door, and there was only one kind of someone who lit fires that high this time of year.

The map-stone ticked louder.
