## 14. What the Sword Says

I want it on the record, whoever ends up reading this — if anyone does, if paper survives what's coming — that I did not go snooping.

That matters to me. It mattered to me at the time, huddled in the lee of a dead fire with the wind trying to peel the mountain off its bones, and it matters now, and I'll tell you why: because when you find out a thing you weren't meant to know, everyone always asks how you found out, as if the finding-out were the crime. Nobody ever asks the thing itself. Nobody asks *what did you learn*. They ask *were you eavesdropping*, and you have to stand there with your whole face going hot and say yes, technically, I was awake in the next room, which is not a crime, last I checked, though on the Thornfell roads it might be, since everything else is.

So: I was awake in the next room. The next room being a shepherd's hut with no shepherd, half a roof, and a wall of stacked stone thin enough that a determined moth could get through it, let alone sound.

And the sword was talking.

* * *

You should understand something about Vesper Eastwick's sword before I go further. Most swords are just bad-tempered metal. Hers is a ledger. That's the gift — swordspeech, they called it, back when there was anyone left to call it anything. A speaker's blade remembers the names of the dead it has avenged, and speaks them, and carries them. Vesper had told me this herself, back in the bothy, in the flat voice she used for facts she didn't want to feel. She'd said it like a woman describing a debt. Which, I understand now, is exactly what it was.

What she hadn't said — what nobody says, because who would — was that a ledger doesn't only hold what you put in it. A ledger holds *everything written under your name*. And Vesper had been writing under her name for a long, long time.

The night it happened we'd made eleven miles up the spine of the Thornfells before the weather closed like a fist. There were six of us by then: me, Vesper, old Tam with his frostbitten fingers, the twins Bren and Sorrel who finished each other's sentences because between them they only had one, a quiet man called Doss who carried Maren's map-stone wrapped in wool like a relic, and the sword, which rode at Vesper's hip and never once complained about the cold, which should have been my first clue that it wasn't entirely an object.

Vesper took first watch. She always took first watch. She said sleep came hard to her and she'd rather spend the sleeplessness usefully, and I believed her, the way you believe things about people you've decided to trust, which is to say completely and without evidence, because trust built through shared hunger doesn't come with receipts.

I woke sometime past middle-night needing — I'll be honest — to piss behind the hut, and on my way back I heard the whispering.

Low. Rhythmic. Almost tender. My first thought was that Vesper was praying, and I felt a small warm shock of it, because I'd privately decided she was the kind of person who thought prayer was a tax paid by other people. Then I realized the cadence was wrong for prayer. Prayer rises. This fell. It fell the way names fall when you say them over graves.

I stood in the doorway gap with the wind at my back and listened.

"—Aldous Wren," the sword whispered, in a voice like a file drawn over iron. "Aldous Wren. Keeper of the Third Archive. Aldous Wren."

Third Archive. I knew those words. Every Thornfell-born child knew them, the way children know the shape of a wound their parents carry. The Archives of Thornfell had held every birth record, every land deed, every song, every name of every person in a country the crown had decided to unmake. You cannot erase a country while its records exist. So the records went first. Everyone knew that. What everyone did not know — what I had never once let myself wonder, because wondering led to asking, and asking led to answers I couldn't afford — was *who burned them.*

"Aldous Wren," the sword said again, patient as snowfall. "Avenged."

And then, softer, in a different register — still the sword, but carrying something underneath it, the way ice carries the river:

"*She signed the order.*"

My blood did something complicated.

"She signed the order," the sword repeated, and this time the voice beneath it was unmistakable, and it was Vesper's, young, and it cracked on the second word. "*I signed the order. I carried the torch to the west stacks myself. Aldous Wren tried to stop me and I—*" A breath. Not the sword's breath. "*He was so old. He kept saying, girl, girl, do you know what you're burning. And I said yes. That was the oath talking. The oath made it true. I knew exactly what I was burning.*"

I should have gone back inside. I want that understood too. I should have gone back inside and lain down and stared at the roof beams and said nothing, ever, the way entire countries say nothing, and maybe we'd all have made it through the pass alive.

But the sword kept going, and I kept standing there, and somewhere in me a boy who'd been told to walk the roads until someone found him — walked, and waited, and was found, and rescued, and trusted, and *trusted back* — decided he deserved to know what he was trusting.

"Corin Vale," the sword whispered. "Corin Vale. Cartographer. Avenged." A pause. "Cartographer," it said again, almost puzzled, the way a thing repeats a word it has carried too long. "*She drew the maps that replaced his. She drew Thornfell blank. Blank, blank, blank, and her hand never shook once, because the oath wouldn't let it shake, and afterward, when the oath slept, it shook for a year.*"

"*I didn't know his name until the blade gave it to me,*" Vesper's buried voice said. "*That's the joke. That's the crown's little joke. They make you erase a man and then your own sword makes you avenge him, so you carry him forever, so you can never again pretend you didn't know.*"

There was more. Names I didn't know, deeds I didn't know, a whole stratum of her life laid open in the dark like sediment in cut earth. But I stopped hearing the individual words after Corin Vale, because a sound had started in my ears like the sea, and under the sea-sound one sentence kept surfacing, over and over, wearing different clothes each time:

She helped burn it. She helped burn it. She helped burn my country, and then she found me, and she didn't know I knew, and she saved me anyway, or — worse — she saved me *because*, some penance arithmetic I couldn't read, some debt she was paying into my account without telling me the account existed.

I went back inside. I lay down. I did not sleep. Outside, faintly, all night, the sword went on saying names, and Vesper went on listening, and neither of them ever paused long enough for me to hear her answer, if she answered at all.

* * *

In the morning the world was white and vicious and beautiful, the way mountains are when they're deciding whether to kill you. We broke camp fast. Tam's fingers were worse; Sorrel bound them without either twin saying a word, which was somehow louder than speech. Vesper moved down the line checking gear with quick competent hands, and when she got to me she crouched, looked me over, and said, "You slept badly."

"I slept fine."

"You slept badly. You've got the look. Everyone gets the look after their first night on the spine." She pulled my pack strap tighter than strictly necessary. "Eat. We climb till the light goes."

I ate. I climbed. All day I climbed with the sentence walking beside me like a fourth companion: *she helped burn it*. And here's the thing I learned about betrayal on the slopes of the Thornfells, at sixteen, with frost in my lashes: it doesn't shout. It doesn't even argue. It just walks with you, quietly, and points things out. The way she avoided my eyes at the midday halt. The way she'd flinched, that first day in the cage wagon, when I'd said the word *Thornfell* — I'd thought it was fear of hunters. It might have been. Or it might have been the flinch of a woman hearing a country she'd helped unmake named aloud by a boy born under its erased sky. The way she never spoke of the fall except in facts, dates, troop movements, the flat recitation of someone keeping a confession locked in a strongbox and reciting the inventory instead of opening it.

By late afternoon I was furious, and the terrible part — the part I still can't make sit straight — is that I was furious mostly at myself. For trusting so fast. For being *findable*. For being, possibly, not a rescue at all but a transaction. Bait, Maren had said he was bait, and I'd swallowed that, and now a new worm turned under it: maybe the bait hadn't been set by the crown alone. Maybe a guilty woman had walked the smuggler's roads for years hoping the crown's trap would spring, hoping to be handed a Thornfell child the way a drowning man hopes to be handed a rope, so she could haul on it and call the hauling redemption.

Or maybe — and this voice was quieter, and crueler — maybe she'd saved me because saving me was easier than facing what she'd done, and none of it, none of it, had ever actually been about me at all.

We sheltered that night in a hollow beneath an overhang of blue ice, packed shoulder to shoulder. Vesper took first watch again. Of course she did.

I waited until the others' breathing went slow and even. Then I got up, crossed the frozen ground, and sat down across the dead fire from her, close enough that the sword hung in the space between us like a third person at a table.

She watched me come. She didn't say anything. She was good at not saying things.

"You were talking to it," I said. "Last night. At the hut."

Something moved behind her eyes — not surprise. Confirmation. Like a hunter who'd heard the trap click shut around her own ankle and had been waiting, almost relieved, for the pull.

"Yes," she said.

"It said names."

"It always says names. That's what it's for."

"It said Aldous Wren." I kept my voice low, for the sleepers, though part of me wanted to shout it off the mountain. "Keeper of the Third Archive. It said *avenged*. And then it said—" My throat closed. I made myself finish. "It said *she signed the order*. In your voice."

The wind filled the silence. Above us the ice groaned, settling, the sound a glacier makes when it shifts its weight, which is the sound I imagine a conscience makes too.

"Rafael—"

"Were you there?" I asked. "At the burning. Were you *there*?"

She could have lied. I want to be clear that she could have. She was, by every measure I knew then, a better liar than I would ever be — years of false papers, false names, a whole false life worn smooth as river stone. One word, *no*, delivered flat, and I'd have spent the rest of the climb gnawing doubt instead of certainty, and doubt is a much more survivable meal.

She looked at the sword. Then at me. And she said, "Yes."

Just that. No armor on it anywhere.

"I was nineteen," she said. "New-sworn. They gave me the west stacks because I was expendable and obedient, and I was both, and I lit them, and an old man named Aldous Wren tried to stand between me and the shelves, and I—" Her hand went to the hilt, not drawing, just resting, the way you'd rest a hand on the shoulder of the only witness left. "I did what oaths do. I cleared him out of the way. He lived another nine years, the blade tells me. Died in Grayford. Alone. And my sword avenged him against *me*, in the only way it could, which was to make me remember his name every night for the rest of my life."

"You drew the maps," I said. "Blank ones."

"Yes."

"You erased my country with your own hand."

"Yes."

"And then you found me." My voice cracked and I hated it, hated the crack, hated that grief and rage sound identical at sixteen. "Was that penance? Was I a coin you were paying? Did you even—" I stopped, because the real question was underneath and ugly and I made myself ask it. "Did you save me? Or did you collect me?"

For a long moment she said nothing. The fire was dead; the only light was starlight off the ice, and it made her face look carved, provisional, like something the mountain was still deciding whether to keep.

"I don't know," she said finally. "I have asked myself that every day since the cage wagon, and I don't know, and I won't lie to you and pretend the answer came back clean. Maybe I wanted to save a boy. Maybe I wanted to save myself. Both things were in the same pair of hands, Rafael. I couldn't separate them then and I can't now."

"That's not good enough."

"No," she agreed, terribly calm. "It isn't."

"You should have told me."

"When?" And there — there the calm cracked, just a hairline, just enough to see the heat behind it. "The first night, in the bothy? When you were starving and I was the only reason you were eating? On the road, with hunters behind us and the pass closing? At Hollowmere, where one wrong word gets remembered wrong forever? Tell me the right moment, Rafael, and I'll tell you why it didn't exist. There is no moment when *I burned your country's memory* is a gift you hand someone who trusts you. There's only sooner or later, and I chose later, and later is now, and I'm sorry."

"Sorry." I laughed, and it came out awful. "Maren died pointing us at a door. Doss carries her stone like it's a saint's fingerbone. Those people gave you their road because they thought—" I stopped.

"Thought what?" she said quietly. "Say it."

"Because they thought you were one of us."

The silence after that was the longest of my life. Somewhere below us, far off, a wolf or a hunter or the wind made a sound and stopped.

"I was," Vesper said at last. "That's the whole obscenity of it, Rafael. I *was* one of you. I was born four valleys east of the Door. I swore the crown's oath because the alternative was a pit and my mother was already in it, and the oath worked, the way their oaths work — it didn't make me evil, it made me *obedient*, which is worse, because obedience lets you keep your own face while you do it." She drew a breath. "Every night the sword gives me back a name of someone I wronged, and every morning I get up and walk toward the Door anyway, because it's the only direction left that isn't away. If that's not one of you, then I don't know what the word means anymore. Maybe it doesn't. Maybe the crown was right that countries are just stories, and mine ended, and what's walking beside you is whatever grew in the ash."

"The sword," I said, "told me what grew in the ash. It talks all night, Vesper. Did you know that? Did you know it doesn't wait for you to hold it?"

Something crossed her face then that I didn't have a name for. Not shame. Something older and tireder.

"I know," she said. "It's why I take first watch. It's why I sleep apart. It's why—" She stopped. Started again, lower. "It's why I was going to tell you. At the Door. Before I went through it. Because the price of the Door is the whole name, Rafael — every memory bound to it, spoken and surrendered — and I thought, let the last thing I am be the truth of what I did, not the comfort of what I wished I'd done. I thought you'd never have to hear it from me at all. I thought I'd be erasing it." Her mouth twisted. "The sword had other ideas. Swords usually do."

"So that's the plan. You walk into a door and unmake yourself, and I'm supposed to feel *grateful*?"

"I'm not supposed to feel anything. Neither are you. Feeling comes after surviving. Ask me in spring."

"Don't." I was on my feet without remembering standing. "Don't do that. Don't make it wry. You burned the archives. You drew the blank maps. And now you're going to die politely at a door and leave me holding a country I've never seen, rebuilt out of *your* guilt, and I'm supposed to call that loyalty?"

"I don't know what you're supposed to call it," she said. "I genuinely don't. I only know what it is."

"And what's that?"

She looked at me for a long time. Behind her the ice field ran silver to the top of the world, and the wind came down it carrying the smell of distance, of the place we were going, of the country that wasn't.

"Mine," she said. "Whatever it is — the guilt, the rescue, the road, the door — it's mine. I'm not giving any of it back. Not to the crown, not to the oath, not to you. Not even to the sword." Her hand closed on the hilt, and the blade, faintly, in the dark, whispered a single word I couldn't catch, and her jaw tightened as if it had bitten her. "Especially not to the sword."

I turned and walked back to the overhang and lay down with my back to her, and I did not sleep, and neither, I'd have bet the map-stone, did she.

But somewhere in the black hours, near dawn, I heard it again — the whisper, low, rhythmic, falling like names fall over graves. And this time I caught the word it had bitten her with. This time the wind was right and the stone was thin and I heard it plain.

The sword was practicing her name.

Over and over, soft as snowfall, the way you rehearse something you're about to lose.

And I lay there in the cold understanding, at last, what I'd actually overheard — not a confession, not a crime, but a countdown — and that whatever Vesper planned at the Door of Anew, she had already begun saying goodbye to herself, one syllable a night, and there were fewer syllables left than any of us knew.
