## 15. Split at the Saddle

He was gone before dawn, and I will tell you honestly: I heard him go.

That's the part that shames me. Not that he ran — I'd have run too, at fifteen, carrying what the sword had told him about me. No, the shame is that I lay in the dark of the saddle cave with my hand on the hilt and listened to his boots crunch away over frozen scree, and I told myself it was better this way. Cleaner. One less mouth on the glacier road. One less boy for Serane Vole to hang.

I told myself a great many things in that hour, and every one of them was a lie wearing sensible boots.

The sword knew before I did. It always does. It lay across my knees and hummed — not words yet, just that low ache of steel remembering — and when I finally stood and looked down the southern trail where the snow showed small, quick footprints heading *away* from the Door of Anew, the blade spoke one name into my skull like a bell struck behind my teeth.

*Rafael.*

"Yes," I said to it. "Thank you. I had noticed."

The wind took my sarcasm and scattered it somewhere over the Thornfells, which is where most of my dignity has ended up these past months.

* * *

Let me be fair to him, because no one else will be now that Maren is dead and buried under two feet of powder with her map-stone broken in my pocket. Rafael hadn't run out of cowardice. He ran out of arithmetic.

I saw it happen, is the thing. The night before, after the confrontation — after he stood there in the firelight with his face gone white as the glacier and said, *"You burned us. You helped burn us"* — I watched him do sums. I could practically hear the abacus clicking behind his eyes. If Vesper carried me to the Door, the hunters follow Vesper. The hunters find the colony's trail. The children who survived the raid — Tam and the little Ostwick girls and the mute one they called Sparrow — all of them die on a mountainside because a witch-hunter followed a swordspeaker's bootprints.

But if Rafael walks into Serane's camp and gives himself up —

If the bait stops running, the hunt stops hunting.

It was wrong. It was the arithmetic of a child who thinks the wolf can be reasoned with if you just stand still enough. But it was *loving* arithmetic, and I have known grown soldiers with thirty years of service who never once did math that generous.

So when I found his trail leading south along the saddle, toward the smoke-smudge on the horizon that marked the hunters' camp of ash lanterns, I understood exactly what I was looking at. And I stood at the fork in the road — literally, physically, one path climbing north toward a door older than the country it exits, the other dropping south toward everything I'd sworn against — and I gave myself until the count of ten to choose the smart option.

I got to four.

"Damn it," I said, and turned south.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about breaking an oath twice: the first time, it tears. The second time, it *pulls*, like a fishhook set deep in the meat of you, and every step in the wrong direction ratchets it tighter.

My crown oath was fraying already — had been since Grayford, since the cage wagon, since the moment I killed three men in spoken steel for a boy who made stones bloom. But the oath-magic doesn't die cleanly. It drags. All that day, walking south off the smuggler roads onto ground the crown still claimed, I felt it hauling at me like a current under ice, trying to turn my feet toward the capital, toward the Hall of Oaths, toward kneeling and confessing and being forgiven by men who burn children.

By midday my teeth ached from clenching against it. By late afternoon I was talking to myself, which is either a bad sign or a long-established habit; with me it's genuinely difficult to say.

"He'll surrender," I told the wind. "Serane will take him, march him to the capital, and the crown gets its speaker and its proof, and the exiles live because the hunt ends." I kicked a stone off the trail. "That's the bargain he thinks he's making."

The wind said nothing, being wind, but the sword murmured low and unhappy, and I've learned to trust the sword's unhappiness more than my own optimism. Because here's what Rafael didn't know — couldn't know, because I'd kept it from him even after he learned the worst of it:

Serane Vole doesn't want speakers delivered alive to the capital. I'd seen her ledger, back in another life, another name. Speakers who reach the capital get questioned about the Door of Anew. And the crown fears the Door more than any gift, which means the questions don't stop until the answers run out, and answers run out considerably faster than boys do.

He wasn't buying the exiles' safety. He was selling himself to an executioner and calling it a fair price.

* * *

I caught sight of the camp at dusk, and I want to describe it properly, because "camp of ash lanterns" sounds like poetry and what it actually is, is worse.

They burn the confiscated belongings of the taken. Books, charms, letters, toys — anything that might carry the gift or carry grief, the hunters don't distinguish. They render it down into lantern-fuel, and the camp burns that, so every light in Serane's encampment is the ghost of somebody's life. From the ridge above the valley, the camp looked like a handful of orange coals scattered in a black bowl, and the smoke rising off it was thick and sweetish and wrong, like a funeral that wouldn't end.

And there, at the edge of the firelight, small and alone and walking with his shoulders up around his ears like a boy wading into cold water — Rafael.

He'd made it. Of course he'd made it; the gods protect children walking toward their own funerals, it's one of their cruelest jokes. I could see the pickets, the sentries, the long pale shape of a cage wagon parked by the command tent — they'd brought the same wagon, or one identical, and something in my chest went hot and metallic at the sight of it.

I crouched in the rocks above the camp and did my own arithmetic, and mine came out different from his.

Option one: walk in unarmed, as I once planned, and bargain. I'd done the unarmed walk-in before. It works right up until it doesn't, and Serane Vole is not a woman who blinks first.

Option two: leave him. Climb north through the night, reach the Door alone, open it with my name, and let the exiles cross while Rafael's surrender bought them time. Clean. Sensible. The kind of choice they give medals for, posthumously, to people who were very good at not loving anyone.

Option three —

Well. Option three required the sword, and the sword requires names, and I had already lost so many of mine to it that some nights I had to recite my own history like a litany just to keep the edges from fraying. *Vesper Eastwick. Born in Thornfell before the burning. Sworn to the crown after. Broke it for a boy.* Every name I spent was a memory gone, and I was starting to spend memories I needed.

But option three was the only one where everybody walked away, and I have never in my life been able to resist the stupidest possible version of mercy.

* * *

Before I moved, I did the thing I always do before blood: I asked the sword who it remembered.

It's a ritual, of sorts. The blade speaks the names of the dead it has avenged, and on quiet nights it recites them to me like a rosary — couriers and smugglers and one poor shepherd caught in crossfire whose name I carry like a stone in my shoe. Tonight it whispered the usual litany, and then, at the end, softer than the rest, it said a name I didn't recognize.

A woman's name. Old-fashioned. Thornfell vowels.

"I haven't avenged anyone new," I whispered. "Who is that?"

The sword pulsed warm against my palm, and I understood, and I nearly wept right there on the ridge like an idiot with frostbite and feelings. It was Maren. It had been Maren since the raid — since she died with her hand pressed over mine and the broken map-stone between us. The sword had been carrying her this whole time, waiting for me to listen.

She died avenged? No. She died *witnessed*. Maybe that's all any of us get. Maybe it's enough.

"All right," I told her, and meant both of them — the dead woman and the living blade. "All right. We're going in."

* * *

I went down the north side of the valley where the sentries' firelight didn't reach, and I'll grant myself this much: I am very good at this part. Twenty years of smuggler roads will teach you how to move through a hunter's camp like a rumor. I counted eight sentries, two officers, one cage wagon, and — there, by the big tent, arms crossed, breath steaming in the lantern-light like a woman smoking with her whole body — Serane Vole herself.

Rafael stood in front of her. Even at this distance I could read the scene like a broadsheet: the boy offering his wrists, the captain not taking them. She was talking to him. Her head was tilted in that particular way I remembered from the old days, the way she tilts when someone has surprised her, and Serane Vole is not often surprised.

I was forty yards out, flat against a supply sled, close enough to catch fragments on the wind.

"—told you the truth," Rafael was saying, voice cracking and steadying, cracking and steadying. "There are others. Children. On the glacier road. If you take me, you don't need to—"

"Boy." Serane's voice carries like a whip over water. "Do you know what happens to speakers who reach the capital?"

Silence. The good kind or the bad kind; I couldn't tell.

"They ask questions," she said. "About a door."

And I watched my whole clever plan die in the space of a heartbeat, because Rafael went rigid, and Serane leaned in, and whatever she said next was too low for the wind to carry — but I saw her glance north, up the glacier road, and I saw Rafael's face do the thing faces do when a child realizes the adult across from him was never going to play fair.

Then Serane turned and gave an order, and two hunters moved toward the cage wagon, and I stopped doing arithmetic entirely.

Some choices you don't make. Some choices make you, and you just try to survive the delivery.

I drew the sword, and the sword sang — one clear note of spoken steel that rolled across the camp and put out, I swear on every name I carry, three ash lanterns at once, as if the ghosts inside them flinched. Every head in the valley turned toward me. Eight sentries reached for weapons. Serane Vole looked up at the dark slope where I stood revealed, and across forty yards of frozen ground, hunter and traitor recognized each other like old dance partners.

"Vesper," she called, and my name in her mouth sounded like a bill coming due. "You're a hard woman to bury."

"You have no idea," I called back, and started down the hill with the sword singing names ahead of me like a herald, toward the boy, toward the cage wagon, toward the camp of ash lanterns where every light was somebody's burned life — knowing that past it, north, in the cold and the dark, a door was waiting that would cost me everything I had left to pay.

The last thing I heard before the fighting started was Rafael shouting my name — my true one, the one the sword knows — and I thought: *hold on to that, girl. Whatever else you lose tonight, hold on to that.*

Then the first spear came out of the dark, and the sword began to speak.
