## 16. Camp of Ash Lanterns

The boy walked into my camp at dusk, and I want it on the record that I did not order him taken. I want it on the record because records are all I have left, and because by morning there would be no one left to write one.

He came down the eastern slope with his hands empty and raised, which is more than most of my prisoners manage, and he stopped at the edge of the lantern light like a man testing whether a river is cold. The ash lanterns — my own invention, though I'll never be thanked for it — burned low and gray around the perimeter, throwing that particular light that makes everything look already mourned. Forty hunters in a horseshoe of tents below the saddle, and one boy standing outside the glow as if the glow were a border between countries.

Which, in a sense, it was.

"Serane Vole," he said. His voice cracked on the second word, and he pushed through it the way you push through a door you've decided to hate. "I'm Rafael. I'm the one you're hunting. And I've come to give myself up."

Behind me, Corporal Dren made a sound like a man swallowing his own opinion. I let him. There was a time for opinions and this was not it.

"You walked three days through hunter country," I said, "to surrender."

"I walked three days to stop something worse from happening." He lifted his chin. He was trying to look older than he was, and failing, and the failure was somehow more convincing than success would have been. "If you take me quietly, no one else has to burn."

I looked at him for a long moment. Snow had gotten into the collar of a coat two sizes too big for him — a dead man's coat, or a living man's, hard to say on those roads — and he hadn't brushed it out. Boys never do. They think the cold is a test they can win by ignoring it.

"Bring him to my tent," I said. "Unbound. If anyone lays a hand on him before I say so, they can explain their reasoning to me in writing."

Dren's eyebrows performed an entire correspondence. I ignored them too.

* * *

Here is what the crown's file on Serane Vole does not contain, because I burned it myself four years ago and have been careful ever since: when I was nineteen, stationed at a garrison on the coast where nothing happens except weather, I told a joke to a dying friend, and the joke *worked*.

Not well. Not grandly. But the wind changed, and the fever broke, and my friend opened her eyes and said, "You absolute menace, what did you do," and I said nothing, because there is nothing you can say. Words that land somewhere deeper than ears. Every speaker knows the moment. The world leans in and listens, and after that you are never again only yourself.

I am not a swordspeaker. I have no blade that remembers names. My gift is small and mean and useful — words that find the soft places in people and set things moving. A witch-hunter with a gift. You may laugh. I did, once, alone, and then I got very practical about it.

Because here is the arithmetic nobody in Grayford wants done aloud: the hunt for speakers is run, in significant part, by a speaker. If I am discovered, I do not face trial. Trials are for people whose existence the crown can afford to acknowledge. I would simply stop appearing in reports, and then stop appearing in memories, and then — if the stories about Thornfell are even half true — stop appearing at all.

So I hunted my own kind with the diligence of a woman digging her own grave slowly enough to enjoy the scenery. It is not courage. It is not cowardice either. It is a cage with very good locks, and I polished every one.

And now a boy sat across my campaign table, drinking the broth I'd ordered for him, watching me with eyes too old for his face, and I caught myself doing the thing I always did — reading him for the gift, measuring the danger, tallying the threat — and underneath that, quieter, the other thing. Recognition. The way one caged animal knows another by how it paces.

"You're not eating," he said.

"I ate."

"You're watching me eat."

"I'm assessing you. It's different."

"It isn't." He set down the spoon. "You look at me the way the matron at the asylum looked at us. Like she was counting exits."

"The matron at the asylum," I said, "was counting exits because she was locked in with you, not because she disliked you."

Something flickered across his face. "You were at the asylum?"

"I put people there. It's in the job description." I folded my hands. "Why are you really here, Rafael? And don't tell me about saving anyone. I've read enough confessions to know they're all written afterward, by someone else."

He looked at the broth. At the lantern. At me.

"Vesper will come for me," he said. "She won't leave me. She's the kind of person who doesn't leave people, even when it's stupid, even when it kills her. So if I'm in your camp, she comes to your camp, and then—" His voice snagged. He pushed through it again; I was beginning to recognize the gesture, the way you recognize a limp. "Then you can take her without burning the colony. Without going back up the mountain. Everyone else gets away. That's the trade. Me for her, and you leave Thornfell's people alone."

"And if I refuse the trade?"

"Then I've still slowed you down by a day." He almost smiled. "You have forty men and one road, Captain. I checked."

A boy who checks roads. Who walks into a camp of witch-hunters with a plan built entirely out of someone else's loyalty. I should have admired it. I did admire it, in the cold professional part of me, the part that keeps ledgers. In the rest of me — the part I keep off the books — it felt like watching someone hand me a knife and ask me nicely to use it exactly as intended.

"Sleep," I said. "We'll talk in the morning."

"There is no morning for this," he said. "Snow's coming. Your own scouts said the pass closes in two days. Whatever you're going to decide, you'll decide tonight."

He was right, which annoyed me, so I didn't answer. I called the guard, and I sat alone with my maps and my ash lanterns and the small mean truth of myself, and I waited for Vesper Eastwick, because of course she was coming. I had known she would come since the day the smuggler described her to me in a cell in Grayford: tall, gray-eyed, moves like water finding a crack. Carries a sword she never draws twice.

What the smuggler hadn't known — what no one knew — was that I'd met her before. Eleven years ago. The day Thornfell stopped existing.

* * *

You have to understand what the erasure looked like from inside it. Not fire. Fire is honest. It was clerks.

I was twenty-three, newly promoted, assigned to a records detail that was itself unrecorded. We went into the Thornfell archive under sealed orders, and we burned nothing — that was the elegant horror of it. We *catalogued*. Every deed, every birth record, every map, every song transcribed by some diligent junior officer with good handwriting. We catalogued a country so it could be removed from every ledger in the realm at once, cleanly, administratively, and then the catalogues themselves went into a vault beneath the capital, and the country went nowhere, officially, because officially it had never been anywhere.

There was a woman in the archive that day. Tall, gray-eyed, wearing a crown officer's coat that fit her badly, as if she'd been dressed in a hurry by someone who hated her. She carried a sword wrapped in cloth, which was against regulation, and she stood at the window while we worked, and once she said, very quietly, to no one: "Every name in here is somebody's whole grief."

I remember thinking: that officer is going to be trouble.

I remember also — and this is the part I have spent eleven years not thinking — that she looked at me when she said it. Directly at me. As if she knew. As if whatever small wrongness lived in my words had shown through my skin the way a brand shows through a sleeve.

I reported nothing. I told myself it was discretion. It was the first entry in a ledger I have been keeping ever since, a ledger of moments where I could have been brave and instead was careful, each one small, each one survivable, each one making the next one easier.

Now she was walking up my mountain to collect a boy, and I had two days of pass left, forty hunters, sealed orders from a crown that feared a door more than any person, and a memory I had buried so deep I'd nearly convinced myself it belonged to someone else.

* * *

She came at moonrise, and she came unarmed, and my sentries nearly died of embarrassment.

They had her at spear-point ten yards from the lantern line before I heard the commotion, and when I came out of my tent, there she was: older, harder, the coat long since traded for smuggler's wool, but the same stillness. The same way of standing as if the ground had asked her permission. Her sword was nowhere visible, which frightened my men considerably more than if she'd been holding it.

"Hunter-Captain Vole," she said. Her voice was level and roughened, a voice that had spent years being quiet in loud places. "I've come to bargain."

"You brought no weapon."

"I brought no weapon."

"That's either trust or contempt."

"It's arithmetic." She glanced past me, toward the tent where Rafael was held, and something moved behind her eyes — quick, suppressed, the flinch of a person checking on someone they love while pretending to check on a hostage. "Forty men. One road. Two days of pass. You don't need weapons to negotiate with numbers."

"Corporal Dren thinks you should be bound."

"Corporal Dren is welcome to try."

Dren, wisely, developed an interest in his bootlaces.

"Inside," I said. "Alone. Anyone follows, and the bargain's off before it starts."

My tent was warm and close and smelled of tallow and ink. She sat without being asked, which I noted, and declined wine, which I also noted, and looked at the maps spread on my table — the passes, the saddle, the glacier beyond — with the expression of a woman reading her own death warrant and finding the handwriting sloppy.

"You know why I'm here," she said.

"I know why the boy thinks you're here. He's under the impression you can be traded for."

"He's not wrong."

"He's fourteen."

"He's right, which is worse." She leaned forward. "Take me. Let him go. Let the exiles go. You get the prize capture of your career, delivered voluntarily, and the crown's hunt up here ends with no fires and no graves. That's the offer."

"And what makes you think I can grant any of it? My orders come from the capital. They are not negotiable, and neither, unfortunately, am I."

"Everyone's negotiable. I've spent years among smugglers; it's ruined my faith in absolutes." Her gaze settled on me, steady, searching. "But you already know that, don't you, Captain. You've known it a long time."

The lantern hissed. Outside, the wind found the guy-lines and played them like bad harps.

"I don't know what you mean."

"Don't you?" Softly. Not cruel. That was the unbearable part — there was no cruelty in it at all, just recognition, offered plainly, like a hand. "Eleven years ago there was a records detail in the Thornfell archive. Sealed orders. A young officer who watched a country get filed away and never said a word. I stood at the window, Captain. I saw your face."

The cold that went through me then had nothing to do with the mountain. "That's not possible. That detail was—"

"Unrecorded. Yes. But my sword remembers things that aren't recorded." For the first time, something in her voice bent. "It remembers the names of everyone Thornfell lost. It remembers the clerks. It remembers you, Serane Vole, sitting at a table with good handwriting, cataloguing songs so they could be killed properly. And it remembers that you looked up once, near the end, and your eyes were wet, and you went on writing anyway."

I stood up. I don't remember deciding to. "You have no proof."

"I don't need proof. I need you to hear it." She stayed seated. She wasn't trying to threaten me; that was the trap of her, the thing that had kept her alive all these years. She simply told the truth and let it sit there, breathing. "You've been hunting us for eleven years, and you've been one of us for longer than that, and you know it, and every night you lock the cage a little tighter so you don't have to look at it. I'm not here to shame you. I'm here because I think you're tired."

"Tired." I laughed, and it came out wrong, jagged. "You think I'm *tired*."

"I think everyone in this story is in a cage, Captain. Mine was an oath. Yours is a secret. The boy's is everyone he loves. The only question worth asking is who's willing to open theirs first."

For a long moment neither of us spoke. The wind worked at the tent. Somewhere out in the dark, a sentry coughed, and the sound was so ordinary it hurt.

Because she was right, and I hated her for it, and I hated myself for hating her, and underneath all of it — far down, in the place where I kept the memory of wet eyes and good handwriting — something shifted its weight. Something that had been lying still for eleven years, playing dead.

"If I open mine," I said quietly, "the crown opens me. You know what they do with hunters who turn. There's no pardon for it. There's no asylum. There's a report that stops, and a name that stops, and then there's nothing."

"Yes," Vesper said. "I know exactly what that's like. I swore an oath to that crown after they erased my country, and the oath has been dragging me toward the capital for weeks like a hook in my ribs. I know precisely what it costs." She rose, slowly, and stood across the table from me, and up close her eyes were the exact gray of the glacier we both meant to die upon. "So here is my last card, Captain, and I'll play it plain: the Door of Anew is real. Maren Ostwick's stone is real. Tonight the snow closes the pass, and tomorrow the world forgets this mountain until spring — unless someone opens a door that leads out of a country your masters are terrified exists."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then in the morning you march forty men up a glacier that wants you dead, to seize a door that eats names, on behalf of a crown that would erase you the moment it learned what you are." She picked up her cloak. "I wouldn't. But then, I've already broken my oath. It turns out the falling is the easy part."

She paused at the tent flap, her back to me, snow-light edging her shoulders.

"For what it's worth," she said, "your handwriting was very good. The songs deserved worse."

Then she was gone into the dark, and I stood alone with my maps and my ash lanterns and the ledger of my life, and I took out the sealed orders from the capital, and I read them again, and for the first time in eleven years I asked myself not whether I could obey them —

but whether I could survive obeying them one more day.

Outside, the first flakes of the closing storm came down through the lantern smoke, gray as ash, and from the prisoner's tent I heard Rafael's voice, low and urgent, saying something I couldn't make out — and then, unmistakably, the sound of my own name, spoken by someone who was not me, in a voice that was not mine, coming from a direction where no one stood.
