## 17. The Door of Anew

The map-stone had been lying against my sternum for three days, and for three days it had been getting warmer.

I don't mean comfortable-warm. I mean the kind of warm a stone has no business being — the kind that makes you check your shirt for embers, then check your skin for fever, then finally accept that the thing Maren Ostwick bled into my hands is doing what stones are not supposed to do. It was guiding me. Or herding me. With Maren dead and Rafael gone, I couldn't have told you which, and I'd stopped trying to care about the difference somewhere around the second night, when the wind came down off the col like a debt collector and the stone stayed warm as a sleeping bird under my coat.

Four of us left. Me; Dagny, who walked like a woman made entirely of stubbornness and frostbite scars; little Corvin, eleven years old, who had survived a raid on his village by hiding in a root cellar and now flinched at his own shadow; and old Tam, who claimed to remember Thornfell's roads from before they were unmade, though half his memories were probably inventions, because that's what happens to a country when you erase it — the survivors start making up the parts the crown took, and nobody can prove them wrong.

We were climbing the spine of the Thornfells in the last week the mountains would allow it. The pass would close within days. Everyone knew it. The snow had stopped pretending to be weather and started being a wall with a slope problem.

And still the stone pulled north.

* * *

I want to tell you we climbed with purpose and grace. What actually happened is that Tam fell into a crevasse up to his ribs on the second morning and I had to speak him out of it — not his name, I didn't know his true name, but the sword knew enough of him by then, enough of his mother's kitchen and his first winter and the girl he'd married in a village that no longer existed, that when I laid the flat of the blade against the ice and said *hold*, the ice held. The sword remembers what the crown burned. That's the whole filthy miracle of it. Somewhere in its steel live the names of every dead thing I've avenged, and lately it had started remembering things I never gave it — roads, doorways, a word in Old Thornish that meant *the place where the map gives up*.

Dagny heard me talking to the blade that night and didn't ask. Smart woman. You don't ask a swordspeaker questions you might have to answer for later.

By the third day the glacier wall rose ahead of us like the side of a fallen cathedral — blue ice shot through with ash-grey strata, so old it creaked. Not cracked. Creaked. Glaciers groan when they move, everyone knows that, but this one made a sound I can only describe as a door deciding whether to open, and I am aware of how that sounds, and I no longer care, because I stood in front of it and listened.

The map-stone went hot against my chest. Hot enough that I fished it out with numb fingers and held it at arm's length, and it glowed — faintly, the way coals glow after the fire's been raked over, a dull orange heartbeat in its cracked face. Maren's blood was still in the fissures. Her last gift, or her last order, depending on how sentimental you felt about dead women who'd tested your loyalty with questions designed to make you confess to burning your own country's records.

I held it toward the ice.

Nothing happened, for the space of ten heartbeats, and I had time to think: *of course. Of course the magic fails now, at the end, with the pass closing and hunters behind us and nothing left but a hot rock and a wall of frozen water.* I had time to feel the specific flavor of despair that comes from having bet everything on an heirloom and a rumor.

Then the seam opened.

It wasn't dramatic. That's what I'll keep telling people, if anyone ever asks, which no one will, because everyone who saw it is either here or dead. No thunder. No light show. The glacier simply developed a line — a vertical crack, thin as a hair, running from the snow at our feet up beyond where I could follow it with my eyes. And around the line, the ice changed color. Went clear. Glass-clear, deep as a well, and inside the clarity something stood.

A door.

Not set into the ice. Standing free of it, the way a door stands free in a field where a house used to be — except there had never been a house here, unless you counted the country itself as the house, and I suppose that was exactly the point. Grey wood, older than any grey wood has a right to be, grain worn smooth as riverstone. Iron hinges gone green-black with age. A lintel carved with letters in a script I shouldn't have recognized and did — Old Thornish, the same tongue the sword whispered, the same tongue the crown had spent twenty years hunting out of mouths. And above the lintel, a single word I could read only because the sword read it aloud in my head, quiet as a confession:

*Anew.*

Tam sat down in the snow. Just sat, like his legs had been repossessed. Corvin stared with his mouth open until Dagny closed it for him, gently, the way you close a book you're not done reading. And I stood there with a warm stone in my hand and a cold knot in my throat, looking at a door older than the country it exits, in a nation that officially does not exist, on a mountain the maps call nothing, and I thought:

*Well. Here's the punchline to twenty years of hiding.*

Because a door means nothing without someone to walk through it. And the ones meant to walk through it were scattered across a valley behind us, hiding from witch-hunters in a colony that had been raided two nights ago, and the boy who was supposed to lead them here —

The boy who was bait. The boy whose whispered words made stone bloom. The boy I'd pulled out of a cage wagon on a high road in spring because killing four men was easier than listening to him cry.

Rafael wasn't here.

* * *

Here is what I did instead of panicking, which is what I do instead of panicking, which is possibly a character flaw: I got practical. We had perhaps two hours of light. The Door stood open in its seam of clear ice — not open, precisely; the wood was shut, but the ice around it had thinned to a membrane, and when I pressed my palm against it the cold went through me like a word going through a letter, addressed and specific. Something on the other side noticed me. I chose not to think too hard about what.

"We wait," I said. "We rest. We eat everything Tam's been hoarding."

"I haven't been hoarding," said Tam, who had absolutely been hoarding.

"You've been hoarding," said Dagny. "Give it over."

So we ate smoked goat and hard bread and a jar of honey Corvin guarded like a dragon, and I made them sleep in shifts while I watched the Door, because I did not trust it, and I did not trust the stone, and most of all I did not trust the silence that had settled over the glacier since the seam opened. Mountains are loud places. Wind, ice-shift, the constant argument of snow against stone. Since the Door appeared, the wind had stopped at an invisible line maybe thirty paces out, curling around us like water around a rock in a stream, and the air inside that line was still as the inside of a church. It should have been a mercy. Instead it felt like held breath. Like the whole mountain was waiting to see what we'd do.

Around midnight the sword spoke.

Not loudly. Swordspeech never is — that's the point of it, that's why the gift survives in smugglers' daughters and dies in courtiers. But it spoke, a low hum in the steel at my hip, and the word it hummed was a name, and the name was *Rafael*.

I sat up so fast I cracked my head on nothing, which tells you how disoriented the stillness had made me.

"He's coming," I said, to no one, to everyone, to the sword. "He's alive and he's coming."

Dagny, awake on her shift, looked at me with the expression of a woman recalculating her odds. "You're sure?"

"The blade knows his name. I put it there myself, back in the bothy, the night he told me he was told to walk these roads until someone found him." I touched the hilt. It was warm. Everything warm in my life lately was either a trap or a promise, and I had learned you usually couldn't tell which until afterward. "He's walking toward us."

"Or being marched," Dagny said, because she was smart, and smart people ruin things.

Yes. Or being marched. Because Serane Vole had taken him — I'd seen it from the ridge, two days ago, the hunter-captain's column moving down out of the saddle with a small figure at its center, and I had chosen, with a knife twisting in my chest, to keep climbing rather than go back for him alone. Four exiles against forty hunters is not a rescue. It's a larger funeral. I'd told myself he was safer as a prisoner than as a corpse, which is the sort of arithmetic you do when you're tired, and it holds up exactly as long as you don't look directly at it.

Now the sword said his name, and the arithmetic collapsed, and all that was left was the looking.

* * *

They came at dawn.

I heard them before I saw them — boots on snow, many boots, muffled but rhythmic, the march of people who have learned to walk quietly and given up on it because the end is near anyway. Then the column crested the shoulder of the glacier below us, forty strong, grey cloaks dusted white, spears slung, and at its center, flanked by two hunters with crossed halberds, walked a boy in a coat too big for him, limping slightly, chin up.

Rafael.

My chest did something complicated and unhelpful. Relief first — he was alive, he was upright, he was *walking* — and then fury, at him, at Serane, at the whole rotten architecture of crowns and oaths that had put a fourteen-year-old on a death-march up a glacier, and then, underneath all of it, shame, because part of me had spent two days hoping he'd escape on his own so I wouldn't have to choose between him and the others, and hope like that is just cowardice wearing a nicer coat.

At the head of the column, apart from it, walked Hunter-Captain Serane Vole.

I had met her once before, properly — in Hollowmere, months ago, before I understood what she was. She'd bought tea from the memory-broken innkeeper and asked careful questions and left a coin too large for the tea, and I'd marked her as dangerous and moved on. Then she'd cornered me alone on the road, offered me pardon for Rafael's capture, and told me — casually, terribly — that the hunt was never really about speakers at all. The crown feared the Door of Anew. The crown feared a way out of a lie. Every speaker burned, every village emptied, every road unmade: all of it scaffolding around one locked door on one glacier, and here I stood, holding the key Maren died to give me.

Serane stopped thirty paces from the Door. Her column stopped with her, a machine obeying its own momentum. She looked at the seam of clear ice, at the grey door standing free in the blue wall, and I watched her face do something I hadn't seen it do before.

It went still.

Not calm. Still. There's a difference, and I know it intimately, because I've been performing calm my whole life and living stillness only in the worst moments. Calm is a choice. Stillness is what happens when every choice leaves the body at once. Serane Vole stood in the falling snow with her breath smoking and looked at a door her crown had spent twenty years pretending didn't exist, and for three full seconds she was not a hunter-captain. She was a woman seeing the thing she'd been promised wasn't real.

Then she turned to me, and the mask came back down, and she smiled the smile of an official beginning a procedure.

"Eastwick." My name in her mouth sounded like a charge sheet. "You're under arrest for treason, murder of crown personnel, abduction of a ward of the realm, and—" she glanced at the Door, and her jaw tightened— "and trespassing on land that does not exist, which I'm advised is also a crime, which tells you everything about the sanity of my employers."

"Morning to you too," I said.

Rafael made a sound. Small, strangled, the sound of a boy watching his rescue arrive in shackles of its own. He tried to step forward and the halberds crossed in front of him, gentle as a fence, and Serane lifted one hand and the whole column froze like puppets with cut strings.

"Step away from the Door, Eastwick," she said. Quieter now. Almost kind, which from Serane was the most frightening register she owned. "You can't open it. Whatever the stone told you, whatever the dead woman promised — a door like that doesn't open for loyalty or love or any of the currency you people trade in. It opens for a name. A true one, spoken whole. And I know what that costs, better than you'd believe." Something flickered across her face then, fast, gone — a look I'd catalogued in Hollowmere and hadn't understood until this moment. "So put the stone down, take your people, and walk south, and I will let every one of you live. That's the offer. It won't come again."

Behind her, Rafael was staring at me. Not pleading. Worse. He was *watching*, the way he'd watched me kill his escort in the blur of spoken steel, the way children watch adults decide things, filing it all away for the person he'd become. He believed in me. That was the unbearable part. Not my skill, not my oath, not my name — me, the specific tired woman who'd fed him from her own pack and taught him which stars meant *keep walking*. He believed I would do the right thing, and he had no idea what the right thing cost, and I loved him for it, and loving him for it made the cost double.

The wind held its breath at its invisible line. The Door stood in its seam of glass-clear ice, patient as geology. The map-stone pulsed in my hand like a second heart, Maren's blood-warmth, her last instruction, her voice if voices could be mineral: *open it, Eastwick, or everything we were dies with us in the snow.*

Forty spears. Two hours of light. One door.

And between me and all of it, Serane Vole, waiting, still, with the terrible patience of a woman who already knew how this ended and needed only to hear me say it.

I looked at the sword on my hip. It hummed, low, one word, over and over, the way it had hummed since midnight.

*Rafael. Rafael. Rafael.*

I stepped forward.
