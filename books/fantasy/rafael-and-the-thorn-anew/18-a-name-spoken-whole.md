## 18. A Name Spoken Whole

The snow between us made a sound I had never heard before, which is saying something, because I had heard the sound of a valley being unmade and the sound of my own oath sealing shut behind my teeth like a door in a burning house. This was quieter than either. It was the sound of forty spears not moving. It was the sound of Serane Vole deciding.

She stood ten paces off with Rafael at her side — not dragged, not held, just *there*, the way you keep a thing you intend to trade — and her breath came out in slow white ribbons that the wind refused to touch. Everything else on this mountain bent to the wind. The exiles behind me huddled against the glacier wall. The hunters fanned out along the ridge in their ash-grey cloaks, frost bearding every spearpoint. But Serane's breath went straight up, and I understood, finally, what I was looking at.

Not a woman. A held breath. The crown had been holding its breath for twenty years, and here it was, exhaled at last onto a glacier older than the country it was about to stop bordering.

"Eastwick." She said my name the way the ledger said it — flat, filed, correct. "You're going to tell me you can open that."

"I'm going to do more than tell you."

"You're going to tell me the price first." Her eyes went to the sword at my hip, and there — there it was, the flicker she'd been hiding since Grayford, since Hollowmere, since the camp of ash lanterns where she'd looked at me across a fire and I'd thought: *she hunts us because she hates us.* Wrong. She'd looked at the sword the way you look at a cell you escaped from. "Because I've read the old interrogations. Thornfell records. There were speakers who tried the Door before the fall. None of them came back through it. And none of them—" her voice did something small and terrible, "—none of them remembered why they'd tried."

Behind me, Maren Ostwick's map-stone pulsed against my palm, warm as a dying hand.

"Maren knew," Serane said. "Didn't she. That's why she gave it to you and not to herself."

"Maren gave it to me because she was dying and I wasn't," I said. "That's the whole of her arithmetic. She was very economical that way."

"And the price?"

I could have lied to her. I want that noted somewhere, by someone, in whatever ledger the mountains keep of the things I did and didn't do: I could have lied. I could have told her the Door opened for blood, or for a spoken word of power, or for nothing at all but the turning of a stone, and she might have believed me, because hope makes even witch-hunters gullible when the alternative is a sealed pass and forty men eating each other's rations by spring.

But Rafael was watching me. He had his mother's way of watching — no, he didn't; I didn't know his mother; that memory belonged to a boy in a cage wagon whose name the sword still hummed — he had *someone's* way of watching, some face I'd seen or invented, and he was sixteen years old and he had already been bait once, and I would not make him bait twice by letting him walk through a door opened on a lie.

"The speaker gives their name," I said. "Their true one. Whole. Every memory bound to it goes into the blade, and the blade pays it to the Door, and the Door opens while the name lasts." I shrugged, which cost me more than any swordfight ever had. "Then it doesn't last."

Serane was quiet for a long moment. Snow ticked down off the ridge. Somewhere below, a hunter's horse complained about existing.

"That's not a pardon," she said at last. "That's not exile. That's—"

"Yes."

"You'd forget the oath."

"I'd forget everything the oath was *made of*," I said. "Which is better. Which is the only mercy in this whole frozen country, if you think about it. The oath-magic holds me together like nails hold a coffin. Take the coffin away and maybe what's left can stand up."

I turned before she could answer, because I had spent years getting good at walking away from conversations mid-sentence, and walked back to the exiles.

They were a poor-looking army: eleven souls, three of them children, wrapped in every stitch they owned, Maren's map-stone cooling in my fist, the mountain closing around us like a fist around all of us. Old Tam had gone two days ago, up near the saddle, and his name was in my sword now, third from the top, and I could feel him there the way you feel a stone in your boot — not painful, exactly. Just always.

Rafael broke from Serane's side and came to me at a half-run, half-stumble, and grabbed my arm with both hands, and his fingers were ice and his eyes were not.

"No," he said. "No, you said we'd both—"

"I said a lot of things to a boy in a cage wagon. You shouldn't have trusted me then either."

"Vesper." He said my name and the sword hummed against my hip, and I realized with a lurch that it had been humming his name since midnight not because it wanted to speak him but because it was rehearsing. It was learning the shape of the silence it was about to be left in. Swords are honest that way. It's their worst quality. "There has to be another—"

"There isn't. There's a door, and it eats names, and I have one to spare." I crouched so we were eye to eye, which I hate doing, because it puts your face where children can read it. "Listen to me. When it opens — the moment it opens — you take them through. All of them. You don't wait. You don't look back. You especially don't look back at me, because I am going to look extremely memorable doing this and I don't need the competition."

"What happens to you?"

"Something new," I said. "That's the whole point of a door called Anew, boy. Try to keep up."

He was crying. Sixteen-year-old boys cry the way glaciers calve — enormous, sudden, and pretending not to. He wiped his face with his sleeve and stood straight and said, in Thornfell accent, thick as plum jam, the words I will keep however long anything of me lasts:

"You were the best smuggler I ever met."

"I'm the only smuggler you ever met."

"You were the best one anyway."

And that was the eulogy, and it was a good one, and I stood up before it could finish killing me.

* * *

The Door stood in its seam of glass-clear ice, and up close it was worse than far away, because far away it was a miracle and up close it was a *door* — oak gone silver with age, iron hinges green with centuries, standing free in the snow with nothing behind it but more glacier, older than Thornfell, older than the crown, older probably than the concept of countries, which was fitting, because it was about to be used by people fleeing one.

I drew the sword.

It came out singing. Not the low hum anymore — a full voice now, all the names at once, Tam and the courier Brannic from the pass and the dead of the high road and beneath them, deeper, older, the sediment of twenty years of avenged griefs, and beneath *that*, at the bottom of the blade like dregs in a cup, one name I had never let it speak.

Mine.

"Vesper Eastwick," I said aloud, to the Door, to the hunters, to the boy. "Born Vespera Ostwick, in the year the maps burned. Daughter of Thornfell. Sworn sword of a crown that erased her, and glad of it once, and sorry for it forever after. That's the true name. That's the whole of it. Take it."

The Door did not move. Doors are patient. It was waiting for the part that mattered.

I put the flat of the blade against the oak, and I began to speak.

Not words. *Memory.* That's what swordspeech is, underneath — not naming the dead but giving them away, handing the blade everything you kept of them so it can carry what you cannot. I had done it in pieces all my life, one name at a time, small payments against a large debt. This was the whole account, settled at once.

I gave it the salt roads. The frozen passes, the contraband loads, the smell of mule and tallow and the particular blue of dawn over the Thornfells that no painter has ever gotten right because painters weren't paid enough to freeze properly. I gave it Brannic's face, young and stupid and dead, and the grave I dug with my hands in ground too hard for digging.

I gave it Grayford. The crier's voice cracking on the word *speakers*. My neighbor Marta being taken in daylight, her garden trampled, her loom still warm. The taste of the paper I burned — my false papers, my false life — smoke and glue and cowardice, delicious, terrible.

I gave it the cage wagon. The boy inside it, whispering to stones until they bloomed. The blur of spoken steel. The exact weight of the decision I made in the space between one heartbeat and the next, the decision that undid twenty years of oath in a single breath: *not this one. Not him.*

I gave it the oath itself. Oh, I gave it the oath. The oath-hall in the capital, white marble, white candles, the crown's man holding the sword while I knelt and swore on the graves of a country I had helped unmake — because I had, that was mine, that was the worst of it, I had carried the ledgers, I had read the names aloud so they could be struck from the record, I had been the erasure's own pen — and the magic coming down over me like a lid, like snowfall, like mercy, telling me *this is peace, this is forgetting ahead of schedule, this is how you survive what you did.*

I gave it Hollowmere, and the woman who couldn't say where she was born. I gave it Maren, and her economical arithmetic, and her blood-warm stone. I gave it the shepherd's bothy and shared hunger and Rafael teaching me a Thornfell counting rhyme I'd known as a child and lost and found again in a boy's cracked voice.

I gave it Rafael. All of him. The cage wagon and the bothy and the road and the raid and the rhyme and the way he cried like a calving glacier and the way he said *you were the best smuggler I ever met,* and I felt each memory leave me the way blood leaves you — not painfully, at first, then all at once, then everywhere.

The sword drank. The Door began, very slowly, to open.

And the hunters came.

* * *

I heard them before I saw them — boots on snow, forty sets, no longer patient — and I understood: Serane hadn't broken faith. Serane had simply run out of alternatives, the way I had, the way everyone does eventually on this mountain. The Door was opening. The crown could not allow a door. These two facts had been at war for twenty years and only one of them got to win.

"Through!" I shouted, and my voice was already strange to me, thinner, missing rooms. "All of you — THROUGH!"

The exiles moved. The children first, Rafael hauling the little ones by the hand, and the Door swallowed them — oak and hinge and darkness that was somehow warmer than the snow — and behind them the others, and behind them the spears, and Serane's voice cutting the wind: "*Hold! HOLD, you idiots, the girl's still—*"

Still what? Still Vesper? I checked. Reaching for myself was like reaching into a house where the furniture was being carried out room by room. The salt roads: gone. Grayford: going. The oath—

The oath went, and I staggered, because I had forgotten it was load-bearing. Twenty years of magic lifted off me at once and what was underneath was small and cold and furious and *free*, and I laughed, I actually laughed, driving the sword deeper into the opening seam as the Door swung wide enough to show a country on the other side that officially did not exist.

Rafael stopped in the doorway. Of course he did. Of course he looked back.

"*Go!*"

"Come WITH—"

"I don't remember how!" I shouted, and it was almost funny, it was the funniest thing I'd ever said, because it was already true — I was losing the sentence as I said it, losing the reason I'd said it, losing the boy-shaped hole the reason left behind. The sword was molten light in my hands now, paying out my name into the dark syllable by syllable, and the hunters were thirty paces off, and Serane had stopped running, Serane was staring at the open Door with an expression I was too empty to read.

The last of me went into the blade. The last of me was the bothy, and the hunger, and the rhyme.

The Door stood open on a country that wasn't.

And I stood in front of it, holding a sword full of everyone I had ever been, with no idea whose hands these were.

* * *

The first spear took me through the shoulder.

I want to say I didn't scream. I screamed. Whatever I was now, it had nerves and opinions about spears. I went down in the snow and the sword rang against the ice beside me, still glowing, still speaking — my name, my whole name, pouring out of it into the Door, keeping it open, keeping it open, keeping it open — and above me the sky was white and the spears were coming down and through the ringing in my ears I heard Rafael screaming my —

no.

Hearing Rafael screaming someone's name. A name that meant nothing to me. A name the wind was already carrying away.

Good, I thought, with what was left. Good. Let it go. Let all of it go. Let the door eat the whole rotten beautiful history and leave the rest standing.

Boots surrounded me. A spear-point hovered. And through the open Door, growing smaller, a boy's voice tore across the snow, calling a name I couldn't remember into a country I'd never see —

and the sword, faithful to the end, spoke it back.
