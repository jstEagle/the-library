## 19. Through

The name was still leaving her when I understood what it cost.

I don't mean that poetically. I mean that Vesper Eastwick stood in front of a door older than any country, her sword planted point-down in the snow like a grave-marker, and she was speaking — not shouting, speaking, low and steady as a woman reading out debts at a funeral — and every word she said took something away from her face.

I watched it happen. That's the part I can't get past, even now, even on the other side of everything. I stood there and *watched*.

"Vesper—" I started, because you say names to hold people to themselves. It's the first thing they teach gifted children before the crown teaches them better: your name is yours, it's the rope other people can throw you.

She didn't turn around. "Count them through," she said, in a voice that had already forgotten how many winters we'd shared. Or maybe it hadn't yet. Maybe it was mid-forgetting, like snow mid-fall, still shaped like something.

Behind us, down the glacier's spine, the hunters were coming. Torches in the blue dark like a procession of small angry suns. Serane Vole among them, or not among them — I'd dragged her this far, half-conscious, her blood freezing in bright fans across my sleeves, and now she lay propped against an ice ridge ten paces from the Door with her sword across her knees and her orders ready.

"Rafael." Her voice was wrecked but it still had that captain's edge, the one that could shave frost off stone. "You put me down and you walk through. Those are my last orders."

"You're bleeding out."

"I'm *dying*. There's a difference, and the difference is that one of them is tidy." She tried to laugh and it came apart halfway. "I'm a witch-hunter. I burn people like you. Leave me for my own."

And here's the thing nobody tells you about desperate gratitude: it doesn't ask permission. It doesn't weigh anything. Maren Ostwick had died two days ago pressing a broken map-stone into my hands, and the last thing she'd said — the very last — was *nobody crosses alone, boy, that's the whole of Thornfell law.* Not the crown's law. Ours.

I picked Serane Vole up off the ice.

* * *

She fought me, of course. She had maybe a quarter of her strength left and she spent all of it, hammering the heel of her hand against my shoulder, hissing curses in three registers — official, personal, and the quiet third one that was almost pleading.

"You are under arrest," she managed, which would have been funnier if her blood wasn't soaking through my coat into my shirt, warm as a confession.

"You're under arrest," I shot back. "Citizen of a country that doesn't exist. You've been illegally occupying yourself."

"That's not—" She ran out of breath for it. Good. Arguing with Serane was like fencing with a glacier; eventually you just got tired and cold.

Here's what I knew that she didn't, what I'd pieced together from the way Vole's own hunters had looked at her in the raid, from the ledger page I'd seen over a dead man's shoulder, from the way she'd flinched — *flinched*, Captain Vole, who didn't flinch at burning villages — whenever someone spoke too near a flame: Serane Vole was gifted. Had been, all along. The woman who hunted speakers carried the same sickness the crown called sickness, and she'd been holding her own leash so long she'd forgotten it was there.

Vesper knew it too. That's why she'd walked into the camp unarmed. *Enemies recognizing each other's cages*, she'd told me after, and I'd been too scared to ask what she meant until the meaning walked up the mountain behind us with torches.

So no. I wasn't leaving her. Maren's law. Nobody crosses alone. Even if alone was exactly what Serane wanted, demanded, bled for.

The exiles were crossing.

That's the image I'll keep, if I get to keep anything: forty-odd people who had been unmade by decree — their country struck from maps, their language fined out of their mouths, their villages renamed by clerks who'd never seen them — walking through a door standing free in the middle of a glacier. Old Tomas with his bad hip. The Ferrow sisters carrying a child between them like contraband bread. The stonemason Aldric, who hadn't spoken above a whisper since Grayford, saying a word in old Thornfell speech as he passed the threshold, just once, testing whether it worked here, and then saying it louder.

They didn't cheer. That surprised me at first, and then it didn't. You don't cheer when you come home to a place that might be a lie. You go quietly, the way you enter a sickroom. They crossed in ones and twos, and each of them paused at the threshold for half a heartbeat — not fear, exactly. Inventory. Checking what they still had of themselves to carry through.

And behind them all, holding the door open with nothing but a dying name, Vesper.

* * *

The Door itself — I should describe the Door, though words keep sliding off it like snow off slate.

It stood in the ice with no wall around it. A frame of grey stone carved with letters worn to suggestion, a door of black wood that showed no grain, and through its gap not darkness but *weather* — wind, green light, the smell of rain on hot stone, a summer that had no business existing on this side of winter. It didn't glow. It didn't hum. It simply insisted, the way a mountain insists, and you either accepted it or you went mad arguing.

The map-stone lay shattered at its base. Maren's last gift, spent. Whatever else happened tonight, nobody was opening this door again without paying what Vesper was paying.

Because that was the price, and everyone on that ice field understood it by then, even the hunters who'd stopped at fifty paces and wouldn't come closer, superstitious as cats at a shrine. To open the Door of Anew, a speaker gives their true name and every memory bound to it. All of it. Every year. Every scar. The oath, the betrayal, the salt roads, the boy in the cage wagon — gone, traded away, spent like coin to buy a country back.

Vesper's voice was getting thinner. I could hear it fraying like rope over a fire.

"Hold her," I told the Ferrow sisters, and dumped Serane against their shoulders, and ran back to the threshold.

"No," Vesper said. It came out wrong — delayed, like sound arriving late over distance. "Rafael. Don't—"

"You don't get to give me instructions anymore," I said. "You gave up your memories, not mine. Mine still work fine, and they say you stay."

"There's nothing left of me to stay *for*, boy." And oh, that hurt worse than the hunters, worse than the cold, because it wasn't true and she'd have known it was untrue an hour ago, and the only reason she could say it now was that the truth had already been fed to the door. "I don't remember why I climbed this mountain. I remember that I meant to. That's all. That's all that's left."

"Then remember this instead." I grabbed her free hand — the sword-hand stayed wrapped around the hilt, knuckles white, because the sword was the instrument of the speaking and she couldn't let go until the name was done — and I pressed it flat against my chest, over my heart, where she could feel it going like a drum. "Feel that? That's a debt. You pulled me out of a cage. You taught me which berries lie. You sang — badly, *badly*, Vesper, don't argue — you sang on the high road when I couldn't sleep. That's not memory. That's *me*. I'll do the remembering for both of us until you can do it again."

Her eyes were going strange. Not empty — I want to be fair to her, they weren't empty — but the look in them was rearranging, shelves being cleared, a room being swept. She stared at me the way you stare at a stranger who claims to know you, polite and lost and a little frightened.

"Who are you?" she asked.

The question hit like a spear through wool. But I'd had practice being nobody's. I was born bait. I'd been a thing the crown used, a thing the colony feared, a thing the hunters chased. Being unknown to Vesper Eastwick was just one more version of being unknown, and I'd survived all the others.

"Rafael," I said. "Thornfell-born. Speaker. Your boy, if you'll have me. Now finish the job."

* * *

The hunters chose that moment to advance, because hunters always have terrible timing; it comes with the profession.

They came up the ice in a wedge, twenty strong, torches guttering in the katabatic wind, and at their head a lieutenant whose name I never learned and whose expression I'll never forget — the particular hunger of a man who has been promised a promotion and a pardon and smells both close enough to taste.

Serane saw them coming. I saw her see them. She was grey-faced against the Ferrow sisters' shoulders, her wound leaking through improvised bandages, and she looked at her own soldiers advancing and then at the door standing open on summer, and I watched a war happen inside her that made our whole journey look like a border skirmish.

Then she did the bravest thing I saw anyone do in the entire war-that-wasn't-a-war.

She stood up.

On a leg that shouldn't hold her. On pure captain's spite. She planted herself between the advancing wedge and the Door, drew her sword — her *hunter's* sword, the one with the crown's warrant etched on the blade — and raised it not against us but against her own.

"Stand down!" Her voice cracked across the ice like a whip, and twenty trained men and women actually stumbled, because you don't spend years obeying that voice and then forget it in a night. "By order of Hunter-Captain Serane Vole, this operation is concluded. The Door is sealed by crown authority — *my* authority. Any man who takes another step toward it answers to me."

"Lieutenant Harl has the writ," someone called back, uncertain.

"The writ is for capturing speakers." She smiled, and it was a terrible smile, blood-flecked and blazing. "There's no speaker here anymore. Go check. Go on. Read her mind if you can find anything in it." And softer, almost to herself, almost kindly: "There's nothing left to burn."

Harl hesitated. That hesitation bought us everything.

Because Vesper's voice had dropped to a whisper, and the whisper was the end of the name, and I felt the change in the air the way you feel weather turning — pressure, hush, the whole mountain leaning in to listen.

She spoke the final syllable.

And the Door opened wide.

Not wider physically. Wider in every way that mattered. The smell of rain and hot stone rolled out over the ice field, and through the gap I saw — briefly, one heartbeat, I'll spend the rest of my life trying to describe that heartbeat — terraced fields under a sun that couldn't exist at this latitude, a river the color of hammered tin, walls built in a style I knew from dreams I'd never had. Thornfell. Remembered Thornfell, invented Thornfell, the country as forty exiles had kept it alive by telling stories to each other in smugglers' bothies and hollow villages where no one could say where they were born.

It didn't matter that it might be half a lie. Half a lie held by forty pairs of hands is heavier than any whole truth the crown ever wrote.

"Through!" I screamed. "Everyone through, NOW!"

* * *

They went. Of course they went. Forty exiles don't hesitate at an open gate; hesitation is a luxury of people with countries.

Tomas first, then the Ferrow sisters with the child, then the rest in a river of coats and bundles and one man carrying nothing but a fistful of mountain soil he'd scooped up at the glacier's foot because he refused to arrive anywhere empty-handed. The hunters broke rank entirely — some advancing, some standing frozen, one dropping her torch outright — and Serane held the line of herself between the two currents like a dam, screaming warrants and threats and finally just her own name, over and over, *VOLE, VOLE, STAND DOWN IN THE NAME OF VOLE,* spending her authority like a woman burning furniture for warmth.

I was the second-to-last across. I had Vesper under my arm — she weighed almost nothing now, or she'd always weighed nothing and I'd only just noticed; the sword came with us, I pried it from fingers gone slack and slung it across my back, and it whispered against my shoulder blade, and I chose not to listen — and I was dragging her through the gap when I heard Serane stop screaming.

I turned. Bad idea. Never turn around at thresholds; every story agrees on this.

Serane had fallen to one knee. Harl stood over her, and there was no confusion left in his face, only arithmetic: a wounded captain, an open door, a witness to everything. He'd decided. His spear came up.

I don't think. I have thought about it since, endlessly, and I still don't think. I moved. Maren's law, Vesper's stubbornness, forty people's borrowed courage — whatever lived in me that night, it moved me back across the threshold into the falling snow, into the torchlight, into the space between a spear and a dying woman.

The spear never landed. Because Serane caught it.

Caught it — bare-handed, on the shaft below the head, wrenching it aside with the last full measure of a strength she'd been hoarding all night — and said, clearly enough for every hunter on that ice to hear:

"Harl. When we get back, look in the mirror and say your grandmother's maiden name. Out loud. See what happens."

He went white. Everyone went white. Somewhere in that wedge of torches, three separate people lowered their weapons at once, and I understood: she hadn't just confessed her own secret. She'd handed them all a grenade with the pin half-out. A hunter company where anyone might be gifted is a hunter company that stops hunting fast.

"Now," Serane said, to me, to everyone, to the world, "get her through the door."

I got her through the door.

* * *

Crossing felt like stepping into a bath drawn by someone who loved you. Warmth, weight, the smell of rain. Then grass under my boots where snow had been, and I turned to watch the last of it.

Serane came through on her own feet, barely, refusing every offered arm with a snarl that could curdle milk. The Door began to close the instant the last exile cleared the frame — not swinging shut, but *unwriting*, the black wood paling, the grey stone losing its letters, the whole structure simplifying itself like a drawing rubbed away.

Vesper stirred against my shoulder. Looked at the closing door with eyes that held nothing recognizable and everything human.

"Was that important?" she asked. Genuinely asking. My heart cracked clean down the middle, and I laughed, because what else do you do.

"Yeah," I said. "Everything about it was important. I'll tell you all of it. Twice, if you need."

The Door became pale wood, then plain stone, then a shape, then a suggestion, then nothing at all. Where it had stood, a wall of glacier ice, blue and blank and seamless, reflecting the impossible summer sky.

No seam. No handle. No map-stone. No way back.

Behind us, forty exiles stood in remembered grass under an invented sun, and someone — old Tomas, I think — began to sing. Others joined. The song was in the old speech, and it was rough and ragged and out of tune in six different directions, and it was the most beautiful thing I had ever heard, because every voice in it belonged to someone the crown said had never existed.

Serane Vole sat down hard in the grass, pressed both hands over her wound, and stared at the blank ice where her whole world had been.

"Well," she said, to no one, to the ice, to whatever god keeps the books on erased countries. "Now what?"

I had no answer for her. But I had a silent sword on my back, a nameless woman at my side, and a country that existed nowhere else — which, I was beginning to understand, meant it existed everywhere, if we were stubborn enough.

First things first, though. Somebody had to tell the nameless woman who she'd been.

Somebody had to start with her name.
