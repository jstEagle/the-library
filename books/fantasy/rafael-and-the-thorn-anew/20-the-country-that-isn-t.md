## 20. The Country That Isn't

The first thing I saw beyond the Door was a river that ran uphill.

I want to be precise about that, because precision was the last thing the crown ever taught me and I have been unlearning it ever since. The river did not *seem* to run uphill. It did not shimmer or waver like some witch's trick of light. It ran uphill, cold and certain, the way water runs downhill everywhere else in the world, and the fish in it swam with their bellies toward the sky, and none of it seemed to think there was anything remarkable about the arrangement.

"Serane." Rafael's hand on my arm. "You're bleeding."

"I'm aware," I said, because my shoulder had been opened by a spear butt back at the camp and I had decided, somewhere between the Door and here, that pain was a problem for later. Later had apparently arrived.

He sat me down on a stone — a stone, I noted, that was warm despite the snow on every side of us — and bound the wound with strips of my own cloak, which he tore with the brisk incompetence of a boy who had never torn anything but bread. Around us, the exiles of Thornfell were coming through the seam in the glacier wall in ones and twos, blinking, weeping, holding each other's sleeves as if the ground might be revoked.

Behind them, the Door stood open. Not a door in a wall — a door standing free in the snow, older than any masonry I had ever inspected, its frame carved with letters in no alphabet the crown's archivists ever bothered to record. Through it I could still see the pass, the hunters' camp, the ash lanterns guttering in the wind. And through it, faintly, I could hear Vesper Eastwick's name being spoken.

Not spoken aloud. Spoken the way a sword speaks — in the marrow, in the teeth. Her true name, given whole, unraveling syllable by syllable as she spent it to hold the passage wide. I had heard her use her gift before, in the dark of the high road, when her blade whispered the names of dead men she had avenged. This was not that. This was a woman reading herself out loud until there was nothing left to read.

"Hold it open," Rafael breathed, half to himself, half to her, half to whatever gods the crown had failed to burn out of these mountains. "Hold it, hold it—"

Maren Ostwick went through first of the wounded, carried on two smugglers' shoulders, her blood leaving a line across the snow that the snow, I noticed, absorbed without complaint. The children went next. Then the old ones. Then everyone else, in the disorderly order of people who have stopped believing in order.

And then the Door began to close.

It did not slam. It narrowed the way a winter day narrows — slowly, gently, the light going thin and blue at the edges — and through the shrinking gap I watched Vesper Eastwick kneel down in the snow of the world we had left, sword across her knees, mouth still moving around a name that no longer had an owner.

The gap sealed. The ice went blank. Somewhere on the other side of it, a woman finished becoming no one, and I felt the loss of her like a tooth pulled — a strange, specific absence I kept touching with my tongue.

"Well," said one of the smugglers, a squat man named Brannic who had once tried to sell me contraband salt and now looked like he wanted to apologize for it. "That's that."

That was not that. That was barely anything at all.

* * *

We walked for two days through a country that should not have existed.

I want to be clear about what I mean by *should not*. I do not mean it was forbidden. I do not mean it was hidden. I mean that the Kingdom of Cael, whose laws I had enforced for nineteen years, had decreed ninety-one years ago that the nation of Thornfell was erased — struck from maps, struck from records, struck from memory by decree and by whatever quiet magic the crown had purchased to make the decree stick. There were men in Grayford who could not say the word if you paid them. There were archives where the shelves held gaps shaped like missing books. I had burned ledgers myself. I had been proud of it.

And yet here were roads. Here were terraced fields sleeping under snow, walls built by hands that the crown insisted had never existed. Here was a village — an actual village, with a well and a shrine and smoke rising from chimneys — sitting exactly where no village could be, because the land it sat on was, according to every document I had ever signed, empty.

"It's wrong," I said, on the second evening, standing at the edge of that village while the exiles streamed past me into its streets like water finding an old channel. "Look at it. The roofs are slate in the north style and thatch in the south style. The shrine has three gods in it and two of them are facing the wrong way. That bridge—" I pointed at a stone span over the uphill river. "That bridge is Caelish. I've stood on six bridges exactly like it. It doesn't belong here."

"No," agreed Maren Ostwick, propped against a fence post with a bandage over the wound that was killing her slowly and without hurry, because even her dying seemed to operate on Thornfell time. "It doesn't."

"The crown erased this country. Erasure doesn't leave villages."

"Erasure leaves holes." She said it the way you'd correct a child's arithmetic. "And holes get filled. By whoever remembers. By whoever *half*-remembers." She nodded at the village, at the exiles moving through it, touching doorframes, weeping at wells. "Every person who came through that Door carried a piece of Thornfell in their head. A street. A grandmother's song. The way the bread tasted. You put enough pieces together and the pieces start agreeing with each other. Start building." She smiled, and it was not a kind smile; it was the smile of someone watching a debt come home. "This country isn't Thornfell, Captain. It's what we remember of Thornfell. Which means it's Thornfell the way a scar is skin."

I turned that over all night, in a house that one of the exiles insisted had always been hers and which had, until forty-eight hours ago, existed only inside her.

* * *

By the third morning I had catalogued the wrongnesses, because cataloguing is the only prayer I know.

The sun rose in a slightly different place than it should have. I checked. Old habit. The days were a finger's width too long. The mountains on the southern horizon matched the Thornfells exactly except that one of them had two peaks where the maps in my head — the real maps, the pre-erasure maps I'd studied during training, the ones the crown thought it had destroyed — showed only one. The village well gave water that tasted faintly of a summer none of us had lived through. An old man greeted me by a name that was not mine but fit me anyway, the way a coat fits someone of similar build.

And the people. The people were the worst of it, or the best of it, depending on how honest I was prepared to be.

They remembered their country in fragments, and where the fragments didn't meet, they invented, and the inventions were already hardening into fact. Two women argued cheerfully about whether the Thornfell harvest festival involved dancing clockwise or counterclockwise, and by the time I passed them again they had settled it — both directions at once, apparently, in different valleys — and were teaching the children a dance that had never been danced anywhere until that afternoon. A boy asked his grandfather what the Thornfell flag looked like, and the grandfather described a white thorn tree on green, and by evening there were three flags hanging from doorways, each a different green, each defended with total conviction.

"They're making it up," I told Rafael. We were sitting on the warm stone bridge, watching the uphill river do its impossible commute. "All of it. They're inventing a country out of scraps and stubbornness."

"Yes," he said.

"That should horrify me."

"Why?"

"Because law is supposed to be the opposite of invention. Law is what holds when everything made-up falls apart. I spent my whole life enforcing the thing that doesn't bend." I picked up a pebble and threw it at the river, which declined to acknowledge it. "And now I'm standing in a place that is entirely bent. Made of bending. Held up by people agreeing to agree."

Rafael was quiet for a while. He had gotten quieter since the Door closed — carrying something, though he wouldn't say what. I suspected I knew. I suspected it had a silent sword and a woman's gait and was currently walking somewhere in the world we'd left behind, and I did not say so, because there are mercies even a witch-hunter learns.

"You enforce the crown's law," he said finally. "What is it? Say it."

"You know it. Every speaker dies. Every record burns. Thornfell never was."

"Say it anyway."

"*Thornfell never was,*" I said, and the words came out of my mouth and fell straight down like stones with nothing under them. Because I was standing in it. Because it was smoking chimneys and arguing grandmothers and a boy beside me who spoke and made stone bloom. Because the decree was a lie the moment it was written, and I had known it was a lie for years, and I had enforced it anyway, because an oath is a comfortable place to hide from the question of whether you're a good person.

"There," Rafael said softly. "You said it, and look. Nothing happened. The country's still here. Maybe law is just a story too, Serane. Maybe the only difference is who gets to tell it."

I wanted to argue. I want you to understand that about me: I am constitutionally incapable of letting a bad argument stand, and his argument was terrible, glib, the philosophy of a sixteen-year-old who had recently discovered that adults can be wrong. But I stood on a bridge over a river running uphill in a country that officially did not exist, and every counterargument I owned drowned quietly on the way to my tongue.

* * *

On the fifth day, Maren sent for me.

She was dying in a house that smelled of pine resin and woodsmoke, in a bed that three exiles swore had belonged to her family for generations, though each of them described a different family. She looked smaller than she had in the colony, shrunk down to essentials, but her eyes were as level as ever, and they fixed on me the moment I came through the door.

"Witch-hunter," she said. "Sit."

"Captain," I said. "Or Serane. Or nothing. I'm not sure what I am anymore."

"Good. Uncertainty becomes you." She coughed, waved off the woman offering her tea. "I'm going to die soon, and before I do, I need to know what you're going to do. So I'll ask plainly, the way we ask things in a country that may or may not exist. Are you going to enforce your crown's law here?"

I looked at her. Nineteen years of the trade, and no one had ever asked me plainly. Hunters ask questions designed to trap. Priests ask questions designed to shame. Only the condemned, in my experience, ask plain questions, and usually it's *why*, and usually there's no answer worth giving.

"What would enforcement look like?" I asked. "Arrest whom? There's no one to arrest. Everyone here is guilty of existing in a country my king says is imaginary. Do I burn the village? It'll grow back tomorrow out of somebody's memory. Do I execute the witnesses? They're the load-bearing structure, Ostwick. Kill the witnesses and the country comes down with them."

"So you've noticed."

"I've noticed that the law assumes a world that obeys it. This world doesn't. Your country exists nowhere else — not on maps, not in archives, not in the king's own head, I'd wager. The decree says Thornfell never was. But a decree is just words, and words need somewhere to stand." I heard myself and almost laughed. "Vesper used to say that. About oaths. Words need somewhere to stand, and if you take the ground away, the words fall."

Something moved behind Maren's eyes — recognition, maybe, or grief. "Is she alive?"

"I don't know. She gave her name away. All of it. What's left of a person after that, I couldn't tell you. I'm not sure she could either." I looked at my hands. Hunter's hands. Ledger-keeper's hands. "She walked into the camp unarmed to bargain for Rafael. For a boy she'd known a month. She broke a crown oath that would have dragged her soul to the capital in chains rather than let him hang. And then she paid the rest. So whatever I decide about your country, Ostwick, I decided it on a glacier, watching her pay for it."

Maren was silent a long moment. Outside, someone was teaching children the two-directional harvest dance, badly, with much laughter.

"When the crown erased us," she said at last, "they didn't just take our country. They took our right to be remembered wrongly. Understand? A living country argues with itself. It gets things wrong and fixes them. Ours was frozen — a perfect Thornfell in the archives, dead and correct. What those people out there are doing—" she tipped her chin toward the laughter, "—is the first wrong thing anyone has done to Thornfell in ninety-one years. It's the most alive it's ever been." Her breath caught; she rode it out. "So. Plainly, hunter. The law or the country."

And here is the thing I have not told anyone, the thing I will perhaps tell Rafael someday, or perhaps carry to my grave the way Vesper carried other people's names: I am gifted.

Not the way she was. Nothing so beautiful as a speaking blade. When I touch a written lie, it itches. That's all. A little crawl along my fingertips, like ants under paper. I discovered it at fifteen, hiding it at fifteen, and I have spent thirty-four years touching documents that made my fingers crawl and signing my name beneath them anyway. The decree of erasure — I read the original once, in the deep archive, gloved, sweating. My hands screamed for a week.

Every warrant I ever served. Every order I ever obeyed. Every page of the hunt. Ants under paper, all of it, and I signed anyway, because the alternative was becoming the thing I hunted.

"Neither," I said.

Maren raised an eyebrow.

"Not the law. Not the country. Both. I'm going to stay, and I'm going to do the one thing I'm actually qualified to do, which is keep records. Honest ones. A ledger of what this country actually is — including every mistake, every invented festival, every flag in the wrong shade of green. Not the crown's version. Not the exiles' sentimental version. The true, messy, changing version, written down while it happens, so that ninety-one years from now nobody freezes you into perfection again." I leaned forward. "You want to live? Live inaccurately. And let someone write it down."

Maren stared at me. Then she laughed — a dry, cracking sound, like ice shifting on a spring river — and it turned into coughing, and the coughing took a long time to leave her.

"A witch-hunter," she wheezed, "proposing to be our historian."

"A liar proposing to keep honest books. Stranger things have happened this month. I watched a river run uphill."

She reached under her pillow and pressed something into my hand: a broken map-stone, cracked clean through, the twin of the piece Vesper must have used at the Door. Dead now, cold, but heavy the way promises are heavy.

"For your ledger," she said. "First entry. *Here begins the country that isn't.*"

* * *

I stayed with her until near the end, and then I went out and stood on the bridge in the blue hour, and that was when I saw the figure on the far road.

Small, at this distance. Walking with a limp that favored the left side, wrapped in a cloak the color of wet slate, leading no horse, carrying a sword slung across her back the way a widow wears a wedding ring — worn, habitual, meaningless now. The snow came down between us, unhurried, and the figure came on through it at the pace of someone who has nowhere to be and all the time in the world to be nowhere.

I knew the walk. You spend enough years hunting people, you learn that faces lie and gaits don't. I had tracked that walk across three provinces. I had heard it stop outside my tent at the camp of ash lanterns. I had watched its owner kneel in the snow and speak herself empty.

But the woman on the road walked like someone who had never been hunted in her life. Like someone who had never been anything.

"Rafael," I called, without turning. He was already there — I'd felt him come onto the bridge, felt the stone under us go faintly warm the way it does when his gift stirs. "Someone's coming."

He looked. I watched his face do several things in rapid succession, none of them orderly.

"She can't remember," he said. "Can she? How much can a person give away and still—"

"I don't know," I said. "I keep records, boy. I don't keep miracles."

The figure reached the foot of the bridge and stopped, looking up at the two of us silhouetted against the village lights, and even at that distance I could see her head tilt — the small, wary, curious tilt of a stranger meeting strangers — and I heard myself say, in a voice I did not recognize as mine:

"Welcome to Thornfell. It isn't, you know. But it's here."

And the woman at the foot of the bridge opened her mouth to answer.
