## 22. Thorn Anew

The first thing I learned in the country that wasn't was how to carry water.

Not the romance of it — no ballads about buckets, as far as I know — just the plain mechanics of it: two pails, a yoke across the shoulders, the long walk down to the stream where the ice had been broken and refrozen so many times it looked like a scar someone kept picking at. The path down was steep. The path back up was steeper. By the third morning my shoulders knew the shape of the yoke better than they knew anything else about me, which, given that I didn't know anything else about me, felt fair.

My name — the name I'd chosen, the one Rafael gave me like a gift he'd wrapped badly because his hands were shaking — is *Wren*.

I'm told I picked well. A wren is a small bird. It sings too loudly for its size. It survives winters by tucking itself into stone walls and refusing, on principle, to die.

That sounds about right, from what people tell me of the woman I used to be.

* * *

They tell me stories, the exiles. That's how I learn myself.

Rafael tells me most of them, usually at the hearth in the long house, usually when he thinks I've stopped listening. He's sixteen now by their counting, seventeen by mine if you round generously, and he has grown half a head taller since the pass sealed behind us, which seems like a personal insult arranged specifically for my benefit. He talks with his hands. He always did, apparently — Maren Ostwick used to say he'd gesticulate his own shadow off its hinges someday.

He tells me about a woman who walked out of the snow carrying a silent sword, who couldn't remember her own mother's face but could still, without thinking, put a knife through the knot of a rope at twenty paces. He tells it like a story about someone else, and then he stops telling it, and looks at me, and I understand that it isn't about someone else at all.

"You don't have to keep doing this," I said to him once, early on, when the hearing-things-about-myself still sat strange in my chest like a swallowed stone. "I'm not going anywhere."

"I know," he said. "That's why I do it. You should know where you came from. Even if you can't go back there."

"Can't or won't?"

He considered this with the seriousness he brought to everything, the boy who'd made stone bloom with a whisper and never once seemed surprised by his own miracles. "Both," he decided. "Same thing here, mostly."

He was right. In Thornfell-as-remembered, the past isn't a country you visit. It's the ground under your feet. The whole settlement sits on top of memory the way a house sits on a cellar — everything anyone recalls clearly, or half-recalls, or invented outright around a fire one desperate winter, is load-bearing. There's a hill east of the palisade that exists only because an old weaver named Tam remembered it existing, and when Tam died two months after the crossing, the hill stayed, stubborn as grief. Nobody knows what's under it. Nobody digs.

I asked once what would happen if everyone who remembered a thing died before teaching it.

Rafael looked at me for a long moment. "Then it goes," he said. "So we teach fast."

* * *

Winter here is not the winter I crossed.

I know this the way you know the difference between a dog and a wolf — not by study, but by the hair on your arms. The winter that sealed the Thornfell passes wanted us dead. This one merely wants us patient. It snows in fat, unhurried flakes that pile up on the eaves like the country itself is tucking us in. The children sled on the remembering-hill. Someone taught them a song with words nobody fully agrees on, so they sing all the versions at once, and somehow it works.

I learned the seasons the way I learned the water path: by body, before mind. My hands knew frost before I knew its name. When the light went thin and gold in the afternoons, something in my chest counted days and reported: *six weeks to thaw, give or take*. I don't know whose counting that is. Maybe the woman I was. Maybe just a body that spent enough years outdoors to develop opinions.

The kindnesses are what undo me, though. Not the dramatic ones. The small ones.

Old Sefa, who runs the smokehouse, leaves fish on my doorstep without comment, every fourth day, and pretends she doesn't see me see it. The twins — Ilse and Ilka, born on the road, named after a river that may or may not exist — follow me around the settlement like small ducklings with opinions about sword maintenance, which they have, loudly, despite knowing nothing about swords. And Rafael's mother, Brenna, who crossed with the second group through the Door before it sealed, took one look at me at the hearth and started mending the sleeve of my coat without asking, the way you'd mend a coat belonging to family.

"Why?" I asked her, the first time.

She didn't look up from her needle. "Because it needs mending," she said. "Why does anyone do anything? Eat your soup."

I have decided, provisionally, that Brenna Ostwick may be the wisest person alive.

* * *

The sword stays silent.

I keep it wrapped in oilcloth under my bed, and every so often — maybe once a week, maybe less — I unwrap it and hold it and wait. Nothing. No names. No whispers. The blade that remembered the dead it avenged remembers nothing now, or perhaps it remembers only me, and there's nothing left of me to say.

At first this felt like deafness. Then it felt like mercy. Now it feels like potential, which is a word I learned from Rafael and immediately resented.

"It's not empty," he told me, sitting cross-legged on my floor one evening, uninvited, in the manner of boys who grew up with no walls between houses. "It's listening. There's a difference."

"You can't know that."

"No," he agreed cheerfully. "But I've been wrong about exactly nothing important lately, so I'd listen to me if I were you."

Arrogance, in him, is not a flaw. It's a survival strategy. The boy was bait once — carried in a cage wagon down roads that didn't exist, meant to draw out survivors of a murdered country — and instead of breaking, he decided the world owed him an apology and started collecting. I admire it enormously. I'm told the woman I used to be admired it too, eventually, after some rocky beginnings involving a betrayal and a saddle and a camp full of ash lanterns.

I don't remember any of it. But when Rafael describes the moment she chose to follow him instead of the Door — *she looked at me like I was worth more than her own name, and then she proved it* — something in my chest goes warm and certain, and I think: yes. Whatever else I lost, I wouldn't have lost that choice. Some things live below memory, in the marrow.

* * *

Spring comes the way debts come due: gradually, then all at once.

Thaw water roars in the streams. The remembering-hill grows actual grass, which feels like showing off. And on a bright cold morning in what the exiles call First Green — a holiday, apparently, though its origins are split three ways among three incompatible memories, so the celebration is mostly an excuse for honey bread — Rafael finds me on the palisade wall, looking south.

The south is where the mountains fold down toward the pass that sealed. Somewhere beyond it is a crown, a hunt, a hunter-captain who walked through a door against her own orders because a boy dragged her. Serane Vole lives in the settlement now, in a house at the far edge, alone, growing vegetables with the grim competence of a woman punishing the soil personally. We don't speak often. But last week she nodded to me across the market square — a real nod, level, one soldier to another — and I found myself nodding back, and neither of us fell over, so progress is possible even for the irreparably armed.

"They're talking about sending word south," Rafael says, climbing up beside me. "Through the low passes, once they open. Finding out what happened out there. Whether anyone followed."

"And?"

"And I want to be the one who goes." He's watching my face carefully. He's gotten good at that — reading people, reading me especially, though I'm told I was always easy to read, which seems like slander against a woman whose entire profession was secrets. "Not to fight. To talk. Serane says the hunt's over — the crown got what it feared, a door that closed. She thinks—" He stops. Starts again. "She thinks they might just let us go. If someone gives them a reason to."

"That's either very wise or very naive."

"Serane said the same thing. Word for word. You two should start a club."

I look at him — this tall, serious, impossible boy who was a stranger in a cage wagon and became the fixed point around which a woman surrendered her entire self — and I feel the thing I feel most reliably these days, which isn't memory at all. It's orientation. Like a compass needle. Whatever I was made of, it points at him.

"When do you leave?"

"When the low pass opens. Few weeks." He grins suddenly, and for a moment he's eight years old again, making stones bloom. "Come with me."

I laugh — actually laugh, out loud, which still surprises me when it happens; joy keeps sneaking up on me like a thief with poor intentions. "You need a guard?"

"I need you," he says simply. "The rest is details. Besides—" he nods south, at the white spine of the world, "—someone should be there who remembers how to survive out there. Even if she doesn't remember anything else."

* * *

The night before First Green ends, they hold the oath-taking.

It's not a ceremony with much architecture. Maren Ostwick designed it, in the early months, from fragments: a fire, witnesses, your hand on whatever matters most to you. For farmers it's soil. For the smith it's his hammer. For Sefa it's, inexplicably, a smoked fish, and nobody questions it, because Sefa's fish oaths are legendary.

Mostly it's for the children born here, or the young ones old enough to choose. You swear to the country. Not the old Thornfell — the erased one, the mapless one — but this one, the remembered one, the one sustained daily like a fire that must be fed or dies. *I swear to Thorn Anew*, they say. *To its keeping and its keeping of me.*

I stand at the edge of the firelight with the crowd, holding my wrapped sword, watching Rafael swear with his hand flat on a flowering stone he grew himself, and I don't plan what happens next. It happens the way the thaw happens. Gradually, then all at once.

I walk to the fire.

The crowd hushes. They know what I am, more or less — the woman who paid for their crossing, the speaker who emptied herself out like a cup. They've been very careful around me all winter, careful the way you're careful around a wound. And now here I am, walking into the middle of it, unwrapping oilcloth from a blade that hasn't spoken since before the Door.

Brenna catches my eye and doesn't try to stop me. She just nods, once, the way she nodded over the coat sleeve. *Because it needs doing.*

I kneel at the fire's edge and lay the sword across my palms.

"I don't remember my oath," I say, and my voice comes out steadier than I expected. "The old one. I'm told I swore it to a crown that burned a country and hunted its children, and I'm told I broke it, and I'm told breaking it should have unmade me." I look up at the faces in the firelight — Sefa, the twins perched on their father's fence rail, Serane standing apart with her arms crossed and her eyes suspiciously bright, Brenna, and Rafael, frozen halfway between the fire and me, his oath-stone still glowing faintly in his other hand. "Good riddance, honestly."

Someone laughs. The tension cracks like river ice.

"So I'll swear a new one. My own. Chosen, not assigned." I turn the blade so it catches the firelight. It's quiet in my hands. It has been quiet for a season, and I have finally stopped grieving that, because Rafael was right — it isn't empty. It's listening. It has been listening all along, to the water paths and the frost-counting and the warmth in my chest whenever this boy walks into a room.

"I swear to Thorn Anew," I say. "To its keeping, and its keeping of me. I swear to Rafael Eastwick—" his breath catches; he didn't know I'd use his name aloud, didn't know I knew it as mine-to-say, "—to his road and his return. I swear to the names we teach fast, so nothing goes. And I swear that what I lost, I will not mourn forever — I will build something in the space it left, and I will live in it."

The fire pops. Snow slides off a roof somewhere, soft as a held breath released.

And the sword speaks.

One word. Only one — it has only one to give, and I only need one. The sound isn't a voice exactly; it's more like the feeling of a name spoken inside your own bones, the way you'd say it to yourself in the dark, if yourself were kind.

"*Wren.*"

Just that. My name. The one I chose. Not the one I was born with, not the one an oath owned, not the one I fed to a door in a glacier wall. The one a boy gave me shaking-handed in the snow, the one a small loud bird wears like armor, the one I built with my own two hands out of bucket-yokes and mended sleeves and honey bread and a nod across a market square.

The crowd exhales all at once. Rafael crosses the space between us in four strides and pulls me up off my knees and hugs me hard enough to creak ribs, and the twins abandon their fence to join, and then Sefa, and then apparently everyone, because Thornfell has never met a hug it wouldn't make communal.

Over Rafael's shoulder, I see Serane turn away and walk toward her vegetable patch, and I catch — I'm almost sure I catch — her wiping her face with the heel of her hand, furious about it, before the dark takes her.

* * *

We leave when the low pass opens, three weeks later: Rafael, me, two scouts, and a satchel of letters written by every hand in the settlement, addressed to a crown that erased us, saying simply: *we are here, we were always here, we are not asking permission anymore.*

On the morning we go, Brenna presses a parcel of food into my hands and mends a strap on my pack I hadn't noticed was fraying, and says only, "Eat your soup out there. Somehow."

The pass is open and the air smells like running water and green things deciding to happen. The sword rides at my hip, silent and listening, and when Rafael looks back once at the settlement — small and smoky and stubborn in its valley — I don't look back at all, because I already know every stone of it by heart, and hearts, I've learned, are where I keep my memories now.

"Ready?" he asks.

"Wren's are born ready," I say. "It's in the name. Probably. I'm still learning the traditions."

He laughs, and we climb, and the country that isn't falls away behind us into the country that's about to find out we exist —

— and ahead, far south, faint on the wind, something answers: not a voice, not yet, but the sword humming once against my hip, like a bird testing its throat before spring.
