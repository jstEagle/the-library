## 1. The Valley Where Magic Cannot Follow

The trouble with talking to the dead, Jonah Whitlock had long ago decided, was not that they talked back. It was that they never learned to let a man finish his tea.

"Mind the grain," said Marta Halloway, who had been dead for eleven years and had strong opinions about everything, including — especially — the way he held his knife. "You're cutting against it again. You always cut against it. You cut against it at my funeral carving too, and I said nothing then because I was busy being dead, but I have had time to reflect."

"You were dead when I carved your memorial," Jonah said mildly. "That was rather the point of you."

"And still I could see your hands." A pause, the particular pause Marta used when she was settling in for a lecture. "The left one is doing fine. The right one is an embarrassment to this valley."

Jonah set down his knife, flexed his right hand theatrically, and took a long sip of tea while she fumed. The bone on his workbench — a shinbone from a cow bought honest at the Harrow market, boiled clean and pale as winter moonlight — waited under his fingers with the patient warmth all bones had before they were finished. Not yet awake. Not yet *occupied*.

He picked up the knife again and cut with the grain, just to spite her.

"There," Marta said, mollified. "Was that so hard?"

It was, in truth, very little trouble at all. That was the secret nobody outside the valley would ever believe: the dead were easy company. They wanted what everyone wanted — to be heard, to be useful, to complain about the weather. And Jonah Whitlock, bone-carver of Hollowmere Vale, third of that name and first of his line to be any good at it, gave them all three.

* * *

He had been six years old the first time he heard a bone speak. His grandfather's workshop, a cold morning, a box of failed carvings destined for the lime pit — and one of them, a small rough bird his grandfather had abandoned mid-wing, had whispered, clear as a bell over water: *it hurts, it hurts, oh, it's nearly done.*

Not the bird. The woman whose finger-bone it had been, two hundred years gone, her last words soaked into the marrow like ink into paper. Every bone kept something, Jonah had learned since. Most kept nothing but silence and mineral. But some — the ones from people who had died with words caught in their teeth, unfinished business or unfinished love — those bones remembered. And when he carved them, shaped them, gave the memory a form to live in, the voice came clear.

His grandfather had heard nothing. His mother had heard nothing. In all the history of the vale, only Jonah heard them, which was why the living of Hollowmere kept a certain polite distance from him, the way you keep distance from a well that echoes back more than your own voice.

He didn't blame them. He'd have kept his distance too, if he could. It was difficult to gossip with a man who might, at any moment, turn to the fence post beside him and say, *hush, Edith, I'm listening.*

So he talked to the dead instead, and the dead, being dead, had nowhere better to be.

* * *

The workshop smelled of beeswax and boiled bone and the woodsmoke that seeped in through every gap in the shutters no matter how many times he mended them. Outside, autumn was doing its usual slow gold work on the birches along the stream, and the light came through the window in long amber bars that crawled across the floor as the afternoon aged. Jonah worked through them one by one, the way he did most days, and if you had asked him — though nobody asked him much of anything — whether he was content, he would have thought about it honestly, the way he thought about everything, and eventually said yes.

Mostly yes. Yes with a stone in it, the way a plum has a stone.

Because here was the thing about the valley, the thing that made Hollowmere Vale different from every other place Jonah had ever walked to the edge of and turned back from: magic could not follow you in.

He hadn't known that was unusual until he was fourteen, when a peddler woman had come up the pass selling charms against toothache and unfaithful husbands, and had stood in the middle of the market square growing paler and paler as her wares sat inert in their box like dried beans. She'd tried three times to light a luck-flame off her own palm, the way travelers did for children, and got nothing but singed fingers and a look of genuine fear.

"No spell takes root here," she'd told him at the edge of town, packing up fast, eyes darting toward the high shoulders of the valley walls as if they might lean in and listen. "Don't you feel it? Like walking into a house where all the fires are out. Nothing grows here, boy. Nothing *other*. Whatever keeps this valley, it wants the world plain."

Plain. That was the word she'd used, and it had stuck with him the way a splinter sticks, working deeper whenever he thought about it. Because the world outside the pass was not plain. He'd been out exactly twice in his life — once to his uncle's funeral in Lowmark, once on a foolish youthful errand he didn't discuss — and both times the air beyond the valley mouth had felt thick to him, humming, crowded. Travelers spoke of hedge-witches and weather-singers, of blessings that stuck to doorposts and curses that stuck to bloodlines, of kings who kept court wizards the way other kings kept falcons. None of it reached Hollowmere. Letters arrived; roads stayed muddy; babies were born and old men died, and that was the whole of the miracle.

Which meant, Jonah supposed, that he himself was the strangest thing in the valley by a comfortable margin. A man who heard the dead, in the one place where nothing else unnatural could live. He'd wondered sometimes — in the long blue hour after the candles went down — whether the valley's silence was why he could hear them. Whether some quiet so total made room for voices that would otherwise be drowned. Or whether he'd simply been born wrong-shaped for the world, and the valley was the only place wrong-shaped things fit.

Marta, when he'd asked her once, had snorted.

"I'm dead, boy, not omniscient. Ask the bones."

"The bones only know last words. Nobody's last words have ever been *why am I like this*."

"Give it time," Marta said. "You're not young."

* * *

The commission on his bench was for the Tuller family, whose grandmother had died in the spring at ninety-one, and who wanted — as half the valley wanted, these days — a memorial piece for the mantel. Not a nameboard. Something finer. Jonah had chosen the shinbone precisely because it was long and clean, and he was carving it into a wren, because Grandmother Tuller had kept wrens the way other women kept cats, and because the voice inside it deserved something quick and bright to live in.

She was already there, faintly, the way a fire is in a pile of kindling before it's lit. He could feel her under the surface of the bone, a pressure of almost-words, waiting for the last cut that would wake her.

"Nearly ready?" Marta asked. She hovered, the way she did, though hovering was a figure of speech — she lived in the small carved thrush on his shelf, dead these eleven years, and her presence was less a location than a quality of attention.

"When it's done."

"She'll want to talk. They always want to talk, the fresh ones. You'll get no work done for a month."

"I know."

"You'll spoil her."

"I know, Marta."

"You spoiled me."

"You were already spoiled. I merely provided an audience."

There was a silence from the shelf that he had learned to read as grudging laughter. He smiled down at the wren-in-progress, its wing half-opened under his knife, and for a moment — a good moment, one of the good ones — the workshop felt exactly full enough. One living man, two dead women, a pot of tea going lukewarm, and the whole golden afternoon to fill with careful cuts.

Then the light changed.

It wasn't dramatic. Light rarely is, when it matters. But Jonah had spent thirty years reading this valley the way farmers read clouds, and he knew the color of its autumns — honeyed, forgiving, reluctant to let go — and the light coming through the shutters just then was none of those things. It was white. Hard-edged. The light of a season that had arrived without knocking.

He set down the knife and went to the door.

* * *

The frost had come in the night, weeks early, and come like an army.

Every leaf along the stream stood rimed in silver, stiff as tin. The mud in the lane had frozen into ridges sharp enough to turn an ankle. Out past the fences, the Tullers' turnip field wore a crust of white that crunched audibly when the wind moved it, and the wind itself had a new voice — a thin, dry, whistling note that Jonah had heard perhaps four times in his life, always in deep midwinter, always for a day or two before the snows closed the pass.

Hollowmere's winters were long but gentle, tucked between hills that broke the worst weather. This was not gentle. This was the kind of cold that came down off the high country and did not ask permission.

Old Tam Bracken was standing in the middle of the lane in his shirtsleeves, staring at his frozen garden patch with the expression of a man who has been personally insulted by God.

"Weeks early," he said, without turning around. He'd known Jonah was there; everyone always knew when Jonah was there, though nobody liked to say how. "Weeks and weeks. My grandfather kept the almanac forty years and never saw frost before Allhallows. Never once."

"It's a hard year," Jonah offered.

"It's a *wrong* year." Tam turned, and his face in the white light looked older than it had at the harvest festival a fortnight past, when it had looked merely ancient. "My knees knew before the sky did. Ached for three days running, like they do before a storm, except there's no storm. Just this." He gestured at the field, at the silvered world, at the thin whistling wind. "You carve for the dead, Whitlock. Do the dead say anything about weather?"

"They complain about it," Jonah said. "Endlessly. But mostly about the weather of their own funerals. Rain, usually. Nobody ever seems to die in good weather."

Tam laughed, but shortly, without his heart in it, and shuffled back inside. Jonah stood in the lane a while longer, hands in his pockets, breath steaming, and listened — really listened, the way he did when something itched at the edge of his attention.

The valley was quiet. It was always quiet, magically speaking — that dead-hearth hush the peddler had named, the plainness, the absence. But this morning the quiet had a texture to it. A held-breath quality. As if the valley itself had noticed the frost and drawn in, the way a rabbit draws in when the hawk's shadow crosses the grass.

From the shelf behind him, faintly, Marta said: "Boy. Come inside. It's cold, and you're standing in the lane talking to Tam Bracken, who thinks you're strange, which you are, and I say that with love."

He went inside. He banked the fire. He picked up the knife and made three more cuts on the wren's wing, each one precise, each one a small act of faith that the world was still the world.

But that night, lying in bed with the wind worrying the shutters like a dog at a bone, he heard the dead of the valley murmuring among themselves — all of them at once, the whole low chorus of Hollowmere's departed that lived in his mantel carvings and his shop stock and the little graveyard pieces he'd made over thirty years. They weren't talking to him. They were talking to each other, in the wordless way the dead had, a sound like wind through wheat.

And underneath it, threaded through it, one phrase rising and falling, from voice to voice to voice, like a wave passing through a crowd:

*Cold coming. Cold coming. Cold such as we have not seen. Cold coming.*

He lay awake until the small hours, telling himself it was only the dead being dramatic, which they were, constantly, being dead and bored.

Outside, the first flakes began to fall — fat, silent, early — and somewhere far up the pass, beyond the valley's keeping, something that the frost had announced began, patiently, to make its way down.

* * *

In the morning the snow lay a hand deep and still falling, and Jonah, wrapped in his coat, went out to fetch wood and found the tracks.

They came down out of the trees above the stream — small, barefoot, child-sized prints, pressed crisp and perfect into the snow. They crossed the frozen garden, passed within arm's reach of his woodpile, and stopped at his door.

Whoever had made them had stood there a long while. Long enough for the snow to drift around two small feet and fill the prints halfway, softening their edges, so that they looked less like footprints than like two small graves.

And then, Jonah saw, turning slowly, heart beginning a slow heavy knock against his ribs — the tracks did not lead away.

They led to his door. And stopped.

He looked at the blank snow, and the blank snow looked back, and from the shelf inside the house, faint and urgent, Marta's voice came through the shutters:

"Boy. Boy, get away from the door. Get away from the—"

A knock. Small. Polite. Patient as winter.

Three raps, in the language of someone who has knocked on many doors and been turned away from all of them, and who has learned that the only thing to do is keep knocking anyway.
