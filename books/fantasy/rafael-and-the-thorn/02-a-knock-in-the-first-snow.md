## 2. A Knock in the First Snow

The first snow of the season came three weeks early, which the dead had been predicting since mid-autumn and Jonah had been politely ignoring, because the dead were always predicting things and half the time they were just lonely and wanted him to fuss.

"Hard frost," said the sparrow bone, which had belonged to a woman named Maren who had died, at a great old age, in the middle of a sentence about her sister's terrible taste in curtains. "Hard frost, hard frost, before the moon fills."

"You said that about the barley harvest," Jonah told it, turning it over in his palm. The sparrow bone was small and yellowed and fit in the crook of his thumb, and Maren's voice lived in it like a bee in a bottle — a faint, warm buzzing of last words, worn nearly smooth by two hundred years of repetition. "And the barley came in fine."

"The barley came in *late*, you great lump, which is not the same as fine—"

And then, the way it always did, the voice ran out. That was the nature of the things he carved. A last word was a last word. Maren had perhaps four sentences total, and Jonah knew them the way he knew his own hands, and he loved her the way you love a song you cannot stop hearing.

He set the sparrow bone back on its shelf among the others — forty-one of them now, arranged not by age or by animal but by *temperament*, because the dead had temperaments the way weather did. There was the ox bone that only ever said *hold, hold, hold* in a voice like a landslide deciding to be patient. There was the heron bone, which wept. There was the small, chipped rabbit bone that belonged to a boy of six who had drowned in the Millbrook two centuries back and whose last word was, heartbreakingly and eternally, *again* — because he'd been asking his brother to throw the stone for skipping one more time when the ice went out from under him.

Jonah talked to them while he worked. He'd stopped being embarrassed about it somewhere around the age of twelve, when he'd realized the alternative was talking to the living, and the living looked at him the way folk look at a door they're not sure is a door.

It wasn't that the valley was unkind. The valley was very kind, in its flat-footed, turnip-honest way. The trouble was that the valley had a peculiar quality — Jonah had never found a better word for it than *quietness* — in which nothing strange could take root. No charms held here. No blessings stuck. A hedge-witch had once passed through and tried to cure his neighbor Tam's wart with a rhyme and a copper coin, and the rhyme had simply fallen out of the air like a bird with a broken wing, and Tam still had the wart, and the hedge-witch had left looking personally offended by the landscape. Jonah's own gift, whatever it was, seemed to be the one exception — the one seed that had already rooted before the valley's quietness could reach it, or perhaps rooted *because* of it, like a mushroom that grows only in the dark.

So he carved bones, and the bones whispered, and he listened, and the people of the valley bought his buttons and his knife-handles and his fishing-lure weights and did not ask why he sometimes paused mid-conversation to tilt his head, as if hearing something under the floor.

The snow began at dusk. It came down fat and windless, the kind of snow that seems polite at first and then, by morning, has moved into the whole house.

Jonah banked the fire, put the kettle on, and was reaching for his supper — bread, hard cheese, an apple with a bruise he'd been avoiding emotionally all day — when someone knocked at his door.

Three small knocks. The sort of knocks that come from a hand the size of a hand you'd find on a child.

He stood very still. His cottage sat at the valley's far end, a twenty-minute walk from the nearest farm, and nobody walked twenty minutes through the first snow of the year after dark. Nobody walked it in *good* weather, much. He was not feared, exactly, but he was not sought out either, except by mothers with sick children, and even then they sent the fathers, and the fathers stood on the doorstep as if the doorstep were a border.

The knock came again. Three small knocks, patient, unafraid.

Jonah opened the door.

The child on his step was six years old, or seven — the age where it's hard to say, because some children are old the way rivers are old, with something in the eyes that predates the face. She was small and thin and dark-haired, with hair cut short in a way that suggested scissors and urgency rather than style. She wore a dress of a fabric Jonah had never seen: not wool, not linen, not silk, but something with a faint sheen to it, the color of the sky ten minutes after sunset, and it was *dry*. The snow around her feet was trampled and deep. Her feet were bare.

Bare. In the snow. And she stood on the step with her weight easy on both legs, the way you'd stand on a warm kitchen floor, looking up at him with an expression of polite inquiry, as if *he* were the one who had turned up uninvited at *her* door in the middle of a snowfall.

"Hello," Jonah said.

The child said something back.

It was not a language Jonah knew. It was not a language Jonah had ever heard, and this unsettled him more than the bare feet, because Jonah had spent his whole life listening — to the dead, to the valley, to the particular creak of his own roof beams — and he knew every tongue that had ever washed through this part of the world, the low-country speech and the high-road speech and the river-folk cant and the three words of mountain dialect old Widow Ashe still used for cursing. This was none of them. It was older. That was the only way he could describe it, and he knew even as he thought it that *older* was the wrong direction — it wasn't that the language sounded like something from far back in time. It sounded like something from before time had gotten around to being spoken in. Every syllable landed with a weight, like a stone set carefully on a cairn.

The child stopped, considered him, and tried again — slower, with a small gesture, one hand pressed flat to her chest.

"Nell," she said.

That word he understood. A name. He crouched down to her level, which was a thing he did with animals and the dead and now, apparently, with impossible children. "Nell," he repeated. "I'm Jonah. Jonah Whitlock."

She studied his face with a thoroughness that would have been unnerving in an adult and was merely astonishing in someone whose head came up to his belt buckle. Then she looked past him, into the cottage, and her whole body changed. The polite inquiry dropped away and something else came up underneath it — a kind of *hunger*, not for food, or not only for food. She pointed past his shoulder.

Jonah turned and saw his own front room as she must be seeing it: the fire, the kettle beginning its small complaint, the shelves. Forty-one bones on the shelves, each in its place, each whispering its few worn words in a chorus only he could hear. *Hard frost. Hold, hold. Again. Again. Again.*

The child walked past him into the house as if the house had been expecting her, which, Jonah thought with a lurch, perhaps it had. He shut the door against the snow because his hands did it without consulting him.

She went to the shelves.

"No — careful, those are—" He didn't finish, because there was no good way to end that sentence. *Those are the dead* was not a thing you said to a child. *Those are merchandise* was a lie. He settled for hovering behind her with his hands half-raised, the universal posture of the helpless.

She stopped in front of the rabbit bone. The boy's bone. The one that said *again*.

She reached out and touched it with one finger.

The silence hit Jonah like cold water to the face.

He had never in his life heard that shelf be quiet. Even the quiet bones — the ones whose voices had worn down to almost nothing — even those made a sound, a kind of hush-with-texture, like snow falling on snow. The rabbit bone had said *again* every waking hour of Jonah's thirty-one years, and in his nightmares too, and there had been nights, in his worst griefs, when he'd held it to his ear like a shell and let the small drowned boy's voice be the only sound in the world.

It was silent. Not worn-out silent. *Held* silent — the way a room goes quiet when someone important walks into it.

Then the bone began to weep.

There was no other word for it. The silence broke, and what came through was not the boy's cheerful, endless *again* — it was the sound a child makes when someone finally, finally picks him up. A sound Jonah had never heard from any of his carvings in all his years of listening. The dead said their last words and nothing else; that was the whole architecture of his gift. They were letters that had been posted and could never be answered.

The rabbit bone was being answered.

Jonah sat down. He didn't decide to; his legs simply resigned. He sat down on his own hearthstone with the kettle shrieking behind him and did not move to take it off, and watched a barefoot child in an impossible dress stand in his front room with one finger on a dead boy's bone, and the bone wept, and the weeping spread.

The sparrow bone caught it next — Maren's voice, Maren's four worn sentences, breaking open into something longer, something *relieved*, a woman's voice saying words Jonah couldn't parse but whose shape he knew the way you know the shape of laughter through a wall. Then the heron bone. Then the ox — *hold, hold, hold* dissolving into a low, deep sound like a herd coming home. One by one, down the shelves, forty-one voices waking out of their long repetition into — what? He couldn't say. Conversation. Recognition. The sound of people who have been knocking on a door for two hundred years hearing, at last, footsteps on the other side.

And the child stood in the middle of it with her finger still on the rabbit bone and tears running down her face, silent tears, the way you cry when crying is a thing you've learned to do without disturbing anyone.

The kettle screamed. Jonah got up and took it off the fire because the dead would have wanted him to — Maren in particular, who had strong opinions about neglected kettles — and the small ordinary act of it steadied him enough to breathe.

The voices faded. Not back to silence — back to their worn, familiar words, one by one, like a congregation sitting back down. The rabbit bone was the last, and before it went, Jonah heard — clearly, unmistakably, in the boy's small voice, but *new*, never-before-heard, a sentence that had never been part of the four:

*Tell her thank you. Tell her we remember her.*

Then: *again. Again. Again.*

The child took her finger off the bone. She turned and looked at Jonah, and her eyes were wet and ancient and six years old, all at once, and she said something in that old, cairn-heavy language, and her tone made the meaning plain even where the words meant nothing: *you heard that too, didn't you.*

"Yes," Jonah said. His voice came out rough. "Yes, I heard it."

She nodded, once, as if a suspicion had been confirmed, and then — the wonder of it, the absurd, domestic wonder — her stomach growled. Loudly. The spell of the impossible broke against the ordinary fact that children, even impossible ones, even barefoot in the first snow, even weeping with the voices of the dead, get hungry in the evening.

Jonah laughed. It came out of him sideways, shaky, half a sob wearing a laugh's coat. "Right," he said. "Bread. Cheese. You can have the bruised half of the apple, I wasn't going to eat it anyway, it offended me."

He cut the bread and she sat at his table — sat the way she did everything, with that easy, unafraid weight, as if the chair had been hers for years — and she ate with the manners of a small duchess and the speed of a small wolf, and between bites she watched him with those old eyes, and Jonah watched her back, and the fire ticked, and outside the snow kept falling with that terrible politeness.

He should have been afraid. He understood, distantly, that he should have been. A child who walks barefoot through snow. A language older than kingdoms. Bones that *weep*. Any one of these was the opening chapter of a story that ended badly — he'd carved enough last words to know how most stories ended, and very few of them ended with everyone warm.

But she was six, and she had snowmelt on her eyelashes, and she was holding the bruised half of the apple in both hands like a lantern.

"What did the bone say to you?" he asked, because he had to ask someone and the dead, for once, weren't talking.

She looked at him for a long moment. Then she set down the apple, pressed her small hand flat over her heart, and said a single word in the old tongue. She said it the way you'd say the name of a country you'd been carried out of in the night.

He didn't understand it. But the rabbit bone, on its shelf, stirred in its sleep, and said — soft, soft, in the boy's drowned voice, a voice Jonah had known for thirty-one years and had thought he knew completely:

*Again.*

And this time, Jonah heard what the word had been, all along, underneath everything: not *throw the stone again*. Not the request of a child to his brother on the ice.

It was a name. Her name. The name of a country.

The boy had been calling her home.

Jonah's hands went cold around his cup of tea. On the shelf, one by one, quiet as snowfall, every bone in the cottage began to whisper — not their worn words, not their last words, but one new word, forty-one voices in ragged, urgent unison, a word he had never heard any of them say in all his life.

*Run,* said the dead of Jonah Whitlock's shelf. *Run. Run. Run.*
