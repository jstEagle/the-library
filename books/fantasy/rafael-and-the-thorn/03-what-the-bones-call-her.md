## 3. What the Bones Call Her

The dead did not repeat themselves.

That was the first rule of bone-carving, the one every apprentice learned before they learned to hold a graver: a voice in the bone said what it had to say and then it was done, forever, like a letter sealed in wax. Forty-one voices saying *run* in unison was not merely strange. It was impossible, the way a river flowing uphill is impossible — you could stand there all day waiting for it to make sense, and it would not.

Jonah stood there anyway, tea going cold in his hand, until the whispering faded back into its usual murmur of last words and small regrets. The girl — Nell, he'd decided to call her, because she had pointed at herself when he'd tried three names and nodded at that one as if accepting a gift she intended to use but not explain — sat at his table eating bread and honey with the solemn concentration of a duchess at a coronation feast.

"You heard that too," he said. "Don't pretend you didn't."

She looked up. Honey at the corner of her mouth. She said nothing, because she never said anything in any tongue he knew, and then she went back to her bread with an air of *yes, obviously, and?*

"Right," Jonah said. "Glad we're agreed."

He set down the cup. His hands were not entirely steady, which annoyed him; a carver's hands were supposed to be the steadiest part of him. He wiped them on his apron out of habit, though there was nothing on them but forty years of fine bone dust worked so deep into the skin that no washing ever got it out.

There was one voice he trusted above all others. Not because she was the loudest or the kindest, though she was both, but because she was old. Two hundred years old, give or take a decade, carved from the shinbone of a woman whose name had worn off the world before Jonah's grandfather's grandfather drew breath. He called her Maren, because that was the name she repeated most often in her long slow loop of last words — *Maren, tell Maren* — and because after thirty years of listening, he had come to feel that a person deserved a name even if only one remained of everything she'd been.

Her carving lived on the top shelf, wrapped in wool, apart from the others. Not because she was better than them. Because she was tired. Old bones spoke slowly, like old people climbing stairs, and Jonah had learned to let her rest between conversations.

He took her down now.

The wool came away and there she was: a piece of bone no longer than his forearm, polished smooth by decades of his thumb, carved along its length with a pattern of wheat stems he'd copied from a design he'd found once in a ruined chapel and never understood. She was warm. Bone always was, a little, when it held someone. Like a stone kept in a pocket.

"Maren," he said quietly. "I need you awake for this one."

The usual words came, soft and worn as river stones. *Maren. Tell Maren.* And then, because she always did, because two hundred years had not worn this out of her: *the river is high, love, mind the ford.*

"I know. I'll mind the ford." He settled onto the stool by the fire, holding her the way he'd hold a bird — firm enough not to drop her, loose enough not to crush. "Something's happened. A child came in the snow. She touched Tam's stone — you remember Tam, the boy on the ice — and he went quiet, and then he wept, and I have never heard a dead man weep, Maren, not once in my life, and now I can't stop hearing it. And tonight every bone on my shelf told me to run, all together, which has never happened, which should not be possible."

*Maren,* she said. *Tell Maren.*

"I'm telling you," Jonah said, and his voice cracked slightly, and he was grateful there was no one living to hear it. "I'm telling you everything. I just don't know what to do."

For a long moment there was nothing but the fire ticking and the girl behind him chewing with terrible dignity. Then Maren spoke again — but differently. Slower. Deeper, somehow, as if the voice were being drawn up from further down in the bone than it usually reached, from some cellar of the dead he hadn't known was there.

*Bring her here.*

Jonah nearly dropped her.

In thirty years, Maren had never once asked about anything outside her own last hour. The dead didn't. That was the second rule, unwritten: they were not ghosts haunting the present; they were moments preserved, amber around a single instant, and the instant was always the same. A voice that could reach forward across two centuries and ask about a living child was a voice that shouldn't exist.

He turned. Nell was watching him over her bread, eyes dark and unblinking, and there was something in her face — not fear, exactly. Recognition. As if she'd been listening to Maren too, in whatever language the dead used among themselves.

"Come here," Jonah said. "Carefully. Don't touch her yet."

Nell slid off the bench and crossed the room, bare feet silent on the floorboards — she still refused shoes, and the cold still refused to trouble her, and Jonah had stopped remarking on either because remarking changed nothing. She stood before him, small and grave, and waited.

"Slowly," he said. "Just a fingertip. If it hurts her—"

She touched the bone.

Maren screamed.

It was not the weeping-scream of Tam, that grief-stricken sobbing Jonah still hadn't fully recovered from. This was different. This was a woman waking from a dream of drowning, gasping, thrashing inside the bone hard enough that it trembled in Jonah's hands like a trapped thing, and the words came tumbling out in a flood — not her worn loop, not *Maren, tell Maren*, but new words, raw words, words with the mud of two hundred years still on them:

*Oh — oh, it's cold, why is it cold, where is the sun, where is Vessia, where is — who is this, who is standing in me, oh, oh, she is —*

And then Maren went utterly, perfectly silent.

The silence lasted ten heartbeats. Jonah counted them, because counting was what he did instead of panicking, and he needed all of his counting.

When she spoke again, her voice had changed. It was steady now. Clear. Younger, almost — as if the child's touch had scoured two centuries of wear off her, the way rain scours a stone, leaving what was underneath sharper than it had been since the day she died.

*Carver.* She had never called him that. In thirty years she had called him nothing at all; he had simply been the warmth her words lived in. *Carver, listen to me. Listen with your whole self, because I will say this once, and then I must sleep a hundred years to pay for saying it.*

"I'm listening," Jonah whispered.

*What do you call her?*

"Nell. It's just — it's a name I gave her. She seemed to like it."

*Nell.* Maren tasted the word, turned it over. *A good shell to hide in. But it isn't her name. Her name is older than names. We — those of us who died under the white sky, those of us who were unwritten — we knew her house. We knew her line. When she walked through the country of the living, the land itself leaned toward her the way wheat leans toward light.* A pause, and when she resumed, her voice had dropped to something almost tender, almost afraid. *We called her the Thorn.*

"The Thorn," Jonah repeated. It sounded wrong in his mouth, harsh and prickling against all that softness, that small solemn face, the honey at the corner of her mouth. "Why would anyone call a child that?"

*Because a thorn is small,* Maren said, *and a thorn is sharp, and a thorn is what remains on the branch when everything else has been stripped away. Because a hedge grows from a thorn. Because if you want to kill a rose forever, carver, you must find every last thorn — and there is always one more thorn than you expected.*

Nell tilted her head, watching Maren's bone with an expression Jonah could not read. Then she reached out and laid her whole palm flat against it, gently, the way you'd touch a sleeping animal, and Maren made a sound he had never heard from the dead before — a sound like relief.

*She remembers us,* Maren breathed. *Oh, mercy. Oh, mercy on us all. She remembers us, and we are not allowed to be remembered.*

"What does that mean?" Jonah's voice came out rougher than he intended. "Who's not allowed? Who forbids it?"

*You know who.* And there was something in her tone that made the hair rise on his arms — not fear of the answer, but the weariness of someone who has answered this question before, long ago, to people who also didn't want to hear it. *You live in a valley where spells cannot root, carver. You think that is an accident of geography. You think your grandmothers settled here for the good soil.* A dry sound, almost laughter, terrible in its smallness. *They settled here because someone told them to run, and they ran, and the valley took them in the way a pocket takes a coin. There are more of us hidden in these hills than you have ever dreamed. Whole families. Whole trades. Whole songs. All of us wearing common names, keeping our heads down, forgetting on purpose so that we would not be forgotten against our will.*

Jonah's mouth had gone dry. "Maren. What are you saying?"

*I am saying that you are asking the wrong questions, and I am too old and too tired to teach you the right ones. So I will tell you only what matters tonight.* The bone grew warmer in his hands, almost hot, as if the voice were spending itself recklessly, burning fuel it could not replace. *The Thorn is hunted. Not by men — or not only by men. Men can be bribed, outrun, outlived. What hunts her is older. It is the erasure itself. It is winter with a purpose. It walks in white, and where it passes, things do not die — they simply stop having been. Your neighbors' grandparents. Your neighbors' graves. Your neighbors' names in their own mothers' mouths. And it is coming here, carver, because the valley's shelter is failing, and because the Thorn has stepped inside it, and a hiding place that hides the hunted is a hiding place no longer.*

Outside, the wind had risen. Jonah noticed it distantly, the way you notice weather in a dream. Nell had not moved. Her palm still rested on the bone, and her eyes were closed now, and her lips were moving — silently, shaping sounds in that old language, and Jonah would have sworn on his mother's grave that she was answering Maren. That the two of them, the two-hundred-years-dead woman and the six-year-old living child, were speaking together in some space he could not enter, about things he could not follow.

*Maren,* he said desperately. *Maren, tell me what to do.*

*Run,* she said, and it was the same word the shelf had spoken, but from her it landed differently — heavier, older, carrying the weight of everyone who had ever said it to everyone who had ever fled. *Take her and run. North, past the ford — no. No, not north. They will watch the roads. Run the way the water runs, carver. Follow the old river road, the drowned one, the one that surfaces only for her. And do not stop for anything white.*

"White," Jonah said. "White what?"

But Maren was fading. He could feel it happening, the way you feel a fire dying — the warmth draining out of the bone degree by degree, the voice receding down into whatever deep cellar it had climbed out of, paying, as she had promised, for every word.

*Carver.* Faint now. A thread. *One thing more. Ask her nothing. She cannot tell you, and every question you ask her is a bell that rings in the dark, and there are ears in the dark that have waited two hundred years for that bell. Keep her fed. Keep her walking. And when the time comes — and it will come, sooner than you want, sooner than you can imagine — remember that a door is only a door if someone is willing to open it.*

"Maren—"

*Tell Maren,* she whispered, back in the old loop now, the worn groove, the safe shallow channel. *Tell Maren the river is high. Mind the ford.*

And she was gone, down into her long sleep, the bone cooling in his hands until it was only bone again, only a carved thing, only wheat stems and polish and dust.

Jonah sat very still.

Behind him, Nell opened her eyes. She looked at him for a long moment — and then she did something she had not done in the two days she'd been under his roof. She climbed onto his knee, carefully, the way she did everything, and put her arms around his neck, and rested her head against his shoulder. She weighed almost nothing. She smelled of woodsmoke and honey and, faintly, inexplicably, of summer.

"We're going to run," Jonah told her, his voice steadier than he felt. "You and me. Tonight, before the snow gets deep. I don't know where yet, and I don't know how, and I've never been further from this valley than the mill at Harrow's Bend. But apparently I'm the sort of fool who lies to Justiciars and talks to two-hundred-year-old women, so I expect running is next on the list."

She patted his shoulder twice. It was, he realized, the closest thing to speech she'd offered him, and it meant, unmistakably: *obviously, and?*

He laughed — a short, cracked, frightened laugh, but a real one — and set her down, and began pulling provisions from the shelves while the wind worried at the eaves.

It was full dark when he saw it.

He had gone out to check the sky — an old habit, reading clouds the way other men read letters — and he stood in the yard with his breath smoking, looking north toward the pass. The stars were out, hard and cold. The frost lay on everything like a skin of glass.

And far off, at the head of the valley, where the road came down out of the hills, three points of light were moving. Not lanterns. Lanterns swung and guttered. These lights rode smooth and level, pale as moonstone, keeping perfect formation, descending toward the valley floor at a pace no horse could sustain for long and no horse would choose.

They were white. Everything about them was white.

Jonah stood in the frozen yard for exactly as long as it took to count them — one, two, three — and then he went inside, banked the fire, woke the child, and began, with shaking hands, to pack.
