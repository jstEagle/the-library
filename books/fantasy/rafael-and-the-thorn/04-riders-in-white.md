## 4. Riders in White

The frost came for the turnips first.

Jonah noticed it on the third morning after the child arrived — a white rime on the garden that should have held until the Feast of Lanterns, six weeks off yet. The turnip greens had gone stiff and glassy overnight, and when he touched one, it crumbled like old biscuit. He stood in his garden with his hands full of ruined leaves and thought: *this is her doing, or something following her.*

Then he thought: *or the world is simply getting worse at its job,* which was a thought he'd been having more often lately, and which he did not enjoy.

Behind him, Nell sat on his doorstep eating porridge with the concentration of a scholar defusing a bomb. She had decided, sometime in the night, that she approved of honey, and had expressed this approval by pointing at the honey jar with such ferocity that Jonah had nearly dropped it. She did not speak. He was beginning to suspect she could not — or would not — and that there was a difference between those two things that mattered enormously.

"You'll want a coat," he told her. "And boots. And possibly a small army."

She looked up at him with the particular patience of someone who has heard a great deal of nonsense from adults and has learned to let them have their fun.

The dead, meanwhile, were being difficult.

* * *

Jonah's workshop smelled of tallow and bone dust and, if you knew how to listen, of two hundred years of last words. He'd inherited the trade from his master, Old Fenwick, who'd inherited it from nobody anyone could name, and in thirty years Jonah had carved perhaps four hundred pieces — buttons and brooches mostly, the occasional chess knight, once a whole set of prayer beads for a woman who wanted to feel her husband near her throat. Every piece whispered. Not aloud, not to anyone else. Only to him. A murmur under the skin of things, like a house settling, like snow sliding off a roof.

This morning, the whispers were wrong.

He picked up the brooch he'd finished two days ago — old Marta Halloway, dead eleven years, whose last word had been *pickles*, to the eternal confusion of her family — and instead of Marta's comfortable grumble he got a thin, stretched silence, like a note held too long.

"Marta?" he said quietly.

Nothing. Then, faintly, as if from very far away through a wall: *—cold here, boy. Cold coming down the—* and gone again.

He tried the chess knight. Same thing. The prayer beads. Same. It was as if every voice in his workshop had turned to face the same direction at once — north, he realized, north, toward the pass — and was holding its breath.

"Right," Jonah said to the empty room. "That's not ominous at all."

Nell appeared in the doorway. She looked at the bones laid out on his bench, then at him, and something passed over her face — not fear exactly. Recognition. The way you look at an old friend's handwriting on an envelope you didn't expect.

She reached out and touched the prayer beads, just one fingertip, and every voice in the room went silent so completely that Jonah's ears rang. Then, softly, terribly, they began to weep. He couldn't hear words. Just grief, pouring out of the bones like water out of a cracked jug, and Nell took her hand back quickly and pressed it flat against her chest as though she'd been scalded.

"I know," Jonah said helplessly. "I know. I don't know either."

* * *

They came at dusk, three days later.

Jonah saw them first from the window: three figures on the road from the pass, riding horses so white they seemed cut from the same paper as the snow. Even at distance there was something wrong about them — the way they moved, all three in perfect time, like figures in a music box. The valley had no visitors. The valley barely had roads worth the name. People came in to buy carvings twice a year and left with the uneasy look of folk who'd stayed a half hour too long in a room where someone was sleeping.

The dead felt them before the horses reached the gate.

Every carving in the house began to whisper at once — not words, just terror, a rising tide of it, and Jonah pressed his palms against his workbench and breathed until it ebbed. Beside him, Nell had gone very still. She climbed down from her stool, walked to the cold hearth, and stood inside it, in the ashes, facing the wall. It was, he reflected, exactly what he himself would do if he were six years old and the walls of the world were leaking.

A knock. Three knocks, evenly spaced, like punctuation.

Jonah wiped his hands on his apron, wiped them again, and opened the door.

Three riders stood in the blue evening light. Their horses stood behind them without being tied, without moving, without — he noticed with a lurch — breathing. Steam should have risen from their flanks in this cold. Nothing rose.

But it was the faces that stopped Jonah's heart mid-beat. They were almost faces. Almost. As if a painter had blocked in the general idea of a man — the suggestion of brow, the placeholder of mouth — and then been called away before adding eyes that meant anything, or lines that had ever moved. One of them smiled, and the smile arrived a half-second late, like thunder after lightning.

"Good evening," said the middle rider. His voice was pleasant. That was somehow the worst part. "We're looking for a runaway."

"A runaway," Jonah repeated.

"A child." The rider produced a folded paper and unfolded it with great ceremony, though Jonah noticed he never actually looked at it. "Small. Dark-haired. May be traveling alone. There is a reward for information leading to her return to her family."

"She doesn't have a family," said the rider on the left. This one's face was even less finished than the others', as if he'd been sketched in haste. "There is no family. There never was a family."

"That's rather sad," said the smiling one, and smiled wider, late.

Jonah became aware that his own heart was hammering, and that the hammering was audible — he could feel it in his teeth — and that these three creatures could probably hear a heartbeat through a stone wall. Behind him, in the dark of the house, he could sense Nell standing in the dead hearth, silent as ash.

"Well," Jonah said. "No children here. I live alone."

It came out easily. That surprised him. He had expected lying to be harder — some terrible grinding of the soul — but the words slid out smooth as a carved bead, and only afterward did the horror arrive, arriving late like the rider's smile.

The unfinished-faced one tilted his head. "Alone? We understood a bone-carver lived here. Bone-carvers are rarely alone. The dead keep such busy company."

For one lunatic moment Jonah almost laughed. Then he understood what the creature was telling him: *we know what you are. We know what you can hear.* His mouth went dry as chalk dust.

"The dead don't talk," Jonah said. Second lie. It hurt more than the first, because it was a lie about himself, about everything he was, and he felt it land somewhere deep, like a splinter driven under a fingernail.

"No," agreed the middle rider pleasantly. "They don't. How fortunate for everyone."

The third rider had not spoken at all. Now it leaned forward in the saddle — a motion like a chair creaking — and looked past Jonah's shoulder into the house. Its unfinished eyes swept the room: the bench, the tools, the bones laid out in rows. Jonah watched it look at the prayer beads, the chess knight, the little brooch of Marta Halloway, and he watched nothing happen, because the valley was magic-dead, because nothing worked here, because the valley where no spell could take root had always been his shelter —

— and for the first time in his life, shelter felt like a cage.

Because he couldn't leave. He understood that suddenly, completely, the way you understand a tooth is bad. These things had ridden into the one place in the world their power failed, and they hadn't seemed troubled by it. Which meant they could wait. Which meant the valley wasn't safety; it was just a smaller cell with a nicer view. And outside it lay a world where his gift screamed at him and hers — whatever hers was — burned like a signal fire, and they would ride them down across any distance, patient as winter.

"We'll ask at the other houses," the middle rider said, folding away the paper that described nothing. "If you see a child — small, dark-haired, alone —"

"There are no children alone," Jonah said. Third lie. "Not in winter. Not here."

"No," the rider agreed. "There shouldn't be."

They turned their horses — all three in perfect unison, hooves finding the frozen ruts in the same order — and rode back up the road toward the pass. They made no sound. Snow did not crunch beneath them. Jonah stood in his doorway until the dark swallowed them, and then stood there longer, because turning around meant going back inside, and going back inside meant looking at Nell, and looking at Nell meant admitting what he'd just done.

* * *

His hands started shaking before he got the door shut.

He lit a lamp with fingers that wouldn't cooperate, and the match skittered across the table twice before it caught. Nell emerged from the hearth grey with ash, and looked at him, and looked at his hands, and did the thing she did sometimes — reached up and took one of them, both her small hands around his one big shaking one, and held it the way you hold a bird that's flown into a window.

"It's all right," Jonah told her, which was the fourth lie of the day, and the worst one, because now she was watching him learn what lies cost.

He put the kettle on because putting the kettle on was a thing hands could do instead of shake. He got down two cups, then remembered she preferred the blue bowl, then stood holding the blue bowl and thinking about the rider's voice saying *there is no family, there never was a family,* and wondering what kind of creature says a thing like that, and what kind of country makes creatures say it.

The tea helped. Slightly. His hands kept shaking anyway, well past midnight, while he sat by the banked fire and Nell slept curled in the big chair with her feet tucked under her like a fox. He tried to carve — working usually steadied him — but the moment his knife touched bone, the whispers rose up in a chorus of *run, run, the white ones, run,* and he set the knife down and didn't pick it up again.

The shaking lasted, all told, four hours. He counted. It seemed important to count — to know precisely what a lie cost, so he'd have the price ready next time. Because there would be a next time. Those riders would come back, or others like them, and next time he'd have to lie faster, and better, and mean it more, and each lie would take its toll out of whatever it was inside him that had spent thirty years being an honest, quiet man whom the living found unsettling and the dead found useful.

Around two in the morning, Nell woke, padded over, and climbed onto the bench beside him. She studied his trembling hands with grave professional interest. Then she took his right hand, turned it palm-up, and traced a shape on it with one finger — deliberate, careful, a symbol he didn't know, drawn in the old language she carried in place of words.

"What does that mean?" he asked.

She looked at him for a long moment. Then she pointed at his hand, then at her own chest, then out the dark window, north, toward the pass where the white riders had gone.

*You. Me. Them,* maybe. Or *hold on. Remember. Run.*

Or possibly, given the company: *we're in trouble.*

"Yeah," Jonah said softly, and closed his fingers around the shape she'd drawn, as if he could keep it. "I'd worked that out."

Outside, the frost crept further down the valley than it had ever come before, and in his workshop, faintly, patiently, the dead began to sing a song none of them had known in life.
