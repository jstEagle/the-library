## 5. Nell

*Vessia*, she thought, and the word was a door with no wall behind it.

She sat on the bone-carver's floor with her knees pulled up to her chin, watching the man — *Yona*, the dead-things called him, though his mouth said Jonah — work at his bench. Shavings curled away from his knife like pale petals. The fire ticked. Outside, the snow kept falling in that patient way snow has, as if it had all the time in the world, which perhaps it did.

Nell did not have all the time in the world. She knew this the way you know a stone is in your shoe: not by looking, but by walking.

*Vessia*, she thought again, harder, the way you press your tongue against a loose tooth. And what came back was —

Glass rivers. That was true, or had been. Rivers you could see straight down into, so clear that the fish seemed to swim through air, and the ferry-boats glided over them casting shadows on the pebbles far below, shadows like slow dark birds. She had ridden one once. Or her mother had carried her onto one. Or she had dreamed it. The memory had edges like wet paper.

*Singing law-courts.* Also true, also fraying. In Vessia you did not settle an argument by shouting. You went to the court-house, which was built of white stone and hollow places, and you sang your grievance, and the other party sang theirs, and the building itself judged — it hummed back whichever song was truer, and the truer song's singer got to go home first. There had been a woman who sued her neighbor because his goats kept eating her washing, and the court had hummed for both of them, because the goats were hungry and the washing was her mother's before her, and everyone had gone home at the same time feeling very solemn and slightly cheated, which Nell understood now was what justice usually felt like.

She almost smiled. Then she tried to remember the tune the courts sang, and could not, and the smile went out like a pinched wick.

The bone-carver glanced over his shoulder at her. He did that often — checked on her the way you check on a candle near a curtain. He couldn't understand her words. Nobody could. Her words were old ones, older than the kingdom whose soldiers had marched past her on the road, older than the coins in the carver's dish, older maybe than the mountain that held this valley shut like a hand around something small and warm.

That was why she had come here, she thought. Wasn't it? Magic could not follow into the valley. The dead-voices went quiet at the boundary line, and the white riders' spells slid off the air like rain off oiled cloth. Here she was only a strange mute child instead of a hunted one.

Here she was safe.

So why did her chest feel like a fist was inside it, slowly closing?

* * *

The trouble with forgetting, Nell thought, was that it didn't feel like anything while it happened.

You would reach for a word — *the word for the smell of rain on hot stone*, say, which in her tongue was a beautiful word, three sounds long, shaped like water going downhill — and you would find nothing there. Not emptiness exactly. Emptiness you could live in. It was more like reaching into a drawer and finding the drawer had never existed, and neither had the dresser, and come to think of it what was a dresser?

She had been losing things since the night her mother pressed the token into her hand.

That memory was the strongest one she had, and she guarded it jealously, turning it over every night before sleep like a coin she was afraid to spend. Her mother's hands were cold. That detail surprised people when Nell managed to convey it — wasn't a mother's touch supposed to be warm? But her mother's hands had been cold for days, cold as river-stones, because she had been holding the Door, whatever that meant, and holding things cost heat. She had knelt down so their faces were level. She had smelled of woodsmoke and of the particular herb they boiled in Vessia against sorrow, the one whose name Nell had lost along with its taste.

And she had pressed the token into Nell's palm and folded her small fingers closed over it, one by one, deliberately, the way you button a coat against weather.

"Run," her mother had said. Not in fear. In the voice you use to tell someone the time, or that supper is ready. A fact, plainly given. "Run, little thorn. Run and remember."

Then — and this was the part Nell hated most, the part that made her want to bite something — then her mother had smiled, and the smile had been *wrong*, and Nell had been too young to know why it was wrong, and now she knew why it was wrong but could not make the knowing help: the smile was wrong because her mother was already forgetting her. Even then. Even kneeling there. She was memorizing Nell's face even as it slipped through her, a woman bailing out a boat with her hands.

Nell had run. She remembered running. After that the memories came in pieces, like a dropped plate.

*A woman in a market who fed her bread and then looked at her strangely, as if trying to place her, and failed.*

*A bridge. Whose bridge? Bridges had names in Vessia. All the bridges had names and opinions.*

*Snow beginning. Snow that fell too early and did not stop.*

*The valley line, and the sudden silence in her head where a great murmuring had always been — not voices, exactly, more like the sense of being watched by many kind eyes, and then nothing, and how frightening the nothing was until she understood it was shelter.*

*And the door of the bone-carver's house, and her own fist rising to knock, and the strange certainty — the only certain thing left in her whole body — that this was the next step, and after this step there would be another, and the steps led somewhere, and the somewhere was called*

— called —

Nell put her head down on her knees.

*The Door.* She knew those two words the way you know your own name. Everything else about them was gone. Where it stood. What it opened. Why a six-year-old girl should walk barefoot across a winter country to reach it while the snow ate the world behind her. She knew only that she must, the way a river knows only that it goes downhill.

It was infuriating. She was, she suspected, a person who liked to understand things. There was evidence of this in the fragments: a girl who asked the singing courts questions just to hear the building think; a girl who took apart her father's — someone's — a man's — astrolabe and put it back together with two parts left over, which had apparently been fine, which had apparently made it *more accurate*, and everyone had laughed, and she had laughed, and whose laugh had it been?

Whose little girl had she been?

*Vessia,* she answered herself stubbornly. *I was Vessia's.* And then, because the thought arrived unbidden and true: *and Vessia is dying, and I am what it does instead of screaming.*

She didn't fully understand that thought. It had simply walked into her head and sat down, like a cat claiming a chair. Some thoughts did that now. They came from further down in her than she could reach, sent up like messages from the crew of a sinking ship — *we're still here, we're still here* — and she could not answer them, and answering wouldn't save anyone anyway.

But she could carry them. Carrying was something she could do.

* * *

"You're doing the face again," the bone-carver said, though of course she understood none of it. He said it gently, and pointed at her, and made an exaggerated frown, and then pointed at the bread he'd set near the fire to warm, and raised his eyebrows in a way that needed no language at all.

Bread was also a language. Nell came and took her piece and held it in both hands and let the warmth soak into her palms. The carver watched her eat with an expression she was learning to read: worry wearing kindness as a disguise, badly.

He talked to himself while he worked, she'd noticed. Not quite to himself. To the carvings. Little reports, the way you'd speak to an elderly relative hard of hearing. *"Cold today. Yes, I know, I'll bring more wood in. No, you can't have the good knife, stop asking."* And the carvings — this was the part that had frightened her the first night, and still unsettled her, though less — the carvings seemed to listen.

When she had touched the finished one, the bird, on her first evening, everything inside it had gone silent. She hadn't known she'd done anything. She had only wanted, desperately, the way you want things at the end of a long terrible day — she had wanted the noise to stop. There had been so much noise since the valley line, since before it, noise under the noise, the dead of a whole country whispering *remember us* into the marrow of the world, and she was seven years old and tired, and she had touched the carved bird and wished for quiet.

And gotten it. And then the quiet had broken into weeping, which was worse, and the carver had looked at her like she was a storm walking upright.

She was sorry about that. She was sorry about most things lately. Sorry seemed to be the main emotion available to her, the way porridge was the main food available to poor houses.

Now she finished her bread and dusted her hands and did something she had been wanting to do for two days. She went to the shelf where the carvings lived — rows of small bones, each one a person once, each one humming faintly to itself like bees in a wall — and she held up her hands to show them she meant no harm, and then, carefully, she began to sing.

Not loudly. Barely above breath. An old song, a counting song, the kind mothers used to sing while braiding hair: *one for the door and two for the stair, three for the glass river bearing you somewhere...* The words were in her tongue, the old tongue, the tongue with no living speakers except one small girl with cold feet.

The carvings went still. Then, one by one, they began to hum along.

They didn't know the song, exactly. But they knew its shape. It was in them the way rivers are in rain. Two hundred years of silence, and here was a child singing the sound of home, and the dead harmonized the way the drowned rise toward light — without deciding to, because it was what they were.

Nell sang all four verses. Her voice cracked twice. On the last line — *and five for the Door that stands open for thee* — the whole shelf hummed so softly and so sadly that the fire itself seemed to lean in to listen.

Behind her, the bone-carver had stopped working entirely. His knife hung in the air. He was staring at his carvings with his mouth open, and his eyes were wet, and he kept touching his own chest as if checking whether the sound was coming from him.

Good, Nell thought fiercely, blinking hard. *Remember it. You remember it too, then. If I lose it, it won't be lost.*

Because that was the thing she was only now, sitting in the warm valley with bread in her belly, beginning to understand about herself. Her mother had not sent her away to be saved. Saving her might have been accomplished by anyone with a spare room and a kind heart. No — her mother had pressed the token into her hand and said *run and remember*, and the token was not the point, and the running was not the point.

The remembering was the point. She was not the heir of Vessia. She was its last page, and pages exist to be read.

And the winter outside the valley — the snow that fell wrong, that fell like it was erasing rather than covering — the winter was coming for the page.

* * *

That night she slept in the cot the carver had made up for her, under blankets that smelled of cedar, and she dreamed in pieces.

She dreamed of the glass rivers, and in the dream they were freezing, frost climbing up through the clear water like white ferns, and the fish below the ice looked up at her with her mother's eyes.

She dreamed of the law-courts, and the white stone building was silent, its hollow places stopped up with snow, and no matter how she sang it would not hum back.

She dreamed of the token — the bone token her mother had folded into her fist — and in the dream she opened her hand to look at it, and it was blank. Smooth. Unwritten. As if it had never held anything at all.

She woke with a gasp and clawed at the cord around her neck.

The token was there. Of course it was there. It lay against her skin, small and worn, and she pressed her thumb into its carving — a spiral, a door seen edge-on, a thing she had looked at ten thousand times — and felt its edges, real edges, honest edges, and her heart slowed.

But her thumb found something else too. Something that had not been there before.

At the token's rim, so fine she might have imagined it, the surface had begun to smooth. One thread of the spiral, the outermost one, was fading — not broken, not scratched. Fading. The way writing fades. The way names fade.

Outside the window, the snow had stopped falling upward into the sky the way it sometimes did here at the valley's edge, magic sliding off the world. Tonight it fell straight down, heavy and purposeful, and beyond the mountains, in the dark, Nell could feel it working — the white winter walking country to country, farm to farm, unwriting as it came.

It had reached the token. Which meant it had reached the last of the tokens. Which meant it was nearly done with everything else.

Nell lay in the dark with her fist closed tight around the fading spiral, and made herself a promise in a language only the dead still spoke:

*I will get there first.*

Two valleys east, something vast turned over in its sleep and began, patiently, to count.
