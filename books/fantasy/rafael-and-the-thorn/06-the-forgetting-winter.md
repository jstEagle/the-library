## 6. The Forgetting Winter

The frost came early that year, and it came wrong.

Jonah noticed it first in the way the dead spoke. His carvings had always murmured — a low tide of last words, complaints, half-finished prayers — but on the morning after the first hard freeze, the voices sounded thin, as though he were listening to them through a wall of ice. Old Marta's carving, a knucklebone he'd polished smooth over twenty years, usually greeted him with her eternal grievance about her son-in-law's fencing. That morning she said only: *cold, so cold, was it always this cold?*

"You're dead, Marta," Jonah told her, setting her bone by the stove. "Cold is sort of your whole situation now."

No answer. Marta had never once failed to answer. Arguing with him was, as far as he could tell, the entire point of her continued existence.

He held the bone up to the window light. It looked the same. It felt the same, warm in his palm the way all his carvings were warm, the way they'd been warm since he was a boy and discovered that the sheep bones from the midden behind his cottage whispered if you held them long enough. But something inside it had gone quiet in a way that wasn't sleep. Sleep had a texture. This was absence.

Behind him, Nell sat cross-legged on the floor, arranging his spare carvings into patterns only she understood. She'd been with him eleven days now, and in that time she had spoken not one word of anything he could understand, eaten an amount of porridge that should have been impossible for a person her size, and developed a firm opinion that the cat — a scarred gray tom named Turnip who had adopted Jonah out of what he suspected was pity — belonged to her specifically.

Turnip, for his part, had accepted this arrangement with the resignation of a creature who had survived worse.

"Nell," Jonah said. "Have you noticed anything strange about the bones?"

She looked up. She had a way of looking at him that made him feel like a question being asked rather than a man asking one. Then she crawled over, took Marta's knucklebone from his hand, and pressed it to her ear the way she did sometimes, eyes closing.

Her face changed. She opened her eyes, looked at the bone, then at him, and shook her head slowly. Then she pointed at the bone, pointed at the frost-ferned window, and made a gesture — fingers spreading outward, dissolving, like smoke off a chimney.

"Gone?" Jonah said.

She nodded. Then, after a moment, she pointed at the window too, at the white world beyond it, and repeated the gesture. Fingers spreading. Dissolving.

The same thing was happening outside.

* * *

The Harrow farmstead lay two miles down the valley, past the alder stand where the stream cut through on its way to the lowlands. The Harrows were not friends, exactly — Tam Harrow had once accused Jonah of witchcraft over a disputed fence line, and their eldest boy had thrown stones at Nell on her second day, which Nell had endured with the serene indifference of royalty reviewing troops — but they were neighbors, which in a valley meant something. Tam sold Jonah mutton in autumn. His wife, Bess, left eggs on the fencepost every third day without ever mentioning it, because mentioning it would have required speaking to Jonah, and speaking to Jonah made people feel as though they'd swallowed something cold.

Jonah walked down on the fourth morning of the frost because Bess hadn't left eggs.

That alone wouldn't have alarmed him. Bess missed days. But there had been no smoke from the chimney when he'd woken, and no smoke now, and the dogs — the Harrows kept three of them, loud enough to be heard across the valley — had not barked at him once on the entire walk down. Not at him, not at a fox he startled near the alders, not at anything. He passed within sight of the Harrow yard before he understood why.

There were no dogs.

There was no yard.

Jonah stood at the edge of the alder stand with his breath smoking in front of him and looked at a field of winter wheat he knew for a fact had never been planted, because the field had been pasture for as long as anyone could remember, grazing land for the Harrows' sheep, and there were no sheep either, and no fence, and no gate, and no lane worn bare by cart wheels, and no farmhouse with its crooked chimney and Bess's herb garden along the south wall.

There was just the field. Untouched snow, unbroken, lying over it like a page no one had written on yet.

"Tam," Jonah said aloud, and his voice cracked in the cold air and went nowhere.

He walked the boundary anyway. Where the Harrow lane should have met the valley road, the road simply continued, smooth and unbroken, hedged with hawthorn that was old, older than any hedge had a right to be, as though it had been growing there for a hundred years instead of thirty. He found the stile where he used to climb over rather than walk around, and the stile was gone, and the gap in the hedge it occupied was gone, the hedge grown whole and seamless.

His legs carried him back up the valley faster than he liked. At the cottage door, Nell was waiting, and she read his face before he got a word out. She took his hand — small, cold-steady, unafraid of anything, including apparently him — and led him inside, and put the kettle on, because even a child who spoke no living tongue understood that a man needed tea before the end of the world.

"They're gone," he said, when his hands had stopped shaking enough to hold a cup. "Not dead. Dead leaves something. Dead leaves a grave, or a burned house, or at least a hole where the dog used to bark. They're *unwritten*, Nell. The valley's forgotten them. I've forgotten—" He stopped. He tried to remember Bess's face and found he could picture the eggs on the fencepost perfectly, the blue of the apron she wore, but the face above the apron was a smudge, a thumbprint on wet clay. "I can't remember her face. I could yesterday. I know I could."

Nell climbed onto the bench beside him. She touched his cheek, briefly, the way you'd touch a wound to see how deep it went. Then she went to the shelf above the hearth, where his carvings lived in their careful rows, each one labeled in his own hand with a name and a date, and began taking them down one by one and holding them to her ear.

Most of them still whispered. He could hear them faintly even from the bench — the usual murmur, thinned by the cold but present, his congregation of the finished. Nell moved down the row like a priest checking lamps.

At the eleventh bone she stopped.

It was a wrist-bone, carved with a shepherd's crook pattern, labeled *Wren Colley, d. 1811*. Wren had been a young woman when she died, drowned in the millrace, and her last words — the words soaked into the bone, the words Jonah had heard every day for fifteen years — were *tell my sister the north field is hers.* She said it eternally, patiently, the way the dead say things, and Jonah had long ago stopped hearing it as words and started hearing it as weather, part of the sound of the cottage, like wind under the door.

Nell held the bone to her ear. Her shoulders dropped. She turned it over twice, looking for something, and then brought it to Jonah and laid it in his palm and stepped back, and her expression was the one she wore for funerals — he'd seen it once, at the graveside of old Widow Petch, whose carving had been the quietest in his collection, respectful and unsurprised, as if death were a thing she'd been introduced to formally and considered dull company.

Jonah listened.

Nothing.

Not thinness, not the ice-wall muffling of the last few days. Nothing. The bone was a bone. Warm still — that was the horror of it, it was still warm, still his work, still humming with whatever faint craft lived in his hands — but empty. A lamp with the flame pinched out. And when he looked at the label, at his own handwriting, *Wren Colley, d. 1811*, the name meant nothing to him. He read it three times. It was ink on bone. He had no memory of carving it, of the millrace, of a sister, of a north field. Somewhere between yesterday and this morning, Wren Colley had been erased, and the erasure had reached into his own hearth and taken her out of it, and taken her out of *him*.

He sat down heavily. Turnip, sensing weather, relocated to his lap and began to purr with the shameless opportunism of his kind.

"The winter," Jonah said. "It's not weather." He looked at Nell, and she was already looking at him, and he saw that she had known — had perhaps known since the night she arrived on his doorstep barefoot in the snow, unbothered by cold that would have killed any ordinary child, because how do you freeze a girl who comes from a country that is being unwritten? "This is what's chasing you, isn't it. Not riders. Not yet. This. The forgetting itself. It's spreading out from wherever it started, and everything it touches stops having ever been."

Nell nodded. Then she did something new. She went to the corner where she slept, dug beneath her ragged blanket, and came out with the bone token she wore on a cord around her neck — he'd seen it before, a small flat piece carved with spirals, the only thing she'd arrived with besides the language no one spoke. She pressed it into his hand and closed his fingers over it.

For one heartbeat, the token *spoke*.

Not in words. In a rush of image: glass rivers under a green sun, towers like held breath, a hall full of singing — law-courts, he thought wildly, courts that sang their verdicts — and a woman's hands, pressing this token into a smaller pair of hands, and a word shaped like a door. Then it was gone, and the token was silent again, and Jonah was sitting in his cottage with a cat on his lap and frost climbing the windows, breathing hard.

"That's Vessia," he said. He didn't know how he knew the name. It arrived in his mouth the way Wren's name had departed — some ledger somewhere, balancing. "That's your country. The one that isn't on any map."

Nell watched him with wide dark eyes. She nodded once.

"And it's going the way the Harrows went. The way Wren went." He looked at the blank bone in his other hand, at the neat useless label. Grief hit him sideways, grief for people he could no longer grieve properly, a mourning with the middle cut out of it. He'd spent his whole life keeping the dead alive by listening, and now the dead were being taken from *him*, and there was nothing to listen to, nothing to argue with, nothing to carve into remembrance, because remembrance itself was what was dying.

He thought of Marta, silent by the stove. He picked her knucklebone up and held it to his ear and begged her, quietly, absurdly, to say something about her son-in-law's fencing, anything, and she didn't, and he set her down with more gentleness than he'd ever handled any bone, and Nell put her hand on his arm and left it there.

Outside, the light was failing early, the way it did now, the winter pressing in like a hand closing.

"We can't stay," Jonah said. It cost him to say it. This valley had been the one place in the world where magic couldn't follow him, where his gift was just a quirk and not a curse, where he'd built a life of porridge and carvings and a cat who tolerated him. But the valley's silence, its blessed magic-deadness, had never been a wall. It had been a delay. And the thing coming wasn't magic, exactly — it was older and hungrier than magic, it was the world's own memory failing — and delays ran out.

Nell tilted her head. Then she stood, went to the window, and pointed north, up-valley, toward the high pass where the road left the valley walls behind. Her finger was steady. Of course it was steady. She'd been running longer than he'd known her; steadiness was the one language she was fluent in.

"Not tonight," Jonah said. "Tonight we pack. Tonight I decide which bones come and which stay, and I'm going to hate every choice." He looked around the cottage — at the shelves, the rows, the little congregation of the finished — and felt the weight of what leaving meant. If the erasure reached the valley fully, the ones he left behind wouldn't suffer. They'd simply never have been. There was a terrible mercy in it, and he hated the mercy more than he'd have hated fire.

He was reaching for the first packing basket when Nell caught his sleeve.

She was staring at the window, at the darkening valley, and her face had gone carefully, deliberately blank — the look of a child deciding not to be afraid. Jonah followed her gaze and saw nothing at first. Snow, dusk, the black line of the alders. Then, far down the valley, where the Harrow farmstead had been and had never been, a light moved. Not lamplight. It was pale and even and wrong, the color of the frost itself, and it was coming up the valley road at walking pace, unhurried, the way winter walks.

Three lights. No — he squinted — three shapes carrying lights, tall, riding horses that made no sound on frozen ground.

Nell's hand found his. Her grip was small and iron.

"Right," Jonah said, his voice steadier than his heart. "Packing can wait. Get under the floorboards, take Turnip, and don't come out until I knock twice."

The lights came on up the valley, patient as the cold, and Jonah blew out his lamp and went to answer whatever the winter had sent him.
