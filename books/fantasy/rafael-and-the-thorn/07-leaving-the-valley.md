## 7. Leaving the Valley

Jonah had spent three days pretending he wasn't packing.

He told himself he was merely tidying the workshop — which was true, in the way that a man drowning is merely getting wet. He wrapped each carving in wool and laid it into the old market basket his mother's hands had never touched, because everything else she'd touched had been sold or burned or simply worn away by decades of a life lived without her. The carvings went in smallest first: the sparrow with a soldier's last breath folded inside its hollow chest; the fisherman's thumb-bone shaped like a wave; the little clay-colored hen that clucked insults at him whenever he worked past midnight, because its dead woman had apparently been the sort who clucked insults at everyone.

"You'll want the big ones on top," said the hen, in a voice like dry leaves arguing. "Weight distribution. Honestly, boy, your mother packed better and she was half your size."

"My mother," Jonah said mildly, "packed me into a basket once too, I expect."

The hen had nothing to say to that. None of them ever did, when he mentioned her. Two hundred years of accumulated dead, and not one of them knew what became of a woman who had walked out of this valley before Jonah was born and never walked back.

He was stalling, and he knew it. Nell sat on his doorstep in the thin morning light, barefoot as always, watching the frost burn off the grass with the patient attention of a cat watching a mouse hole. She had been watching things like that for three days now — frost, smoke, the way water went glassy over the stones in the creek. Watching the valley the way you watch a face across a sickbed.

She didn't know it was dying. Or maybe she did. Children always knew more than they could say, and Nell couldn't say anything at all.

"Right," Jonah said to the empty workshop, because saying it aloud made it realer than thinking it. "Right."

* * *

The trouble with leaving a place where magic cannot follow, he reflected as he shouldered the pack and took Nell's small cold hand, is that you spend your whole life there believing the world outside is like the world inside. Quiet. Lawful. Bones are bones, wood is wood, and the dead stay politely where you put them.

He had crossed the valley's edge exactly twice in his life — once as an infant, carried out and carried back by whoever had found him, though he remembered none of it; and once at nineteen, on a dare, walking as far as the old boundary stone at Harrow's Ford and standing there with one boot on either side of some invisible line, feeling nothing at all, until he'd laughed at himself and gone home.

He did not laugh this time.

They came down through the birch stand above Harrow's Ford just past midmorning, and the first thing that changed was the light. That was how it began — not thunder, not any grand tearing of the sky, but light. It went from the flat pewter gray of an overcast autumn to something golden and trembling, as if the sun had been holding its breath for years and finally let it out. The birches blazed white and gold. Every dewdrop on every spiderweb caught fire.

Nell stopped walking.

Her hand tightened around his fingers — small, strong, colder than the morning justified — and then her shadow moved.

Not *with* her. Not the way shadows do, faithful and dull, copying whatever the body gives them. Her shadow peeled up off the ground like a sheet lifted by wind, stretched tall and thin against the blazing birches, and turned its head to look back the way they had come. Toward the valley. Toward home.

It had no face. But Jonah felt it looking anyway, the way you feel someone reading over your shoulder.

"Nell," he said carefully, "your—"

She was already looking down at it. She didn't scream. She crouched, slow as syrup, and touched the ground where the shadow's shoulder would be, and the shadow leaned into her hand like a dog leaning into a scratch, and then poured itself back under her feet and lay still, quivering faintly, the way a struck bell keeps humming after the sound stops.

Nell looked up at him. Her eyes were enormous and dark and utterly calm, and she pointed — first at herself, then at the world beyond the ford, then at herself again — and shrugged, as if to say: *well. Here we are.*

"That's one way to put it," Jonah said, and his voice only shook a little.

Then they crossed the water, stepping stone to stone through the singing shallows, and the world finished waking up.

* * *

Later — much later, when he could bear to describe it — Jonah would say it was like being born. He meant no romance by it. He meant the sheer, undifferentiated *everything* of it: light and noise and pressure all arriving at once, with no order and no mercy, into a person who had spent thirty years living in a room where almost nothing happened.

Because the valley was silent. He had known that the way you know water is wet. What he had never understood — what no one could have told him, because no one in the valley could feel the difference — was how loud the world was. How *crowded*.

Every dead thing within miles began to speak at once.

The ford itself was built on bones. He felt them the moment his second boot left the last stone: the riverbed gravel was shot through with them, centuries of travelers and drowned cattle and one unlucky toll-keeper whose skull still muttered about unpaid fares somewhere beneath the current. The willows along the bank held the ashes of funeral fires from before anyone alive could remember, and the ashes *remembered*, and they wanted to tell him. A crow flew overhead and it was only a crow, thank all the quiet gods, but the fence post it landed on had been cut from a tree that grew over a plague pit, and the tree's grain still held forty voices braided together like rope, and they all started talking the instant Jonah's gift woke up and heard them.

He went to his knees in the mud of the far bank.

It wasn't words, mostly. It was *pressure* — grief and grievance and confusion and the terrible simple loneliness of the dead, all of it pouring into him through every carved surface and every uncarved one, because out here, apparently, his gift didn't need bone. Out here the dead could see him. Out here he was a lit window in a dark country, and they were all coming to the glass.

"*Stop,*" he gasped, and pressed his palms over his ears, which helped exactly as much as pressing your palms over your ears helps against a flood. "*Please—*"

Small hands found his face. Nell knelt in front of him, mud soaking through her dress, and did the thing she had done to the sparrow carving on her first night in his house: she laid two fingers against his temple, and *pushed*.

The silence that fell wasn't empty. It was the silence of a mother drawing a blanket over a sleeping child — deliberate, warm, close-fitted. The roar of the dead dimmed to a murmur, then to a hush, then to something like the memory of sound. Jonah sagged forward until his forehead rested against hers, and breathed, and breathed.

"How," he whispered. His lips were numb. "How are you doing that?"

She tilted her head. Then, with great seriousness, she mimed — clumsily, with a child's economy — taking something heavy out of her own chest and setting it down between them. Then she pointed at the space behind him, toward the valley, and drew her finger slowly across her throat.

*In there,* she was telling him, *it couldn't reach me. Out here, it can reach both of us. So I am choosing what reaches us.*

Or perhaps she was only saying: *I don't know.* With Nell it was often both.

The dead, muffled now behind whatever wall she'd raised, settled into a distant ocean-sound. Jonah got his legs under him. He stood, swaying, and looked back the way they'd come.

Harrow's Ford glinted behind them, ordinary and golden. Beyond it the birches climbed the slope, and beyond those, invisible, the valley lay in its long quiet like a sleeping animal — the good brown fields, the crooked lanes, his workshop with its banked fire and its empty shelves. Thirty years of the only peace he had ever known.

And above the whole of it, so faint he might have imagined it, hung a shimmer in the air. Like heat-haze in reverse. Like the world forgetting to look at something.

The winter was already inside the valley. He understood that now. The frost that had come weeks early, the farmsteads going strange and half-remembered, the carving whose dead voice had simply stopped mid-word — the erasure hadn't been circling them. It had been *seeping*. And the valley's magic-deadness, the blessed silence he'd been born into, hadn't been protecting them from it.

It had been hiding them from it. Hiding them the way a stone hides a beetle — not out of love, but because the stone doesn't know the beetle is there, and doesn't care.

Out here, in the loud bright world, they could be seen. By the dead. By the land. By whatever rode the white horses and wore faces like unfinished sketches.

"We can't go back," Jonah said. It wasn't a decision. It was arithmetic. "Can we."

Nell took his hand. Her grip was surprisingly strong, and her palm, for the first time since he'd known her, was warm.

* * *

They walked north through the afternoon, following a road that was barely a road — two ruts and a memory, more moss than stone — while Jonah relearned how to live inside his own skin.

It came back in waves. Nell's blanket-silence held, mostly, but it frayed at the edges whenever they passed something that had died hard. A gallows hill, an hour out, nearly took him off his feet again: the oak at its crest had grown straight up through the graves of the hanged, and its roots were threaded with their vertebrae, and even muffled behind Nell's wall they *sang* — a low, grinding chord of old injustice that made his teeth ache. Nell took one look at his face, marched up the hill alone, laid her hand flat against the bark, and stood there for a long minute with her head bowed, like a child apologizing on behalf of the whole world.

When she came back down, the chord had softened. Not silenced. Softened. As if someone had finally listened.

"You're going to be the death of me," Jonah told her hoarsely, "or possibly the opposite."

She smiled. It was the first full smile he'd seen from her — gap-toothed, lopsided, devastating — and he felt it land somewhere under his ribs and take root, and he thought, with a kind of dread entirely separate from Justiciars and erasing winters: *oh no. Oh no, I love her already. That was fast. That was dangerously fast.*

They made camp at dusk in the lee of a fallen wall, and Jonah discovered the second price of the loud world: out here, his carvings were not keepsakes. They were *beacons*.

He had the basket open barely a quarter hour, checking on them, when the sparrow began to whisper. Not to him — around him, outward, its dead soldier's breath seeping into the evening air like warmth from an oven. And the night answered. Things rustled in the hedgerow. Something vast and patient turned its attention toward their little fire from very far away, the way a man in a dark house turns his head toward a match struck in another room.

Jonah wrapped the sparrow in three layers of wool and buried it at the bottom of the basket, and the attention withdrew, slow and reluctant, like a hand pulled back from a door handle.

"They can hear them," he murmured. "Out here, the carvings — everyone can hear them. Everything."

Nell nodded, unsurprised, and then did something that undid him completely. She dug through the basket, chose the little insulting hen, and set it upright on the flat stone beside the fire, facing outward, like a sentry. Then she patted its head with one finger.

The hen, muffled and indignant, clucked once.

"She says we're fools," Jonah translated. "She says the fire's too smoky and my bedroll smells of sheep."

Nell giggled — a bright, startling sound, the first he'd heard from her — and for a few minutes the camp was almost cozy, the fire crackling, the hen muttering complaints, the dead beyond Nell's wall reduced to a distant tide.

Then the tide changed.

Jonah felt it before he understood it: a shift in the ocean-sound, the way a beach feels the water pull back before the wave comes. Every dead thing within miles — the gallows oak, the ford's drowned toll-keeper, the nameless ash in the willows, thousands upon thousands of voices — all of them went quiet at once.

Not resting. *Listening.*

And then, from everywhere and nowhere, from the ground and the dark and the spaces between, they began to say one word. Thousands of throats that had no throats, speaking in unison, in a language Jonah didn't know and yet understood perfectly, the way you understand falling:

*Thorn. Thorn. Thorn. Thorn.*

Nell had gone rigid. Her shadow stood up off the ground without her, tall against the firelight, and faced north.

Far off down the black road, where the hills folded together, a light appeared. Small. White. Moving.

Then another.

Then a third.

The hen, in a voice gone suddenly grave, said: "*Boy. Pick up the child. Run.*"
