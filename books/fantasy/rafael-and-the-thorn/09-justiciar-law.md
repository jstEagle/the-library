## 9. Justiciar Law

The men in white came at the hour when the bread was rising and the fire was low, and they did not knock.

Nell felt them before she saw them — a coldness like the memory of cold, arriving through the walls of the ferryman's house the way water finds the crack in a boat. Her thoughts, which lived mostly in the old tongue these days, went very still inside her, the way small animals go still when the hawk's shadow crosses the grass.

*Vessarun*, she thought. *They come. The white ones come.*

The old word for Justiciar was older than that — *vessarun*, the law-that-walks, the ones her mother had spoken of only in whispers, pressing the bone token into Nell's hand and saying *run* without ever saying why. Nell had run. She had run across a country that kept forgetting her name, and now the running had stopped at this door, because the door had opened before anyone could knock, and three figures stood in it wearing faces like sketches someone had abandoned halfway through.

"Good evening," said the one in front. His voice was kind. That was the terrible thing about it. It was the voice of a man who had never once raised it in anger, because anger required wanting something for yourself, and he wanted nothing except what his duty wanted. "We're looking for a child."

The ferryman, Bren — who claimed to have known Jonah's mother, or claimed to have known someone, or perhaps only claimed things because claiming was how lonely men filled their houses — stepped between them and the hearth where Nell sat. "There's no child here."

"There is," said the Justiciar gently. "There always is. That's rather the point of children." He looked past Bren's shoulder, past the hanging herbs, past the sleeping dog, and found Nell as easily as a lighthouse finds a ship. "Come along, little one. No one will hurt you. We are not in the business of hurting. We are in the business of *healing*."

Nell did not move. Jonah, who had been mending a net by the fire and pretending very hard to be an ordinary man doing ordinary things, set down the net slowly, the way you set down something you might need to throw.

"She's my niece," Jonah said. "Mute. Simple. She's no use to anyone."

"Everything is of use to someone," said the Justiciar, "or no use at all. Those are the only two categories, in the end." He smiled at Nell, and his smile was unfinished too, like the rest of him — a smile drawn by someone who remembered that smiles existed but had forgotten what they were for. "You'll come with us. Just for a little while. Just to be sure. And then—" he spread his sketch-hands, "—you'll be free to go wherever people go."

*Nessavar*, thought Nell. *The lie that walks on two legs.* But even as she thought it, some quieter part of her — the part that had watched the world forget whole farmsteads into blankness — understood that he did not think of it as a lie at all. He believed it. He believed in mercy the way other men believed in gods: completely, and without evidence, and in ways that hurt.

She stood up. She took Jonah's hand for one moment — his hand was shaking, poor bone-carver, poor frightened kind man — and then she walked toward the white riders, because she had learned already that running from them made the running worse, and because somewhere under her fear a small stubborn Vessian voice reminded her: *a witness goes where the witnessing is.*

* * *

They did not bind her. They did not need to. They put her in a wagon with no sides, on a seat with no rails, and rolled through the winter-dark countryside, and Nell understood that the absence of bars was itself the cage: there was nowhere in the world to run that the forgetting would not follow.

The Justiciar who had spoken rode beside the wagon. He seemed to feel that silence was rude.

"You're wondering about our faces," he said, conversationally, as if they were two neighbors discussing weather over a fence. "People do. We gave them up when we took the oath. A face is a thing that wants, little one. It frowns when it shouldn't and smiles when it mustn't and it remembers grudges the way a riverbed remembers rain. We put ours aside so that we could carry out the law evenly, for everyone, forever." He turned his unfinished profile toward her. "It's a great relief, actually. You should try it sometime."

Nell looked at him and thought, in the old tongue, *poor man*, and was surprised by her own thought. She had expected monsters. Monsters were easy. Monsters could be hated cleanly, the way winter can be hated. But this was not a monster; this was a man who had amputated himself and called the wound a virtue, and who genuinely, sincerely, down to the last unfinished corner of him, believed he was helping her.

"You've been through something terrible," he continued, mistaking her stare for trauma, which was fair enough. "Whatever you saw, whatever you remember — and I suspect you remember quite a lot — none of it needs to stay with you. Memory is heavy. We lighten loads. That's all we do. That's all we've ever done." He said it with such tenderness that Nell's chest ached. "The world was sick with an old war once. Do you know how wars end? Not with swords. With forgetting. Someone has to hold the broom after the feast, little one. Someone has to sweep."

*Sweep*, thought Nell. *He calls my country dust.*

But she kept her face smooth, because her mother had taught her that much before the end: *the witness watches, and watching is a weapon too.*

* * *

They stopped at a village just past midnight — a huddle of houses around a well, smoke standing straight up from chimneys in the windless cold. The Justiciars had been summoned, the lead rider explained to no one in particular, by a report. Reports were how the law heard the world now; the law had no ears of its own anymore, having given those up along with everything else.

They brought the elder out of the largest house. He was old, bent like a question mark, wrapped in a blanket against the cold, and he was weeping quietly, without shame, the way old men weep when they have outlived the energy for pride.

"He sang it," a woman was saying, wringing her hands. "At the burial. Old Tam's burial. He sang the — the song. The one from—"

"Don't say it," whispered another villager.

"I don't even know what to say! There's nothing to say! There's never been any such place!"

"That's right," said the lead Justiciar warmly. "There hasn't. And yet he sang."

They set up the trial in the village square, and it was the strangest court Nell had ever seen, though she had seen courts — real ones, glass-floored and singing, where the law itself had a voice like bells. This court had no floor but frost, no voice but the Justiciar's, and no judge but a book.

The book was white. Everything about them was white. The lead Justiciar opened it on a small table brought from someone's kitchen, and the pages were blank, and he read from them anyway.

"Tomas Ellery," he said. "Village elder. Sixty-one years. Accused of retention." He looked up, and his unfinished eyes were full of something that in a complete face might have been sorrow. "Tomas. You know what the charge means."

"I sang a song," the old man said. "My mother sang it to me. Her mother sang it to her. It's a song about — about going home. About a river that—" He stopped. His face worked. "I can't say it. Isn't that funny? I can't say the name of the river. But I can sing the tune. The tune stayed. The words go, but the tune stays, like a smell in a coat."

"The tune is the problem," said the Justiciar gently. "Words fade on their own. Tunes don't. Tunes live in the body, and the body is a stubborn archive." He turned a blank page. "Do you understand why we can't allow it?"

"No," said Tomas simply. "I understand that you won't. I don't understand why. It's a lullaby. It never hurt anyone. It's about sleep, and safety, and a mother's voice. What war does that start?"

And here the Justiciar did something Nell had not expected. He closed the book. He came around the table, and he knelt — knelt, in the frost, in his white uniform, so that his unfinished face was level with the old man's bent one — and he spoke softly, so softly that Nell had to lean forward on her unsided wagon-seat to hear.

"Tomas. Before the erasure, there was a war that lasted ninety years. Ninety. Your grandfather's grandfather was born in it and your grandmother's grandmother died in it and neither of them ever knew a single year of peace. Whole generations who never once heard a night without screaming in it. And it ended. Do you know how it ended? Not because anyone won. Because everyone agreed to stop remembering why they fought. All of them. Every side. They paid everything they had — every song, every story, every name of every dead soldier — and they bought peace with it. Real peace. Your village has had sixty years without a single burned roof. Children here have grown up never once hiding in a cellar." His voice caught, or performed catching; Nell could not tell the difference, and suspected he couldn't either. "Every time you hum that tune, you spend a coin from that purchase. And the coins are nearly gone, Tomas. Can't you feel it? The winter coming early. The frost in autumn. The world is calling in its debts. And if the account runs empty—" he shook his head, "—then everything comes back. Every grudge. Every grave. Every reason. And the peace dies, and it takes everyone you love with it, and it will be your fault, yours personally, because you couldn't let one lullaby die with the rest."

The square was silent. Even the wind held off.

"So you see," the Justiciar said, rising, brushing frost from his knee, "we are not cruel. Cruelty would be letting you keep it. Cruelty would be letting the world burn again for the sake of a melody. We are the opposite of cruel. We are the most merciful institution mankind has ever built, and we are hated for it, and we accept that hatred, because hatred is just love pointed the wrong way, and we have room in our hearts for that too."

*Tessavara*, thought Nell, and the old word meant something like *the trap built of kindness*, and her mother had used it about many things — about sweet medicine, about flattering kings, about the whole gentle machinery of forgetting. But she had never used it with such precision as it fit here.

Because here was the horror, and Nell saw it clearly, six years old and older than the hills: the Justiciar was *right*. Or half-right, which was worse than wrong. The peace was real. The sixty years were real. Somewhere under the erasure there really had been ninety years of war, and someone — maybe everyone — had chosen forgetting as the price of their grandchildren's sleep. Who was she, the last witness of a drowned country, to say the price had been too high? Maybe it hadn't. Maybe her whole nation had agreed to die so that strangers' children could sleep soundly.

Maybe. Except.

Except that nobody had asked *her*. Except that her mother had pressed a bone token into her hand and said *run*, and mothers do not say *run* when they have consented. Except that the elder's mother had sung the lullaby to him in secret, generation after generation, passing the tune down like contraband, like a candle carried cupped through a long dark — and people do not smuggle what they have freely given away.

"They didn't all agree," Nell said.

She had not meant to speak. Her common tongue came out rusty and small, and every head in the square turned to her, and Jonah, standing at the edge of the crowd where the Justiciars had permitted him to watch, went pale as milk.

The lead Justiciar looked at her for a long moment. Then he smiled his unfinished smile.

"There she is," he said softly. "There's the retention." He turned back to Tomas. "You see? This is what it looks like when it spreads. One tune becomes a voice, a voice becomes a memory, a memory becomes a country, and a country becomes a war. It's not malice, Tomas. It's just — gravity." He sighed, and gathered his book, and Nell braced herself for the sentence.

But the sentence, when it came, was the cruelest thing yet, because it was gentle.

"We're not going to punish you," the Justiciar told the old man. "Punishment implies guilt, and guilt implies choice, and you didn't choose this — the tune chose you, the way moss chooses a north wall. We're going to *treat* you." He gestured, and one of the other riders produced a small white instrument, thin as a tuning fork. "It doesn't hurt. I promise you that. Most men say afterward that it's like setting down a weight they'd forgotten they were carrying. You'll sleep better. Everyone sleeps better. That's rather our whole trade."

"No," said Tomas. Not loudly. Just *no*, the way a tree says no to the axe, without hope and without apology. "It's my mother's voice. It's the last of her voice. Take anything else."

"We will," said the Justiciar kindly. "That's what I'm telling you. We'll take anything else. We're taking everything else, everywhere, forever. The fork is just faster."

And Nell, watching, understood the final shape of it — the shape her mother had died trying to show her. The Justiciars were not wolves. Wolves take because they hunger. These men took because they *loved* — loved the world, loved peace, loved sleeping children — and they had loved so hard and so long that they had sawn off every part of themselves that could object. You cannot argue with a wolf. But you also cannot argue with a man who has removed the parts of himself that could be argued with. He will kneel in the frost for you. He will speak gently. He will promise you mercy with his whole hollow heart. And then he will touch the fork to your mother's last remaining voice, and watch you with genuine compassion while it dies, and mean every tender thing he says, and sleep perfectly well that night.

*This*, thought Nell, gripping the bone token through her dress, *this is what we ran from. Not cruelty. Kindness with the soul taken out of it.*

The fork touched the old man's temple. He stiffened. His lips moved — following the tune one last time, silently, a conductor finishing the piece — and then his hands dropped, and his face smoothed, and he looked around the square with mild, pleasant confusion, like a man waking from a nap.

"What a cold night," he said cheerfully. "Someone should fetch me inside. Whose burial was it, anyway?"

The villagers wept. The Justiciars packed their instruments with the care of nurses putting away tools. And the lead rider turned to Nell, and his unfinished face grew somehow more unfinished, as though looking at her completed some missing line and erased another.

"And now, little one," he said, "it's your turn. You've seen how painless it is. You've seen we keep our promises. So I'm going to ask you one time, nicely, the way I'd want someone to ask me: will you give us what you're carrying? Or shall we find it ourselves?"

Behind him, at the edge of the square, Jonah was moving — edging along the fence line, hand drifting toward the long carving-knife at his belt, his face doing something desperate and stupid and brave. Nell met his eye and gave the smallest shake of her head: *not yet, not yet.*

Because she had just realized something, watching the fork work, watching the tune die: the instrument erased what it touched. Which meant it could not touch what it could not find. And the bone token against her skin was warm, warmer than it had ever been, humming faintly, like a door remembering it was a door.

She smiled at the Justiciar — her first lie, and it fit her face perfectly, because her face was finished.

"All right," she said. "I'll show you what I'm carrying."

And she reached into her dress, and drew out the bone, and held it up to the light — and every Justiciar in the square took one step backward, all together, like men recoiling from a snake.
