## 10. Escape by Bone-Light

The ferryman's map was drawn on a scrap of hide, and it had exactly one instruction on it: *Ask the river nicely.*

Jonah stared at it for a long time after the old man shuffled off into the reeds, and then he looked at the river itself — broad, brown, unhurried, carrying a skin of ice at its edges like a coat it hadn't decided to put on yet.

"Nicely," he said aloud.

Behind him, Nell tugged his sleeve and pointed at the water, then made a gesture he'd come to know well: her fingers curling like something blooming. *It's listening,* she meant. She'd been making that gesture since they left the valley, and Jonah had learned that when she made it about a thing, the thing usually was.

"All right." He crouched at the bank, aware of how ridiculous he must look, a grown man in a patched coat addressing a frozen river like a neighbor over a fence. "Good evening. I don't suppose you take passengers."

The river did not answer. Rivers rarely do, in words. But the ice at the edge stopped growing, which Jonah had begun to understand was the river's way of clearing its throat.

He explained their situation as best he could without saying any of the dangerous parts. Two travelers. A long way to go. Pursuers on white horses whose faces looked like sketches someone had abandoned halfway through. He kept his voice low and reasonable, the way you'd talk to a dog you suspected of being smarter than you.

When he finished, the current changed. Not much. Just enough that the water began flowing *toward* the bank instead of past it, folding inward like a hand beckoning.

And then she rose out of it.

She was not what Jonah expected, though he couldn't have said what he'd expected instead. She was tall and dripping and made mostly of suggestions — hair like weed, eyes like two coins at the bottom of a well, a mouth that smiled with too many opinions behind it. She wore a gown of silt and stood on the water as if it were a floor she owned, which, he supposed, she did.

"You're the bone-man," she said. Her voice was the sound water makes under ice, if the ice were gossiping. "The one who hears. The little dead things whisper about you all up and down my length. *Bone-man, bone-man, he listens, he doesn't interrupt.* Do you know how rare that is? The dead are terrible conversationalists. They repeat themselves."

"I've noticed," Jonah said carefully.

"They also lie," the spirit said, delighted. "Everyone assumes the dead are honest because they're dead. No, no. Death just gives them more time to rehearse." She drifted closer, and the cold coming off her smelled like deep places, like stones that had never seen sun. "You want passage downstream. Past the shallows where the King's men watch. Past the ford where they're burning letters. All the way down to the drowned city, or near enough."

"The city that isn't there," Jonah said.

"If you like. It's there. It simply isn't *admitted* to be there, which is a different problem entirely." She tilted her head, and her coin-eyes fixed on him with an interest that made his skin prickle. "I can carry you. My way is quiet. My way is safe. But nothing is free, bone-man, and you already know what I want."

"I imagine you'll tell me."

"Three years." She held up three fingers, webbed with light. "Three years of your hearing. You walk into my water with all those voices crowding your skull, and every dead thing for fifty miles will feel you coming like a bonfire in a dark field. The white riders hunt by listening too, you know. Not the way you listen. Cruder. But loud is loud." She smiled. "Give me three years, and you go down my river deaf as a stone. Quiet as a stone. Safe as a stone. And in three years' time, if you're still alive, your gift comes back to you, all of it, everything you heard in between included. I keep excellent accounts."

Three years. Jonah thought of the carvings in his pack — the ones that still whispered, the woman dead two hundred years who had named Nell *the Thorn*, the soldier, the miller's wife, all of them murmuring their last words against his spine like a bundle of sleeping birds. Three years of silence. Three years of walking alone in a way he hadn't been alone since he was a boy, before the first bone ever spoke to him.

He thought of Nell, barefoot in snow, unafraid of cold, waiting behind him with her patient blooming hands.

"What happens to the voices while I can't hear them?" he asked. "Do they—"

"Sleep," the spirit said, and for a moment her smile softened into something almost kind. "They rest. It's not cruel, what I'm offering. Ask anyone who's lived with ghosts for forty years whether a nap sounds bad." She extended her hand, and water ran off her fingers upward, which was the sort of thing Jonah decided not to think about too hard. "Well? Nicely?"

He took her hand.

It didn't hurt. That was the strange part. It felt like sinking into a warm bath after a day of hauling wood, like setting down a load so heavy he'd forgotten he was carrying it. The murmur that had lived behind his ears since childhood — the soft chorus of last words, last wishes, last jokes — simply... stopped. The silence rushed in so fast and so complete that he staggered, and Nell caught his arm with surprising strength.

For the first time in thirty years, Jonah Whitlock heard nothing but the living world: wind, water, his own breath, and the small sound of a child watching him with enormous worried eyes.

"There," the spirit said briskly, already dissolving back into the current. "Downstream, half a mile, you'll find a boat. Don't name it. Don't thank it. And bone-man—" her voice came from everywhere now, from the water and the ice and the mud, "—be nice to my passengers. They've had a long week."

* * *

The boat was a coffin-boat.

Jonah had heard of them — the river folk built them for the poor, a coffin wide enough for two, fitted with a crude mast and a tiller, so that a body could be sent downstream to burial in some dignity even when the family couldn't afford the road. This one was older, its wood gone silver-grey, its lid propped open like a jaw.

Inside lay four corpses.

They were fresh enough to be offended. The nearest one, a heavyset man with a fisherman's beard and a bruise across half his face, opened his eyes as Jonah leaned over the gunwale, and sighed the sigh of a man whose evening has just gotten worse.

"Oh, wonderful," the corpse said. "A passenger."

"You can talk," Jonah said, before remembering that he couldn't hear anything anymore.

Except he could. Which was interesting, and alarming, and something he filed away to panic about later: the spirit had taken the dead who lived in his bones, the carried dead, the archived dead. These dead belonged to the river. Apparently her jurisdiction ended at the waterline.

"He can talk," the second corpse agreed. This one was a young woman, laid out neatly with her hands folded, wearing the expression of someone enduring a family dinner. "Of course he can talk. Everyone can talk. Nobody can *listen*, that's the problem. Are you going to get in or are you going to stand there gawking? Because the tide doesn't wait, and neither does the frost, and I, personally, would like to arrive at my burial sometime this decade."

"Nesta," the bearded man said wearily. "Manners."

"My manners drowned with me, Aldric, along with the rest of me, in case you've forgotten."

"We haven't forgotten. You mention it hourly."

Jonah helped Nell into the boat. She climbed among the corpses without a flicker of fear — of course she did; she'd never once been afraid of the dead, only of the living things that hunted her — and settled herself in the narrow space between the third body, an old man with a peaceful face, and the fourth, which was small.

Too small.

Nell went very still beside it. Then she reached out and tucked the boat's spare blanket more closely around the little form, the way you'd cover a sleeping child, and Jonah understood without hearing anything at all.

"Fever," Aldric said quietly, all the grumbling gone out of his voice. "From the mill village. Her father paid for the boat with his winter boots. He's walking the towpath alongside us as far as the ford." He nodded toward the bank, where indeed a thin figure trudged through the snow in the dark, keeping pace, unwilling to let his daughter travel even this far unaccompanied. "He asked us to keep her company. We said we would. We keep our word, us river dead. It's about all we've got left."

Jonah got in. The coffin-boat was built for the dead, who complain less about legroom, and his knees were immediately, decisively unhappy. Nell leaned against his side. The old man with the peaceful face turned his head — a slow, creaking motion, like a door in a house nobody visited — and studied them both with milky eyes.

"A bone-carver," he said. "And a Vessian child." His voice was dry as autumn leaves. "My name was Tam. I was a schoolteacher, sixty years, and I know trouble when it climbs into my boat. You're the trouble everyone's whispering about. The Thorn."

"I don't know what that means," Jonah said honestly.

"No," Tam agreed. "But she does." He closed his eyes again, settling back as the boat slid away from the bank, gathering the current. "Well. Keep your head down, both of you. We've an agreement with the river, and part of the agreement is that we arrive quietly. No singing. No arguing. And Nesta—"

"I know, I know."

"*No* telling the bone-man how you died."

"It was a *good story*—"

"It was a *long* story, and we have a night of water ahead of us."

* * *

The river at night was a country unto itself.

Jonah sat with his back against the coffin wall, Nell asleep against his chest, and watched the banks slide past — dark fields, dark trees, and here and there a farmhouse window glowing orange, full of people who had no idea that erasure was rolling toward them like weather. Above, the stars burned hard and cold. The winter was getting worse. Even Jonah, deaf now to the dead, could feel it: a pressure in the air, a whiteness gathering at the edges of things, as if the world were a page and something vast was rubbing it out from the corners inward.

The corpses, deprived of Nesta's storytelling, had found other entertainment.

"—all I'm saying," Aldric was muttering, "is that if you'd married the cooper like your mother wanted, you wouldn't have been on the dock at all, and you wouldn't have drowned, and I wouldn't have had to hear about the cooper for the entire voyage."

"The cooper had *hands like hams*, Aldric."

"Better than the smuggler you did marry, whose hands apparently were in everyone's pockets."

"That's slander. I'm dead. You can't slander the dead."

"You've proven otherwise for six miles."

The old man, Tam, chuckled softly, a sound like pages turning. Jonah found, to his surprise, that he was smiling. There was something deeply, absurdly comforting about it — bickering like this, warm and human and pointless, in a boat full of death on a river running toward the end of the world. The dead were people too. Annoying ones. Stubborn, funny, grieving ones. He'd known that his whole life; it was the whole reason for his gift, maybe. Somebody ought to listen to them. Somebody ought to know they were still in there.

He wished, suddenly and fiercely, that he could hear his own bones right now. The woman dead two hundred years. The soldier. The miller's wife. Were they sleeping, as the spirit promised? Did they dream? Would they know him when the three years were up, or would the silence have worn them smooth?

A small hand patted his chest. Nell, awake, looking up at him. She pointed at his ear, then made a question of her eyebrows.

"It's all right," he whispered. "Quiet. Just quiet."

Her brow furrowed. She didn't believe him — she was six, possibly seven, and wise in ways that made Jonah's chest ache — but she let it go. Instead she reached into her shirt and drew out the bone token her mother had pressed into her hand, in a country that no longer existed, on a morning that no longer existed either. She held it up so the starlight caught it.

Then she pressed it briefly against each of the corpses in turn, the way you might greet people at a door, and finally against Jonah's hand.

Blessing them, he realized. Or introducing them. In her language, maybe, there wasn't a difference.

Tam opened his eyes. For a moment the old teacher looked at the token, and his peaceful face grew troubled. "That mark," he said slowly. "I saw that mark once, long ago, on a traveler who passed through our town. A woman. Traveling alone, quick and quiet, like someone always listening behind her." He looked at Jonah. "She had your chin, boy. Or you have hers. One of the two."

Jonah's mouth went dry. "When? When was this?"

"Before you were born, I'd say, by the look of you. Going south." Tam's milky eyes drifted shut again. "Toward the Door, if the stories are true. Funny thing, memory. The river keeps what the land forgets. Ask the water sometime why that is."

The boat rocked gently. Somewhere upstream, faint and far, Jonah thought he heard hoofbeats — or maybe it was only ice cracking, only the river talking in its sleep. Nell's hand found his in the dark and gripped it tight.

Ahead, around the next bend, the water ran black and smooth toward the ford where the Justiciars were burning letters — and beyond it, past the shallows, past the watchfires, down toward a drowned city that waited in the reflections, holding its breath.

"Hold on to something," Aldric advised cheerfully. "The rapids start here, and Nesta screams."

"I do *not* scream. I narrate. There's a difference."

The coffin-boat tipped over the first fall of white water, and the night swallowed them whole.
