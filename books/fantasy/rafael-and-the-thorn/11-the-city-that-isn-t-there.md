## 11. The City That Isn't There

The road had been leading them downhill for three days, and Jonah had stopped trusting it.

Roads, in his experience, went where people wanted them to go. This one seemed to go wherever it pleased — appearing out of frost-killed grass when Nell walked ahead of him, sinking back into the earth like a shy animal when he tried to walk it alone. He'd tested this twice. Both times the path had simply... declined to exist under his boots, leaving him floundering through bracken while Nell watched from a stretch of ancient paving with an expression he was beginning to recognize as her version of laughter. She didn't laugh, exactly. Her whole face did something instead — eyebrows up, lips pressed tight, eyes bright with a mischief she was too dignified to release.

"You're enjoying this," he said, untangling his coat from a hawthorn.

She shrugged in a way that meant *obviously.*

The dead in his satchel had opinions about it too. Old Marta, the miller's wife who'd died in chapter — no, who'd died some sixty years back complaining about her son-in-law — muttered from inside her carved knucklebone that roads which only worked for one person were roads with secrets, and secrets were how you ended up drowned in your own well. Jonah had learned to take Marta's counsel with a grain of salt, since she'd also once predicted the end of the world over a bad harvest.

But even Marta had gone quiet as they descended into the valley of the lake.

It wasn't a dramatic silence. It was more like walking into a church, or a sickroom — the sense that noise would be unwelcome. The trees thinned. The air grew cold and glassy-smelling, like the moment just before snowfall, except the sky above them was clear and blue and utterly empty of birds. Not one. Not even a crow. Jonah hadn't noticed until he tried to point one out to Nell and realized there was nothing left alive up there to point at.

Then the land opened, and there was the lake.

Jonah had seen lakes before. He'd lived his whole life within sight of water, in one form or another. But this lake was wrong in a way his bones understood before his mind caught up: it did not move. Not a ripple, not a shiver, not so much as a breath of wind across its surface. It lay in the basin of the valley like a pane of grey glass set into the earth, so still that it looked less like water and more like a hole in the world with sky at the bottom.

Nell stopped at the shore and stood very straight, the way she did when she was remembering something she couldn't say.

"Nell," Jonah said carefully. "What is this place?"

She turned and looked at him — that look again, the one that said *you'll see* without any words at all. Then she took off her shoes.

"Nell—"

She waded in to her ankles, unbothered by the cold the way she was unbothered by all cold, and looked down at the surface. And Jonah, standing on the shore, saw the city.

He would spend the rest of his life failing to describe what happened next, and every attempt would come out sounding like madness. There were towers in the lake. White towers, slender as candles, rising out of the reflection — or rather, the reflection showed towers where the empty sky should have been mirrored, and the towers were *in* the mirror the way a fish is in a stream, moving slightly, alive, present. Streets spread beneath the surface like veins of silver. Bridges arced between rooftops. And people — small, distant, going about their business along the reflected quays, hauling reflected carts, hanging reflected washing between reflected windows.

A city, entire and bustling, that existed only upside down and underwater and nowhere else at all.

"Marta," Jonah whispered, "are you seeing this?"

For the first time in two hundred years of death, Old Marta had nothing to say.

* * *

They walked the shore for the rest of the morning, because Nell insisted — tugging his sleeve, pointing, insistent as a magpie. And everywhere she pointed, the city answered. Where she stood, the reflection deepened: streets grew clearer, crowds denser, the towers taller. When Jonah walked alone along the bank, the vision dimmed, the way a face goes vague when you stop concentrating on it.

"It follows you," he said. "Or... you hold it up. Like carrying a lantern."

Nell nodded, then hesitated. She crouched at the waterline, traced something in the mud with her finger — a shape, deliberate, practiced. A circle crossed by a single line, like an eye shut by one stroke.

"What is it?"

She looked up at him, frustrated, hands opening and closing. She knew the word. He could see her knowing it, could see the word sitting right behind her teeth in whatever old language she dreamed in, and could see her reaching for its common-tongue cousin and finding nothing there. Finally she pressed both palms flat against her chest, then swept them outward, wide, embracing the whole lake, the whole hidden country.

"Vessia," Jonah said quietly. It wasn't a guess. He'd heard the word somewhere — in the bones, maybe, in some half-heard syllable from a dead man's last breath. It had never meant anything before. Now it sat in the air between them like a stone dropped into still water.

Nell's whole body changed. She stared at him, and her eyes filled, and she didn't cry — she was too proud, or too old inside, for crying — but she nodded once, hard, and pressed her hand flat against the mud beside her drawing, as if sealing a promise.

That was when Jonah made his mistake. He leaned down to look more closely at her drawing, and his own reflection leaned down with him.

Except it wasn't his.

The face looking up at him out of the lake was his face the way a song is its own tune played in another key. The jaw was broader. The cheekbones stood higher, sharper, angled in a way he'd never seen in any village mirror. The eyes — his eyes, but shaped differently, tilted slightly at the corners, the kind of eyes he'd seen on old coins and painted on the prows of river-boats in stories about countries far away. His hair in the reflection fell differently. Even his posture was different — the reflected man crouched with a straightness, a certainty, that Jonah had never once felt in his life.

And the reflected man was wearing clothes Jonah had never owned. Grey and white, fine-woven, with a collar worked in that same symbol Nell had drawn in the mud — the closed eye.

Jonah scrambled backward off his heels, heart slamming.

"That's not—" He stopped. Started again. "Marta. Tell me I'm tired. Tell me I've been eating strange mushrooms."

Marta's voice came faintly from the satchel, thin and shaken: *"That's not you, carver."*

"Thank you."

*"That's someone else wearing your face."*

"That's worse."

* * *

They made camp that night in the lee of a fallen tower — a real one, half-swallowed by the hillside, its stones fitted together so finely that Jonah couldn't have slid a knife-blade between them. No mortar. No chisel marks. Stone grown, not built. While he fumbled with the fire, Nell sat facing the lake, cross-legged, watching the city lights come on beneath the water as the sky darkened. Windows, hundreds of them, glowing soft gold in the reflection. Somewhere under that still surface, a whole civilization was lighting its evening lamps for no one.

He brought her roasted turnip and the last of the bread. She ate mechanically, eyes never leaving the water.

"You know this place," Jonah said. It wasn't a question anymore; he'd given up on questions with her. Statements worked better. "You lived here. Or somewhere like it."

A small nod.

"And the Justiciars took it." Another nod, slower. "And they're trying to make sure nobody remembers it ever existed."

This time she didn't nod. She picked up a stick and wrote in the dirt, laboriously, in the common letters he'd shown her during the long road days: **STILL HERE.**

Jonah looked at the lake, at the lamplit windows burning faithfully beneath the water, and felt something twist in his chest.

"Yeah," he said. "I see that."

She erased the word with her palm and wrote again: **NOT DEAD. WAITING.**

"WAITING FOR WHAT?"

She looked at him for a long moment. Then she pointed — not at the city, not at herself.

At him.

"Don't do that," Jonah said. "Whatever you mean by that, don't do it. I'm a bone-carver from Harrow Vale. My mother's grave is on the hill above the chapel. I've got a chisel my master gave me and a bad knee and—" His voice cracked, embarrassingly. He steadied it. "I've got a bad knee. That's the whole of me. Whatever you think I am, I'm not."

Nell considered this. Then, with the terrible practicality of children, she reached over and tapped the satchel where the bones slept.

*Marta,* Jonah translated wearily. *She wants Marta's opinion.*

He drew out the old woman's knucklebone and cupped it in his palm. "Marta. The girl wants to know what you see when you look at me. Be honest. You've never once managed otherwise."

The bone warmed in his hand. Marta's voice came slow, reluctant, like a woman being asked to repeat gossip she'd sworn she wouldn't:

*"Two hundred years I've been dead, carver, and I've heard ten thousand last words, if you count the ones you've told me secondhand. Every soul that comes through your hands sounds the same. Valley-born. Common-tongued. Dying in common ways."* A pause. *"But you — the first day you held me, I heard something underneath you. Underneath your voice. A hum. Like a word being spoken a long way off, in a room I couldn't enter. I thought it was your gift. I thought all you hearers had it."*

"I don't understand," Jonah said.

*"No," said Marta. "But she does." And the bone went quiet, in the particular stubborn way Marta went quiet when a conversation had gotten away from her.*

Jonah sat back. The fire popped. Out on the lake, the golden windows burned on, patient as stars, and Nell watched him with an expression that wasn't pity, exactly. It was closer to recognition — the look you give someone when they finally arrive somewhere you've been waiting a long time for them to reach.

"I need to sleep," Jonah announced to no one in particular, and lay down facing away from the water, and did not sleep at all.

* * *

In the grey hour before dawn, Nell woke him by shaking his shoulder, urgent, and pulling him toward the shore.

The mist lay thick over the lake — real mist, the first thing that had moved on that water since they'd arrived. And in the mist, the city had come up out of the reflection. Not fully. Not solidly. But the towers stood ghost-pale above the surface now, and the bridges, and here and there a street lamp still burning, and figures — translucent, unhurried — walking the quays as though morning in Vessia were simply overlapping with morning in the world, the way two conversations overlap in a market square.

Nell was gripping his hand hard enough to hurt. She was pointing, and her finger was shaking.

Not at the towers. Not at the crowds.

At a woman standing at the near quay, closest to shore, closest to them. Grey-cloaked. Dark-haired. Standing very still among all that gentle motion, and facing the shore, and facing them, as though she had been waiting there all night, as though she had walked out of her house in the reflected dark and come down to the water's edge the moment she heard them arrive.

Jonah knew her.

Not remembered her — *knew* her, the way you know the shape of your own hand in the dark. Some door in his chest that he'd kept locked his whole life, telling himself it was just a wall, just a scar, just the place where ordinary people keep ordinary nothing — that door blew open, and what came through it was the smell of bread and a lullaby in a language he didn't speak and arms around him and a voice saying words he suddenly, horribly, almost understood:

*Run fast, my love. Forget me kindly.*

The figure raised one hand. Not waving. Pressing it flat over her heart, the way Nell pressed hers to the mud beside her drawing.

The mist began to thin. The towers faded. The woman faded last, and she did not stop looking at him until there was nothing left of her but the impression of dark hair and grey cloth, and then not even that, and the lake lay flat and empty and silent as it had always been, and Jonah Whitlock of Harrow Vale stood on the shore holding a child's hand, shaking, knowing — knowing the way you know after a fever breaks — that everything he had been telling himself about who he was had just quietly, permanently, come apart.

"Nell," he said. His voice sounded like a stranger's. "There's a vault under that city, isn't there. That's where we're going."

She looked up at him. And for the first time since the night she'd knocked on his door, barefoot in the snow, Nell spoke.

One word. Rusty, careful, worn smooth by disuse — but common tongue, plain and clear, offered to him like a gift:

"Yes."

Then, after a pause, softer, almost apologetic:

"She's been waiting a long time."
