## 12. The Archive of Last Words

The way down was through the water, which was the first thing wrong about it.

"You want me to *walk into* the lake," Jonah said.

Nell stood at the shore with her arms crossed in that particular way she had — a six-year-old's imitation of a magistrate, learned from a country that no longer existed — and pointed again at the water. In its mirror-still surface, the towers of Vessia rose pale and impossible, and among them, at the base of the great glass court, a doorway stood open. Not reflected. Open. The reflection's door was dark where every other door in the drowned city showed lamplight behind it.

"It only works if you don't splash," Jonah translated from her gestures, badly. "That's what you're saying. There are rules."

Nell nodded once, gravely, as though he were the slow student.

He'd been talking to her for weeks now — to her, to the bones, to himself when the silence got too wide — and somewhere on the road that remembered itself under her feet, he'd stopped minding how it looked. That was new. The living had always found him unsettling; children especially. But this child had been raised by a dead country, and dead countries don't teach their children to flinch.

"All right," he said, and took off his boots, because even miracles deserve common sense. "But if I drown, I'm going to be extremely annoying about it afterward. You have no idea. I'll haunt you in rhyme."

The water was cold for exactly one step, and then it wasn't anything at all. It didn't wet him. It held him the way a held breath holds a word — carefully, temporarily, waiting to see what he'd say. Above him the sky wavered like heat-shimmer, and then they were walking down a long pale stair into the foundations of a city that had never finished being built, or had never quite finished being unbuilt, and Jonah understood with a lurch that the lake wasn't hiding Vessia.

The lake was Vessia's grave-marker. And graves, his whole life had taught him, keep talking if you know how to listen.

* * *

The vault opened off a corridor of white stone, and Jonah stopped on its threshold the way a man stops at the edge of a cliff he has dreamed about.

Shelves. Shelves upon shelves upon shelves, rising into a dimness that no lamp seemed to reach but that was not dark — lit faintly from within, the way his carvings glowed when a voice was strong in them. And on every shelf, rows upon rows of bones. Ribs and finger-bones and small delicate things he couldn't name, each one polished, each one labeled in a script like falling rain, each one —

Each one *singing.*

Not aloud. Not the way his valley-carvings whispered. This was a chorus heard through water, through years, through the erasure itself: ten thousand last words layered over one another until they weren't words anymore but weather. Grief as climate. He put a hand against the doorframe because his knees had opinions.

"Nell," he managed. "What is this place?"

She looked up at him with an expression he was coming to dread — the expression of someone about to hand him something heavy and trusting him to carry it. She touched her own chest. Then she swept her arm out at the shelves. Then she pressed both hands flat over her heart, and closed her eyes, and made a shape with her mouth that he realized was the old tongue's word for *kept.*

"The Archive of Last Words," a voice said behind him, "though we never called it that while there was anyone left to need it."

Jonah spun so fast he cracked his elbow on the stone.

The woman standing in the aisle between the shelves was translucent the way breath is translucent on a cold morning — there and shaped and made of nothing. She wore the grey robes of some office he didn't recognize, and she was looking at him with an expression he knew intimately, because he'd spent forty years seeing it on the faces of the living before they decided to leave the room.

Recognition. Followed by fear. Followed by something underneath both of those, soft and terrible.

"You can hear me," she said. Her voice frayed at the edges like cloth rubbed thin. "After all this time. Oh — oh, you *can hear me.*"

"I hear all of them," Jonah said, and hated how his voice shook. "It's my — it's what I do. Bones talk to me. They always have." He swallowed. "Who are you? What is this?"

"I am Warden Third Class Ede Marrow-Singer," the ghost said, with the crisp formality of someone reciting a title to a country that had forgotten titles, "Keeper of the Vault of Final Utterances, and I have been alone down here for" — she paused, and did arithmetic he could see cost her — "a very long time. And you, bone-carver, are going to want to sit down."

"I don't want to sit down."

"He's going to want to sit down," Ede told Nell.

Nell, who had already fetched a small stone bench from against the wall and was patting it with the patience of someone who had explained this exact thing to slower people before, agreed.

* * *

He sat. He was glad of it almost immediately.

"The spell of forgetting," Ede began, pacing the aisle with her hands folded, a lecturer's habit outliving the lecture hall, "does not destroy. Understand that. The Justiciars will tell you it does, and they may even believe it, because mercy is easier to administer when you lie to yourself about the dosage. But the spell cannot unmake what happened. It can only unmake the *knowing.* So every person Vessia lost — every soul unwritten, every name pulled out of the world like a thread from a hem — their final moment remained. Recorded. Held. A country's worth of dying, filed and shelved, waiting for someone with ears."

"And I have ears," Jonah said numbly.

"You have *the* ears." She stopped pacing. "Child, do you know why your gift exists? Bone-listening isn't common. It isn't a knack scattered through the population like red hair. It is a single inheritance, passed down one line, guarded by one family, for exactly one purpose: so that when the forgetting came — and we always knew it would come, someday, some king, some war — someone would be able to read the archive back. To give Vessia its last words. To make the country say what happened to it."

The chorus of ten thousand voices washed over him, and for the first time Jonah heard it not as noise but as *address.* All of it aimed forward. All of it aimed at him.

"My mother," he said. His mouth had gone dry. "My mother carved. She — I remember — I remember her hands. I remember her singing while she worked, and I remember thinking the songs were nonsense syllables, and I remember—"

He remembered nothing else. That was the shape of it, the hole he had carried his whole life without knowing it was a hole: a childhood with a beginning sawn clean off. A mother's hands, and then a valley, and then a life. He had assumed grief. Everyone grieves. He had never once assumed *surgery.*

"She brought you here," Ede said gently. "In the last winter. Through the Door Out. She was one of ours — a warden, like me, sworn to the archive. And when the riders came, she made the trade the Door demands. Memory for passage. Hers for yours."

"No." He heard himself say it from very far away. "No, she wouldn't — she wouldn't just *leave* me—"

"She didn't leave you." For the first time the warden's composure cracked, and what showed through was not pity but fury, old and banked and burning still. "She *stayed.* Do you understand the difference? The Door opens outward for the one who pays. But she paid double. She paid her memory of you to send you through — and then she walked back up these stairs and she stood at the Door and she *held it open,* because a Door held by love stays open, and she meant to hold it until the child was beyond any rider's reach. Until the forgetting closed over her too, and she forgot why she was standing there, and she kept standing there anyway, because the body remembers what the mind is made to lose."

The shelf nearest him was glowing brighter than the others. Jonah hadn't noticed. He noticed now.

One bone among thousands, a small curved rib, burned with a light like a coal under ash, and from it came a voice that cut through the whole drowning chorus as if the chorus had parted for it — a woman's voice, worn thin with effort, speaking not to the vault, not to the spell, not to history.

To him.

*"If you ever find your way back here — if the bones bring you home — I want you to know it wasn't a sacrifice. Everyone will tell you it was. Your father's people, if any of them remember, the wardens, the ghosts, all of them will call it a sacrifice and look solemn and be wrong. I got to be your mother for two years. Some women get a lifetime and squander it. I got two years and I spent every hour of them well. That's not a sacrifice, my darling. That's just arithmetic."*

A pause in the recording. A breath. And then, softer, the voice of a woman who knows she is speaking to a man and not a baby, and is doing it anyway:

*"You'll have my hands. I'm sorry about that — they'll unsettle people. But they'll also mean you're never lonely, not really, because the dead are excellent company once you stop being afraid of them. Be kind to the dead, Jonah. They're just people having a very bad day."*

The light faded. The chorus closed over the silence like water.

Jonah discovered he was on the floor. He had no memory of getting there. Nell was beside him, small and warm and solid, her hand flat on his back between his shoulder blades, patting in a slow rhythm — and he understood dimly that this too was a ritual, some Vessian comfort older than kingdoms, the gesture you made to someone whose world had just ended.

"She sang to me," he said. His voice came out wrecked. "When I was little. Before. I thought — I used to think the songs were nonsense. They were Vessian. She was singing Vessian lullabies to a baby she was about to forget, so that some part of me would — so that I'd—"

"So that you'd recognize them," Ede said quietly, "if you ever heard them again. Yes. Wardens plan in decades, bone-carver. We had to. It was the only weapon we had left: *preparation.*"

He laughed. It came out as something between a laugh and a sob, and he let it, because there was no one alive down here to see, and the dead, as his mother had apparently known he'd learn, are excellent company once you stop being afraid of them.

"Forty years," he said. "I've been doing this forty years. Talking to bones. Comforting strangers' grandmothers. Carving last words into river-stones for farmers who couldn't afford priests. I thought it was a curse. I thought it was — I don't know. A trick the valley played on me. An illness." He wiped his face with the heel of his hand. "It was homework. My whole life was *homework* my mother assigned me before I could walk."

"Every gift is an assignment," Ede said. "The question is only whether you do the reading."

Nell tugged his sleeve. When he looked down, she was holding out her own token — the bone her mother had pressed into her hand in another lifetime, the one she guarded like a heartbeat. She turned it over to show him the underside, where a mark was cut that he had never seen before because she had never shown anyone: a spiral, open at one end, the sigil of the warden's line.

His mother's line. Their line.

"Oh," Jonah said. Very quietly. "Oh, you *knew.*"

Nell's chin trembled, just slightly — the first crack he'd ever seen in the little magistrate's composure — and she nodded, and signed something quick and fierce that he only half understood but fully felt: *I looked for you. I looked for you everywhere.*

* * *

They stayed in the vault a long time. It was Ede who finally broke the quiet, and when she spoke, the warmth had gone out of her voice and the warden was back, crisp and formal, because what she had to say next was not comfort but duty.

"There's something else you must hear, and I regret it, because you've had enough for one descent. But the solstice is close, and the spell is finishing, and you did not come all this way for a family reunion."

Jonah, sitting on the floor with a child asleep against his shoulder — when had she fallen asleep? even miracles deserve naps — looked up. "Tell me."

"The archive is not only a record. It is a *quorum.*" Ede spread her hands at the glowing shelves. "Ten thousand last words, held in witness. Under Vessian law — real law, singing law, the kind the courts made before the wars — a quorum of witnesses can convene testimony. Can convene a *trial.* If the right witness speaks before the quorum before the spell completes, the erasure can be contested. Not undone," she added quickly, seeing his face. "Nothing can be undone. But *answered for.* A country that is erased silently is gone forever. A country that is erased *on the record* — with testimony taken, with guilt named, with truth spoken into the archive — leaves a seed. Somewhere. Someday. Something that remembers how to be born again."

"And the right witness," Jonah said slowly, looking down at the sleeping child, at the bone token in her curled fist, "would be someone chosen to remember. Someone the country itself picked. Someone small enough to slip through a spell built to catch kings."

"The Thorn," Ede said. "Yes."

Outside, far above the lake, far above the drowned towers, Jonah heard — or imagined he heard, or heard with the ears his mother had given him — a sound he had been dreading since the white riders first appeared on the valley road like unfinished sketches of men.

Hoofbeats. Many of them. Coming down toward the water.

And threaded through the hoofbeats, faint as a hairline crack in glass, a voice he recognized now, though he'd only heard it once across a frozen field: the Justiciar captain, Aldous, calling out in a language older than any kingdom —

calling out in Vessian,

and weeping as he did it.
