## 13. What Duty Demands

The man came out of the snow the way a word comes back to you — all at once, and too late to do anything about it.

Nell saw him first. She had been walking at the front of their small procession because Jonah's knees complained on the downs and because the road rose up under her feet like a dog greeting its master, pale stones surfacing through the frost-crusted grass wherever she stepped. *Vess-road*, her grandmother's voice said in her head, in the old tongue that was not so much a language anymore as a country she carried behind her ribs. *The road knows its heir. Walk proud, little witness.*

She was not walking proud. She was walking cold, and hungry, and tired of a world that kept forgetting things — the ferryman's name, half of it, gone from his own mouth by morning; the village they'd passed two days ago where the innkeeper had looked at them with the flat politeness of a man meeting strangers he was certain he'd never see again, because he wouldn't, because by spring there would be no inn.

So when the figure appeared on the ridge ahead — tall, white-cloaked, still as a fencepost — Nell did what her mother had taught her. She stopped. She put one hand on the bone token at her throat. And she listened with the part of herself that was older than fear.

Jonah bumped into her back. "Oof. Nell, love, you can't just— " He followed her gaze up the hill, and she felt him go quiet beside her, felt the particular quality of his silence, which was different from other people's silences the way a held breath is different from an empty room. Jonah was always listening to something she couldn't hear. Right now, whatever it was, it had frightened him.

"That's him," Jonah said. "The captain. The one with the unfinished face."

*Unfinished*, Nell thought, tasting the word in both tongues. She remembered the Justiciars from the trial-village, their faces like sketches someone had abandoned halfway — eyes suggested rather than drawn, mouths that moved without quite committing to expression. She had understood something in that courtroom that she had not yet told Jonah, because telling it would make it true: the Justiciars were not wearing masks. They had *paid* with their faces, piece by piece, the way her people had paid with their country.

The man on the ridge began to walk down toward them.

"Get behind me," Jonah said, and there was something almost funny in it — a bone-carver with a knife meant for whittling, putting himself between a child and a King's Justiciar as though his body were any kind of wall. But Nell loved him for it, the way you love a lamp in a dark room: not because it can fight the dark, but because it insists on burning anyway.

She did not get behind him. She stepped forward instead, into the trampled snow between them, and lifted her chin the way the law-singers of Vessia had lifted theirs when they spoke truth to a court.

The captain stopped ten paces away.

Up close, his face was worse than unfinished. It was *unfinishing* — she could see, faintly, like watermarks under paint, the ghost of features that had once been specific. A crooked nose. A scar over one brow. Someone had been here once, someone with a mother who must have kissed that scar, and the erasure-oath had sanded him down to a suggestion of a man.

His eyes found her. And then —

Nell would think about this moment for the rest of her life, in both of her languages, and later in a third language that did not exist yet. The captain of the King's Justiciars, hunter of children, eraser of countries, knelt down in the snow.

Not a bow. Not a soldier's kneel before a superior. He went down the way a tree goes down — slowly, heavily, all his weight surrendering at once — until he was kneeling in the slush with his head bowed and his white cloak pooling around him like spilled milk, and he said, in a voice cracked with disuse and grief:

"*Sela veth annua.*"

Nell's breath left her.

It was Vessian. Old-formal, court-register, the tongue of oaths and law-courts, the tongue her mother had used only twice — once to name her, and once to say goodbye. *I am your servant unto death.* The oldest courtesy in a dead language, spoken by a dead country's living traitor, to a seven-year-old girl standing in the ruins of everything he had helped bury.

Behind her, Jonah made a small confused sound. "What — what did he say? What's happening?"

"He knelt," Nell said, in common, because Jonah could not hear the old words anymore than he could hear the bones sing in anything but fragments. Her own common-tongue was getting better; she practiced in her head at night while Jonah slept. "He said... he is my servant."

"That's — no. No, that's wrong." Jonah's hands were doing the thing they did when he was frightened, flexing and unflexing at his sides, reaching for bones that weren't there. "Nell, these are the ones who erase people. These are the ones who took the Hollis farm off the face of the earth. Get behind me *now*."

But the captain had raised his head, and Nell saw his eyes properly for the first time, and she understood why he had knelt.

They were brown. Warm brown, ordinary brown, the brown of river water in summer, and they were full of a sorrow so old and so carefully maintained that it had become the architecture of the man. He was not looking at her the way a hunter looks at prey. He was looking at her the way you look at the last light in a house you have been ordered to put out.

"*Thorn,*" he said, in common this time, and the word was gentle in his mouth, which somehow made it worse. "You shouldn't have run. Running makes it hurt more."

* * *

They did not run. Later, Nell could not fully explain to herself why not — except that some part of her, the part that remembered glass rivers and singing courts, recognized something in the kneeling man that her body trusted before her mind agreed to. Grief recognizes grief. It does not check credentials first.

They sat in the lee of a fallen drystone wall, the three of them, because the captain asked — *asked*, politely, in accented common — and because Jonah's legs were shaking and he pretended it was the cold. The captain set his sword on the ground between them, hilt outward, which was either a courtesy or a message, and Nell suspected it was both.

"You're Vessian," Jonah said. It wasn't a question. His voice had a strange flatness she'd learned to hear as the sound of him reorganizing his whole understanding of the world. "You're Vessian and you work for the men who erased it."

"I work for the peace," the captain said. "Aldous. My name is Aldous." He said it like a man setting down something heavy to free his hands. "Or it was. Names are the first thing the oath takes, if you let it. I've held onto mine the way you hold onto a rope over a drop."

"How?" Jonah demanded. "How is a Vessian a Justiciar? How does that even — you *kneeled* to her. You called her—" he gestured helplessly at Nell, "*servant*. To a child."

"To the witness," Aldous corrected softly. His eyes did not leave Nell. "You know what she is, carver? Or do you just carry her around the countryside hoping the mystery resolves itself?"

"She's Nell," Jonah said, with a stubbornness that made warmth bloom in Nell's chest despite everything. "That's what she is. That's enough."

Something flickered across the captain's unfinished face — envy, maybe, or its cousin, regret. "Yes," he said quietly. "I imagine it is, for now. For a little while longer." He turned back to Nell, and he spoke slowly, in careful common, as if translating from something deeper. "Child. Do you know what happens at solstice?"

Nell knew. She knew the way you know a stone is in your shoe — constantly, uncomfortably, without being able to stop knowing it. The winter was not weather. The winter was the spell finishing its work, the great forgetting drawing its last circle, and when it closed, every trace of Vessia everywhere — every bone-token, every half-remembered lullaby, every carved whisper in a bone-carver's workshop — would go silent forever. Including, she suspected, including, *including*, the small stubborn country she carried behind her ribs.

"The spell completes," she said. Her common was rough but it held. "Everything goes."

"Everything goes," Aldous agreed. "And I am the one who makes sure it goes *cleanly*. That is my duty. That has been my duty for thirty years." He looked at his own hands, long and capable and resting open on his knees. "Do you want to know how a country comes to agree to its own murder, little witness? Because you should know. You're going to be angry at us — at me — for the rest of your life, and you deserve to be angry at the *true* thing, not the story."

Nell folded her hands in her lap, the way the law-singers did. *Speak*, she did not say. *We are listening. We are the last ones listening.*

* * *

"There was a war," Aldous said. "Of course there was a war. There is always a war; that is the oldest law there is, older than any kingdom. Vessia fought the coalition of the eastern crowns for eleven years. Eleven years of burned valleys and poisoned wells and children learning the shapes of shrapnel before the shapes of letters. And we were *winning*, do you understand? Vessia's law-singers could unmake a siege with a well-chosen word. Our bone-speakers —" his eyes flicked, briefly, to Jonah — "our bone-speakers kept the names of our dead, and a country that keeps its dead does not break. We were winning, and so the eastern kings did the thing that losing kings do."

"They cheated," Jonah said.

"They despaired," Aldous said. "Which is worse. Despair is honest. They came to our own council with an offer, and the offer was this: end the war, or we will end *you* — not with armies, but with forgetting. They had bought a spell from somewhere older than kingdoms, a spell of unbeing, and they would spend it on every man, woman, child, field, song, and grave in Vessia, and within a generation no one would remember there had ever been anything to hate. No Vessia, no grudge, no war ever needing to be refought. Peace. Real peace. Permanent peace." His mouth did something that was not a smile. "And our own council — exhausted, grieving, watching another winter of war come down like a blade — our own council *said yes*."

Nell's hands had gone still in her lap. Behind her ribs, the old tongue whispered: *traitors, traitors, the ninth council, the cowards, the tired* — but her grandmother's voice caught itself, gentled. *No. The tired. Say it fairly, little witness. They were so tired.*

"Some refused," Aldous went on. "There was a faction — call them loyalists, call them fools, history will never call them anything because history is what we are erasing. They tried to smuggle out what they could. Children, mostly. Tokens. Witnesses." His gaze rested on her, warm and terrible. "One infant was carried out through the Door Out by his mother, who stayed behind to hold it open, and paid for his passage with every memory of him. You may know the story, carver. You may have heard it recently, from the dead."

Jonah made a sound like a man who has been struck quietly.

"But most of us," Aldous continued, "most of us stayed, and were given a choice, because the eastern kings were not monsters, only victors, which is nearly the same thing with better manners. We could be forgotten along with everything else — painless, total, a sleep without dreams. Or we could take the erasure-oath: serve the forgetting. Keep the peace we had purchased with ourselves. Hunt down what escaped. Make sure the wound closed clean." He spread his hands. "Thirty years I have done this work. I have unwritten farms. I have watched villages forget their own grandmothers mid-sentence. I have judged an elder for a lullaby — you saw that, didn't you, little witness? I know you did. Your eyes at that trial have been following me down every road since."

"You think it's mercy," Nell said.

"I *know* it's mercy," Aldous said, and here his voice finally broke its careful surface, and underneath it was a young man's voice, a soldier's voice, a voice that had watched friends die for eleven years and would have watched more. "That is the whole horror of it, child, and I need you to understand it before you hate me. If Vessia returns — if you open that Door and pour a country back into the world — the eastern kings will remember why they were afraid. The treaties built on our grave will crack. There are grandchildren of soldiers on both sides who have lived thirty years believing the war ended in grief and compromise, and if Vessia rises, they learn instead that it ended in *erasure*, and every debt comes due at once. Millions, little witness. Millions of lives balanced against one country's memory. Against one child's homesickness." He leaned forward, and his unfinished face was naked now, stripped of office. "I have carried this arithmetic for thirty years. I am so tired of carrying it. So believe me when I tell you what I came here to offer, and believe that it costs me more than you can know."

He reached into his cloak and drew out a small object: a bone whistle, white as the snow, plain as an unlit candle.

"Forgetting," he said, "does not have to be violent. The great spell takes everything, all at once, whether you are ready or not. But there are smaller mercies. This —" he set the whistle on the stone wall between them, "— is a sleeper's door. Blow it, and you will forget Vessia gently. Not torn away — *laid down*, the way you lay down a burden at the end of a long walk. You will forget the glass rivers and the singing courts and the mother and the token and the Door. You will forget that you were ever hunted. You will wake tomorrow as an orphan of nowhere in particular, and a kind bone-carver will take you in, because he is the sort of man who takes things in, and you will grow up ordinary and safe and *happy*, child. Happy. Do you understand what I am offering you? Everyone else in your country gets the knife. I am offering you the sleep."

The whistle sat on the stone between them, small and white and patient.

Nell looked at it for a long time.

And here is the thing she discovered about herself, in the cold, with the wind moving through the drystone wall like a slow breath: she was tempted. Not the way the weak are tempted — the way the *tired* are tempted. She was seven years old. She had walked out of a country that no longer existed carrying nothing but a bone and a language that was dissolving in her mouth a little more each day. She missed her mother with a pain that had stopped being sharp and become structural, load-bearing, part of how she stood up in the morning. An ordinary life. A warm kitchen. Jonah's bad knees and worse jokes and hands that made beautiful things. A childhood. She was so *tired*, and the whistle sang to her tiredness in a voice with no melody at all, which was somehow the most seductive music in the world.

Beside her, Jonah had gone very still. She could feel him wanting to speak — wanting to say *take it, Nell, please, be safe, be happy, let me keep you* — and she loved him for the wanting and she was glad, so glad, that he bit it back. Because it was not his burden to choose. That was the first law of witnesses, written in a country with no maps: *the witness chooses. No one chooses for the witness.*

"Aldous," she said.

His breath caught at his own name, spoken in the old tongue, in the formal register, the way a law-court would say it. As if naming him might summon the man he had been before the oath.

"If I forget," Nell said, in common, carefully, "who remembers the ones who chose the knife? Who remembers *you*?"

The captain stared at her.

"Your thirty years," she said. "Your arithmetic. Your tiredness. If I blow the whistle, all of that goes too, doesn't it. Nobody will ever know that a Vessian man knelt in the snow and offered a child mercy. Nobody will know you held your name over the drop for thirty years." She picked up the whistle. It was lighter than it should have been, the way sad things are. "You are not offering me escape, Aldous. You are offering to make yourself the last Vessian alive, alone, forever, with no one left who knows what you gave up. That is not mercy. That is just the war, finding one more way to take everything from everyone."

For a long moment the only sound was the wind and, far off, the creak of frost tightening its grip on the world.

Then Aldous laughed. It was a terrible sound, rusty and cracked, half sob — the laugh of a man hearing a truth he had spent thirty years avoiding. "Oh," he said. "Oh, *there* you are. The law-singers' blood. They told me the witness would be a child, and I thought, good, children are easy, children are frightened, children want to be safe." He shook his head slowly. "But you sit there like the whole parliament of the dead, weighing my soul in your lap."

He stood. He did not take the whistle back.

"Keep it," he said. "Blow it tonight or don't. I cannot force you — the oath forbids it, and the oath is the last thing in me that still works properly. Solstice is nine days away. When the spell completes, the choice makes itself." He retrieved his sword, and paused, and looked down at her with those warm brown river-water eyes. "For what it is worth, little witness — and it is worth nothing, less than nothing, it is worth exactly one old man's shame — I hope you find your Door. And I hope, when you stand in front of it, that the price is one you can pay without becoming what I became."

He turned and walked up the hill into the falling snow, and the white took him the way the white took everything lately — gradually, then completely.

* * *

They walked until the light failed, and made camp in the shelter of a hollow, and Jonah built a fire with the fussy competence of a man who needed his hands busy. Nell sat close to the flames and turned the whistle over and over in her fingers.

"Nell." Jonah's voice was low. "What he said. About the infant. About the mother who held the Door open." He was staring into the fire, and his face did something complicated. "The bones have been saying things. Since we crossed the valley line. Things about — about a woman. About me." He laughed, short and unhappy. "That's mad, isn't it? I'm a bone-carver from Harrow Vale. My mother was a washerwoman. I'd know if I was — if I was from a country that doesn't exist."

*Would you*, Nell thought. *Would you, Jonah, whose gift arrived from nowhere, whose valley was the one place the spell couldn't reach, who the road loves and the dead gather to?*

But she said none of it. Some truths, her grandmother's voice murmured, must ripen before picking.

Instead she held up the whistle, white in the firelight. "Nine days," she said.

Jonah looked at the whistle, and then at her, and Nell watched him arrive — slowly, reluctantly, like a man wading into cold water — at the understanding of what she was holding. Of what she had been offered. Of what she had refused.

"You didn't blow it," he said.

"Not yet."

"And you're not going to."

Nell smiled at him, and it cost her more than he would ever know, and she said: "Ask me again at solstice."

Far away, at the edge of the firelight, where the dark pressed thickest, a single bone in Jonah's pack began — very softly, for the first time — to sing.
