## 14. The Bargain at the Ford

The river at the ford was the color of old pewter, and it had opinions.

Jonah felt them through his boots before he heard anything — a low grumble in the water, the way a dog grumbles before deciding whether to bite. The ford was narrow here, stones showing their backs like sleeping cattle, and on the far bank Aldous Vane stood with his three Justiciars behind him, white cloaks beaded with sleet, faces like sketches someone had abandoned halfway to finished.

"You're out of road, carver," Aldous called across the water. His voice carried strangely, without echo, as if the air itself were reluctant to repeat it. "And out of country. What's left past this ford is already forgetting."

Nell's small hand found Jonah's. She didn't squeeze it. She simply held on, the way you hold a rail in a storm — not for comfort, but because letting go would be a decision.

Jonah looked at the man who had knelt once in a burned chapel and confessed to being Vessian, who wore his oath like a coat two sizes too small, and he said the thing he'd been turning over all night by the fire while Nell slept against the coffin-boat's hull.

"No," he said.

Aldous tilted his head. Behind him, one of the riders shifted, snow sliding off a shoulder with a soft hiss.

"She doesn't want your mercy," Jonah went on, and his voice came out steadier than he had any right to expect. "I've asked her. In every way a man can ask a child who speaks in a language older than bread. And she says no. So I'm saying it for her too, since your law apparently needs it in common tongue." He swallowed. "No. We don't take the forgetting. Not painless, not kind, not wrapped up pretty with a bow of duty on top. You can write that down in whatever ledger Justiciars keep. Refused. By both of us."

For a long moment there was only the river muttering its pewter-colored opinions.

Then Aldous did something Jonah hadn't expected. He sighed — a real sigh, tired down to the floorboards of him — and walked forward into the shallows. The water parted around his boots without wetting them, which told Jonah everything about what still lived under that unfinished face.

"I hoped you'd say yes," Aldous said, stopping an arm's length away on the near bank. "Truly. It would have been easier on everyone. Easier on *her* most of all. Forgetting doesn't hurt, Whitlock. That's the whole cruelty of it. It feels like rest."

"So does drowning," Jonah said. "I've heard enough dead people describe it."

Something flickered across the Justiciar captain's sketch of a face — amusement, maybe, or grief wearing amusement's clothes. "You have heard them. That's the other thing I came to tell you, before we stand on opposite sides of this properly." He glanced back at Nell, and his voice dropped, almost gentle. "The spell is nearly done. Solstice. Nine days. When the winter completes, Vessia will be finished — not destroyed, understand, never destroyed, just... unwritten so thoroughly that even the idea of it will have nowhere to live. And everything bound to Vessia goes with it. Her blood. Her bones." A pause. "Her ghosts."

Jonah felt the words land somewhere beneath his ribs, cold as the ford water.

"Nine days," he repeated.

"Nine days," Aldous agreed. "After that, my duty ends, because there will be nothing left to enforce. Until then—" He straightened, and the gentleness folded itself away like a letter returned to its envelope. "Until then I must do what I swore to do. I'm giving you until solstice, carver. Not because I'm soft. Because I was Vessian once, and some part of me wants to be wrong about how this has to end. Use the time however you like. Pray, run, dig. But when the winter finishes, if she's still standing in this world—" he looked at Nell again, and this time he couldn't finish the sentence. One of the riders behind him made a sound like a page turning.

"Why?" Jonah asked. "Why give us nine days? You could take her now. You've had chances."

Aldous was quiet long enough that the river filled the silence with its grumbling. Then: "Because when I knelt in that chapel, I told you I took the oath to end a war. What I didn't tell you is that the war had already taken my daughter. Both sides of it. And when they offered me peace bought with forgetting, I thought — let no one else's daughter pay. I've spent thirty years believing that price was worth it." His unfinished eyes found Jonah's. "Don't make me spend nine more days finding out it wasn't. It's undignified at my age."

He turned, waded back — the water still refusing to touch him — and the four white figures went up the far slope and into the sleet until the world closed over them like water over a stone.

Nell tugged Jonah's sleeve. She pointed at the ford, then at herself, then made a shape in the air with both hands: a door. Then she shook her head, fierce, her dark eyes blazing with a certainty that needed no language.

*Not yet,* the gesture said. *But soon.*

"Yeah," Jonah said hoarsely. "Soon. Nine days soon." He picked up his pack, and the carved bones inside it rattled together like nervous teeth. "Let's get off this riverbank. I don't trust water that won't wet a Justiciar."

* * *

They sheltered that night in the hollow root of an oak so old it predated the erasure, or claimed to — trees were liars about their age, Jonah had always felt, the way old men were about their fishing. He built a small fire, fed Nell the last of the ferryman's black bread toasted on a flat stone, and laid out his carvings in a careful row along a root shelf, the way he'd done every night since he was nineteen years old and first heard a dead grandmother complain about the draft in his workshop.

There were eleven of them now. There had been fourteen at the start of the journey.

He didn't look at the empty places in the row. Looking at them was a thing he was saving for later, when there was time, though some part of him knew there would never be time enough for that particular looking.

"Evening, all," he said, because ritual mattered, and because talking to the dead was the one conversation he'd never had to lie in. "News from the living world: we've got nine days, a hostile escort with a conscience, and half a loaf. Also the oak above us snores, which I didn't know oaks did, so that's new."

The voices rose the way they always did, overlapping, bickering, alive in the only way left to them. Old Marta, dead sixty years, wanted to know why he never toasted the bread properly. The drowned ferryman from two counties back offered unsolicited advice about rivers. Tam Weller, the poacher whose last words had been a complaint about the unfairness of magpies, demanded to know if they'd seen any magpies lately, because even dead, he was keeping score.

And Hedda — Hedda of the valley, the woman dead two hundred years who had named Nell *the Thorn*, whose carving was the oldest thing Jonah owned — Hedda was quiet.

"Hedda?" Jonah touched the worn bone gently. "You still with us?"

A pause. Then her voice came, thinner than he'd ever heard it, like a thread pulled nearly through the cloth. *Still here, bone-man. Still here.* Another pause, longer. *But it's getting harder to hold on. The winter isn't weather, Jonah. You know that now. It's a tide going out, and we are things it wants to carry away.*

"Don't go," he said, and hated how small it sounded. "Hedda, you're the oldest voice I have. You taught me half of what I know about listening."

*Half,* she agreed warmly. *The other half you learned by being stubborn, which no one can teach.* A sound that might have been laughter, fraying at the edges. *Listen to me. When we go — and we are going, one by one, starting soonest with those of us who are oldest and tiredest — do not grieve like it's a death. It's a homecoming we've waited two hundred years to be allowed. Grieve like it's a wedding. Loudly. With food.*

"I'll cry at your wedding, then," Jonah managed. "It's what you'd have wanted. You'd want to embarrass me one last time."

*Oh, absolutely,* said Hedda, and her voice was warm and clear for one more moment — and then, like a candle guttering in a doorway, it dimmed. *Bone-man. The child. Tell her—*

The silence that followed was not the ordinary silence between words. It was the silence of a room after someone leaves it.

Jonah sat very still, his hand resting on the carving, feeling nothing come back through the bone. Nothing. For the first time since he was nineteen, the bone was just bone. Cold, smooth, dumb as any stone.

"Well," he said to the fire, because his voice would break otherwise. "That's one."

* * *

They went fast after that, and the going was a funeral procession stretched thin across the countryside.

Marta went on the third day, mid-complaint about the toast, her voice stopping so abruptly that Jonah actually stumbled on the road, as if a hand he'd leaned on for years had been withdrawn. Tam Weller lasted five more days and used them well, spotting seven magpies and demanding each one be reported to him individually. The drowned ferryman went quietly, in the night, without saying goodbye, which Jonah decided was exactly the sort of thing a ferryman would do — slip off at the far bank while you weren't looking.

Each loss was small. Each loss was enormous. Jonah learned that grief could be both at once, the way a coin is both metal and money.

Nell watched him mourn, and something in her changed. She began sitting closer to the carvings at night, laying her small palm flat against each bone in turn, and where she touched, the voices strengthened — briefly, like coals breathed on. She was feeding them somehow, or holding the door against the tide with her own two hands, and Jonah understood that this was costing her something he couldn't see.

"You don't have to," he told her on the seventh night, catching her small hand trembling against Marta's empty carving. "Nell. You don't have to hold them. They lived their lives. They can go home."

She looked up at him with those old, old eyes and shook her head once. Then she pointed at him, and at his chest, and pressed her palm flat over his heart, and made the door-shape again with her other hand.

*You need them,* the gesture said. *So I hold them. While I can.*

"It works better the other way round," Jonah said thickly. "That's supposed to be my job. Holding doors."

Nell smiled — the first true smile he'd seen from her in days — and patted his chest twice, briskly, the way you pat a large foolish dog you love anyway.

On the eighth night, camped in the lee of a ruined milestone that no longer remembered what distance it measured, Jonah counted his row of bones. Four remained. Hedda's silent carving, which he couldn't bring himself to leave behind. The drowned ferryman's, blank as river ice. And two still speaking: Tam Weller, faintly, complaining that the magpie count stood at seven and he refused to die on an odd number; and a voice Jonah had carried longest of all, older than his career, older than his name — a woman's voice with no history attached, who had been in the first bone he ever carved and had never told him who she was.

She spoke now, quiet, in the firelight.

*Jonah.*

She almost never used his name. He went still.

*I've been thinking about what to say to you, these eight days, while the others went ahead of me. And I keep coming back to the same thing, which is that I owe you an apology and a thank-you, and I only have breath for one. So: thank you. Forty years of listening. Forty years of not being alone in the dark. No dead woman ever had it better.*

"Apology for what?" he whispered. "What could you possibly—"

*For what I'm about to not-tell you,* she said, and there was warmth in it, and sorrow, and something that sounded almost like mischief. *Some doors, bone-man, a person has to open herself. Even if it breaks her heart. Especially then. Ask the child. Watch her hands. And when you get to the Door Out — and you will, stubbornness travels — remember that the ones who go quiet aren't gone. We're just on the other side of the ford, waiting to see if you'll wade out.*

"Wait," Jonah said. "Wait — who are you? Who were you? Tell me that much, at least, before—"

But the tide was going out, and the winter was finishing its work, and the ninth dawn came gray and still over a world gone white and hushed, and the last voice but one faded from his keeping like breath from a mirror.

Tam Weller held on until noon, purely out of spite, and died — went home, went ahead, went *across* — announcing with his final fading syllable that he had spotted an eighth magpie, and that the universe owed him, and that somebody should write it down.

Jonah wrote it down. On the back of his hand, in charcoal, because somebody should.

Then he packed the four silent bones — no, three silent and one still whispering, the nameless woman, holding on past all reason, humming something that might have been a lullaby in a language older than bread — and he shouldered his pack, and Nell took his hand, and they turned toward the lake where a drowned capital waited in the reflections, and the Door beyond it.

One day left. The winter stood at the edge of the world like a held breath.

And far behind them, at the top of a white hill, four figures in white stood watching, unmoving, counting down with them.

Beside him, Nell stopped walking. She was staring at the hilltop, and her hand in Jonah's had gone rigid — and slowly, deliberately, she raised her other arm and pointed past the Justiciars, past the horizon, toward the pale smudge of the lake, and made the door-shape one final time.

Then she looked up at Jonah, and for the first time since the valley, she opened her mouth — and spoke.

"One day," she said, in cracked, rusted, magnificent common tongue. "Hurry."
