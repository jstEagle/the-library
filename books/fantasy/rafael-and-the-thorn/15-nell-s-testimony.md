## 15. Nell's Testimony

The vault beneath the lake did not feel like a place where a child should stand trial.

It felt like the inside of a shell held to the ear — vast, curved, and full of murmuring. The walls were shelves, and the shelves were bones, and every bone was whispering. Not all at once. They took turns, politely, the way Vessians had always taken turns, because that was the first law of the old country: *everyone speaks, and everyone is heard, and no one speaks twice until everyone has spoken once.*

Nell stood on a floor of white stone that had been polished by ten thousand feet and then by no feet at all for two hundred years. Above her, through water she could not see but could somehow feel, the lake lay flat as a held breath. Jonah stood at her left shoulder, close enough that she could smell the bone-dust on his sleeves. Aldous stood far away, near the door, with his hands folded like a man at a funeral who is not sure he is welcome.

She had not planned to speak.

That was the truth of it, and Nell had decided — sometime during the long cold walk, sometime between the ferryman's fire and the coffin-boat and the terrible singing of the river — that she would not lie, even inside her own head. She had run from the palace-that-was-because-it-had-been because running was easier than saying. Saying was the hard thing. Saying was the whole job.

But the bones had asked. All of them, in their turn, in their whispering way, they had asked: *Who are you? Why have you come? What do you want of us?*

And a court that asks must be answered. That was the second law.

So she opened her mouth, and out came the old tongue.

---

*Vessa-sel, vessa-kei,* she said. *I am small, and I am here.*

The words felt strange in her mouth, like wearing her mother's coat. She knew them perfectly — they lived behind her teeth the way her own heartbeat did — but she had never once used them for something this large. At home, in the country that no longer existed except in reflections and in her, the old tongue had been for lullabies and lawsuits and the naming of rivers. It had been a language you spoke when what you said mattered too much for common words to hold.

What she had to say mattered that much.

*I am not the heir,* she told them, and heard the word travel up through the shelves, bone to bone, the way a shiver travels through a sleeping dog. *I know you hoped. I know some of you died believing the bloodline survived, that somewhere a true daughter of the glass courts grew up safe, and that if you only kept her name alive she would come back and set everything right again.*

*I am sorry. There is no one coming back.*

The vault went very quiet. Even the lake above seemed to lean closer.

Jonah's hand found her shoulder. He could not understand the old tongue — she had watched him try, kindly, hopelessly, for weeks, mouthing her syllables like a man tasting soup in a language he didn't speak — but he understood the shape of grief in any language. His hand stayed there, warm and rough and certain, and she drew strength from it the way a fencepost draws strength from the ground.

*Here is what I am instead,* she said.

---

*When the forgetting began — when the white winter came down off the mountains and started eating names — the wise ones of my mother's mother's line met in the singing law-court one last time. I know this because my mother told me, and she was told by hers, all the way down the chain of mothers like beads on a string. And the last bead of that string is me, which means the string ends in my mouth, which is why I ran, and why I am standing here being brave, which I want you all to know is not going well from the inside.*

A few of the bones made a sound that might have been laughter. Dead people, Nell was learning, laughed easier than living ones. Perhaps because nothing worse could happen to them. Perhaps because they had all, at last, finished being afraid.

*They argued,* she went on. *Of course they argued. We are Vessian; arguing is how we pray. Some said: hide an heir. Grow a secret queen in a far country and wait two hundred years, and when the spell thins, bring her home and rebuild the towers and reopen the courts and put the maps back the way they were.*

*And others said — and these were the older voices, the tired ones, the ones who had actually seen the war — said: no. Never. If we restore it exactly as it was, we restore the reasons it burned. The pride. The borders drawn in glass. The neighbors who feared us enough to help the Justiciars erase us, because a country that sings its laws is a country that makes other countries feel deaf.*

*So they chose a third thing. Not a queen. A witness.*

*Me.*

---

She stopped there, because her throat had closed, and Jonah, without understanding a single word, knelt down beside her so that she wasn't speaking up at a room full of shelves anymore. She was speaking across at it. Eye to empty eye-socket, more or less. It helped more than she could say in either language.

*They chose a child not yet born,* she said, quieter now. *Not to rule. To remember. Do you understand the difference? A queen carries a country on her back and tells it where to go. A witness carries a country in her eyes and tells it what it looked like.*

*The heir was a mercy for us. The witness is a mercy for the world.*

*Because here is the secret, the one my mother pressed into my hand along with the bone token, the one she could not say aloud because the Justiciars' listening spells were already in the walls:* she lifted her chin, and her voice, though small, went all the way to the top shelves. *The erasure is not only cruelty. It ended the war. It bought peace. Real peace, for real children in real villages who sleep now without fear of glass-bladed riders. If I march home and raise Vessia from the lake like a drowned bell ringing, those children pay the price of our resurrection. Aldous knows it. That is why he kneels and threatens in the same motion. That is why his sorrow is honest.*

*And the forgetting is not only mercy, either. It is eating outward. It ate the Harrow farmstead. It will eat the valley. It will eat the world's edges until there is nothing left but white silence and men in unfinished faces guarding nothing. Mercy that grows without limit becomes just another hunger.*

*So someone must stand in the middle and hold both truths. Someone small enough to be overlooked and stubborn enough to refuse. Someone who remembers the glass rivers AND the burned villages. The singing courts AND the war-dead. The beauty AND the cost.*

*Someone who can carry the memory without swinging it like a sword.*

*That is why I ran. Not to escape. To arrive. You do not give a country back to itself the way you steal bread. You give it back the way you plant an orchard — slowly, honestly, with permission, in soil that wants it. I am the seed, not the harvest. My job is only to survive long enough, and remember well enough, and tell it truly enough, that when the world is ready to grow a new country — a better one, one that learned — the pattern exists somewhere outside the spell.*

*In me. In my telling. In whatever I make of it.*

---

She switched to broken common then, because she saw Jonah's face, and because the second law said everyone must be heard, and Jonah was part of everyone now whether the old country liked it or not.

"I am not princess," she said carefully, stacking the words like a child building with unfamiliar blocks. "I am — remembering-person. Witness. Like your carvings." She touched his sleeve. "You keep dead voices in bone. I keep live country in head. Same work. Different material."

Jonah made a sound that was half laugh and half something wetter, and wiped his face with the heel of his hand, and nodded as if she'd said something very wise, which she privately suspected she had.

"The old ones wanted a queen," she told him, and the vault, hearing her, translated her common back into whispers along the shelves so the dead could follow. "Queen is easy. Queen is a story with armor on. But easy stories are how the first war started — everyone wanting to be the hero of the same one." She frowned, hunting for the words. "Hard story is better. Hard story is: *we were wonderful and we were wrong.* Both. Same breath."

"Can you do that?" Jonah asked softly. "Carry both?"

Nell considered lying, just briefly, just to be kind to him. Then she remembered her own resolution.

"No," she said. "Not alone. That is why witness needs — " she searched, found it — "witnesses. Plural. One person remembering alone is called madness. Many people remembering together is called history." She shrugged, a small sharp shrug she had learned from no one, that was entirely her own. "I am one person. I need to become many. That is the whole plan. It is not a big plan. But big plans fell down already."

The bones rustled. It sounded like wind through wheat. It sounded like approval.

---

One shelf near the ceiling began to whisper louder than the rest, and Nell tilted her head back to listen, because she was beginning to learn the vault's grammar. This was an old voice, a court-voice, dry as paper and twice as formal. It spoke slowly, in the cadence of judgments, and the other bones hushed to let it through.

*Child of the chain-of-mothers,* it said. *You ask us to be remembered, but not restored. You ask us to be seeds, not ghosts. You ask us to trust a six-year-old with the entire soul of a nation.*

"I am nearly seven," Nell said, with dignity.

*Nearly seven,* the voice agreed, and there was, unmistakably, humor in it now, dry as its formality. *Forgive us. Two hundred years underground makes seven seem young.*

"It is young," Nell said. "I am not pretending otherwise. I cannot do this alone. That is my whole testimony. I am asking you — " and here her voice cracked, and she let it crack, because hiding it would have been a kind of lying — "I am asking you not to make me do it alone."

Silence, long as a held note.

Then, shelf by shelf, bone by bone, beginning at the floor and rising to the ceiling like dawn climbing a mountain, the dead began to answer. Not in words. In consent. Each carving hummed once, low and brief, and passed the sound to its neighbor, and the neighbor to the next, until the whole vault was ringing with it — a chord made of thousands of throats, the sound a country makes when it agrees.

Jonah gripped her hand. "What are they saying?" he whispered. "What does it mean?"

Nell listened with her whole body, the way her mother had taught her, the way you listen to ice before stepping on it.

"They say yes," she translated. "They say: *we were wonderful and we were wrong, and we choose the hard story.*" Her chin wobbled. "They say I may be small, but so is a seed."

She looked up at him, and for the first time since the snow at his door, since the frost and the riders and the white winter eating the world edge by edge, Nell felt something in her chest unclench.

"They also say," she added, because honesty was the whole job, "that you owe three years of hearing to a river spirit, and spirits talk, and apparently you snore."

"Nell—"

"And," she continued relentlessly, "one of the grandmothers on the third shelf says your carving technique is sloppy and your chisel angle is a disgrace to the craft."

"That's — I've been carrying her for eleven years—"

"She says she noticed."

He laughed then, really laughed, the sound bouncing off two hundred years of shelved souls, and Nell thought: *this. This is what I am saving. Not towers. Not maps. This exact sound, in a room full of the dead, made by a living man who forgot how.*

---

But the chord faded, as chords do, and when it faded the cold came back into the vault like a debt collector.

Nell felt it first in her teeth. Then in the bone token against her sternum, which had gone from warm to cool to *cold*, the specific cold of something being erased. Around the walls, faintly, one shelf dimmed — not broke, not silenced, but *thinned*, the way a candle thins before the wax gives out. Somewhere above them, beyond the still black mirror of the lake, the white winter was walking. She could not see it. She could feel it, the way animals feel weather, the way she had felt it the night the Harrow farmstead unwrote itself: a great soft pressure at the edge of everything, patient as snowfall, hungry as forgetting.

The formal voice at the ceiling spoke one last time, and its dryness had gone thin too.

*Winter comes, little witness. The solstice is close. When it passes, the spell completes, or it breaks. There will be no third season.*

"I know," Nell said.

*Then rest tonight,* said the dead, all of them, gently, in the voice they must have used for frightened children two hundred years ago, in the law-courts and the glass houses and the country that was. *Tomorrow you will need to be brave again, and bravery, like bread, must rise overnight.*

Nell took Jonah's hand. Together they turned toward the door, toward the ladder, toward the thin cold surface of the world — and found Aldous standing in it.

He had taken off his white rider's gloves. He was holding them in both hands like a man holding the skin of something he used to be. Behind him, through the vault's high entrance, the light of the lake had gone the color of milk.

"The roads are closing," Aldous said quietly. "All of them. Tonight or tomorrow, the snow reaches the shore of this lake, and when it does—" He stopped. Looked at Nell, at the token at her throat, at Jonah's ink-stained, bone-dusted hands. Whatever he had climbed down that ladder intending to say, he said something else instead.

"You testified," he said. "I heard it. All of it." His unfinished face did something complicated. "God help me. So did my oath."

Above them, muffled by fathoms of still water, the first flakes of the long winter began to fall on the lake — and did not melt.
