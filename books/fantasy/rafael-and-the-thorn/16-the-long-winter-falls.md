## 16. The Long Winter Falls

The letters stopped coming on the twelfth day.

Jonah had not expected them to keep coming — the ferryman's wife had promised only to write until the roads closed — but there was something particular about the way they *stopped*. Not a thinning. A severance. One morning there was a letter describing the ferryman's new grandchild and his terrible singing; the next morning there was nothing, and when Jonah tried to remember the man's face, he found he could hold it for about as long as a soap bubble holds its shape.

"It's like walking backward through your own house with the candles going out behind you," he said to Nell, who was sitting cross-legged on the vault floor, sorting bones by weight.

She looked up. In the lamplight her eyes were very old and very young at once, which was a trick Jonah had given up trying to understand. "The ferryman," she said carefully. Her common speech had grown sturdier these past weeks, though it still arrived in her mouth like a guest unsure of the furniture. "You will forget him."

"I'm doing my best not to."

"That is not how it works." She said it without cruelty. She said most true things without cruelty, which somehow made them worse. "Forgetting is not a door you can bar. It is weather."

Jonah snorted and went back to the letter, reading it again, mouthing the words, pressing the paper flat against his knee as if he could iron the man into permanence. *Hobb. The ferryman's name is Hobb. He has a wart on his left thumb shaped like a kidney bean and he cheats at cards and he knew my mother.* He repeated it like a prayer, because that was what it was.

Above them, muffled by forty feet of earth and older stone than any kingdom, the winter went on falling.

* * *

Nell heard the snow before she saw it.

Not with her ears — with the place behind her ribs where Vessia lived now, folded up small and bright as an ember carried in cupped hands. The ember flickered whenever the erasure came near, the way a candle leans when someone walks past too quickly. All day it had been leaning. All day the world outside had been leaning toward white.

She climbed the vault stairs two at a time, because she was six-going-on-seven and some things were simply true regardless of how old your eyes were, and pushed open the trapdoor just enough to see out.

The valley beyond the lake had gone the color of unwritten paper.

Snow fell, yes, but that was not the thing. Snow was honest. Snow landed and piled and melted and got in your boots and made your nose run, and all of that was *weather*, and weather belonged to the world. This was different. This whiteness did not land. It *arrived*, the way a word arrives when someone decides to say it, and where it arrived, things grew quiet in a way that had nothing to do with sound. A farmhouse stood at the valley's edge — she had seen it three days ago, with smoke from its chimney and a dog barking at nothing — and now the chimney stood bare and the windows were dark holes and the dog was nowhere, had never been anywhere, and if Nell squinted she could almost see the shape of the *absence*, like a footprint pressed into the world from underneath.

She let the trapdoor fall shut and sat down on the top stair, hugging her knees.

Below, faintly, she could hear Jonah talking to the bones. He did this every evening now, more urgently each time, as though he were a host at a feast trying to keep his guests from slipping out the back. "Stay," he'd say. "Just — stay. I know it's cold up here. I know. But stay." And the bones would murmur their agreement, and then one of them would go silent anyway, mid-sentence sometimes, and Jonah would set it aside gently, the way you set aside a plate at a funeral meal, and pick up the next one.

Tonight there were four new plates set aside.

Nell counted them from the stairs. Four. Yesterday, two. The day before, one. She was good at patterns; Vessian children learned patterns before they learned letters, because letters were only patterns that had agreed to stand still. One, two, four. Tomorrow would be eight, or sixteen, or all of them at once, because the erasure did not care about arithmetic except when arithmetic frightened people, and it had clearly decided tonight to frighten Jonah.

She went down and sat beside him without asking, because asking took time and time was a candle burning at both ends now.

"How many?" she asked.

"Four." His hands were in his lap, turned upward, empty. Jonah's hands were never empty; they were always holding a bone or a knife or a thought. Empty hands meant something had gone out of him. "Old Marta. The twins from the mill. And—" he stopped, swallowed. "And Hobb."

"The ferryman?"

"He came through last night. Said goodbye. Said—" Jonah's voice did something complicated. "Said he was glad he'd known me. Said he hoped I'd find who I was looking for." He laughed, a small broken-hinged laugh. "I wasn't looking for anyone. Then. Before her." He nodded at Nell, and Nell understood that she had become, somewhere along the road, the answer to a question Jonah hadn't known he was asking, and that this was both wonderful and unfair, because answers should not be small enough to fit inside a coat.

"You remember him wrong," Nell said gently. "Or right. It does not matter. What matters is that you remember him *at all*, and that you tell me, so that I carry him too."

So Jonah told her. About the wart like a kidney bean. About the cheating at cards, and how Hobb always lost anyway because he cheated badly, cheerfully, with the skill of a man who had taken up dishonesty as a hobby late in life. About how he'd known Jonah's mother — or claimed to — and how his eyes had gone soft and unfocused when he spoke of her, the way eyes go when they're looking at something the rest of us can't see.

"Tell it again tomorrow," Nell said. "And the day after. Even if I nod like I've heard it. Especially then."

"Why?"

"Because stories are how we refuse," Nell said, and then, because she was six-going-on-seven: "Also I like the part about the cards."

* * *

They heard the riders at dusk.

There was no drumming of hooves — Justiciars did not announce themselves, announcement being a courtesy extended only to people who existed — but the bones felt it first. Every carving in the vault went still at once, a held breath, and then old Marta's voice, thin and papery from her bone, said: *White riders. At the shore. Three of them, and the tall one carries sorrow like a lantern.*

"They're early," Jonah said, standing so fast his stool skidded. "He gave us until solstice. Tomorrow *is*—"

"Solstice eve," Nell said. "Yes. He is punctual. Punctuality is a kind of mercy, when you are the one who must do a hard thing. My mother said so." She paused. "My mother said many things. I am finding that nearly all of them turn out to be useful, which is annoying, because when she said them I mostly wanted to be eating plums."

Despite everything, Jonah smiled. It was a crooked smile, worn at the edges, but it was real. "What do we do?"

"We go up," Nell said. "We meet him. You cannot fight a tide from under the water."

* * *

Aldous stood at the lake's edge with his two riders behind him, and the snow-erasure fell around the three of them like a curtain that had decided, out of politeness, not to touch them.

He looked worse than he had at the ford. That was the first thing Jonah noticed. The second thing was that Aldous had dismounted, which Justiciars did not do, and had removed his white hood, which Justiciars *never* did, and that beneath the hood his hair was gray at the temples in a way that suggested worry rather than age.

"You're punctual," Jonah said.

"I try." Aldous's voice was low and even, the voice of a man who had practiced calm so long it had grown into his skeleton. "I've come to ask — not to demand, Jonah. To ask. Bring the child out. Let her sleep. By morning she'll remember a village that raised her, a family that loved her, a life that fits her like a well-made shoe. No pain. No running. No cold."

"And no Vessia."

"No Vessia." Aldous said it the way a priest says an amen. "No war either. No borders drawn in blood because two peoples remembered too well why they hated each other. I have read the histories, bone-carver. I have read what remembering did. Forty years of it. Fields salted. Children burned in barns because of a grudge their grandfathers' grandfathers signed. We ended that. Not with a sword — with a mercy. We made everyone forget the reasons, and the killing stopped within a season. Do you know how rare that is? Do you know how many kings would trade their crowns for a spell that worked half so well?"

Behind Aldous, one of the riders shifted. Their face, in the failing light, was still unfinished — features like a sketch someone had abandoned halfway through, eyes that were suggestions rather than statements. Jonah had gotten used to it. He suspected that was the point.

"She isn't asking to raise armies," Jonah said. "Listen to what she's actually saying. She doesn't want Vessia *back*. She wants it *witnessed*. There's a difference, and it's the whole difference, and you know it, because otherwise you'd have taken her at the ford instead of giving us a deadline."

"I know it." For a moment — just a moment — something moved behind Aldous's composure, the way a fish moves under ice. "That is what makes this unbearable. If she were a pretender, I could sleep. If she were mad, I could pity her. But she is a child who tells the truth, and the truth is that my oath and her hope cannot both survive the winter, and I am the one holding the sword." He looked down at his own hands, gloved in white. "Do you know what I was, before? Before the oath?"

"A Vessian," Nell said quietly. She had come to stand beside Jonah, small and straight-backed, her breath making little ghosts in the air. "You were a Vessian. You kneel differently than the others. They kneel like men following orders. You kneel like a man visiting a grave."

Aldous flinched. It was a small flinch, quickly mastered, but Nell saw it the way she saw everything — completely, and without blinking, which was perhaps the most frightening thing about her.

"Yes," he said simply. "I do."

* * *

They talked until full dark, there on the frozen shore, and the talk went nowhere, and both sides knew it went nowhere, and neither side stopped, because the talking itself had become the point — a way of marking time, of honoring the other, of putting off the hour when words would have to be replaced by deeds.

Aldous would not break his oath. He explained it twice, patiently, the way you explain to a child why the stove must not be touched, and Jonah understood that the patience was genuine and that it changed nothing. An oath, for Aldous, was not a chain. It was a spine. Remove it and the man collapsed into something that could not stand, could not be called anything, could not even grieve properly because grief requires a self and the self would be gone.

Jonah would not give up Nell. This required less explaining. Some things are load-bearing walls and you do not negotiate with them; you simply notice they are holding up the roof and you stop suggesting renovations.

Nell, for her part, offered Aldous a plum.

It was the last plum. She had been saving it since autumn, wrapped in cloth, hoarded against exactly such an evening, though she could not have said how she'd known there would be one. She held it out to him across the snow, small hand steady, and said, in her careful common tongue:

"You will do your duty tomorrow. I understand duty. Duty is a country too, and you were born in it." Aldous stared at the plum as though it might be enchanted, which, knowing Nell, it possibly was. "But eat this tonight. Tonight is not duty. Tonight is just an evening, and evenings belong to everyone, even enemies. Especially enemies. Especially on the night before."

And Aldous — Justiciar of the Erasure, breaker of remembered lullabies, the immovable sorrowful law of a dead country — took the plum.

He ate it slowly, standing in the falling white, and when he finished he looked at the pit in his palm for a long moment, and then he put it in his pocket instead of dropping it, and Jonah saw him do it, and neither of them said anything about it, and it was somehow the most important thing that happened all evening.

"Tomorrow at dawn," Aldous said finally, turning back toward his riders. "I'll come alone to the Door. My riders will wait at the ridge. Whatever happens at the arch — it should happen between people, not between armies." He hesitated. "Bone-carver. When this is over, whichever way it goes — if you ever carve a bone again, and if any dead voice ever speaks to you again — tell them Aldous kept his word. Tell them I didn't want this. Tell them—"

"I'll tell them," Jonah said. "They'll believe me. They're used to last messages."

Something crossed Aldous's face that was almost a smile and almost a wound. He mounted, and the three white shapes rode up out of the valley, and the snow closed behind them like water over stones.

* * *

Back in the vault, the siege stillness settled over everything like dust.

They ate bread and the last of the dried apples. Nell arranged her bones in a circle around her sleeping place, the way she did every night, a small parliament of the dead keeping watch. Jonah banked the lamp low and sat with his back against the wall, listening to the voices that remained — fewer every hour, thinner every hour, like guests leaving a party early and apologizing on the way out.

*Hold the line,* whispered old Marta, who had been dead two hundred years and had opinions about everything. *In my day we held lines. None of this erasing business. If you wanted someone gone you had to look them in the eye while you did it.*

"Marta," Jonah murmured, "you died falling into a well."

*I died falling into a well with dignity,* Marta said, *which is more than you'll manage.*

Nell giggled from her circle of bones, and the giggle echoed strangely in the vault, bright and small and alive, and Jonah thought: *this. Whatever happens tomorrow, this is what I am fighting for. Not a country. Not a memory. A child laughing in the dark because a two-hundred-year-old woman is rude about wells.*

"Nell," he said. "If it goes badly tomorrow. If I have to pay something at that Door — anything — I want you to know—"

"Don't," Nell said. She said it softly, but with the absolute authority of a person who has decided something at the cellular level. "Don't make bargains with tomorrow tonight. Tonight we sleep. Tomorrow we walk. That is the whole plan, and plans should be short when the world is ending, because long plans require a world to happen in."

Jonah blinked at her. "Did you make that up?"

"My mother," Nell admitted. "She made it up about plums. But it generalizes."

She curled up among her bones and was asleep within minutes, breath slow and even, one hand resting on the smallest carving — a bird, unfinished, that Jonah had made for her on the road and that had never yet spoken, though sometimes, when she slept, it seemed to lean toward her.

Jonah stayed awake.

Outside, far above, the winter fell and fell, patient as an oath, white as an unwritten page. Somewhere on the ridge, three riders waited without sleeping, because Justiciars did not sleep before duties. Somewhere in the dark, the Door Out stood in its hollow of old stone, bone-white and silent, waiting with the particular patience of things that feed on price.

And somewhere between all of those — between the waiting riders and the waiting Door and the waiting winter — a ferryman named Hobb was forgetting himself out of existence, a plum pit sat in a Justiciar's pocket like a seed nobody had planned, and a bone-carver lay awake in the dark rehearsing the names of the dead, one by one, like a man counting coins he already knew he was about to spend.

At midnight, every remaining voice in the vault spoke at once — not words, just a single long indrawn breath, the sound a crowd makes before a verdict —

and then, from the direction of the lake, faint and clear across the frozen water, came the tolling of a bell that no one had ever built.
