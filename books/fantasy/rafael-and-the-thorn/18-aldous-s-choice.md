## 18. Aldous's Choice

Aldous Vane had killed eleven people in his thirty years of service, and he remembered every one of them.

That was the cruelty of the Justiciar oath. People assumed the white riders forgot — that the erasure was a mercy they granted themselves, wiping their own consciences clean along with the country. It was not. The spell took Vessia from the world, but it left the men who enforced its dying exactly as they were, memory intact, so that someone would always remember what had been done and why. He was, in a sense, the last archive of Vessia's death, and he carried it the way a mule carries salt: without complaint, and with a back permanently bowed.

He remembered the glass rivers. He remembered the singing law-courts, where verdicts were chanted in three-part harmony and a bad judge was one who sang flat. He remembered the taste of honeyed ice on festival mornings, and his mother's hands, and the name of the street he'd been born on — Lantern Row, though there had been no lanterns on it since before he could walk, only the glow of the river under frost.

And he remembered the war. Eleven years of it. The burning of the granaries at Sennet Ford. The winter the enemy learned to unmake names, so that soldiers died not knowing whose son they were. He had been nineteen when the elders of both sides met in secret and agreed upon the only peace anyone could imagine: that Vessia would be unwritten, root and branch, so thoroughly that no one would ever again know what they were avenging. The oath-takers — himself among them, a boy too tired and too guilty to refuse — would hold the erasure shut from the outside until it finished its work. One generation. That was the promise. One generation of forgetting, and then no child would ever again burn for a country's name.

One generation had become two. Two had become forever, because forever is what fear calls patience when it grows old.

Aldous rode up the last frozen mile to the Door with three riders behind him and a headache like a nail being driven slowly through his conscience.

* * *

The Door did not look like much, which was fitting. Nothing about Vessia's ending had ever looked like much. It was an arch of bone-white stone standing alone in a fold of the hills, taller than a man, thin as a held breath, with snow falling through it and coming out the other side unchanged. No door swung in it. No light came from it. But Aldous felt it the way you feel a tooth you've lost — a constant, aching absence shaped exactly like something that used to be there.

The bone-carver stood before it with the child at his side.

Aldous had expected this moment for weeks, and he had rehearsed it the way he rehearsed everything: coldly, thoroughly, with contingency plans nested inside contingency plans like wooden dolls. Take the girl. Speak the quieting words. Carry her to the solstice and let the winter finish what the elders began. She would feel nothing. That was the part he clung to on the long rides, the part that let him sleep, more or less: the erasure at the end was painless. Gentler than sleep. You simply stopped having ever been afraid.

What he had not planned for was that she would wave at him.

She did it shyly, the way children wave at strangers they've decided are probably all right — a small hand half-raised, fingers opening and closing, her head tipped to study him. Her other hand was buried in the carver's coat, holding on. Behind them the arch stood silent, and around them the world was going white at the edges, the snow falling slower now, almost reverent, as if the sky itself were drawing a sheet up over the land.

"Stay behind me," Aldous said quietly to his riders. "No one draws unless I draw first."

"You're doing the voice," said Tam, the youngest of the three, who had been with him nine years and had never once learned when to be afraid.

"What voice."

"The court voice. The one where you've already decided how it ends and you're just waiting for everyone else to catch up." Tam squinted at the little group by the arch. "There's a child down there, Captain."

"I'm aware there's a child."

"And a man who smuggled her past us in a boat full of corpses who were, according to the river spirit's invoice, *extremely put out about it.*"

"That was a different jurisdiction," Aldous said, with dignity, and walked his horse down the slope.

* * *

Up close, the carver looked worse than Aldous remembered, which took some doing, because the last time Aldous had seen him the man had been lying to three Justiciars with his hands shaking so hard he'd nearly dropped the teapot. Now his face was grey with exhaustion, his coat was scorched at one cuff — the vault beneath the lake, Aldous guessed; word traveled even through a forgetting world, if you knew which silences to listen to — and he stood in front of the child with the particular stubbornness of a man who has decided exactly how he intends to die and resents any suggestion of rescheduling.

"Justiciar," the carver said. His voice was steady. His hands, folded carefully in front of him, were not.

"Jonah Whitlock." Aldous dismounted. Up close, the arch hummed faintly, a note below hearing, and he saw the carver flinch at it the way a man flinches at a sound only he can hear. Which, Aldous supposed, was precisely what it was. "You've brought her to the Door."

"She brought me."

"It doesn't matter who brought whom." Aldous heard his own court voice settle over the words, smooth and final, and hated it a little. "At sundown tomorrow the spell completes. Everything inside the boundary goes. Every carving, every grave, every last word your dead have been whispering to you since you were a boy. And the girl with it — gently, painlessly, into a morning where she was never born and no one grieves her. That is the law I swore. That is the peace I bought with—" He stopped. Recalibrated. "It is not a punishment, carver. It is a mercy. The kindest thing anyone will ever do for her."

The child tugged the carver's sleeve and whispered something. The carver shook his head slightly. Then he crouched down to her level, and whatever passed between them passed in that old language, the one older than kingdoms, and Aldous felt it go through him like a fishhook: he understood every word.

*She says you look sad,* the carver translated, straightening. *She asks if it's because of your oath.*

"My oath is not her concern."

*She says oaths are heavy things to carry alone.* The carver's eyes, when they met Aldous's, were not hostile. That was the worst part. He had prepared for hatred, for bargaining, for desperate pleading. He had not prepared for a tired man looking at him with something close to sympathy. *She says she watched her whole country carry theirs, and it broke every back on Lantern Row.*

The street name went through Aldous like a blade slipped between ribs.

"You don't get to know that street," he said, and his voice cracked on the second word, and behind him Tam made a small involuntary sound, because in thirty years none of them had ever heard the Captain's voice crack.

"She knows it," the carver said quietly. "It's in the bones. All of it is in the bones, Aldous. Every street. Every song. Every verdict sung flat. Your eleven dead — she can tell you their names if you ask. Not from records. From *them*. They're still in my keeping, most of them. They'd like to talk to you, I think. One of them — a ferryman, drowned at Sennet Ford — has been asking after you for two hundred years. He wants to know if you ever got the honeyed ice. Apparently he promised you some and died before the festival."

"Stop." Aldous's hand had gone to his sword without his permission. He made himself take it away again. Made himself breathe. The snow fell through the arch, unhurried, patient, and somewhere beneath his breastbone the nail in his conscience hammered twice.

"I have killed eleven people," he said, when he could trust his voice. "I remember each of them. Do you understand what that means, carver? Everyone thinks we forget. We don't. The spell takes the country and leaves us the bill. I am the last man alive who remembers Vessia, and I remember it *because* I helped kill it, and I will remember it until I die, and then Tam will carry it, and then whoever comes after Tam, forever, because someone must. Because if no one remembers why the peace cost what it cost, then someday someone will decide it wasn't worth it, and they'll dig the country back up, and the war starts again, and children burn in granaries, and—"

"And a six-year-old dies tomorrow," the carver said, "so that adults can keep feeling noble about a bargain they made before she was born."

The silence after that was very long. Snow ticked softly against Aldous's white cloak. Somewhere down the valley a crow complained once and thought better of it.

"I cannot break my oath," Aldous said at last. It was not the court voice. There was nothing smooth left in it at all. "You think I haven't wanted to? Thirty years, carver. Thirty years of riding out to snuff out every candle of remembering — a lullaby here, a carved token there, an old woman who kept a pressed flower she couldn't name. I told myself it was mercy every time. And it *was*, mostly, I believe it was, the peace has held, no one has burned — but I cannot break the oath, because the oath is the only thing left of me that isn't grief. Take it away and what am I? A man who remembers a dead country and did nothing to save the last living piece of it. Hollow. Free and hollow, and hollow men do terrible things trying to fill themselves back up."

"Yes," Jonah Whitlock said. "Exactly that. That's the price."

Aldous stared at him.

"The Door doesn't open for free," the carver said. He turned and laid his palm flat against the bone-white stone, and the humming deepened, and the snow around the arch began to fall upward, gently, like ash returning to a fire. "I found that out the hard way. Someone paid once so that I could walk out of this country as an infant. My mother. She stayed behind to hold it open, and the spell took her — not her life, her *self*, her name, her place in the world. And the Door will open again tomorrow, for Nell, but it wants paying. It wants memory. Real memory, given willingly." He looked at Aldous, and there was no triumph in his face at all, only a terrible gentleness. "You said you can't break your oath and you can't keep it. You're right. Both are true. So don't do either. *Give it up.* Walk to the arch and pay with the oath itself. Let the Door take the duty out of you — the vow, the law, the thirty years of court voice. Not your memories of Vessia. Keep those. Keep your mother's hands and Lantern Row and the honeyed ice. Just set down the weight. You said yourself what's left: a man, free and hollow. Well. Hollow can be filled, Aldous. Duty can't. Duty only gets heavier."

"That's not—" Aldous laughed, and it came out wrong, wet and short. "That's not how oaths work. You can't just *pay off* an oath like a debt at market."

"Why not?" said the child.

They both turned. She had spoken common — broken, accented, each word placed with the care of someone stacking stones across a stream — and she was looking up at Aldous with an expression of frank, unsentimental curiosity, the way children look at large animals they are deciding whether to pet.

"Why not?" she said again. "Oath is a word. Door eats words. You said. Heavy thing. Put it down." She shrugged, a small, complete shrug, the shrug of someone who had personally watched a country fail to survive taking itself too seriously. "Then carry something else. Something light. Honeyed ice, maybe."

Behind him, Tam made a noise that in a lesser man would have been a snort.

Aldous looked at the arch. The snow fell upward through it, patient as tide. He looked at his hands, which had held swords and writs and quieting words and, once, long ago, a cup of honeyed ice on a festival morning, promised by a ferryman who died before he could deliver. He looked at the child, who was watching him with no fear whatsoever, which was either innocence or the most devastating judgment he had ever received, and he suspected — he knew — it was the second one.

"If I do this," he said slowly, "the oath dies with me. There's no one after. Tam, Renna, the others — the spell completes tomorrow regardless. The order ends tonight, one way or the other. Either it ends with a dead child and a clean ledger, or it ends with me standing here hollow, having failed at the only thing I was ever for."

"Or," said Jonah Whitlock, "it ends with you standing here having succeeded at the only thing that was ever actually asked of you. Peace, Aldous. Not the ledger. The peace."

The nail in his conscience stopped hammering.

That was how he knew. All those years he had called the ache duty, had worn it like a badge, had shown it to young riders as proof of seriousness — and now, at the prospect of setting it down, it simply *stopped*, the way a tooth stops aching the moment the decision to pull it is made, and the relief was so vast and so shameful that his eyes filled before he could stop them.

"Captain?" Tam's voice, small, from the ridge. "Sir?"

"Ride back," Aldous said, without turning around. "All three of you. Go south past the ford and keep riding until spring, and when spring comes, find work that doesn't involve white cloaks. That's an order. It's the last order I'll ever give you, so make it a good one." He managed something that was almost a smile. "Tam. When you're a ferryman, or a baker, or whatever you become — be kind to strange children. All of them. Consider it the order's last clause."

"Sir, I—"

"*Go,* Tam."

Hoofbeats, fading. Snow falling upward. The child slipped her hand out of the carver's coat, crossed the trampled snow, and stood in front of Aldous, looking up at him. Then, gravely, formally, she extended her small hand, palm up, in the gesture Vessian children used on festival mornings to ask for sweets — a gesture no living person should have known, offered to him as if thirty years of white cloak and iron law were simply a costume she had politely waited for him to take off.

Aldous Vane knelt in the snow — not the kneeling of surrender, he noticed distantly, but the kneeling of a man about to lift something heavy from a child's shoulders, except the something was his — and took her hand.

"All right," he said, to her, to the arch, to the ferryman two hundred years dead who had owed him honeyed ice. "All right. Show me where to pay."

The arch began, very softly, to sing — three-part harmony, one voice flat — and Jonah Whitlock stepped forward with his palm still on the stone, and his face had gone pale as the bone.

"There's a problem," the carver said quietly. "The Door's counted what your oath weighs. It's not enough. It was never going to be enough — not for her. It wants more, Aldous. It wants—" He swallowed. "It wants everything I have. Every voice I've ever carried. And I don't know," he said, and his steady voice finally, finally broke, "I don't know if I can pay it."

The snow fell upward. The Door sang. And Aldous, free and hollow already in his heart, gripped the child's small hand tighter and said the only thing left to say:

"Then we'd better find out together."
