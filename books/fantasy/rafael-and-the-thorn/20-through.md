## 20. Through

The Door was not a door.

Nell had known this in the way she knew all the oldest things — not with her mind, which had been fed on common-tongue words for so long that her Vessian came out broken now, cracked like river ice in thaw — but with her bones, her blood, the small carved token her mother had pressed into her hand a lifetime and one winter ago. *A door is a thing that belongs to a wall,* she thought, in the language of glass rivers and singing law-courts, *and walls belong to countries, and we are standing at the edge of everything my country ever was.* The arch before them was bone-white and older than bone, older than the word for door, older perhaps than the word for edge. It did not lead anywhere. It simply stood in the snow at the heart of the erased world, patient as a held breath, waiting for someone brave enough or foolish enough to pay what it asked.

Snow fell upward here. Nell had noticed it an hour ago and decided not to be frightened of it. There had been too much to be frightened of this winter to spend any of it on falling snow that changed its mind.

She looked up at Jonah.

He was kneeling in the drifts beside the last of his carvings — the ones that still had voices in them, three of maybe forty, the rest gone silent one by one as the erasure reached into the valley and unwrote the dead like ink lifted from a page by careful invisible thumbs. His hands were bare. He never wore gloves when he worked, even in the killing cold; he said — he had said, once, in another life that had lasted only until autumn — that the dead deserved warm hands if anyone could give them.

"Almost," he said to the little carving in his palm. It was a sparrow, worn smooth, holding the voice of a woman who had died two hundred years ago complaining about her neighbor's fence. "Almost time."

The sparrow said something back. Nell heard it as a rustle, a whisper shaped like speech but not quite arriving there, because the dead spoke to Jonah alone and had only ever spoken to him. But she saw his face change, the way it always did — the small tilt of attention, like a man turning toward a window.

"She says," he translated, and his voice caught, and he laughed instead, wetly, "she says I was always too sentimental for a bone-carver. She says good riddance to the fence."

*Jonah,* thought Nell, in the old tongue, where his name sounded less like a name and more like a promise: *the-one-who-listens-when-nothing-left-is-speaking.*

Then Aldous stepped forward, and everything happened very fast, the way the truest things always do.

* * *

Later — much later, years later, though she would not know for years how much later — Nell would try to arrange the moments in order and find that they refused. They came back to her all at once or not at all, a fistful of snow thrown against memory:

Aldous unclasping the white badge of the Justiciars from his cloak and letting it fall into the drift, where it sank without a sound, as though the snow had been waiting for it.

Aldous saying, in a voice scraped hollow: *"I cannot open it. My oath-memory is all I have left of them — of any of them — but if it is the toll, then take it. Take it and be free of me."*

Jonah pressing both hands to either side of Aldous's face, forehead to forehead, the way you comfort a child or bless a king, and the light that passed between them — not bright, nothing here was bright, but *warm*, warm as bread, warm as a hearth in a house that no longer existed anywhere but inside a six-year-old girl.

And Aldous stepping back afterward and blinking at the sky with the mild, lost, unburdened look of a man waking from a long illness, saying, *"I'm sorry — where — was I going somewhere?"* And meaning it. And being happy.

Mercy, Nell understood then, watching him wander to the edge of the clearing to admire the upward-falling snow like a man seeing weather for the first time, mercy is the sharpest weapon there is. She resolved to remember that forever. She was the witness. Remembering was the whole of her job.

Then Jonah turned to her, and the fast part ended, and the slow part began.

* * *

"Nell." He crouched down to her height, which he always did, which had been the first kind thing about him — a grown man who came down to the level of a child instead of making the child climb up to his. His breath made clouds. Behind him the bone-white arch waited with the patience of a grave and the warmth of a womb, and Nell hated it, suddenly, completely, with her whole small furious heart.

"You'll go through," Jonah said. "You understand that, don't you? You're the witness. You carry all of it — the glass rivers, the law-courts, the lullabies, the names. Every name." His voice wobbled on *names*. "That's why it opens for you clean. Because you're not paying with anything. You *are* the payment. The country goes out through you and gets to start over honest."

"Incorrect," said Nell, in Vessian.

Jonah smiled the sad smile. He had learned some of her language over the winter — a carver's ear, quick with shape and sound — but he missed the grammar, and so he missed that she had just told him he was wrong.

"It won't hurt," he lied gently.

It would hurt him. She knew it the way she knew the old tongue: not learned but remembered. The Door took memories, and Jonah was going to feed it everything — every dead voice he'd ever carried, two hundred years of last words, the sparrow-woman and her fence, the ferryman's songs, the archive beneath the lake, the whole vast choir that had kept a lonely man company his whole quiet life. That was the price of opening it wide enough for a child to walk through carrying a country.

But that wasn't all. She had heard the dead whispering about it for days, in their fading voices, arguing with him. *Take the girl's grief too,* they'd urged. *Don't make her carry your dying alongside her nation's birth. Don't let her remember losing you. Let her walk out light.*

And Jonah — stubborn, gentle, mule-hearted Jonah — had agreed.

So she would step through into spring, and she would turn around to call his name, and she would not remember why her eyes were wet. She would not remember him at all. Not the knock in the first snow. Not the soup he burned while trying to learn her word for *thank you*. Not the night she woke screaming from the forgetting-dreams and he sat up with her till dawn, telling her the dead's funniest complaints, the woman with the fence, the farmer who died mid-argument about drainage ditches and whose last word was *anyway—*

Not any of it.

"No," Nell said, in common tongue this time, loud, so there could be no mistake. "No. Jonah. *Listen to me.*"

"I am listening," he said softly. "It's all I've ever done."

"You listen to the *dead*, you great stubborn — " she reached for a Vessian curse and got halfway there — "*thorn-briar*, listen to the *living* for once! Don't — "

"Nell." He gathered her in, and she beat her fists against his chest, and he let her, and his heart under her ear was steady and slow and unbearably alive. "Listen. All winter I've watched a country die. I've watched its last heir carry it alone through the snow, six years old, barefoot, braver than any Justiciar, braver than me. And the whole time, do you know what the dead kept telling me?" He pulled back and held her shoulders, and his eyes were shining and certain. "They kept telling me: *this one is yours to see through. This one is your last work.* And a carver doesn't keep the piece, Nell. A carver sets it free."

"I don't want to be set free," she whispered. "I want you to come with me. Both of you. The you-now and the you-after. I don't care if it's heavy. Heavy is fine. I've carried heavy all winter."

"I know," said Jonah, and kissed the top of her head. "Heavy is fine. But you shouldn't have to."

The arch began, very quietly, to sing.

It was the law-court song — she recognized it from her mother's humming, from the fragmenting music-box of her own memory — the sound Vessia made when it opened its courts each morning, when it married its people, when it named its children. The snow around the arch melted in a perfect circle. Green showed through. Actual green, impossible green, the green of a spring that had no right to exist on the far side of a door in deep winter.

"It's ready," Jonah said. He stood. In his hand was a single blank bone — smooth, white, featureless, the first carving he had ever made that held no voice at all. "Go on through, sweetheart. Spring's waiting. Whole world's waiting. Room in it for a new country — I'm sure of it. Someone very wise told me so." He winked, and it was terrible and wonderful and she wanted to kill him.

"You will forget me," she said.

"I know," said Jonah Whitlock, bone-carver, listener, liar, hero, and he was crying now, openly, not hiding it, giving her even that honestly. "But you won't. That's the trade. That's always been the trade. Somebody remembers, somebody gets to live. My mother knew it. Now I do." He knelt one last time. "Be happy. That's all. Be so happy it makes up for — "

"*Goodbye is incorrect grammar,*" Nell sobbed, in Vessian, which means: *goodbye is a lie we tell because the truth has no tense.* And she turned, and she walked through the singing arch into the green.

* * *

Spring.

Real spring — not the false green of the Door's threshold but a whole morning of it, spread out before her like a country that hadn't been written yet, which was exactly what it was. Rolling hills still soft with new grass. A river running bright and unhurried, doing the work of a glass river without knowing it. Birds — ordinary birds, common birds, birds with no history at all — singing in the hawthorn. The air smelled of mud and blossom and growing things, smelled of *future*, and it poured into Nell like water into a cracked cup.

Behind her the arch stood silent in a hillside that had no snow on it at all, as though the winter had happened somewhere else, to someone else, which in every way that mattered it had.

She turned around to call his name.

And Jonah walked out.

He came through the arch smiling — a wide, easy, empty-eyed smile, the smile of a man out for a morning stroll in weather he approved of — and the sight of it broke her heart in a place she hadn't known hearts went. He looked lighter. That was the cruelty of it. He looked *happy*, unweighted, a man with no dead in his pockets and no ghosts at his elbows for the first time since boyhood, and he didn't know what it had cost, and he never would, and she was the only keeper of it, and she was six years old, and the whole weight of two nations settled onto her small shoulders like a mantle cut exactly to fit.

In his hand hung a single blank bone. He turned it over, frowning pleasantly, the way you frown at a stone you picked up because it felt nice.

"Huh," said Jonah, to nobody, to the spring, to her — to her most of all, though he didn't know it. "Wonder where I got this."

He looked up. His gaze found her and slid off, friendly and vacant, the way water slides off oiled wood. No flicker. No ghost of recognition. Nothing.

Nothing — except that his feet, entirely without instruction from his face, carried him across the grass to stand beside her. Close. Closer than a stranger stands. And when a cold wind came down off the hills, unexpected in all that sunshine, he crouched without thinking and tucked his coat around her shoulders, murmuring, "Here, love, you'll catch your death," in the tone of a man who had said it ten thousand times to someone, sometime, somewhere he couldn't name.

Nell looked up at him through the coat-collar, at the smiling empty blue eyes, and made the choice that would define the rest of her long life:

She let him.

Some prices, she thought — in the old tongue, in the new tongue, in whatever tongue the new country would grow speaking — *some prices should not be undone. Some debts are paid in the wrong direction on purpose. He bought me a spring. The least I can do is live in it loudly enough for both of us.*

"Thank you," she said clearly, testing the common words, and Jonah blinked at her, pleased and puzzled.

"You're welcome," he said, having no idea what for. "Er — do I know you?"

"No," said Nell truthfully, and took his hand anyway, and his fingers curled around hers as naturally as roots finding soil. "But you're going to."

Together they started walking downhill toward the river, toward the spring, toward the blank page where a country waits for its first word — and behind them, faint as a heartbeat, almost too soft to hear, the blank bone in Jonah's other hand gave the smallest possible shiver.

As if something inside it were waiting patiently to be remembered.
