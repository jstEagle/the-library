## 21. Spring, Unremembered

The world on the other side of the Door smelled like rain.

Nell stood in wet grass up to her ankles and breathed it in — real rain, ordinary rain, the kind that fell because clouds were heavy and not because a country was being unwritten. Behind her, Jonah came through slowly, one hand trailing along the bone-white arch as if he needed it to remember how walking worked. His eyes were the color of a sky that had forgotten what it was for. He was smiling. He was holding a single blank carving, turning it over in his fingers like a man trying to recall where he'd set down his cup.

He looked at her.

"Do I know you?" he asked. Not unkindly. The way you'd ask a stranger at a well.

Nell had carried three languages across a dying country: the old tongue of Vessia, the common speech of the kingdoms, and the wordless language of grief. She reached for all of them and found none of them would do. So she did what she had done since the first snow, since the door of a bone-carver's cottage opened onto a barefoot child who could not explain herself.

She took his hand.

"No," she said. "But you will."

* * *

They walked out of the hills together, because there was nowhere else to go and because he seemed to trust her without knowing why, which was its own kind of heartbreak. In the first village — a river town called Marrowford, which made Nell laugh until she cried, though Jonah couldn't have said why either — they found work. A carver was always welcome somewhere. Bones and antler and driftwood waited everywhere for patient hands, and Jonah's hands were still patient even if the rest of him was new-minted.

He rented a room above the chandler's shop. He hung his tools on pegs in an order he didn't remember learning but couldn't bear to change. And every evening when the light went amber over the river, he sat on the step outside, and Nell sat beside him, and she told him stories.

That was the arrangement. She never asked permission. She simply began, the way spring begins, and he never once told her to stop.

"Tell me about the hero," he said, the third evening. He'd taken to calling for it the way other men called for supper. "The one from your country."

"You don't have a country," Nell reminded him gently. "You're from here. You've always been from here. You told me so yourself this morning."

"I know." He frowned at his hands, which were nicked and scarred in patterns that suggested decades of work. "It's just — sometimes when you talk about him, my chest does something strange. Like a door opening onto a warm room I've never been in."

Nell looked out at the river so he wouldn't see her face do what it wanted to do.

"Well," she said. "Once upon a time there was a man who lived alone in a valley where magic couldn't follow, and he talked to the dead, because the living found him unsettling."

"That sounds lonely."

"It was. But he was good at it. Loneliness, I mean. He'd practiced." She turned the blank bone over in her lap — the one he'd carried through the Door, the one he'd left on the mantel and kept almost touching ever since, the way you keep a chair empty for someone. "He carved bones, and the bones remembered their people's last words, and he listened to them all. Every night. Hundreds of voices. He thought it was a curse."

"Wasn't it?"

"He thought so." She smiled. "But here's the thing about a man who listens to the dead: when a child comes knocking in the snow, barefoot and mute and speaking a language older than any kingdom, he doesn't turn her away. He makes her soup. He lies to the King's own Justiciars — the first lie of his whole life, and his hands shook for hours after, and he'd tell you it was worth it if he could remember telling it."

Jonah laughed. It was a good laugh, unburdened, and it hurt her in the place where her love lived.

"What happened to him?" he asked. "The carver?"

Nell had decided, in the first week, that she would never lie to him. That was the whole point of everything — Vessia had died because someone decided forgetting was mercy, and she had been chosen to remember honestly, not kindly. But honesty had rooms in it, and she was allowed to choose which ones to show.

"He gave everything away," she said. "To save someone. And the giving took the memory of the giving, so he'll never know what he paid. Which means—" she tapped the blank bone, "—somewhere in the world is a debt with no debtor, a sacrifice no one owns. I think about that a lot."

"That's a sad story."

"It's not finished yet."

* * *

Spring deepened. The chandler's wife taught Nell to dip candles and pronounced her "quick as a ferret," which Nell chose to receive as a compliment. Jonah took commissions — buttons, chess pieces, a memorial plaque for the ferryman's late dog, a magnificent animal by all accounts — and his reputation grew the way reputations do in small towns, one astonished customer at a time.

"They say you're the finest carver in three counties," Nell reported one evening, delivering bread and gossip in equal measure. "Old Marta says your hands remember things your head doesn't. She says it like it's spooky. I told her that's ridiculous."

"Is it?"

"Completely," said Nell, who knew it wasn't.

Because it was true, and she watched it happen daily. His fingers found grain-lines without looking. He hummed a tune while he worked — a low, looping melody — and once, when she asked what it was, he stopped, bewildered. "I don't know. Something my mother sang, maybe? Did I have a mother?" And Nell had said, "Everyone has a mother," which was true, and also the most dishonest sentence she'd ever spoken, and she'd carried it around for days like a stone in her shoe.

Some prices shouldn't be undone. She believed that. She had watched Aldous walk out of the Door behind them, a man without duty, free and hollow, and within a month he'd sent them a letter from the coast saying he'd taken up fishing and found, to his surprise, that he liked it — that the sea asked nothing of him but patience, and patience turned out to be enough. Hollow places fill, given time. If she poured Vessia back into Jonah — the valley, the vault, the mother who stayed to hold a door open while her son escaped — she would be pouring a country's grief into a man who had already paid to set it down. Twice.

So instead she built beside him. That was the phrase she used in her own head, in the old tongue, where it sounded like laying stones for a house: *build beside*. Not restore. Not replace. Add.

And every evening, the stories.

* * *

"The thing you have to understand about the Thorn," Nell said one night in early summer, "is that she was insufferable."

Jonah nearly dropped his pipe. "Insufferable? You've only ever described her as brave."

"She was both. Bravery and insufferability are cousins." Nell counted virtues off on her fingers. "She refused to wear shoes in winter, which everyone said was magic, but really she just hated boots. She argued with ghosts. Actual ghosts. They'd say *flee, little Thorn, the riders are coming*, and she'd say *I'm eating my soup, wait your turn.*"

Jonas laughed so hard he had to set the pipe down. "She sounds terrible."

"She was six," said Nell. "You're allowed to be terrible at six. It's practically the law." She let that sit a moment, watching him smile at the fire, before she went on, softer. "There was a man who protected her, though. Through the whole winter. He crossed a country that was forgetting itself, and the road only rose up out of the ground when she walked on it, and he never once complained. Well. Once. There was an incident with a coffin-boat."

"A coffin-boat."

"The dead can be very chatty when you smuggle them downstream. He traded three years of his hearing to a river spirit for safe passage, and then spent the whole night shushing corpses." Nell grinned. "One of them was a tax collector. Even dead, he had opinions about tolls."

"I feel like I've heard this story," Jonah said slowly. He said it most evenings now, with increasing puzzlement. "Not heard it — I mean it feels like remembering. Like the shape of it fits some hollow in me."

"Stories do that," Nell said. "Good ones, anyway. They find the empty rooms and move in."

He looked at her across the firelight for a long moment. "You're a strange child, Nell."

"I'm seven."

"You're seven going on two hundred."

* * *

In high summer, the letter came.

It was from a scholar in the capital — a woman named Professor Ilvane, who wrote on thin paper in a cramped, excited hand. Word of the carver of Marrowford had traveled, as word does, and the professor had a proposition: there were old bones surfacing all over the northern provinces, bones that predated every kingdom's records, and the universities were desperate for someone who might read them. Rumor said the carver had a gift. Rumor said stranger things, too — that he'd come out of the western hills last winter with a child, from a direction where there were no roads.

Jonah read the letter twice at the kitchen table while Nell pretended to practice her letters and actually watched his face.

"They want me to come look at bones," he said. "Old ones. Older than anything." He rubbed his thumb along the edge of the page. "Nell, when I hold old bone, I get — I don't know how to describe it. An ache. Like standing outside a house where people are singing and you can't quite make out the words."

Nell's pen stopped.

She had known this day would come. Of course it would. The dead of Vessia were not gone; they were scattered, waiting in antler and femur and riverbed ivory for ears that could hear them, and there was exactly one pair of such ears in the world, and she had helped carry them out of the dark. The gift hadn't died at the Door. It had gone dormant, like seed under snow. And seeds, she had learned, were the entire point of everything.

"We could go," she said carefully. "In autumn, maybe. When the roads are quiet."

"You'd come with me?"

"You'd be hopeless without me. You'd forget to eat. You forget to eat *now*, and we live above a chandler's shop with a woman who force-feeds you."

He smiled, but his eyes stayed on the letter, on the word *older than anything*, and Nell saw the ache move through him like weather. She thought of her mother pressing the bone token into her hand. She thought of the archive under the lake, ten thousand last words, patient as snowfall. She thought: *not yet. Let him have the summer. Let him have the dog memorial and the bad pipe tobacco and the evenings. Let the hollow fill a little more first.*

Then she thought something else, something that arrived with the certainty of the frost that had started everything:

*When we go, the bones will speak to him again. And the first voice he hears — the very first — will be hers.*

* * *

That evening they sat on the step as they always did, and the river went gold, and the chandler's wife shouted up that supper was ready and then immediately began eating it herself.

"Tell me the ending," Jonah said.

"There isn't one yet."

"Every story has an ending."

"Not the good ones. The good ones end with *and then they built something*." Nell pulled her knees up. "But I'll tell you where it stands right now. The Thorn got out. The country she came from stays sleeping — and that's right, that has to stay right, because waking it wrong would be worse than losing it. But she remembers all of it. Every glass river, every singing courtroom, every name. And she's decided what to do with all that remembering."

"What?"

"She's going to build a new country. Small at first. Maybe just a house above a chandler's shop. Maybe just two people and a bad pipe and a dog memorial that's honestly far too large." She bumped her shoulder against his arm. "And when it's ready — when the foundations are honest — she'll carve the first word of it into bone. And whatever that word turns out to be, it won't be *Vessia*. It'll be something new. Something that earned itself."

Jonah was quiet for a while. The fireflies came up out of the grass, tentative, testing the dark.

"Nell," he said finally. "Can I tell you something strange?"

"You tell me something strange every day."

"When you tell the stories — the carver, the Thorn, the white riders, all of it — I feel like I'm *in* them. Not like a listener. Like..." He turned the blank bone over in his hands. He still kept it close. He still didn't know why. "Like I'm the house the story lives in."

Nell looked at him — this man who had given away every voice he'd ever loved so that a stranger-child could have a morning like this one, who would never know it, who sat beside her now holding the proof in his palms like a stone he couldn't stop touching — and she felt the truth rise in her throat, whole and enormous, wanting out.

She swallowed it. Not tonight. Maybe not for years. Some doors open only once, and some gifts are only gifts if the giver stays anonymous.

"Then take care of the house," she said. "It's a good one."

He laughed, and knocked his shoulder against hers, and inside the chandler's wife shouted that if the stew went cold she was feeding it to the dog memorial, and the fireflies rose over the river like slow green sparks, and Nell — witness, heir of nothing, keeper of a country that slept — sat in the doorway of the life she was building and added one more evening to its foundations.

Tomorrow, she thought, she would start teaching him the old tongue. Just a few words. *Thank you*, maybe. *Home.* Words a person could carry without knowing what they weighed.

And someday — when the ache in his hands grew too loud, when the professor's letter could wait no longer — she would take him north to the bones, and stand beside him in the silence afterward, and catch him when the first voice came home.

She already knew whose it would be.

She already knew what it would say.
