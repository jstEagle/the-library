## 1. The Charm That Bit Back

The trouble with Veymark in festival season was that everyone remembered too much.

They hung lanterns shaped like teardrops from every balcony on Copperlace Row, and the glass caught the light and threw it back amber, so the whole street seemed to be weeping honey. Bakers sold little cakes pressed with the faces of dead relatives — sugar eyes, sugar smiles — and children ran through the crowds clutching ribbons printed with names they were too young to have known. Remembrance Eve. The one night a year when the city was permitted to miss what it had lost out loud.

Ulyana Coldharbour moved through all of it like water through a net, unnoticed, unremarkable, her collar up and her hands in her pockets where three empty charm-bottles waited against her palm like small cold eggs.

She had been lifting memories for eleven years, and she had rules. Rule one: never take from someone who was weeping — grief soured in the bottle, turned bitter as burnt sugar within a day. Rule two: never take from a crowd; charms lifted from festivals came out muddled, full of other people's laughter bleeding through the edges. Rule three, the rule above all rules: never look at your own face in a shop window while you worked, because a thief who saw herself became someone with something to lose, and thieves with something to lose got caught.

Tonight she broke none of these rules, which is why it took her so long to understand that she should have invented a fourth.

* * *

He was standing at the edge of Lanternmaker's Square, this stranger, apart from the crowd the way a stone stands apart from a river. Tall, dark-haired, with the kind of stillness that most people spent their whole lives failing to achieve. He wasn't watching the lanterns or the dancers or the sugar-cake sellers. He was watching his own hands, turning them over slowly, palm to ceiling, palm to floor, as though checking whether they still belonged to him.

Ulyana clocked him the way she clocked everyone: coat, boots, purse-weight, the telltale bulge at the breast pocket where memory-charms rode against the heart. His bulge was wrong, though. Too large. A single charm, but a heavy one — heavy the way a bottle is heavy when it's been filled to the brim instead of the usual swallow.

A man carrying a lifetime's worth of memory in one vial. Either he was a collector, or he was moving house, or —

Or he was exactly the kind of man who deserved to be robbed, she thought, and smiled at herself, and began to drift toward him through the crowd with the unhurried grace of smoke.

She chose the moment well. The dancers wheeled past in their ribbon-lines, the fiddler hit the fast turn of the *Remembrance Reel*, two hundred pairs of feet stamped at once and shook the cobbles, and in that thunder Ulyana's fingers did what eleven years had trained them to do: found the seam of his coat, found the pocket, found the bottle, and lifted it out smooth as a fish slipping upstream.

He didn't flinch. That was the first strange thing. Most marks felt *something* — a shiver down the arm, a phantom itch where the charm had ridden warm against them. This man simply kept turning his hands over, palm to ceiling, palm to floor, as though the theft hadn't happened at all.

As though there was nothing left in him worth guarding.

Ulyana melted into the crowd, rounded the fountain, ducked under the awning of a shuttered candle-shop, and only then allowed herself the small private ritual: the bottle held up to the lantern-light, tilted, examined, appraised before she ever touched the stopper.

It was beautiful work. That was the second strange thing. The glass was old — old the way heirlooms are old, with a faint green tint and bubbles suspended in it like held breath — and the stopper was sealed not with wax but with a thread of silver wire wound seven times. She'd seen fine charms before. She'd stolen from collectors and archivists and once, gloriously, from a visiting duke. But she had never seen a charm made like this one, because nobody made charms like this anymore. Nobody had for a hundred years.

Her thumb brushed the stopper.

The city noise fell away.

Not faded — *fell*, cut clean, as though someone had dropped a curtain between her and Lanternmaker's Square. The lantern-light went thin and grey. And inside her head, in a voice that was not a voice, more like ink spreading through water, words began:

*On the eve of Remembrance, when the lanterns are lit and the city remembers what it has lost, the thief will come. She will lift the vessel from the vessel and find no memory within, for the vessel holds none.*

Ulyana's hand went numb. The bottle grew cold enough to burn.

*What she takes is not a life but a page, and the page says: tonight the Veiled King wakes, and the crown remembers its purpose, and the man who wears the smile beneath it begins to forget.*

*Let her read and let her run. It changes nothing. It was written a hundred years ago tonight.*

The voice stopped. The square crashed back in — fiddle, laughter, the smell of hot sugar and lamp-oil — and Ulyana stood under the candle-shop awning with her heart slamming against her ribs and the charm dangling from fingers that had gone white at the knuckles.

She looked up, wildly, toward the fountain.

The stranger was gone.

* * *

She told herself it was a trick. Of course it was a trick. There were charm-makers in the Undermarket who sealed curses into bottles and sold them to paranoid merchants — wards dressed up as wares, hexes disguised as heirlooms. Someone had known she worked the festival crowds. Someone had baited a trap with pretty glass and old-fashioned silver wire, waiting for the famous Ulyana Coldharbour to come along and pick it up.

Except traps didn't know your trade. Traps didn't call you *thief* in a voice like spilled ink. And traps certainly didn't name tonight's date — the eve of Remembrance, the exact night, the very hour — in words that claimed to be a century old.

By the time she reached the Undermarket, down the wet stairs behind the tannery and through the door painted with a fish that had been repainted so many times it had become an abstract eel, she had constructed and demolished six theories and settled on a seventh: sell it fast, sell it blind, never open it again.

Which was why she was standing in the back room of Doss Halloway's counting-house, lamplight low, watching the best fence in Veymark hold her century-old prize up to the light with the expression of a man examining a corpse.

Doss was sixty if he was a day, built like a collapsed chimney, with spectacles thick enough to see tomorrow through. He turned the bottle. Turned it again. Held it to his eye. And then, slowly, deliberately, set it down on the felt of his table and pushed it away from himself across the cloth, the way a man pushes away a plate of food that has begun to move.

"No," he said.

"It hasn't even been opened—"

"I don't need it opened." He wiped his palms on his waistcoat, twice, though they weren't visibly dirty. "I can feel it from here. You know how I buy, Ulyana? I put my thumb on the glass and I listen. Every charm sings a little. Grief sings low. Love sings high. First kisses, last days, the taste of a mother's bread — they've all got notes." He looked at her over the spectacles, and his eyes behind the glass were frightened, which was itself a thing she had never seen. "This one doesn't sing. It *reads*. Like a letter. Like somebody sat down and wrote me a message and sealed it in glass and addressed it to whoever was fool enough to steal it."

"Then it's addressed to me," Ulyana said. Her voice came out steadier than she felt. "So sell me the address."

"Girl." Doss leaned forward. The lamplight made hollows of his face. "There are charms in this city older than the Order of the Quill. There are charms that were buried with kings. There is exactly one kind of charm that predates the Ban, and every one of those was supposed to have been melted down a hundred years ago, because the people who made them weren't bottling memories." He tapped the table beside the bottle without touching it. "They were bottling *instructions*."

The word went through her colder than the charm had.

"You opened it," Doss said. It wasn't a question. "Don't lie to me, you've got the look. What did it say?"

And because lying to Doss Halloway was like lying to a scale — it always showed in the weight — she told him. The lanterns. The thief. The vessel holding no memory. The Veiled King waking tonight, the crown remembering its purpose, the man beneath the smile beginning to forget. *Written a hundred years ago tonight.*

When she finished, Doss sat very still for a long time. Somewhere overhead, muffled by floors and distance, the festival roared on — the fiddle, the feet, the laughter of a city that didn't know what it was dancing over.

"The Veiled King," he said finally, "is a story grandmothers use to frighten children off the palace walls. There's no such thing."

"Then the prophecy's wrong."

"Prophecies aren't wrong, girl. That's what makes them prophecies. They're just early." He stood, abruptly, and crossed to the window, and stood with his back to her looking out at nothing. "Take it and go."

"You won't touch it?"

"I wouldn't touch it with a bishop's hand." He glanced back at her, and for a moment the fear in his face softened into something almost gentle. "Eleven years I've fenced for you, Ulyana. You're the steadiest hand I know. So hear me steady: whatever that bottle is, it knows your name. Things that know your name don't get sold. They get *answered*."

She picked up the charm. It was warm now — warm as skin, warm as a living thing — and through the green-tinted glass she could see the faintest shift of movement inside, like a page turning in a book no one was holding.

"Doss," she said quietly. "The man I lifted it from. He didn't feel it leave. He just stood there turning his hands over like he was checking they were real. What kind of man carries a hundred-year-old prophecy against his heart and doesn't notice it's gone?"

Doss didn't answer right away. When he did, his voice had gone flat and careful, the voice of a man stepping around a hole in the floor.

"The kind," he said, "who isn't carrying it. The kind it's carrying."

Ulyana walked home through the dying festival with the charm in her pocket and the city's laughter ringing false around her. At the corner of Copperlace Row she passed a poster fresh-pasted to the wall, the paste still shining: the Order of the Quill announcing the festival's closing ceremony. Beneath the official seal, in letters tall enough to read from a moving carriage, was the portrait of the king — beloved, smiling, crowned.

She had seen that face ten thousand times. Everyone in Veymark had.

But she had never noticed, until tonight, that the king's painted smile didn't reach his eyes — and that his eyes, if you looked long enough, looked less like a man's eyes than like windows in a house where all the lights had gone out.

Behind her, in her pocket, the charm turned warm.

And somewhere across the sleeping city, in a room she could not yet name, a quiet stranger was reaching into his breast pocket for a bottle that was no longer there — and finding, for the first time in three days, that his hands had stopped.
