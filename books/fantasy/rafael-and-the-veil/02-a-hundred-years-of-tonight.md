## 2. A Hundred Years of Tonight

The Order of the Quill kept its archive in the old customs house on Candlewick Stair, a building that had been sealed for longer than Ulyana had been alive and was said to be warded by oaths rather than locks. Oaths were harder to pick than locks. Locks had tumblers; oaths had teeth.

She crouched in the shadow of the customs house's buttress and watched the moon slide behind the bell tower of Saint Verity's, counting her breaths the way she'd taught herself at nine years old, when breathing slow meant not being caught. Below her, Veymark was dressing itself for the Remembrance Festival. Lanterns went up along the canal lines one by one, little amber hearts strung between the leaning houses, and somewhere a fiddler was tuning for tomorrow's dancing. The whole city smelled of hot sugar and river mud and, faintly, of the memory-smoke that always thickened this time of year — the collective exhale of ten thousand people remembering their dead.

Ulyana did not remember hers. That was the joke she never told anyone.

The charm sat against her sternum where she'd tucked it inside her coat, cold as a coin left overnight in snow. She had tried to sell it, and Sable had thrown her out. She had tried to open it herself over a candle and a bowl of saltwater, the way she opened every charm she lifted, and the wax had guttered black and the water had frozen in its bowl in a room warm enough to steam. So now there was only one place left to look, and it was the one place in Veymark with more wards than Sable's paranoia and colder water than a frozen bowl.

*Every page blank except one.* The thought arrived uninvited, the way thoughts do when they're true. *And the one page knows your name.*

It didn't know her name. It couldn't. She was careful. She was so careful it was practically a religion: gloves in summer, aliases in triplicate, no charm ever traced back twice to the same buyer. She had built an ordinary life out of extraordinary caution, brick by brick, and last night a stranger with no memories had put the first crack in it, and tonight she intended to find out how deep the crack went.

The customs house door was iron-banded oak, and the oath on it was visible if you knew how to look — a shimmer like heat off summer cobbles, laid across the wood in a lattice. Ulyana knew how to look. What she didn't know, until she pressed her gloved palm flat against the grain, was whether the Order's old oaths still answered to anything at all.

They answered to blood. Of course they did. Everything in Veymark worth having cost something.

She opened her palm with the little crescent blade she kept for exactly this purpose — not her blood, never her own name attached to anything — and smeared it along the seam. The shimmer flared white, tasted whatever it tasted, and found nothing in her it recognized as forbidden. A thief with no past, offering a hand with no history. The oath had nothing to hold onto. For the first time in her life, being nobody was useful.

The door swung inward without a sound.

Inside, dust lay like first snow, undisturbed, luminous in the light of her shuttered lantern. The archive rose around her in tiers of iron shelving, and every shelf held books — thousands of them, tens of thousands, spines cracked and swollen, ledgers and registries and confessionals going back past the founding of the city. This was where the Order kept everything magic had ever written down, back when writing about magic wasn't a hanging offense. They had sealed the doors a century ago and walked away, because some knowledge is easier to bury than burn.

Ulyana moved up the central stair, reading spines by slivers of lantern-light. *Registry of Bound Spirits, 812–840. Confessions of the Ashwright Circle. On the Nature of the Veil, vol. III.* Her breath made ghosts in the cold air. Somewhere above her, water dripped with the patience of a thing that had been dripping for a hundred years and intended to continue.

She found the prophecy book on the top tier, alone, in a case of black glass that should have been locked and wasn't — or rather, whose lock hung open, hinges rusted, as though someone had opened it long ago and simply never bothered to close it again. As though the book had been waiting to be taken.

It was small. That was the first wrongness. She had imagined a tome, a grimoire heavy as a paving stone, and instead it fit in one hand: grey leather gone soft as skin, no title, no clasp. The second wrongness was the weight. Books are heavier than they look. This one weighed nothing at all, like carrying a folded shadow.

She set it on the reading desk beneath the high window and opened it to the first page.

Blank.

Second page. Blank. Third, fourth, fifth — blank, blank, blank, the paper clean and cream-colored and utterly empty, not faded-blank but *never-written*-blank, as if the book had been bound from a hundred sheets of pure potential and then forgotten. She turned faster. Blank. Blank. Blank. Her heart began doing something arrhythmic and unpleasant. She was halfway through the book when the pages stopped being paper.

The page under her thumb was vellum, and it was full of ink, and the ink was fresh.

*On the eve of the Remembrance Festival,* it read, in a hand that looped and slanted like handwriting learned in another century, *a thief will lift a charm from a man who has none. The charm will hold no memory but this page. The thief will come here, to the sealed house, seeking the original, and she will find it exactly as I have described, because I have described it, and description is a kind of binding.*

Ulyana's hands went very still. Outside, the fiddler had stopped tuning. Even the drip upstairs seemed to pause.

*Her name is not important,* the page continued. *Names can be changed. What matters is what she carries: a childhood bought in bottles, hoarded ten years, never once worn. A thief with no past of her own. Only such a one can stand at the crown and cut the knot, for the Veil cannot take what a person has already given away.*

She read it three times. The words did not rearrange themselves, which was somehow worse. They just sat there, patient as the dust, written — she checked the colophon at the bottom with fingers that had begun to tremble — *in the year 741.* One hundred and six years ago.

*Written a century ago,* she thought, and the thought had teeth, and it bit. *Describing tonight. Describing me. Describing this exact moment, my glove on this exact page.*

Because that was the third wrongness, the one she'd been circling since the beginning: the passage didn't end. It continued onto the next leaf, and the next, an entire quire of vellum, and it was describing the room. The high window. The black glass case hanging open. *She will count the shelves and find there are more than she counted before.* Ulyana looked up sharply at the iron tiers, counted, looked back down. There were more. There were absolutely more.

*And while she reads,* the book said, *they will come for her.*

The knock, when it came, came from below — three blows on the front doors, iron on iron, polite as a tax collector and twice as inevitable. Then voices. Not many. Witch-hunters never needed many; they needed certainty, and certainty carried well.

"Order business," someone called up the stairwell, in the tone of a person who already knew the answer. "We know you're in there, charm-witch. The door remembers whose blood it drank."

Ulyana snuffed her lantern and stood in the dark with a century-old book that knew her evening better than she did, and did the arithmetic she'd been avoiding all night. Rafael's charm had named tonight as the night the Veiled King wakes. The hunters had come within the hour of her crossing the threshold. Either the Order watched this house — possible — or something had told them exactly when to come — also possible, and worse, because things that gave orders to witch-hunters were not generally things that could be outrun.

She heard the front doors open anyway, though she'd smeared them shut with her own blood. That was the part that turned dread into something with edges. Wards that let hunters through and kept thieves out were wards. Doors that opened for anyone who knocked were invitations, and invitations meant someone wanted her here, cornered, with the book.

Fine. She'd been cornered before. Cornered was just a small room with bad odds, and she had spent her whole life learning the exits.

She took the book. It slid into her coat against the dead charm, two wrongnesses pressed together, and she was halfway to the loading window at the rear — the one she'd scouted, the one with the drainpipe — when the second knock came.

It came from inside the wall beside her.

* * *

There was a door in the archive wall that was not a door. Ulyana would swear to that afterward, to Marisol, to anyone who asked: she had scouted this building for two days, and buildings were her scripture, and there had been no door. But there it was now, standing proud of the brickwork like a bone through skin — a plain door, dark wood, iron latch, entirely without frame or reason, and it was opening inward as she watched.

Rafael stood in the gap.

He looked worse than he had at the festival market. Paler, as if the lamplight had been drained out of him along with everything else, and his eyes had the fixed brightness of a man holding himself upright by will alone. But his voice, when he spoke, was steady, and it was the steadiness that frightened her most, because it wasn't his. It sounded borrowed, like the calm of a puppet whose strings had been pulled taut.

"You've read it," he said. Not a question.

"Some of it." She kept her voice low, eyes flicking to the stairwell, where boot-heels were beginning their deliberate ascent. "It knows things it shouldn't. It knows *me*, or a version of me, and it's been waiting a hundred years to be right."

"It's always right." He said it the way other people said *it's raining*. "That's the problem. Come away from the window, Ulyana. The pipe breaks four feet down. They know you'd use it."

"How do you—"

"I have instructions," he said, and something crossed his face then, quick and human and anguished, there and gone like a fish under ice. "I hate the instructions. But I have them, and the first one is that you don't die tonight. So." He held out his hand, palm up, and in the thin light from the high window she saw the scar across it — curved, ridged, old beyond its apparent age, shaped unmistakably like a crown.

Behind him, the door showed not the customs house stairwell but somewhere else entirely: a corridor lit by candles that burned without melting, stretching back farther than any building in Veymark was deep. Cold air came off it, smelling of stone and incense and something older underneath, something like the inside of a locked room that had never been opened.

The boots reached the second tier. A hunter's voice, bored and certain: "Check the reading desks."

Ulyana looked at the drainpipe she could no longer trust, at the stairs she couldn't beat, at the stranger wearing a borrowed body who had come through a wall to keep her alive. Every instinct she owned said *run, alone, now, the way you always have.* But the book in her coat was warm now, faintly, like a sleeping animal, and it had called her *a thief with no past of her own*, and for the first time in ten years Ulyana Coldharbour wanted very badly to know why.

She took his hand. The crown-scar was cool against her palm, and it pulsed once, gently, like something acknowledging receipt.

"Don't let go," Rafael said, and stepped backward into the impossible corridor, and pulled her through a door that shouldn't exist, into the dark that had been waiting a hundred years for both of them.
