## 3. What the Veil Wears

I did not know my own name until she said it back to me.

That is the first thing I should confess, and the last thing I wanted to say aloud: that Rafael was a word I wore like a borrowed coat, handed to me three days ago along with a set of instructions and a body that ached as though it had been sleeping for a very long time. Ulyana Coldharbour said it — *Rafael* — in the doorway of the archive, with witch-hunters' lanterns burning behind us and a door standing open where no door had been, and something in my chest turned over at the sound, the way a lock turns when it recognizes its key.

She stepped through first. I followed. The door closed behind us with a sound like a held breath released, and then we were standing in an alley behind a tannery, in air that stank of lime and old hides, and the festival bells of Veymark were ringing somewhere far above us, counting down the hours to Remembrance.

Ulyana put her back against the wall and laughed — one short, sharp note with no humor in it.

"You don't have a door," she said. "You don't have doors. You're a man I pickpocketed six hours ago."

"I know." My voice came out flat and strange. "That's rather the problem."

* * *

We walked because walking was better than standing still. She led; I followed; the city folded itself around us in the grey hour before dawn, when even Veymark's smugglers and lamplighters go home and the streets belong to cats and to anyone else who has nowhere to be. Rain had come and gone while we were inside the archive, and now every cobblestone held a small drowned reflection of the gaslamps, so that we walked on a sky full of dim orange stars.

She kept glancing at me sideways. I could feel her doing it, the way you feel weather changing.

"Say it again," I said finally. "The name."

"Rafael."

"Again."

"Rafael." Her eyes narrowed. "You're not joking."

"I'm trying to make it stick." I flexed my right hand inside my glove. It had been aching since the archive — a deep, grinding ache, like a tooth, centered in the palm. "Three days ago I woke up in a room above a chandler's shop on Cordwainer Street. I knew the city. I knew which coins were which. I knew how to read, how to fight, how to lie convincingly about my past — though I didn't know what my past was supposed to be, which made lying about it difficult. And I knew four things with absolute certainty, the way you know fire burns."

Ulyana stopped walking. A cat regarded us from atop a rain barrel with the contempt only cats and fences can manage.

"Tell me," she said.

"One: my name is Rafael. Two: tonight is Remembrance, and something wakes tonight. Three: there is a woman called Ulyana Coldharbour who bottles memories, and I must let her steal from me." I watched her face do something complicated. "Four: when she does, she will understand, and she will help me."

"That's not knowledge," she said quietly. "That's a script. Someone wrote you a part and wound you up and pointed you at me."

"Yes."

"And you just — obeyed? You walked into the Lantern Market knowing a thief would lift your charm?"

"I walked into the market because the instructions left no room for anything else." I pulled off the glove. "But there was one thing they didn't tell me about. One thing I found on my own."

I opened my hand under the gaslamp.

In the center of my palm, printed into the skin like a brand that had healed decades ago, was a scar the shape of a crown. Five points, a band beneath them, fine as engraving — the kind of mark a jeweler might press into wax, except that it was pressed into me, and it moved. Not much. Only when I wasn't looking directly at it. In the corner of my eye the five points seemed to shift, subtly, the way a sleeping animal shifts its weight.

Ulyana went very still. Thieves, I was learning, could go stiller than statues; statues at least had the decency to breathe visibly.

"May I?" she said, and reached out without waiting for permission, because of course she didn't wait; she was a thief, and asking was a formality she performed for the comfort of others. Her fingers hovered over the scar but did not touch it. Even hovering, I felt it — a cold radiating up from my palm, like holding a hand over winter water.

"It's anchored here," she murmured. "Whatever's in you. Whatever wrote your instructions. It's not wearing you like a coat, Rafael. It's nailed in."

"The scar appeared the second morning," I said. "I woke and it was there, and I remembered — I don't know how to describe remembering something that never happened — I remembered being crowned. Not by men. By *darkness*. Something vast and patient pressing a shape into me and saying, *hold this*. I've been holding it ever since. It aches more each day. Tonight—" I closed my fist. The ache flared white-hot and then settled, sulking. "Tonight it aches like it's almost time."

* * *

She took me to a place she clearly trusted, which is to say a place she had robbed before and knew the sightlines of: a disused counting-house near the river, its windows boarded, its ledgers long since looted, leaving rows of empty pigeonholes like a wall of little coffins. She lit a stub of candle, checked the street twice through the gap in the shutters, and only then sat down across a scarred oak table from me, close enough that the candle flame bent between us.

"Now," she said, "you're going to tell me what's in your head besides instructions. All of it. Because I read that page, Rafael. I read the prophecy, and it says the Veiled King wakes tonight, and it says a thief with no past unbinds him, and it says—" her jaw tightened— "it says things about me that were written a hundred years before I was born. So either I'm mad, or you're mad, or the world is, and I'd like to know which before the sun comes up."

"I can't tell you what's in my head," I said. "Because there isn't anything. That's what I've been trying to say. I didn't lose my memories, Ulyana. I never had them. There's no boyhood behind me, no mother, no first kiss, no grief. When I search backward, I find the chandler's room and nothing before it — not fog, not darkness, just *nothing*, smooth as the inside of an egg. Everything I am was written three days ago."

"Nobody begins three days ago."

"Somebody did." I leaned forward. "And here is what I've pieced together, from the instructions and the scar and the page you stole. The Veil — whatever it is, whatever it calls itself — needed a vessel. But you can't pour a century of hunger into a living man without the man spilling out. So it built one. Grew one. Made a body and wrote a name on it and gave it just enough self to walk and talk and follow orders, and it anchored itself in his hand like a ship at mooring. I'm not possessed, Ulyana. Possession implies there was someone here first to possess."

The candle guttered. Somewhere outside, the river slapped against pilings, patient as a clock.

"Then what are you?" she asked. Not cruelly. Curiously, the way she'd asked about the name.

"I don't know." It was the truest sentence I owned. "A question the Veil asked itself, maybe. An empty coat it tailored to fit. I know that when I stood in front of you in the market, some part of me that isn't script — some small stubborn thing the Veil forgot to write over — thought: *she looks like someone who has also been stolen from.* And I wanted to be caught by you specifically. Does that count as a self? I genuinely don't know."

Ulyana looked at me for a long moment. Then she laughed again, softer this time, and it transformed her whole face — took ten years of watchfulness off it and left someone younger underneath, someone who might once have been a girl before the city got its hooks in.

"A thief with no past and a man with no self," she said. "The prophecy has a sense of humor."

"Do you want to hear the worst part?"

"There's a worst part?"

"There's always a worst part. I've read enough of that book to know its style." I turned my scarred palm upward on the table between us, crown-up, an offering or a surrender. "The instructions included faces. Names. People I'm meant to recognize on sight tonight. And one of them — the one the Veil circles most carefully, the way you circle a trap you've already sprung — is the king himself. Aldric the Beloved. They still call him that. The king who opened the granaries in the famine year. The king who walked the plague-streets unarmed. Every coin in this city carries his young face."

"And?"

"And the instructions call him *the shell*." My voice came out lower than I intended. "Not the enemy. Not the host. The *shell*. As if he's an egg the Veil has been eating from the inside for years, and tonight it finishes the meal and cracks him open and steps out wearing his smile, and the whole city will cheer, because they'll see their beloved king on the balcony at Remembrance, and none of them will notice that the man behind the eyes is gone."

Ulyana's hand had gone to her pocket, where the stolen charm-page sat. I saw her fingers move against it, unconsciously, like a tongue finding a broken tooth.

"He's still alive in there," she said. "Isn't he. Underneath."

"I think so. I think that's why the binding needs cutting rather than breaking. If we shatter it, we shatter him with it." I hesitated. This next part I had carried alone for three days, and saying it aloud felt like setting down something heavy in front of a stranger and hoping she wouldn't steal it. "There was one more instruction. The last one. It came with a feeling attached — dread, real dread, the first feeling I think I've ever had that wasn't installed on purpose. It said: *when the thief understands, the Veil will know, and the Veil will come.*"

The candle flame stood perfectly straight, as if listening.

"You understand now," I said.

"I understand." She snuffed the candle with two fingers, quick and practiced, and the dark came down around us like a curtain. In the blackness her voice was steady, but I heard the pulse under it. "So we have until the festival bells finish. We need allies, a plan, and a way into the palace. And Rafael — listen to me. Whatever wrote you, whatever nailed that crown into your hand — it made one mistake."

"What mistake?"

"It gave you to *me* first." I heard her stand, heard cloth shift, heard the faint metallic music of a woman full of stolen charms. "Nothing in this city gets stolen twice. Come on. I know a kitchen where a hedge-witch owes me a favor, and we're going to need every favor in Veymark before midnight."

Outside, far across the rooftops, the first bell of Remembrance began to ring — and beneath its bright bronze voice, so faint I might have imagined it, something answered from the direction of the palace. A second bell. Older. Hungrier.

My palm began to ache in time with it.
