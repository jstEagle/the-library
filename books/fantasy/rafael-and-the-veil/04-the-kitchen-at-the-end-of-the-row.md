## 4. The Kitchen at the End of the Row

Marisol's tenement stood at the crooked end of Tanner's Row, where the buildings leaned together like conspirators and the gutters ran amber with old dye. Ulyana had sold charms to its residents for six years and had never once been invited past the threshold of the ground floor. That was the arrangement. Marisol bought what Ulyana bottled, paid in herbs and silence, and neither woman asked where the other's hands had been.

Tonight, Rafael knocked on the door with the brass knocker shaped like a sleeping fox, and Marisol opened it before his knuckles fell a second time, as though she'd been standing behind it all evening, waiting.

"You brought it here." Her voice was low and rough as a scrubbed floorboard. She was a broad woman gone soft at the edges, silver-streaked hair pinned up with what appeared to be two pencils and a fishbone. Her eyes went from Rafael to Ulyana to the satchel between them, and something in her face folded inward. "You brought *that* here."

"It knows where we are anyway," Ulyana said. "I'd rather it knew somewhere warm."

Marisol looked at her for a long moment. Then she stepped aside, and the smell of the kitchen rolled out to meet them — onions and bay leaf, woodsmoke, the particular sweetness of beeswax candles burned down to their stubs. It was a narrow room, everything worn smooth by decades of use: a table scarred white with knife-marks, copper pots hanging like a congregation, a window over the sink that looked out on nothing but the neighbor's wall, so that whoever washed dishes washed them in green reflected light, underwater, dreaming.

"Sit," Marisol said. "Both of you. And whatever you do, don't touch the third jar on the top shelf."

Ulyana sat. She hadn't realized how badly her legs wanted to until they stopped holding her up by will alone. Three days since the archive. Three days since Rafael had pulled her through a door that opened onto a stairwell that shouldn't exist, and they had been moving ever since — safehouses that weren't safe, a night in the bell tower of a deconsecrated chapel, an afternoon spent motionless in a coal cellar while witch-hunters walked the street above and their lanterns made the cracks in the ceiling glow like veins.

Rafael sat across from her. In candlelight he looked less like a stranger and more like a sketch of one — handsome in an unfinished way, as if the artist had gotten the bones right and run out of time for the details. He kept his left hand curled closed. The scar beneath it, crown-shaped, had begun to itch; he'd told her that much. He didn't say what else it did.

The kitchen door banged open and a boy came through it carrying a crate of turnips nearly as big as himself. He was maybe fourteen, all elbows and dark curls, with flour on his cheek and an expression of tremendous grievance.

"Mam, the miller tried to short me again, so I told him about the thing with his brother and the goat, and then he—"

He stopped. He looked at Rafael. He looked at Ulyana. His eyes, quick and brown and far too clever, narrowed.

"That's her," Tam said. "That's the charm-witch. You've got her *face* pinned up behind the flour bin, Mam, I've seen it — no offense," he added to Ulyana, "but you look worse than the drawing."

"Tam." Marisol's voice could have curdled milk.

"No offense meant," he repeated, and set down the turnips, and stared at Rafael with the frank, unblinking appetite of a boy who has never once been taught to fear the right things. "Who's he? He looks like a man who's been read to death."

"*Tam.*"

"What? He does."

Ulyana found, to her surprise, that she was almost smiling. It hurt her face. She hadn't done it in three days.

* * *

Oskar arrived after dark.

They heard him on the stairs before they saw him — heavy boots, trying to be quiet, failing at it with the particular discipline of a man who had spent twenty years not needing to. Marisol opened the door before he knocked, which seemed to be her way with doors, and a large man filled the frame: gray at the temples, gray in the coat, wearing the cut-down uniform of the Order of the Quill with the insignia torn off but the shadow of it still pressed into the wool, like a scar of its own.

"Marisol." He ducked under the lintel. "You look terrible."

"You look employed."

"I look *ex*-employed as of this morning." He set a wrapped bundle on the table — bread, cheese, a bottle of plum brandy — the way other men laid down weapons. "I burned my oath-book. Well. Most of it. There were pages I couldn't reach without flammable assistance, so I borrowed some."

"You're a fool," Marisol said, and poured him a cup of tea anyway, and Ulyana watched the two rituals happen simultaneously and understood that this was simply how love sounded in this kitchen: insult and pour, insult and pour.

Oskar turned to Ulyana. Up close his eyes were pale and tired and honest, which was the most frightening combination she knew. "You're the thief. Coldharbour. Your name's been on every board in the city since the archive."

"I noticed the weather turning."

"They think you stole the prophecy book."

"I *read* the prophecy book. There's a difference. One of those is a crime; the other is apparently also a crime, in this city."

Something moved at the corner of Oskar's mouth. Not quite a smile. A crack where a smile used to live, before the Order got hold of it. "I spent eleven years hunting memory-charms," he said. "Eleven years telling myself it was mercy. That forgetting is cleaner than grieving." He looked at his own hands, big and blunt on Marisol's small table. "Then last Remembrance, my sister's face was in the king's retinue, and I don't have a sister anymore, because I reported her myself, nineteen years ago, and the Order took the memory of her as my reward." His voice stayed level. That was the worst part. "So now I'm a man who grieves someone he can't remember, and I've come to ask thieves and hollow men how to stop a god."

The kitchen was very quiet. The kettle ticked as it cooled. Somewhere below, a baby cried and was hushed.

"We're not stopping anything tonight," Marisol said briskly, and began ladling out stew as if the fate of Veymark were a scheduling matter. "Tonight you eat. Tomorrow there'll be time for doom."

* * *

And it was, absurdly, almost a family.

Tam talked. Tam talked the way water runs — constantly, finding every gap, wearing grooves in the conversation. He wanted to know if Ulyana could really bottle a memory (she could), whether it hurt (not the bottling; the running out), whether he could sell his memory of the goat incident because it was causing problems (absolutely not). He asked Rafael questions with the tactlessness of the very young and the very kind: What was your favorite food? Do you remember your mother? What's the first thing you remember?

"A door," Rafael said quietly. "A door opening. And your mother saying *sit*."

"That's not so bad," Tam decided. "First words being *sit*. Very practical."

Marisol fed them until the table groaned. The stew was root vegetables and rabbit and something secret, and the bread was yesterday's warmed back to life, and the plum brandy went around in thimblefuls because Marisol claimed it was medicinal and Oskar claimed it was a war crime and drank his anyway. Candlelight moved on the copper pots. Rain started against the window, soft, unhurried, washing the green glass light across the ceiling so the whole room seemed to sway gently at the bottom of some deep and kindly sea.

Ulyana sat in it and felt something she had no charm for.

She had spent ten years selling other people's yesterdays. Weddings, first snows, dead mothers' voices, the smell of a house you can never go back to — she had held hundreds of lives in her hands, had learned to tell real grief from performed grief by weight alone, and she had never once been inside a memory while it was still happening. But this — the argument about whether turnips should be peeled or merely scolded, Tam reenacting the miller's outrage until Marisol laughed despite herself, Oskar quietly cutting the boy's bread into smaller pieces without appearing to notice he was doing it — this was what her customers had been buying. This was the contraband. Not the bottled moments. This.

Her hand went, without her permission, to the inner pocket of her coat. To the little iron case she had carried for ten years. Inside it, five charms, each one a piece of a childhood she had stolen back from the people who bought it off her when she was nine years old: her mother's voice singing; the first theft, a honey cake, the taste of triumph; a winter morning; a lullaby's ending; and the last one, sealed in black wax, which she had never dared open because she suspected it held her real name.

She had told herself she would use them someday. When she was safe. When she was old. When it wouldn't hurt.

Across the table, Rafael caught her looking at the pocket. He didn't say anything. He just put his right hand flat on the table, palm up, open — the gesture of a man offering the only thing he had, which was nothing — and Ulyana understood that he knew. That whatever the Veil let him keep of itself, it had shown him the shape of her iron case, because the Veil was hungry, and hunger recognizes a pantry.

She took her hand away.

"More stew?" Marisol asked, and Ulyana said yes, please, and meant a great deal more than stew.

Later, Tam fell asleep sitting up, mid-sentence, his head tipping onto Oskar's shoulder, and the disgraced witch-hunter went absolutely rigid, as though entrusted with something explosive, and did not move for an hour. Marisol banked the fire. Rain kept its soft arithmetic on the glass. And Ulyana lay on the pallet they'd made her beside the stove, listening to four people breathe in one small room, and thought: *this is what I would give them, if I could bottle it. This exact hour. Warmth, rain, breathing.*

She thought: *no wonder they pay so much.*

She slept better than she had any right to.

* * *

She woke to silence of the wrong kind.

Not sleeping-silence — held-breath silence. The fire was ash. The candles were out. And the crown-shaped scar on Rafael's palm, visible now in the gray pre-dawn light where his hand hung loose off the pallet, was glowing faintly gold, like a coin at the bottom of a well.

He was awake too. She could tell by his breathing. Neither of them spoke.

On the top shelf, untouched, unopened, the third jar had begun — very softly, patiently, the way frost forms on glass — to sing.
