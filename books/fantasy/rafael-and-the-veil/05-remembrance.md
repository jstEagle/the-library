## 5. Remembrance

Oskar Vane had sworn the hunter's oath at nineteen, kneeling on cold marble with a candle in each hand, and he had believed every word of it. *To guard the city's memory. To hunt the charm-thieves. To serve the crown that keeps us whole.* Ten years later, his knees still remembered the chill of that marble — which was ironic, because remembering was the one thing the Order claimed to protect and the one thing Oskar felt himself losing, year by year, like sand through a fist he'd stopped noticing was open.

The Remembrance Festival began at dusk, as it always did. Lanterns went up along the Veymark canals in their thousands, paper skins glowing amber, each one supposed to hold a wish for the dead. The air smelled of frying dough and canal water and woodsmoke, and beneath it all — faint as a hairline crack in porcelain — something sweet and wrong that Oskar had never been able to name until this year. It smelled like the inside of an empty house.

He stood his post on the Processional Bridge in full hunter's regalia: grey coat, silver pin, the oiled leather gloves that marked him as one of the Order's memory-wardens. Around him the crowd pressed thick and festive, families with children on shoulders, old men selling roasted chestnuts, girls with ribbons the color of festival wine. A woman beside him laughed at something her husband said, and Oskar found himself staring at her mouth, trying to memorize the shape of her joy, though he didn't know why.

*Because you're forgetting how,* whispered a thought he'd been refusing to think for three years.

The bells rang seven. The crowd hushed the way water hushes before it freezes.

And then the Veiled King came out onto the balcony of the Old Palace, and Veymark lost its mind with love.

Oskar had seen the king before — from a distance, at investitures, at the annual blessing of the archives. He had always seemed a fine figure of a man: tall, silver-templed, with a smile that made crowds feel personally blessed. Tonight that smile caught the lantern-light and threw it back tenfold, and the people below surged against the bridge railings, weeping, calling his name — *Aldric, Aldric* — a name Oskar realized he had never once heard spoken aloud before tonight, and yet everyone around him seemed to have carried it all their lives.

*That's not right,* he thought. *We don't say his name. We've never said his name.*

The thought slid off him like rain off waxed cloth. He watched it go without being able to hold it.

Then the king raised one hand, and the feeding began.

It was gentle, at first. That was the horror of it — how gentle. The crowd's collective memory rose off them like steam off morning bread, invisible to anyone who wasn't wearing warden's gloves, but Oskar's gloves were warded, and through the oiled leather he felt it: a great soft exhalation, thousands of small warm things lifting free. A grandmother's lullaby. A boy's first snowfall. A wedding dance in a kitchen by candlelight. They drifted up toward the balcony like sparks rising in reverse, and the Veiled King breathed them in, and his smile deepened, and the crowd below sighed in unison — a sound so contented it made Oskar's skin crawl — and forgot.

Not everything. Never everything. Just the edges. Just enough. The woman beside him stopped laughing mid-breath, her face going smooth and puzzled, like a page someone had erased a word from. Her husband took her hand and neither of them could remember what they'd been laughing about, and they smiled anyway, because the festival was lovely, wasn't it? The festival had always been lovely.

*Remembrance Festival,* Oskar thought, and the name turned to ash in his mouth. A hundred years of festivals. A hundred years of the city gathering to offer itself up, dressed as devotion.

His hands were shaking inside the gloves. He told himself it was the cold.

* * *

The retinue came out onto the balcony after the king — the Veil's court, grey-robed, faceless behind gauze veils, carrying candles that burned with no heat. Oskar knew the protocol; he'd helped write half of it. The wardens were meant to keep their eyes forward, count the retinue, log any irregularities in the evening report, and never — *never* — look too long at the veiled ones, because looking too long was how wardens went strange, how they ended up in the Order's quiet rooms being asked gentle questions about what they remembered.

Oskar looked.

There were twelve of them, as there were always twelve. Eleven moved with the smooth, practiced grace of the long-serving. The twelfth — last in line, smallest, standing slightly apart as if she hadn't yet learned where her body ended — tilted her head at the crowd below, and the lantern-light caught the edge of her jaw beneath the gauze, and Oskar's heart stopped like a struck clock.

He knew that jaw. He knew the little crooked nick in it, left of center, from the night she was nine and fell off the wall behind the tannery and he carried her home bleeding and laughing both.

*Mira.*

His sister Mira, who had died of marsh-fever when he was fourteen — no. Who had *not* died. Who had vanished, taken by the Order for training, or so the letter said, and then the letters stopped, and then one day he tried to remember her face and found only a smooth warm blankness where her face should have been, and the Order's healers told him grief did that sometimes, wore the features away, and he had believed them because believing was easier than the alternative.

She was standing in the Veiled King's retinue, veiled, holding a candle that gave no warmth, and when she tilted her head again — that same tilt, his sister's tilt, the one she used when she was deciding whether you were worth arguing with — Oskar understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, that the Order had not trained her.

The Veil had eaten her. Slowly, over years, the way it ate the crowd below: edges first, then names, then faces, until nothing remained but a shape that could stand where it was put and hold a candle. And somewhere in the Order's ledgers, her death had been recorded as a kindness.

The crowd roared its love. The king smiled and drank. Mira stood among the other hollowed ones, and did not know her brother was twenty feet below her, breaking apart.

Oskar walked off the bridge. He did not remember deciding to. His feet simply took him, down the lantern-lit lane, past the chestnut sellers and the singing children, into the dark between two warehouses where he knelt and was sick into the canal, and the water carried the bile away and kept none of it, the way the Veil kept everything, the way the Order kept everything, the whole city a larder laid in for a hunger that never ended.

When he could breathe again, he sat on the wet cobbles with his back against the bricks and pulled off his right glove. His bare hand looked pale and ordinary in the dark. Ordinary, and the only honest thing left on his body.

He thought about the reports he'd filed. The raids he'd led. The charm-sellers dragged from their stalls while their neighbors watched and forgot them by morning. He thought about a fence named Marisol who had gone quiet last winter, and a girl called Ulyana Coldharbour whose file crossed his desk twice — *charm-theft, minor, watch only* — and whom he had let walk, twice, for reasons he'd told himself were administrative.

He thought: *they're hiding from this. They already know.*

The Order's secret was not well kept, only well buried, and Oskar had spent ten years shoveling the earth on top of it. But he had read the sealed correspondence — every warden of his rank had, once, and was made to forget most of it, though some things sank too deep for even the Veil's tidy erasures. He remembered now, pulling the memory up like a man hauling a drowned body from deep water: the binding was woven *into* the crown, thread by thread across a century, anchored in the king's own grief, and no blade or charm or prayer from outside could cut it. The Quill's founders had written it plainly, in a hand gone brown with age:

*What is bound within may only be unbound from within. The key must pass through the crown itself.*

From inside. From the throne room, from the anchor-point, from the place no thief or witch or hunter had ever reached and lived.

Oskar put his glove back on. It felt like putting a hand back into a dead animal.

He stood. Above the rooftops, the palace blazed with festival light, and the bells rang eight, and somewhere in the crowd a woman was asking her husband why she was crying when nothing sad had happened.

Oskar Vane walked north, away from his post, away from his oath, toward the tenement row where an exiled hedge-witch kept a kitchen that smelled of rosemary and defiance. With every step he rehearsed what he would say — *I know where your king sleeps, I know what the crown is made of, I know how to get inside* — and with every step he felt the silver pin at his collar grow heavier, until at the door of Marisol's building he stopped, unpinned it, and dropped it down a storm drain, where it landed without a sound.

The door opened before he knocked. Tam stood there, lantern in hand, eyes wide.

"You," the boy said. "You're a hunter."

"I was." Oskar's voice came out rough as gravel. "I need to speak with Ulyana Coldharbour. Tell her the crown can be cut — but only from inside. And tell her—" His throat closed on his sister's name, on the tilt of her head, on the candle with no warmth. He swallowed it down. "Tell her we don't have much time. The king appears again tomorrow night. And after that—"

Behind Tam, at the end of the warm kitchen hallway, a shadow that was not a shadow peeled itself slowly off the wall, and turned its attention toward the door, and Oskar felt every hair on his body rise as he understood that the Veil had heard him too.
