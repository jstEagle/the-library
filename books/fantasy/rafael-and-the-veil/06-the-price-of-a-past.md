## 6. The Price of a Past

The prophecy book had followed them home.

Not physically — Marisol's kitchen was too warded for that — but in the way a debt follows a debtor. It sat on the scarred oak table between the bread board and the salt crock, bound in grey leather gone soft as old skin, and it waited. All morning it had waited while Marisol baked bread she didn't eat and Tam whittled a boat he'd never sail and Oskar cleaned a crossbow he was no longer permitted to carry. All morning the book had sat there like a guest who knows something terrible about everyone in the room.

By late afternoon, Ulyana could no longer pretend not to see it.

She'd slept badly. In the narrow hours before dawn she had dreamed of her mother's kitchen — not this one, the real one, the one from before — and woken with salt on her lips that might have been tears or might have been the sea off Veymark's harbour blowing through a gap in the shutters. She'd lain in Marisol's spare cot and pressed her hand flat against her sternum, where the charm-bottle usually hung, and felt the small cold weight of it even though she'd left it locked in its box beneath the floorboards. Ten years of carrying it had worn a groove in her. Some weights do that. They hollow out a place for themselves in you, and then they are load-bearing.

"Tea," Marisol said, setting down a cup Ulyana hadn't asked for. The hedge-witch had a way of appearing with exactly what people needed and pretending it was coincidence. Her grey braid was coming loose at the temple; flour dusted her knuckles. "You've been staring at that book since breakfast like it owes you money."

"It doesn't owe me money." Ulyana wrapped both hands around the cup. The clay was chipped, warm, ordinary. She held on to its ordinariness the way a drowning woman holds driftwood. "It owes me an ending."

"Books don't owe anything," Marisol said. "That's what makes them dangerous. Everything else in this city wants something from you. A book just *sits* there being true."

Ulyana looked at the book. Through the window behind it, Veymark was putting on its festival face — bunting strung between chimneys, lanterns going up along the canal bridges one by one as the light failed, the smell of frying dough and lamp oil and far too many bodies all breathing the same cold air. Tomorrow night was Remembrance. Tomorrow night the Veiled King would walk among his people, and whatever remained of Rafael would be standing in the crowd with a hole in his hand shaped like a crown.

Rafael himself was asleep in the loft above the stable, exhausted from another day of the Veil chewing through him. He'd come down at noon having forgotten the name of the street they were hiding on. He'd laughed about it. That was somehow worse than if he'd cried.

Ulyana drank her tea until it was cool enough to be an excuse rather than a comfort. Then she set the cup down, wiped her palms on her trousers — an old thief's ritual, dry hands for delicate work — and opened the book.

* * *

She had read the first pages already, of course. In the archive, by witchlight, with hunters' boots thundering somewhere above her: the page that described a thief lifting a charm from a quiet man on the eve of Remembrance, written a hundred years before she was born. The pages after that she had skimmed in stolen moments, in the gaps between Oskar's warnings and Marisol's wards and Rafael's slow, patient disappearing. She knew the shape of the prophecy the way you know the shape of a country from a map — accurate and useless. You don't understand a country until your feet hurt walking through it.

Now she read properly. Now she read the way she used to pick locks: slowly, feeling for the catch.

The middle pages were the worst kind of history — the kind that rhymes. A beloved king, young when crowned, who loved his city so much he could not bear to watch it grieve. A plague winter. A war. Grief upon grief, and the king grieving hardest of all, until something in the dark of the world took notice. The Veil did not conquer, the prophecy said. The Veil was *invited*. A king who wished to feel nothing opened the door himself, and the thing that came through wore his sorrow like a mantle, and then wore his smile, and then simply wore him.

*And the crown shall be the lock,* the old ink read, in letters that shifted slightly when Ulyana wasn't looking directly at them, *and the binding shall be the hasp, and only a key freely given may turn it.*

*Freely given.* She'd read that line four times over three days and each time her mind had slid off it, the way a pick slides off a stubborn tumbler. Keys could be lots of things. Words. Names. Blood, in the cruder traditions. She'd let herself believe it would be something she could steal. Stealing was the one language she was fluent in. Give her a key that existed anywhere in the world — in a vault, in a heart, in a grave — and she would have it in her hand by morning.

Then she turned the final page.

The paper was different here. Older. Thicker, with a texture like moth wing, and the ink had sunk so deep into it that the words seemed to float beneath the surface, visible through ice. And the handwriting had changed — looser now, hurried, as if the scribe had been writing against some deadline of their own.

*Let it be set down plainly, so that none may claim ignorance,* the page began, and Ulyana's stomach dropped before she'd read a single sentence more, because prophecies that begin with *let it be set down plainly* are never plain, they are only honest, and honesty is worse.

*The binding was made of surrendered things. A king gave up his grief; the Veil took it and made itself of it. What is built of surrender can only be unmade by surrender — but not the surrender of the bound, nor of the willing servant, nor of any soul who still holds memories enough to miss them. Such gifts the Veil will take and grow fat on.*

*Only this may cut the hasp: memories freely surrendered by one who owns nothing else — who has no past but the memories themselves, so that in giving them, the giver gives their whole inheritance entire. Not a tithe. An estate. The gift must be everything, freely meant, or it is merely food.*

Ulyana read it again.

Then a third time, mouthing the words, because surely she had misread, surely there was a clause further down, a footnote, a merciful loophole tucked into the margin —

There was no margin. There was only one more line, in the same sinking ink:

*The Veil has spent a hundred years building a vessel with no past, so that the key might exist. But vessels cannot give freely; they have nothing that is theirs. Therefore look not to the vessel. Look to the thief with no history of her own, who carries a childhood that was never hers by right — and ask her what her past is worth.*

The kitchen was very quiet. Somewhere outside, festival children were singing the counting song — *one for the year, two for the tear, three for the name we lost last year* — their voices thin and bright in the blue evening.

Ulyana sat perfectly still.

Her hands, she noticed distantly, were steady. Thieves' hands. Hands that had learned long ago not to shake, because shaking hands drop things, and dropped things make noise, and noise gets you caught. Ten years of discipline held her body together like stitching while everything underneath it came quietly apart.

*A childhood that was never hers by right.*

She had been eight years old when the memory-charms first appeared in the market stalls of Lowbridge, sold by a woman with a burn-scarred jaw who claimed they came from a house fire in the Salt Quarter. Ulyana had bought one with coins lifted from a drunkard's coat: a charm the size of a robin's egg, stoppered with wax, and inside it — her mother's voice, saying her name. Her real name. The one she hadn't heard since before the workhouse, before the streets, before she'd taught herself to answer to *girl* and *hey, you* and *thief*. Inside the charm was a whole childhood: a kitchen that smelled of dill and coal smoke, a father's laugh like a door swinging, snow coming down past a window while someone sang. Ten charms in all, gathered over three desperate years, bought and bartered and stolen from fences who didn't know what they held. Ten years since, hoarded. Carried from cellar to cellar, from city to city, always within reach of her hand, always the last thing packed and the first thing unpacked.

They weren't hers. She had known that, in the way you know the floor is solid without thinking about it. Some other child had burned in the Salt Quarter, or drowned, or simply grown up and sold their past to buy bread. Ulyana had taken that childhood the way she took everything — cleanly, without apology, because the world was cruel and you survived by taking.

But knowing a thing and being told it are different animals. Knowing was a fact. This — *ask her what her past is worth* — this was a bill presented for payment, and the payment was everything, and the ledger said she had never had the right to any of it in the first place.

"They're mine," she said aloud, to the empty kitchen, and her voice cracked on the second word like river ice.

Marisol appeared in the doorway with an armful of kindling, took one look at Ulyana's face, and set the wood down very slowly, the way you move when you've startled something wild.

"The last page," Ulyana managed. "It wants—"

"I know what it wants." Marisol crossed the room and put her flour-dusted hand over the book, closing it gently, as if it were a mouth. "I read it yesterday while you were sleeping. I hoped you wouldn't get there tonight."

"You hoped I *wouldn't read the prophecy we're living inside?*"

"I hoped you'd have one more night of not knowing." Marisol's voice was soft, unhurried, the voice she used on frightened animals and dying men. "Knowing keeps. There'll be time enough for it tomorrow, and the day after, and every miserable day after that."

"And what does it matter?" Ulyana laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, jagged, a sound with broken edges. "It's not even my past. That's the whole point. That's the *cruel joke* of it. The prophecy says it right there — a childhood never hers by right. I stole it. From some dead child in the Salt Quarter. So why shouldn't I pour it into that thing's crown and be done?"

"Because you've been keeping it alive for ten years," Marisol said. "Dead children's memories go stale, Ulyana. Fences have told me. Images blur, voices flatten, whole rooms go dark. Yours haven't. Your mother's kitchen still smells of dill. Do you know why?"

Ulyana said nothing.

"Because you loved it," Marisol said simply. "Ten years of loving something makes it yours, whatever the ledger says. Love is a deed of ownership no thief can forge and no law can revoke." She straightened, wincing at her back. "But I'm not going to tell you what to do with it. I'm a hedge-witch, not a priestess. I'm only going to tell you that Rafael saw your face in his dreams last night. He says you were standing in a doorway holding a bottle, and you couldn't decide whether to keep it or throw it into the sea."

"He dreams my dreams now? That's — the Veil is bleeding through him, that's—"

"That's what I thought too," Marisol said. "Until he described the bottle. Green glass, cork sealed with red wax, a chip out of the base where you dropped it in the workhouse yard when you were nine." She met Ulyana's eyes steadily. "You never told anyone that. Not me. Not Tam. Not Oskar."

The kitchen tilted, very slightly, the way a ship tilts when the swell changes.

* * *

Ulyana went up to the loft alone, by ladder, past the smell of hay and horse and lamp oil. Rafael was asleep in the straw, on his side, one hand curled open near his face — the other palm turned upward, showing the crown-shaped scar, pale and puckered, like a brand from a ring too small for any human finger.

He looked so young asleep. Younger than her, though neither of them knew their ages for certain. His brow was furrowed even in sleep, and his lips moved faintly, shaping words she leaned close to hear.

"Not yet," he murmured. "Please. Not her."

Below, faint through the floorboards, came the ordinary sounds of the kitchen: Tam's low humming, Marisol banking the stove, the clink of dishes. The sounds of the only family she had ever built, assembled piece by piece from other people's castoffs, the way she built everything.

Ulyana sat down in the straw beside Rafael and pulled her knees to her chest.

She thought about the box beneath the floorboards. Ten charms, green glass glowing faintly in the dark like a nest of sleeping fireflies. Her mother's voice. Her real name. Snow past a window. Dill and coal smoke. The whole of a childhood that had kept her alive when nothing else did — not hope, not pride, not even spite, though spite had carried her far. Every night for ten years, when the day had stripped her bare, she had uncorked one charm and breathed it in and been, for ninety seconds, a girl who was loved in a house that was warm.

*Ask her what her past is worth.*

"No," Ulyana said, to the loft, to the prophecy, to the hundred-year-old stranger who had written her into a book like a debt. Her voice was low and hard and did not shake. "Find another key. Find another thief. I didn't survive the workhouse and the streets and ten winters of Veymark to surrender the only thing that ever loved me back."

The scar on Rafael's palm twitched, in his sleep, like a thing waking.

And below them, so softly that only a thief's ears could have caught it, the front door of Marisol's kitchen opened, and closed, and Tam's humming stopped.
