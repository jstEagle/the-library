## 7. Tam's Bargain

Tam had never been good at sleeping in Marisol's kitchen, and tonight the reason was simple: he could hear it breathing.

Not the house — the house only creaked, the way old houses do, settling its bones into the dark. This was something else. A slow, tidal sound beneath the floorboards, beneath the street, beneath Veymark itself, as if the city were a lung and something vast were drawing breath through it. He lay on his pallet by the stove with his eyes open and counted the rhythm. In. Out. In. Each exhale smelled faintly of cold iron and cut grass, which made no sense, because there was no grass within three miles of the Row.

Marisol slept in the loft above, her breathing thin but steady. She'd taken her tonic early and gone down hard, the way she did when the ache in her hands got bad. Ulyana and Rafael had claimed the corner behind the flour sacks, and Oskar — the hunter who wasn't a hunter anymore — kept watch by the door out of habit, though his eyes were closed and his chin had dropped to his chest.

Tam sat up.

The Veil had spoken to him once before. Three nights ago, when he'd gone down to the cistern for water and found his reflection waiting for him in the black surface without bothering to wait for the ripple. It hadn't said much. It never needed to. It had simply looked at him with his own eyes and let him understand, the way you understand a debt is coming before the letter arrives: *You love her. The hedge-witch. Love is a door.*

He'd told no one. Not Marisol, who would have burned sage until the kitchen smoked like a chimney and then blamed herself for every hour of it. Not Ulyana, who carried enough weight already — he'd seen her at the table that evening, turning an empty charm-bottle over and over in her fingers like a rosary, like a question she couldn't stop asking. And not Rafael, who was kind in the specific way of people who knew they might not be real, and whose kindness Tam didn't trust himself to receive.

But tonight the breathing under the floorboards had changed key. Tonight it sounded *closer*. And Tam had done arithmetic all his life — sums in the fish-market, tallies for Marisol's herb-trades, the cold accounting of how much food four fugitives could eat before the neighbors started asking questions — and the sum he kept arriving at was this:

The Veil wanted memories. Ulyana had memories worth taking. The prophecy wanted Ulyana. And Marisol — Marisol, who had fished him half-drowned out of the canal at nine years old and never once asked where he came from, because she said everyone's past was their own business and their future was hers to help cook — Marisol would stand between the Veil and anyone it came for, and Marisol was old, and her magic was hedge-magic, kitchen-magic, the kind that kept bread rising and fevers breaking and nothing more.

So someone had to be worth more than Ulyana. Someone the Veil would take instead.

Tam looked at his own hands in the dark. Fisherman's hands, cook's hands, hands that knew nets and knives and the particular gentleness required to hold something small and frightened. They weren't remarkable hands. That was the point. Nobody would miss what they remembered.

He rose without a sound — years of slipping out before dawn to check the lines had taught him that — and took the stub of candle from the shelf, and the chalk from beside the slate where Marisol wrote the day's menu, and went down into the cellar.

* * *

The cellar smelled of turnips and tallow and the ghost of every soup Marisol had ever made, and Tam loved it so much his chest hurt. That was the first thing the ritual asked of him, and he understood immediately that this was going to be worse than he'd thought: not a bargain struck at arm's length, but a memory handed over piece by piece while he watched it go.

He drew the circle in chalk on the flagstones. He didn't know the proper geometry — that was witch-work — so he drew it the way he drew everything, round and generous, a circle like a table, big enough for everyone to sit at. Then he set the candle at its center and knelt, and pressed both palms flat to the stone, and said the only prayer he knew, which was the one Marisol said over bread: *May it rise. May it feed. May it come back to us doubled.*

"Hey," he said to the dark. "I know you're listening."

The breathing stopped.

In the silence that followed, the candleflame stood perfectly still, like a held note, and Tam felt the attention of the thing settle over him the way fog settles over the harbor — total, patient, ancient. His mouth went dry. Every sensible part of him screamed to run up the stairs and wake the others. But sensible had never been the operative word in his life. Marisol had taught him that too: *the heart decides first, Tam-love, and the head just does the carrying.*

"I'm offering," he said. "Instead of her. Instead of any of them." He swallowed. "My memories. All of them. I don't — I don't have prophecies or stolen childhoods or anything important. But I remember things. I remember the smell of low tide. I remember the first time Marisol let me salt the soup and it was terrible and she ate the whole bowl anyway. I remember—" His voice cracked, and he hated that, and pushed through it. "I remember being happy here. Take that. Take all of it. Just leave them alone."

The flame bent toward him.

It didn't speak in words. It spoke in *taking* — and the first thing it took was the tide. He felt it go the way you feel a tooth leave the gum: a socket suddenly empty, a shape where a sensation used to live. *Low tide*, the cellar whispered, in a voice like wet sand sliding away, *what is low tide?* And Tam reached for the answer and found the hole where it had been, smooth-edged, already healed over, as if he had never once stood ankle-deep in the retreating sea with the gulls screaming and the whole gray world smelling of salt and possibility.

"No," he breathed. "Wait. I didn't mean—"

But there was no negotiating with hunger. The second memory went faster: the canal, the cold shock of the water, the small drowning boy he had been — gone, and with it the knowledge of why he flinched at rain on his neck. The third: Marisol's face the day he'd called her *Mama* for the first time, testing the word like ice on a pond — gone, and now he looked at the stairs leading up to her loft and felt a warmth he could no longer name, a kindness from a stranger, charity from a woman who took in strays.

That one hurt worst, because he could feel it *going*, could feel the love draining out of the memory even as the memory itself dissolved, and he understood with sudden horrible clarity that this was how the Veil fed — not on facts, but on the feeling inside them. Facts were bottles. Love was the wine.

"Stop," he whispered. "Please. I'll give you the rest, just — let me keep that one. Let me keep her."

The flame leaned closer. Somewhere far above, muffled through floorboards, the city's Remembrance bells began to toll the midnight hour, and each bell-strike seemed to loosen something else in him. He grasped at names. Tam. His name was Tam. Wasn't it? Tam, yes, Tam, the fish-boy, the foster-son, the — the —

The cellar door opened above him.

Light spilled down the stairs, and with it came Ulyana, still in her clothes, her dark hair loose and her eyes wide with a horror that sharpened as she took in the circle, the candle, the boy kneeling in it with his palms bleeding faintly where he'd pressed too hard against the stone.

"TAM!"

She didn't hesitate. That was the thing about Ulyana that Tam had always liked best, back when he'd had the capacity to like things — she moved like a thief, which meant she moved *decided*. She crossed the cellar in three strides, snatched the candle from the circle's center, and snapped it in half across her knee. The flame died. The chalk circle, starved of its focus, shivered like water disturbed by a dropped stone.

Then she did something he didn't expect. She spat on her palm and dragged it through the chalk, erasing the circle line by line, muttering under her breath — not witch-language, he realized dimly, but ordinary words, fierce and profane, a thief's curse, a dockworker's curse, the vocabulary of a woman who had grown up learning that unmaking was sometimes as powerful as making.

The pressure lifted.

Tam sagged forward onto his hands. The cellar was just a cellar again — turnips, tallow, soup-ghosts — and the breathing under the floorboards had withdrawn, sullen, unsatisfied, like a guest shown the door mid-meal. He tried to say thank you and discovered his mouth wouldn't shape it. He tried to say *who are you* and got as far as opening his lips.

Ulyana knelt and gripped his shoulders. Her hands were shaking. "Look at me. Look at me, Tam. What did you give it?"

He searched himself the way you search pockets. "The tide," he managed. "And — the canal. And—" He stopped. Something was missing and he couldn't find the edges of the missing thing, which was the worst part, like probing a gap with your tongue. "There was a woman. Upstairs. She — I think she matters. I can't remember why."

Ulyana's face did something complicated and terrible. Behind her, on the stairs, Marisol stood in her nightdress with one hand braced on the wall, having heard enough, and her expression was the expression of a woman watching a second child nearly drown in the same house.

"You absolute fool," Ulyana said, and her voice broke on it, and she pulled him against her shoulder and held on hard. "You brave, stupid, wonderful fool."

He held on back, because holding on was instinct, deeper than memory.

And that was when Rafael appeared at the top of the stairs, pale as paper, and said quietly, "It knows where we are now. I can feel it turning toward us. We have until dawn — maybe less."

Above them, through the ceiling, the Remembrance bells finished their midnight count, and somewhere across the sleeping city, something vast and patient began to move.
