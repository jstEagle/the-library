## 8. The Hollow Court

The palace did not want them to find the throne room.

Rafael understood this before he understood anything else that night. The corridors had opinions, and every one of those opinions was *no*. Staircases they had counted on their way in now climbed to landings they hadn't seen, doors opened onto rooms they had already left, and once — only once, but once was enough — a hallway breathed. He felt it against his neck as they passed: a long, slow exhalation that smelled of candle smoke and old snow, like something enormous asleep behind the plaster.

"Left," Ulyana whispered at the junction of two galleries hung with portraits whose faces had been painted over. Not scratched out — painted over, kindly, by some careful hand, so that each frame held only smooth blankness where a face should be. "I remember turning left."

"You don't remember," Tam said. His voice was thin. He was walking pressed close to Marisol's side, and his hands would not stop moving, folding and unfolding, as if he were trying to fold himself smaller. Since the ritual — since Rafael had watched Ulyana tear him out of the Veil's bargain like someone pulling a drowning boy from ice water — Tam's memories had been coming back wrong. Small things first. Then larger things wearing small things' clothes. "You remember what I remember, and I don't trust anything I've got anymore."

"Then we go right," Oskar said flatly, and went right, because Oskar did not wait for consensus. The disgraced hunter moved through the palace like a man walking through a house he'd been thrown out of — knowing where everything was, hating that he knew. His boots made almost no sound. In his hand he carried not a weapon but a ledger, the Order of the Quill's secret, its spine cracked from being read too many times. "The binding is cut from inside the crown," he said again, for perhaps the tenth time that night, as if repetition could make it less impossible. "Which means one of you has to reach it while it's still willing to let you close."

"And if it isn't willing?" Marisol asked. She had not let go of Tam since the kitchen. Her fingers were hooked in the collar of his shirt, gentle and unbreakable, an anchor line in a storm she refused to name.

"Then we learn what happens to bait when the fisherman stops needing it," Oskar said.

*Bait.* The word sat in Rafael's chest like a swallowed stone. He had felt it there since the archive, since the prophecy page that described him in detail written a hundred years ago — *a vessel grown from grief, a loophole shaped like a man* — and he had been carrying it without letting himself look at it directly, the way you carry a lamp past a window in a city full of hunters.

The corridor ahead narrowed. The walls here were older, older than the painted-over portraits, older than Veymark's law against magic, and they were covered in writing. Not carved. *Grown*, Rafael thought — the letters rose from the stone like scar tissue, looping lines in a language he should not have known and knew anyway. He stopped. The others kept three steps before they noticed and turned back, and in the lantern light he saw their faces change as they read what he was reading.

It was a list. A census. A hundred years of names, each struck through in turn, and at the end of the column, still wet-looking though it had been written decades before any of them were born:

**RAFAEL.**

"Not my name," he heard himself say. His voice sounded far away, borrowed. "That's not — I woke up three days ago with instructions and nothing else. No name. I gave myself that name because it was the first word on the first page I found." He touched the letters. They were warm. Everything in this palace was warm, faintly, like skin. "This is a tally. Of attempts."

"A hundred years of vessels," Ulyana said quietly. She had come to stand beside him, and her shoulder was almost touching his, which was how she said things she couldn't say aloud. "Each one failed. Each one hollowed too fast, or broke, or—" She stopped. Struck-through names. She didn't need to finish.

"Or wasn't enough," Rafael said. "Grief alone wasn't enough. It needed grief that could walk. Grief that could choose."

He looked down at his palm. The crown-shaped scar had been burning all night, but now it burned differently — not pain, exactly. Recognition. Somewhere above them, past floors and floors of breathing corridors, something had felt him touch the wall, and it was pleased.

"We keep moving," Oskar said. But even Oskar's voice had gone careful, the way men speak near wounded animals.

* * *

They found the throne room by falling into it.

One moment Rafael was climbing a staircase that had been going up for too long — counting steps under his breath because counting was the only thing keeping the palace's arithmetic from rewriting itself around them — and the next, the stairs simply ended, and the dark ahead of them opened like a mouth, and they were through.

The throne room of Veymark was not a room. It was a memory of a room, and a bad one, remembered by someone who had been weeping. The ceiling arched away into darkness that no light seemed able to fix in place; pillars stood at angles pillars cannot stand; and the floor was black glass that reflected not the five of them but other figures, other nights, a century of processions and coronations and festivals layered over one another like sediment. When Rafael walked, a hundred reflections walked with him, and most of them were not him.

And on the dais, beneath a crown of woven shadow, sat the king.

Aldric the Beloved. Rafael had never seen him — had no memories of coins or statues or festival banners — and yet he knew the face instantly, intimately, the way you know your own face in a mirror. Because it was nearly his face. Older, worn, kind even now, even *here* — but the same architecture of bone, the same set of the jaw, the same eyes that had looked back at Rafael from every still pond and polished window for three days and been a stranger's eyes.

Except they weren't strangers' eyes. They had never been.

"There you are," the king said, and smiled, and the smile was the worst thing Rafael had ever seen, because the smile did not belong to the king at all.

The Veil wore him the way a thief wears a stolen coat. Rafael could see the seams now, up close: the way the king's left hand lay too still in his lap, the way his breathing ran slightly out of time with his words, the way his eyes — his real eyes, deep underneath — moved. They found Rafael across the whole black-glass distance of the room, and they were frantic, and they were begging.

*Please,* said the eyes, saying nothing aloud. *Please. Please.*

"He's still in there," Ulyana breathed beside him. Her hand found Rafael's sleeve. "Gods. He's still *in there*."

"I am more than in here," the king said pleasantly, rising. The shadow-crown shifted on his brow like a living thing resettling. "I am the coat *and* the man, little thief. I am a hundred years of patient tailoring." His gaze slid to Ulyana, and Rafael watched the Veil taste her the way a tongue tastes the air. "Ah. And you brought me my key. How thoughtful. Do you know how long I have been fitting this lock? A vessel grown from discarded grief — his own grief, poured off like whey from curds, the part of himself Aldric couldn't bear to carry after his daughter died. Left to ripen in the dark for a hundred years until it walked. Grief that *chose* to come home." The smile widened. "Only grief may cut grief loose. Only the cast-off can unmake the casting. Your Order wrote it down and then spent a century making sure no one read it. And here you all are, delivering yourselves to my door."

"You're lying," Tam said, but his voice cracked on the second word.

"I never lie," the Veil said. "Lying is a waste of a good truth. Ask him." And the king's body turned — stiffly, the joints moving a half-second late, like a puppet remembering its strings — toward Rafael. "Ask yourself. You've felt it, haven't you? Every time you look at him. The recognition. The *homesickness*."

And Rafael had. All night. All three days. He had told himself it was the prophecy, the scar, the strangeness of waking empty. But standing here, looking at the king's desperate eyes inside the Veil's borrowed smile, he felt it rise in him like floodwater: not hatred, not fear. Grief. A grief with no object, no memory attached, just pure and bottomless mourning, and he understood suddenly that it was *his* — that it had always been his, that it was the only inheritance he had ever been given. He was what Aldric had wept out and abandoned. He was the shape left in the air after a father finished crying.

"That's why the binding needs me," Rafael said. His voice came out steady, which surprised him. "Not because I'm strong enough. Because I'm *made of the same stuff*. The binding is grief knotted around a king, and I'm grief that learned to walk."

"Now you understand," the Veil said warmly. "Come up here, Rafael. Come home. Cut the knot, free us both, take the crown your father threw away. You'll be doing him a kindness. Look at him. He's so tired."

The king's eyes were streaming now, silently, while his mouth kept smiling. His real hand — the left one, the still one — twitched against the arm of the throne, once, twice, and Rafael saw the fingers move in a pattern, deliberate, repeated. Not random. Letters. A message spelled out against the black glass armrest by a man who had been imprisoned inside his own smiling face long enough to grow very, very patient.

Marisol saw it too. Her breath caught. "*Tam*," she whispered. "Read it. Read his hand."

Tam stared. His ruined, re-stitched memory worked, faltered, worked. "F-forget," he said hoarsely. "Forget... the... *throne*. The binding — the binding isn't the crown." His eyes came up, wide and horrified. "It's the seat. The chair. The crown is just what we're supposed to look at."

For the first time in a hundred years, the smile on the king's face flickered.

Something moved in the dark behind the pillars — many somethings, tall and wrong, the retinue Oskar had seen feeding on the festival crowd, wearing faces that belonged to people who had forgotten them. Oskar stepped forward, ledger raised, planting himself between the shadows and the children he had decided, somewhere on the walk here, were his to protect.

"Ulyana," Rafael said softly. He did not take his eyes off the throne. Off the chair. Off the desperate, weeping, hopeful eyes of the man who was, in a way that hurt worse than any memory ever could, his father. "Get ready."

Above the dais, unseen, the ceiling began — very slowly — to breathe in.
