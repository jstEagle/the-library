## 9. Unbinding

The throne room had no doors anymore.

The halls behind us had eaten themselves — corridors that Oskar swore he'd walked as a young hunter folding shut like hands closing over coins, archways becoming blank stone, the palace digesting its own geography to keep us from the crown. We ran through what remained: a throat of marble narrowing around us, lit by lamps that burned without oil, their flames leaning toward the throne like courtiers bowing.

Tam limped at my side, hollow-eyed, his memories leaking back into him in fragments Marisol kept pressing into his hands like bandages — *your name is Tam, you fell out of a plum tree when you were seven, you hate the smell of cloves* — each one catching, holding, stitching. He would not be whole tonight. But he would not be empty either. That was more than I could say for myself by morning.

Rafael walked ahead of us all, and the Veil walked inside Rafael, and I could no longer always tell which of them was leading.

His face had begun to change. Not dramatically — that was the horror of it. A millimeter of stillness in his eyes. A smile arriving half a second before it should. When he spoke, the voice was still his, but underneath it, faint as a second heartbeat, something older was learning the shape of his mouth. In the last corridor he stopped and looked back at me with an expression of such perfect, gentle kindness that my skin crawled, because Rafael had never once been kind gently. He was kind the way a man is kind who has nothing left to lose — roughly, quickly, like handing someone bread in a storm.

That smile was not his.

"Ulyana." The word came out doubled, harmonized with itself, one voice a breath behind the other. "You've come so far. You may still turn back. No one will remember you were ever here."

*I* would remember, I almost said. And then understood that was exactly the mercy being offered — that I wouldn't. That I would walk out of this palace with my hoarded childhood intact and no memory of why I'd come, and Rafael's body would stand up wearing its new king, and the city would go on loving a thing that ate it.

"No," I said.

The doubled mouth smiled wider. "I hoped you'd say that."

* * *

The throne room opened all at once, the way a wound opens.

It was vast and wrong. The ceiling was painted with clouds, but the clouds were moving, slow and soft, though there was no wind in a sealed room, and I understood after a moment that they weren't paint remembering sky — they were memories. Thousands of them, harvested, hung overhead like laundry. Somewhere up there was someone's wedding day. Someone's last conversation with a mother. Someone's first snow. The Veil had been feasting on Veymark for a hundred years, and it wore its meals.

And on the throne sat the king.

He was smaller than his portraits. Older. His crown sat on his head the way a yoke sits on an ox, and his face — his face was the worst thing in the room, because it was smiling, beautifully, warmly, the smile of a beloved father on festival night, while his eyes above it screamed. Not moving. Just screaming, constantly, silently, the way a house screams in a fire. He had been screaming for decades. Perhaps he had been screaming since the beginning, since whatever grief or hunger had first let the Veil put its fingers around his mind.

Rafael stopped ten paces from the throne and swayed.

"It's time," said the thing wearing him. "Vessel. Kneel."

And Rafael — my Rafael, the quiet stranger whose stolen charm had unraveled my whole careful life — Rafael fought. I saw it happen. Saw his jaw lock, his shoulders shake, saw sweat break across his forehead as two wills strained against each other inside one borrowed body. "Ulyana," he ground out, through teeth that weren't entirely his own, "the scar. My hand. It has to be *me*. The binding anchors through me — cut it at the anchor or it just — finds another vessel, another hundred years, another—"

"Kneel," the Veil said again, and this time it wasn't a request; it moved his legs for him, took his knees like a puppeteer, and brought him down before the throne with his scarred palm turned upward, offered.

Oskar moved first. The disgraced hunter drew the Order's black iron blade — the one weapon forged to cut memory rather than flesh — and got three steps before the ceiling came down at him. Not stone. Memory. A wall of harvested moments crashed between us and the throne, and inside it I glimpsed faces — Oskar's sister among them, laughing at a table that no longer existed anywhere but here — and Oskar made a sound I will hear until I die, and swung anyway, and the blade passed through the wall like it was smoke, because to iron it was smoke, but the wall did not care about iron. It closed around him and held him, drowning him in his own stolen grief.

Marisol did what hedge-witches do. She cursed, magnificently, and then she began to sing — low, cracked, a lullaby in a border dialect the Order had tried to burn out of the world — and where her song touched the memory-wall it thinned, frayed, forgot itself for a few precious seconds at a time. "Go," she shouted at me over her own music. "Girl, *go*, I can't hold this long — Tam, with me—"

And then it was only me, walking toward the crown, past the kneeling vessel, past the screaming king, while the thing in the ceiling noticed me and began, slowly, to pay attention.

* * *

The binding was not a chain. I had imagined chains, seals, knots of light. It was none of those things.

When I reached the throne and looked closely at the king's crown — old gold, unremarkable, the kind of object that survives centuries precisely because no one thinks to steal it — I saw that it was stitched. Stitched to his head, to his thoughts, with thread made of memory. A hundred years of surrendered moments, woven strand over strand, holding the Veil to the man the way ivy holds to a wall. And every strand, I realized, heart slamming, had been given freely. That was the trick of it. The Veil could take nothing. It could only receive. Every citizen who had ever loved the king too much, every mourner who had whispered *take my sorrow instead*, every festival crowd pouring joy upward — they had fed it willingly, and willingness was the mortar.

Which meant the prophecy had told the truth in the cruelest possible grammar. To unbind it, the threads had to be replaced — matched with stronger ones, freely given, from someone who owned nothing else. From someone whose entire self was already a hoard of stolen childhood, carried ten years in glass and charm-silver against her heart.

From me.

"You don't have to," Rafael gasped from the floor. He had dragged himself up onto his knees by sheer stubbornness, fighting his own body for the right to speak. "Ulyana — listen to me — there's another way, there's always another way, we'll find it, we'll—"

"There isn't," I said. "You know there isn't. You woke up knowing it. That's what you are — you're the loophole *and* the bait, and the bait only works if someone loves the loophole enough to spring it."

"I've known you three weeks," he said, and his voice broke down the middle, both voices breaking together, his and the other's, and for once they sounded the same. "Three weeks. Don't spend your whole life on three weeks."

"That's the point," I said. "It isn't spending. It's giving. There's a difference, and I learned it from a kitchen full of people who taught me in one evening what ten years of charms never did."

I took off my coat. Beneath it, strapped against my ribs, was the case — my case, the one thing I had guarded harder than my own heartbeat. Inside, nested in velvet: forty-one charm-bottles. Forty-one pieces of a childhood I had bought back bottle by bottle from fences and collectors and one very patient nun, over ten years of theft and trade and hunger. My mother's voice, singing. My first theft — a green apple, age six, from a market stall in a town I couldn't find on any map. My real name, which I had not spoken aloud since I was nine years old, because names are the first thing fences take and the last thing they sell.

The ceiling leaned closer. The clouds parted, and something looked down at me with an appetite so old and patient that my knees nearly went. It didn't threaten me. It didn't need to. It simply showed me what I'd be losing — played my mother's voice back to me in the dark of my own skull, one verse, perfect, and let the silence after it stretch.

I had imagined this moment a hundred times on the way here, and in every imagining I hesitated.

I did not hesitate.

"Freely given," I said, to the Veil, to the king, to whatever law governed such transactions. "Do you hear me? Nobody's taking this. I'm *giving* it. Write that down in your hundred-year-old book."

I pulled the stopper from the first bottle.

* * *

It did not hurt. That surprised me most. I had braced for pain, for tearing, for the cold hook of magic in the meat of me. Instead it felt like exhaling after holding my breath for ten years.

My mother's voice went first — poured out of the bottle and into the golden thread, and the thread drank it, and the binding shuddered. Then the apple, green and cold and triumphant, six years old and wicked-fast. Then the rest, faster now, forty-one small eternities streaming from glass into gold: scraped knees, a dog whose name I'd forgotten even as I gave it away, snow, a door painted blue, a woman's hands braiding hair, my name — my real name, spoken once, freely, into the dark, and never mine again.

The Veil convulsed. It had expected theft, resistance, a struggle it could savor. It had never been *fed* like this — not crumbs of borrowed feeling but a whole human childhood, dense and specific and irreplaceable, and the binding threads seized on it, thickened, hardened — and then, exactly as the prophecy promised, the strands that held the king began to pull *away* from him, drawn toward the richer meal, the fresher grief, the freely surrendered past that outshone a century of scraps.

The crown lifted.

Not far. An inch. Two. But the king's scream changed — from silent to audible, from agony to something rawer, and I knelt beside him and saw his eyes for the first time clearly, and they were looking at me, and they were *his*, only his, the man surfacing through a century of drowning.

"Thank—" he rasped, and could not finish, and did not need to.

Oskar burst free of the dissolving memory-wall with the black blade raised, and Marisol's song rose to a peak, and Rafael — freed the instant the anchor-thread slackened — caught the falling crown in his scarred palm, and Oskar struck it, once, through the center, where a hundred years of willing memory had knotted itself into a single shining cord.

The sound it made was not loud. It was the opposite of loud — a silence so complete that the whole room heard it, the sound of a story ending mid-word.

The Veil collapsed inward. There is no other way to describe it. The clouds in the ceiling rushed toward a point above the throne and shrank and shrank, all that stolen joy and grief compressing into a darkness the size of a coin, a fingernail, a grain — and then it was gone, starved out, a fire that had finally been given nothing left to burn. The memory-wall fell as ordinary dust. The lamps guttered out honestly, needing oil now like anything else.

The king died in the space of a breath, human to the last, and his face at the end was not smiling, and it was the most beautiful face I have ever seen, because it was only a face, tired and old and finally, finally alone in his own head.

Rafael stayed kneeling, the dead crown in his open hand, and I knelt beside him, and neither of us said anything, because we were both, at that moment, entirely empty — and emptiness, I was only beginning to learn, is not the same as nothing. Emptiness is a room with the windows open.

"Your name," he said at last, hoarse. "You gave it away. What do I call you now?"

I thought about it. The place where my childhood had been did not ache; it *waited*, the way a shelf waits.

"Whatever you call me," I said, "make it worth keeping."

He smiled — his own smile, rough and quick, bread handed in a storm — and outside the broken windows of the throne room, for the first time in a hundred years, dawn came to Veymark without anyone forgetting it.
