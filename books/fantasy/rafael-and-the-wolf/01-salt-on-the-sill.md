## 1. Salt on the Sill

The trouble with selling luck was that everyone wanted it on credit.

Odile Quintana had learned this in her first month at Vellamare's gray market, back when she still flinched at loud voices and counted her coins twice a night out of habit rather than need. Seven years later she counted them once, and only because the fishmongers' quarter flooded every spring and coins left in a drawer grew a fine green fur that took forever to scrub off.

"Salt," said Marta Ferran, planting herself in front of Odile's stall with the confidence of a woman who had buried one husband and intended never to bury anything else if she could help it. "For the sill. The new one."

"You've had salt on your sill since the flood."

"The flood *took* my salt." Marta said this as though the water had committed a personal offense, which, Odile supposed, it had. "And since then my nets come up empty, my chimney smokes sideways, and my neighbor's rooster has taken to crowing at midnight like it's being paid to."

Odile kept her hands busy with the little cloth bundles while she listened. This was the trick of it — the whole architecture of her survival, balanced on a market table no wider than a rowboat: keep your hands moving, keep your voice bored, and never, ever let anyone see you do anything that couldn't be explained by an honest woman with good stock and better manners.

She had three kinds of luck-charms. The first were nothing at all — knotted thread and dried lavender, sold for a copper to people who mostly needed someone to listen to them. The second were almost nothing — a whisper of intent worked into the knot, enough to tip a coin toss or soften a headache. Those cost two coppers and required her to be very sure no one was watching. The third kind she did not sell anymore, and hadn't in seven years, and thought about approximately four hundred times a day.

"Sill-salt's a copper," she said. "But you're not paying me a copper for salt, Marta, we both know where the salt comes from."

"I'm paying you for knowing which salt."

That was the other thing about the gray market: its customers were not fools. Fools went to the temple and bought blessed candles at triple the price. The people who came to Odile's corner of Fishgut Lane — officially the Lane of Saint Elmo's Lantern, though nobody living could remember who Saint Elmo had been or why he'd wanted a lantern — were the ones who understood that the world had seams, and that some women knew how to sew.

Odile measured coarse grey salt into a twist of paper, and while Marta watched the harbor gulls fight over a fish head, she breathed over it once, briefly, the way you'd breathe on a window to fog it. A small working. A *tidy* working. It would keep the worst of the ill-luck off the doorstep the way a broom keeps off leaves — not forever, but enough.

"For the sill," she said, handing it over. "Throw a pinch at the corners too. And Marta — the rooster isn't cursed. Roosters are just terrible."

"He knows something," Marta muttered darkly, paying.

"Roosters know nothing. That's their whole character."

Marta smiled despite herself, then didn't, then looked at the ground. Odile knew what was coming before the widow said it, because widows always got there eventually, circling the same grief from different angles like gulls over a wake.

"You'll do something for the boat? For Tomás's boat. It sits there and I can't—" She stopped. Started again, quieter. "I can't look at it sitting there doing nothing. It's like it's sulking."

*It is sulking,* Odile did not say. Boats held impressions the way wool held smells, and Tomás Ferran's boat held seven years of him — his laugh, his swearing, the particular tune he'd hummed when gutting fish, badly, because Tomás had been a man of many talents and pitch had not been among them. She could feel it from here, a low warm hum of a thing, like a hearth banked for the night.

What she said was: "Two coppers for a knot-work. Fair warning, it won't bring him back and it won't make the boat sail itself. It'll just... settle it. So it rests easy instead of grieving at you every morning."

"That's worth four," Marta said, and put four coppers down before Odile could argue, which was the widow's own form of magic and considerably more forceful than anything Odile could work.

So Odile took the twine, and under cover of measuring it against her forearm — everyone measured twine, nobody looked twice at a woman measuring twine — she worked the knot. Three loops for the past, two for the present, one left open, because boats and people both deserved a future even when it hurt to think about. She murmured nothing aloud. Words were for the kind of witches who wanted to be caught.

"There," she said. "Tie it to the tiller. When the wind's right, it'll feel less like a ghost and more like a memory."

"What's the difference?" Marta asked, and Odile, who had built her entire life on the difference, found she had no answer ready that wasn't true in a dangerous way.

"About a mile of walking," she said finally, and Marta laughed, and pocketed the knot, and went off toward the harbor with her salt and her grief and her terrible rooster waiting at home.

* * *

The gray market opened when the tide did, which meant Odile was at her stall by gray dawn, arranging her wares into the picture of harmlessness: bundles of herbs any grandmother might keep, candles of honest beeswax, twine, salt, dried fish-skin for polishing boots. Nothing enchanted sat in plain view. Everything enchanted sat within reach of plain view, which was different, and which was the entire point.

Her stall was wedged between a mender of nets who was also, unofficially, a mender of marriages, and a man who sold songbirds and could tell you, for a fee, what your dreams meant, though his interpretations ran heavily toward *you should buy another songbird*. Down the lane, the oyster-woman kept a running argument going with the weather. It was, in its way, a village. It was, more importantly, invisible. No crown official had ever bothered to inventory Fishgut Lane, because Fishgut Lane smelled like exactly what it was, and clerks avoided it the way cats avoid water.

This was how Odile liked it. Invisible, unimportant, and smelling faintly of brine.

She was restocking her candle basket when the feeling arrived.

She had no better name for it than *the feeling*, though her old mistress would have called it something longer and more precise, in that language she used for important things, the language she'd made Odile memorize and then made her promise to forget. The feeling was simple: it was the sensation of being *looked for*. Not looked at — markets were full of looking; a woman selling charms got looked at all day. Looked *for*. The difference was the weight of it. Looking slid off you like rain off oilcloth. Looking-for stuck, the way a hand sticks to frost.

Odile set down the candle she was holding and did not turn around.

There was a protocol for this. She'd written it herself, years ago, in the flat practical voice she used for such things: *If the feeling comes, finish the transaction. Do not hurry. Hurrying is a flag. Pack slowly. Leave by the cooper's alley, not the main lane. Go home by the long way. Count to one hundred before lighting a lamp.*

She finished the transaction — a boy buying tallow ends for his mother, haggling with the ferocity of a dockworker over a half-copper — and packed slowly, and left by the cooper's alley, and went home by the long way, along the seawall where the wind came off the water hard enough to make eavesdropping impossible.

Nothing followed her. Of course nothing followed her. Nothing ever followed her. In seven years, the feeling had come eleven times, and eleven times it had amounted to nothing: a tax collector, a runaway apprentice, once a man from the Temple of the Tidewife asking questions about unlicensed blessings, whom the oyster-woman had drowned out with a lecture on the theological implications of mollusks until he fled.

Eleven times nothing. But the feeling didn't care about odds. The feeling was older than odds. It lived somewhere below her thinking mind, in the place where the gift actually resided — that warm coal behind her breastbone that she fed so carefully, so sparingly, like a woman rationing the last firewood of a long winter.

Because here was the truth she carried, folded up small and kept in the same inner pocket as her real name: the gift grew when you used it, and starved when you hid it, and Odile had spent seven years keeping it on the edge of starvation — enough to live, not enough to shine. Her mistress had told her once, in that language, that a gift starved too long turned strange. Turned inward. Began eating its keeper.

Odile had decided, at nineteen, that she'd take her chances with strangeness.

She climbed the stairs to her room above the chandler's shop — one room, one window, one door with a lock she'd improved in ways a locksmith couldn't have managed and wouldn't have believed — and went through the evening ritual. Lamp lit, shutter checked, salt line renewed at the threshold, not for spirits but for feet: the salt whispered to her, faintly, when crossed by anyone meaning her harm. In seven years it had whispered twice. Both times it had been the landlady's cat, whose feelings about Odile were genuinely hostile and, Odile suspected, magical in origin.

She ate bread and sardines. She counted her coins once. She took out the little book — not a grimoire, nothing so grand, just a ledger of accounts, because a charm-seller who didn't keep accounts was a fool — and noted the day's takings. Four coppers extra from Marta. She'd return two of them somehow, tucked into a bundle of twine next week. Kindness was a currency too, and unlike coin, it appreciated.

Then she sat by the window with the lamp turned low, and let herself do the thing she only allowed after dark, in private, with the salt-line fresh: she let the gift stretch.

Just a little. Just to the edges of the room. It unfurled like a cat waking, warm and grateful and slightly reproachful — *seven years, and you give me a knot-work and a breath on some salt* — and Odile breathed through the ache of it, the way you breathe through a cramp. Somewhere across the city, a hundred small workings of hers were quietly doing their quiet work: knots easing grief, salts turning away bad luck, a candle in a shrine to no particular saint that burned a little steadier than physics allowed, for the benefit of whoever needed steadiness that night.

Small kindnesses. Small enough to notice only in their absence. That was the doctrine she'd built from the wreckage of everything her mistress had taught her: be useful, be tiny, be forgettable.

Be forgettable. She mouthed the word sometimes like a prayer, and like most prayers, she'd stopped expecting an answer.

Outside, Vellamare settled into evening. The harbor bells rang the tide. Somewhere a rooster crowed at midnight, punctual as a debt, and Odile smiled despite herself, and blew out the lamp, and slept the way she always slept: lightly, with one ear open, like a woman sharing a bed with the past.

* * *

Three days later, the feeling came back, and this time it brought company.

She was at her stall in the gray light of morning, arguing with the net-mender about whether a certain knot was romantic or merely practical, when the bells began. Not the harbor bells — those rang the tide, six notes, reliable as sunrise. These were the cathedral bells, high on the hill, and they were ringing wrong. Fast. Overlapping. The sound they made for proclamations.

Fishgut Lane went quiet the way a schoolroom goes quiet. Even the oyster-woman stopped mid-argument, mouth open, one hand raised against the weather that had, for once, done nothing to provoke her.

Down the hill, in the great square by the customs house, a crier was reading. Odile couldn't hear the words from the lane. She didn't need to. She could read the shape of it in the backs of the crowd gathered at the lane's mouth — the way people stood very still, then turned, all together, slowly, like a field of flowers tracking a sun that had just changed color.

They turned and looked at her.

Not at her, she corrected herself, heart hammering. At the lane. At the idea of the lane. At the corner where a woman sold charms too small to notice.

But the feeling — the looking-for — was back, and this time it had a name attached, arriving in her mind with the cold clarity of a knot pulled tight. She didn't know how she knew it. The gift knew things sideways sometimes, through walls and miles and sense, and what it handed her now was a single word from the crier's parchment, carried uphill on a thousand mouths beginning to repeat it:

*Edict.*

Behind her, the net-mender said, very quietly, "Odile. Girl. You'd best pack up."

She was already reaching for her baskets, hands steady, face bored, the whole careful architecture of invisibility assembled around her like armor — and finding, for the first time in seven years, that she could not stop them from shaking.
