## 2. The Proclamation

The morning the criers came to Saltrow Lane, Odile Quintana was arguing with a hen.

"You cannot eat that," she told it, prying a beetle from its beak with the stern gentleness she'd learned from seven years of persuading stubborn things to behave. "That beetle is for the charm. You have your own breakfast. It's right there. In the dish. That I bought you."

The hen—named Duchess by the widow Marchetti who owned her and had no imagination whatsoever—regarded Odile with one yellow eye, clearly weighing whether the rules of property applied to chickens. They did not, as it turned out; Duchess swallowed the beetle with a smug little cluck, and Odile sighed and went out to the garden wall to dig up another.

This was the whole of her morning: beetles, hens, salt, thread, and the small careful work of being nobody. She had gotten very good at it. There was an art to invisibility that no spell could teach—it lived in the way you walked (unhurried, slightly bored), the way you dressed (mended twice, mended again), the way you answered questions (with questions, preferably dull ones). *Lovely weather.* *Terrible price on fish this week.* *No, I've never had a thought more interesting than soup in my life.*

The charms themselves were almost embarrassingly small. A knotted red thread to keep a child's fever from climbing past the third night. A pinch of salt sewn into a hem so a drunk husband's fists would miss by an inch. A dried beetle in wax—not even a rare beetle, just the common brown kind—to help a fisherman find his way home through fog. Nothing that would make the hair stand up on a magistrate's neck. Nothing that would make anyone look twice at the gray-faced woman selling them from a basket, three coppers each, four if you wanted them blessed, which they weren't, because blessing implied a blesser, and blessers drew attention.

Her dead mistress had called the gift a debt. *You don't get to spend it on yourself, girl,* old Serafina had said, hands like tree roots folded over her stick. *It isn't yours. It's owed forward.* Odile had spent seven years paying it forward in increments too small to notice, and she intended to go on doing exactly that until she died of old age in a chair, unremarked, unmolested, and extremely smug about it.

She was threading the replacement beetle onto its wax when the bells started.

Not the chapel bells—they rang on the hour, patient and tuneless. These were the harbor bells, the great bronze ones that only spoke when ships were in distress or when the city itself had something to say. They boomed down the lanes of Saltrow like the voice of something enormous clearing its throat, and every dog in the district began howling, and Duchess flew off the wall in a clatter of feathers as though the apocalypse were specifically a chicken problem.

Odile stood very still with the beetle swinging from its thread.

Then came the footsteps—many feet, in step—and around the corner of the chandlery marched six men in the livery of the crown, followed by a clerk on a cart, followed by a crier in the blue-and-gold sash of the Provost's office. The crier climbed onto the cart, unrolled a paper with the ceremony of a man unveiling a portrait of himself, and cleared his throat with the confidence of someone who had never once been interrupted.

"Hear ye! By decree of His Majesty's Grace and the Council of Purity, the Edict of Ash is proclaimed throughout Vellamare and all territories subject to the crown!"

Odile put the beetle in her pocket. Her hands wanted to do something, and she refused to let them tremble where people could see.

"Let it be known that the practice of the hidden arts—the working of charms, hexes, blessings, bindings, scryings, or any craft of power not licensed by the Church—is hereby declared a crime against the realm. All persons so gifted shall present themselves at the Registry Hall within thirty days to declare their nature and submit to assessment."

*Assessment.* What a soft word for whatever happened behind the doors of a building that had been a granary until last spring.

"Those who register shall be catalogued and, where their gifts prove useful to the crown, retained in service." The crier's voice took on the particular relish of a man reading the good part. "Those who do not register shall be deemed *concealed practitioners*, subject to hunt, seizure, and judgment. Informants presenting verified reports shall be compensated fifty crowns per name."

Fifty crowns. Odile did the arithmetic without wanting to. Fifty crowns was two years of a dockhand's wages. Fifty crowns was a new roof, a season free of hunger, a daughter's dowry. Fifty crowns was the exact price of a neighbor.

The crier rolled up his paper. The soldiers moved on toward Cooper's Walk, boots striking stone in that rhythm that made her think, absurdly, of a loom—weft and warp, weaving something over the whole city. The crowd on Saltrow Lane dispersed the way crowds do when there is nothing to throw and nowhere safe to look: gradually, then all at once, everyone suddenly very interested in their own errands.

Except Widow Marchetti, who came puffing up Odile's steps with Duchess tucked under one arm like a feathered loaf of bread.

"Did you hear," the widow whispered, though whispering was not among her talents; the whole lane could have heard her. "Fifty crowns. For a *name*. My own sister would sell me for twenty."

"Your sister loves you."

"My sister loves me *and* owes money. Love has never once stopped her collecting." The widow set Duchess down and lowered herself onto Odile's step with the groan of a woman whose knees had opinions. "What will you do?"

"I'll sell charms," Odile said. "Same as always. Nobody's going to report me. I'm the least interesting person in Vellamare."

It was mostly true, and she believed it the way you believe a rope bridge holds: fully, right up until the moment you're standing on it.

* * *

She should have gone home. That was the sensible thing—bar the door, bank the fire, sit with her fear like a fever until it broke or didn't. Instead she found herself walking toward Tanners' Row, basket on her arm, telling herself it was a delivery route and not a pilgrimage.

Matteo the bookbinder kept a shop narrow enough that he could touch both walls at once, which he claimed made him impossible to burglarize and merely made him impossible to visit. He was a big man gone soft at the edges, with ink permanently staining his fingers to the first knuckle and a laugh that started in his belly and took the scenic route out. He had been coming to Odile for three years—for a charm against damp rot for his leathers, for a knot of blue thread his wife tied round the bedpost when her lungs went bad in winter, for nothing at all some weeks except conversation and the two cups of chicory he insisted on pouring.

He was the only person in Vellamare who knew what she was. Not guessed—*knew*, because three winters ago his wife had been dying and Odile had sat by the bed all night doing work she couldn't disguise as luck, and Matteo had watched her hands move and said nothing then and nothing ever after, except once, quietly: *"I know what you are. And I know what you did. The second thing is the only one I'll ever talk about."*

She trusted him the way you trust a load-bearing wall. You don't think about it. You just build your life against it.

The bell over his door jingled as she entered, and there he was at his bench, pressing gold leaf into a psalter with a brush made of one squirrel hair and infinite patience.

"If it isn't the least interesting woman in Vellamare," he said without looking up. "I heard the bells. I assume you've come to tell me it's all nonsense and we can go back to pretending the world is run by reasonable people."

"That was my plan, yes."

"Excellent. Chicory?"

"Matteo." She set her basket down. "Thirty days. Then they start hunting."

"And I'm meant to panic?" He looked up at last, and his smile was the wide, easy thing it always was, but she saw now that it was painted on over something else, the way gilt is laid over board. "Odile. Listen to me. This is Vellamare. We have survived three sieges, two plagues, and the Great Flood of the Doge Ruggiero, during which the entire city council rowed to safety in a boat built for twelve carrying forty. Do you know why? Because we are a city of liars, cowards, and excellent negotiators. In a month the Registry Hall will have registered four madmen, a man who thinks he can talk to eels, and one actual hedge-witch who turned herself in by mistake. The crown will declare victory. Everyone will go back to cheating each other at cards."

"Four madmen and an eel-charmer," Odile repeated.

"At most. Sit down. Your cup is the chipped one, because you're family and family gets the chipped one."

She sat. She drank the chicory, which tasted the way it always did, like coffee's poorer cousin who'd been through some things. And for a quarter of an hour it was almost possible to believe him, because Matteo believed it, and Matteo was a wall, and walls don't fall.

They fell anyway. Walls always do. It's just a question of which morning.

* * *

She was halfway back up Saltrow Lane, basket light, purse lighter, when she heard the second set of bells—and under them, worse, a sound she'd never heard in Vellamare before: running feet in armor.

They came down Tanners' Row at a trot, eight of them with the Provost's greyhound badge on their breasts, and the crowd peeled away from them like skin from an onion. Odile pressed into the doorway of the ropemaker's shop, heart going hard, and watched them surround the bookbinder's.

Matteo came out himself. Of course he did. He came out wiping his hands on his apron, big and unhurried, wearing the face of a man greeting late customers.

"Gentlemen. If you're after the psalter for Canon Bellorini, it needs another week. Gold leaf waits for no—"

"Matteo Colombo?" The sergeant had a list. Of course he had a list. Lists were cheaper than witches.

"Bookbinder, yes. What's the charge?"

"A report of concealed practice. Charms sold from these premises. Healing works performed upon one Lucia Colombo, deceased last winter." The sergeant said it flatly, the way you read a bill of lading. "You're to come to the Registry Hall. Not as accused—" and here the sergeant smiled, a small administrative smile, "—as a *witness*. Names, friend. Give us names, and you walk out the front door instead of whatever door they use."

The lane had gone silent. Forty people hearing every word, forty people suddenly finding their own shoes fascinating. Odile stood in the ropemaker's doorway with her basket on her arm and understood, with a clarity that felt like cold water poured down her spine, that she was watching her own future arrive early, wearing someone else's coat.

"They healed my wife," Matteo said. His voice was still level. Still warm. But Odile heard the change underneath it, the sound of a man discovering the floor is thinner than he thought. "Whoever they were. I never saw a face. I paid in bread and firewood, like anyone does."

"The report says otherwise."

"The report," Matteo said, "is wrong."

There was a pause in which the whole street held its breath, and Odile silently begged him—*lie, you enormous kind fool, lie the way you promised, lie about everything*—but Matteo Colombo had never lied successfully in his life. It was, he often said, his single failing as a businessman: he could gild a book but not a story.

So he said, "And even if it weren't wrong, I wouldn't give you the name," and the sergeant sighed the sigh of a man whose afternoon had just gotten longer, and nodded, and two soldiers took Matteo by the arms.

It was done gently. That was the horror of it. No chains, no shouting—he was simply walked, at a civilized pace, between men with swords, past his own shop with its bell still swaying above the door, while his apprentice stood in the window with gold leaf stuck to his fingers and his mouth open. At the corner Matteo turned his head, just once, and looked back down the lane—at the crowd, at the shop, at nothing in particular—and Odile, flattened into the shadows of the doorway, would swear until her dying day that his eyes passed over her and deliberately, kindly, moved on.

Then the corner took him, and the lane exhaled, and everyone began talking at once in the low urgent murmur of people assuring each other they had seen nothing worth fifty crowns.

* * *

She got home. She didn't remember the walk, which frightened her more than anything else—that her body could carry her through streets full of soldiers while her mind stood entirely elsewhere, in a bookbinder's shop, holding a chipped cup.

Inside, she barred the door and stood in the middle of her one room with her heart slamming and did the arithmetic again, because arithmetic was the only prayer she knew.

Thirty days. Fifty crowns a name. Matteo knew her face, her name—or the name she used, which was thin as paper—and the address of her door. Whatever they did to him behind the Registry Hall's doors, they would do it competently. She had seen competent men work before. She had fled competent men before, seven years ago, from a house in the hills, and she had sworn she'd learned enough to never be caught unready again.

So: the stock. Every charm in the hiding place under the floorboards was evidence now—a knotted thread, a wax-sealed beetle, a sachet of salt, each one a small signed confession. Thirty days was a luxury; informants worked faster than edicts. Tonight, maybe sooner, she would burn everything. Melt the wax, scatter the ash in the harbor, keep nothing that could be named.

She knelt and pried up the loose board, and the smell of the hiding place rose to meet her—wax and dried herbs and seven years of careful small work—and she began lifting the charms out one by one, laying them on the cold hearthstone, counting her sins into a pile.

Near the bottom of the cache, wrapped in oilcloth against the damp, she found a charm she did not remember making.

She knew her own work the way she knew her own hands. This was hers—the knotting was hers, the wax was hers—but the thread was older, faded to the color of weak tea, and the knot was a pattern she hadn't used in years, a pattern from the hills, from the house in the hills, from before. Her fingers, unwrapping it, moved with a familiarity that made her stomach drop, as though her hands remembered a morning her mind had carefully misfiled.

Inside the wax, folded small, was a scrap of paper. Her handwriting. Older, younger, rounder—but hers, unmistakably, the way a voice is unmistakable.

She didn't read it. She held it over the hearth, over the pile of everything else waiting for the flame, and her hand stopped, and stayed stopped, and outside the sun went on setting over Vellamare as though the day had been an ordinary one.

From the scrap of paper, in her own seven-years-younger hand, written in ink she had no memory of buying:

*If you are reading this, you already forgot. Go back to the beginning. Ask what Rafael really taught you.*

The name sat on the page like a stone dropped into still water.

Odile Quintana, the least interesting woman in Vellamare, sat down slowly on the floor among her evidence, and did not light the fire.
