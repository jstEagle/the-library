## 3. What the Charm Knew

The fire in Odile's little room was too small for what she asked of it.

It was a chipped clay brazier, the kind fishwives used to warm their toes, and it had spent its whole life heating soup and drying stockings. Now she knelt before it with a basket of her life's work balanced on her hip, and it crackled up at her as if to say, *you want me to do what?*

"I know," Odile told it. "I'm sorry. We'll take it slow."

She had come home from the proclamation square with her heart still galloping and done the sensible thing first, which was to make tea, because a woman who makes tea is a woman who has decided not to scream. Then she had sat on her stool by the window and watched the street below while the tea went cold, counting the people who passed. A cooper's apprentice with sawdust on his sleeves. Two women arguing cheerfully about eels. A boy dragging a stick along the cobbles like he was writing something only he could read.

Ordinary people. Ordinary morning.

And somewhere among them, sooner or later, someone who had seen her face at the market stall, or bought a charm off her, or simply noticed that the widow Sera's nets came home full while everyone else's came home empty — someone who would remember, when the reward money made remembering profitable.

*All gifted persons must present themselves for registration.*

Registration. As though the gift were a dog you could leash and license. As though it were hers at all, really — it had never felt like property. It felt more like a tenant that lived behind her ribs and paid its rent in small mercies: milk that didn't sour, fevers that broke a night early, knots that untied themselves when a child's fingers were caught in them.

Seven years she had kept that tenant quiet. Seven years of charms so small they barely qualified as magic — a button that never fell off, bread that rose a little prouder, a hairpin that found lost earrings. Nothing anyone could point to. Nothing that would make a neighbor say *her, I always thought there was something about her.*

And now the crown had gone and said it for them.

Odile set down the cold tea, took up the basket, and began to feed her work to the fire.

* * *

It went against every grain of her. Each charm had been made with care, and care does not like burning. The first one — a knotted red thread for safe childbirth, sold three years ago to a woman whose daughter had, thank all the patient saints, needed it for nothing worse than a long labor and a loud baby — went up in a puff of smoke that smelled briefly of lavender and iron.

"Nothing personal," Odile murmured. "You did your work."

The second batch hurt more, because it was stock she'd been meaning to sell: little wax seals pressed with a thumbprint, good for one honest night's sleep apiece. She'd carved those during a bad winter, working by rushlight, thinking about how many people in Vellamare lay awake counting fears instead of sheep. She fed them in one at a time so the brazier wouldn't choke, and each one gave a soft *whump* as it caught, like a small sigh of relief. Sleep-charms, dying in their sleep. There was a joke in there somewhere, and she was too frightened to find it funny, which was how she knew it was a good joke.

Then the knot-charms. Then the weather-blessings, tiny things, just enough to nudge a fog off a fishing lane or hold rain back from a wedding until the vows were done. Then the bundle of buttons-that-never-fall-off, which burned with an offended blue flame, as if even the fire were embarrassed.

She worked through the morning. The room filled with smoke and the smell of everything she'd ever been careful about, and outside her window Vellamare went on shouting about fish prices, and somewhere a fiddler was practicing badly, and none of it knew. That was the strange part. The world didn't know yet. It was like watching a wave gather far out at sea and knowing that everyone on the beach was still laughing.

By midday the basket held only scraps: misshapen pieces, failed experiments, the leavings of seven years. And one item wrapped in oilcloth at the very bottom, which she had no memory of putting there.

Odile frowned at it.

The oilcloth was old. Not dirty-old — *aged*-old, the way cloth goes when it has been folded and refolded many times and then left alone in the dark to think about itself. She turned it over in her hands. Her fingers, which knew every charm she owned the way a mother knows her children's voices, reported nothing. No warmth, no hum, no tenant-twitch behind her ribs. Whatever this was, it wasn't hers.

Except.

Except that when she picked at the knot holding it shut, her hands moved without instruction. Three tugs and a quarter-turn, a sequence she had never been taught and could not have described a moment earlier, and the knot sighed open like it had been waiting years for exactly that greeting.

Her mouth went dry.

"Right," she said aloud, to the brazier, to the room, to nobody. "That's fine. That's completely fine."

It was not fine.

Inside the oilcloth lay a charm the size of a walnut, wrapped in waxed linen and tied with thread gone gray with age. And stitched into the linen, in thread the color of dried blood, were words. Her words. She knew the hand the way you know your own reflection — the slightly crooked *h*, the *d* that always leaned too far into the next letter's space, the little hook she put on the end of an *e* because a boy named Anselmo had once told her it looked like a smile.

*For O.Q., from O.Q.*

She read it four times. The words did not rearrange themselves, did not soften, did not turn out to say anything else. *For O.Q., from O.Q.* For Odile Quintana, from Odile Quintana.

She had not written it. She would have remembered writing it. You remember the things you stitch in blood-colored thread; those are not the sort of thing that slips a mind.

But it was her handwriting. Not similar. Not close. *Hers*, down to the crooked h, down to the smiling e — and older, worn, faded the way thread fades across years, not months. She held it up to the window light and her stomach performed a slow, deliberate revolution, because the fading was not the fading of seven weeks. It was the fading of seven years.

Seven years ago she had been in another city entirely, under another roof, learning the trade from a man whose name she had not spoken aloud since the night she ran.

"No," she said. Firmly. Conversationally, the way you'd correct a child reaching for a knife. "No, we're not doing this."

The charm sat in her palm and did nothing whatsoever, which was somehow worse than if it had glowed, or whispered, or grown teeth. It just lay there being impossible, patient as a debt.

* * *

She tried to burn it.

This deserves describing properly, because Odile Quintana was not a cowardly woman — fear and cowardice are different animals, and she had spent seven years feeding one and starving the other — but when she carried the charm to the brazier on the tongs and held it over the coals, her arm stopped. Not shook. *Stopped*, the way a door stops when something on the other side is leaning against it.

The coals reached up. The linen began to brown at the edges. And then the smoke, instead of rising, bent — bent sideways, away from the charm, curling around her wrist like a cat declining to be bathed.

Odile set the tongs down very carefully, walked to the window, opened it, and breathed Vellamare's fish-and-tar air until her heart agreed to rejoin the conversation.

When she came back, the charm lay where she'd left it, untouched, unbothered, its blood-colored stitching catching the afternoon light.

"All right," she said. "New plan. We don't burn you. We *understand* you. Understanding is free."

Understanding, it turned out, was not free either.

She tried the obvious things first. She unwound the outer thread — it let her, grudgingly, the way an old dog lets a stranger pet it — and found beneath the linen a core of something dark and dense, not wax, not clay, not any herb she knew. When she touched it, the room went briefly, horribly quiet: the fiddler outside, the gulls, the shouting fishwives, all of it cut off as cleanly as scissors through string. One heartbeat of silence. Then the world rushed back in, twice as loud, as if apologizing.

So: the charm ate sound. Or held it. Or *remembered* it — and that word arrived in her head uninvited and settled there like a stone dropping into a well.

Remembered.

She sat down hard on her stool.

Because here was the thing she had never told anyone, the thing she barely told herself: the gift had always been a little bit like this. Not the eating-silence part — the *knowing-things-it-shouldn't* part. Charms she made sometimes came out already suited to their buyer, before she'd heard a word of their troubles. She'd tell herself it was observation, deduction, the ordinary cleverness of a woman who watched people for a living. Mostly she believed it. On certain nights, alone, with the lamp low, she had not believed it at all, and had lain awake wondering what else the gift knew that she didn't, and what it was doing with the knowledge, and whether a person could be said to own a thing that kept secrets from her.

Now here was the answer, sitting in her palm, wearing her handwriting like a mask.

"You're mine," she said to it. "Aren't you. From before."

The silence it made when she touched it again lasted two heartbeats this time. Progress, or threat. With charms, those were often the same thing.

* * *

Evening came down over Vellamare the way it always did, gray going violet going black, lamps lit one by one like cautious thoughts. Odile lit no lamp. She sat in the dark with the charm on the table in front of her and a pot of tea she kept forgetting to drink, and she reasoned with herself the way you reason with a horse that smells wolves.

Option one: she had made it and forgotten. People forgot things. Seven years was a long time, and the year she'd fled had been full enough to lose whole seasons in. Except that her hands remembered knots her mind had never learned, and the thread had aged seven years, and forgetting was a leaky bucket, not a locked box. Things forgotten leaked back. This hadn't leaked. This had *waited*.

Option two: someone else had made it and forged her hand. Which raised the question of who could forge a hand so perfectly that its own owner couldn't tell — and why they'd hide it in her basket, in her room, in her life, and wait. Unless they hadn't hidden it. Unless it had always been there, at the bottom, under everything, and she had simply never dug deep enough to find it. She looked at the empty basket. She thought about all the times she'd restocked, refolded, rummaged. Had it been there all along? Had she carried it through seven years of markets and checkpoints and narrow escapes like a stone in her shoe she'd never noticed?

Option three was the one she didn't want, so naturally it was the one that fit best: the gift had made it. Before she fled, in some hour she'd sealed over with scar tissue, the tenant behind her ribs had taken her hands and stitched a letter, addressed it to the woman she would become, and tucked it into the deepest fold of her life — because it had known, even then, that someday she would need to burn everything, and that some things must survive the burning.

Which meant the past wasn't buried after all. It was *posted*. Somewhere out there was a version of her who had known this day would come, and had trusted this day's Odile to open the letter.

"Well," Odile said to the dark room, and her voice came out steadier than she felt, which was the family trick, the only inheritance she'd admit to. "I hope past-me knew what she was doing. Because present-me is about to do something stupid."

She picked up the charm. She pressed it to her ear like a seashell.

Silence poured in — not absence, but *held* sound, layered and layered, years of it compressed to a shine. And underneath, faint as a pulse through a wall, something that was almost a voice, almost a tune, almost a name—

Three sharp knocks landed on her door.

Odile went absolutely still, charm cupped against her cheek, listening. The knock came again. Unhurried. Polite. Nobody knocked politely at this hour unless they wanted something, and nobody wanted something from her at this hour unless the Edict of Ash had taught them what she was worth.

She slid the charm into her bodice, where it lay cold against her heart like a second, smaller heart that had opinions.

"One moment," she called, in her best bored-fishwife voice, and blew out the candle she had never lit, and crossed the floor on feet that made no sound at all — which, she noted distantly, was not a skill she had ever practiced.

Through the gap in the shutter, lamplight from the street below threw a shadow up her wall: tall, broad-shouldered, standing at her door with the patience of a man being paid by the hour.

On his sleeve, where the light touched it, she could see the stitched mark of the crown's new hunt.
