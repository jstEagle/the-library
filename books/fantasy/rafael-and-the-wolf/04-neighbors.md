## 4. Neighbors

Tomas Belo had three talents in this world, and he'd made a careful inventory of them over forty-one years, the way a man counts coins before paying a debt he can't quite cover.

The first was lifting. Not stealing—lifting. Crates, barrels, children, once a horse that had fainted in the July heat and needed carrying to shade like an enormous drunken cousin. His shoulders were the shoulders of a man who had spent thirty years on the Vellamare docks, and they announced him before his face did.

The second was knowing when to keep his mouth shut. This was rarer than strength on the waterfront, where every man narrated his own life at volume, and it had kept Tomas employed through four harbor masters, two wars nobody remembered starting, and one brief, confusing season when the fish all vanished and everyone blamed everyone else.

The third talent, the one he was least proud of, was arithmetic. Specifically: the arithmetic of what things cost.

He was doing that arithmetic now, sitting on an upturned crate behind the Saltcellar tavern with a mug of small beer going warm in his hand, turning over the paper the crier's man had pressed on him that morning like it might bite.

*By order of the Crown: information leading to the apprehension of unregistered practitioners of the hidden arts shall be rewarded to the sum of fifty crowns.*

Fifty crowns. He read it again, though he'd already memorized it, the way you memorize a wound.

Tomas owed Odile Quintana eleven crowns.

That was the whole trouble, right there. If he'd owed her nothing—if she were just the little charm-woman from the third floor above the rope-maker's, the one who sold luck-knots to fishermen's wives and fever-salves to anyone who knocked after dark—then the arithmetic would be simple. Fifty crowns was more than a dockhand earned in a season. Fifty crowns was his daughter Mirela's cough medicine for a year, was the roof fixed before winter, was freedom from Petri the tally-keeper whose ledger grew interest like mold.

But he didn't owe her nothing. He owed her eleven crowns and a good deal more than eleven crowns, and the more-than part was the problem.

* * *

It had started, as most of his debts did, with a death.

His wife Sefa had gone in the winter seven years back—not quickly, not slowly, just steadily, the way a candle goes, and by the end Tomas had spent everything they had on physicians who smelled of vinegar and knew less than the cat. What was left afterward was Mirela, aged six, with a fever that wouldn't break and a father who had already buried too much that year to bury her too.

The physician wanted five crowns for a visit. Tomas had four coppers and a boat-hook.

He'd carried Mirela through the gray market anyway, because carrying things was what he did, and because a man in motion feels like a man doing something. And it was there, between the eel-stalls and the woman who sold secondhand prayers, that Odile had stopped him. Small woman, gray shawl, eyes that took your measure and gave you back something kinder than you deserved.

"Put her down," Odile said. "On that crate. No—there. Good."

"I haven't any money," Tomas said, which was true, and which he said the way men say such things, braced for contempt.

"I know," Odile said. "I could smell the physician's bills on you from across the square." She was already unwrapping a twist of cloth. "This is willow-bark and something else. The something else is why it works. Give her half now, half at moonrise. Don't tell anyone where you got it."

Mirela's fever broke by morning. By the end of the week she was stealing bread off the high shelf again, and Tomas had gone back to find the charm-woman and put himself in her debt properly, because a man who doesn't pay his debts is not a man, whatever else he is.

She'd refused. That was the maddening thing. She'd waved off his coins and finally, to shut him up, named a sum so small it was almost an insult—eleven crowns, payable whenever, no hurry, don't be silly about it—and then she'd kept selling him fever-salve at cost for Mirela, and once, when the harbor master's men had decided Tomas's nephew looked like a thief, she'd sold Tomas a charm of looking-forgettable that got the boy home past three patrols without so much as a glance.

Eleven crowns. Seven years of interest paid in kindness. There wasn't a number big enough.

And now here was this paper saying fifty crowns for exactly her, or people exactly like her, and the crier's man had put it in his hand personally, had looked at Tomas with the flat patience of a man collecting names, and said, "You know anyone, friend? Everyone knows someone."

Which was the worst part. Because it was true.

* * *

"You're doing the face," said Branka, dropping onto the crate beside him. Branka worked the winch crews and had forearms like mooring rope and the conversational subtlety of a dropped anchor. "The face where you're counting."

"I'm not counting."

"You're counting. Your lips move. You count like a man praying to a god who charges interest." She nodded at the paper in his fist. "That the reward notice?"

Tomas folded it away. "It's nothing."

"Fifty crowns," Branka said, to the air, to the gulls, to the general congregation of the dockyard. "For turning in a witch. My aunt used to buy charms off a woman near the tannery. Wonder if I should turn in my aunt. She's got a wart. Practically a confession."

"Branka."

"What? It's free money. All you need is a neighbor and a grudge, and I've got both." But her voice had gone flat under the joke, the way voices did lately, and she took a pull of Tomas's beer without asking, which was how she said serious things. "They took Dario yesterday."

Tomas went still. Dario kept the chandler's shop off Rope Lane, a wide soft-spoken man who extended credit to half the waterfront and never once mentioned it, and who had sold Tomas lamp-oil through two bad winters at prices that were charity wearing work clothes. Dario, who'd bought a luck-charm from Odile every solstice and hung them in his window like a row of small brown eggs.

"Took him how?"

"Six soldiers and the new captain. Ferro. Neat as an audit." Branka stared out at the water. "His boy ran to the neighbors first, you know. Knocked on doors. *Someone say you saw nothing.* And do you know what old Vesna from upstairs said?" Branka's laugh had no humor in it anywhere. "She said, *I saw nothing, but I'll remember if I do.* Remembering. For pay. That's what we've come to. They've invented a way to make forgetting cost money."

Tomas said nothing, because his mouth had gone dry around the shape of a secret.

Because here was the thing he hadn't told Branka, hadn't told anyone: he knew. He knew exactly where an unregistered practitioner slept, three floors above the rope-maker's, behind a door painted the blue of deep water. He knew her hours, her habits, the nights she came and went along the aqueduct stair. He knew it the way you know anything about a person you've been quietly grateful to for seven years—which is to say completely, and without ever meaning to.

Everyone knows someone. He was someone.

* * *

That evening he climbed the rope-maker's stairs, and stood outside the blue door for a long time with his knuckles raised, feeling like the world's largest coward and its largest fool simultaneously, both titles earned honestly.

He'd told himself he was coming to warn her. He had also, in some cellar of himself he didn't want to open, told himself he was coming to look at her face while he decided. Both things were true, and the second one sat in his chest like a swallowed hook.

She opened the door before he knocked. She had a way of doing that.

"Tomas." Her eyes went over his shoulder, down the stairwell, cataloguing the dark the way she always did. "You're carrying something heavy. It's not a crate."

"It's—" He'd rehearsed this on the walk over. He'd had a whole speech. It left him entirely. "Can I come in?"

Inside was small and neat and smelled of rosemary and hot wax, and it was full, as always, of the small evidence of her trade: bundles of dried herbs hanging like sleeping bats, knots of colored thread, jars labeled in her tiny precise handwriting. A kettle was already on. It was always already on when he arrived, as if she'd heard him coming from three streets off, which, he supposed now, she probably had.

"Mirela's cough is better," he said. "The salve worked."

"Good." She poured tea into two cups that didn't match, and pushed the better one toward him, and waited. Waiting was another thing she did well. She could wait at you until you confessed to things you hadn't done yet.

"There's a notice," Tomas said. "Fifty crowns. For—" He couldn't say it. He turned his mug in circles. "For people like you. And they took Dario off Rope Lane yesterday, and the crier's man gave me the paper personal, and he said everyone knows someone, and Odile, I—" He looked up. "I owe you eleven crowns and I'd have paid it years ago if you'd ever let me, and I want you to know that not one copper of that fifty has ever seemed like money to me. Not once. Even when Petri's men broke our shutter. Even when Mirela needed the physician again in spring. It never once looked like money. It looked like a price tag on the only decent thing in my life, and I wanted to say so, out loud, to your face, before—" He stopped.

"Before," Odile said quietly.

"Before somebody says it differently." He set the folded notice on her table, smoothing it flat with one huge hand, an apology in paperwork. "I don't know who'll talk. That's the truth of it. I'd like to believe nobody would. But Branka's right—it only takes a neighbor and a grudge, and this city's growing grudges like mold on a wet wall. You should go. Tonight, if you can. I mean it. I'll carry whatever needs carrying. I'm good at carrying."

Odile looked at the notice for a long moment. Then she looked at him, and something in her face made him feel seven feet tall and two inches tall at once.

"Thank you," she said. "For coming to say it to my face. Do you know how rare that's about to become?"

"It shouldn't be rare. It should be—" He gestured helplessly with hands built for hauling, useless for this. "It's just what's owed."

"Eleven crowns," she said, and for a moment she smiled, tired and real. "I forgive the debt, Tomas. Consider us even."

"We are not remotely even," Tomas said, with the fervor of a man defending his own soul, "and if you think I'm letting you cross the city alone tonight with soldiers on the streets, you've badly misjudged how much I hate owing money."

* * *

He walked her as far as the mouth of the aqueduct stair, carrying her bundled stock in a grain sack over one shoulder like it weighed nothing, which for him it nearly didn't. At the bottom step she paused.

"If anyone asks," she said, "you walked me to the shrine of Saint Elosia, because I prayed for your daughter. Which is even true, if you want it to be."

"It's true," Tomas said. "Mostly. Add the tea."

"Add the tea," she agreed.

He watched her go up the stair, small and straight-backed against the last gray light, and then he turned for home, and that was when he saw the clerk.

A thin man in a registry office coat, standing at the corner of Rope Lane with a lantern and a list, asking questions of anyone slow enough to answer. Asking, by the set of his shoulders, the same question the crier's man had asked. *Everyone knows someone.*

Tomas crossed the street rather than pass him, heart hammering, furious at himself for crossing the street, furious at the clerk for existing, furious at the paper in his pocket that he still hadn't thrown away—he told himself he'd kept it as evidence, which was a lie so comfortable he nearly believed it.

At home, Mirela was awake, twelve now and sharp-eyed as her mother had been. She looked at him, and at the grain sack he'd forgotten to empty, and at his face.

"Papa," she said. "Whose boots are those prints on your conscience? You're tracking it everywhere."

"Bed," said Tomas.

But he lay awake long after, listening to the patrols in the street below, counting—not crowns this time, but doors. Every door on Rope Lane where someone owed the charm-woman something. Every door where someone owed her nothing and might want fifty crowns anyway. Arithmetic again. It was his best talent and his worst, and tonight it told him a number he couldn't bear:

Whoever talked first, talked soon.

And two streets over, in a narrow bed above a shuttered shop, a thin clerk with a lantern and a list was writing down names—and among them, entered neat and patient seven years ago, waiting in the ink like a seed, was hers.
