## 5. The Name on the List

The Hall of Records smelled of tallow, dust, and the particular despair of men who had spent thirty years alphabetizing other people's lives.

Odile had been inside it exactly twice in seven years—once to dispute a tax on her stall that she was fairly certain she didn't owe, and once to look up whether a dead woman named Marisol Ferran had ever owned the house Odile now rented. She hadn't found out. She'd decided some answers were best left unasked, like questions about what was really in sausage.

Today she needed a third answer, and it couldn't wait for courage to arrive. Courage, she had learned, rarely came when summoned; you had to start walking and hope it caught up.

She wore her plainest shawl and carried a basket of onions as camouflage, because no one suspects a woman of dangerous secrets if she also smells of allium. The registry office occupied the second floor, behind a counter scarred by generations of impatient knuckles. Behind it sat four clerks, three of whom looked at her the way clerks everywhere look at petitioners: as weather, to be endured.

The fourth looked up and flinched.

He was young—younger than her, certainly—with ink stains halfway up both forearms like failed tattoos, and hair that appeared to have lost an argument with his own comb. He recognized her. Of course he did. He bought a headache charm from her every market day, two coppers, wrapped in blue paper, and every market day he said, "You're a lifesaver, doña," which was flattering and inaccurate, since what she sold him was mostly willow bark and suggestion.

"Master Elio," she said pleasantly, as though they were meeting at a fountain and not across the threshold of the building where the lists were kept.

"Doña—" He glanced left and right, lowered his voice to a whisper that still carried to the far wall. "You shouldn't be here."

"I need to see the registry."

"You *really* shouldn't be here."

"Elio." She set the basket on the counter. Onions rolled gently against each other, a small domestic percussion. "I brought you something."

His eyes went to the basket, then to her face, and something in him wavered the way a candle wavers—not extinguished, just undecided. "That's bribery," he whispered.

"It's *onions*. Bribery is when you offer something someone wants. This is offering something everyone needs and nobody wants to admit needing."

A sound escaped him that was almost a laugh, quickly strangled. The senior clerk, a woman built like a filing cabinet with spectacles, glared down the counter. Elio straightened, assumed an expression of bureaucratic hostility, and said loudly, "State your business," while quietly, under the noise of a cart passing outside, "Back room. Two minutes. Don't touch anything."

* * *

The back room was narrow as a coffin and twice as organized. Shelves climbed to the ceiling, stuffed with ledgers bound in leather gone soft and brown as old bread. A single window let in a blade of light thick with floating dust, and in that light Elio looked terrified and resolute, a boy standing guard over a fortress made of paper.

"The Edict lists went out this morning," he said. "Every district office got copies. Registration opens in six days." He swallowed. "After that, they hunt."

"I know."

"They're saying—" His voice dropped further, until she had to lean in, close enough to smell the ink and the nervous sweat beneath it. "They're saying there's a map. That somebody gave the crown names years ago. Whole families. Places where the gifted used to gather, before it was dangerous to say so."

Odile kept her face very still. "Rumors."

"Rumors with signatures." He turned to the shelf, ran his finger along the spines, and pulled down a ledger so heavy it landed on the table with a thump that made them both wince. "This is the master registry. Everything the crown knows about gifted persons in Vellamare and the coast districts. It's supposed to be sealed until registration day."

"But it isn't."

"It is *extremely* sealed." He was already opening it. "I'm the one who seals things. It's most of my job. Sealing, stamping, initialing. I have a stamp. I love my stamp." He said this the way other men describe their children.

"Why are you showing me?"

Elio stopped. His hand rested flat on the open page, and for a moment he looked much older than his ink-stained arms suggested. "Because last week I bought a charm from you that let me sleep through the night for the first time since my brother drowned. You charged me one copper and told me it was leftover stock." He looked at her. "It wasn't leftover stock. I know what those cost you to make. So." He shrugged, awkward, defiant. "Some debts you pay forward before they come due."

The ledger lay open between them. Columns of names in a clerk's careful hand—dates of registration, districts, notes in margins. Page after page. Odile felt her heart begin its slow climb toward her throat.

"What am I looking for?" she asked, though she already knew, in the way you know a storm is coming from the taste of the air.

"Yourself." Elio's voice was gentle. "Doña. I've known for two years who you are. Not your real name—I'm not a fool, and neither are you—but I know a hedge-witch's hands when I see them. When the Edict was announced yesterday, I started looking. And I found you." He turned the pages backward, seven years' worth, his finger walking down columns of strangers. "Except here's the thing that's been sitting in my stomach like a bad oyster since dawn. Your name wasn't added last week. It wasn't added when the Edict was drafted. It was entered *years* ago. Before anyone was hunting anybody."

The blade of light shifted. Dust swam.

"Show me," Odile said.

He turned one more page, and there it was.

**QUINTANA, ODILE M.**

Her mother's surname. Her own first name, spelled correctly, which somehow hurt worse than a misspelling would have—a stranger's carelessness she could have dismissed, but this was precise. This was *known*. Below the name, in the cramped notation of record-keepers: *Coastal district. Hedge-craft, minor charms. Apprentice registered under household of R. Villalobos.*

And beneath that, the signature.

She knew it the way you know your own reflection in a dark window—the slight forward lean of the letters, the way the R hooked like a gull's foot, the little flourish after the final syllable that Rafael called "a signature should bow, Odile, it is how the paper knows you mean it." She had watched him sign hundreds of documents across that desk in the tower room, had practiced copying it secretly in the margins of her primers because everything he did seemed worth learning, even his handwriting.

*Especially* his handwriting. She had loved his handwriting.

Seven years ago. The date sat beside the entry like a headstone. Seven years ago she had been seventeen, grinding herbs in his kitchen, believing herself safe inside the walls of the only person in the world who understood what she was. Seven years ago, while she slept upstairs, he had walked—or sent—a piece of paper down the mountain with her true name on it, and given it to the crown like a man settling an account.

"He sold me," she said. Her voice came out level, which surprised her. Inside, something enormous was falling, silently, the way trees fall in forests no one visits.

Elio winced. "There's more. Look at the margin."

Margin notes, in Rafael's hand, dated across years:

*Subject cooperative. Low flight risk.*
*Retained for instruction; value exceeds immediate utility.*
*Defer collection. Asset productive under current arrangement.*

*Asset.* She read the word three times. It did not improve with familiarity. Somewhere in the building above them a door slammed, and she flinched hard enough that Elio reached out as if to steady her, then thought better of it.

"Collection," she repeated. "He wrote *collection*. Like I'm a debt owed."

"Aren't you?" The words came out before she could stop them, bitter and old, the voice of a girl taught that her gift was not a birthright but a bill. She pressed her palm flat against the page, over her own name, as if she could smother it. "Seven years I've been running from hunters I thought were random. Bad luck. The world being the world." She laughed, and it came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "It wasn't the world. It was a map. He drew them a map, Elio. Every village I've skipped, every market I've avoided, every friend I never let myself make—he knew where I'd go, because he's the one who taught me how to hide."

The senior clerk's footsteps sounded in the hall, unhurried, terrible. Elio snapped the ledger shut with a crack like a bone breaking.

"You can't be here when she does her rounds," he whispered. "Doña—Odile—listen to me. Six days. After that, your name goes on the active list, and the active list goes to Captain Ferro, and Ferro doesn't read margin notes, he reads *targets*." He shoved the ledger back onto its shelf, aligning its spine with a precision that was clearly a comfort to him. "There are roads out. River traffic, mostly. Smugglers, mostly. Expensive, mostly."

"How expensive?"

"For you?" He almost smiled. "One copper. Apparently that's the going rate for saving my life." Then the smile fell away, and he gripped her arm, quick and fierce. "Go tonight. Not tomorrow. Tonight. And Odile—" He hesitated, and she saw him choose honesty over comfort, which she would remember about him for the rest of her life. "Whoever signed that page. Whoever wrote *asset*. People like that don't sell you once. They sell you every time it's useful. Whatever you think you owe him, whatever he taught you—you don't owe him your name."

The footsteps passed. The dust settled. Outside, distantly, a crier was calling the hour, and beneath it, fainter, another voice calling the Edict's terms to a street that had gone very quiet.

Odile picked up her basket of onions. They weighed nothing at all.

"Six days," she said.

"Five and a half," said Elio, "if Ferro's riders are fast, and they're always fast."

She walked out through the Hall of Records, past the counters and the cabinets and the three clerks who looked through her like weather, carrying her name in her body like a splinter working toward the heart. Seven years of careful invisibility, of charms too small to notice, of a life built like a sandbar—and all along, the tide that would take it had been charted by the hand that taught her to swim.

By nightfall, she told herself. By nightfall she would be gone.

She was almost to the door when she realized she was already deciding what to pack, and that the first thing her mind had chosen, without asking her, was the old charm in blue paper that she didn't remember making—the one addressed, in her own younger handwriting, to herself.

* * *

The gray market was closing when she crossed it, awnings folding like tired wings, fishwives hosing blood off their tables. Old Sefa was counting her day's coins into a kerchief, lips moving, and Odile stopped beside her stall without quite meaning to.

"You're leaving," Sefa said. Not a question. Sefa had sold dried cod in Vellamare for forty years and could smell departure the way sharks smell it.

"I might travel."

"Mm." Sefa tied the kerchief, looked up. Her eyes were pale and shrewd and kind. "My grandmother used to say, when the mountain starts throwing rocks, don't stand around admiring the view." She pressed something into Odile's hand—a twist of paper, warm from her pocket. Inside, two coppers. "For luck. Don't argue, it's bad manners."

Odile wanted to say a great many things—that she couldn't take it, that luck was her trade and she knew its wholesale price, that kindness in a cracking world was the heaviest thing a body could carry. What she said was, "Thank you," and meant it so thoroughly that Sefa nodded once, satisfied, and went back to her counting.

Odile walked home the long way, past the aqueduct's dark arches, listening to the water move underneath the city. Running water, her mistress used to say. The one thing they can't write down.

Five and a half days, Elio had said.

She intended to use one of them.
