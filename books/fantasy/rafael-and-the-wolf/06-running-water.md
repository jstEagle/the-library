## 6. Running Water

The aqueduct left Vellamare the way secrets did: quietly, at an unglamorous hour, through a gap in the wall that everyone pretended not to see.

Odile climbed down into its shadow with her shoes in one hand and her heart somewhere around her knees. The stone was cold and slick with moss, and below her the channel ran shallow and quick, carrying snowmelt from the hills out to the harbor. Above, the arches marched away into the dark like a line of patient giants, each one holding up a piece of the city's forgetting.

She had lived under this aqueduct for seven years. She had bought bread from a baker whose stall sat in its shade. She had never once looked up and thought, *that's a road*. That was the trouble with hiding, she was learning. You got so good at not seeing things that you stopped seeing the exits too.

*Left at the third arch,* the clerk had whispered, pressing a scrap of paper into her palm as if it burned him. *Follow the water. There's a woman at the weir. Tell her the rain owes you.*

The rain owed her. Well. It was as good a password as any, and better than most, because at least it had the virtue of being true. Odile had spent her whole life in debt to weather of one kind or another.

She put her shoes back on, tied the laces with fingers that would not entirely stop shaking, and began to walk.

* * *

The city fell behind her by degrees. First the noise went — the criers had gone home hours ago, though their proclamations still hung in the air like smoke, *register or be hunted, register or be hunted*, painted on every wall in letters the size of doors. Then the light went, lamp by lamp, until there was only the moon doing its pale best over the hills. Last to go was the smell: salt and tar and frying fish, the smell she had told herself for seven years was home.

It should have been a relief, that smell fading. Instead it sat in her chest like a stone she'd swallowed.

"Stop that," she told herself sternly, in the voice she used on customers who haggled over charms that cost less than their hats. "You're alive. Alive is the whole assignment."

But her feet kept doing arithmetic without her permission. Seven years of small kindnesses, left behind. The widow Marta and her luck-charm. The boy who sold chestnuts and whose cough she'd quieted with a charm stitched into his scarf. Tomas, who owed her money and whose face, when she'd last seen it across the market square, had been doing something complicated that she refused to interpret.

She had left a charm on his doorstep anyway. Not a luck-charm. A finding-charm, keyed to nothing, which meant it would find whatever he needed most. She didn't let herself think about what that might be. Some gifts you gave blindfolded, so you couldn't watch them land.

The channel narrowed. The arches grew older, their stones furred with ivy, and here and there the aqueduct's old carvings showed through the moss — faces, mostly, worn to suggestions of faces, river-spirits from before anyone decided the river had no spirit worth naming. Odile trailed her fingers over one as she passed. Its mouth had eroded into a small permanent smile.

"You find this funny," she said to it.

The stone said nothing, but the water chuckled over the stones, and she chose to take that as agreement.

* * *

An hour out, maybe two — time went strange when you were walking away from everything you knew — she heard the dogs.

Not close. Not yet. But behind her, back toward the city, a chorus of baying rose and fell, and every hair on her arms stood up and pointed home like compass needles. Somewhere back there, hunters were following a map drawn by the man who taught her everything she knew about hiding. Every hedge she'd ever slept under, every cellar she'd ever charmed against damp, every name she'd ever trusted — he'd had seven years to write them all down.

"He sold me," she said aloud, to make it real, because saying it made it worse and worse was at least a shape you could fight. "He wrote my name down and sold me."

Her voice echoed off the arches and came back sounding smaller than she felt, which she hadn't thought possible.

She walked faster.

The trouble with walking fast along an aqueduct at night is that the aqueduct has opinions about your footing. Twice she slipped on wet stone and caught herself on the parapet; the second time her palm came away green with crushed ivy, and she stood there breathing hard, absurdly comforted by the smell of it. Green things. Growing things. Things that did not care what the crown decreed.

*Keep moving.* Her dead mistress's voice, or her memory of it, which amounted to the same thing now. *Grief is a fine spice and a terrible meal.*

"I'm not grieving," Odile muttered. "I'm fleeing. It's different. Grieving has more crying."

She walked on. The dogs faded behind her, or she outpaced them, or they turned after some other poor soul, and she hated herself a little for the flicker of relief in that last possibility, and kept walking.

* * *

Dawn was still only a rumor when she smelled woodsmoke and heard, unmistakably, someone singing.

Badly.

The song drifted up from where the aqueduct met the weir — a low stone lip where the channel spilled into a wider pool before continuing on toward the hills. It was a smuggler's song, or half of one; the singer knew perhaps four lines of it and appeared to be inventing the rest, cheerfully, in no known key.

"*...and the customs man said, what's in the barrel, / and I said, sir, it's pickled eels, / and the barrel said—*" here the singer paused, apparently stuck, "*—something. Something rude. And that's why we don't fish on Tuesdays.*"

Odile crouched behind the parapet and considered.

The clerk had said *a woman at the weir*. What crouched at the weir's edge, gutting a fish by lantern-light, was indeed a woman, in the way that a storm is a kind of weather. She was young — younger than Odile, surely not past twenty — with a coil of black hair escaping its knot and hands that moved through the gutting with the bored precision of someone who could do it asleep. Beside her lay a flat-bottomed boat, long and narrow and ugly as a Monday, riding low in the pool with cargo hidden under oilcloth.

A boat. On the river. Going away from Vellamare.

Odile's whole body leaned toward it like a plant toward light.

*Tell her the rain owes you.*

She stood up out of cover, because crouching any longer would turn her from a fugitive into a stalker, and said, "The rain owes me."

The girl did not startle. That was the first remarkable thing. Her knife paused mid-stroke, her eyes came up — sharp, dark, unhurried — and she looked Odile over the way a horse trader looks at a lame mare: thoroughly, and without much hope.

"Does it," she said. "And what does the rain owe you, exactly?"

The password had not come with instructions for follow-up questions. Odile improvised. "Seven years of it," she said. "I hid indoors the whole time. It can owe me a boat ride."

Something shifted at the corner of the girl's mouth. Not quite a smile. An opening where a smile might later be installed.

"Huh," she said. "The clerk said you'd be funny." She jerked her chin at the boat. "Brisa."

"Odile."

"Nope." Brisa went back to her fish. "Don't tell me that. I just told you mine because I'm careless and my mother despaired of me. Your name stays yours until we're past the ford, and then it stays yours anyway, because names are how people get found, and I have a professional policy against being findable." She waved the knife at the space beside her. "Sit. Eat. We leave when the lantern burns down, not before, because the river guards sleep worst in the hour before dawn and I intend to insult them by slipping past while they snore."

Odile sat. The fish, when Brisa tossed her a piece, was fresh and salted and the best thing she had eaten in a week, possibly a year. Fear, she discovered, was an excellent seasoning.

They ate in silence for a while. The lantern hissed. The water talked to itself over the stones.

"You're the one from the registry," Brisa said at last, conversationally, eyes on the water. "The one the whole river's humming about. Clerk emptied his desk getting you out. Man looked like he'd aged a year overnight."

"I'm the one from the registry," Odile agreed quietly.

"They say your name's been in the book seven years." Brisa's knife turned over in her fingers, once. "That somebody signed it a long time ago and everybody's only now reading the page."

Odile's stomach, which had been enjoying the fish, reconsidered. "That's the story."

"Cruel thing, a signature like that." Brisa said it lightly, but her eyes weren't light at all. "Worse than a knife. A knife you can dodge." She flicked the offal into the water, where the river accepted it without complaint. "Well. My boat doesn't ask questions and neither do I. That's the arrangement. You sit where I put you, you touch nothing that's under oilcloth, and if we're boarded you're my cousin from upriver with a bad memory and worse teeth."

"I have excellent teeth."

"Not tonight you don't. Tonight you've got scurvy and a tragic history. Boarded boats go smoother with a tragic history — makes the guards feel noble instead of greedy." Brisа stood, stretched until something in her spine popped, and began untying the mooring line. "Tragic histories are practically currency on this river. I keep three myself. Rotating."

Despite everything — the dogs, the ledger, the signature burning behind her eyes like a brand — Odile felt the laugh escape before she could catch it. It came out cracked and rusty, a door opening in a house long shut, but it came.

"There she is," Brisa said, satisfied. "Knew the clerk wouldn't send me a moper. Mopers weigh the boat down."

* * *

They launched in the gray hour, when the world is undecided about whether to exist.

The boat slid over the weir's lip and onto the wide dark river, and Vellamare dropped away behind them — the walls, the towers, the criers' paint, the whole loud machinery of the hunt — and Odile watched it shrink and could not decide what she felt. Grief, probably. Relief wearing grief's coat. Exile, certainly, though exile is a word for later; in the moment it was mostly just cold air on her face and the particular loneliness of moving water.

She had expected freedom to taste like something. Triumph, maybe. Or at least like bread after fasting.

Mostly it tasted like morning. Cold, faintly of fish, and of everything she'd left on the far bank.

"First run?" Brisa asked, poling them out into the current.

"First anything."

"Mm." Brisa shipped the pole and took up the oars, rowing with lazy economy, letting the river do most of the work. "Everyone thinks the running is the hard part. The running's easy. Running's just fear with legs." She nodded upstream, where the hills were assembling themselves out of the dark, ridge by ridge, like an audience taking their seats. "The hard part's tomorrow, and the day after, when you wake up safe and realize safe isn't a place you know how to live anymore. That's when people either break or become somebody new." She glanced sideways. "No pressure."

"None taken," Odile said faintly.

The river carried them between reed banks and drowned willows. Once, a heron lifted off the water ahead of them, enormous and offended, and Brisa saluted it with two fingers. Once, far behind, a bell began to ring in Vellamare — not the alarm bells, Odile noted, something slower, sadder, a funeral cadence — and both of them went quiet until it faded.

"Somebody didn't register in time," Brisa said finally.

"Yes."

"That'll be most mornings now, I expect. Bells." Brisa rowed on. "Country's going to be nothing but bells and empty houses by winter."

Odile looked at her own hands in the gray light. Ordinary hands. Charm-maker's hands, scarred at the knuckles from needle-work, currently doing nothing at all. For seven years she had kept them closed, kept the gift folded up small inside her like a letter never sent, because her mistress had called it a debt and debts frightened her, and because Rafael had taught her that power noticed was power hunted.

Rafael, who had written her name down while teaching her to hide it.

Her hands curled shut again. Old habit. But underneath the habit, for the first time, something else stirred — not quite anger yet, anger's seed, the thing anger grows from. The feeling of a bill coming due.

"The clerk said you'd want to go upstream," Brisa said. "Past the renamed villages, past the defaced shrines, all the way to the hill country nobody maps anymore." Her oars dipped and rose, dipped and rose. "There's nothing up there, officially. Officially it's all blank space and warnings."

"And unofficially?"

Brisa smiled properly this time, and it changed her whole face, and Odile understood suddenly why smugglers survived: not because they were hard, but because they were delighted.

"Unofficially," Brisa said, "there's a bell-keeper who lives alone with his dead, and he knows about a door."

The river ran on. The hills waited. And Odile Quintana, fugitive, orphan-of-a-sort, betrayed apprentice, holder of one set of excellent teeth and a tragic history she hadn't even had to invent, settled back against the oilcloth and let the current take her toward the blank spaces on the map.

Behind them, faint and fading, Vellamare rang its bells for the lost.

Ahead, in the country that wasn't, something was waiting to learn whether she'd ring one back.
