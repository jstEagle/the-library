## 7. The Country That Isn't

Brisa had a rule about passengers: she didn't take them, and if she absolutely had to, she didn't learn their names.

Names were how you got attached. Attached was how you got stupid. Stupid was how you ended up in the bottom of a net bag at the bottom of the Vellamare customs barge, explaining to a man with cold hands why your boat smelled of lamp oil and secrets instead of fish.

So when the woman in the gray shawl had come down the aqueduct steps in the dark with a bundle on her back and fear coming off her like heat off a kiln, Brisa had done the correct thing. She'd said nothing, taken her coin, and pushed off into the current before the moon finished clearing the hills.

She still hadn't asked for a name. It had been three days. The woman slept in the bow under an oilcloth like a folded wing, ate what Brisa gave her without complaint, and had a way of touching the gunwale when the water got rough — two fingers, quick, almost a pat, as if reassuring the boat itself — that Brisa was trying very hard not to find charming.

"You're steering us into the reeds," the woman said now, from behind her.

"I'm steering us *through* the reeds," Brisa said. "There's a difference. The difference is the patrol skiffs don't fit."

The reed channel was barely wider than the boat. Green walls rose on either side, taller than a standing man, whispering against each other overhead so that they traveled through a kind of green corridor, a secret hallway stitched into the middle of the country. Brisa loved this stretch. She called it the Needle, and she threaded it twice a year, and every time she did she felt like the one true thing left in Vellamare: small, quick, and going where she pleased.

The woman — she'd finally surrendered to calling her *the charm-seller* in her head, because you had to call people something — sat up and looked around with an expression Brisa couldn't read.

"Who cut this channel?"

"Nobody. It's old." Brisa nudged the tiller with her hip. "Older than the tax rolls, my grandmother said. Some say there was a witch who kept it open. Pushed the reeds apart every spring so the river folk could move their goods without paying the bridge tolls at Ferren's Crossing." She snorted. "Bridge tolls. As if the Ferrens ever built a bridge in their lives. They just stood where a bridge would go and charged you for the idea."

"And do the reeds still part for witches?"

Brisa glanced back. The charm-seller's face was unreadable in the dappled light, but her hands were busy in her lap, folding and unfolding a scrap of cloth.

"The reeds don't care who you are," Brisa said. "That's the nice thing about reeds."

It wasn't entirely true, and she knew it wasn't, because half a mile on, where the Needle spat them out into open water, they came upon the shrine — or what was left of one.
