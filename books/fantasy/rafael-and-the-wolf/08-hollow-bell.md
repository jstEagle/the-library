## 8. Hollow Bell

The rain found them an hour after dark, and by then Odile's hands had gone from cold to a kind of distant numbness she distrusted. Brisa poled the little boat into the reeds with the ease of someone who had been born knowing how to lie low on water.

"There," Brisa said, nodding at a light. Not a village light—those were all shuttered now, or burned—but a single yellow point on a hill above the river, steady as a held breath.

"That's not a farm."

"No," Brisa agreed. "Farms don't sit on hills like that. Farms are sensible." She shipped the pole and squinted through the drizzle. "That'll be the old bell tower. Half-ruined. There was a man."

"You know him?"

"I know *of* him. My mother knew of him. He rang warnings, back when there were warnings worth ringing." Brisa's mouth did something complicated—not quite a smile. "They say he still keeps one bell. For the dead."

Odile looked at the light for a long moment. Every instinct she'd sharpened over seven years said: lights mean people, people mean questions, questions mean names written down somewhere. But her fingers were numb, and Brisa's boat leaked in two places, and the reeds were already shivering with the cold coming down off the mountains.

"Well," she said. "The dead can hardly report us."

"That," said Brisa, "is the most sensible thing anyone's said to me all week."

* * *

The bell tower turned out to be less ruined than promised and more inhabited than either of them liked. A goat met them at the door—a large, gray, judgmental goat who looked at Odile the way tax collectors look at ledgers—and behind the goat stood a man holding a lantern so old its glass had gone amber as honey.

He was tall and stooped at once, the way willows are, with a beard gone white in patches and hands like tree roots. His eyes went from Odile to Brisa to the boat-pole Brisa still carried like a weapon, and he sighed the sigh of a man whose evening had just acquired complications.

"Travelers," he said. "In the rain. At my door. In this year of all years." He stepped aside anyway. "Wipe your feet. The goat is called Duchess and she believes she owns the floor. Do not argue with her; you will lose."

Duchess butted Odile's knee as she passed, which Odile chose to interpret as a toll paid.

Inside, the tower opened up like a throat. The staircase spiraled along the wall, and above—where a roof should have been—the night sky sat directly on the brickwork, and hanging in that open crown of sky was the bell.

It was not large. That surprised her. She had expected something that could be heard across valleys, a voice for emergencies. This bell was modest, hand-span wide, bronze gone green at the lip, hung from a beam by a rope frayed soft with handling. It swung slightly in the wind, without ringing, like something asleep.

"You keep it under the sky," Odile said.

"Where else?" The man set his lantern on a table. "A bell for the dead should be able to hear the weather. They were fond of it, most of them." He looked at them properly then. "Ivo. Formerly bell-keeper of Saint Verany's, formerly warden of the warning-line, currently keeper of nothing much and one goat. You'll be hungry. Sit down before you argue about whether to trust me. Arguing takes energy you haven't got."

Brisa laughed despite herself, and Odile felt something in her chest unclench half a notch. It was a dangerous feeling. She let it happen anyway.

* * *

The meal was barley soup with wild garlic, bread hard enough to require commitment, and tea made from something piney that Ivo called "whatever the hill offers, which tonight is pine." He ate slowly, watching them eat fast, and asked no questions, which in Odile's experience was rarer than kindness and more valuable.

It was Brisa who broke first. She couldn't help it; she was young that way.

"They say you used to ring for the witches," she said. "The hedge-folk. Before the Edict. Before any of it."

Ivo set down his spoon. "I did."

"Why?"

"Because someone had to, and because I was good at it." He said it plainly, without pride, the way a man says *I hauled nets*. "There was a time—you're too young, both of you, and the world has worked hard to make sure you'd never learn it—when the gifted didn't hide. Not the way they hide now. They lived out where the roads thinned. Herb-healers, charm-menders, weather-readers, women who knew what a fever meant and men who knew when to plant. And they were scattered, see? Scattered folk are safe until they aren't. So there was a line of bells, up and down these hills. Twenty-three towers. When soldiers came, or tax-men with special orders, or a lord who'd decided his neighbor's grandmother was a hag—the nearest keeper rang. Three strokes for danger. Five for soldiers. Nine—" he paused, and refilled his cup though it wasn't empty, "—nine for *run now, take nothing, they're burning houses.*"

"And the bells passed it along," Odile said quietly. "Tower to tower."

"Faster than any rider. Faster than any rumor. A warning crossed forty miles in the time it took those soldiers to finish their supper." His eyes were on the bell above, black against the stars. "My teacher rang for thirty years and was never caught. I managed eleven."

"What happened?" Brisa asked, though her voice said she already knew.

"The same thing that always happens. Someone sold the line. One keeper, up near the pass—he took gold, or took fear, I never learned which, and it doesn't matter, because they weigh the same on a scale. He rang false warnings to lead the gifted into ambushes. Within a season, half the towers were ash and the other half were full of informers pretending to be friends." Ivo smiled, and it was the worst smile Odile had ever seen, because it was gentle. "You can't poison a river if nobody trusts it to drink. Trust was the whole mechanism. That's what they understood, whoever drew up the plan. You don't hunt witches. You hunt the *web*."

The fire popped. Duchess, sprawled by the hearth like a discarded rug, snored.

"So now," Ivo went on, lighter, deliberately lighter, "I keep one bell. Not for warnings—there's no one left to warn, and I'm too old to be rung for. I ring it for the dead. Once for each name, when I learn one. It's a short service these days." He rubbed his thumb along the table's grain. "Though lately it's gotten longer. Lately I need both hands to keep count."

Odile set down her cup. Her heart was doing something she didn't authorize.

"How do you learn the names?" she asked.

"People come through." He shrugged. "Smugglers. Refugees. Folk carrying grief like luggage they can't put down. They tell me who they lost, because telling is the only thing left to do with it. And I ring. And the wind takes it wherever wind takes things." He glanced at her, sharp under all that woolly mildness. "You've got names too. I can always tell. It's in how people hold their shoulders—like they're carrying a sack of stones they've stopped noticing the weight of."

Odile said nothing. But her arms, under her sleeves, felt suddenly warm.

* * *

Later, wrapped in blankets that smelled of sheep and rosemary, Odile climbed partway up the spiral stair because sleep wouldn't come and the bell kept drawing her eye. Ivo was already there, of course, sitting on a step with his tea, looking up at it.

"He talks to it," Brisa whispered from below, appalled and delighted. "The goat told me. Well—I'm guessing the goat told me. I've been projecting onto the goat."

"She's a very communicative animal," Odile murmured, and climbed another step.

"I heard you two conspiring," Ivo said without turning. "Come up, then. The bell doesn't mind company. It minds silence, but it's learned to live with that."

Odile settled beside him. Up here the rain had stopped and the sky was doing that enormous cold glittering thing that only happens far from cities. The bell hung between them and the stars, patient as a held question.

"Ivo," she said. "Brisa mentioned—on the river, she heard talk. Among smugglers. About a way out. A crossing. They called it—"

"The Hollow Door." He said it without surprise, which told her everything about how many desperate people had sat exactly where she sat. "Yes. I know the stories."

"Are they true?"

He was quiet long enough that she thought he might refuse. When he spoke, his voice had changed—dropped its host's warmth and picked up something older, careful, like a man handling a relic.

"When I was a boy, my teacher took me up the mountain to show me the boundary stones. The old ones, from before Vellamare was Vellamare. And she told me: every country draws itself twice. Once on paper, once in the ground. And sometimes the two drawings disagree." He tapped the stone step beneath them. "There are places where the map says *here ends the kingdom* and the ground says otherwise—or the reverse. Seams. Disagreements. Most of them close up when the kingdom redraws itself hard enough. But one—" his finger traced a line eastward, toward the high passes, "—one never closed, because it was never drawn. A crossing that isn't on any map because it was never *in* the country to begin with. The Door sits in the seam. It opens for—"

"For whom?" Odile asked, when he stopped.

Ivo looked at her then, really looked, the way he'd looked at the bell.

"For those who can say who they are," he said. "Not their name—names can be forged, bought, sold, entered into registries by men with clean handwriting." Something flickered across his face at that, some old anger banked but not dead. "The Door wants more than a name. It wants the ones you carry. Everyone you lost, everyone who couldn't come, everyone whose name lives nowhere else now except in you. You stand at the seam and you speak them. All of them. And if you can—if you truly carry them, if they're not just words to you—the seam recognizes the weight, and it opens."

Odile's mouth had gone dry. "And if you can't?"

"Then it stays shut, and you've wasted a very dangerous journey." He sipped his tea. "Most people can't, you understand. Not because they loved too little. Because they never learned the names properly. We're taught to remember *events*—the raid, the fire, the arrest. Events are easy. Names are harder. A name means you knew them as a person, and knowing people is expensive."

The wind moved through the tower and the bell swayed, silent, and Odile thought of a charm in seven-year-old handwriting addressed to herself, hidden in a bundle she had burned—except that she hadn't burned that one, had she, she'd kept it, it was sewn into her coat lining right now, riding against her ribs like a second heartbeat.

"There's a price," she said. It wasn't a question. Hope in her experience always came itemized.

"There's always a price." Ivo set down his cup. "Here's this one. The road to the Door goes through the high passes, and the passes are watched—more every week. The crown has maps now, better ones, drawn by someone who knows the old roads *and* the new patrols. Whoever built that map knows this country the way I used to." His jaw tightened. "So the price is distance, and cold, and being hunted across ground chosen by your enemy. And the other price—" he hesitated, and she saw him decide to say it, "—the other price is that you have to *want* to arrive. Whole. Some folk climb that mountain carrying their dead like a debt they resent. The Door knows the difference between grief and baggage."

Below them, Brisa's voice floated up, drowsy and irreverent: "If the goat comes up these stairs I'm sleeping in the boat."

"Duchess respects the bell," Ivo called back serenely. "She has standards."

Odile almost laughed, and the laugh turned into something else halfway up her throat, and she was glad the dark hid her face.

"Ivo," she said. "The names. The ones people bring you. Do you—do you write them down?"

He was quiet again. Then he reached into his shirt and drew out a cord, knotted along its length with dozens upon dozens of small, tight knots, each one different, each one deliberate.

"Bell-rope," he said. "Old habit. A knot for a name. Rope remembers better than paper—paper burns, paper gets confiscated, paper gets *filed*." He wound the cord slowly around his fingers, and in the starlight Odile could see that some of the knots were worn smooth from touching. "Every person who ever told me a name is in here. And every year the rope gets longer and I get shorter, and someday somebody else will have to hold it."

He looked at her sideways then, and there was nothing woolly in his expression at all.

"You're going to ask me for the road," he said. "Tomorrow, probably, after you've slept on it and decided I'm trustworthy, which is flattering but unnecessary—I decided *you* were trustworthy the moment you apologized to my goat."

"I didn't apologize to your goat."

"You bowed to her. It's the same thing." He tucked the rope away. "Sleep, Odile-of-the-careful-hands. Tomorrow I'll tell you what I know of the passes, and you'll wish I hadn't, and then you'll go anyway. That's how it always goes."

She started down the stairs, three steps from sleep, when his voice stopped her—quiet, almost to himself, almost to the bell:

"One thing. The man who draws the crown's maps now. The advisor. Ferro carries his papers, but the hand is Rafael's—I've seen the annotations on a stolen copy." A pause, long enough for the wind to move the bell. "You know that name. Don't tell me how. But if you're climbing toward the Door, child, know that the man waiting at the top of your past may already be standing at the end of your road."

Above them, the bell stirred in the wind and did not ring, and Odile stood in the dark with her hand pressed flat against her coat, over the shape of a charm seven years old, and did not sleep for a very long time.
