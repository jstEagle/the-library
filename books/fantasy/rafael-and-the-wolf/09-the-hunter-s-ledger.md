## 9. The Hunter's Ledger

Captain Ferro believed in three things: the crown, the ledger, and the honest arithmetic of a well-kept list.

Everything else—the sermons about purity the priests shouted from the cathedral steps, the ballads the court poets had composed about cleansing fire, even his own wife's tearful certainty that witches had soured the milk in Vellamare for a generation—was decoration. Useful decoration. It moved men to march and neighbors to inform. But decoration was not policy, and policy was not results, and results lived in ledgers.

His ledger lived in a lacquered case that traveled with him everywhere, including, once memorably, into a bathhouse, where he had sat steaming among naked dockworkers with the case balanced on a stool like a modest guest. The dockworkers had assumed it held wine. Ferro had let them assume it. A man who understood what the ledger actually held would have drowned him in the cold plunge, and there were perhaps forty such men in the city, and Ferro kept their names on page one.

The ledger held, as of the morning this chapter concerns, four hundred and eleven names.

Each name had a column beside it. *Registered.* *Confirmed.* *Detained.* *Processed.* And a final column, narrow, ruled in red ink, which Ferro had labeled simply *Resolved*, because "dead" upset the clerks and "cleansed" upset him—it sounded like laundry, and he refused to launder anything.

He was reviewing the Resolved column at his desk in the customs house when Sergeant Dalla knocked, which meant she opened the door without waiting, because Dalla had never once in eleven years waited for permission to enter a room she'd been assigned to guard.

"Quota report," she said.

"Read it."

"Vellamare district, month three." She squinted at the paper. "Eleven registered, six detained, two resolved."

Ferro set down his pen. "Two?"

"Two."

"The quota is five."

"I know what the quota is, Captain. I wrote the quota on the board myself. In chalk. Large letters. I drew a little box around it."

"You draw a box around everything. It doesn't make the numbers obey you." He rubbed the bridge of his nose. "Who's shorting us? The harbor wardens again?"

"The harbor wardens are claiming the fog interferes with identification."

"Fog."

"Fog."

"It's the same fog that lets smugglers run brandy past them every night, I suppose. Convenient how fog only obstructs the things one doesn't wish to see." He made a note. "Dockhands?"

"Dockhands delivered. Fourteen names last week, nine of them confirmed gifted. Old Man Sarto alone gave us six—he's informing on half the fishing quarter over an inheritance dispute."

"Good old Sarto." Ferro said it without warmth, the way one might say *good weather* before a storm one has already decided to sail into. "Remind me to have him watched. Men who sell six neighbors in a week will eventually sell us, if the price is right, and I want to know the price before he finds it."

Dalla almost smiled. Almost. Dalla's smiles were rationed like wartime bread, and Ferro had learned not to waste them on jokes that were merely funny rather than true.

"And the shortfall?" she asked.

"Reassign the harbor wardens to gate duty. Gate duty is boring, and bored men count things properly. Pull two investigators off the shrine demolitions—" he paused, corrected himself, "—off the *renovation* of certain shrines, we're still calling it renovation until the paperwork catches up—and put them on door-to-door registration in the Tanner's Row district. Tanners keep dogs. Dogs bark at things that shouldn't be there. Half of hedge-craft is things that shouldn't be there."

He dictated; Dalla wrote. This was the machinery, and Ferro loved the machinery the way other men loved horses or mistresses—with a devotion that required no justification and tolerated no criticism. The Edict of Ash was, on its face, a howl: mobs, bonfires, priests screaming about abomination. But a howl accomplishes nothing. A *system* accomplishes everything. You did not hunt witches by hunting witches. You hunted them by making registration easier than hiding, by making betrayal cheaper than loyalty, by making the ordinary business of living—buying flour, renting a room, selling fish—slowly impossible for anyone whose name carried the wrong annotation.

No mob had ever caught a witch worth catching. Ledgers caught them. Ledgers caught them in their beds.

* * *

The maps arrived at midday, as they always did, wrapped in oilcloth and delivered by a boy who never spoke and never blinked, whom Ferro privately suspected of being either a mute or a golem, and had decided not to investigate, because some questions ruin a good working relationship.

He unwrapped them on the long table in the map room. Seven sheets, covering the river country east of Vellamare, each annotated in a fine, slanted hand that Ferro knew better than his own late father's.

Rafael's hand.

The advisor did not visit. Rafael sent maps. They arrived monthly, sometimes weekly when the season turned, and each one was a small miracle of terrible knowledge. Villages marked with dots. Dots circled in different inks—black for nothing found, blue for rumor, red for confirmed gift, and a fourth color, a green so dark it was nearly black, which appeared beside perhaps one dot in twenty and which no legend explained.

Ferro had asked about the green once, by letter. The reply had come within the week, in the same fine hand:

*Green means the place remembers. Burn the memory first. The people are easy; the remembering is hard.*

He had read it four times, then filed it under *Doctrine*, then gone home and drunk more wine than usual and kissed his sleeping children goodnight with unusual ferocity. That was the arrangement he had made with himself years ago: he was permitted to understand the work completely during daylight hours, and permitted to be a man after sundown. So far the arrangement held. So far.

Today's maps covered the upper river. He spread them and weighted the corners with inkwells, and began, as always, with the annotations.

A village called Osterhagen—no. He checked the margin. *Formerly Osterhagen. Renamed Solmere by edict 44-C.* Beside it, in blue: *shrine to the River Mother, active, offerings of bread and thread observed quarterly. Recommend renovation.*

A ford, marked in red: *crossing used by unregistered travelers. Post watchers at full moon.* Someone had already posted watchers; someone always had; Rafael's instructions arrived so far ahead of events that Ferro occasionally wondered whether the man was merely brilliant or whether brilliance was the polite word for something older and less comfortable.

And then, near the top of the third sheet, where the river narrowed into hills, a mark Ferro had never seen before.

It was not a dot. It was a small, careful symbol like a keyhole drawn sideways, and beneath it, in that fine slanted hand, a single word:

*Hollow.*

No instruction. No recommendation. No *renovate* or *watch* or *resolve*. Just the word, and the strange sideways keyhole, and—this was the part that kept Ferro standing over the map while the afternoon light crawled across the table—a small notation in the corner of the sheet, in fainter ink, as though written earlier and half-erased:

*If asked, this does not exist.*

Ferro stood there for a long time.

He was not a superstitious man, which in his profession was nearly a professional deformity. He had watched men walk into burning houses to retrieve ledgers and he had watched witches curse their captors with dying breaths and he had concluded, in both cases, that belief was a tool that cut whoever held it. But the word *Hollow* on that map produced in him a sensation he could only describe as the map looking back.

"If asked, this does not exist," he murmured. "Asked by whom?"

Dalla appeared in the doorway. "Courier from the capital, Captain. And—" her voice did something complicated, "—the clerk Pell is here. From the registry. Says it's urgent. Says it's about a name."

"Pell says everything is urgent. Last week it was urgent that his stool wobbled."

"This one might actually be."

* * *

Clerk Pell was a thin, damp young man with the permanent apologetic posture of someone raised by books, and he stood in Ferro's office clutching a registry folio against his chest like a shield.

"Captain. I want to state first that I've done nothing wrong."

"That's usually the first thing guilty men say."

"Yes, well." Pell swallowed. "I've also brought evidence, which innocent men rarely bother with. So you may weigh the two statements together."

Ferro gestured him to the chair. "Show me."

Pell opened the folio with the reverence of a priest handling relics. Inside lay the master registry of the Edict—the great book from which all the smaller lists were copied, the book whose entries were entered seven years ago, back when the Edict was a plan rather than a law, back when someone had walked the country quietly writing down names while everyone slept.

"Routine verification," Pell said. "We're cross-checking the old entries against current registrations. Errors, duplications, deaths. And I found—" he turned pages with damp fingers, "—this."

He rotated the folio. An entry, seven years old, in the careful administrative script of the original surveyors:

*Quintana, Odile. Gifted: charm-work, minor. Status: unlocated. Note: apprentice of R.*

And below it, in the margin, in a different hand entirely—a hand Ferro recognized, because he had spent the whole morning reading it on maps—a single word in that fine slanted script:

*Spare.*

"Spare," Ferro repeated.

"Spare," Pell agreed miserably. "Do you know what that means, Captain? Because I don't, and it's been keeping me up. Every other entry has a disposition. Detain. Watch. Resolve. This one just says *spare*, and it's in the advisor's own hand, and there's no follow-up, no cross-reference, nothing. Like someone wrote it and then—" Pell made a vague gesture, "—closed the door on it."

Ferro looked at the word for a long moment.

Seven years ago, Rafael had written down the name of his own apprentice, and then, instead of a sentence, had written mercy—or what passed for mercy in that fine slanted hand. *Spare.* And the girl had vanished, and the entry had gathered dust, and the machinery had rolled on over four hundred other names without ever circling back to ask why the advisor had spared one.

Until now.

"Why did it surface?" Ferro asked.

"Because of the market lists." Pell looked genuinely pained. "There's a pattern, Captain. Small charms turning up in the gray markets—luck-knots, salt-charms, the kind too minor to register on any sensing. They've been appearing for years, but the new analysis protocols flagged the handwriting consistency across vendors. Same maker, moving stalls, staying small. Deliberately small." He hesitated. "Someone who knows exactly how large a charm can be before it shows. That's not hedge-craft, Captain. That's training."

Training. Ferro thought of the apprentice of R., seven years hidden, threading luck-knots too small to notice, and felt the peculiar cold pleasure of a pattern completing itself—the sound a lock makes when the last tumbler drops.

"Where do the charms surface most?"

"Fishmarket, mostly. The widow Maren bought one last month. And—" Pell flipped to a copied vendor list, "—there's a cluster around the aqueduct road. Which is odd, because there's nothing out that way except water."

Water. Ferro pulled the third map sheet toward him, the one with the sideways keyhole and the word *Hollow* and the instruction that it did not exist. The aqueduct road ran along the river. The river ran east, into the hills, toward the mark that did not exist.

He sat very still.

Dalla, from the doorway, watching him with the expression of a woman who had served him eleven years and could read his silences like weather: "Captain?"

"Get me the fishmarket vendor rolls," Ferro said. "All of them, three years back. And pull the file on the widow Maren—gently, Dalla. If our charm-maker sold her a luck-knot, I want to know what the knot was *for* before anyone knocks on the widow's door. Luck for what? Luck against what?" He tapped the map, once, beside the word that did not exist. "People don't buy luck for no reason. They buy it when they're planning to need it."

Pell was gathering his folio with trembling care. "And the entry, Captain? The *Spare*? Should I... resolve it? Update the status?"

Ferro considered. Somewhere east of the city, a girl who had spent seven years being too small to notice was, at this very moment, becoming noticeable—and the one man in the kingdom who had chosen, seven years ago, to write *spare* beside her name was the same man whose maps told Ferro exactly where the roads ran and where they stopped.

"No," Ferro said slowly. "Leave the entry open. And send word to the capital—routine correspondence, nothing marked urgent. Tell the advisor that his old annotation has surfaced in verification, and ask whether he wishes to update the disposition himself."

Dalla raised an eyebrow. "You're asking *him*?"

"He wrote the word. He gets to explain it." Ferro returned his attention to the map, to the fine slanted hand, to the sideways keyhole in the hills where the river went quiet. "Besides. If the advisor answers quickly, we'll know she matters to him. And if he answers slowly—"

He did not finish the sentence. He didn't need to. Dalla had served him eleven years; she finished it herself, silently, on her way out the door.

*If he answers slowly, then whatever's in those hills matters more than she does. And then, Captain, we'll know exactly where to look.*

Outside, the fog was coming off the harbor, thick and convenient, obscuring precisely the things certain men wished obscured. Ferro drew the map closer, weighted its corners, and began, in his own neat hand, to plan a journey east.
