## 10. Rafael

Rafael Villalobos had always believed that a man's conscience was like a cellar: dark, useful, and best visited only when you needed something from it.

He kept his in excellent order.

The morning began, as his mornings did for six years now, with the ledger. Not the crown's ledger — the other one, the true one, bound in green leather gone soft as a cat's ear at the corners. He sat in the tower room the court had given him, high above the Ministry of Records, and read it while his tea went cold, because reading it was a kind of prayer and prayers are not meant to be comfortable.

*Alba Serrano, hedge-charmer, Millbrook. Registered 3rd of Thaw. Useful: yes. Fate: employed, Royal Infirmary.*

*Tomas Ilic, weather-sense, coastal. Registered 9th of Thaw. Useful: marginal. Fate: —*

He dipped his pen and wrote *relocated, northern garrisons*, which was a mercy, or would be once the garrisons stopped being northern, which they would, eventually, when the Edict settled into law the way silt settles into a riverbed. Everything settled, given time. That was the whole of his philosophy, and he had built a career on it.

Three hundred and eleven names. He knew them all. He could recite them the way other men recited their sins, which was fitting, since that was what they were — except that sins were things you did to yourself, and this had been done *for* them, every one, whether they understood it or not.

The knock came at seven strokes of the bell, soft, two knuckles, the way Captain Ferro knocked when he wanted to be heard and not seen.

"Enter."

Ferro came in smelling of rain and horse and something faintly scorched, which Rafael chose not to ask about. The captain was a good instrument: honest the way a blade is honest, sharp where sharpened, no opinions of its own beyond an admirable enthusiasm for the work. He set a folder on the desk.

"Vellamare report," Ferro said. "The gray market's been swept twice. We took a charm-seller Tuesday — old man, river-cures, nothing much."

"Name?"

"Doesn't matter. He wasn't on the list." Ferro hesitated, which he almost never did. "There's a woman. Small charms, luck-work, love-knots. Been selling under the name Odile Quintana for — well, the records say seven years, but the records say she arrived out of nowhere, which nobody does."

Rafael's pen stopped moving.

It was a small thing, the stopping. A hairline crack in porcelain. He covered it by reaching for his tea, which was cold, and drinking it anyway, because a man who flinched at cold tea would flinch at worse.

"Quintana," he said. "That's not a gifted name. That's a shopkeeper's name."

"The description matches someone, though. My informer says she works small — salt, thread, knots. Says she's careful. Says—" Ferro consulted his notes, and Rafael watched him do it and thought, distantly, how strange it was that after everything, the world could still ambush a man through a clerk's handwriting. "Says she has a way of making a charm feel like it knows you. Like it's been listening."

The tea sat in Rafael's stomach like a stone wearing a coat.

Seven years ago there had been a girl in his house who worked exactly like that. Who talked to her charms under her breath as though apologizing to them. Who salted windowsills not against spirits but against sadness, which was nonsense, and which had worked anyway, which was worse than nonsense.

"Odile," he said aloud, testing the word.

"You know her?" Ferro asked, and there it was — the eagerness, banked but glowing, the hunter's oldest hunger. A name on the list was paper. A name in the advisor's mouth was meat.

"I knew an apprentice," Rafael said carefully, "many years ago. She died. Fever, in the eastern provinces. It was one of the reasons I left private practice." He smiled, and it was a good smile; he'd practiced it on mirrors until it stopped being a lie and became simply a face he owned. "Grief makes poor company. I don't keep it anymore."

Ferro nodded, satisfied, and Rafael thought: *there*. That was how it was done. You didn't hide the truth; you buried it under a better story and let the ground settle over both.

But his hands, when Ferro left, were not steady, and he looked at them with something like betrayal. Traitors, hands. They remembered things the mind had filed away.

* * *

He went down to the archive that afternoon, which he told himself was routine, which it was, which was also a lie so smooth he nearly mistook it for the truth himself.

The registry hall smelled of dust and lamp oil and, beneath that, of fear — fear soaked into paper over years, the way smoke soaks into a house. Row upon row of folios. Every gifted soul in the nine provinces, entered in ink, annotated in his own hand. He walked the aisles slowly, trailing one finger along the spines, and let himself feel the thing he permitted himself to feel about it, which was pride.

People misunderstood him. He'd made peace with that too, mostly. They saw the Edict of Ash and thought cruelty, thought fire, thought some old man's hatred of the gift that had made him great. Fools. He loved the gift. He loved it the way a father loves a son who is ruining himself — fiercely, desperately, with a growing need to take the boy's knife away before he cuts his own throat.

Because that was what the gift was doing. Had been doing, for a century. Hedge-witches cursing neighbors over fence disputes. Charm-mongers selling hope to widows at three coppers a knot. Whole villages leaning on one clever girl until she burned out at thirty, hollowed, used up. And underneath it all, the slow tide: the crown's fear, the priests' sermons, the pogroms that came every generation like fever, each one worse, none of them final. He had watched the country work itself toward a slaughter and done the arithmetic no one else would do.

So he had gone to the chancellors with a map. His map. Every witch he'd ever known of, marked, graded, *annotated* — and the annotations were the mercy, whatever anyone said. *Useful.* *Harmless.* *Dangerous.* Three words standing between three hundred souls and the pyre. Registration was not persecution. Registration was a list of who to *spare*.

You cannot save everyone, he had told the girl once, in this very city, in another life. She had been seventeen and furious about some injustice — a neighbor's cow, a cheated widow, he couldn't recall; she'd been furious about injustices the way other girls were furious about suitors — and she had said, *then save everyone anyway, and fail honestly.*

He had laughed at her then. He thought of it now, standing among the folios, and did not laugh.

He found the registry for Vellamare's gray district and ran his finger down to the Q's.

*Quintana, Odile. Arrived Vellamare, spring, seven years past. Trade: minor charms. Assessment pending.*

Assessment pending. Seven years pending. Because he had written the entry himself, years ago, from memory and guilt, and then — he remembered this now with terrible clarity — he had never finished it. Never sent the assessors. Never flagged it. He had told himself it was oversight. An entry among hundreds. Paperwork drowned in paperwork.

Standing there under the lamps, Rafael admitted, in the cellar of himself, behind the shelves where he kept the things he visited rarely: he had left it blank because leaving it blank felt like leaving a door open. A door through which, someday, if she ever needed it, a girl might slip.

And now Ferro had found the door.

* * *

That evening he dined with the Chancellor of Interior, who was a fat, frightened, decent man, and who said, over the fish, "They say your former student may be alive. The one from the old days. The talented one."

"They say many things," Rafael said.

"If she surfaces, the captain will want her. Badly. She'd be a prize — the master's own apprentice, unregistered all these years. It would look very well in the reports." The chancellor dabbed his mouth. "Of course, if you vouched for her—"

"I vouched for no one," Rafael said, "because everyone on my list is accounted for, and the accounting is why we still have an infirmary full of gifted healers instead of ash. Sentiment is how hunts become massacres, Chancellor. You know this."

"Of course. Of course." The chancellor subsided, relieved to be told what to think, as most men were.

Rafael walked home through the wet streets afterward and found, to his irritation, that he could not stop seeing her. Not the woman she might be now — he had no picture for that — but the girl. Sixteen, arriving at his gate with nothing but a bundle and a name she wouldn't say aloud, as if the name were a coin she was saving. Seventeen, salting his windowsill against sadness. Eighteen, the night she left, standing in the doorway with her bundle packed again, saying, *I won't be a tool, teacher. Even a kind hand is still a hand around your throat.*

He had said something clever back. He always said something clever back. He could no longer remember what it was, only that she had looked at him the way you look at a house you've realized is haunted — not with hate, with grief — and walked out, and he had told himself for seven years that she was dead, because dead was simpler, and because dead required nothing of him.

Now she was alive. Now she was selling luck-charms in a gray market to fishermen's widows, small kindnesses at small prices, hiding, careful, *listening* — and somewhere below his ribs, in the cellar, something that was not quite joy and not quite dread turned over like a sleeper.

She was alive, and she was on no one's list but his, and his entry said *assessment pending*, and Captain Ferro had a nose like a hound and a folder with her trade-name in it.

There were moves available. He counted them the way he counted everything, dispassionately, laying them out like cards.

One: warn her. Impossible. He had no channel to her, and warning her meant admitting the hunt followed his map, and he was not prepared — he discovered this with mild surprise, examining the feeling like a specimen — he was not prepared to have that conversation, even alone with himself.

Two: lose the file. Misfile it, mark it assessed-and-clear, bury it. Possible. Dangerous. Ferro already had the name independently. Losing the file would not lose the woman; it would only remove Rafael's ability to control what happened to her.

Which led to three, and three was the move he had been circling all evening without letting himself land on it, the way you circle a cold pool before committing.

Control it. Bring her in himself. Before Ferro. Before the machinery. Sit her down across a table — no, not a table, a table was an interrogation, a hearth was a conversation — sit her down and explain. She was clever. She had always been clever, clever past the point of wisdom, but she would see it eventually, she must: the list was a shield, not a sword. Three hundred names alive because of him. The infirmary. The garrisons that would stop being northern. A country that would burn its witches in one great bonfire within the decade unless someone graded the wood first — and he had graded it, he had *saved* them, and if she would only understand, if she would come inside, register, take a place, be *useful*, then—

Then she would be safe.

The thought settled over him like a warm coat, and he stood in his doorway in the rain and let it, because it fit so well, because it had always fit, because a coat tailored over twenty years fits perfectly no matter what shape the man underneath has grown into.

*Save the useful ones,* he murmured to the rain. *Someone has to.*

The rain, which had listened to better justifications from worse men, said nothing.

* * *

He worked late. He always worked late. At midnight he drew a fresh sheet toward him and began, in his small precise hand, the annotation he should have written seven years ago.

*Quintana, Odile. Apprentice, private practice, four years. Gift: exceptional, sympathetic, self-effacing. Assessment—*

His pen hovered.

*Useful*, said the part of him that had built the list, the part that had looked at a nation of hedge-witches and seen timber to be sorted. *Harmless*, said a smaller, older voice, the one that remembered a girl salting a windowsill against sadness and laughing at herself for it. *Dangerous*, whispered the smallest voice of all, barely a draft under the door, and it was not dangerous-meaning-threat. It meant: dangerous-because-she-was-right-that-night-in-the-doorway, dangerous-because-she-had-failed-honestly-when-he-had-never-dared-to-fail-at-all, dangerous-because-if-she-stood-in-this-room-and-looked-at-the-ledger-with-those-clear-unforgiving-eyes-he-did-not-know-what-would-be-left-of-him.

He wrote: *Assessment: mine.*

It was not a category the ministry recognized. He wrote it anyway, and felt, absurdly, like a boy again — like the boy who had found the gift in himself at nine years old and sworn, kneeling in a sheep field, that he would use it for everyone, that power was only real if it served.

Then he rang for the night clerk and gave him two instructions.

The first: the Quintana file was to be transferred to the advisor's personal custody, effective immediately, no copies.

The second: a message to Captain Ferro, sealed, official. *The name Quintana is under ministry review. No action without my authorization.*

Ferro would obey. Ferro always obeyed. But Rafael had spent a lifetime reading men the way he read charms, and he knew what lived under obedience in a hunter: appetite. Ferro wanted the prize. A seal would delay him. A seal would not stop him. And somewhere out there in the rain-soaked dark, a woman with salt on her fingertips and seven years of careful invisibility behind her was about to discover that the net had a shape, and that the shape was familiar.

Rafael sat back and looked at what he'd written. *Assessment: mine.*

"Well," he said to the empty room, in the voice he had once used to teach a furious girl her letters of art, warm and reasonable and utterly certain. "Let's see if you've finally learned to listen."

Far to the south, past the aqueducts and the renamed villages, a river ran toward mountains he had drawn on a map with his own hand — and marked, in ink the color of dried blood, with a single word he had never explained to anyone:

*Door.*
