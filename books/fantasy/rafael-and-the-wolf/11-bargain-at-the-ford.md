## 11. Bargain at the Ford

The river they followed out of Vellamare had a habit of changing its mind.

Brisa called it the Serpiente, though she admitted no map still living used that name. "Maps are just opinions with ink on them," she said, poling the flat-bottomed boat through a stretch of reeds. "And opinions get people hanged. So we go where the water goes and let the cartographers argue."

Odile sat in the stern with her knees drawn up, watching the banks slide past. Three days since the city. Three days of sleeping in the boat's bilge under a fish tarp that smelled like it had outlived several empires, eating hard bread and olives Brisa produced from nowhere, learning the smuggler's small liturgies: duck when you hear hooves, wave like an idiot when you see farmers—idiots are never arrested—and never, ever light a fire after dusk, because smoke is a confession.

"You're doing it again," Brisa said without turning around.

"Doing what?"

"The face. The one where you're chewing on a thought too big to swallow." She poled them around a fallen willow. "Whatever it is, eat it in smaller bites or spit it out. Big thoughts capsize boats."

"It's nothing."

"Nothing makes your left eyebrow do that thing. Your eyebrow is a terrible liar. Worse than you, and you're not good."

Odile smoothed her brow with two fingers, as if she could press the thought flat. It was the charm. Of course it was the charm—the one she'd pulled from her own burning stock, written in her own younger hand seven years ago, addressed to herself, its purpose still folded shut like a letter she wasn't ready to open. She'd carried it against her ribs ever since, and some nights she could swear it was warm, the way a stone stays warm after a hand leaves it.

"I was thinking about my old master," she said, which was true, and also not the whole truth, which was how she'd survived seven years in Vellamare.

"The one who sold your name?"

"The same."

"And you're thinking what? That he did it by accident? That there's a very reasonable explanation involving pigeons?"

"No," Odile said. "I'm thinking about why I ran from him in the first place, and whether I've been running toward something this whole time or just away."

Brisa considered this with the seriousness she gave to everything except danger. "Both, probably. Rivers work the same way. They're not trying to reach the sea. They're just falling downhill. The sea's a side effect." She grinned over her shoulder. "You'll figure out which kind of current you're in eventually. Preferably before the waterfall."

"There's a waterfall?"

"Not on this stretch. Probably."

* * *

They smelled the soldiers before they saw them—woodsmoke where there should have been no woodsmoke, and under it the particular sourness of men who have been marching for days and have stopped caring about the smell of themselves.

Brisa heard them first, because Brisa heard everything. She shipped the pole mid-stroke and held up one finger, and Odile went silent so fast her teeth clicked.

Hooves. Two, maybe three horses, upstream, moving parallel to the bank. And voices—not shouting, but that carrying casualness of patrols who know nobody dares run.

Brisa eased the boat into a stand of reeds thick enough to hide a cathedral and held it there with one hand on a root. Through the screen of stalks Odile could see the far bank: a meadow, a dirt road, and beyond it the glint of the ford—a wide, shallow place where the river spread itself thin over pale stones, the only crossing for miles. She knew it even without being told. Brisa had been steering toward it for two days. On the far side, the hills began in earnest, and somewhere beyond those hills was a bell-keeper named Ivo and, if half the stories were true, a Door.

The riders came into view. Three of them, gray cloaks, crown badges dulled with road dust. Not hunters proper—hunters wore black and traveled in packs—but conscripts, the king's ordinary soldiers given extraordinary orders. One of them dismounted at the ford and knelt, and even across the water Odile saw what he was looking at: tracks. Hoofprints and bootprints along the near bank, the leavings of other travelers who'd crossed here recently, refugees like them, and the soldier reading the mud the way a priest reads scripture.

"They're not looking for us specifically," Brisa breathed. "They're sweeping. Herding everyone toward the main roads where the real hunters wait." Her jaw worked. "We can't cross here now. They'll sit on that ford till sundown."

"So we go around."

"Around means two days north through the fens, and the fens mean leeches, lost roads, and at least one drowned village full of things I don't discuss before breakfast." Brisa chewed her lip. "Or we wait. They can't camp here forever. Patrols rotate back at dusk."

So they waited, which was its own special torture. The reeds itched. A heron judged them silently for an hour. Odile practiced being a stone, which was a skill her dead mistress had taught her in another life: *be small, be dull, be the kind of thing eyes slide off.* She was very good at it. She hated how good she was at it.

It was nearly noon when the second group arrived, and this time it was bad.

Six men on foot, driving three prisoners ahead of them on ropes. A family, Odile saw as they came down to the water—an older man, a woman, a boy of maybe twelve. The woman's sleeve had been torn back and there were fresh red marks on her forearm, the crude test-cuts the patrols made now, watching the skin for the wrong kind of healing. Odile's stomach turned over. She knew those marks. She'd spent seven years making sure her own arms told no stories.

At the ford, the mounted sergeant stood and exchanged words with the foot patrol's leader. Too far to hear clearly—snatches only, carried broken over the water. "...registry says three... boy's age matches..." And then, horribly casual, the sergeant gestured, and one of his men began unsaddling the horses to make camp. Right there. At the crossing. As if the universe had decided to be thorough about ruining their day.

"They're staying," Odile whispered.

"They're staying," Brisa agreed grimly. "And those poor devils—" She nodded at the roped family, kneeling now in the shallows while a soldier checked the boy's arms. "That's the quota walking. Ferro's people count heads like tax collectors."

"You know about Ferro?"

"Everyone knows about Ferro. Captain of the hunt. Man's got a ledger instead of a heart." Brisa's voice had gone flat, which Odile was learning meant she cared a great deal. "My cousin's name is in a ledger like that. Page forty-something. 'Processed.'" She spat, delicately, into the reeds.

The afternoon crawled. Odile watched the family through the reeds and did arithmetic she didn't want to do. If the soldiers took them back toward Vellamare—if they were processed, registered, marked—then the boy would grow up inside the Edict's machinery, and the woman's arms would carry more than test-cuts. There were charms for that. Small ones. Charms too minor to notice, the kind Odile had sold for coppers all these years, the kind that could numb rope-burn and quiet a cough and, if a person were clever and desperate, loosen a knot without touching it.

"Brisa," she said slowly. "How good are you at looking harmless?"

"I'm *excellent* at looking harmless. It's my single greatest talent, after poaching and modesty." Then she saw Odile's expression and her grin died. "No. Oh no. Whatever you're doing behind your eyebrows, stop doing it."

"If I distract them at the waterline, can you get the boy loose? Just the knot. You're better with knots than I am."

"With six armed men ten feet away? With *horses*?" Brisa stared at her. "You want us to rob the king's patrol of one (1) child, in broad daylight, at the one ford in twenty miles, while we sit in a boat that floats exactly as well as a coffin that changed its mind."

"When you say it like that it sounds foolish."

"I said it every way and it sounds foolish every way!" But Brisa was already looking at the soldiers, counting them, the way she counted everything—guards at checkpoints, coins in a purse, days of food left. Odile recognized the look. It was the look of someone who had already started saying yes and was furious about it.

* * *

The plan, such as it was, relied on three things: Odile's hands, Brisa's nerve, and the universal truth that soldiers get bored.

Boredom arrived on schedule, an hour past noon. Four of the six footmen drifted downstream to wash and smoke. The sergeant conferred with his mounted corporal over a map. Two men stayed with the prisoners, but even they had begun leaning on their spears, eyes on the river, dreaming of supper.

Odile slipped from the reeds into the shallows upstream, wading with her skirts hitched, letting herself be seen the way you let a cat be seen—unhurried, unremarkable, mildly confused. She'd dressed for it that morning: drab wool, no charms visible, hair bound in a widow's kerchief. A woman gathering watercress. Nothing.

"Hoy!" one of the bored sentries called, straightening. "You there! Woman!"

She turned with the perfect slowness of the slightly deaf. "Eh?"

"Come here! Come out where I can see you!"

She came, hands visible, basket swinging, wearing the expression of someone whose biggest worry was whether the cress would keep. Up close the soldiers were younger than they looked from the water—one of them couldn't have been nineteen, with a peach-fuzz mustache he clearly thought was working. Odile fixed her gaze on the middle distance and let her fear sink below the waterline where it belonged.

"Where from?" the sergeant demanded, striding over.

"Upstream. Mill lane." She pointed vaguely. There was no mill lane; there hadn't been a mill since the Edict burned the miller, but vague points were a currency soldiers accepted.

"Papers."

She patted her apron with theatrical confusion, then produced a travel pass she'd forged in the boat two nights ago—good enough for a bored conscript, bad enough to be believable. He squinted at it. Behind him, at the waterline, Brisa emerged from the reeds on the far side of the horse-line, bent double, playing drunk. She staggered toward the prisoners singing something tuneless and mournful, and the nearest guard turned, exasperated, human, distracted—

—and Odile moved.

Not her body. Her gift. It rose in her like warmth rising off a sunlit wall, and she let it flow down her arm and into the ground, into the water, into the small spaces between things. The guard holding the boy's rope felt his knot slip—knots she'd whispered loose a hundred times in a hundred kitchens. The horses shifted and stamped as a fly found each of them at once, though no flies flew in this cold. And in the sergeant's hand, the forged pass curled at its corner and smudged, drawing his eye down, buying seconds, buying seconds.

The boy felt the rope go slack. To his eternal credit, he didn't hesitate—he simply poured himself sideways into the reeds like water finding a crack, and Brisa's hand shot out of the greenery and caught his wrist, and then both of them were gone into the tall growth, quick as a fish turning.

It lasted four seconds. It lasted a lifetime.

"BOY'S GONE!" The shout went up like a crow scattering. The sergeant dropped the pass. Boots thundered. The young soldier drew his blade on Odile, hands shaking, and she raised her empty palms and let her face crumple into terrified incomprehension, the performance of her life, while inside she screamed at Brisa to *run, run, don't look back.*

They caught Brisa at the tree line.

Odile saw it happen as if through water: the girl breaking cover, the boy shoved ahead of her toward the willows, a soldier lunging, Brisa turning—turning *back*, fool, magnificent fool—to pull the boy free of a grabbing hand, and then three of them on her at once, and the boy wriggling free alone into the deep brush while Brisa went down under gray cloaks, laughing, actually laughing, spitting some final insult Odile couldn't hear over the blood in her ears.

"Run," Odile mouthed, to the boy, to herself, to no one. *Run.*

She ran.

* * *

She ran upstream along the bank until her lungs burned and the shouts faded behind her, and then she kept running anyway, because stopping meant feeling things. The river narrowed, quickened, threw itself over rocks. Dusk came down blue and cold. Somewhere in it she lost a shoe and didn't notice until much later.

She finally stopped in the lee of a fallen oak, shaking, soaked to the knee, and made herself take inventory the way her mistress had taught her: *What do you have? What do you need? What is true?*

She had: one shoe, one forged pass worth nothing now, a bundle with bread and olives, Ivo's direction memorized, and the old charm against her ribs, warm as a held hand.

She needed: to keep moving. The soldiers would sweep upstream by morning. Every hour mattered.

What was true: Brisa was in chains, or worse, and it was Odile's fault. Not the soldiers' fault, not the Edict's fault—*hers*. She had walked into that ford with a plan built out of kindness and arithmetic, and the sum had come out with a girl in ropes.

"She knew," Odile said aloud, to the oak, to the river. "She said yes. She chose it."

The river said nothing. Rivers were terrible comforters. They just kept falling downhill toward their side effects.

She thought about going back. She actually got three steps toward Vellamare before her legs staged a mutiny, and she stood there in the dark hating herself with a clean, bright hatred, because the honest truth was that her body wanted to live even when her heart wanted to be noble, and there was no charm for that. There was no charm for being the one who runs.

Then, faintly, threading through the sound of the water, she heard it: a voice, high and cracked, from downstream. A child's voice. Singing.

The boy. Lost in the dark, following the river, singing the tuneless thing Brisa had been singing in the reeds—her song, passed on like a coal from a dying fire—and Odile understood, with a lurch, that Brisa's last act hadn't failed at all. The boy was free. The boy was alive. And he was alone out here, twelve years old, with soldiers and night and a country eating itself, and every instinct Odile had spent seven years sharpening said *keep moving, don't be seen, survive*—

—and every other part of her was already turning downstream.

She found him an hour later, huddled in the roots of a willow, knees to chin. When her lantern-light touched him he flinched like a struck animal, and she did the only thing she could think of: she sat down in the mud at a respectful distance, held out the bundle of bread and olives, and said, "Your friend Brisa taught me that song. It's worse than a cat in a drain. Did she teach you the second verse, or did she mercifully run out of time?"

The boy stared at her. Then, wet-eyed, hiccuping, he laughed—one small, astonished laugh—and Odile felt something in her chest crack open and refuse to close.

"We're going north," she told him. "There's a man who keeps a bell for the dead, and past him, if the stories are true, a door. I can't promise anything except that we'll walk it together." She swallowed. "Her name was Brisa. We're going to carry it with us. Names are the one cargo they can't confiscate."

The boy wiped his nose and nodded, and took her hand.

Behind them, far downstream, a bell began to ring in some border town—alarm, pursuit, the machinery waking. Ahead, the hills waited, black against the stars, and somewhere past them a defrocked keeper and a hollow door and whatever the old charm against her ribs had been waiting seven years to say.

Odile picked up the boy's pack in one hand and stepped uphill, limping on one shoe, running toward the only thing left to run toward.
