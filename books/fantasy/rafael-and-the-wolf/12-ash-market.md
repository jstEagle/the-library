## 12. Ash Market

The camp had no name, which was the safest thing about it.

Odile came down off the ridge at dusk and saw the fires first — dozens of them, strung along the dry riverbed like a necklace someone had dropped in the dark. Then the smell: woodsmoke, wet wool, lentils burning on the bottom of pots because everyone was too tired to stir. Then the sound, which surprised her most. Not weeping. Arguing. A woman insisting her bread needed more salt; a boy swearing he'd seen a deer the size of a horse; two old men disputing, with real venom, whether the king's nose had always been that shape or only since the Edict.

Refugees argued the way other people prayed. It meant they still believed tomorrow would come and be worth complaining about.

She stood at the edge of the firelight longer than she should have, doing the arithmetic she'd done for seven years. One face among how many? Two hundred, maybe three hundred souls, packed into the bend of a dead river where no tax collector bothered to look. She could pass through unseen. Buy a heel of bread with the last of Brisa's coin — *Brisa's* coin, and don't think about that, not yet — sleep with her back against a stone, and be gone before dawn.

Or.

The word sat in her chest like a swallowed coal. *Or.* Because three days upstream from here, alone in a cave with nothing but an old charm for company, she had learned something about herself that could not be unlearned: the gift grew when it was spent. Hoarded, it starved. And she was so tired of starving.

A child found her before she decided anything. They always did. Children could smell a stranger the way dogs smelled weather, and this one — a girl of six or seven, hair cropped short against lice, cheeks sharp as knuckles — planted herself directly in Odile's path and stared up with the frank appraisal of a customs officer.

"You're new," the girl said.

"I'm passing through."

"Nobody passes through." The girl said it with terrible certainty. "The river's dead past the ford. The soldiers took the bridge at Marrow. You're stuck like everybody else."

"Then I'm stuck," Odile said, "like everybody else."

The girl considered this, found it acceptable, and held out her hand. In her palm lay a button, a bent nail, and a beetle that was either dead or very committed to its performance.

"I want to trade," the girl announced. "My brother snores. I want something to make him stop."

Odile crouched. Seven years of habit said *small charms, small kindnesses, never let them see your hands work.* But her hands were already moving, turning over the nail, and the coal in her chest warmed.

"A snoring charm," she said gravely. "That's serious work. What have you got?"

"The nail's good. Mam says good iron."

"The nail is excellent iron." She closed the girl's fingers around it. "Here's what you do. You put this under his pillow, flat side down, and you say to him, very quietly, *the river runs east, the river runs east.* Don't say it loud. If he hears you, it won't work."

"Why?"

"Because snoring is a man arguing with the river," Odile said, "and the river wins if you let it hear you doubt it."

The girl nodded slowly, absorbing this nonsense as revealed truth, and ran off clutching her nail like a scepter. Odile watched her go and felt something in her shoulders loosen by a degree she hadn't known was clenched.

*Well,* she thought. *That's done. That's one.*

She walked into the camp.

* * *

By morning she had a stall.

It wasn't a stall, really — a crate turned on its side, a scrap of canvas for shade, set up between a woman selling fishhooks and a man who claimed to sharpen anything but whose whetstone was, on close inspection, a brick. But word traveled through a refugee camp faster than fire through stubble, and by the time the sun cleared the ridge there were nine people waiting, and by noon there were more than she could count, and someone — she never learned who — had chalked a sign on her crate:

**CHARMS. HONEST PRICES. NO QUESTIONS ASKED OF YOU OR BY ME.**

She hadn't written it. She suspected the fishhook woman. It was exactly the sort of joke a fishhook woman would make.

And here was the thing that kept catching in her chest like a fishbone: nobody whispered. Nobody glanced over their shoulder. Nobody paid her in hushed tones or slid coins across as though buying contraband. A farmer's wife held out a sprig of rosemary and asked, loudly, for a charm against nightmares, and when Odile tied it with red thread and told her to hang it over the door, three other people in line leaned in to hear the instructions.

In Vellamare, this line would have been a pyre waiting for a match.

Here it was just — a line. People grumbled about the wait. Someone's goat ate somebody's cloak and there was a scene. An old man ahead of a young mother tried to insist she go first, and she refused, and they argued about courtesy for four minutes while Odile worked.

"What are you all doing?" she asked the fishhook woman during a lull, keeping her voice light, though her hands weren't steady. "Standing in the open like this. Chalking signs. Anyone could—"

"Anyone could what?" The woman — Marta, she was called, sixty-odd, forearms like dock rope — shrugged and began sorting hooks by size. "Report us? To who? Captain Ferro's men came through eleven days ago. Took the miller's son because somebody swore they saw him light a candle without flint." Her mouth went flat. "He was fourteen. He'd soaked the wick in oil as a trick, that's all it was, a boy's trick. They took him anyway, for the quota."

The line had gone quiet. Everyone was listening. Nobody pretended otherwise.

"So no," Marta went on, louder now, addressing the whole riverbed, "we can't run, half of us. Bad hips, bad lungs, babies. We can't fight — look at us. But we can stand in a line in broad daylight and buy a nightmare charm from a witch, and every soldier who hears about it has to wonder how many of us there are, and whether the next town's full of us too, and whether the reward's worth walking into a camp of three hundred people who've decided, all together, that we're done being afraid."

She looked back at Odile. "You're the first one who stopped running long enough to open shop. So. Open shop."

Odile's throat was tight. "I charge honest prices," she managed.

"See that you do. I've got hooks to sell and a reputation."

* * *

It was the third day, near sundown, when the pilgrim arrived.

He came down the ridge on a mule so old it appeared to be held together mainly by opinion, and he walked through the camp leaning on a staff, and people greeted him — not with the wariness strangers got, but with something closer to relief, the way a village greets rain after drought. He was perhaps fifty, gray-bearded, sunburnt, with the bowed legs of a lifelong walker. When he reached Odile's crate he didn't ask for a charm. He just looked at her hands — ink-stained, thread-marked, working — and smiled.

"You're the charm-witch," he said. "They told me on the ridge. Said there was a woman here selling openly, and I said either that's a lie or it's a miracle, and I've been wrong about miracles before."

"And which is it?"

"Ask me at supper." He lowered himself onto a rock with the groan of a man whose knees had opinions. "Name's Anselm. I walk. That's my whole profession. I walk from camp to camp, ford to ford, and I carry news, because news is the one cargo no soldier thinks to weigh."

Marta drifted over, pretending to restock hooks. Others gathered, casual as cats, and Odile understood that this was a scheduled event — the pilgrim's arrival was the camp's theater, its newspaper, its mass.

"Tell them the Door story," a boy demanded. "Tell the one about the sisters."

Anselm held up a hand, savoring it. "Two sisters," he said, "from the tobacco country, east of the Salt Hills. Wren and Sorrel. Hedge-talents both, small things — Wren could keep milk sweet, Sorrel could call moths out of the dark, useless and lovely. When the Edict came, they walked west. Everyone walks west eventually; the Door pulls like the tide." He paused. "You know of the Hollow Door?"

The camp had gone very still. Odile kept her hands busy with a knot of red thread and did not trust her voice.

"Only stories," someone said.

"All true ones start as stories." Anselm accepted a cup of barley tea from Marta without looking, the way kings accept crowns. "So. The sisters reached the Door in the autumn, in the cliffs above the old salt road. And here's the part I need you to hear properly, because everyone gets it wrong." He leaned forward. "The Door doesn't open for gold. Doesn't open for blood, or sacrifice, or any of the grim things the ballads say. The Door opens for names."

"Names?" the boy said, disappointed.

"The names of the lost." Anselm's voice dropped, and the whole camp leaned with it. "Everyone this country has erased. Every hedge-witch burned, every cunning-man driven into the sea, every shrine pulled down and every saint struck from the calendar and every village renamed until the old names died of embarrassment. The Door is a seam in the world, and the world sealed it shut with forgetting. So the only key is remembering. You stand before it and you speak the names — aloud, one by one, all of them you can carry — and for each name truly spoken, the seam widens a finger's width. Speak enough, and you can walk through. Speak none—" he spread his hands, "—and it's a cliff face like any other cliff face, and you die of disappointment or exposure, whichever comes kinder."

Silence. Somewhere a pot boiled over and nobody moved to save it.

"How many names?" Odile asked. Her voice came out rough.

"That's the cruel arithmetic." Anselm looked at her directly, and she felt seen straight through, ledger and all. "No one knows the number. The sisters spoke for a night and a day — everything they'd ever been taught, every name their grandmother kept in a song, every neighbor taken. And when dawn came, the seam was wide enough for one." His jaw worked. "One of them went through. The other stayed behind on the salt road, and the last anyone heard, she was walking back east, going door to door, teaching children the old names. Collecting them. So the next person to reach the Door would have more than she did."

He drank his tea. Let it land.

"That's the demand," he said. "Not courage. Arithmetic. The Door asks: how much did you carry? How many did you keep alive in your mouth? And most of us—" he gestured gently at the camp, at the fires, at all of them, "—most of us can't answer well. We came away with our own skins and our own griefs and nothing else. That's why I walk. I'm collecting names. I've got four hundred and eleven in my head, and I recite them every night like a rosary so I don't lose a single one." He smiled, tired and fierce. "Four hundred and twelve as of last week. A midwife outside Corvo. She's in my rosary now."

Nobody spoke. The boy had tears running freely down his face and made no effort to hide them, and neither did anyone else, and neither, Odile discovered, did she.

Because she was thinking of a ledger in a library she'd broken into, and the names she'd copied onto her own arms in a hand that shook — forty-one of them, forty-one witches Rafael had sold with annotations in his tidy script, and she had carried them west like contraband, like embers cupped against wind. Forty-one names. Enough to widen a seam by less than a handspan.

Not enough. Not nearly. But more than she'd had a month ago, and more than she'd had yesterday, and—

*Courage goes viral,* she thought wildly, watching Anselm pull a stub of charcoal from his pack and begin, right there, teaching the boy the first three names of his rosary while the boy repeated them back, solemn as ordination. *It spreads like measles. One person stands in a line in daylight, and then a camp does, and then a pilgrim carries it over a mountain, and somewhere a sister walks door to door filling children with names like seed grain.*

* * *

That night she couldn't sleep. She sat by her cold fire with her sleeves pushed up, reading her own arms by moonlight, lips moving through the forty-one. When she finished, she started over, because Anselm was right — it was a rosary, and rosaries only work if you never stop saying them.

Near midnight, footsteps. Anselm lowered himself onto the rock beside her, uninvited, comfortable, ancient.

"You knew some of those names already," he said. Not a question. "Before I ever told the story. I watched your face."

"My teacher sold me to the crown seven years ago," Odile said. It came out easily, which frightened her — how easily the worst fact of her life had become a fact, portable, sayable. "I found his ledger. I copied what I could."

"Forty-one."

"You counted."

"I count everything. It's my second profession." He was quiet a moment. "The woman who stayed behind on the salt road — Sorrel, the moth-caller — she has a saying. *A name spoken is a door opened; a name carried is a door carried.* She means that whoever holds the names holds the way out, for everyone, forever. There will always be another Door. There will always be another hunt. The question is only who remembers when it matters."

He looked at her arms, at the faint ink ghosts of forty-one names, and something in his expression shifted — recognition, almost, like a man finding a fellow officer behind enemy lines.

"I'm old," he said. "My feet are going. Another winter of these ridges and the ridges will win. Four hundred and twelve names is a heavy thing to bury with a man." He reached into his shirt and drew out a cord — plain leather, knotted along its length in clusters, dozens of clusters, each knot a name, each cluster a region, a life, a grave. "I had thought to give it to the Door itself, when I finally walked there. Speak them all and let the seam take me through or leave me dead on the salt road, either way, done."

"But?"

"But now I've met a charm-witch who sells in daylight and cries at stories and has forty-one names tattooed on her skin like a woman planning to outlive her own execution." He held out the cord. "Take it. Learn it. Add to it. And when you stand in front of that Door — and you will, girl, I've watched enough walkers to know one when I'm sitting next to her — speak mine too. All of them. Make my four hundred and twelve worth the carrying."

Odile didn't take it. Not yet. Her hands were in her lap and her heart was doing something complicated and painful, the way it had done when Brisa shoved the boat toward her and stayed behind on the bank, smiling, telling her to row.

Everyone kept handing her things. Names, ropes, chances. Everyone kept paying her passage forward, and the debt was getting so large she could feel it changing her, widening her, like a seam.

"If I take this," she said, "you'll teach me every knot tonight. All four hundred and twelve. I don't carry what I haven't earned."

Anselm grinned, and for a moment he looked twenty years younger, a boy on a road with his first name memorized.

"Girl," he said, settling in, cracking his knuckles like a scholar opening a book, "I hoped you'd say that. Now — pay attention, because the first one is a midwife from Corvo named Teodora, and she deserves to be said correctly."

They began.

And far to the north, though neither of them knew it yet, a captain was reading maps by lamplight, and a road was closing, and the mountains were learning her name.
