## 13. Ivo's Map

The river had opinions about her return, and all of them were bad.

Odile felt them through the soles of her boots as she climbed away from the water — a low grumble in the stones, the same wrongness she'd learned to read in charms that were about to fail. The Hollow Bell had rung three days ago. She had heard it from the reed shallows where Brisa's boat lay hidden under nets, and the sound had gone through her like a fishhook: one clean note, then silence, then nothing at all.

Bells rang for warnings. Bells rang for hours. A single note, followed by silence, was how you told a village someone had died.

She told herself it hadn't been Ivo. She told herself it was some other bell, some other keeper, some other old man with a crooked back and a tongue like a whetstone. She told herself this the whole two days it took to walk back up the valley, past the burned shrine at Marrow Ford, past the field where somebody had plowed a protective circle into the barley out of pure stubbornness and would probably be hanged for it, and every step of the way the telling did absolutely nothing, because she already knew.

You always knew. That was the worst part of the gift nobody put in songs. You always knew, and knowing changed nothing, and you went anyway.

The bell tower stood on its knoll above the drowned village of Saint-Aubry — or whatever name the maps gave it now; the maps kept changing their minds — leaning slightly eastward like an old man listening for gossip. Its shingles were the color of wet slate. Ivy had taken the north wall and showed no signs of negotiating. And the door, which Ivo had slammed in her face with theatrical offense when they'd first arrived ("No witches in my tower, madam, I keep a *clean* house"), stood open to the evening air.

Open doors meant either welcome or wolves. With Ivo it could honestly have been either.

"Ivo?" Her voice came out smaller than she wanted. She cleared her throat and tried again, louder, putting the fishwife into it. "Ivo! It's the witch you wouldn't let in! I've come to steal your spoons!"

Silence. Then, from somewhere above, a sound like a laugh being dragged uphill.

"Spoons," said the dark at the top of the stairs. "Seven years I keep the only honest bell in Vellamare, and my eulogy is going to be about *spoons*."

* * *

He was lying in the narrow bed under the bell chamber, propped on pillows gone flat as roadkill, and he looked — there was no kind way around it — he looked like a man who had been used up. His hands, which she remembered as quick and blunt and always busy with rope or oil or bread, lay quiet on the blanket like tools hung up for the last time. His face had gone fine-boned, the way faces do when the flesh finally stops arguing with the skull.

"You look terrible," Odile said, because he'd have been insulted by anything gentler.

"I look *honest*. There's a difference." He patted the stool beside the bed. "Sit. You're letting the cold in, standing there wringing your hands like a laundress."

She sat. Up close she could hear his breathing, a thin whistle on each draw, like wind finding a crack in a window frame. "How long?"

"Since winter, if we're being clinical. Since Tuesday, if we're being dramatic." He smiled, and it was still the same smile, crooked and unrepentant, the smile of a man who had spent forty years ringing warnings for hedge-witches and considered every hour of it time well wasted. "The bell rang itself, you know. Three nights back. I didn't touch the rope."

"Bells don't ring themselves."

"Mine does. I taught it to. Forty years of ringing for people who never thanked me — you think I couldn't teach a bell to say goodbye?" He coughed, wet and long, and she held the cup of water to his lips without being asked, and he drank without thanking her, which between the two of them was a form of courtesy. "It rang for me, girl. One note. I heard it from inside myself, if that makes any sense. Like hearing your own name called from another room."

"It makes sense," Odile said quietly. "More than I'd like."

"Mm. You've got the look of someone who's been carrying names." His eyes, still sharp as awls, went over her face, her arms, the ink-stained cuffs of her sleeves. "Brisa told me what happened at the ford before she ran your errand downstream. The soldier girl. Taken buying you time."

"She's alive. Ferro doesn't waste prisoners who can be made useful." Odile heard her own voice go flat and hard and didn't recognize it for a moment. "I'm going to get her back."

"With what army? Your charm stock? Your winning personality?" But there was no mockery in it, or rather there was, but it was the old kind, the kind that meant *I believe you, now let me make sure you mean it.* "Girl. Sit still a moment and stop planning revenge like it's a market stall you can set up by dawn. I have something for you, and I haven't got the night to watch you pretend you're not crying."

"I'm not—"

"You are. It's very becoming. Now listen."

* * *

He made her fetch the rope himself.

It hung beside the bell — the great cracked bronze bell, older than the village below it, older probably than Vellamare's claim to be a city at all — a bell-rope as thick as her wrist, braided from hemp and horsehair and, Ivo claimed, one strand of a hanged witch's hair, though he told that story differently every time and had once insisted the strand came from a bishop. Knots worked down its whole length. Dozens of them. Some simple, some fiendish, spaced along the rope with no pattern she could see.

"Take it down," he said. "Careful. It's not just rope anymore. It hasn't been since the day you left."

She lifted it from its peg and the weight of it surprised her — heavier than hemp should be, warm, almost, like something recently alive. When she carried it to the bed, the knots seemed to shift under her fingers, rearranging themselves the way a sleeping person shifts, and she nearly dropped it.

"What is this?"

"That," said Ivo, with the satisfaction of a man presenting a prize marrow at a harvest fair, "is a map."

She looked at him. She looked at the rope. She looked at him again.

"A map," she repeated.

"A map that can't be burned, can't be confiscated, can't be read by anyone who wasn't taught, and can't lie, because knots don't know how." He struggled up onto one elbow, wincing, waving off her help with the imperiousness of the very ill. "Every keeper before me added their knots. My master. His master. Back and back, girl, further than the Edict, further than the crown, back to when the Door was just a door and nobody thought to be ashamed of it. Each knot is a turning. A bend in the river. A cairn. A tree that fell and a tree that grew in its place. The spacing is the distance — a hand's width for a league, more or less, depending on how much the knotter liked walking. And the last knot—" his voice caught, and he covered it with a cough, badly, "—the last knot tells you how to knock."

Odile sat down slowly on the edge of the bed, the rope across her knees, its weight settling against her thighs like a sleeping cat. "Ivo. Why give this to me? You've known me a week."

"Nine days, and I counted, because I'm dying and it passes the time." He glared at her, but his eyes were bright and wet. "Because you came back. Brisa offered to take you downriver to the coast. Free passage, no questions, a new country by month's end. And you came *back*, upriver, alone, toward the hunt, because you heard a bell." He shook his head. "Forty years I rang warnings for the gifted. Do you know how many ever came back for me? Two. You're the second, and the first was my master, and he was coming back anyway because he'd forgotten his hat."

A laugh broke out of her, sudden and helpless, and turned halfway through into something else, and she pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes until it passed.

"There it is," Ivo said gently. "There's the girl under the fishwife. Took you long enough."

"The others," she managed. "The ones who couldn't come back. The ones the hunt found first. Someone has to ring for them too."

"And that," he said, "is why it's you."

* * *

He taught her the rope while the light died.

That was his way — no ceremony, no deathbed gravity, just instruction, brisk and profane, as if she were an apprentice bell-keeper and the lesson might run long and there was no reason to hurry. He made her hold each knot while he named it. The shepherd's loop at the top: the drowned chapel at Saint-Aubry, start counting from there. The double hitch: where the river splits around the black rock, take the left channel even though it looks wrong, especially because it looks wrong. A gnarled cluster of knots bunched together: the switchbacks above the ash market, count them twice, the knotter who made those had drunk half a bottle of plum brandy and refused to redo them on principle.

"This one's mine," he said, guiding her thumb to a knot midway down, tight and clean and ugly. "Tied it the night the Edict came. First thing I ever tied angry. You can tell — it's got no manners."

"It's a good knot."

"It's an *angry* knot. There's a difference. Good knots hold. Angry knots hold and resent it." He watched her fingers move along the cord. "You'll want to learn them by heart, not by eye. Eyes can be fooled. Fingers remember what the head forgets. Tie them blind, tie them in the dark, tie them while a soldier's asking you your name. Can you do that?"

"Yes."

"Liar. But you will." He settled back against the pillows, breath whistling. "Now. The last knot."

His hands shook when he reached for the end of the rope, so she brought it to him, and he held the final length of it — a span of cord maybe a foot long, still unknotted, waiting — and looked at it for a long time.

"Every keeper ties the last knot for the next keeper," he said. "My master tied his for me thirty years ago, and I thought, the old fool, I'll outlive the need for it. Then the Edict came, and I untied it and retied it four times, because the Door moves, see. Not far. A few feet, sometimes a few yards, whenever the country forgets itself a little harder. The knot has to be true to where it *is*, not where it was." He looked up at her. "I retied it a week ago. It's current. But I'll teach you the tying, not just the reading, because if you live long enough — and girl, I am ordering you to live long enough — someday you'll have to tie it for someone else."

"Ivo—"

"Hush. A hand's width for a league. The Door sits in the cliff above the Weeping Stair, where the stone runs wet year-round. Wet stone takes a tighter pull. So—" and his big ruined hands moved, slow but certain, looping and passing and drawing home, and the knot closed like a fist, like a promise, like a small dark door of its own. "Like that. Now you."

Her hands weren't shaking. She noticed that with distant surprise. Somewhere between the ford and this bed she had stopped shaking, and she didn't know when, and she suspected she'd pay for it later. She looped and passed and drew home, and the knot came out tight and true, and Ivo examined it with the critical squint of a master craftsman determined not to be impressed.

"Hm. Adequate."

"You're weeping."

"I'm *dying*. It's different. Everything leaks." He laughed, and the laugh cost him, and she took the rope back and held it and didn't know what to do with her hands, so she smoothed the frayed end of the cord over and over like soothing an animal.

"Ivo. What do I owe you?"

"Nothing. That's the joke of it. Forty years ringing for people who never paid, and now I won't take payment even if you offer." He closed his eyes. "But I'll take a favor. One favor. Small thing. Trivial, really."

"Name it."

"When you get to the Door — and you'll get there, you've got the two things that matter, a map and a temper — when you get there, ring the bell."

She blinked. "What bell? The Door doesn't have a—"

"My bell." He opened his eyes, and they were clear now, terribly clear, the clarity of the nearly gone. "One ring. Just one. For me. Nobody ever rang it for me while I was living — I was always the one ringing for everybody else, that's the keeper's lot, you stand in the tower and everyone else gets warned. So when you're standing at the last door in Vellamare, and you're about to walk out of a country that erased itself, you take a moment, and you ring once." His voice frayed. "Not a warning. Not a tolling. Just — one note, to say Ivo was here, and Ivo kept faith, and Ivo is done. Can you do that, girl? Can you do that one small thing for a stubborn old man?"

Odile leaned forward and pressed her forehead against his, careful of the sharp bones, and stayed there while the candle guttered.

"One ring," she said. "Loud enough to reach you wherever you are."

"Loud enough to wake the dead," he corrected. "I intend to complain about the noise."

* * *

He died near dawn, in the way he had done everything: without fuss, without permission, and slightly ahead of schedule.

She knew it was coming — had known since the river, had known since the stairs — and still, when the whistling breath simply stopped between one draw and the next, she sat bolt upright in the chair beside his bed with her heart slamming, as if the silence itself had shouted. The candle had burned to a puddle. Gray light stood in the window. On the bed, Ivo's face had relaxed into an expression she'd never seen him wear in life: mild surprise, as if he'd just remembered where he'd left something important, and was pleased about it.

She sat with him while the light strengthened. She closed his eyes. She straightened the blanket the way he'd have hated, and then pulled it crooked again, the way he'd have liked.

Then she climbed the tower stairs, because there was work, and work was the only prayer she knew.

The bell waited in the gray morning, huge and cracked and patient, its bronze gone green at the lip. The rope's peg stood empty where she'd taken the map down. For a moment she just looked at it — at the worn place on the beam where forty years of rope had grooved the wood, at the view from the arch, the whole drowned valley holding its mist like a held breath.

"No," she said aloud, to him, to the tower, to whoever was listening. "Not yet. You get your ring at the Door. That was the bargain. I keep my bargains."

The bell said nothing, but the silence had a satisfied quality to it, and she chose to take that as agreement.

She buried him at noon, behind the tower, under the rowan he'd once claimed was planted by a witch and once claimed was planted by a bishop, depending on his audience. She dug the grave herself with his own spade, which fought her the whole way, and she marked it with a knot — a good one, not an angry one — tied in a strip of his bell-rope and pinned to the earth with a stone. No name. Names were dangerous now. But anyone who came looking, anyone who knew knots, would read it plain:

*Ivo was here. Ivo kept faith.*

She packed at dusk. Rope-map coiled at the bottom of her bag, wrapped in his spare shirt, which smelled of lamp oil and hemp and stubbornness. Half a loaf. The flint. The little iron bell-handled knife he'd pressed on her the second night, insisting she needed it more than he did, which she had privately doubted and now understood.

Last of all, from the inner pocket where it had ridden against her ribs since the burning, she took out the charm she didn't remember making. Her own handwriting, seven years old, addressed to herself. All this way it had stayed shut, staying shut, and she had let it, because some doors you don't open in company.

But she was alone now. Alone was becoming a specialty.

By the light of a stub of candle in the empty tower, with a dead keeper's rope across her knees and a dead keeper's grave fresh in the yard, Odile turned the old charm over in her fingers and began, carefully, to break the seal of her own past.

The paper opened like a mouth.

And the first line, in her own younger hand, stopped her breath:

*If you are reading this, you hid instead of helping, and someone paid for it.*
