## 14. What the Gift Is

The cave smelled of wet stone and old smoke, the way caves do when someone has sheltered in them before and left their fear behind like a coat. Odile had found it an hour past dusk, a seam in the mountainside above a stream that talked to itself all night, and she had decided—sitting down hard on the cold floor, her legs simply refusing another step—that this was where she would stop running for one day.

One day. She could afford one day. Brisa's boat was gone, taken at the ford along with Brisa herself, and Odile had run upstream through reeds that cut her arms until the shouting faded behind her and the mountain swallowed her whole. Her palms were still raw. Her ribs ached where she'd fallen. Somewhere below, soldiers were probably dragging a smuggler girl with river-colored eyes toward Captain Ferro's tidy, terrible paperwork, and Odile was sitting in a cave eating the last of a loaf gone hard as roof tile.

She chewed anyway. Grief, she had learned, did not excuse you from chewing.

When the bread was gone she sat with her hands open in her lap and watched the last gray light die at the cave mouth. And then, because there was nothing left between her and the silence except her own thoughts—and her own thoughts had become dangerous company lately—she took out the charm.

* * *

It had traveled with her for seven years without her knowing it. That was the part that still made her skin crawl, seven days after finding it in the ashes of her burned stock: a charm made in her own handwriting, addressed to herself, tucked into the lining of her satchel so long ago that the thread holding it had nearly fused with the cloth. She remembered burning everything else. The luck-knots, the fever-wards, the little salt-and-thread protections she'd sold for coppers to people who needed them more than they knew—all of it consigned to the brazier while the Edict of Ash was being read out in the square, because a witch caught with stock was a witch caught confessing. She had fed seven years of careful work into the flames and felt nothing but relief.

And then, in the ash, this.

A flat square of linen, folded into quarters, stitched shut with red thread in a pattern she recognized because she had invented it—or thought she had—in her first year of apprenticeship. On the outside, in ink gone brown with age, her own handwriting:

*For O.Q., who will need this when she forgets what she is.*

She had not opened it yet. Seven days, and she had not opened it. She told herself it was prudence—opened charms lost their strength, everyone knew that—but prudence was a lie she wore the way she wore her gray shawl and her lowered eyes and her small, forgettable smile. The truth was simpler and worse. She was afraid of it. She had spent seven years being afraid of everything, and this little square of linen had waited longer than that, patient as a debt, and some animal part of her understood that once she opened it, the life she had built out of hiding would be over.

Well. The Edict of Ash had already seen to that.

"Right," she said aloud, to the cave, to the stream, to no one. Her voice sounded strange after two days of disuse. "Let's have it, then."

She picked the stitches loose with a fishhook from her pocket—the same fishhook she'd used for charm-work a thousand times, its barb worn smooth—and unfolded the linen.

Inside was salt, gone gray with age. A curl of dried rowan bark. A knot of red thread tied in the pattern her mistress used to call *the remembering*, which Odile had never been taught properly because Maren had died before getting around to it. And beneath the salt and bark, written on a scrap of paper in handwriting that was *not* hers—handwriting she would know anywhere, the sharp confident slant of it, the way the letters leaned forward like runners at a starting line—was a single line:

*The gift is a fire. It eats what you feed it. Feed it to yourself and it starves you. Feed it to others and it grows.*

Odile read it three times. Then she laughed—a short, cracked sound that echoed off the cave walls and came back sounding like someone else laughing, someone less tired.

Maren. Of course it was Maren. Dead eleven years, and still slipping lessons into her pockets.

* * *

Here is what Odile had understood about her gift, for most of her life, and what she had believed with her whole frightened heart: it was small. That was the word she used, even in her own head. *Small.* A warmth in the fingertips, a knack for knots that held, a way with fevers that might have been herbs and might have been something else. Small enough to hide. Small enough to sell in thimblesful to fishermen's widows without anyone noticing. She had built her whole survival on the smallness of it, the way a mouse builds its whole survival on being too little to bother catching.

But Maren had never said her gift was small. Maren had said—Odile closed her eyes and let the memory come, because it hurt and she was done flinching from things that hurt—Maren had said, one evening in the kitchen at Rafael's estate, with the smell of bread and rosemary in the air:

"You're rationing again, girl."

"I don't know what you mean."

"You do." Maren had taken Odile's hands and turned them palm-up, examining them the way a farrier examines hooves. "You've got a well in you, child, and you keep drawing up one cupful at a time and drinking it yourself, and then wondering why you're always thirsty. Magic isn't water. It doesn't run out. It's more like—" she'd waved a floury hand around the kitchen— "like a hearth. A fire burns brighter when there's more than one person to warm. You sit alone with yours, hoarding it, feeding it nothing but your own fear, and you wonder why it's shrunk down to embers."

"And if I use it?" Odile had asked. "Openly? They'll see me. He'll see me." *He* meaning Rafael, though she hadn't dared say the name with anything but reverence back then.

"He'll see you," Maren agreed. "That's not the same as the end of the world, though I know it feels like it. Listen." And here Maren had leaned close, and her voice had dropped into the register she saved for the truest things, the voice she'd used to tell Odile about the Hollow Door, about the country that wasn't on any map. "The gift grows when it's spent on other people. Every charm you make for someone who needs it makes the next one stronger. Every fever you break adds a log to your fire. But hide it—starve it, keep it locked up inside where it can only eat itself—and it'll start eating *you*. Fear is a fuel too, girl. It's just a dirty one. Burn nothing but fear long enough and you'll wake up one day and find there's nothing left in you but the burning."

Odile had nodded the way apprentices nod, hearing the words and filing them away under *things Maren says*, and had gone on doing exactly what she'd always done. Because Rafael's household was not a place where you experimented with being visible. Because Maren died before Odile could ask her what she'd meant by the look on her face when she said it. Because fear is patient, and it will wait out any lesson.

Seven years. Seven years of one-cupful-at-a-time. Seven years of charms made small on purpose, damped down, diluted, sold to the widow Marta for a copper and a smile, each one a candle pinched to a stub so no one would see the light. And now, sitting in a cave with a dead woman's handwriting in her hands, Odile finally did the arithmetic she'd been avoiding since Vellamare.

If Maren was right—if the gift grew when spent on others—then every small charm had been a seed she'd planted without knowing it. Seven years of seeds. The luck-knot for the widow Marta, whose nets had come up full ever after, so full that half the harbor bought knots off the quiet woman with the gray shawl. The fever-ward for the cooper's son, who should have died in the spring fever and hadn't. The salt-on-the-sill protections, dozens of them, scattered through the gray market like a gardener's forgotten bulbs. None of them large. All of them real. All of them fed by something in her that had never actually stopped working, only worked hungry.

And the hunger—she flexed her fingers, feeling for it, the way you probe a bruise—yes. There it was. The thing she'd always called tiredness. The hollowness she'd blamed on bad sleep and thin soup. It wasn't exhaustion. It was starvation. She had been a hearth burning nothing but her own fear for seven years, and fear, as Maren promised, was a dirty fuel. It kept the fire alive the way seawater keeps a man alive: technically, briefly, at a cost.

*Feed it to others and it grows.*

She looked at her hands in the dark. Ordinary hands, chapped at the knuckles, a fishhook scar across the left thumb. Hands that had made hundreds of small kindnesses and called them commerce. Hands that were shaking now, though whether from cold or something else she couldn't have said.

"All right," she whispered. "Show me."

* * *

There was a spider in the corner of the cave. She noticed it because it was struggling—caught in its own snared web, or perhaps in web older than itself, some ancestor's silk gone stiff with dust. Its legs beat against the threads in the small frantic way of small creatures, and Odile, who had spent seven years perfecting the art of noticing nothing, found she could not look away.

Old habit said: leave it. Old habit said: you are a mouse, mice don't meddle, meddling is how mice get caught.

New habit—no, not new. *Remembered* habit. Maren's habit. Maren had once stopped an entire afternoon's lesson to free a moth from a window, saying, "You think kindness is beneath magic? Girl, kindness is the *engine* of it,"—old habit reached out before Odile decided anything at all.

She didn't reach with her body. She reached the other way, the way she hadn't let herself reach in years: down into that warm place behind her sternum, the place she kept locked, and instead of drawing up her usual single careful cupful, she simply... opened the door.

It was like unclenching a fist she hadn't known she was making.

Warmth poured out of her—not much, not dramatically, nothing that would light the cave or frighten the birds—but it poured, freely, the way water pours when you stop damming it, and she laid it over the spider's tangle of silk the way you'd lay a hand over a cramp. The stiffened threads softened. The web loosened its grip. The spider dropped free, sat a moment on the stone in apparent astonishment, and then scuttled away into the dark with what Odile chose to interpret as dignity.

And the warmth did not diminish.

That was the thing. That was the thing that made her press both hands over her mouth. She had given—actually given, spent, expended—and the fire in her chest was *bigger*. Not by much. By exactly as much as a single freed spider warranted, which is to say a coal's worth, a breath's worth. But bigger. Measurable. Real. Maren had not been speaking in parables. Maren had been giving her operating instructions, and Odile had spent seven years reading them upside down.

"Oh," she said, into her hands. "Oh, you clever, stubborn woman."

She laughed again, and this time it didn't sound like someone else. It sounded like a girl in a kitchen smelling bread and rosemary, a girl who hadn't existed in a long time and had apparently been waiting in the lining of a satchel the whole while.

* * *

She didn't sleep, not really. She sat by the cave mouth as the moon crossed and the stream went on talking, and she took inventory the way merchants take inventory, except the goods were true ones.

She had a name that had been sold—Rafael's signature on the registry, neat and forward-leaning, the runner at the starting line. She had a friend taken at a ford because Odile had been slow, because Odile had hesitated, because seven years of hiding had taught her hesitation the way other people learned prayers. She had a bell-rope knotted with a secret she didn't yet know how to read—Ivo's gift, Ivo's dying request, *ring it once for me*—and a Door somewhere ahead of her that opened, if the stories were true, only for those who spoke the names of the lost.

Names. She turned that over. The Door wanted names, and she was carrying a head full of them: Marta, the cooper's son, the widow who'd bought fever-wards for her granddaughters, the cobbler dragged from his shop in chapter after chapter of the Edict's ugly arithmetic. And behind those, further back, the names in Maren's stories, the hedge-witches Ivo's bell had warned, the ones who'd already gone into the ground while the country repainted its maps.

She had been hiding her gift because she thought it was small. But it had never been small. It had been *rationed*. And the difference between those two things was the difference between a candle and a bonfire waiting for a reason.

The sky was going gray at the edges when she finally moved. She gathered her few things—the satchel, the fishhook, the empty bread wrapper, the bell-rope coiled safe against her ribs. Then she paused, and did something she hadn't done since Vellamare: she made a charm. Openly, in the gray dawn light, where anything with eyes could have seen her. Salt and rowan and red thread, tied in the remembering-knot, worked slowly and at full strength for the first time in seven years—for Brisa, wherever the soldiers had taken her, a charm for rivers to run kind and locks to remember they were only wood. She poured everything into it, all the hoarded heat, and when she tied the final loop her fingers were steady and the fire behind her sternum roared up like a banked blaze given air.

It was the strongest thing she had ever made. It wasn't close.

"There," she said softly, and tucked it into her pocket, because she couldn't deliver it, not yet, but she would. That was the difference. Hiding had no deliveries. This had a route.

She stood at the cave mouth and looked down the mountain, toward the refugee camps the rumors spoke of, toward the passes Ferro's net was closing, toward the Door. Toward Rafael, though she didn't know it yet, though the thought of him was still just a cold spot in her chest she hadn't examined. She was done examining things in the dark. From here on, she would do her examining by firelight—her own.

The morning wind came up the valley and she stood into it, and it occurred to her, with a clarity that felt almost like laughter, that she had spent seven years practicing how to be forgotten, and had gotten very good at it, and that none of it mattered anymore, because you cannot forget a fire. You can only put it out, or watch it spread.

Hers intended to spread.

She took the first step down the trail, and then stopped, because far below her—far off across the valley, at the tree line where the refugee road bent toward the camp the smugglers called Ash Market—she saw smoke rising. Not the thin gray smudge of cookfires. A column of it, black and deliberate, the kind of smoke that meant either a signal or a pyre.

And moving along the ridge opposite, small with distance but unmistakable in their formation, a line of soldiers, fanning out across her path like fingers closing into a fist.
