## 15. Ferro's Net

Captain Emilio Ferro had a theory about maps, and it was this: a map never lies, but the man who draws one might.

He had learned this at his father's knee, or rather at his father's workbench, where old Ferro the surveyor spent forty years measuring the honest bones of Vellamare and then, in the last decade of his life, watched cartographers paint over those bones with whatever the crown wished to be true. Rivers that weren't there. Shrines that had never existed. A whole country redrawn like a face after bad surgery. His father used to drink and mutter about it, and young Emilio would sit on the floor winding surveyor's twine into neat coils, listening to a man grieve for geography.

So when Captain Ferro unrolled Rafael's map of the northern passes—annotated in that small, precise hand, every goat trail and smuggler's cut marked with little red symbols—he did not feel admiration. He felt something closer to recognition. He knew what he was looking at. Not a map of mountains. A map of a mind.

And the mind belonged to someone who had once loved these hills enough to memorize them, which meant the mind could be predicted.

* * *

The morning began, as all good mornings in the hunt did, with paperwork.

Ferro sat in the requisitioned customs house at Fonda del Río, a room that smelled of ink, cold stone, and the particular staleness of men who had been drinking government coffee since before dawn. On his desk: three reports, two requisition forms, and a letter from the Ministry that made him want to weep into his collar, though not for sentimental reasons.

The letter said, in essence: *Quotas are expectations, not suggestions.*

Forty-one registrations this month. The Ministry wanted sixty. It did not matter that the northern districts were mostly shepherds who considered a blessing muttered over a lamb to be ordinary husbandry rather than sorcery. Numbers were numbers. Somewhere in the capital a clerk with soft hands drew a line on a chart, and somewhere below that line, real men with real boots had to go make the line true.

"Sixty," said Lieutenant Arriaga, reading the letter aloud because apparently no one else could bear to. "From where, exactly? Shall I arrest the goats?"

"The goats are registered," said Sergeant Mola, deadpan, from the corner where he was cleaning his boots with a rag that had once been a church banner. "I did them myself. Very cooperative."

Arriaga laughed. Ferro did not. Not because he lacked humor—he liked to think his humor was simply of a drier vintage—but because the joke had a body count attached, and he'd stopped finding those funny around the time he'd started counting.

"We'll get the numbers," Ferro said instead. "We always get the numbers."

It was true, and the truth of it sat in his chest like a swallowed stone.

* * *

Then came the interesting part of the morning, which arrived in the form of a courier from the capital, mud to the thighs, carrying a sealed folio with the advisor's seal pressed in green wax.

Rafael's annotations.

Ferro cleared his desk, weighted the corners of the great map with candlesticks and an inkwell and, for reasons of symmetry, a dried orange he kept as a paperweight because it amused him. Then he read.

The annotations were beautiful. That was the horror of them. Every pass through the Cordillera de los Muertos was rendered in Rafael's hand with the tenderness of a lover's letter: *the ford below the old shrine floods by midmorning in thaw; the shepherd track above it holds; there is a cave behind the waterfall at Serravalle, dry, with room for six; the smugglers' path past the ruined bell-tower is visible only from the east, at dawn.* Little red circles marked water sources. Little black crosses marked places where a fugitive might hide. And in the margins, in the same hand, notes like prayers:

*A gifted person running will follow water. Water cannot be tracked. Water remembers nothing. Choose the river routes first.*

*She will avoid roads after the third day. Pride curdles into caution.*

*If she is who I believe she is, she will go toward the Door. She has no other destination worth the walking.*

Ferro read that last line twice, and both times his hand drifted toward it, hovering, as if the words were warm.

"She," he thought. Who was she? The advisor had not given him a name. He had given him something better and worse: a portrait in probabilities. A woman who sold charms too small to notice. Who had survived seven years in Vellamare by being invisible. Who had, according to the intercepted reports from the ash market at Puente Seco, recently begun trading charms *openly*, which was either courage or madness or the specific thing that happens when a hunted animal stops caring whether it is seen.

Ferro spread out the other documents. The report from Puente Seco described her: middle years, dark hair going gray at the temples, hands that smelled of herbs, a voice that made people tell her things. Witnesses disagreed on everything except the effect. One refugee woman had wept while describing how Odile—yes, the name had surfaced now, Odile Quintana, entered in the registry seven years ago in a handwriting Ferro now knew intimately—had traded a charm against fever for half a loaf and a story, and had listened to the story like it mattered more than the bread.

"That's the one," Ferro said to the empty room, quietly, the way a man confirms something to himself.

* * *

By noon he had done what he did best, which was turn other people's knowledge into geometry.

The map went up on the wall of the customs house, pinned at four corners, and Ferro stood before it with a grease pencil and began to think like a fleeing woman. This was, he reflected, a strange profession. Somewhere in the capital, Rafael thought like her too—or rather, Rafael *was* the shape of her thinking, having trained it, having built the habits she would run by. Ferro was merely the man who translated prophecy into roadblocks.

Water first. She would follow the Río Blanco upstream, because the Río Blanco ran toward the high country, and the high country held the passes, and the passes held—according to Ivo the bell-keeper's testimony, extracted weeks ago from a dying man who had given up one secret to buy mercy for another—the Hollow Door.

Ferro circled the three viable crossings. The Passo del Lupo, wide enough for mules, closed by snow until late spring except in its lower reaches. The Garganta, a slot canyon that could be held by four men against an army. And the Shepherd's Stair, unmarked on any official map, known only to Rafael and to the smugglers who paid Rafael's estate for the privilege of forgetting they knew it.

Three crossings. Three nets.

He assigned the Garganta to Arriaga with twenty men and instructions to be invisible, which Arriaga took to mean camping without fires, eating cold sausage, and developing a personality like wet slate. The Shepherd's Stair went to Mola, who complained cheerfully that the altitude would ruin his digestion and then packed extra paprika, because Mola's relationship with suffering had always been culinary. The Passo del Lupo Ferro kept for himself, with the main force, because his instinct told him the quarry would aim for the hardest, highest way—the way that looked impossible on paper and was merely difficult in fact—and because Ferro had long ago noticed that his instincts were right often enough to be frightening and wrong just often enough to keep him humble.

By evening the orders were written. By nightfall the couriers were riding. The net was closing, link by link, and Ferro stood alone in the lamplight looking at the map and felt the cold satisfaction arrive on schedule, punctual as a tax collector.

She was in the net. She didn't know it yet. The rivers she trusted would funnel her. The caution she'd earned would slow her. The Door she sought sat behind three walls of soldiers, and every hour she walked toward freedom she walked deeper into the fold.

He should have gone to bed satisfied.

Instead he stood there another hour, moving the dried orange from one mountain to another, tracing her likely route with a finger, and found that the satisfaction had a crack in it, and through the crack something else was leaking.

* * *

What leaked in, over the following days, was obsession, and it came dressed as diligence.

It began with the details. He reread the ash market report and fixed on the story about the bread. Half a loaf for a charm and a story. He caught himself wondering what the story had been. Whose name had the woman needed heard? He told himself it was operational curiosity—understanding the asset's methods, building rapport models, standard intelligence work. But at night, in the cot in the customs house, he lay awake composing conversations with a woman he had never met, in which she explained herself to him, and he understood, and neither of them raised their voice.

This was new. Ferro had hunted deserters, smugglers, a bandit chief with a price on his head worth more than the customs house itself. He had never lain awake wondering whether the bandit chief slept warm.

He examined this development with professional suspicion. He wrote it down, actually, in the back of his field journal, in cipher, under the heading *Anomalies*, because Ferro believed that a hunter who refused to audit himself would eventually be audited by worse. The entry read:

*Subject displays pattern of small generosities. Effect on witnesses: disproportionate loyalty. Effect on pursuing officer: [data pending].*

*[Data pending].* He had underlined it twice.

The days in the passes sharpened everything. Snowmelt roared in the gullies. The pines dripped. His men, strung along the Río Blanco in pairs, checked papers and questioned shepherds and grew slowly damper and more miserable, and Ferro moved between them like a rumor, appearing at dawn, disappearing by full light, asking questions in a voice so level that people answered honestly just to make him stop being patient at them.

A shepherd at the lower ford told him about a woman who had passed three nights ago, traveling alone, who had mended his grandson's cradle-charm without being asked and refused payment. "She said," the shepherd reported, with the careful diction of a man reciting scripture he doesn't fully believe, "she said the charm was already paid for. By somebody. Long ago."

Ferro wrote it down. Then he sat with it longer than the report required, because the phrase snagged in him like a fishhook in a sweater. *Already paid for. By somebody. Long ago.*

He knew, from the registry copies, whose handwriting was on her entry. He knew the advisor had been her master. He knew the shape of the betrayal—sold, seven years ago, entered in a book by the man who taught her to hide. And here was the hunted woman, mending strangers' charms in the mountains, talking about debts paid long ago by some unnamed somebody, and Ferro's cold satisfaction developed a second crack, wider than the first.

Because it occurred to him—not as sympathy, he insisted, but as analysis—that the advisor's map might be the most complete confession ever filed with a ministry. Every red circle on water, every note on her habits, every prediction of her pride and her caution: these were the observations of a man who had loved his student's mind well enough to predict it, and had then sold the predictions to the people burning minds like hers. Ferro was not hunting a fugitive. He was executing a mentor's betrayal at three removes, with soldiers' boots instead of a signature.

He did not write that down anywhere. Some entries are safer unciphered even to oneself.

* * *

On the sixth day, the net caught something, and it was not her.

Mola's men at the Shepherd's Stair took a smuggler girl's cousin—a boy of nineteen, half-frozen, carrying a bundle of knotted rope he claimed was fishing line. The knots, Mola reported with unusual gravity, were not fishing knots. They were a record. Places, dates, names, tied in a code the boy swore he couldn't read and clearly could. The boy had been carrying word to someone ahead of him. Word about a bell-rope. Word about a door.

Ferro interviewed the boy himself, in the customs house, with tea, because Ferro had found that tea loosened tongues faster than irons and cost less to clean up afterward.

The boy was frightened and stubborn in equal measure, which is the natural condition of nineteen. He gave a false name, then a truer one, then, over the second cup, fragments. He talked about his cousin Brisa, who had a boat and no questions. He talked about a woman called Odile whom he had never met but whom everyone in the ash camps spoke of like weather—present, inevitable, arriving. He talked about the Hollow Door in the tone people use for things they need to believe exist, and then he stopped talking entirely, clamped like a mussel, and gave Ferro a look that contained, unmistakably, pity.

Pity. For the captain. Sitting across the table with the crown's seal on his papers and thirty men within shouting distance.

"Why are you doing this?" the boy asked suddenly, in the lull, the way children ask questions adults have forbidden themselves. "The door lets people leave. Nobody comes back through it. Nobody steals anything. What does the crown lose?"

"Order," Ferro said. It was the official answer. It came out of him smooth as a coin from a purse, worn blank by handling.

The boy looked at him the way you look at a man who has said a prayer he doesn't mean, and said nothing, and was taken away to hold in the storeroom, and Ferro sat alone with his cooling tea and turned the question over anyway, later, in the dark, because he was honest with himself in the way that was most inconvenient: thoroughly.

What did the crown lose? Nothing. What did Ferro lose? Nothing. What was the quota for? To feed a chart in the capital, to feed a minister's certainty, to feed the great machine that had eaten his father's maps and repainted the country over its own bones. And somewhere upstream of all of it, drawing the maps that fed the machine, was a man who had taught a girl to hide and then sold the hiding places, and who signed his annotations with a green seal and probably slept perfectly well.

Ferro got up, lit the lamp, and looked at the wall map for a long time.

Her route was a ghost-line in his head now, so familiar he could walk it blindfolded: up the Río Blanco, past the defaced shrines, through the ash camp, the ford where Brisa had been taken buying time—his men had taken her; the report had been brisk and unsentimental and had mentioned that the prisoner had spat in excellent form—then the high country, the caves, the stair. Toward the Door. Always toward the Door.

He picked up the grease pencil. He drew her line, faintly, in yellow, the color of caution, and stood back, and saw what he had built: not a net anymore. A portrait. Thirty years of soldiering reduced to the shape of one woman's flight, drawn in his own hand, annotated in his own margins with notes like prayers.

*She will mend something on the way. She will refuse payment. She will carry names.*

He hadn't written that last one yet. He wrote it now, and then stared at it, because he did not know where the knowledge had come from, and not knowing was the sound of the crack widening.

* * *

The dispatch from the capital arrived on the ninth day, green-sealed, and Ferro opened it expecting routine confirmation of dispositions. Instead it contained one paragraph in the advisor's own hand, which was rare, and a single instruction, which was not.

*Captain—if she reaches the Door, do not destroy it. Hold it. Report to me before any action. This is a direct order and a personal request, and I do not distinguish between them.*

Ferro read it three times.

A direct order and a personal request, undistinguished. From the man whose map hung on his wall, whose betrayals he had been executing link by link, whose handwriting he now knew better than his father's. And underneath the order, audible only if you held the page to the light of your own anomalies, something else: urgency. Fear, even. The advisor was afraid of the Door, or afraid of her reaching it, or afraid of what she would say when she stood in front of it—and Ferro, who had begun keeping a private tally of the advisor's tells the way his father had kept rainfall records, added the paragraph to the collection.

That night the snow came down heavy on the Passo del Lupo, and Ferro stood in the doorway of the customs house watching it fill his tracks, and he permitted himself the thought he had been circling for nine days:

He no longer wanted to catch her.

He wanted to *meet* her. He wanted to stand across from the woman who mended charms for half a loaf and a story, and ask her the question the shepherd's grandson's cradle had lodged in him: *who paid, long ago?* He wanted to know whether a person could spend seven years being hunted and still talk about debts as though they were gifts. He wanted, with an appetite that alarmed him precisely because it was not professional, to hear her side of the map.

The snow kept falling. In the storeroom, the boy with the knotted rope slept, guarded by men who had begun leaving him extra bread without orders. On the wall, the yellow line waited. And down in the valley, unseen, unwritten, a woman was climbing toward a door in a cliff face with the names of the dead written on her arms, and the net Ferro had built with such cold satisfaction was tightening around the only man it had ever truly been fitted for.

In the morning, the scout came down from the high watch with frost in his beard and a report that made Ferro's heart perform a maneuver he declined to name even in cipher: fresh boot-prints above the waterfall at Serravalle. Single traveler. Heading for the Garganta.

Heading straight for Arriaga's twenty silent men.

Heading, Ferro realized, checking the map with a steadiness he had to manufacture, for the one crossing where he himself was not.

He reached for his cloak, and his sword, and the green-sealed paragraph, and he gave the order that would define him, whichever way it broke: "Saddle the horses. I'm taking the Garganta myself."
