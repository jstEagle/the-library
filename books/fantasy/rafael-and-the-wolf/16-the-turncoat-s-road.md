## 16. The Turncoat's Road

The map Ivo had knotted into his bell-rope was honest about one thing and dishonest about everything else. It was honest about the Garganta—the great throat of a valley that cut south through the mountains like a scar someone had tried to stitch shut with rivers. Every other road out of the high country was closed. Ferro's soldiers sat on the passes the way stones sit in a wall, patient and permanent, and Odile had watched from a ridge at dawn as their cook-fires bloomed one by one across three horizons, neat as beads on a string.

Which left the Garganta. Which left the estate.

She had stood on that ridge for a long time, telling herself there had to be another way, the way you tell yourself a fever will break if only you lie still enough. The Garganta ran straight through Rafael's land. His walls, his orchards, his private bridge over the white water. And beyond it—she checked the knot-pattern again, tracing it by touch because she could not bear to look—the Hollow Door, four days' climb past the head of the valley.

Four days between her and freedom, and the first mile of them belonged to the man who had sold her name.

"Well," she said aloud, to the frost, to the crows, to nobody. "He always did say the best lessons were the ones you paid for."

* * *

The trick, she decided on the second day, was not to sneak in. Sneaking was what guilty people did, and guilty people got caught. Servants did not sneak. Servants were invisible in the way furniture was invisible: present, useful, beneath notice. She had spent seven years in Vellamare perfecting a smaller version of exactly this, selling charms too modest to attract attention, and so she did what she knew.

She stole laundry.

Not just any laundry. From a washing line behind a roadside inn she took a servant's gray dress, darned at both elbows, and an apron gone soft with boiling, and a cap that smelled of lye and someone else's life. She left, in exchange, a luck-charm tucked under the wash basin—a good one, generous, the kind she used to make before she learned to make them small—and told herself the trade was fair. Then she dirtied the hem of the dress deliberately, rubbed ash into the apron, and practiced walking.

That was the hard part. Seven years of walking quietly, of being a rumor in her own life, and now she had to walk like a woman with somewhere dull to be. Shoulders down. Hands busy. Eyes on the middle distance, where nothing interesting ever happened. She practiced until a mule driver passed her on the road without a second glance, and she could have wept with pride at being so thoroughly ignored.

By late afternoon the estate rose out of the valley floor, and Odile stopped pretending her hands weren't shaking.

It was smaller than memory had made it. That was the first betrayal. In her mind the house had been a palace, vast and shadowed, every corridor long enough to get lost in—which, when you were nine years old and newly sold, it certainly had been. But she was seeing it now with a woman's eyes, and it was only a large stone house with a slate roof going green at the edges, two wings around a courtyard, chimneys smoking thin and gray. A grand house that had once been grander, perhaps, before its master stopped entertaining anyone who might look too closely at him.

The orchard was still there. The apple trees he'd grafted himself, six varieties on one rootstock, his proudest trick—"you see, child, a thing can be many true things at once"—and the trees were heavy with fruit nobody was picking. Windfalls lay rotting in the grass, sweet-rotten smell drifting all the way to the gate.

A thing can be many true things at once. She spat, quietly, where no one could see.

The gate itself was open. That frightened her more than guards would have. There were no soldiers on the walls, no dogs, no challenge at the door—only a serving girl hauling water who looked up, saw a gray-dressed woman with an ash-dirtied hem, and said, "You're for the kitchen? They're short since the levy. Go round."

Just like that. The hunt's chief architect, the man whose maps were emptying a country of witches, couldn't keep servants, because the war had taken them, and so his house swallowed strangers without chewing. Odile went round, and was handed a basket of windfall apples, and was told to cut out the bad parts, and just like that she was inside.

Inside. Where the bread smelled the same.

That was the thing nobody warned you about, coming home to a place that had hurt you. Not the big things. The bread. The particular yeast-smell of the kitchen, sour-sweet, unchanged after seven years, and her body responding to it before her mind could object—with warmth, with a horrible animal comfort, the smell saying *safe, safe* in a language older than betrayal. She stood at the cutting board with a paring knife in her hand and let herself feel it, all of it, because Ivo had taught her that too, near the end: *a feeling you refuse will follow you; a feeling you carry will walk beside you.*

She carried it. She cut apples. Around her the kitchen clattered and steamed, and a scullery boy argued with a goose, and nobody looked at her twice.

* * *

She learned the shape of the house the way water learns a channel, over one evening and into the night, by flowing along the paths already worn for her.

The servants talked, as servants always talk, and she listened the way she'd once listened to Rafael's lectures: completely, and showing nothing. She learned that the master came and went "on crown business" and was away more than home. She learned that the east wing was shut, dust sheets on the furniture, except for one room on the second floor that was cleaned weekly and entered by no one but the master himself. She learned that Cook crossed herself when the greenhouse lights burned late.

"The greenhouse," Odile repeated, peeling apples.

"He grows things out of season," Cook said, with the tone of a woman reporting a miracle she'd decided not to be impressed by. "Roses in the frost. My mother would have called it witchery." A pause, a snort. "My mother called lots of things witchery. Half this valley's named for witches, if you go back far enough. Well. Named for other things now, isn't it."

"Yes," Odile said. "Isn't it."

And that was the rot, she thought, working the paring knife. Not the soldiers on the passes. Soldiers were just weather; weather happened to everyone. The rot was quieter. It was a cook who had grown up in a valley full of witch-names reciting the new names instead, fluently, without bitterness, the way you recite your times tables. The erasure didn't need to break down doors anymore. It had moved indoors. It lived in kitchens now, in the space where a shrug should have been.

She asked, casually, which room had been hers.

"Yours?" Cook frowned.

"When I was small. Before. I'm told I lived here once—I was found on the road, they say, and kept a season." A lie with a true bone in it; those were the only lies that worked.

"Oh—up in the old dormitory wing, love, but that's all sheeted now. Nothing up there but mice and memories." Cook said it kindly, and then, because kindness seeks company: "You'll not find much. When the master came back from the city seven years back, he had the whole wing cleared out. Burned a cartload of things in the yard, papers and little trinkets and such. Made quite a smoke. We thought—" she lowered her voice, though there was no one to hear, "—we thought maybe plague things. Kept quiet about it."

Seven years ago. The arithmetic landed in Odile's chest like a dropped stone. Seven years ago was when she had run. Seven years ago was when her name had gone into the registry, in his handwriting. He hadn't just sold her and moved on. He had come home and burned the evidence of her—the little trinkets, the charms, whatever of hers had been lying about—and stood in the yard watching the smoke, and then gone to Vellamare and signed his name beneath hers.

She thanked Cook for the history lesson and peeled apples until her fingers ached, and thought about fire, and how it always seemed to be the answer men reached for first.

* * *

Past midnight, when the house had settled into its creaking sleep, Odile climbed.

Not to the dormitory wing—there was nothing there but ash and mice, and she had no wish to visit her own ghost. She went up the back stair toward the one clean room in the dead east wing, the room cleaned weekly, entered by no one but the master. Her feet knew these stairs. That was the worst part. Seven years, and her feet still knew where the fourth step complained, still knew to hug the wall where the rail wobbled, still turned left at the landing without asking her permission. Her body had been apprenticed here longer than her mind had been free of it.

The corridor smelled of dust and cold stone and, underneath, faintly, of the tallow candles he'd always favored—cheap ones, he'd said, because a man who wasted good light on himself would waste anything.

The door wasn't locked.

She stood before it a long moment, hand raised, listening to her own heart conduct its foolish percussion. Unlocked meant either nothing to hide or nothing worth locking, and with Rafael it had never yet meant nothing. She pushed it open.

It was the schoolroom.

Her schoolroom—or close enough to stop her breath. The same slanted desk built for two students, though there had only ever been her. The same slate on its frame. The same window seat where she'd sat with her knees drawn up while he lectured, and the same view from it: the orchard, the river, the mountains stacked blue beyond. Someone had cleaned it, yes—but cleaning wasn't the word for what had been done here. Preserved was the word. Dust sheets over everything else in the wing, and this room kept like a reliquary, like a saint's bones arranged just so.

On the desk lay a child's copybook. She knew it without touching it. Her handwriting, age ten, laborious loops: *A charm is a promise made small.* Beside it, in his strong slanted script, the red correction she had hated: *No. A charm is a debt made portable.*

She had forgotten the correction. She had built seven years on the forgetting.

Odile sat down on the window seat, in the exact hollow her body had worn into the cushion at ten years old, and understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, what this room was. It wasn't sentiment. Rafael did not keep relics out of love; she doubted the man could spell love, though he could lecture on it for an hour. This room was a trophy. Or a tool. He had preserved the schoolroom the way Ferro preserved his ledgers—as evidence, as leverage, as a version of the past he controlled. Somewhere in that careful preservation was a use for her. There had always been a use for her. The apprenticeship, the name in the registry, the burning of her things—all of it, one long transaction, and she had finally been invited to see the account book.

"You always said," she murmured to the empty room, "that a teacher's real lesson is what he does, not what he says."

The candle stub on the desk—fresh, unburned, waiting—seemed almost to nod.

She took the copybook. It was theft, technically. She found she didn't care; she had stopped caring about a great many technicalities somewhere on the aqueduct outside Vellamare, holding Brisa's rope. She tucked it inside her dress against her skin, where the names written on her arms already lived, and stood, and that was when she heard it.

Voices below. Horses—many horses, the deep collective breathing of them in the courtyard, and lantern-light swinging up the walls, and a voice giving orders in a clipped, carrying tone she did not know.

But the voice answering it, unhurried, faintly amused, warm as a banked fire—

She knew that voice the way the stairs knew her feet.

Rafael was home.
