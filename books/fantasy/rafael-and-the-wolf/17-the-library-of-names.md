## 17. The Library of Names

The house had not changed.

That was the first cruelty of it. Seven years since Odile had fled Rafael's estate with a stolen name and a burning throat, and the halls still smelled of beeswax and dried lavender, still creaked in the same three places on the back stair, still held their breath at dusk the way old houses do when they are pretending to be empty.

She moved through them as Marta now—a laundress from the coast, gray shawl, gray hands, gray everything. The lye she'd rubbed into her knuckles had eaten away the last of her charm-glow, and the servant's smock hid her arms, which was fortunate, because her arms were about to become the most dangerous documents in the country.

*In and out,* she told herself, for the hundredth time since the orchard wall. *Ledger, ink, out. No detours. No sentiment.*

She was already lying to herself. She had taken a detour past the kitchen garden where he'd taught her to tell foxglove from comfrey by taste alone—one leaf on the tongue, *feel how it numbs? That's the plant telling you it matters.* The beds were weeds now. Someone had let the garden die, and Odile felt a small, shameful satisfaction about that, and then felt worse about feeling satisfied.

Sentiment. Detours. Both already underway.

The library was on the second floor, east wing, behind a door that had never once been locked in all her apprentice years. Rafael's one superstition: *A house that locks its books is a house that fears its own mind.* He used to say it like a proverb worth carving over a lintel. She wondered, pushing the door open on its familiar sigh of hinges, whether he still said it, or whether the door had learned to lock sometime in the years between.

Lamplight from the window—the moon nearly full, thank every patient saint—and there it all was, exactly as memory had kept it. The ladder on its brass rail. The globe with the painted countries, half of which no longer existed anywhere except on that globe. The reading chairs, one deep and green, one straight-backed, and Odile knew without looking which one was hers, which one he had given her because *a student who slouches thinks better.*

Her chest did something complicated. She ignored it professionally.

The ledger was not on the desk.

That stopped her cold. She had built the whole plan around the desk—slip in, slip out, the arrogance of a man who keeps his sins in easy reach. But the desktop held only an inkwell, a letter knife, and a small dish of candied ginger, which was such a *Rafael* detail that she nearly laughed aloud. The man had survived a revolution of his own making and still kept sweets by his correspondence.

Think. Where would he keep it now?

Not the desk—he'd have moved it after the Edict, when the registry became the crown's favorite toy. Not the bedroom; he slept like a cat, one ear open. Not the cellar; damp was death to paper and he knew it.

The shelf above the fireplace, behind the false row of agricultural treatises he kept purely to annoy visiting nobles. He'd shown her that hiding place when she was sixteen, delighted with himself, certain she'd never need it. *Everyone hides things behind books, Odile. So hide your things behind boring books.*

Odile crossed the room, ran her fingers along the spines until she found the wrong-feeling one—*On the Rotation of Root Crops*, thick as a brick and twice as dull—and pulled.

The panel swung out.

The ledger sat inside, fat and buckram-bound, with a brass clasp that was, insultingly, unlocked. Because who would dare. Who could possibly climb through his own front door wearing someone else's name.

She carried it to the window, set the lamp low, and opened it.

And there was her own handwriting staring back at her.

Not hers-hers. Her *old* hand—the careful, looped script he'd drilled into her, each letter standing at attention. The first page was a title, in Rafael's bold slant: *Registry of the Gifted, Province of Vellamare and Dependencies.* Below it, columns. Name. Gift. Locality. And then a third column that made her stomach drop through the floor and keep falling:

*Disposition.*

He hadn't just registered them. He'd tracked them. Every entry annotated in that same confident hand, updated in different inks across the years—black, then brown, then, lately, a red so flat and administrative it might have been blood that had been asked to calm down.

She found herself on the first page. Of course she did. Alphabetical, and Quintana comes early.

*Quintana, Odile. Charm-work (minor). Vellamare, Salt Lane quarter. Registered 7 yrs prior by R.V., guardian. Disposition:* and here the ink changed, newer, the brown of maybe two years ago: *Departed city. Unlocated. Non-priority.*

*Non-priority.* She read it four times. It should have been a relief—a word like that meant the hunters weren't looking hard. Instead it felt like being a footnote in a book that had once been about you. Seven years of fear, of salt on sills and charms too small to notice, and to him it was a single dry syllable filed between Quintero and Quiroga.

*Non-priority,* she whispered. "You absolute—" She didn't finish. There was no word big enough and she needed her voice for other things.

Because she had turned the page, and the dispositions had turned with her.

*Alba, Serafina. Weather-sense. Disposition: registered. Compliant. Employed at court dairy.* Fine. Alive, probably. She let herself feel that for half a heartbeat before the next line.

*Bassat, Mirren. Mending-charms (strong). Disposition: refused oath. Conveyed to Ashford. Died winter, fever.*

*Conveyed to Ashford. Died winter, fever.* Written the way you'd note a shipment gone moldy. And below it, smaller, crammed against the margin as if even his handwriting had hesitated: *fever of the yard, per report.*

Per report. He hadn't seen it. He'd had it *reported* to him. That was the trick of the whole ledger, Odile realized, turning pages faster now—that column wasn't cruelty, it was *bookkeeping*. Ashford was the camp upriver; Brisa had called it the place boats don't stop. Fever of the yard was what happened when you packed two hundred people into winter barracks and called it registration.

*Calloway, Tomas. Dockhand. Minor gift: knots hold true. Disposition: informant, active. Reward accruing.*

Tomas. Sweet, frightened Tomas, who owed her eleven coppers and had looked at her like a drowning man looks at driftwood. So the clerk hadn't lied, and neither had the registry. Somewhere in Vellamare, Tomas was earning her life away, coin by coin, and probably hating himself thoroughly while he did it, which was somehow both the saddest and most human thing in the entire book.

She kept turning. She couldn't stop. That was the design of it, she thought wildly—it read like a novel where everyone you loved died in alphabetical order.

*Dorn, Petra. Hedge-healer. Disposition: refused oath. Conveyed to Ashford. Released, ill. Died spring.*

*Estevez, Lulo. Beast-speaker. Disposition: fled. Pursuit ordered. Taken at Marrow Ford. Executed.*

Taken at Marrow Ford. Her eyes snagged on that. Marrow Ford was upstream, on the very river Brisa poled them down, the ford where soldiers' torches had swept the reeds and Brisa had shoved the tiller into her hands and said, *Go, don't be stupid, go.* Had Brisa known? Had she steered them past the exact water where a beast-speaker named Lulo had been dragged ashore?

The cost of being the one who runs, she thought, is that you find out afterward what the running was for.

Page after page. Some entries short and clean—*compliant, compliant, employed, compliant*—the lucky ones, the useful ones, the ones Rafael had saved by feeding the rest to the fire. She understood the arithmetic now, understood it the way you understand a trap by stepping in it. He had told himself a story: give them the names of the strong, protect the harmless. And the ledger showed exactly what that story cost, because the harmless had *also* died—of fevers, of winters, of yards and fords and springs—while his annotations grew shorter and more bored, year by year, until the deaths stopped getting their own words at all and simply said:

*Deceased.*

Two syllables. A whole woman named Petra Dorn, who had pulled Odile's tooth when she was fourteen and told her the pain was just the tooth arguing, reduced to *deceased* in brown ink.

Something in Odile went very quiet and very clear.

It wasn't rage, not exactly—or it was, but rage the way a river is water, something with direction and weight now instead of foam. She had come here to steal a book and burn a house, some shapeless child's revenge. What she left with was going to be heavier than either.

She looked down at her arms. Gray hands, laundress's arms, plain skin from wrist to elbow.

Paper burns. Ledgers get locked, lost, rewritten. But a body carries what's written on it out of any fire.

There was a pen on the desk—his pen, silver-nibbed, ridiculous. She took it anyway. Petty, but she was entitled to one petty thing tonight. She dipped it in his inkwell, rolled her sleeve to the shoulder, and began at the beginning.

*Alba, Serafina,* she wrote along her forearm, small and neat, letters marching wrist-ward. *Bassat, Mirren.* Around the bend of her elbow. *Calloway, Tomas—not dead, but written, and I will carry that too.* Up over the muscle. *Dorn, Petra.* She blew gently on the wet ink and started down the other arm. *Estevez, Lulo, taken at Marrow Ford.*

It hurt more than she expected, not the writing but the rhythm of it—each name a small stone laid in a wall, and the wall growing up both arms, and her standing inside it. By the time she reached the F's the lamp was burning low and her shoulders ached and she had run out of clean skin on her forearms and begun, grimly, to consider her collarbones.

*Ferro, Captain. Non-gifted. Disposition: instrument.* She paused at that one. Even Ferro was in the book. Filed, annotated, dispositioned—*instrument*, the way you'd write *hammer*. She almost pitied him. Almost. Then she wrote his name too, because the wall didn't get to choose its stones, and somewhere in a country that remembered, someone ought to know that the hunter had also been hunted, catalogued, and spent.

Then the G's, and the H's, and—

*Ivo, bell-keeper, defrocked. Disposition: unregistered, overlooked. Deceased, this autumn, per parish record.*

She sat down in the green chair. Her chair. The one for students who slouch and think better.

Ivo. Old Ivo with his one bell for the dead and his rope of knots, who had pressed the bell-cord into her hands while his breath rattled like a draft under a door, who had said *ring it once for me* with the last of his voice. Overlooked. Unregistered. The only kind thing the ledger ever did anyone was lose track of them, and it hadn't even managed that in the end—some parish clerk had fed his death back into Rafael's machine, and Rafael had noted it, and moved on to the J's.

"He kept your bell," she said out loud, to nobody, to the room. "Your warning bell. It's rusting in his boathouse. I saw it."

The library said nothing, but libraries are good listeners, and this one had heard seven years of apprentices recite their lessons, and it seemed to Odile that the silence now had a different quality—less like a room, more like a witness.

She went back to work. K's, L's. *Luna, the twins, charm-weavers. Disposition: separated at registration. One deceased. One unlocated.* She wrote both names, together, on the soft skin inside her elbow, side by side, where they belonged.

Somewhere below her, a floorboard sighed.

She froze, pen lifted, listening. Wind in the chimney—old houses lie about footsteps. She counted to thirty. Nothing. Back to the M's.

*Marrow, Ansel. Gift unknown. Disposition: deceased, birth to death unrecorded—*

No. Wrong entry. Her eye skated off it. She went back.

*Gift unknown.* How could the gift be unknown? He'd interviewed them all; registering was interrogation dressed in paperwork. Unless—

Unless the entry predated the interviews. Unless some names had gone into the book before the people were ever questioned, entered from a map, from a list, from a *plan*—names gathered not to save anyone but to fill quotas, to make the registry impressive, complete, undeniable. Names collected the way a collector collects, alive or otherwise.

She checked the earliest entries. There it was, faint, in the oldest black ink: whole clusters of *gift unknown, deceased, per census*—people entered by household count, dead on paper before the hunt ever knocked. Rafael hadn't merely betrayed his students. He had invented people's guilt in advance and let the crown collect on it.

The clarity in her chest hardened into something with edges.

She was still writing—now on her collarbone, now along the edge of her jaw where the shawl would hide it, the pen scratching like a small determined animal—when the light changed.

Not the lamp. The hallway. Lamplight moving under the library door, slow, unhurried, accompanied by the particular tread she would have known blindfolded, drunk, dead, or dreaming. A tread that paused, always, on the loose board outside the door, because he liked to arrive announced.

Rafael was coming to his library. At midnight. For his ledger.

Odile looked at the book in her lap, half-finished, hundreds of names yet unread, hundreds more uncopied onto skin that was running out. She looked at the window—three stories, ivy, a drop that would break a leg and announce her to every guard on the estate. She looked at the false shelf, gaping, evidence plain as a confession.

The tread stopped on the loose board.

She closed the ledger, slid off the chair, and pressed herself into the narrow dark between the reading desks as the door began to open—ink drying on her skin, names riding her arms like a second pulse, and the one thought in her head bright and cold as a blade:

*He taught me to hide. Now let's see what else he taught me.*

* * *

The door opened on its familiar sigh, and Rafael stood in it, lamp raised, candied-ginger dish suddenly missing from his desk because it was in her pocket, because she was entitled.

"Odd," he said softly, to the room, in the voice he'd used for lessons. "Someone's been reading ahead."
