## 18. Confrontation in the Greenhouse

The greenhouse smelled the way it had always smelled: wet loam and citrus and something sweeter underneath, something that had no name because Rafael had never allowed anyone to name it. Odile stood just inside the door with a serving girl's tray of cut lemons balanced on one hip, and for three full breaths she let herself be fifteen again—let herself believe that if she stood very still among the orange trees, the years would fold up like paper and she would hear his voice from somewhere behind the trellis, saying *you've overwatered the southern bed again, little wren*, warm as banked coals.

Then she saw what he'd done to the beds.

Every plant that had ever hummed was gone. The moonwort, the whispering sage, the little blue flowers that opened only when someone lied nearby—all of it ripped out, roots and all, and in their place grew rows of ordinary roses, staked and labeled in a clerk's careful hand. *Rosa gallica. Rosa damascena.* Roses that answered to no one. Roses that would never, in a hundred years, do anything but be roses.

He had planted obedience where the magic used to live.

Odile set down the tray. Her hands were steady, which surprised her. Seven years of hiding taught you how to keep your hands steady while everything behind them screamed.

"You were supposed to dust the lemon leaves," said a voice from the far end of the greenhouse, "not stand there breathing on them."

* * *

Rafael knew her footsteps before he saw her. That was the trouble with apprentices—you built them yourself, plank by plank, and then you could never unhear their particular creak. He had known hers at once through the doorway, lighter than any servant's, placed like a person who had learned to walk on floors that reported to other people.

He kept his back turned and his hands in the soil of the rose bed. There was a comfort in dirt. Dirt did not lie about what it wanted; you gave it water and light and it grew or died honestly, unlike people, unlike students, unlike governments.

"Forgive me, master," the servant's voice said, pitched low and roughened at the edges, and it was such a bad imitation of servility that he almost laughed. She had never been good at pretending smallness. It was the first thing he'd loved about her and the last thing that would get her killed.

"The lemons can wait," he said. He stood, brushing earth from his knees, and turned around.

Seven years. She had gone thin and watchful, with gray salted into her dark hair far too early, and she stood the way a deer stands at the edge of a clearing—not frozen, not yet running, doing the arithmetic of distance. But her eyes were the same. That was the unbearable part. The eyes were exactly the same, still carrying that look she'd given him on her first day, when she'd asked if the plants could really hear and he'd said *they can hear better than you can, so mind your language around the mint.*

"Hello, Rafael," Odile said.

Not *master*. He felt the omission land between them like a dropped knife.

"Hello, Odile," he said. "You're taller than I remembered."

"You're older."

"Guilt does that." He gestured toward the bench beneath the window, the old one, iron-legged, where they'd eaten lunch a thousand times. "Sit. Please. I have tea coming, and you look like a woman who has run farther than food can fix."

"I'm not staying."

"No one ever is." He smiled, and watched her flinch at the smile, and hated himself for noticing that he could still make her flinch. "Humor me. You came all this way through my house dressed as a scullery maid. The least you can do is drink my tea before you tell me what a monster I am."

"I wasn't going to tell you that."

"No?"

"No." She sat, perching on the edge of the bench like a bird ready to lift off. "I already know what you are. I read your ledger."

Ah. So that was the smell under the fear—the particular smoke-smell of someone who had spent a night with his handwriting. He had wondered, since the alarm was raised in the library, whether she'd found it. Of course she had. He had taught her to find things.

"Then you know why I did it," he said.

"I know what you told yourself."

* * *

The tea arrived—a servant, ordinary, unremarkable, who poured and left—and Odile wrapped her hands around the cup without drinking. Poison seemed unlikely. Not because Rafael was kind, but because Rafael was efficient, and poison was a messy instrument that left questions. Everything he did left no questions. That was his genius. That had always been his genius.

"The Edict of Ash," she said. "You wrote it."

"I refined it." He settled across from her, and up close she could see the cost of his refinement—the fine tremor in his fingers, the way his eyes moved constantly to the window as though soldiers might be needed at any moment, or perhaps as though he expected the roses to testify against him. "There was an edict before me. Cruder. Register and hang, all of them, no distinctions. Do you know what I changed?"

"You made it worse more politely."

"I made it *selective*." His voice sharpened, and for a moment he was the man from the workroom, correcting her knot-work, impatient with imprecision. "Before me, a hedge-witch who eased a fever was the same as a blood-worker who drowned a village. Before me, there were quotas—Captain Ferro's beloved quotas—villages required to produce a certain number of the gifted each season, like wool or tin. I abolished the quotas. I built categories. Useful, harmless, dangerous. I built appeals. I built *mercy*, Odile, with a signature line."

"And your signature is on mine."

He went quiet. Outside, wind moved through the roses, and the labeled stakes clicked against each other like small wooden teeth.

"Yes," he said finally. "It is."

"Seven years ago."

"Six years, eleven months. I remember the date." He looked at his hands. "You want to know why."

"I want to know how you say it out loud and still call it mercy."

"Because it *was*." And here he leaned forward, urgent now, the old teacher's fire lighting him from inside, and Odile understood with a sick lurch that he believed it—he had believed it so long and so hard that the belief had grown bones. "They were going to burn us all. All of us, every gift, every charm, every child who talked to bees. I sat in that room with men who wanted ash, and I traded. I gave them a map of the country's magic, and in exchange I drew lines around the ones worth saving. The healers. The weather-singers. The ones whose gifts fed people. Yours—" he hesitated, and she watched him choose his words the way he chose ingredients, carefully, deceptively, "—yours was too visible. Charms that work too well draw attention. I put your name on the list to move you off the pyre list. Registration instead of rope. I saved your life and you ran from me before I could explain, and I have spent seven years—"

"Don't." The word came out flat and cold, and it stopped him mid-breath. "Don't you dare spend seven years on my behalf. You didn't save my life. You sold my name, and then you sold the *map* of where I'd go, because Ferro isn't following rumors, is he? He's following your annotations. He knew the ford. He knew the passes. He took Brisa at the ford because you drew the ford on his paper."

Something moved behind his face—grief, or its well-made imitation.

"Brisa," he said softly. "The smuggler girl. I'm sorry. If I'd known she was with you—"

"You'd have drawn the ford anyway."

He didn't answer, and the silence was the truest thing he'd said all afternoon.

Odile set down the untouched tea. "Why am I alive right now? Soldiers everywhere, and I'm sitting in your greenhouse drinking tea I don't trust. Why?"

"Because I'm offering you what I offered myself." He rose and walked to the window, silhouetted against the gray garden, and when he spoke again his voice had the smoothness of a speech rehearsed in front of mirrors. "Come inside. Work with me. The crown needs the gifted—it knows it needs us now, the way a body knows it needs its heart only after the heart starts failing. There will be a sanctioned order. Licensed magic. Charters, protections, salaries. And I need—" he turned, and his eyes found hers with terrible sincerity, "—I need someone beside me who remembers what we were building before all this. Someone I trust. Someone who was *mine*."

"There it is," Odile said quietly.

"What?"

"*Mine.* You still say it like that. Like I'm a chair you misplaced." She stood. Her legs were shaking now, but her voice wasn't, and she thought: *let the shaking stay in the legs, the voice has work to do.* "You want to know what I learned in seven years of being nothing, Rafael? I learned that your mercy has a shape, and the shape is a leash. Your protected witches will heal the crown's soldiers and curse the crown's enemies and teach their children to be grateful. And the ones who won't hold the leash—the Brisas, the Ivos, everyone whose gift doesn't fit in a category—they'll burn exactly as they would have anyway. You didn't abolish the fire. You just made yourself the one who feeds it."

"That is *unfair*—"

"It's the fairest thing I've ever said." She stepped closer, and now she could see him clearly, this old man in his beautiful house full of obedient roses, and what she felt was not hatred, which surprised her. It was grief, enormous and specific, the grief of a funeral for someone still breathing. "You taught me everything I know about charms. You never once taught me anything about courage. I had to learn that from a fisherman's widow who paid me in soup. From a bell-keeper who rang warnings until his hands broke. From a smuggler girl who bought my life with hers at a ford, and she couldn't even do magic, Rafael. She didn't need to. She just *did it*."

"Odile—"

"I'm not your apprentice anymore." She said it simply, the way you close a door. "I haven't been for seven years. I was never yours. I was my mistress's debt, and then I was my own, and the only thing you ever owned was my gratitude, and I'm taking that back now. Consider it collected."

His face went white, then gray, then settled into something old and terribly tired. When he spoke, his voice was almost gentle.

"You don't even know where the Door is, do you. You came all this way and you still need something from me."

"I know where it is."

That landed. She watched it land—watched the flicker of recalculation, the merchant in him re-pricing everything.

"Then you understand your position," he said. "Ferro has the passes. My soldiers are at every gate. There is nowhere in this country for you, and there will be nowhere in any country, because I will sign whatever edict makes it so. Unless." He spread his hands, reasonable, generous, monstrous. "Unless you give me the Door. Bring it into the charter. A controlled crossing, regulated, taxed—no one hunted, no one burned, everyone safe on *my* terms. You'd be a founding mother of the new order. Your name in the histories. Isn't that better than dying in some cliff-face with a smuggler's knot in your pocket?"

And Odile laughed.

She hadn't meant to. It came up out of her like a bubble from deep water, and it startled them both. Because it was suddenly, perfectly clear—clearer than it had been in the cave, clearer than in Ivo's bell tower—that Rafael had never understood anything at all. Not the gift, not the Door, not her. He thought the Hollow Door was a resource. An asset. A crossing to be chartered and taxed. He had spent seven years cataloguing magic and had learned precisely nothing about it.

"You think it opens for power," she said. "Or for payment. Or for cleverness." She shook her head. "It opens for names, Rafael. For the ones nobody else will say. And you—" she looked at him with something close to pity, "—you couldn't get through it if you lived a thousand years. You erased too many."

For the first time since she'd known him, Rafael had nothing to say.

In the silence, she heard it: boots on gravel, many boots, and the far-off clang of the estate bell ringing the alarm. Ferro's men, moving through the gardens. He had let her talk this long because he'd already sent for them. Even his confession had been a cage with the door left open.

Rafael heard it too. Something desperate flickered across his face—the mentor, the father-thing, the man who had once bandaged her burned fingers and told her *pain is just information, little wren*.

"Odile. Wait. I can call them off—I can—"

"You can't." She was already moving, snatching up the serving girl's shawl, backing toward the greenhouse's far door, the one that opened onto the orchard and the long slope toward the cliffs. "You sold the map. Live in the country you drew."

She slipped out into the green shade of the orange trees, and behind her she heard him shout her name—just her name, no title, no bargain attached—and for one absurd heartbeat she almost turned around.

Then the dogs began to bark, and she ran.
