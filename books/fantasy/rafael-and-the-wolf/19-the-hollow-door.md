## 19. The Hollow Door

The mountain had been lying to her for two days.

Odile had learned to read lies in stone the way other people read weather. The cliff face above the frozen tarn looked like every other wall of gray granite in the Serranthes—until you stood exactly where Ivo's knotted rope said to stand, and then the lie announced itself. The rock was *too* smooth. Too patient. It had the stillness of a held breath.

She pressed her palm against it and felt nothing, which was itself a kind of answer. Every stone she'd ever touched had an opinion about her. This one was pretending not to be home.

"All right," she told it. "I see you."

The wind saw her too. It came down the pass in a long white howl, carrying grit and the smell of snow that hadn't decided to fall yet, and it found every gap in her coat like a customs officer looking for contraband. She turned her back to it and dug out the bell-rope.

Ivo's knots. She'd spent three nights with them by candlelight, unpicking their logic the way he'd taught her to unpick anything: patiently, respectfully, as if the thing might have feelings. There were forty-one knots. The first thirty-nine were a path—the ford, the burned chapel, the notch in the ridge shaped like a sleeping dog, all of it rendered in cord the way other men rendered it in ink. The fortieth knot was tight and strange, tied left-handed, and when she'd finally worked out its meaning she'd sat back and laughed until the candle guttered, because it didn't say *here is the Door*. It said *the Door is wherever you stop pretending it isn't*.

Ivo, even dying, had been unable to resist a joke.

The forty-first knot was simple. A single loop, pulled snug. *Say their names.*

She'd asked him, in those last grey hours, what would happen if she got there and the names wouldn't come. Grief does funny things to memory; fear funnier. He'd taken her hand—his grip already bird-light—and said, "Girl, you've been carrying forty people on your arms for a month. You think they're going to let you forget?"

She looked down at her forearms now. The ink had faded from black to a bruised blue-grey, but the names were all still there, written in her own careful hand, copied out of Rafael's ledger by candlelight while his household slept. Maren Ostrova, who could call fish. Danel and little Pia Voss. The twins from Ash Market whose names were only half-known even to themselves. Forty-one names, counting Ivo's own, which she had added last, in the cave, with shaking hands and no small amount of argument—he'd have hated being first and loved being last, so last he went.

Forty-one knots. Forty-one names. She should have known then. Ivo had probably tied it that way on purpose, the old goat.

She began to walk the base of the cliff, following the too-smooth stone, looking for the seam. The tarn lay flat and iron-colored at her feet, holding the sky upside down. Somewhere behind her, three days south, Brisa was in a soldier's wagon or a cell or worse, and Odile had made herself a promise about that somewhere: *after*. After the Door. After she was through. After she could do more than weep and run. Guilt was a heavy coin but it spent, if you let it buy you forward instead of pinning you in place.

"There," she said aloud, and heard her voice go thin against the rock.

The seam was barely a seam—a vertical line in the granite, hair-thin, running from the tarn's edge up past the reach of any ladder. Frost had gotten into it over centuries and widened it by nothing at all. If you weren't looking for a door, you'd see a crack. If you were looking for a door, you'd see a joke. It wasn't tall enough for a child to walk through sideways.

But doors, her mistress had told her once, in the tone she reserved for correcting particularly stupid errors, are not holes. Doors are agreements. And this one, Odile thought, laying both hands flat against the cold stone, had agreed to wait a very long time.

She took a breath that hurt on the way in.

"Maren Ostrova," she said.

Her voice cracked on the second syllable. She hadn't said any of the names aloud since the cave—hadn't dared, as if speaking them used them up—but the name came out whole anyway, and something happened to the air. Not a sound, exactly. More like the feeling of a room when someone enters it who belongs there.

"Danel Voss. Pia Voss."

The frost in the seam glittered. Or shivered. She couldn't tell, and didn't stop.

"Yeva of the Ford. Tomas Alday—" she faltered, because Tomas wasn't dead, Tomas was maybe alive in Vellamare hating himself, and she'd written him in anyway, because the ledger had marked him *fled* and fled was its own kind of lost. "Tomas Alday, wherever you're hiding. I hope it's somewhere warm."

The seam had begun, very faintly, to hum. Or her bones had. Hard to say.

* * *

She was on the twelfth name when she heard the dogs.

Not barking—Ferro's dogs didn't bark, she'd learned that much from the refugee stories at Ash Market. They made a low, continuous sound like a kettle about to boil, and they made it only when they'd found what they were sent for. The sound came down the pass from the south, off the switchback trail, and it was followed by the thing Odile had been listening for since the ford without letting herself know it: boots. Many boots, moving in step, which meant soldiers, which meant discipline, which meant Ferro.

Her hands went cold in a way that had nothing to do with the mountain.

*Keep naming,* said the part of her that sounded like her mistress. *A ritual interrupted is just words. Finish it.*

*And if I can't finish it?* said the part of her that sounded like a girl hiding charms under floorboards.

*Then you'll die mid-sentence,* said the mistress-part, briskly, *which is more than most of us get.*

Odile laughed—one short, wild bark of it, swallowed by wind—and kept her palms on the stone and kept talking.

"—Sela Imre, who mended what was broken. Old Piotr, who never told me his gift and I never asked, and I'm sorry, Piotr, I should have asked—"

The boots came around the shoulder of the trail below the tarn. She counted without meaning to: sixteen soldiers, in column, red-and-iron, and ahead of them a man on foot in a grey officer's cloak with the hood back, and she knew the set of his shoulders from the stories before she ever saw his face. Captain Ferro. He walked like paperwork given legs—everything squared, everything filed, nothing wasted.

He stopped at the far side of the tarn. So did his soldiers, fanning out along the shore with the unhurried competence of men who had done this many times and been paid well for it. Sixteen rifles, though she couldn't see them clearly across the water; sixteen reasons to hurry.

"Quintana!" Ferro's voice carried flat across the tarn, unamplified and somehow louder for it. "You're a hard woman to file."

She didn't turn her head. Her lips kept moving. *Thirteenth. Fourteenth.* The seam under her hands had gone from humming to warm, which on a granite cliff in the Serranthes in late autumn was as good as shouting.

"I'll tell you what this is," Ferro called. "This is a document. You're standing inside a document, Quintana. Your master drew the map. I'm executing it. You can come away from that rock, sign your name in the registry like a citizen, and live. That's the offer. It doesn't improve with the weather."

*Fifteen.* Her arms ached. The names wanted to come faster now, tumbling, and she had to hold them in line like children crossing a road.

"You know what's funny?" she called back, not turning, raising her voice enough to carry. "You keep saying *my master*. Like he's mine. Like I chose him."

"He registered you himself. I've seen the entry. His hand, his seal."

"Yes." *Sixteen.* "I've seen it too."

Something shifted in Ferro's voice—not doubt, nothing so useful as doubt, but the small recalibration of a man discovering the script has changed. "Then you know there's no version of this where you win."

"Oh, Captain." *Seventeen.* The stone beneath her palms was genuinely warm now, and the seam had widened—not much, a finger's width, a hand's width, dark air breathing out of it smelling of green places and rain on dust, of a country that remembered. "That's the difference between us. I'm not trying to win. I'm trying to *finish*."

*Eighteen. Nineteen.* The soldiers were wading the shallows at the tarn's edge. Someone shouted an order. The kettle-hiss of the dogs rose to a shriek and was cut off, silenced, leashed.

*Twenty. Twenty-one.* Her voice was going hoarse. She spat to one side and kept on. The dark in the seam was wide enough now that she could see movement in it—or thought she could, shapes like trees deciding whether to be trees.

*Twenty-two. Twenty-three.* A rifle cracked, and the bullet sparked off the cliff a hand's breadth from her ear, and the whole mountainside rang like a struck bell, and Odile—who had spent seven years making herself small, quiet, ignorable, a charm too minor to notice—stood up straight, turned her head, and looked at Captain Ferro across the tarn with forty-one names on her arms and twenty-three of them already spoken into the stone.

"That one nearly cost you a name," she called, and her voice came out steadier than she had any right to expect. "Maren Ostrova. Say it. Go on. You shot her cousin yourself, I read it in the ledger. Say her name, Captain, and see how it sits in your mouth."

For one heartbeat—she saw it happen, across the whole width of the tarn—Ferro's certainty flickered like a lamp in a draft. Then he raised his arm and brought it down, and the column advanced into the shallows, and Odile turned back to the Door and gave it everything she had left.

*Twenty-four. Twenty-five. Twenty-six—*

The bullets came again, three together, and the cliff screamed, and a chip of stone opened her cheek, and she did not stop. The seam was a gap now, a true doorway, tall as a barn and narrow as a grave, filled with slow golden light that had no sun in it, and through it she could see—she would swear until her dying day she could see—a hillside with orchards on it, and someone walking there, and the someone turning, and lifting a hand.

*Ivo?* she thought, absurdly. *Already? You couldn't wait?*

*Twenty-seven. Twenty-eight.* The soldiers were close enough now that she could hear their breathing, their wet boots, a young one's voice cracking on *halt, halt, what do we do, sir, she's not running—*

No. She was not running. That was the whole point. That was the entire lesson, seven years and forty-one names long.

*Twenty-nine. Thirty.* Her knees were buckling and she locked them. *Thirty-one. Thirty-two.* The light from the Door poured over her like warm water. On her arms, the faded ink had begun to lift—each name rising off her skin as she spoke it, drifting in tiny bright letters toward the gap, going home ahead of her.

*Thirty-three. Thirty-four. Thirty-five—*

"Cease fire!" Ferro's voice, sharp, wrong-footed. "Hold! HOLD—"

Because the soldiers had stopped on their own. She heard it in their silence: the names were reaching them too. Each one spoken passed over the tarn like a stone skipped across still water, and she could feel where they landed—in the young one's cracked voice, in the sudden slackness of the man nearest the shore, in Ferro himself, standing hip-deep in freezing water with his arm still half-raised and his face doing something his face had clearly never been ordered to do.

*Thirty-six. Thirty-seven.* Only four left. Her voice was a ruin. She whispered them now, and the Door bent down to hear.

*Thirty-eight. Thirty-nine.*

Behind the soldiers, up the southern trail, hoofbeats. Fast ones. One horse, ridden hard, and a voice she had known since she was nine years old calling her name across the tarn—*Odile!*—in a tone that wanted so badly to be a mentor's and had always really been an owner's.

Rafael. Of course. He'd mapped the route himself; he'd arrive precisely when it would hurt most.

*Forty,* said Odile, and did not turn around. "Ivo Bell-Keeper, who kept one bell for the dead and told me the truth with a knot."

The rope in her hand grew light. The last loop slipped loose on its own, untied, finished.

One name remained. She saved it the way you save the last swallow of something precious, and she spoke it not to the Door but to the man floundering through the shallows with his ledger-heart full of everyone he'd sold:

"And Odile Quintana," she said. "Who was never yours."

The Hollow Door opened like an eye deciding to trust.

And Rafael reached the shore, and Ferro raised his hand, and nobody—soldier, captain, or traitor—moved to stop her, because forty names were still hanging in the air between them all, and every one of them was asking its question.

Odile stepped to the threshold, and paused, and looked back.
