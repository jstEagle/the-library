## 21. One Set of Footprints

The Door was still open.

That was the thing Odile would remember afterward—not the fire, not the soldiers, not even the sound of Rafael's voice breaking on her name. She would remember that the seam in the cliff stayed open, patient as a held breath, while everything around it went to ruin. The Hollow Door did not care about urgency. It had waited a hundred years for someone to speak enough names; it could wait a few minutes more for her to decide what kind of person she intended to be.

Ferro's soldiers had stopped at the tree line. She didn't know why, at first. Then she saw the captain himself standing apart from them, his sword still sheathed, his face doing something complicated in the light of the burning ledger pages drifting down from the greenhouse road like black snow. He had heard the names. All of them. Two hundred and eleven souls read aloud into the mountain air while arrows meant for her heart sat unloosed in their quivers, because it is one thing to hunt a witch and quite another to shoot at a funeral.

*Alba Ferrero, drowned in the well at Torren,* she had said, and the captain had gone gray as ash.

She understood then that names were not just a key. They were a mirror, and Ferro had looked into it and flinched.

"Odile."

Rafael stood ten paces behind her, at the edge of the seam's pale light. His fine coat was scorched at one sleeve. His hands—those clever, careful hands that had taught her to knot a charm so small a customs man would pat his own pockets looking for what he'd lost—hung empty at his sides. No ledger now. The ledger was cinders on the wind. Just him, and the terrible nakedness of a man whose paperwork has finally failed him.

"You should come with me," he said.

She almost laughed. It came out as something between a laugh and a cough, which, she supposed, described her entire life.

"Should I," she said.

"The Door closes when you cross. You know that. Ivo told you—it opens once, for one naming, and then it's stone again until the world forgets itself enough to need it." He took a step closer. The soldiers stirred; Ferro raised one hand and they stilled. "If you go alone, everything on this side goes with you into the dark. Every hedge-charm, every birth-blessing, every grandmother's remedy for colic. Erased. But if we both cross—if the gift crosses *in company*—it carries. That's how it works, Odile. You know it is. Your mistress knew it. Magic kept alive by one person is a candle in a windstorm. Kept by two, it's a hearth."

It was a good argument. That was the worst part. It was exactly the kind of argument he had always been good at—the kind that made cruelty sound like arithmetic.

"That's not how it works," Odile said quietly. "I learned that in a cave two weeks ago, reading a charm I wrote to myself when I was nineteen years old and too frightened to know what I was saying. The gift grows when you use it *for others*. Not for yourself. Not for your legacy. Not for the country you decided deserved to survive more than the people in it." She turned to face him fully. "You taught me knots, Rafael. My mistress taught me the rest. And the rest says you've been starving for thirty years."

His jaw tightened. "I saved lives."

"You *sorted* them." Her voice cracked on it, and she let it crack. Seven years of hiding, seven years of selling charms too small to notice, seven years of flinching at boots on stairs—and here, at the end, she found she did not want a steady voice. A steady voice was his instrument. Hers could shake. "You made lists. Useful, not useful. You wrote my name in your neat hand and sold it to men who burn grandmothers, and you called it mercy because you drew a little star next to the ones you liked. Brisa doesn't have a star, Rafael. She's sixteen and she laughs too loud and she bought me my freedom with a boat and a lie about tide tables, and your ledger never once thought of her at all."

"Brisa is alive," he said. "They took her at the ford. Alive."

"And where?" Odile stepped toward him, and this time the soldiers did stir, and Ferro did not stop them, because there are distances a man will allow and distances he won't. "In a cell? In a registry? In a column of your handwriting with a note in the margin saying *hold for questioning, may be useful*?" She shook her head. "You want to come through the Door with me so that the gift survives. But the gift isn't knots, old man. The gift is *this*. Naming the dead. Refusing to sort people. Carrying everyone who couldn't run." She pressed her palm flat against her own arm, against the ink. Two hundred and eleven names, copied in the library by candlelight while he slept above her like a fat spider in a very tidy web. "I'm carrying them through. You're carrying nothing but yourself, and yourself weighs too much for this crossing."

"Odile—" His voice changed. The advisor fell away, the architect fell away, and for one unguarded moment there was only the young man she had met at fourteen, brilliant and lonely, who had shown her that her small ugly talent was actually beautiful. "I am sorry. I mean it. I have been sorry for seven years. When I entered your name I told myself the registry would protect you—that a name on a list is a name under a roof. I was wrong. I have been wrong in ways I cannot fix. But let me spend what's left trying. Let me carry half. Let me—"

"No."

It came out simple. That surprised her. She had expected it to cost more.

"I forgive you," she said, because it was true, and because forgiveness is not the same thing as a door held open. "I forgave you somewhere on the river, I think, watching villages that no longer remembered their own saints. You were a frightened boy who grew into a frightened old man and dressed the fear up as statecraft. I forgive you. And I am leaving you here. Both things are true. Both things can fit inside one woman at the same time. That's another lesson my mistress taught me that you never learned: you don't have to choose between grief and going."

Behind him, Captain Ferro cleared his throat. It was an oddly gentle sound for a man built entirely out of quotas.

"Witch," Ferro said. "Quintana." He seemed to be testing which name to use, and settling on hers. "My orders are to take you to Vellamare. Alive, preferably. My orders do not mention doors in cliffs, because my orders were written by a man who believed this place was a rumor." He glanced at Rafael, and there was something in his face that Odile recognized from Tomas the dockhand, from every neighbor who had ever weighed a reward against a debt: the arithmetic of complicity, running its sums. "My orders also do not say what to do if the man who wrote them turns out to be standing on the wrong side of a miracle."

"Ferro," Rafael began.

"I read your ledger, sir." Ferro's voice was flat as a frozen pond. "All of it. Before I burned my copy. Did you know you annotated my sister?"

Odile went still.

*Alba Ferrero, drowned in the well at Torren.* She had written it on her forearm with the rest. She hadn't known whose sister she was naming. Names don't come with footnotes.

"She was a midwife," Ferro said. "You gave her three stars. *Useful.* You wrote to the provincial governor recommending her exemption." He smiled, and it was the worst smile Odile had ever seen, because there was no anger in it at all, only a vast tired understanding. "The governor's clerk lost the letter. Clerks lose things. So my sister died in a well, and you got your three stars back, and life went on, and I became the kind of man who hunts children for coin because the alternative was becoming the kind of man who asks questions." He looked at the seam of pale light. "I find, at fifty-one years of age, I have become both."

He stepped aside. Fully aside. He gestured, actually gestured, with one gauntleted hand, the way a man waves a guest through a doorway at a wedding.

"Captain," Rafael said sharply, "think about what you're—"

"I have thought about nothing else for twenty years, sir. It's very restful to stop." Ferro turned to Odile. "Go. And when you get wherever that goes—I don't want thanks. I want you to say her name again. Alba Ferrero. Say it somewhere it'll stick."

"It already stuck," Odile said, and touched her arm. "It's coming with me."

Something moved across the captain's ruined face that might, in better weather, have been relief.

Then Rafael moved.

Not toward her—toward the Door. She realized with a lurch that he had never intended to ask. Asking was for people with other options. He was going to walk through beside her whether she willed it or not, and the Door—she felt this in her teeth, in the bell-rope knotted around her wrist, in the old charm warm against her sternum—the Door would permit it, because the Door only required names, and Rafael had spent thirty years collecting them. He knew more names than anyone alive. The Door did not check intentions. Doors never do.

"*No.*"

She said it the way Ivo must once have rung his warning bell—one clear stroke, no ornament. And she did the thing she had sworn on the aqueduct she would never do again, the thing hiding had starved nearly to death: she used the gift. Openly. For someone else.

For him, as it happened. Which was the joke of it, and she thought Ivo would have appreciated the joke, and Brisa definitely would have, loudly, twice.

She named him. Not his name—everyone has that—but the truth of him, spoken into the seam of the world the way she'd spoken the dead. *Rafael Villalobos-Quintana, who taught a frightened girl to knot light. Who sorted the living like laundry. Who is sorry, and sorry changes nothing, and is still worth something, because it means the boy in him isn't entirely dead.* The words came out of her glowing faintly, the way the names of the dead had glowed, and Rafael staggered as though she'd struck him, because being *known* is the one weapon no one learns to parry.

And the Door answered.

The pale seam narrowed. Not closing—*weighing*. Odile felt it weighing him, the way a market woman thumbs a coin. And she felt, with absolute clarity, what the Door found: a man full of names, yes, but names taken, names catalogued, names *owned*—a hoard, not an offering. The gift grows when used for others. Rafael had spent thirty years using it for files.

"He can't cross," she said, wonder making her voice soft. "Not because I forbid it. Because he's empty, Rafael. You gave it all away and kept the receipts."

"Odile." He was reaching for her now, actually reaching, and his hand was shaking, and she hated how much it hurt to see. "Please. I'll learn. On the other side, whatever it is—I'll learn. Give me a year. Give me—"

"You had seven years to write me a letter," she said. "You wrote a registry instead."

She stepped backward into the seam's light. It took her weight the way water takes a dropped leaf—no drama, no cold, just a gentle insistence that she was elsewhere now, or elsewhen, or else-*what*, and her body accepted the terms before her mind finished negotiating.

Through the narrowing gap she watched him. Watched him stand in the wind with his scorched sleeve and his empty hands, while behind him his great work burned page by page off the mountainside, and before him the last door in the world sealed itself against his particular kind of knowing. He did not rage. That was the final surprise. He simply stood there growing smaller, the way a shore grows smaller from a departing boat, until he raised one hand—not pleading, not commanding, just *raising* it, the way you wave to someone on a train you chose not to board.

She did not wave back.

She wanted to. She noted that she wanted to, filed it away with all the other costs, and did not.

* * *

The other side was grass.

That was her first coherent thought, lying flat on her back in a meadow under a sky the color of a robin's egg: *all that, and it's a meadow.* Somewhere nearby water moved the way water moves when nobody has argued with it. The air smelled like rain that had fallen on thyme. There were mountains behind her—or rather, there was a cliff face behind her, and when she rolled over and looked, there was no seam in it at all. Only stone, old and lichened, with a shelf of wildflowers growing out of a crack exactly the width of a woman.

She lay there a long time. Long enough for the sun to move. Long enough for her to understand that she was crying, and that the crying was not entirely grief, and that the part that wasn't grief was worse, somehow, or better, or both.

Alone.

She had done it. She was free, and safe, and alone, in a country that supposedly remembered magic, and there was no one—no Brisa to laugh too loud, no Ivo to grumble about rope maintenance, no widow Marta to overpay for luck-charms and pretend they both believed in them. Freedom, it turned out, was mostly just a room with the walls removed. The loneliness came in through the space where walls used to be.

Then her arm itched.

She pushed up her sleeve. In the new light, the two hundred and eleven names had changed. The ink was the same ink—she'd written it in a library by tallow-candle—but the letters had settled deeper, the way letters do on skin that has decided to keep them. And beneath the last name, in handwriting that was unmistakably her own but that she had not written, fresh and dark:

*Brisa Solano — taken at the ford — alive.*

Her breath stopped.

Below that, in the same impossible ink:

*Ivo Marchetti — rang true — died well.*

And below that:

*Alba Ferrero — remembered.*

Odile sat up in the grass, heart hammering, and stared at her own arm, at the ledger that had rewritten itself in crossing, at the proof that names carried through after all—carried not by the gift, not by the Door, but by her, by the specific stubborn freight of her. The names weren't ink anymore. They were seeds. She could feel that now, the way you feel a splinter: each one a small insistence, a small *plant me*.

Brisa's name pulsed faintly against her skin. *Alive.* Taken at the ford, alive, and Odile was on the wrong side of a sealed door with no map, no boat, and no idea how to get back to a country that had erased itself—

Except.

She looked at the wildflowers growing out of the crack exactly the width of a woman. She looked at the meadow, and beyond it, at a thread of smoke rising from a valley where someone, evidently, still lit fires. She thought about a charm written by a frightened girl to her future self, and about a bell-rope knotted with a location, and about a Door that opened for those who spoke the names of the lost.

Doors, she was beginning to suspect, were less rare than people willing to knock.

Odile Quintana got up, brushed the grass off her skirt, tucked Ivo's bell-rope safely into her bodice, and started walking toward the smoke—with two hundred and fourteen names on her arms, one set of footprints behind her, and, for the first time in seven years, absolutely nowhere to hide and no reason on earth to want to.

The smoke smelled, when she got closer, like bread.

Someone was baking. Someone, in a country that remembered magic, was having an extremely ordinary morning, and had no idea yet that a woman was coming down the hill with a cargo of the dead and a list that included one girl who needed stealing back from the wrong side of the world.

Odile quickened her pace. Bread could wait. Names couldn't.
