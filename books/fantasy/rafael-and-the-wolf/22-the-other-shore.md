## 22. The Other Shore

The first thing Odile did on the other side of the Hollow Door was sit down in the grass and cry for an hour.

She was not proud of it. But there was no one to see, and the grass was soft, and the sun—real sun, unrationed, falling out of a sky with no ash in it—lay warm on her shoulders like a hand she had almost forgotten existed. So she sat among flowers she could not name and wept until her ribs ached, and when she was finished she blew her nose on her sleeve, which Ivo would have scolded her for, and stood up, and looked around at the country that remembered.

It looked, at first glance, disappointingly ordinary.

Rolling green hills. A river running silver and unhurried toward somewhere kinder than Vellamare's coast. Distant rooftops with smoke rising from chimneys in fat, contented columns. A road, rutted and honest, leading downhill toward all of it. Somewhere a dog barked. Somewhere else, someone was baking bread, because the wind carried yeast and woodsmoke and something sweet that might have been apples or might have been honey or might simply have been the smell of a place where no one had ever needed to hide.

Odile had expected wonder. She got a farm lane.

And yet her hands were shaking.

Because the charms knew. The little workings stitched into her cloak hem—the ones she'd made half-asleep over seven years, the ones too small to notice—were singing. Not loudly. Charms never sang loudly; that was rather the point of them. But here they didn't have to whisper. Here they hummed like kettles left happily on the stove, like bees in clover, like every small magic she'd ever made finally allowed to be exactly as big as it wanted to be.

"Oh," she said, to no one. "Oh, you poor things."

She crouched and touched the hem, and felt them lean into her fingers like cats.

*The gift grows when used for others,* her mistress had written, seven years and one lifetime ago, in handwriting Odile now knew better than her own. *It starves when hidden. You have been feeding it crumbs, little bird. Come where there is bread.*

"All right," Odile told the empty hillside, the distant rooftops, the dead whose names rode on her arms under two layers of sleeve. "All right. I'm here. Now what?"

The hillside did not answer. But down the lane, the bread smell strengthened, and a woman appeared at a gate, shading her eyes, and called out in a voice that carried across forty years of Odile's life:

"You'll be from over the water, then!"

Not a question. Not an accusation. A welcome with its coat already on.

Odile's heart performed a maneuver that would have alarmed a physician. She looked down at her arms—the names inked in Rafael's library, copied in her own careful hand onto skin that had once been the only map she trusted—and then at the stranger, and made the first free choice of her adult life.

"Yes," she said. "I will be."

* * *

The village was called Bellharbor, which nearly undid her on the spot, and the woman was Maren Ostrow, widow, baker, and—as it emerged over the course of one loaf, two cups of cider, and three hours of Odile talking faster than she had since before the Edict of Ash—a hedge-witch of the fourth circle who thought nothing whatsoever of it.

"Fourth circle?" Odile had said faintly.

"Charms, weather-sense, birthing, and the laying-out of the dead." Maren shrugged, flour to the elbows. "My grandmother was fifth. There's a woman up the valley does seventh-circle work, though she's insufferable about it." She squinted at Odile. "What circle are you?"

"I—" Odile looked at her hands. Hands that had spent seven years making charms so small even she pretended not to see them. "I don't know. Where I come from, we didn't—we didn't count. We hid."

Maren set down the knife she'd been using and looked at her for a long moment, not with pity—that would have been unbearable—but with the particular attention of a woman assessing how much bread a person needed.

"Then you'll stay," she said, "until you know. And after that, if you like. We've room. We always have room. That's rather the whole point of this country."

"Which country is it?" Odile asked. "Every map I ever saw ends at the mountains."

Maren smiled, and it was a smile with weather in it, old storms long past. "That's because your maps were drawn by people who were afraid of us. We stopped being on them on purpose. It's a long story involving a great deal of spite and one very good enchantress." She slid a fresh loaf across the table. "Eat first. Stories keep. Bread doesn't."

Odile ate. And discovered, somewhere between the crust and the crumb, that she was starving in a way that had nothing to do with food, and that Maren knew it, and that this was fine.

* * *

She stayed through autumn.

She meant to stay three days. Then a week. Then the frost came and there were charms to mend—old ones, generous ones, woven into door lintels and well-posts by generations of hands, sagging like tired soldiers, and Odile found she could not bear to watch them fail. She repaired a hearth-blessing the second week. By the third she had re-hung the warding on the millpond and argued politics with the seventh-circle woman (whose name was Petra and who was, indeed, insufferable) about whether river-wards should be renewed annually or biannually, and won, and been so pleased about it that she forgot to be afraid for a full hour.

The names on her arms faded slowly, the way good ink does—not vanishing but settling, going brown-gold, becoming part of the skin rather than sitting on it. She checked each morning. She would check each morning for the rest of her life. That was the bargain she had made with herself on the far side of a cliff-face seam while soldiers' boots rang on stone: *you carry them, you say them, you do not let the carrying become hiding.*

Every evening, she said them. All of them. Brisa, who had bought her escape at a ford with her freedom and possibly her life. Ivo, who had rung his last bell for her. The fisherman's widow from the gray market. Tomas, the dockhand, whose choice she would never learn and had decided to forgive either way, because forgiveness was lighter luggage. Every name from Rafael's ledger, copied in rage and kept in grief.

On the first night, saying them aloud in a strange room had felt like screaming into a pillow. By midwinter it felt like setting a table.

"They're not prayers," Petra observed, having walked in on the ritual without knocking, because Petra knocked for no one. "You're doing it wrong if you think they're prayers."

"What are they, then?"

"Attendance." Petra examined a jar of Odile's salves with proprietary interest. "You're taking roll. The dead are present. Mark them down and move along." She set the jar back. "This is good work. Your mistress taught you well."

"She tried to teach me to hide."

"And what did you actually learn?"

Odile opened her mouth. Closed it. Thought of a charm addressed to herself, seven years old, waiting patiently in a burned stock-pile's ashes for her to be ready. *The gift grows when used for others.*

"That," she admitted, "apparently."

* * *

In spring, they planted the bell-rope.

It was not a planting anyone had done before, which suited Odile fine; she had spent enough of her life following instructions. Ivo's rope had crossed the mountains coiled against her heart, knotted in his pattern—the Door's location tied into it in a language of loops he'd learned from bell-keepers dead two hundred years, and beyond the Door's location, other knots, other knowledge, the whole drowned archive of a trade the crown had defrocked out of existence. She had studied those knots all winter by firelight. Rope remembers. That was the first thing Ivo had ever taught her, back in his tower full of one bell: *rope remembers, girl. Why d'you think we trust it more than paper?*

So on a bright cold morning in March, with Maren and Petra and a dozen curious villagers looking on, Odile planted the rope.

Not metaphorically. Actually. They dug a hole at the top of Bellharbor's low central hill, and Odile laid the rope in it in a spiral, knot-side up, and covered the tail with soil the way you'd cover a seed, and spoke over it the only incantation Ivo had ever given her, which was less an incantation than an instruction: *"Ring true, and mind who's listening."*

Nothing happened, for approximately nine seconds.

Then the rope sprouted.

Petra made a sound like a teakettle being insulted. Maren gripped Odile's arm hard enough to bruise. Because the rope—hemp, old, salt-stained, smelling faintly of Ivo's tower and the river and the inside of a smuggler's boat—put down roots and sent up a shoot, and the shoot became a sapling, and the sapling, growing at the speed of something with somewhere urgent to be, became in the space of a quarter hour a slender silver-barked tree with leaves like small green bells, which shivered in a wind that wasn't blowing anywhere else on the hill.

"Well," said Maren, after a while.

"It's a bell-tree," said Odile, who was crying again, and had stopped being embarrassed about it sometime around midwinter. "He'd have hated it. He'd have said trees can't keep time." She wiped her face. "He'd have visited it every day."

When the tree was grown—which took until sundown—they cut three branches, at Odile's insistence, and cured them, and by summer solstice Bellharbor had three new bells cast from old bronze, hung in the meetinghouse tower, and Odile Quintana, charm-witch of uncertain circle, late of Vellamare, rang the noon bell herself.

For Ivo. Once, as asked. Then, because the sound came out clean and true and rolled down the valley like a blessing with its sleeves rolled up, she rang it eleven more times, one for each year she had hidden, and then handed the rope to Maren and went and sat in the grass until her legs worked again.

* * *

The teaching began the way most true things begin: accidentally.

A neighbor's boy—Tamsin's lad, eight years old, all elbows and questions—caught Odile mending a ward and asked why the air went *shimmery* when she did it. His mother arrived at a run, mortified, ready to apologize for him, and Odile waved her off and crouched down to the boy's level.

"Why do you ask?"

"Because I do it too," he said, "but mine goes all wrong and Mam says don't, and Gran says *never* don't, and they shout about it."

Odile looked up at Tamsin, and Tamsin looked at her grandmother, and the grandmother lifted her chin in the universal gesture of women who have been right for decades and are prepared to wait out the opposition.

"How would you like," Odile said to the boy, "to learn to make it go right instead?"

By harvest there were six of them on her step every rest-day: the boy, two sisters, a shepherd's daughter who could already quiet animals with a look, a ferryman's son who heard rivers talking, and one solemn child who produced sparks when frightened and therefore required, more than instruction, reassurance. Odile taught them the way she wished she had been taught. No debt. No fear. No *this gift is a burden I am saddling you with.* Just: *here is what your hands can do. Isn't it marvelous? Let's find out together.*

She started them, on the first day, the same way every time.

"Hold out your arm," she'd say. "No, the other one. Now—these are names. Can you read? Good. This one is Brisa. She was nineteen and she steered a boat like she was arguing with it and she saved my life. This one is Ivo. He kept a bell for the dead when nobody else would. Say them with me. Saying them is the first spell. Everything else is just practice."

They said the names. Children's voices, clear and unafraid, carrying down the hill in the evening light. Attendance taken. Present, present, present.

And the gift grew. Hers, theirs, everyone's. She could feel it happening, season over season, the way you feel a muscle strengthening: the wards she wove held longer, the charms sang louder, the small magics she'd spent seven years shrinking down to invisibility unfurled to their natural size like plants finally given a window. Some nights she made a working so large and easy that she laughed out loud alone in her room, astonished, like a woman discovering she'd been walking on a broken leg for years and the leg had healed.

*Come where there is bread,* her mistress had written. Well. She had come. There was bread. There was, God help her, an entire bakery.

* * *

One evening in the second autumn, Maren found her on the hill under the bell-tree, copying names onto fresh linen strips with ink she'd ground herself.

"More dead?" Maren asked gently.

"No." Odile shook her head. "New ones. Refugees came over the pass last month—three families, and a hedgerow-witch named Sorrel who crossed with nothing but her grandmother's recipe book. Their dead aren't in my ledger. Nobody wrote them down over there; there wasn't time, there wasn't safety." She blotted a strip. "So I'm starting a new book. Anyone who comes through gets their lost recorded. Properly. With attendance taken every evening, if they want it."

Maren sat down beside her, joints cracking, and was quiet for a moment.

"You know what you're building, don't you."

"A school?"

"A memory," Maren said. "The thing your country tried to kill you with was forgetting. Names off maps, shrines scraped blank, whole trades unwritten. And you're over here doing the exact opposite, on purpose, with children." She nudged Odile's shoulder. "That's not a school, girl. That's a counterattack. The slowest kind. The kind that wins."

Odile looked down at her arms, gold-brown with names, at the linen strips drying in the last light, at the bell-tree dropping its small green leaves that would, next spring, ring faintly in the rain—Ivo would have *hated* it—and at the lights coming on one by one in the village below, behind doors that bore wards old and confident and thick as blankets.

Somewhere on the far side of the mountains, a country was still erasing itself. Somewhere a ledger might still exist with her name in it, in a hand she had once loved. Somewhere Rafael was alive or dead, redeemed or rotting, and she had decided, finally and permanently, that it was no longer hers to know. She had carried him across the border in the only way that mattered—in the lesson she refused to repeat—and there she had set him down.

But here: bread. Bells. Six children on her step who would grow up never learning the word *register* except as a piece of foreign history, ugly and far away.

"Slowest kind wins," she agreed. "Ask me how I know."

"How do you know?"

"Because I was the slow part." She tied off the last strip and stood, brushing grass from her skirts, and the evening bell waited below, and the names waited on her arms, and the future—a large, unmapped, gloriously unhidden country of it—waited everywhere else. "Come on. Time to take attendance."

They went down the hill together, and behind them the bell-tree shivered, though there was no wind, ringing its small green note for the dead, who were present, all of them, every one, marked down and moving along into whatever came next.
