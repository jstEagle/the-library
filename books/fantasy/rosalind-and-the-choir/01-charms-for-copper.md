## 1. Charms for Copper

The lamps of Vellamere's night market came on one by one as the light failed, and Rosalind Hartwell counted them the way other men counted money — not because they were his, but because he needed to know exactly how much of the world was watching.

There were forty-two lamps along Cooper's Row. He had counted them every evening for five years. On the night the Inquisitorial clerk first lingered at his stall, there were forty-one, because the lamp above the fishmonger's had gone dark again, its wick drowned in its own oil, and no lamplighter had come to mend it. Tamsin Ferro would have mended it if she'd walked this route tonight. She didn't always. The city assigned her wherever the darkness was worst, and lately the darkness had been worst near the river.

Rosalind spread his cloth across the trestle table with the same care a priest might show an altar. Luck-knots in three colors — red for love, green for coin, blue for safe passage over water. Fever-charms sewn into felt squares, each one stitched with a pattern that meant nothing and everything, depending on what the buyer needed it to mean. Dried rowan berries strung on thread against nightmares. Little clay whistles shaped like wrens, which did nothing at all but made children happy, and happy children made mothers generous.

None of it was magic. That was the whole point. None of it could hang him.

He had learned, in the early years, how close the line ran. A charm worked true left a warmth in it, a faint hum under the fingers, like a struck bell long after the sound had gone. Buyers sometimes said they could feel it. So he had taught himself to make things that felt warm without being warm — wool dyed with saffron so it held the sun's heat past sundown, knots pulled tight in a specific order so that the tension in them seemed almost alive. Craft, not craft. The difference between a lie and a performance of a lie, and Rosalind had become very good at both.

"Three coppers for the green," he said to the woman now leaning over his table, a cooper's wife with flour still on her sleeves. "Five if you want it blessed."

"Blessed by who?"

"By me." He smiled the smile that cost him nothing anymore, because he had worn the grooves of it so deep it moved on its own, like a door on a well-oiled hinge. "I say the words my grandmother said. Whether anyone's listening is between you and the sky."

The woman laughed and paid five. Everyone in the fens beyond the city walls knew the Hartwell name had once meant something — cunning-women, hedge-healers, the sort of folk who came when the midwife couldn't and left before the priest arrived. His grandmother had been the last of them openly. After her, the family learned silence the way other families learned trades.

Rosalind had learned it young, and then he had learned it again, harder, at nineteen, when he fled Master Aldous Verel's house in the hill country with nothing but the clothes he wore and a scar across his palm he told people came from a kitchen knife. Five years now. Five years of selling luck to people who had none, and fever-charms to parents who would pay anything, anything at all, for one more night of their child breathing easy. And every time he took the copper and handed over the felt square, some quiet part of him whispered what he never let rise to his lips: *I could make this work. I could make it truly work. One word, half a breath, and the fever breaks.*

He never said it. He thought it, though. He thought it every single night, and the thinking of it was its own slow punishment, a thirst with a river running past the mouth.

* * *

The market thickened around seven bells. Steam rose off the chestnut cart. Somewhere a fiddler played a tune about a drowned sailor, badly, and nobody minded. Rosalind sold a blue knot to a ferryman, two fever-charms to a grandmother with a coughing grandchild — she haggled him down to four coppers and he let her, because he could see the worry in her face and worry deserved mercy — and a wren-whistle to a boy missing his front teeth, who immediately blew it until his mother confiscated it.

This was the life. This was the whole of it. A stall, a cloth, a stock of harmless charms, a room above a chandler's shop on Tanner's Lane that smelled of tallow and damp, a friend who brought him bread on Thursdays and never asked why he flinched when soldiers passed. Tamsin Ferro, lamplighter, twenty-three years old, with forearms like cable from hauling the lighting-pole and a laugh that could be heard across the whole of Cooper's Row. She had decided, somewhere in their second year of friendship, that Rosalind was a man with a past he didn't discuss, and she had decided this was fine. People were allowed their locked rooms. She had one herself. Everyone in Vellamere did, if you looked.

He loved her for it. He loved her the way you love the person who makes your exile livable — fiercely, quietly, and without ever once letting the love show clearly enough to be dangerous. Because everything in Rosalind's life was measured against danger now. Everything. The way he spoke, the way he stood, the names he used, the ones he avoided. He had a story ready for every question a stranger might ask, rehearsed until the answers came smooth as poured honey. Where are you from? *Southreach, before the floods.* Your trade? *Charm-seller, third generation.* Family? *A sister in Southreach, we don't write.* All lies, or nearly all, and he kept them oiled and working the way a miller keeps his stones.

It was exhausting. That was the thing nobody warned you about — not the fear, exactly, though there was plenty of that, but the sheer unending labor of being someone else. Every conversation was a performance. Every friendship had a ceiling built into it, a height past which it could not climb, because past that height lay questions, and past the questions lay the truth, and the truth was a rope with a noose already tied in it. Magic was punishable by death in Vellamere. Not fines. Not branding. Death, by the Inquisitor General's own writ, and the writ was read aloud at every assizes so that no one could claim ignorance.

So Rosalind lived small. He lived carefully. He lived a life the size of a stall and a rented room, and he told himself, most nights, that this was wisdom, and some nights he almost believed it.

Almost.

* * *

The clerk appeared at half past eight.

Rosalind noticed him the way he noticed everything now — sideways, peripherally, without turning his head, because turning your head toward a man in grey-and-white livery was itself a kind of confession. The livery of the Office of Inquiry was distinctive: dove-grey doublet, white collar, a small pewter badge shaped like an open eye. Most people saw the badge and looked away quickly, the way you look away from a wound. Clerks went where Inquisitors sent them, taking down names, checking licenses, noting faces. They were the net before the net. The soft silk that preceded the wire.

This one was young. Twenty-five at most, narrow-faced, ink-stained fingers, a ledger under his arm with the swollen look of a book that got rained on once and dried crooked. He drifted along the row the way clerks drift, pausing at stalls without buying, asking small questions — *and how long have you held this pitch, sir? And is your license current?* — and writing the answers down in a hand so neat it looked printed.

At the chestnut cart. At the fiddler. At the fishmonger, whose lamp was out, which the clerk noted, because the clerk noted everything, that was his entire purpose in the world.

And then he came to Rosalind's stall, and stopped, and did not move on.

"Evening," Rosalind said, cheerful, easy, the voice of a man with nothing behind his eyes but felt squares and thread.

"Evening." The clerk set his ledger on the corner of the trestle, squared it, opened it. "Name?"

"Hartwell. Rosalind Hartwell." The name rolled out of him without friction. He had said it ten thousand times. It fit him the way a glove fits a hand that has worn it for years — snug, broken-in, and entirely not his skin.

"And you hold a vendor's license, Mr. Hartwell?"

"I do. Number fourteen-oh-six, renewed at Michaelmas." He produced the paper from the oilcloth sleeve beneath the table before the clerk finished asking. Speed mattered. Hesitation was a smell, and dogs of the law could smell it.

The clerk copied the number. Then he did something Rosalind had not seen a clerk do in five years of markets: he picked up one of the luck-knots.

He turned it over. Ran his thumb along the weave. Held it up to the lamplight — the good lamp, the one over Rosalind's own stall, which burned steady because Rosalind trimmed and fed it himself every dusk, because a charm-seller whose own light guttered invited comment.

"These are pretty," the clerk said. "What's in them?"

"Thread, mostly." Rosalind kept his tone light, almost bored, the tone of a man asked about his trade for the thousandth tedious time. "Wool core, linen wrap. Dyed with madder and weld. Nothing in them but thread and patience."

"Mm." The clerk set the knot down, then picked up another. Then a fever-charm. He examined the stitching closely, tilting it, counting stitches almost — Rosalind watched him count, or seem to count, and felt the old cold slide up the back of his neck. "Interesting pattern. Where'd you learn it?"

"My grandmother." Smooth. Instant. True, even, in its way — the patterns *were* his grandmother's, or close enough to be indistinguishable, patterns she'd used for charms that *did* work, stripped now of everything but their shapes. "She sold at fairs down Southreach way. Family trade."

"Family trade," the clerk repeated, and wrote it down. He wrote slowly, deliberately, each letter placed like a stone in a wall. "Hartwell is a fen name, I think. Cunning-folk, the Hartwells. There was a record — I recall a record."

Rosalind's stomach tightened, but he kept his face pleasant, his hands busy, sorting knots that needed no sorting. "Wouldn't know, sir. We were poor folk. Poor folk don't leave records."

"Everyone leaves records." The clerk smiled — a thin, correct smile, the smile of a man who has been trained to put people at ease and knows precisely how much ease to permit. "Baptism rolls. Tithe books. Assize lists. The city remembers what families were, even when the families forget." He turned another knot over. Set it down. Aligned it with the others, neatly, fussily, so that the display looked better than it had before, which was somehow worse than if he'd mussed it. "You've been at this pitch five years, you said?"

"Four, sir. Renewed twice."

"Four years." Another note. "And in four years, no complaints? No accusations? No one claiming your charms did more than advertised?"

"No, sir." A beat, then, pitched just right, rueful and self-deprecating: "Mostly the complaint is they don't do enough. My grandmother's did better business, I'm told. But she had gifts. I've got thread."

The clerk looked up at that. Looked at him properly, for the first time — a long, level, unhurried look, the look of a man filing a face away the way other men file documents. Ink-stained fingers resting on the ledger. Grey eyes, pale as weather.

"You speak very carefully, Mr. Hartwell."

"Do I?" Rosalind laughed, and the laugh was perfect, and he hated how perfect it was. "Comes of selling to grandmothers. You learn to say things twice, nicely, or they take their custom elsewhere."

"Mm." The clerk closed the ledger. Picked it up. Stood there a moment longer than he needed to, in the way that was the whole message — *I could stay longer. I could come back. I am deciding whether you are worth my time.* "Cold tonight. Supposed to snow by week's end, they say. First real snow."

"Be good for the chestnut trade."

"It always is." The clerk touched the brim of his hat — a small courtesy, exact, mechanical — and moved on down the row, to the next stall, and the next, writing, writing, writing.

Rosalind exhaled through his nose, slow and controlled, the breath of a man letting a bowstring down without loosing it. His hands, he noticed, had gone cold despite the gloves. Cold and faintly trembling, the tremor he knew too well, the body's honest report filed against the face's false testimony.

He had handled it well. He knew he had handled it well. Every answer clean, every pause natural, every deflection wearing the costume of candor. Four years of practice, five, and the machinery of his deception ran smoother than ever.

And yet.

*Family trade,* the clerk had written. *Cunning-folk. There was a record.*

Somewhere in some registry office across the city, in a room Rosalind would never see, there was a page with his grandmother's name on it. Maybe his mother's. Maybe — the thought arrived like ice water — maybe a page with a boy's name on it, an apprentice enrolled nineteen years ago in the household of one Aldous Verel, licensed thaumaturge, whose license had been revoked six years back amid whispers Rosalind had fled rather than hear confirmed. If the clerk pulled that thread — if he connected a fen name to a revoked master to a runaway — then the careful architecture of Rosalind's small life was not architecture at all. It was scaffolding. And scaffolding exists only until the building comes down.

He packed early that night. Sold the last few knots to stragglers, folded the cloth, stacked the trestle, doused his lamp with two fingers, careful, always careful, leaving nothing burning behind him that might be reported as witch-fire. He walked home the long way, past the chandler's, up the outside stair, and sat in his dark room on the edge of his narrow bed with his boots still on, listening to the city settle.

Tanner's Lane was quiet. The chandler below snored like a tide coming in. Somewhere distant, a dog, and then nothing.

Rosalind sat in the dark and did the arithmetic he did every night and never once finished. Five years. Eighteen hundred nights of vigilance. How many more? Ten? Twenty? Until his hands shook too badly to tie a knot and some charitable soul found him frozen in a doorway, and they buried him under a name that wasn't his, and the truth of him went into the ground unwitnessed, unsung, a whole life spent holding a single breath?

He was twenty-four years old. He had been holding the breath since he was nineteen.

*One word,* the quiet part of him whispered, as it did every night, patient as water. *Half a breath. Light the candle without the match. Warm the room without the brazier. Just once. Just to remember what you are.*

He pressed his palms against his eyes until the dark bloomed red, and he did not answer the whisper, and the not-answering was the hardest work he did, harder than the market, harder than the lying, harder than all of it. A life half-lived. A fire banked so far down it had become indistinguishable from ash — except that ash, he knew, ash remembered. Ash held its shape. Under the right pressure, in the right dark, at the wrong moment, it glowed.

* * *

He slept badly, as he slept most nights, and woke before dawn to the sound of footsteps on the stair.

Not Tamsin's. Tamsin climbed stairs like a girl who carried a lighting-pole for a living — quick, heavy, two-at-a-time, announced from the bottom by a whistle she thought nobody recognized. These steps were different. Slow. Dragging. A pause on the fourth step, long enough that Rosalind, already upright with his knife in his hand, heard the wood groan and hold, groan and hold.

Then a weight came against his door. Not a knock. A slump, shoulder-first, the sound of a body that had run out of the strength to stand.

Rosalind opened the door on a man he had never seen before — young, mud to the knees, road-dirt to the collar, one hand pressed flat against his side where the blood came through his fingers in a rhythm too fast and too steady to be anything but bad. His eyes found Rosalind's and fixed there with the terrible clarity of the dying, and he said, before his legs gave and Rosalind caught him, catching blood as well as weight:

"The Choir must not sing for her."

Then he pressed something into Rosalind's palm — a roll of parchment, wax-sealed, warm from being carried against a beating heart — and said nothing else at all.
