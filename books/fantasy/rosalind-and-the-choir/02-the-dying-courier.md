## 2. The Dying Courier

The weight of a grown man is an argument no one wins. Rosalind went down under it with his shoulder against the doorframe and the man's blood soaking through his sleeve in a spreading warmth that smelled of iron and river-mud and something sharper beneath — spell-sickness, some old instinct whispered, though he had not let himself think in those terms in five years.

"Hey. Hey." He got the stranger's arm across his neck and hauled him over the threshold one staggering half-step at a time, kicking the door shut behind them with his heel. The latch caught. Small mercies. "You're in. You're inside. Sit down before we both—"

The man's knees folded on cue.

They ended up against the wall by the cold hearth, Rosalind cradling him half off the floorboards, and for a moment there was nothing to do but breathe. The room was small — one bed, one chair, one window with a view of the tannery roof and the grey slice of sky between Vellamere's eaves — and it had never felt smaller than it did now, holding another person's death in it like smoke.

The stranger was young. Younger than Rosalind had first taken him for; the road-dirt had aged him. Twenty at most, with a courier's cropped hair gone lank with sweat and a face that might have been handsome if someone had ever fed it enough. His hand was still pressed to his side, fingers white-knuckled, and between them the blood welled and welled, black in the dimness.

"You're bleeding out," Rosalind said stupidly. "Let me see."

The man's other hand clamped Rosalind's wrist. His grip was astonishing — feverish, desperate, the grip of a man spending his last coin of strength all at once.

"No healer," he rasped. "Swear it. Swear you'll send for no one."

"I wasn't going to—"

"*Swear.*"

Rosalind looked into his eyes and understood, with a cold sliding sensation, exactly what kind of wound this was. Not a knife-fight wound. Not a fall from a horse. This was the wound you got from people who wanted to know where you'd been and who you'd told, and had gotten impatient. The kind of wound that came with watchers behind it.

He thought of the clerk at the night market, three nights past, lingering over his luck-knots with those flat, patient eyes. The clerk whose name Rosalind had never learned because he had made very sure never to learn anything about anyone official, ever.

"I swear," Rosalind said. "No healers. No one."

The grip eased a fraction. The man's head tipped back against the wall. "Good," he breathed. "Good lad. Then listen. Listen fast, because I haven't got long, and she'll have eyes on this street already."

"Who's *she*? Who did this to you?"

But the man shook his head, slow, like a man refusing wine when he's already drunk. "Doesn't matter. Names are how they follow. You don't say my name. I don't say hers. That's how I got as far as I did." A ghost of something crossed his face — pride, maybe, or its corpse. "Three days ahead of them the whole way. Three days. Nobody's done that before."

"From where? Ahead of them from *where*?"

The man smiled. It cost him visibly. "North. Out of the fens." His hand slid from Rosalind's wrist down to the parchment roll still clenched between them, crushed half-flat in the crush of their bodies. He worked it loose with trembling fingers and pressed it back into Rosalind's palm, folding Rosalind's own fingers closed around it as if teaching a child to hold a spoon.

"The Choir must not sing for her," he said again, and now that the panic of arrival had drained away, the words landed differently. Slower. Heavier. Like a bell struck once and left ringing. "Whatever she offers you. Whatever she promises. She'll make it sound like mercy. She's very good at mercy." His breath hitched, wet. "It isn't mercy. It's just a different cage with prettier bars."

"I don't know what any of this is," Rosalind said. "I sell charms. Luck-knots. Fever-strings. I don't—" He heard his own voice do the thing it always did, flatten and dull itself, sand the grain out of the words until they were the voice of a man who had never held anything more dangerous than dyed thread. Five years of practice. It came out smooth as water and he hated himself for how smooth.

The courier studied him. Even dying, even grey-lipped, there was a long assessing moment in which Rosalind felt seen straight through — felt the flattened voice, the sanded words, picked up and turned over like a counterfeit coin.

"Yes," the man said softly. "You do know what it is."

* * *

Afterward, Rosalind could never reconstruct the order of things. There was water, fetched from the jug and held to cracked lips; most of it ran down the man's chin. There was the fire, coaxed alight from banked embers with shaking hands, because dying men go cold and cruelty was not among Rosalind's vices. There were questions asked and mostly unanswered, the courier rationing his strength like a miser rationing coin.

What he gave, he gave in pieces.

"They burned the safehouses in Marrowgate first," he said, staring at the fire. "Then the fen-stations. Anyone who carried for the old network — gone or running. I'm the last one who made it out with the cargo." His mouth twisted. "*Cargo.* They called it that. As if it were salt."

"What network? Carrying for whom?"

"Aldous Vey." The name dropped into the room like a stone into still water, and the ripples went through Rosalind so hard he nearly dropped the cup.

His master. His dead master. The man who had taught him to knot a charm so it would hold, and then taught him, harder lesson, to unlearn everything else — to walk small, speak flat, want nothing. The man who had sent him south to Vellamere with a purse, a false name, and a final instruction: *Never write to me. Never come back. If I find you, I will not know you.*

*Aldous Vey is dead,* Rosalind said aloud, and heard how the flat voice cracked anyway.

"So was I, an hour ago," the courier said, "and here I am ruining your floor." He coughed then, long and deep, and brought up blood, and Rosalind held him through it and felt the ribs move wrong beneath his hands. When it passed, the courier sagged, and his voice came thinner. "He's alive. Or he was. I carried to him and I carried from him, and the seal on what you're holding is his. You'd know that if you looked. Have you looked?"

"No."

"Liar," the courier said, without heat. "Everyone looks. But you haven't opened it, have you. Wax unbroken?" Rosalind nodded. Something eased in the man's face. "Good. Because it's keyed. Opened by the wrong hands, it goes blank. He told me. He said — he said the one who carries it has to be someone who understands why we hide." His eyes found Rosalind's again, and there was apology in them, terrible and unmistakable. "I'm sorry. I looked for anywhere else. There wasn't time. And when I saw you at the market — the way you work your knots — I knew. I knew what you are."

The word hung between them, unspoken, enormous.

*I am nothing,* Rosalind almost said. *I am a charm-seller. I am careful. I have been so careful.*

What he said was: "You should rest."

"There's no rest coming for me, lad. Spend the time on yourself instead." The courier shifted, grimacing, and pressed his back more firmly to the wall as if he could hold himself upright by will alone. Outside, somewhere down the lane, a dog barked twice and stopped, and both of them flinched, and neither acknowledged the flinching. "Listen. The map shows the road north. Through Mireholt, up the fen causeways, to the Hollow Choir. You'll know the place from the stories if you grew up anywhere near the marsh country. Every child does."

"The vault," Rosalind said slowly. "The one from the war. The one where they put the—"

"Don't say its name either. Names are how they follow." A thin, exhausted laugh. "Gods, you sound like him. Aldous used to say that. *Names are how they follow.* Drove us all mad with it." The laugh died. "Here's what matters. The Inquisitor General wants what I carry. Her clerks, her hunters, her — she has ways of finding things that aren't natural, and I don't mean magic, because she hates magic worse than anyone breathing. I mean money. Patience. Cruelty, well-organized. She'll trace me. She'll trace the market. She'll trace *you*, because I'm dying in your room and dead men leave debts."

"Then help me move you. There's a chandler two lanes over, he owes me—"

"And put this on some poor chandler's floor instead? No." The courier's hand found Rosalind's sleeve again, weak now, the grip of a child rather than a soldier. "Listen to me. Two roads in front of you. Burn the map and stay hidden, and pray they decide a dead courier is a closed account — they won't, but pray. Or take the road north and get ahead of her. Put the thing where she can't reach it, or give it back to the man who sealed it, or — I don't know. That part was never mine to carry." His breath rattled. "Only this much was: *the Choir must not sing for her.* Say it back."

"The Choir must not sing for her."

"Again. So it's in your bones, not your ears."

"The Choir must not sing for her." And it was strange, saying it a second time, how the words changed — how they stopped being a stranger's delirium and became something lodged in him, a splinter working deeper with each repetition.

"Good lad." The courier closed his eyes. For a moment Rosalind thought he'd gone, and lurched forward, and the man's mouth twitched. "Not yet. Annoying, isn't it. Everyone assumes." He opened his eyes again, and they were clearer than they had been since the door — clear the way the sky is clear before snow. "One more thing. The friend question."

"What friend question?"

"You've got someone. Everyone like us has someone. The lamplighter girl who buys fever-charms for the widow on Candle Lane and pays with gossip instead of copper. Don't look shocked, I watch doors, it's the trade." His smile was faint and sad. "When they trace you — and they will — they'll look at her too. So whatever you choose: burn it, run it, bury it in the fens and stand on top of it — do it *fast*. Every hour you spend deciding is an hour she spends reading your life like a ledger."

The fire popped. Down in the street, the tanner's cart rattled past on its morning round, ordinary as bread, and Rosalind listened to it fade and thought: *this is the last ordinary sound I will hear as an ordinary man.* He didn't know why he was sure of that. He simply was, the way you're sure of weather in your joints.

"I didn't ask for any of this," he said.

"Nobody does. That's how you know it's real work and not ambition." The courier's eyes drifted toward the window, the grey light strengthening there. Snow, Rosalind realized. The first flakes of the year, fat and slow, drifting past the glass. "Look at that. Made it to winter after all." He said it like a victory.

* * *

He lasted four hours.

Rosalind spent them the way you spend time with the dying — badly, usefully, in a fog of small tasks. He propped the man up so the blood could pool less. He boiled water and cleaned what could be cleaned, and the wound beneath the man's hand confirmed what he'd already known: a thrust, deep, angled up and in, made by someone who knew exactly what they were doing and had done it before. He fed the fire. He sat close enough that the man could grip his forearm during the worst of the shivering, and the grip came often, and each time it loosened a little less returned.

In between, the courier talked in fragments, drifting. A sister in Marrowgate — *don't look for her, that's an order, looking is how they find.* A horse lost at the fen's edge — *best animal I ever stole.* The taste of green plums, for no reason at all. Twice he asked Rosalind's name, and twice Rosalind lied, giving the flat market name he wore like a coat, and the second time the courier looked at him with something like approval and said, "Still lying to a dead man. He trained you well," and laughed until it hurt.

Near the end, the talking stopped altogether. The shivering stopped too, which Rosalind knew was worse. The courier's breathing went long and spaced, like footsteps growing further apart down a corridor, and his eyes fixed on the window where the snow was thickening now, laying its first white skin over the tannery roof, softening the edges of the world.

"It's beautiful," he said. "That's the trick of it. It's all beautiful, right up until—"

He didn't finish. His hand, resting in Rosalind's, tightened once — a whole life compressed into a single squeeze, or so it seemed — and then it was only a hand, heavy and cooling, and the long corridor had reached its door.

Rosalind sat with him a while. He didn't weep. He noted that he didn't weep, distantly, as a fact about a stranger, and understood that the part of him that would have wept had been buried years ago, on the road south, at his master's instruction, alongside everything else. Later, perhaps. Later there would be time. Or there wouldn't, and that was the shape of the life he'd chosen.

The practicalities arrived on schedule, grim and insistent. A dead man in a rented room. A landlady downstairs with sharp ears and rent due at week's end. A body that could not be found here, not now, not with Inquisitorial clerks already sniffing along the night market like hounds along a hedge.

He worked through the afternoon. There was an abandoned kiln by the tannery wall, disused since the plague year, its access a collapsed stretch of fence that no one inspected. Getting the courier there took every ounce of strength and cunning Rosalind had, wrapped in a rug, moved in stages while the snow covered tracks faster than he could make them — the first kindness the winter had shown anyone in this story. He laid the man out decently. He had no rites to give; mages' rites were outlawed along with everything else, and besides, he'd never learned them. So he did the only thing his hands remembered how to do.

He knotted.

Quick, small, wordless — a traveler's knot, the kind hawkers tie into a dead man's collar in the marsh country so his spirit finds the road home instead of circling the fens. It was half superstition, half craft, and not one drop of true magic in it, and his fingers shook the entire time, and when it was done he stood in the falling snow in a ruined kiln beside a stranger who had trusted him with a kingdom's worth of terrible cargo, and he felt — nothing he could name. Hollowed. Scoured. Awake in a way he hadn't been in five years and would gladly have traded away.

*Names are how they follow,* he reminded himself, walking back through the whitening streets with his hood up and his heart slamming. He had given the courier no name. The courier had given him none in return. There was nothing to trace except a rented room and a night-market stall and a lamplighter girl with a laugh like—

Like Tamsin's.

He stopped in the middle of Copperlane, snow gathering on his shoulders, and made himself think it through, because the courier had told him to think fast and dead men's instructions deserve keeping. *She'll read your life like a ledger.* The stall. The room. The landlady's ledger with its dates and sums. Tamsin, who came by every third evening with bread and complaints, whose name was written in his own handwriting on a charm-tag he'd given her last spring — *For Tam, so the dark stays friendly* — tucked inside her coat, next to her heart, where she kept everything she loved.

Anyone who traced the courier to the market could trace the market to him, and him to her, in an evening's work.

Back in the room, the blood had soaked into the floorboards between the cracks, and no amount of scrubbing would lift it entirely. The parchment roll lay on the table where he'd left it, wax-seal intact, snow-light gleaming dully along its curve. It looked so small. It had killed a man — or the carrying of it had — and it lay there like a sleeping bird, and Rosalind stood over it for a long time, hearing his master's voice as clearly as if Aldous Vey stood behind him: *Want nothing. Know nothing. Be no one.*

He picked it up.

He meant to throw it in the fire. He had the fire stoked high for exactly that purpose, and his arm actually drew back, and the seal was warm against his palm — warm, absurdly, as if freshly carried, as if the dead man's heartbeat still lived in the wax — and through the paper he could feel the faintest texture beneath, fine lines, dense and deliberate, a hand he almost recognized.

Almost.

He lowered his arm. He did not open it. He wrapped it instead in oilcloth and hid it beneath the loose board under the bed, among the honest junk of a dishonest life, and then he sat on the edge of the mattress with his head in his hands as the snow came down harder over Vellamere, sealing the city's streets and the kiln and the dead man and the whole widening circle of consequences under white silence.

*Burn it and stay,* said one voice, his master's voice.

*Run north, fast, before she reads the ledger,* said another, younger and colder and still cooling in a ruined kiln by the tannery.

And beneath both, faint as the hum in a struck bell that hasn't finished ringing, a third voice he didn't recognize at all — his own, maybe, five years buried — whispering the courier's warning back at him like a prayer he hadn't chosen:

*The Choir must not sing for her.*

Outside, the first knock came at the door below — the landlady, probably, or a neighbor, or the snow slipping off the eaves. Probably.

Probably.

Rosalind held his breath and counted the seconds, and did not answer.
