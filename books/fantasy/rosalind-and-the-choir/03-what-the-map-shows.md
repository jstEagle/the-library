## 3. What the Map Shows

The candle had burned down to a stub by the time Rosalind dared to unroll the map.

He had done everything else first. Bolted the door. Wedged a chair beneath the knob, though he knew the gesture was theater — if the Inquisition wanted in, a chair would stop them about as long as a sigh. He had drawn the curtain over his one window, checked the gap behind the wardrobe where a boy could hide and a grown man could not, and set his little iron kettle on the brazier not for tea but for the sound of it, the small domestic hiss that made a room feel inhabited by someone innocent. Only then, with the dead courier's blood still ghost-staining the seam of his floorboards where he'd scrubbed it twice, did he carry the wax-sealed roll to the table and sit before it like a man sitting before a confession he hadn't yet made.

The seal had cracked when the courier died. Rosalind had felt it give under his thumb that first terrible night, a small surrendering click, and had hated himself for how quickly his fingers moved after that — how little ceremony grief got from him. Now he peeled the last flakes of red wax away and flattened the parchment with both palms, weighting the corners with his thimble, a spoon, a chipped clay button he'd never thrown out because it had come from Tamsin's coat.

The map was drawn in brown-black ink on skin-fine vellum, and it showed the fen country north of Vellamere: the drowned meadows, the black alder stands, the frozen meres rendered as delicate stippled circles like frost on glass. A route wound through them in fine dotted line, beginning at the Marsh Gate and ending at a symbol Rosalind did not need a legend to read — a circle of small upright marks, seven of them, arranged around an eighth point at the center.

Standing stones. He knew that arrangement. Every child raised within a hundred miles of the fens knew it, the way children in other places knew the shapes of wolves or the sound of floodwater. It was the sign old women drew in spilled flour to frighten them, the shape carved into the lintel above the Marsh Gate itself, half worn away, half forbidden. The Hollow Choir.

*The Choir must not sing for her.* The courier's words came back with their load of blood still attached. Rosalind pressed his knuckles to his mouth and looked at the map until the looking became its own kind of endurance.

Because there was something worse than the destination. There was the hand that had drawn it.

He recognized it the way you recognize a voice in a dark room — before thought, before permission. The tall ascenders looping backward at their tips. The way the *r*s were built like little leaning gates. The numeral seven crossed with two strokes, always two, never one, because Aldous Verel had said once, laughing, that a single-stroked seven invited sloppiness into every number that followed it. Five years since Rosalind had seen that handwriting. Five years since he'd stood in a doorway at dawn with a packed bag and a master who would not look at him, and heard him say *go south, boy, and be small* — and then watched the letter arrive three months later announcing Aldous Verel's death, peaceful, expected, his effects dispersed, his apprenticeship dissolved without a body ever mentioned, because there had been no body to mention, and Rosalind had been too young and too frightened to ask why.

His master's hand lay all over this map. Not just the route — the marginalia. Tiny annotations in that gate-built script crowded the edges: *ice sound by third moonrise; do not trust the causeway at Mireholt; the ward-stones remember their tuning.* And along the bottom border, written so small Rosalind had to hold the parchment to the candle, a single line that made his stomach drop through the floor:

*Rosalind — if it has reached you, then I am beyond apologizing, which I expect will not surprise you.*

He set the map down. He stood up. He walked to the window and back, four paces each way in a room barely eight feet long, because his body needed to move even while the rest of him refused to understand.

Aldous was alive. Or had been alive when this was sealed. Either his master had faked his own death five years ago and let Rosalind grieve him alone in a southern city where he knew no one — or someone had learned to forge the most distinctive hand Rosalind had ever known, down to the double-crossed sevens, well enough to fool the one person on earth who'd spent six years reading it.

Both possibilities were unbearable. He kept picking them up alternately, like two stones of equal weight, unable to decide which hurt more.

And underneath both, quieter and colder, ran the courier's warning. *Her.* Someone wanted the Choir to sing. Someone for whom that would be a catastrophe — or a coronation.

Rosalind sat back down and forced himself to study the route properly, because fear was no excuse for ignorance; Aldous had drilled that into him before anything else. *Understand the thing that frightens you, boy. Fear is just ignorance wearing armor.*

The route avoided every road. Of course it did — roads had tolls, tolls had clerks, and clerks fed the Inquisitorial registries. Instead the dotted line threaded the fen proper: across the reed-flats below Saltmere, along a frozen channel marked only *the old singing cut*, past a waypoint labeled with a tiny drawing of a heron standing on ice. Two days' travel, perhaps three in snow. For a trained walker in winter, hard but possible. For a runaway charm-seller who hadn't left Vellamere in five years —

For him, it was suicide dressed as a journey.

Unless he wasn't meant to walk it. Unless the map wasn't meant for his feet at all, but simply for his eyes — a message, a summons, a hook baited with his master's handwriting and cast into the night market knowing that somewhere in the crowd, a dying man would find him. Except the courier hadn't looked like part of a plan. He'd looked like a man leaking his life onto Rosalind's floor, and plans didn't bleed like that.

Or they did, if the planner was good enough.

Rosalind caught himself smiling at the thought — a thin, bitter expression he didn't recognize as his own — and stopped. This was what vigilance did to a person after five years. It turned every kindness into a mechanism, every accident into design. He was becoming the kind of mind the Inquisitors wanted everyone to have, seeing plots in weather.

But the handwriting remained. The double-crossed sevens remained.

He should take it to the Inquisitors. The thought arrived dressed in reason: he was innocent, he'd merely received a dying man's parcel, better he surrendered it than be found holding it. But he had lived in Vellamere long enough to know what happened to innocents who walked into the Inquisitorial Hall carrying mage-worked things. Innocence was a rumor there, not a defense. The question was never *what do you have*, only *how long have you had it, and why didn't you report it sooner*. He could see the clerk's face already — the same clerk from the night market, maybe, the one who'd lingered over his luck-knots with eyes like a man counting coins he intended to collect later.

No. Not the Inquisitors.

Then destroy it.

It was the obvious answer, the clean one. Burn the map, keep the memory — no, burn the memory too, refuse the whole inheritance, go on selling fever-charms woven from nothing but string and confidence until some ordinary death took him. That was the life he'd chosen at nineteen. That was the bargain: *be small, be safe.* Aldous's own instruction, and here was Aldous's own hand reaching across five years to break it.

Rosalind took the candle.

He held the corner of the vellum to the flame the way you'd hold a hand over a fire — close enough to feel the heat, committed to the pain. Vellum catches readily. Everyone knows this. He'd seen documents burn; he'd once watched an Inquisitorial pyre consume a cartload of confiscated grimoires, and the smoke had smelled sweetly, horribly of knowledge going up.

The flame licked the corner of the map and slid off.

Not resisted — *slid*, the way water slides off oiled cloth. The fire touched the vellum and flowed around it, curling against the surface like a living tongue finding a taste it refused. Rosalind pressed harder, tilting the wick directly against the parchment until the flame bent sideways and guttered, and still the corner stayed cool and pale in his fingers. When he pulled the candle away, the fire shrank back to its wick almost sulkily, and the map bore no scorch, no browning, not even the smell of heat. It lay on his table looking freshly made.

His hands were shaking. He noticed it distantly, as if the hands belonged to a colleague he was observing for signs of spellcraft.

Warded parchment. He knew the theory — every apprentice did, though actually casting such a working was far beyond hedge-craft; it was deep-magic, covenant-grade, the sort of working that took a circle of mages or one very great one. Fire-warding layered into the fibers themselves, keyed to persist until the holder chose to release it. Which meant the map could only be destroyed by the person it trusted.

Which meant, some cold arithmetic concluded in the back of his skull, that the map trusted *him*.

Rosalind sat very still for a long time. The candle burned. Somewhere below, the building settled and groaned against the cold, and a cart passed in the street with that particular squeak of a wheel needing grease, and the world went on being ordinary outside his window while inside it, the shape of his life quietly rearranged itself.

He thought about the stories. That was the danger, and he knew it even as he did it — he went looking for the childhood tales the way a tongue goes to a broken tooth. The Hollow Choir, the buried vault where the seven sealers had sung the Emberstone silent and ended the mage war at the price of everything. His grandmother's version had the sealers turning to stone themselves, still singing, voices locked in the rock. The lamplighters' version — Tamsin's version — said the vault flooded every solstice and anyone who heard the song would hear their own true name spoken back to them. The Inquisitorial version, printed in cheap pamphlets, said there was nothing there at all, that the Choir was a fable mages told to make themselves seem descended from greatness.

Seven versions in seven mouths, and not one of them agreed except on this: something enormous slept under the fens, and everyone agreed it should stay asleep.

*The Choir must not sing for her.*

Rosalind looked at the map — at the patient dotted line, at the seven stones and the eighth point, at the gate-built *r*s marching along the margins in his dead-alive master's hand — and felt the dread in his chest undergo a slow, horrifying transmutation. It was still dread. But braided through it now, unwelcome and undeniable, was fascination. Curiosity. And beneath even that, in a register he hadn't visited in five years, a faint low hum of recognition, like an instrument remembering a note.

He was, he realized, already planning the journey. Some part of him had begun planning it the moment he'd read the marginalia, and had continued working all through the burning and the shaking and the grief — checking his coin, weighing his winter coat, calculating the Marsh Gate's watch rotations. The traitor part. The part that had never entirely believed in being small.

"No," he said aloud, to the room, to himself. His voice sounded thin in the cold.

The map said nothing. Warded parchment is a patient listener.

He rolled it closed. He did not reseal it — there was nothing left to seal it with, and besides, the seal had never really been the lock; he understood now that *he* was the lock, that the whole thing had been addressed to him in ink only he could read rightly. He wrapped it in his spare shirt instead and knelt, prying up the loose floorboard by the bed where he kept his emergency coin, and laid it in the dark beneath the floor of the city that wanted him dead for what he was.

The board went back down. He sat on his bed and listened to his own heartbeat and told himself, with the flat insistence of a charm repeated too many times, that he was not going. That he would sell the map — no. That he would leave it. That in a week it would be a strange story he told no one, and in a month the handwriting would fade from his memory the way all dangerous things faded when you starved them.

He was still telling himself this when the knocking started.

Three blows on the door downstairs — the outer door, the street door — measured and unhurried, the knock of someone with authority and all night to use it. Then a pause exactly long enough to be courteous, and three blows more.

Rosalind rose without deciding to. His body knew the choreography of inspection: snuff the candle, straighten the blanket, arrange the face. He moved through the ritual with the smoothness of long practice, and only when his hand was already on the bolt of his own door did he register the detail that turned his blood to fen-water.

Whoever stood below had knocked at the *street* door. But the stairs to his rented room began inside that entryway — meaning the knock was not for the house's other tenants, of whom there were none at this hour, or for a wrong address, which no official ever admitted to.

Three floors down, a voice spoke politely to the darkness, testing the quiet. It was a voice Rosalind had heard only once before, across a night-market stall, asking gentle questions about luck-knots.

"Master Hartwell?" it called up the stairwell, warm as banked coals. "I wonder if I might trouble you for a moment. About your visitor."

* * *

The clerk waited on the landing, hat in hand, snow melting on his shoulders, smiling the smile of a man who has already read every document in the building.

"I do apologize for the hour," he said. "But you'll want to hear this while it's fresh."

Behind him, at the mouth of the stairwell, stood two men in plain gray wool who were not trying to look like anything other than what they were.

Rosalind's hand rested lightly on the banister, above the floorboard, above the shirt-wrapped silence of his master's handwriting — and he smiled back, and lied with his whole face, and stepped aside to let them in.
