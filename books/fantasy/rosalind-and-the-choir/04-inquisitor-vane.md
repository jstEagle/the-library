## 4. Inquisitor Vane

The candle had burned down to a thumb's width before Sera Vane found the discrepancy.

She sat in the archive beneath the Inquisitorial Hall, a room the clerics called the Bonecell because the ledgers stacked along its walls looked — in bad light, to tired eyes — like ribs. It was past midnight. Her tea had gone cold an hour ago, and her right hand ached where the old scar pulled across the knuckles, as it always did when the weather turned. Outside, the first true cold of winter pressed against the high slit windows, and somewhere above her the city of Vellamere slept under a sky the color of wet slate.

Vane did not sleep well in winter. She had not slept well in any season for nineteen years, but winter was worst, because winter was when it had happened.

So she read. She always read. The registries of Vellamere were her vigil: births and deaths, licenses and levies, the great slow bookkeeping of two hundred thousand lives. Most Inquisitors delegated the tedium to under-clerks and trusted the informers. Vane trusted no one's eyes but her own, because she had learned — at fourteen, kneeling in the ashes of her family's house on Corver Street — that catastrophe does not announce itself. It arrives wearing ordinary clothes. It signs its name in ordinary ink. It is only visible afterward, in the margins, if someone is patient enough to look.

The discrepancy was small. That was what made it interesting.

A courier of the Grayline Post, one Merrick Dole by name, licensed for fen routes, had died four days ago in the tenement warrens off Cooper's Row. Fever, said the parish register. But the burial record told another story: the body had been committed to the ground within six hours of death, without a physician's certificate, and the fee had been paid by an unnamed benefactor through the parish clerk. And the coroner's ledger — the third book, the one almost nobody cross-referenced against the other two — recorded that when the watch finally reached the room, the corpse was already gone into the earth, and the landlady's description of the death included a detail she had evidently thought unremarkable.

*He was bleeding from places bleeding don't come from,* the woman had said, according to the watchman's transcription. *And there was a smell. Like a forge left banked.*

Fever did not bleed a man from his palms. A forge-smell meant burned blood, and burned blood meant one thing only in Vellamere, whatever the law pretended: power spent too fast, or spent badly, or spent to hide itself.

Vane closed the three ledgers, aligned their edges precisely, and sat for a moment with her hands flat on the covers, feeling the old cold move through her chest like water through a broken wall.

*There you are,* she thought. Not a person yet. Not a face. Just a thread, sticking up out of the fabric of the city, waiting for a patient hand.

She lit a fresh candle from the stub of the old one, took out a clean sheet of paper, and began to write.

* *

By morning she had requisitioned everything the Grayline Post knew about Merrick Dole, which was nearly nothing, which was itself informative.

Dole had been a reliable man for eleven years — the sort the Post used for sensitive parcels because he asked no questions and drank exactly enough and never more. His route book showed a routine circuit down into the fen country: Mireholt waystation, the eel-camps, the drowned villages where the crown collected its thin rents and forgot the people between collections. He had returned early from his last circuit. Two days early. And his return had been logged not at the Grayline office but at the south tollgate, at dawn, on foot, though his route book listed him as mounted.

Something had made him hurry. Something had made him afraid. And then something had killed him in a rented room in Cooper's Row while a luck-charm seller lived one floor below.

Vane learned about the charm-seller by mid-morning, standing in the Grayline's counting-house while the postmaster sweated and wrung his cap and assured her that Dole had been a good man, a quiet man, no trouble ever.

"The tenant below," Vane said. "Who keeps the register for that building?"

"It's — a lodging house, madam Inquisitor, the rooms let by the week, mostly to tradesfolk—"

"The register."

He produced it with hands that trembled slightly, whether from fear of her or fear of what she might write in it, she neither knew nor cared. She ran her finger down the column. *R. Hartwell, upper back room. Charms, sundries.* Paid weekly, always on time, no visitors recorded, no disturbances recorded. Five years in the same room. Five years of perfect, unremarkable silence.

Perfect silence was its own kind of noise, if you listened long enough.

"Charms," she said. "What manner of charms?"

"Luck-knots, madam. Fever-strings. The little things peddlars sell at the night market. Knotted cord and dried herbs, nothing—" He caught himself. "I'm sure it's nothing."

"Everything is nothing," Vane said, "until it isn't." She copied Hartwell's name into her notebook in her small, exact hand. "Has this tenant come to report the death? A body died directly below his floor. A curious neighbor would have been at the watchhouse by now."

The postmaster blinked. "I — I couldn't say, madam."

"Find out. Quietly. If anyone asks why, tell them the Inquisitory is auditing burial fees." This was true in the technical sense that all her lies were true in some technical sense; deception, done properly, was just bookkeeping with the columns rearranged.

She walked back across the city rather than take a chair, because walking was thinking, and Vellamere in early winter rewarded both. Frost silvered the gutters. Lamplighters were going along the avenues snuffing the dawn-burned wicks, and one of them, a young woman with soot on her cheek, nodded to her the way citizens nodded to the Inquisitorial gray coat — respectfully, and with their eyes already moving away. Vane was used to it. Fear kept order better than love did, and she had stopped minding being feared around the same year she'd stopped sleeping.

Her mind kept returning to the detail that didn't fit, turning it over like a coin she suspected of being clipped.

Merrick Dole had bled from impossible places and smelled of a banked forge. That meant magic — but whose? If a mage had killed him, why bury him so fast, so carefully, paying through a third hand? Murderers of that kind left bodies in ditches. And if Dole himself had carried magic — a hedge-talent running messages down the fens, where the law was thinner than the ice — then who had he been running messages *for*, and what had the message been worth dying over?

And beneath all of it, patient as sediment, the older question stirred: the question she had been asking since Corver Street.

Nineteen years ago, a fire had taken her family's house in the space between one bell and the next. No spark, the investigators concluded. No lamp overturned, no stove cracked, no neighbor's chimney sharing flame. Just a house full of burning with no origin, and inside it her mother, her father, her little brother Tomas who had been learning letters and still wrote his *b* backwards — and outside it, at the corner of the street, a man in a plain brown coat watching the flames with an expression she could not read at fourteen and could read perfectly now, because she had seen it again and again in nineteen years of hunting.

Not horror. *Satisfaction.*

The Inquisitors of that day had called it a chimney flaw and closed the file. Vane had reopened it herself, at twenty-two, and found in the margin of the original survey a note in a dead archivist's hand: *witnesses spoke of green light in the upstairs window before the blaze.* Green light. Magefire. The color of power spent carelessly, or cruelly, or simply by someone who had never considered what a house full of sleeping people was worth against his own convenience.

That was the truth Vane had built her life inside: that magic was not a gift misused but a power whose misuse was structural, inevitable, the way a river floods. Every hedge-witch selling fever-strings was one bad winter from being the man in the brown coat. She did not hate them. Hate required heat, and heat was imprecise. What she felt was colder and more durable than hate — the conviction of someone who has stood in ashes and understood, with total clarity, that the world contains a thing which will burn children for no reason at all unless someone stands in its path forever.

The law agreed with her. Spellcraft punishable by death; the Edict of Ashes, signed in the same year as her family's burning, as if the crown had finally heard what she had heard. Nineteen years she had served that law, and in nineteen years she had executed forty-one convicted practitioners and never once lost sleep over a single one — and had lost whole weeks of sleep over the ones she'd suspected and could not prove. Because the proof was always the problem. Magic hid. It had to; that was its nature now, crouched in cellars and night markets, wearing the costume of knots and herbs and harmless trade.

Which was why discrepancies mattered. Magic lied, but bookkeeping did not. A courier buried too fast. A tenant too silent. A route ended two days early. Somewhere in the arithmetic between those numbers was a face, and behind the face a purpose, and behind the purpose — perhaps, if the frost was kind — the proof that would let her cut this particular cancer clean.

She arrived at the Hall as the ninth bell rang and sent for three things: the parish clerk who had handled Dole's burial, the watchman who had transcribed the landlady's account, and the registry of licensed peddlars for the Cooper's Row district, last five years.

Then she sat down at her desk, opened her notebook to a fresh page, and drew a line down the middle. On the left she wrote *HARTWELL, R. — charm-seller — 5 yrs, Cooper's Row.* On the right, after a moment, she wrote *DOLE, M. — courier — fen routes — died bleeding, buried fast.*

Between them she drew a single connecting stroke, thin as a wire.

It was not evidence. She knew that. Two names near each other proved nothing; coincidence was the static of every investigation, and the impatient hunter chased static into swamps. Patience was the discipline. Let the picture assemble itself. Feed it light.

But her scarred hand ached, and the ache had a rhythm to it today, and Vane had learned long ago to trust her body's weather over her mind's arguments. Something was moving under the ice of the city. She intended to be standing on it when it broke through.

* *

The parish clerk came at noon: a soft man named Ellery who smelled of ink and onions and who grew progressively damper with perspiration as the interview went on.

"I paid the fee, madam, that's all. A man came to my window, said the family wished the poor fellow put down quick, no ceremony, they'd cover costs. Happens often enough. Charity."

"What man?"

"A stranger. Gray cloak. Kept his face turned." Ellery wiped his upper lip. "Left the coin wrapped in paper. I counted it, it was correct, and — and the parish prefers quick burials anyway, madam, in fever cases. Public health."

"Of course." Vane turned her notebook toward him. "Describe the stranger."

"Youngish. Gloves. Voice like — like gravel in a sack, if that means anything, he seemed to be forcing it low. And—" The clerk hesitated, and his hesitation was more honest than anything he'd said aloud. "He had an odd way about him, madam. When I handed back the receipt, our fingers touched on the paper, and I felt — a shock. Like wool in dry weather. Only stronger. I've wondered since if I imagined it."

Static. Dry-weather shock. A touch that stung.

Vane wrote it down without letting her pen speed up, without letting her breathing change, while inside her chest something old and cold leaned forward like a dog scenting wind. Static discharge was nothing. Wool and winter were nothing. But mages leaked, when they were frightened or hurried; the archives were full of testimony — lamps guttering, hair rising, milk souring in a jug on the sill — the small electrical sins that gave the guilty away. A courier dies of impossible bleeding. A stranger pays to bury him deep and fast, gloves on his hands, voice forced low, and leaves a sting in a clerk's fingertips.

"And the watchman," she said. "The one who reached the room too late. Where is he?"

"Cooper's Row beat, madam. Watchman Pell."

"Send him to me at his shift's end. And Master Ellery—" She rose, and the clerk flinched as if she'd drawn steel, which she noted with distant, clinical regret; fear was useful but it corrupted testimony, frightened men remembered what they thought you wanted. "You've done nothing wrong. You're owed thanks for your thoroughness. There will be a mark in your file saying so."

The gratitude that flooded his face was pitiful, and she turned away from it and looked out her window at the city instead, at the smoke rising from a thousand lawful chimneys, and let herself feel, briefly and privately, the thing she never said aloud: that she would give all of it back — the rank, the gray coat, the fear — for one hour of her old life, for the sound of Tomas writing his backwards *b*s at the kitchen table while the soup heated, in a world where green light in a window meant nothing worse than a festival lantern.

Then she closed that door, as she always did, and got back to work.

Watchman Pell came at dusk: young, honest, nervous, and — once she fed him tea and asked questions in order, smallest first — unexpectedly useful.

"No, madam, I never saw the body. Earth had him already. But the landlady talked my ear off, and she'd seen the end of it." Pell turned his cap in his hands. "She said a second man came up the stairs not an hour after the courier died. Heard them through the door, she thought. Voices low. Then quiet. Then the second man left carrying nothing, which struck her strange, because couriers always have bags, don't they, and she'd seen Dole come in with a satchel over his shoulder."

"He had a satchel," Vane said. "You're certain she said satchel."

"Waxed leather, she said. Big as a bread loaf."

A courier dies. Within the hour, a second man is in the room. The satchel — the cargo Dole had hurried two days through the fens to deliver — vanishes into that second man's empty-looking hands, and by morning the courier is underground and the second man has reported nothing to anyone and gone back to selling luck-knots one floor down.

Vane set down her pen.

"Mistress Pell — Watchman Pell. This tenant. R. Hartwell. Has anyone laid eyes on him since?"

Pell frowned. "Now you mention it, madam, the night market folk said he wasn't at his usual corner last night. First time in five years, they said. Man's regular as the tide, they said." He paused. "Regular as tide, until the night his floor gets a corpse on it."

"Yes," Vane said quietly. "Until exactly then."

She dismissed him with thanks, sat alone in her office while the candles burned, and looked at the page in her notebook where the two names stood joined by their thin wire. On the left: a charm-seller gone invisible the moment scrutiny fell. On the right: a dead courier, a vanished satchel, a stranger with stinging fingers and a gray cloak.

It was still not proof. It was a shape. But she had hunted long enough to know that shapes preceded faces the way thunder precedes the strike, and everything in her — the scarred hand, the sleepless winters, the girl kneeling in ash on Corver Street — told her the strike was coming soon.

Vane took out a fresh sheet and began the requisition she had hoped not to need. Warrant authority for district interrogation. Watch cooperation, covert. And at the bottom, after a pause long enough that the candle guttered twice, the third line — the one that would cost her something, though she could not yet have said what:

*Request access to holding cells, sub-level three. Subject: Fenrow, hedge-practitioner, captured autumn last. Talent alleged: finding.*

They had a finder in the cells. They had held him for months without using him, because using a mage's talent, even chained and oath-bound, even for the crown — even for *her* — was a door that did not close again. She had argued against it in council. She had written papers against it.

She signed the request anyway, and her signature came out steady, and the steadiness frightened her more than trembling would have.

Outside, the first snow of winter began to fall on Vellamere, fat and silent and endless, erasing the streets one by one. Somewhere under that whiteness a man named Hartwell was deciding what to do with whatever lay hidden in a wax-sealed map, and somewhere north in the fens a vault waited with a song in it, and none of this did Sera Vane know — not yet.

She knew only that the static had begun to speak, and that by the time it resolved into a voice, she meant to be listening.

She pinned the requisition, sealed it, and rang for a runner.

"South tollgate records," she told him. "Everyone who crossed out of the city at dawn, five days ago. Names, descriptions, destinations. Bring me everything."

The snow kept falling. The wire between the two names in her notebook gleamed in the candlelight like a fuse.

At dawn, Rosalind Hartwell would slip out of Vellamere and into the fens — and Sera Vane, pulling threads in a city asleep under its first snow, would find the empty room, the loose floorboard pried and replaced a half-inch crooked, and the faint scorched smell that no broom entirely clears.

And she would smile, without warmth, and reach for her coat.
