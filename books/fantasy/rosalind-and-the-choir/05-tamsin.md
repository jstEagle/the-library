## 5. Tamsin

The knock came at the wrong hour.

Rosalind knew every rhythm of the tenement on Candlemaker's Row — the groan of the stairs under Mrs. Pell's heavy tread at dawn, the children thundering through the corridor at dusk, the drunk from the third floor singing to a woman who had left him two winters past. But this knock was light, doubled, patient. He knew it too. That was the problem.

He slid the map beneath the loose floorboard by the hearth, laid the plank back over it, and dragged his chair across the gap before he let himself breathe. The courier's blood had soaked into the boards of his room and he'd scrubbed at it until his knuckles were raw, but in the corner where the man had fallen there was still a shadow of rust-brown that no amount of lye would lift. He turned that corner toward the wall with his hip as he passed it, an old habit of hiding things, and opened the door.

Tamsin Ferro stood in the corridor with her lamplighter's pole over one shoulder and a paper sack of chestnuts in her free hand, her breath fogging in the draft from the stairwell. She was small and quick and brown as a wren, with soot worked so deep into the creases of her knuckles that she'd stopped trying to scrub it out years ago. Her eyes went over his face the way they went over a lamp bracket — quick, thorough, noting everything.

"You look like something the tide brought in," she said.

"Good evening to you too."

"It's nearly midnight." She shouldered past him into the room without waiting for invitation, because Tamsin had never once waited for an invitation in the eleven years he'd known her, and set the chestnuts on his table. Then she stopped. Her head tilted, birdlike, and Rosalind felt the moment stretch thin as wire.

She was looking at the corner. At the stain.

"What happened here?" she asked.

"I spilled wine."

"You don't drink wine." She crouched, touched the floorboards, rubbed her fingers together. "That's not wine. That's blood, Roz, and it's days old, and you've been over it with lye like you're trying to scrub through to the cellar." She straightened up and looked at him, and there was nothing soft in her face at all, which was how he knew she was frightened. "Whose is it?"

* * *

He had known Tamsin since he was nineteen and new to Vellamere, all elbows and terror, sleeping in the loft above her father's chandler shop because it was warm and because nobody looked twice at a boy who smelled of tallow. She had taught him the city the way you teach someone to swim — by throwing him in. Which streets the Inquisitorial clerks walked and which they avoided. How to read a face in the night market for the difference between a customer and an informer. How to laugh at the right moments so people remembered you as simple and cheerful and harmless.

She was the only person in Vellamere who called him Roz. Everyone else got Rosalind, or nothing at all. She was the only person who had ever seen him cry, once, drunk on festival gin the winter his mother died, and she had never mentioned it since, which was its own kind of love.

And she did not know what he was. Eleven years, and she did not know. He had built the lie brick by brick the way masons build a wall — not all at once, never all at once, just a course of stone each year, mortared with small omissions, until the wall was load-bearing and the whole house of his life rested on it. She thought he'd come to Vellamere fleeing a bad apprenticeship with a cruel master. She thought the scars on his forearms were from a kiln accident. She thought his restlessness, his watchfulness, his habit of sitting with his back to walls, was just a country boy's nerves that had never quite worn off.

Five years ago, when the purge had come through the low quarter and the hedge-witch Mother Casslin had been burned in Tanner's Square with half the neighborhood made to watch, Tamsin had raged about it for weeks. *Barbaric,* she'd said. *She was a bonesetter, Roz. She set my cousin's leg when he fell off the warehouse roof. They burned a woman whose whole crime was knowing how to mend what's broken.* And Rosalind had nodded along and said nothing, and said nothing, and said nothing, and every silence had been another brick.

Now she stood in his room looking at dried blood and asking him whose it was, and he understood with terrible clarity that whatever he said next would either be another brick or the first crack in eleven years of wall.

"A man came here," he said. "Sick. I tried to help him. He died."

It wasn't even a lie. That was the trick of it, the thing Aldous had taught him in another life: the best concealment isn't falsehood, it's truth with the weight removed. A man came here. Sick. Dead. All true. And none of it — none of the map pressed into his hands, none of the words *the Choir must not sing for her*, none of the wax seal hidden three feet from where Tamsin stood — any of it present in the room.

"He died," Tamsin repeated. "In your room. And you didn't tell anyone?"

"And tell them what? That a stranger bled out on my floor? You know what the clerks do with unexplained deaths in the low quarter. They ask questions for a month. They search things." His voice was steady. He marveled at it from somewhere outside himself, the way you marvel at a tightrope walker. "I buried what needed burying and I scrubbed the floor. It's done."

"Buried—" She stopped. Something moved behind her eyes, some recalculation. "Rosalind. Look at me."

He looked at her. It cost more than she could know.

"Who was he?"

"I don't know. I told you. A stranger."

"You don't take strangers into your room." She said it flatly, without accusation, which was worse. "You're the most careful person I've ever met. You lock your door to fetch water. You flinch when anyone touches your hands. You've slept with your boots by the bed pointed at the door for eleven years like a soldier. So either you've changed completely in the last week, or this man wasn't a stranger to you, or—" she paused, and he watched her arrive at the edge of it, watched her stand on the brink of the question that could undo everything— "or you're in trouble, and you won't tell me what kind."

The fire ticked in the grate. Somewhere below, the drunk on the third floor had found a new song.

"Tamsin." He made himself smile, and hated how easily it came, how well-practiced the muscles were. "I'm tired. It's been a bad week. Let me have the chestnuts and you can tell me about the lamps on Gull Street, and we'll both feel better."

For a long moment she didn't move. Then she sat down across from him, dropped the pole against the wall, and thrust the sack at him. "They're cold. I bought them an hour ago and then walked around deciding whether to come up."

"Why wouldn't you come up?"

"Because I was afraid of exactly this." She gestured at the room, at him, at the space between them that had gone strange and formal as a magistrate's office. "Because I've watched you get quieter for two weeks, and jumpier, and now there's blood on your floor and a lie on your face, and I thought — maybe tonight's the night he finally tells me. Or maybe tonight's the night I find out I don't actually know him at all."

The chestnut shell cracked under his thumbnail. He ate it without tasting anything.

"I'm not lying to you," he said.

And there it was. The first outright lie, spoken to her face, in his own room, by his own mouth. Not an omission, not a truth with the weight removed — a lie, plain as a coin, and he felt it leave him like something physical, a small dark stone dropping into the well of everything they'd built. He waited for some consequence. For her to recoil, for the room to darken, for the old stories to be true and magic to curdle when spent on deceit. Nothing happened. The fire ticked. She believed him, or wanted to, and somehow that was worse than if she hadn't.

"Fine," she said quietly. "Then I'm going to say a thing, and you don't have to answer it, and I'll never mention it again if you tell me to stop."

"Tamsin—"

"When Mother Casslin was burned." Her voice stayed level, but her hands, wrapped around her own cold chestnut, had gone white-knuckled. "You disappeared for three days. Nobody knew where. When you came back you had burns on your palms, like you'd been near a fire, and you told me you'd been helping clear the crowd and someone fell against a torch. I helped you dress them, remember? I was seventeen and I believed you because I loved you and because it was easier to believe you." She looked up. "But I've thought about those burns for five years, Roz. Every time. And lately I think about them more."

The room seemed to tilt very slightly, the way a boat tilts when something large passes underneath it in deep water.

"Stop," he said.

She stopped. Immediately, completely, the way she always kept her word — which was the cruelest thing she could have done, because now the silence was his, entirely his, a door he had closed with his own hand. She picked up her pole and stood, and at the door she paused with her hand on the latch.

"Whatever it is," she said, not turning around, "whatever kind of trouble finds a man careful enough to sleep with his boots pointing at the door — you should know that I'm good at carrying things. Heavy things. Things other people can't lift. My da used to say I was born with a porter's back and a fool's heart, and he meant it kindly." Now she turned, and her face in the lamplight from the corridor was open and fierce and unbearably fond. "You don't have to tell me. But if you ever need someone to carry something at midnight, or dig a hole, or stand somewhere dangerous and look innocent — I want you to remember that I offered. Before. When it was easy to say no."

"Tamsin—"

"Don't." She held up a hand, smiling now, though it didn't reach her eyes. "If you thank me I'll cry, and I've got forty lamps to light before dawn and no time to be red-eyed on Gull Street. The clerks talk." She pulled the door open, then hesitated. "There's something else. Small thing. Probably nothing."

"Probably-nothing things are usually the only things worth hearing."

"There's a man been asking after you. Down at the lamplighters' depot, two nights running. Neat fellow, gray coat, clerkish. Says he's from the Registry of Deaths, following up on some paperwork." She shrugged, but her shoulders were tight. "Asked whether you'd been home regular. Asked whether you had family. I told him you were dull as ditchwater and twice as boring, and he smiled like I'd confirmed something instead of denied it." She stepped out onto the landing. "Lock your door, Roz. Both locks."

Her footsteps faded down the stairwell. Rosalind stood in the middle of his room with cold chestnut shells scattered on the table and the taste of his first lie still sour in his mouth, and listened to the building settle around him.

A neat fellow in a gray coat. From the Registry of Deaths. Two nights running.

He crossed to the window and eased back the shutter a finger's width. Below, the street lay empty under a sky the color of wet slate, and the first flakes of the season's true snow were coming down, slow and deliberate, settling on the cobbles like a message being written in a language he couldn't yet read. Snow meant the fens would freeze. Snow meant roads that could be traveled and tracks that could be followed.

Snow meant the map beneath his floorboards would soon be the only thing standing between him and the question Tamsin had almost asked — the one he had forbidden her, the one the gray-coated man was already asking in his stead.

Rosalind let the shutter close and began, quietly, in the dark, to pack.
