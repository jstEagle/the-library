## 6. The Clerk's Visit

The knock came at the ninth bell of the morning, and Rosalind knew before he touched the latch that it was trouble.

Trouble had a particular sound in Vellamere — not the quick double-rap of a neighbor or the impatient hammer of a landlord, but a measured, patient knock, three beats with a pause between each, the knock of someone who knew the door would open and was merely observing the formality. He had heard it once before, five years ago, on the last morning he'd seen Aldous's face.

He set down the fever-charm he'd been restringing, wiped his palms on his apron out of habit rather than need, and looked around the room once — the small rented room above the chandler's shop on Candlewick Lane, twelve feet by fourteen, containing a bed, a stool, a table, a cold hearth, and nothing else that mattered.

Except what was beneath the floor.

He crossed to the corner by the hearth where the board sat loose — he'd pried it up two nights ago, after the courier died, after he'd spent an hour staring at the map and understanding whose hand had drawn it. The wax-sealed parchment went into the gap between joists, wrapped in oilcloth, weighted under a brick from the collapsed wall of the tannery next door. He pressed the board back into place. It fit flush. He'd planed it himself at midnight, sweating over the work though the room was freezing, because a loose floorboard was a confession and a flush one was only a floor.

The knock came again. Same rhythm. Patient as a noose.

Rosalind opened the door.

The clerk from the night market stood in the gray light with snow settling on his shoulders. Up close, away from the lantern-glow and the crowd, he was younger than Rosalind had taken him for — perhaps five-and-twenty, with a narrow face, ink-stained fingers, and eyes the color of dishwater that missed absolutely nothing. His coat was good wool but not fine, cut in the plain style of the Inquisitorial bureaus, and at his belt, where a knife might hang on any working man, hung instead a slim leather case for documents. A chain ran from the case to his belt. Official paper, carried like a weapon.

"Master Hartwell." He said the name as if tasting it. "I hope I'm not intruding."

"You are, a little." Rosalind kept his voice flat, faintly irritated, the voice of a man interrupted at honest work. "It's early."

"It's past nine." The clerk smiled without warmth. "My name is Corvess. Under-clerk to the Office of Records and Inquiry. May I come in? There's a wind coming off the river that I'd rather not stand in."

*May I come in.* Not *I'm coming in.* That was the trick of it, Rosalind thought. The Inquisition had learned long ago that a man who refuses entry looks guilty, and a man who consents has already surrendered the high ground of his own doorstep. There was no good answer, so there was no point pretending otherwise.

"If you must." He stepped aside.

Corvess entered the way water enters a cracked hull — quietly, finding every gap. His gaze moved across the room in one slow sweep: the bed, made; the table, scattered with cord and beads and half-finished charms; the hearth, swept but ash-cold; the single window with its view of the chandler's yard. It lingered nowhere and everywhere. Rosalind had watched buyers appraise his luck-knots with the same professional sweep, and he knew exactly what it meant. *What is here that shouldn't be.*

"Charm-work," Corvess said, nodding at the table. "You sell these?"

"At the night market. Luck-knots, fever-strings, birthing charms. Nothing you couldn't buy from a dozen stalls on Candlewick."

"Mm." Corvess picked up a fever-string between two ink-stained fingers and turned it, examining the knotwork with the air of a man who knew knots. "My grandmother wore one of these. Died anyway, in the grey fever, winter of '61. But she believed in it, and belief is worth something when you're frightened." He set it down precisely where it had lain. "Do you believe in them, Master Hartwell?"

"I believe people sleep better wearing one than not. That's worth two coppers to most folk."

"A craftsman's answer." Corvess turned from the table and regarded him with those dishwater eyes. "Honest. Modest. Exactly what a man selling string ought to say." He let the silence sit for three heartbeats. "Which is why I'm curious about your other guest."

And there it was.

Rosalind felt the old cold move through him, the cold he'd lived with for five years, the cold that was really a kind of discipline — the practiced stillness of a body trained never to react first. He let his brow furrow. Let confusion arrive a beat late, the way it honestly would have.

"My guest?"

"The man who died in this room." Corvess produced a small notebook from inside his coat, opened it, wet a pencil stub with his tongue. "Tuesday last, according to the chandler's wife, who hears everything on this street and reports most of it. A stranger, tall, mud to the knees, came up your stairs at dusk looking like death already. Left — or didn't leave — before dawn. Found floating by the Tanner's Stair Wednesday noon." The pencil hovered. "The Watch ruled it drink and the river. The Watch rules everything drink and the river; it saves paperwork. But the Office of Records keeps better accounts. And the Office noticed that this same man passed through Mireholt ten days ago carrying a courier's satchel, and that the satchel was never recovered from the river."

"That's terrible," Rosalind said, and meant it, which helped. Grief and fear feel similar in the face; he'd learned to let one wear the other's mask. "I found him on my step, near dead. I brought him up. I put blankets on him. He was raving — fever, I thought. Toward morning he stopped raving. I went for the physic on Fetter Lane, and when I came back he was gone. Out the window, I supposed, or down the stairs while my back was turned. I looked for him. I asked at the market. Nobody'd seen him." He shook his head slowly. "Then yesterday the chandler told me they'd pulled a body from the river. I've been sick about it since. Was it him?"

Corvess wrote something. Three words, maybe four. Rosalind fought the urge to try to read upside-down script and won, barely.

"You went for a physic," Corvess said. "At dawn. Which physic?"

"Old Marta, on Fetter Lane. She wasn't up yet. By the time she was, I'd decided the man had gone on his own way, and—" he spread his hands, rueful, self-mocking— "and I'll be honest with you, clerk, I didn't want to spend the coppers if he'd already gone. Times are thin. That's a shabby thing to admit, but there it is."

Another note. Corvess's pencil scratched like a mouse in a wall.

"You gave no name to the physic?"

"I don't know her well enough to give names. I said a man was sick on my floor and did she make house calls. She said bring him or don't. Very Christian of her."

Something flickered at the corner of Corvess's mouth — not quite a smile, but the ghost of appreciation. Rosalind recognized it and hated how much it pleased him. This was the sickness of deception done well: it felt like craft, like mastery, like the old lessons coming alive in his hands, and every moment of it was a small treason against the person he'd promised himself he'd be. Aldous had taught him this too. *A lie held lightly passes; a lie gripped tightly trembles.* He had hated the lesson then. He hated how well he'd learned it now.

"Describe him for me," Corvess said. "The dead man."

"Tall. Forty, maybe older — hard to say, he was in a bad state. Grey beard, cropped short. Soldier's bearing, or courier's. A scar through his left eyebrow." All true. All safe. The description matched the corpse in the river and would match nothing else, because Rosalind had burned the man's satchel contents — except the map — and buried the ashes under the midden behind the chandler's yard, and the man's boots were at the bottom of the privy, which was a waste of good boots but a necessary one.

"Clothing?"

"Traveling leathers, fen-cut. Soaked through. I dried them by the fire and they were gone with him."

"Gone with him," Corvess repeated softly. He closed the notebook. For a long moment he simply stood in the middle of the twelve-by-fourteen room, and Rosalind watched him do the thing that Inquisitorial clerks did, the thing that made them more dangerous than any hooded interrogator: he built the room twice in his mind. Once as Rosalind had described it, once as it must have been, and looked for the seam between the versions.

His eyes drifted toward the hearth. Toward the corner by the hearth.

Rosalind's heartbeat did not change. Five years of practice saw to that. But some animal part of him, deep under the discipline, began to scream.

"Mind if I look around?" Corvess said. "Formality. You understand. When a courier dies and his satchel vanishes, the Office likes to close its books neatly. Unrecovered property makes ugly footnotes."

"Look wherever you like. You won't find a satchel. If I'd found one, I'd have sold it — or turned it in for the reward, if there is one."

"There isn't." Corvess knelt at the hearth, examined the ash, rubbed a pinch of it between his fingers. Cold ash, ordinary ash. Then he rose, crossed to the bed, lifted the blanket, looked beneath. He checked the window latch. He tapped the wall once, listening to it, which Rosalind thought was theater more than method — plaster over brick tells the same story everywhere on Candlewick Lane.

Then he came to the corner by the hearth, and stood over the loose board, and said nothing at all.

The silence stretched. Snow ticked against the window. Somewhere below, the chandler's boy was singing a bawdy song about the ferryman's daughter, badly.

"You keep your floor well," Corvess said at last.

"I sweep it."

"Yes." He crouched. Ran one ink-stained finger along the seam of the board by the hearth — along the seam Rosalind had planed smooth at midnight, sweating in the cold. The finger traveled its length, unhurried, and Rosalind understood with perfect clarity that the man could feel the difference, that wood cut recently and fitted carefully had a texture that old flooring didn't, that he had perhaps already known before he knocked, that all of this — the questions, the notebook, the performance of routine — had been a frame built around a picture he'd already painted.

Or he was fishing. Or he was nothing but what he seemed: a bored clerk closing ugly footnotes.

That was the poison of it. You could never know. Certainty was the one thing the Inquisition never sold.

Corvess stood. "Well." He brushed off his knee. "Your account matches the chandler's wife's, more or less, and she's the closest thing this street has to a sworn witness. I'll write it up as misadventure. Drink and the river." He smiled his bloodless smile. "Unless something occurs to you. Sometimes things occur to people later. Nights are long this time of year, and memory is a strange beast — it likes to wander in the dark and come home by the wrong door. If anything wanders home to you, Master Hartwell, the Office pays for recovered property. And for recovered *correspondence*." He tapped the document case at his belt. "Particularly correspondence bearing certain seals."

"What seals?" Rosalind asked, and it was almost easy, because he genuinely didn't know what seal was on the map's wax — hadn't dared break it, hadn't dared not break it, and had finally, two nights ago, slid a hot knife under the edge with his hands shaking so badly he'd scorched his thumb. The seal had shown a tower and a broken circle. He didn't know it. He suspected Aldous would.

"Now that," Corvess said, "would be telling." He moved to the door, then paused with his hand on the latch, and turned back, and for just a moment the bureaucratic mask slipped and something else looked out — something younger, wearier, almost human. "A word of advice, freely given, from one man who survives by his wits to another. The Office doesn't send under-clerks out into the snow for misadventures. Someone upstairs flagged this file, and whoever it was doesn't flag files for the pleasure of paperwork. Men who carry sealed maps die in rivers, Master Hartwell. Men who *find* them tend to follow, one way or another. Whatever occurred to you — whatever wanders home by the wrong door — decide quickly whether it's worth dying for. Most things aren't. Almost everything isn't."

"And if it is?" Rosalind heard himself ask.

Corvess considered him for a moment, and there was something almost like pity in the dishwater eyes.

"Then sweep your floor very well," he said, "and don't be home when they come to check it."

He let himself out. His footsteps went down the stairs, unhurried, patient, and faded into the snow-hush of Candlewick Lane.

* * *

Rosalind stood motionless until the ninth bell's echo had fully died and the chandler's boy had run through his song twice more. Then he crossed the room, knelt, and pried up the board.

The oilcloth bundle lay untouched. He unwrapped it with scalded, shaking hands and looked at the map for the hundredth time — at the fens rendered in faded ink, the causeways and drowned churches, the route winding north and east toward a blank space at the margin where the parchment grew dark and dense, as if soaked in something older than ink. And there, in the corner, in a hand he had known since childhood, since warm kitchens and candlelit lessons and the safest years of his life:

*Aldous's handwriting.* The route notes. The tide-tables. And one line, small, pressed hard into the vellum:

*If you are reading this, the Choir has chosen its heir. God forgive me. Run north.*

"He chose me," Rosalind whispered to the empty room. "He didn't choose me. He *sent* for me."

The distinction mattered. It was the whole world, and it changed nothing, because either way the map led north into the fens, and either way a clerk with dishwater eyes had felt the seam of a freshly planed floorboard and walked out into the snow without arresting him — which meant the arrest was coming later, with soldiers, when it couldn't be avoided. Corvess hadn't been closing a footnote. He'd been measuring the cell.

Rosalind repacked the map, replaced the board, and sat on the edge of his bed for a long time, watching snow fill the window.

He thought about Tamsin, who would come by at dusk with bread and gossip, who would see the packed bag and know everything and forgive him nothing. He thought about Old Marta on Fetter Lane, who would remember his face when the clerks came asking. He thought about the fever-strings on the table, hours of careful, harmless, half-alive work, the whole small safe life he'd built out of copper and cowardice.

Then he got up, took his pack down from behind the chimney breast, and began, quietly and without ceremony, to pack.

Outside, the snow thickened. North of the city, beyond the walls, beyond the river country, the fens waited under the first white of winter — and somewhere in their frozen heart, a vault full of standing stones held its breath, waiting to learn whether the heir would come.
