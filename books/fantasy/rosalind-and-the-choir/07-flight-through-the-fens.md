## 7. Flight Through the Fens

He did not sleep. He had told himself he would — told himself that whatever came, dawn would come easier to a man who had rested — but the map lay beneath the floorboards like a buried heart, and every time he closed his eyes he could feel it beating.

Somewhere past midnight he gave up the pretense and lit a stub of candle. The room looked different by its small light: the chipped basin, the coat on its single nail, the window with its view of the chimney-pots of Copperlane. Five years he had lived here. Five years of luck-knots and fever-charms, of counting coppers twice, of learning the exact weight of an ordinary life so he could carry it without spilling anything dangerous.

He had been good at it. That was the bitter joke. He had been *good* at being small.

Rosalind knelt and pried up the loose board. The map was cold, colder than the room had any right to make it, and when he lifted it the wax seal caught the candleflame and threw a red gleam across the ceiling like something bleeding overhead. He did not unroll it again. He knew what it showed. He knew the handwriting on it better than he knew his own — Aldous's narrow, patient script, the letters leaning forward as if walking somewhere.

His master's hand. On a map to a place out of childhood stories. Carried by a dead man who had died saying *the Choir must not sing for her*.

He should burn it. He had tried; the parchment would not catch. He had held it in the flame until the candle guttered and his fingers ached, and the map had lain in the fire like a stone in a stream, untouched, faintly amused.

So instead he would carry it north, into the fens, toward the Hollow Choir, because the alternative was to sit in this room and wait for the knock on the door. And Rosalind Hartwell had spent five years learning to read the city's weather, and the weather had changed. The clerk with the lingering eyes. The questions asked too gently at the night market. The way the lamplighters had been stopped and searched on Tuesday, all of them, as if someone were looking for something small enough to hide in a coat.

Something like a map sealed in wax.

He packed the way he had learned to pack as an apprentice on the run: quickly, ruthlessly, everything he owned weighed against whether it would slow him. Two shirts, the good wool socks Tamsin had given him last winter, his knife, his needle-case, a brick of dried fish, the flint. His charms he left behind — the little braided knots, the copper tokens stamped with signs that meant nothing and worked only because people believed they did. Those were the honest lies of his trade, and he could not take them where he was going.

The dishonest one went inside his shirt, against his skin, where it stayed cold no matter how long he carried it.

At the door he stopped. He looked back once at the room — the whole of his safe life, four walls and a leaky roof and a window that faced away from the sea — and he made himself memorize it, because some part of him already understood he was not coming back to it. Not to this. Even if he returned to Vellamere, even if the world turned kind, this version of his life was over. You could not carry a dead courier's secret for three days and go back to selling luck.

He blew out the candle and left the rent under the basin, exactly what he owed through the month, because even fugitives have their pride.

* * *

Copperlane slept the way poor places sleep — lightly, in shifts, with one eye on the stairs. Rosalind went down the back way, past the tannery vats breathing their sour breath into the dark, and out through the gate where the wall gave onto the drainage ditches. The watchman there was old Hobb, who took a copper to look elsewhere and had taken coppers from half the smugglers in the city and never once remembered a face.

"Early," Hobb said, not looking up from his brazier.

"Market day," Rosalind said. "Fish won't wait."

"Nothing waits, this time of year." Hobb fed the fire another stick. "Snow coming. Feel it?"

Rosalind felt it. He had always been able to feel weather coming — not magic, just the ordinary animal sense everyone claimed and no one trusted — but tonight the air had a pressure to it, a hush, the particular silence that meant the sky was deciding something. The first true snows. Winter arriving like a debt collector.

"Good luck," Hobb said, which was funny, given the trade, and Rosalind laughed because it was expected, and the laugh came out wrong, and Hobb still didn't look up.

Beyond the ditch the road ran north through scrubland, pale under a thinning moon. Rosalind walked it at a pace that looked unhurried and covered ground like a man pursued, because he had learned long ago that the trick to running was never to appear to run. By the time the sky went grey he had put six miles behind him, and Vellamere was a smudge of smoke on the horizon behind, and the land had begun to change.

The fens announced themselves gradually. First the trees thinned — alder and willow giving way to bare black stands standing in water. Then the ground softened, went spongy, began to speak in wet syllables underfoot. Then the reeds started, tall as a man, silver-headed, endless, whispering among themselves in a language that sounded almost like words. Rosalind had crossed the fen-edge before, years ago, on errands for Aldous, and he had forgotten how much of it was sound. The marsh talked all the time. It talked in birdsong and wind and the plop of things moving in dark water, and underneath all of it, so low you heard it in your teeth rather than your ears, a hum he tried very hard to tell himself was the wind in the reeds.

It was not the wind in the reeds.

He stopped at midmorning on a hummock of higher ground to eat, and that was when the snow began. Not the timid flurry the city got, melting on contact, but real snow, fat flakes falling straight down out of a white sky with the confidence of something that intended to stay. Within a quarter hour the world had gone soft-edged and silent. The reeds bowed under it. His footprints filled behind him as fast as he made them, which was a comfort and a terror both — a comfort because it hid him, a terror because it hid whoever else might be walking.

Because someone was walking. He had known since the second mile.

It was nothing he could point to. A flicker at the edge of vision, there and gone, that might have been a heron. A sense, twice, of having been matched — his pace copied a heartbeat after he changed it. Once, crossing a frozen ditch, he had looked back along his own trail and seen, far off, a shape that resolved into nothing the moment he named it. Birds did that. Shadows did that. Fear did that most of all, and Rosalind knew his fear intimately, had lived with it five years like a roommate, and he tried now to ask it calmly: is this real, or are you making it up?

His fear declined to answer. His fear just kept looking over his shoulder.

He walked on. The map, against his chest, stayed cold.

* * *

By late afternoon the snow had thickened and the light had gone the color of old pewter, and Rosalind admitted he needed shelter before dark, because the fens at night were not a place you survived by stubbornness. The map — he allowed himself to consult it now, hunched against a willow trunk, shielding it with his body out of pure habit — showed a dotted line threading the meres, and a mark like a bird's foot two days in, annotated in Aldous's hand: *Mireholt. Ask for the soldier.*

Ask for the soldier. As if his master had arranged all of this years ago and was simply waiting for Rosalind to walk into it. The thought sat in him like a swallowed stone. Twenty years ago Aldous had helped seal the Hollow Choir. Five years ago Aldous had taught Rosalind to hide so well that hiding had become his whole self. And now a map in Aldous's handwriting was pulling him north through the snow toward a vault out of legend, and Aldous himself was — where? Dead, said the city. Dead these ten years, said everyone who had ever spoken of him. But the courier had died saying *she*, and maps did not write themselves, and Rosalind was beginning to suspect that his master's death was one more lesson in concealment, the largest one, practiced on the whole world at once.

He folded the map away and stood in the falling snow and let himself, for one moment, feel the grief.

It arrived the way grief does — not dramatically, but sideways, through something small. Through the thought of Tamsin climbing her ladder at dusk with her pole and her oil-can, lighting the lamps along Ferrier Street, and him not there anymore to walk the opposite side pretending to be going the same direction. Through the memory of the night market stall, the striped awning, the woman with the sick daughter who bought fever-charms every month and paid in eggs sometimes and never once asked why the charms worked better than they should. Through the simple fact that tomorrow morning there would be no stall, no awning, no coins counted twice into a tin, and that this absence was permanent in a way he could not yet feel properly, only know, the way you know a limb is gone before the pain starts.

Five years building a small safe life. Three days to lose it. He hadn't even lost it to discovery — he'd lost it to a dying stranger's last breath, to compassion, to the trap of being the sort of person who couldn't walk past a bleeding man. He wondered, not for the first time and not for the last, whether kindness had simply been the shape his ruin chose.

Then he wiped his face — the snow gave him cover for it, which he was grateful for — and walked on.

* * *

Dusk found him at the edge of a wide mere, black water steaming faintly where springs kept it from freezing, and it was there, in the last grey light, that he finally saw his follower clearly enough to stop pretending.

A figure stood perhaps three hundred yards off across the flat, at the margin of the reeds. Dark-cloaked, hooded, motionless. Not walking his trail — standing on it, the way a hunter stands when the quarry has finally looked back. The distance was exact. Close enough to be seen. Far enough to be deniable.

Rosalind stood still and let the cold climb into him and studied the figure until his eyes watered. It did not move. It did not need to. Everything about its stillness said *I am here, and I know that you know*, and that was worse than pursuit. Pursuit you could outrun or outlast. This was a message, delivered in the only language the Inquisitor General's people spoke fluently: *we see you. We have always seen you. Run wherever you like.*

Vane. Or Vane's people. The clerk with the lingering eyes, maybe, promoted from questions to weather. Rosalind's hands wanted to shake and he made them not, because the figure across the mere might be watching for exactly that — might be too far away to see it at all, and the not-knowing was part of the design.

*All right,* he told it silently. *You can follow me. But you'll follow me where I'm going, and we'll see which of us likes what's at the end of the map.*

The figure tilted its head, very slightly, as if it had heard.

Rosalind turned north and walked into the deepening snow, and behind him, at a courteous distance, the dark shape moved too — keeping pace, keeping patience, keeping him company all the long way toward Mireholt, where a soldier waited who claimed to have known his master, and where the first true question of his journey would finally be asked:

Not *who follows you* — he knew that now.

But *who sent you*.
