## 8. The Wayhouse at Mireholt

The wayhouse announced itself an hour before Rosalind reached it, which was the only reason he did not turn and run.

He had smelled woodsmoke first — a fat, resinous reek of pine burned green, the smell of someone burning whatever they could find because proper fuel was long gone. Then, cutting through the fen fog, came light: a single yellow window glowing like a coin dropped in gray water. And beneath both, faint but unmistakable, the sound of voices. More than one. Laughter, even — rough and brief, the kind that dies quickly when strangers approach.

Rosalind stopped at the edge of the causeway and let himself feel the whole weight of what he was about to do.

Five years of discipline said: *no fires, no faces, no names.* Five years of sleeping in stairwells and haylofts, of buying bread from the baker who never looked up, of knowing every back alley in Vellamere the way other men knew their mothers' kitchens. The city had been dangerous, but it had been *legible*. He had learned its grammar. Out here there were no rules he understood — only marsh, snow, and a building full of armed strangers with one door.

But the cold had teeth now. It had found the gap between his collar and his jaw somewhere past the second mile marker and had not let go since. His fingers had gone from aching to silent, which was worse. And the map — he touched it through his coat, that stubborn unburnable parchment — the map wanted him here. Mireholt sat on its inked line like a bead threaded on wire, marked not with the usual wayhouse sign but with a small careful symbol he'd spent half the night puzzling over: a circle crossed by a single vertical stroke.

Aldous's handwriting. Aldous's mark. Which meant either this was a safehouse his master had trusted once —

— or a trap his master had built and never dismantled.

*Both are possible,* Rosalind told himself. *Plan for both.*

Behind him, at the edge of visibility, the dark shape paused when he paused. It had kept pace all day, never closer than thirty paces, never farther than fifty, patient as weather. He still didn't know what it was. A spy of Vane's? Some fen-thing out of the old stories? Or — and this thought arrived unwelcome and stayed — something sent by whoever had sent the courier. Something making sure the map arrived.

*Let it watch me walk into a warm room,* he thought bitterly. *At least one of us will be comfortable.*

He squared his shoulders, pulled his hood lower, and went to knock on the only lit window for ten miles.

* * *

The door opened before he touched it.

That was the first wrong thing. He stood in the snow with his knuckles an inch from the oak and the door simply swung inward, and a man filled the frame — a big man, wide as a doorway himself, with a beard going iron-gray at the chin and a scar that split one eyebrow into two pale halves. He looked at Rosalind the way a butcher looks at a carcass: assessing weight, condition, likely yield.

"You're late," the man said.

Rosalind's blood went cold in a way the wind hadn't managed. "I'm sorry?"

"The courier was due a week past." The man stepped aside, jerking his head at the warmth behind him. "In. You're letting the heat out, and I've burned half my winter's peat already waiting."

"I think you have me mistaken for someone—"

"Boy." The man's voice didn't rise. It didn't need to; it had the flat authority of a man who had spent years making soldiers move by speaking quietly. "You walked the fen road alone, in the first snow, off the ferry at Corlan's Stair, following a line no honest traveler would take. You flinched every time the ice cracked under you like you expected it to open up and swallow you, which means you know exactly what's buried under some of these causeways. And you've been followed since noon by something I can smell from my doorstep." He folded his arms. "So either you're the worst liar ever to carry that map, or you're about to lie to me anyway. Come inside and do it by the fire, at least. My mother raised me better than to let people freeze in my yard."

Every instinct Rosalind owned screamed at him to run. But running meant the dark shape behind him, and the dark, and the cold that had already taken feeling from two of his fingers. And the man had said *that map* — casually, certainly, the way you'd say *that hat*.

He went inside.

* * *

The common room was small and low-beamed and held four people: the big man; a woman mending a net by the fire who never once looked up; a sallow youth asleep in the corner with his boots on; and an old woman behind the bar who watched Rosalind with eyes like tarnished coins. A fire cracked in a hearth big enough to roast an ox. The heat hit Rosalind's face like a hand and he had to stand very still for a moment, blinking, while feeling flooded back into his extremities with a pain that was almost sweet.

"Sit," the big man said. "Marta, ale. Two."

"I can pay for—"

"You'll pay for your own. His is mine." The man lowered himself onto the bench across the table, and the bench groaned under him. Up close, Rosalind could read the rest of him: the soldier's posture even seated, spine straight against the wall so no one could come at his back; the sword at his hip worn honest, hilt leather polished by decades of the same grip; the way his eyes did a slow circuit of the room every few breaths without appearing to move at all. Not a bandit. Bandits didn't sit with their backs to walls.

"My name is Brannoc," the man said. "This is my house, or near enough — I hold the lease from the fen-court, and the fen-court holds it from nobody, which suits everyone. You'll give me a name too. Any name. I don't care if it's true tonight. We'll get to true later."

"...Hartwell," Rosalind heard himself say, and then cursed inwardly. Not any name. His name. The fire and the exhaustion had loosened something.

Brannoc's eyebrows rose a fraction. "Bold choice."

"It's mine."

"Mm." Brannoc took the ale Marta set down and drank half of it in one motion. "And the man who taught you to hide that name — the man whose writing is stitched into the lining of that map you're sitting on like a hen on one egg — what did he call you?"

The floor seemed to tilt. Rosalind kept his face still through sheer five-year practice, but his heart was slamming. *How does he know the map's there? How does he know Aldous?*

"You knew my master," he said carefully.

"I asked first."

"You asked nothing. You stated things."

Something flickered across the scarred face — not quite a smile, but the ghost of where a smile might once have lived before life got at it. "So you're not entirely stupid. Good. Stupid couriers die fast and I've buried enough friends." He set the mug down. "Yes. I knew Aldous Vey. Twenty-two years ago, in another country entirely, when he still laughed and I still believed in causes. I haven't seen him since the year they sealed the Choir, and I have spent those twenty-two years hearing him called dead, traitor, hero, and ghost, usually in the same sentence." He leaned forward. "Now. My questions. Answer them honestly and I'll guide you to the door of that place myself, and I promise you the fen will not eat you on my watch. Lie to me, and I'll take the map off your corpse and do it anyway, and I'll sleep fine after. Understood?"

Rosalind's hands had gone cold again despite the fire. "Understood."

"Who gave you the map?"

"A courier. Dying. In my rooms in Vellamere."

"Name?"

"He didn't give one. He was—" Rosalind stopped. The memory rose sharp: the blood, black in the lamplight, soaking through the boards; the wax seal pressed into his palms still warm from the man's body. "He was stabbed. Inquisitorial work, or dressed like it. He said—" The words came back with their original terror intact. *The Choir must not sing for her.* "He said the Choir must not sing for her. And then he died."

The room went quiet. Even the fire seemed to lean in. By the hearth, the mending woman's needle had stopped moving.

Brannoc sat back slowly. "And 'her' being?"

"I don't know."

"That's not an answer. That's a confession of ignorance. Different thing."

"It's the only thing I have." Rosalind made himself hold the older man's gaze. "I've spent three days asking the same question you just did. To a dead man, to a map, to a piece of parchment that won't burn. Whoever she is, she has the Inquisitor General's clerks asking questions in the night market and killers walking the fen roads, and she wants whatever's in that vault badly enough to bleed for it. That's everything I know about her. If you know more, then you're not my interrogator, you're my only source, and maybe we should stop pretending otherwise."

Silence stretched. The needle resumed. Somewhere upstairs a beam popped in the cold.

Then Brannoc laughed — one short bark, genuine surprise. "Gods below. Aldous taught you to hide, did he?" He shook his head. "He never learned to speak plain in sixty years, and here's his student doing it in a snowdrift." He drained his ale and signaled Marta for two more. "All right, Hartwell-that-is-your-name. Here's what I know, and you'll judge how much of it to believe, same as I judged you on your doorstep.

"Twenty-two years ago, seven people sealed the Hollow Choir. Mages, all of them — real ones, war-grade, the kind the histories pretend never existed. They went down into that vault at the end of the mage war and they bound the Emberstone so it would sing for no one. Six of them gave their lives to the working. That was the price and they knew it going in." His jaw worked. "Six graves in the fens, unmarked, because marking them would tell the crown where the door was. And one man walked out. One. Carrying the only copy of the binding, half-mad with grief, and swearing on everything holy that the sixth death — his own — had simply failed to take."

"And you believed him."

"I *wanted* to believe him." Brannoc's voice dropped low and hard. "He was my friend. I carried him out of that marsh on my back while he wept like a child and swore he'd spend the rest of his life guarding what they'd bought. And then he vanished. And the years went on, and hedge-witches started burning, and the purges came, and I waited for Aldous Vey to come out of hiding and *do something* — he was the last sealer alive, he had the knowledge, the standing, the name. And he never came. He hid. He taught others to hide. He built a whole quiet little religion of survival out of running away." He looked at Rosalind, and there was something raw under the gruffness now, something twenty-two years old and unhealed. "So when a boy walks out of the snow wearing his handwriting and his habits and his *face*, a little, around the eyes — you'll forgive me if I want to know whether the old coward finally grew a spine, or whether he's using you the way he used all of us."

Rosalind stared at him. Around the eyes. *His face.* He had never once considered that he resembled Aldous; masters and apprentices rarely did, and yet—

"The courier," he said slowly. "Sent by Aldous. You think my master sent him."

"I think somebody did. The seal on that map — show me."

Rosalind hesitated, then drew the map from his coat and laid it on the table between them, keeping his palm flat over the wax. Brannoc bent close. The firelight caught the sigil pressed in the crimson seal: a circle, crossed by a single vertical stroke.

"There it is," Brannoc breathed. "The Sealers' Mark. Seven circles in a ring originally — six closed, one left open, for the one who walked out. Aldous broke the ring down to this after. One circle. One stroke. *One survivor.*" He straightened, and his expression had changed — harder now, and sadder. "No one else alive has that seal. No one. So either your master is dead and someone robbed his grave for it — unlikely; I'd have heard — or Aldous Vey is alive, in hiding, and has decided after twenty-two years of silence to send a terrified apprentice-boy into the most dangerous place in the north carrying the most dangerous object in the world."

"Why?" The question escaped Rosalind before he could weigh it. "Why me? Why now? Why not come himself?"

"That," Brannoc said, "is the right question. And I notice you didn't ask the other one."

"What other one?"

The big man's eyes were steady and pitiless as the fen outside. "If your master is alive, and he sent a dying man to put that map in your hands instead of a letter — if he chose a boy he trained for five years in the arts of *hiding*, specifically, and aimed him at the Choir like an arrow — then ask yourself what Aldous needs that he can't do himself. Ask yourself why the arrow needed to be someone with magic in his blood, young enough to climb down where old bones can't go, and loyal enough not to run." He tapped the table once, softly. "Ask yourself, Hartwell, whether you were sent to fetch the stone — or to *open the door* for whoever's really coming through it."

The fire crackled. Rosalind became aware that the mending woman had stopped sewing again, and that the old woman behind the bar had drifted closer along the counter, and that all of them — the whole frozen house — were waiting on his answer.

"I don't know," he said at last. It cost him something to say it. "He was kind to me. He was the only person who—" He stopped, restarted. "He saved my life when I ran. He taught me everything. And everything you've just told me says he also lied to me for five years about who he was and what he'd done." He looked down at the map, at the familiar slanted script of the man he'd loved like a father, and felt the ground shift under everything. "So I'll go to the Choir. Because the stone can't sing for *her*, whoever she is, and because it's the only lead I have to the truth. But I'm not going as his arrow."

"No?"

"No." Rosalind met Brannoc's eyes. "I'm going to ask him myself. And this time, I want answers I can burn."

For a long moment Brannoc said nothing. Then he pushed back from the table, took up a lantern from the bar, and jerked his head toward the stairs.

"You'll have the corner room. Marta'll bring stew. We leave at first light — the ice road's safest before the sun softens it." At the foot of the stairs he paused, not looking back. "One more thing, boy. That thing that's been following you since the ferry."

Rosalind went rigid. "You've seen it?"

"We all have. It's been standing at the treeline since dusk, polite as a mourner." Brannoc turned, and in the lantern light his scarred face was grim. "Fen-folk have a word for it. *Ward.* Old workings leave them behind — guardians bound to a place or a purpose, walking until the purpose is done. My grandmother said they follow the bloodline of whoever woke them." He held Rosalind's gaze. "Which raises my last question of the night, and you should sleep on it, because I'll want the answer on the road:

"If the ward woke when you took the map — whose blood, exactly, does Aldous Vey think you are?"

Rosalind climbed the stairs on legs that felt borrowed, and lay down in the narrow bed without undressing, the map beneath his coat, the fire's glow seeping under the door. Outside, at the treeline, something patient stood in the snow.

And for the first time since the courier died in his arms, Rosalind wondered not *what* he was carrying —

but *who*.
