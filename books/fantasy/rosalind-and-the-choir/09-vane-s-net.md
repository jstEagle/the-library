## 9. Vane's Net

The courier had a name, and names were where Sera Vane always began.

*Corwin Ashe, twenty-nine, licensed under the Guild of Swift Post, no dependents, one lodging above a chandler's shop on Tanner's Row.* She read the registry entry a third time by the light of her office lamp, though she had already committed it to memory. The ink was ordinary. The facts were ordinary. It was the gaps that interested her — the two-day stretch between Ashe's last recorded delivery and his death, unaccounted for in any ledger; the sealed satchel reported missing from his body when the watch found him in the gutter off Cooper's Lane; and the fact that he had died three streets from the night market, in the shadow of the very stalls where, if her clerks' informants were worth their salt, charms and luck-knots changed hands for coppers on any given evening.

Coincidence was a word Vane did not believe in. Coincidence was what lazy investigators called patterns they hadn't yet earned the right to see.

She pinned Ashe's likeness to the corkboard above her desk — a thin-faced man with anxious eyes, rendered in the flat charcoal of the guild's licensing portraits — and beneath it, in her small precise hand, wrote: *What did you carry? And who did you give it to?*

Her office sat at the top of the Bastion of Inquiry, a tower the citizens of Vellamere called the Needle behind its back. From its windows she could see most of the city: the slate roofs going white with the season's first real frost, the harbor cranes standing like gallows against a pewter sky, the smoke of ten thousand hearths rising straight up into windless cold. Somewhere down there, whatever Corwin Ashe had carried was still moving through the city's veins, passed hand to hand like a coin that didn't know it was being hunted.

Vane poured herself tea gone lukewarm and drank it anyway. Her father had taught her that habits kept a mind sharp — the same cup, the same hour, the same route walked each morning so that anything *different* announced itself like a false note in a familiar song. He had been a wool merchant with a scholar's temperament, dead thirty years now alongside her mother and her sister Ede, in the fire that had started in a hedge-witch's cellar on Candlemas Street and eaten an entire terrace of houses before the watch could rouse itself. The witch had survived. Vane remembered standing in the ash with her uncle's heavy coat around her shoulders, nine years old, watching the woman weep and protest that the charm had been meant to *warm*, only to warm, never to burn —

She set the cup down before the memory could finish. It never finished. That was the discipline of it.

*Magic killed my family,* she had told the Inquisitor General at her interview, nineteen years old and hollow-eyed, and he had said, *Then you understand better than most why we do this work,* and he had been wrong, but she hadn't known it then. She understood nothing of the kind. What she understood was that power without accountability burned houses down, and that someone had to stand between it and the sleeping city, and that the work required a person willing to be disliked. She had made her peace with being disliked a long time ago.

She rang for her junior clerk.

"Corwin Ashe," she said when the young man appeared, breathless from four flights of stairs. "Pull everything. His delivery routes for the past month, his guild dues, his debts, his drinking companions. I want to know every door he knocked on in his final week."

"And the missing satchel, ma'am?"

"The satchel is the whole case, Pell. Find me the hands it passed through."

* * *

It took Pell six days, and Vane spent them badly.

Not lazily — she was never lazy — but with a friction she could feel in her own joints, like a wheel turning through sand. The trail resisted her. Ashe's final week showed deliveries to eleven addresses, all legitimate, all verifiable, none of them remarkable. His debts were modest. His companions were other couriers and a widowed aunt who wept convincingly and knew nothing. The chandler downstairs from his lodging remembered him as quiet, punctual, fond of boiled mutton. A man built out of nothing remarkable, which was either the truth or a very good disguise, and Vane had learned that the second thing usually wore the face of the first.

On the fourth day she went down into the holding cells beneath the Bastion, because there was a prisoner there whose file she had been avoiding, and avoidance, she told herself sternly on the stairs, was also a form of laziness.

His name was Merrick Dole, and he was a hedge-mage — a finder, the arrest report said, able to locate lost objects and lost people, the sort of low, damp talent that flourished in river towns and market slums. He'd been taken outside Mireholt three weeks ago trying to scry for a stolen pig. He was perhaps fifty, weathered as old rope, and when they put him in the interview room he looked at Vane the way men looked at the headsman: not with hatred, exactly. With arithmetic. Weighing how much of himself he could afford to lose today.

"You want me to find something," he said, before she'd even sat down.

"I want to talk about your sentencing," Vane said. "Understand me clearly, Master Dole. The law says your talent makes you guilty of existing. I don't write that law. But I enforce it, and enforcement has — ranges." She laid a paper on the table between them. "There is a version of tomorrow where you go to the pyre. There is another version where you go to the penal colony at Greywater, alive, with work, with years. The distance between those versions is measured in cooperation."

Dole's cracked lips moved around something bitter. "You want me to use the gift they'll kill me for. To help them kill more like me."

"I want you to find a satchel."

"A satchel." He laughed, and it turned into coughing. "A satchel, she says. You'll want the man who carried it next. Then whoever he gave it to. Then everyone they ever broke bread with. I know how the ladder works, madam Inquisitor. My father climbed it ahead of me, back in the purges. First you ask us to point at monsters. Then you notice we're standing in the same mud."

Vane folded her hands and made herself wait three full seconds before answering, because she had learned that silence did more work than threats.

"I have read the purge records," she said quietly. "All of them. Including the ones the crown would rather stayed closed. I know what was done at Ashford Fen. I know about the children." Dole's eyes flickered; he hadn't expected that. "I am not here to tell you the law has been kind, because it hasn't. I'm here because something left a dead courier in a gutter last week, and whatever it was, it is moving toward something worse than a stolen pig. If it's what I think it is, there will be fires, Master Dole. Real ones. And the people who burn will be ordinary folk with no talent at all, the way they always are." She slid the paper closer. "Greywater. Your cooperation noted in writing, by my hand. That is the offer. It does not improve with time."

He stared at her for a long moment, and she watched him weigh it — not whether she was good, because he'd correctly guessed she wasn't, but whether she was *true*. Whether the offer was real. It was the strangest part of her work, Vane thought, this moment when her enemies had to decide to trust her competence if not her conscience.

"What am I finding?" Dole said at last.

"A trail. Cold by now, mostly. A man named Corwin Ashe, and the last hands his cargo touched." She hesitated — a hair's hesitation, but Dole caught it, because finding things was what he did. "And anyone connected to those hands."

"There's a name already," Dole said softly. "You've got a name and you're not saying it."

"I've got a suspicion. Suspicions need proof."

* * *

The proof came on the sixth day, and it came wearing Pell's apologetic expression.

"The chandler's wife, ma'am. Under the second questioning she remembered — Ashe came home the night before he died, later than usual, wet to the knees. Said he'd been caught in the marsh fog coming up from the fens road. Only—" Pell consulted his notes, though Vane could see he had it memorized, "—the fens road doesn't come within four miles of any of his routes. And the landlady at the wayhouse in Mireholt confirms a courier matching his description took a room there that same night, asked after the old chapel track, and left before dawn."

"He went into the fens deliberately. Off-route, off-ledger." Vane stood, crossing to the corkboard. "Then he comes back, dies three streets from the night market, and his satchel vanishes. Who found the body?"

"Watch patrol. But a witness statement was taken — a stallholder closing up late. Name of Hartwell. Rosalind Hartwell, luck-charms and knotwork, licensed hawker, five years resident." Pell held out the sheet. "No prior flags. Clean record. Pays his taxes on time."

Vane read the statement twice. It was beautifully dull. *Saw the man stumble, went to assist, he refused aid, appeared intoxicated, she continued closing her stall.* Every sentence doing exactly the work a sentence should do and not one stroke more. She had interviewed enough liars to recognize the architecture of a well-built lie, and this was one — load-bearing, symmetrical, with no ornament anywhere.

Nobody lied this well by accident. People lied this well out of practice.

"Hartwell," she said. "Where does he lodge?"

"Tanner's Row. Above the cooperage. Two streets from where Ashe died." Pell paused. "Ma'am — the night market stalls near the cooperage. There's been talk for years. Nothing proven. Charms that work too well. Luck that turns on a copper. If half of it's true—"

"If half of it's true, then a licensed hedge-talent just became the last living link to a dead courier who snuck off to the fens carrying something worth killing for." Vane took down Ashe's portrait and pinned Hartwell's beside it — a lean young man with careful eyes and ink-stained fingers, staring out of his hawker's license with the fixed, pleasant blankness of someone photographed while thinking hard about something else. "Send Wren and Cade. Not to arrest. To knock, to question, to watch his face. If he runs, we learn more from the running than from the man."

"And Dole, ma'am? Do we bring him?"

"Not yet." She looked at the window, at the frost climbing the glass in feathered white lattices, and felt the old cold certainty settle over her like armor being buckled on, plate by plate. "First we see if our charmer spooks. If he runs north — toward the fens, toward whatever Ashe found — then we stop investigating and start hunting. And hunting means the tracker."

Pell shifted his weight. "The General will want justification in writing. Using a condemned mage's craft. There are those on the council who'll call it hypocrisy."

"Let them." She heard her own voice come out flatter than she intended, and made no effort to warm it. "When the Emberstone surfaces — and it will surface, Pell, because men don't die in gutters over luck-knots — every mage in this country will smell it like sharks smell blood. Aldous Renwick's ghost is attached to that stone, and half the underground still sings his name. If it falls into the wrong hands, we won't be arguing about hypocrisy. We'll be arguing in the ashes." She turned back to the board, to the two faces pinned side by side, the dead courier and the too-careful charmer. "I will sign whatever justification is required. My name goes on it. That's what my name is *for*."

"Yes, ma'am." Pell gathered his notes, hesitated at the door. "For what it's worth — the Hartwell statement. It bothered me too. Too clean."

"You'll go far, Pell," Vane said, and meant it, and wondered briefly why the compliment tasted like ash.

* * *

They came back at dusk with empty hands and a story that made her blood run slow and cold.

"No answer at the door, ma'am. Neighbors say he left before first light, alone, on foot, with a pack. The cooper's boy saw him take the north road." Wren, the elder of the pair, delivered it in the clipped tone of a man reporting a failure he couldn't explain. "We checked the room. Tidy. Rent paid through the month — paid *in advance*, which the landlady said he'd never done before. Left the charms stock untouched. And this."

He set a small object on her desk: a luck-knot, braided cord, cheap and skillful, the kind sold at a hundred stalls. Except that when Vane picked it up, her fingers prickled with a faint warmth, like holding a bird that had recently flown.

"He left it on the sill," Wren said. "Facing the door."

Facing the door. Where anyone entering would see it. Where *she* would see it, if she came looking — and she had come looking, and they both knew it now.

Vane stood motionless for a long moment, the little knot warm in her palm, feeling the shape of the move. It wasn't panic. Panic packed hastily and fled without paying rent. This was a message, courteous and chilling: *You found me. I knew you would. I'm sorry, and I'm gone, and please don't follow.*

Which meant Rosalind Hartwell was not merely hiding. He was *guilty* — of something large enough to be worth abandoning a five-year life over — and he was kind enough to apologize for it, and clever enough to weaponize the apology.

"North road," she said. "Toward Mireholt. Toward the fens." She crossed to the map on her wall, the great survey chart of the northern reaches, and her finger traced the route Ashe had secretly ridden, the route Hartwell was now walking, until both lines converged on the same blank space in the marsh country where the surveyors had written, in faded eighteenth-century script, *old chapel lands, unassessed* — and beneath it, older still, almost scraped away: a name.

*Hollow Choir.*

"There," Vane said softly. "That's where the song ends." She turned to Wren. "Wake Cade. Provision for a week. You leave within the hour, and you send birds daily." Then, after a breath: "And have them bring the tracker. Dole rides with the second party. Tell the General I'm invoking the emergency clause — Article Nine. Full authority."

Wren's face tightened. "Article Nine, ma'am? That gives you warrant to... the methods won't be limited."

"No," Vane agreed. "They won't."

She looked down at the luck-knot in her hand, at the small warm ghost of craft in it, and thought of Ede, who had been nine years old forever, and of the ash on Candlemas Street, and of the long cold winter coming down over the fens like a lid on a pot. Somewhere north, a young man with careful eyes was running toward the thing she had given her whole life to preventing, and she found — and this was the part she did not examine, would not examine, folded away quick and neat as a letter into a drawer — that some small stubborn part of her hoped he was fast.

But hope was not policy. Policy was Article Nine, signed in her own hand, and the tracker in chains, and the net drawing closed over the marsh country strand by strand.

"Go," she told Wren. "Before the snow."

After he'd gone she stood alone in the lamplight a long while, the frost feathering up the dark windows, the luck-knot lying on her blotter like a small accused heart. Then she took out a fresh sheet of paper, dipped her pen, and began to write the order that would teach her, over the weeks to come, exactly how far she was willing to go — and how much of herself the going would cost.

North, the snow was already falling.
