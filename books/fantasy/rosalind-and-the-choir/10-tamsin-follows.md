## 10. Tamsin Follows

Tamsin Ferro had lit the lamps of Vellamere for eleven years, and she knew the city the way a sailor knows weather — not by maps but by feel, by the pressure in the air before anything breaks.

The break came on the third night after Rosalind vanished.

She was working her round along Candlemaker's Row when she saw it: his window, dark. Not merely dark — stripped. The shutters hung crooked, one torn from its hinge, and through the glass she could see the room beyond, bare as a picked bone. His blanket gone. His kettle gone. Even the chipped blue cup he'd kept on the sill for six years, the one he'd refused to replace no matter how many times she offered, gone.

Lamplighters notice things. It was the whole of their trade, really — noticing which wicks had drowned, which brackets had rusted, which doors on a street stood open that should be shut. And Tamsin noticed, standing in the slush with her pole cold in her hands, that the door of Rosalind's building had been forced. Not broken open by thieves; forced *officially*, with the neat black wax seal of the Inquisition still clinging to one splintered jamb like a leech.

She stood there a long time. A constable passed and nodded to her, because everyone nodded to lamplighters; they were furniture, fixtures, safe. She nodded back and did not move until his footsteps faded.

Then she climbed the stairs.

* * *

The room told its story in the way rooms do — obliquely, in negatives. No blood, but a dark stain on the floorboards near the bed, scrubbed at hastily with something that smelled of lye and hadn't quite finished the work. Two chairs. One overturned. And beneath the loose floorboard by the hearth — the board Rosalind thought she didn't know about, because he didn't know she'd watched him check it a hundred times over six years — nothing. The hiding place was empty, but the dust around it was disturbed, swept clean by recent hands.

Someone had taken what was there. Or Rosalind had taken it himself and run.

Tamsin crouched there in the cold room, her breath fogging, and made herself look at the stain again. It was a great deal of blood. More than a nosebleed. More than a bar fight. Someone had bled out or nearly bled out on these boards, and then someone else had come with questions and seals, and Rosalind — steady, careful, laughably cautious Rosalind, who counted his coins twice and never gave his true name to anyone but her — had run north before dawn without telling her.

Without trusting her.

That was the part that sat in her chest like a swallowed stone. Not fear for him, though there was plenty of that, a whole cold ocean of it. But the other thing underneath: *he left me.* Six years of shared suppers and mended coats and her covering for him every time an Inquisitorial clerk looked too long at his stall, and he had not knocked on her shutter in the night. He had not left so much as a chalk mark, the old signal they'd invented years ago — a circle with a slash, meaning *trouble, lie low* — on any wall between here and the north gate.

He had decided, all on his own, that she was better off ignorant.

"Damn you," she said quietly, to the empty room. Her voice cracked on it, which annoyed her.

She got up. She took the only thing left in the room worth taking: a scrap of paper caught behind the bed frame, half-burned at one edge, bearing four words in a stranger's shaking hand — *the Choir must not* — and nothing more. She folded it into her glove, where it lay against her palm like a coal.

Then she went to find Odile Marchetti.

* * *

Odile ran a pie shop off Tanner's Lane that sold, according to its sign, the best eel pies in Vellamere, and which sold, according to anyone who actually needed it, information. Tamsin had done the woman small favors for years — oil for her shop lanterns at cost, a word in the right ear when the ward tax collectors came sniffing — and favors, in Vellamere, were currency that never rotted.

"You want to know about dead couriers," Odile said, rolling dough with forearms like a smith's. "Funny. So does everyone this week."

"Who's everyone?"

"The Inquisition, mostly." Odile glanced up. Her eyes were quick and gray and missed nothing. "Vane's people have been through every registry, every toll book, every ferry manifest since the courier died. They're looking for whoever he touched last. And here's the interesting bit, lamb — they've already found him. A charm-seller from the night market. Hartwell, some name like that. There's a warrant drawn up. Quiet one. They want him breathing."

Tamsin kept her face still. Eleven years of lighting lamps in neighborhoods where the wrong expression could cost you taught you that much. "And has anyone seen him?"

"Not since the north gate, dawn three days past. On foot, in the snow." Odile's rolling pin paused. "Now here's my question, and you'll answer it or you'll buy your pies elsewhere forever. Why does a lamplighter care?"

Tamsin weighed lying against truth and chose something between them, which was usually safest. "He's my friend. He's in trouble. I want to reach him before the Inquisition does."

"And if reaching him means the Inquisition reaches *you*?"

"Then I'll have company on the road."

Odile laughed — a short, hard bark — and wiped her hands. "You've got more spine than sense, which is the Vellamere way." She jerked her chin toward the back. "Come through. I'll give you two things. One: the fen roads are watched from Mireholt south, but the peat-cutters' paths aren't, because nobody official remembers they exist. I do. I'll draw them for you. Two—" she hesitated, and for the first time the sharpness in her face flickered into something older and wearier, "—there's a woman in Saltrow, past the fens. Widow Anselm. If you get that far, tell her Odile still keeps the ledger. She'll know what it means."

"What ledger?"

"The one the Inquisition burned," Odile said. "Or thought it did."

* * *

Tamsin left Vellamere two nights later, at the change of the watch, wearing her father's old drover's coat and carrying a pack that held bread, cheese, a flint, and the folded scrap of burned paper. She left a chalk mark on Rosalind's doorframe — circle, slash — though she knew he'd never see it. Some habits were prayers, not messages.

The peat-cutters' paths were exactly as Odile promised: invisible, miserable, and empty. For three days Tamsin walked north through a country dissolving into winter, the fens freezing in patches, black water going to gray glass, reeds standing in the ice like the ribs of drowned things. She slept in cutters' huts with her boots on. She ate snow when the water skin froze. Her hands cracked and bled around the lamp-pole calluses she'd never lose.

On the fourth day she found the first grave.

It was off the path, in a stand of frozen alders — a low mound, unmarked except for a strip of cloth tied to a branch, dyed a deep, deliberate red. She might have walked past it. But there were others. Once she noticed the red strips she saw them everywhere: on alder branches, on fence posts, on the leaning doorframes of ruined cottages half-swallowed by the marsh. Dozens. Then hundreds. A whole country of small red flags, each one marking a mound of earth or sometimes just a memory of earth, a hollow where something had been removed.

Near Mireholt she found a cottage still standing, barely, its roof fallen in on one side, and inside, sheltered from the worst of the rot, someone had painted words on the wall. The paint was old, faded, but legible:

*Here lived Mara Quill, hedge-wife. Healer. Burned under the Edict, 4th of Frostmoon. She pulled nine children out of the marsh fever and the ninth one grew up to inform on her.*

Tamsin stood in that ruined room for a long time.

She had grown up on the Edict the way fish grow up in water — it was simply the medium of life, unquestioned. Magic was forbidden. Magic had started the last war; everyone knew that; the Emberstone had ended it and the crown had sworn never again. Hedge-witches were criminals, and the Inquisition protected honest folk from them. That was the story, told in schoolrooms and sermons, smooth as river stone.

But here was the stone's rough underside, scratched onto a wall by some grieving hand: a woman who healed children, killed by one of the children she'd saved. And all around it, across miles of fen, the red flags — not a few scattered criminals, but a *people*, rooted, ordinary, beloved enough to be mourned in cloth and paint for twenty years.

They hadn't been driven underground because they were monsters. They'd been driven underground because being visible was death, and they had chosen to live.

Tamsin thought of Rosalind counting his coins twice. Of the way he flinched — just slightly, just once, the first time she'd ever mentioned the Inquisition in his hearing, quickly smoothed away. Of six years of friendship built on a foundation she'd never examined, because he'd never let her see it, because letting her see it would have killed him.

*He wasn't hiding something from me,* she thought, standing among the red flags with the wind keening through the alders. *He was hiding me from it.*

Her loyalty, which had always been a simple thing — warm, uncomplicated, *my friend, right or wrong* — shifted under her like ice underfoot. It did not break. It deepened. It stopped being loyalty to a person and became loyalty to a truth: that what had been done to these people, and to him, was wrong, had always been wrong, and that she had spent eleven years lighting lamps for a city that burned healers and called it safety.

In Saltrow, Widow Anselm turned out to be a small, fierce woman of seventy with ink-stained fingers, who listened to Odile's name and the phrase *still keeps the ledger* and then looked at Tamsin for a long, long moment.

"You're the friend," Anselm said at last. "The one from the city. He spoke of you."

Tamsin's heart slammed. "Rosalind. He came through here?"

"Two days ago, with a soldier called Brannoc. Heading for the Choir road." Anselm rose, stiff-kneed, and began pulling things from cupboards — dried meat, a flask, a pair of fur-lined mittens far too big for Tamsin's hands. "And you're three days behind the Inquisitor's second wave, girl, so drink this and listen. Vane herself is coming now. Not her clerks. *Her.* And she's bringing something on a leash that can smell magic like a hound smells blood."

"A mage?" Tamsin said. "She uses a captured mage as a tracker? After everything—" She gestured helplessly at the walls, the fen, the twenty years of graves.

Anselm's mouth twisted. "Now you understand the shape of it. Fear makes strange harnesses." She pressed the pack into Tamsin's arms. "The Choir road forks at the frozen causeway. Take the high fork, even though it's longer. And Tamsin Ferro—" the old woman gripped her wrist, surprisingly strong, "—when you find him, and you will, tell him the ledger survives. Tell him the names are all written down. He'll know what it means. He was raised on those names whether Aldous ever admitted it or not."

Outside, the sky had gone the color of pewter, and the wind had changed, swinging north, carrying with it the first fat flakes of a storm that meant to bury the world.

Tamsin pulled on the too-big mittens, turned her face toward the causeway, and began to run.
