## 11. The Master's Ghost

The fire in the wayhouse's back room had burned down to a red heart by the time Brannoc finally spoke.

Rosalind had been waiting for it all evening. The old soldier had been circling something since Mireholt, since the ice road, since the moment Rosalind had pulled fire out of the frozen air to save his life — a thing that could not be unsaid, unshown, untaken back. Brannoc had watched him do it with an expression Rosalind couldn't read at the time. Now, watching the man turn a tin cup slowly in his scarred hands, he understood that the expression had been recognition.

"You knew," Rosalind said. "Before I ever did anything. You knew what I was."

"I knew what you looked like." Brannoc set the cup down. "You have Aldous Wren's hands. Long fingers. A scholar's fingers, not a laborer's. And you hold your shoulders like a man carrying something breakable inside his chest." He looked up, and the firelight caught the gray in his beard and the old white scar that split one eyebrow. "I told you I'd known your master. That was true. What I didn't tell you is how."

Outside, the wind worried at the shutters. Somewhere in the fens, ice was thickening over water that would rather stay moving. Rosalind sat very still on the bench across the hearth, the map's weight a familiar ache against his ribs where he'd sewn it into his coat lining.

"Then tell me how," he said.

Brannoc took his time about it. He fed another split of pine onto the fire, waited for it to catch, watched the flames climb the resin-dark wood as though composing his answer there among the sparks.

"Twenty-two years ago," he said, "there were seven of them. Seven mages who walked into the Hollow Choir together, and only one who walked out."

Rosalind's mouth went dry. "The sealing."

"You know the story they tell children. That the Choir sealed itself. That the Emberstone chose silence, or the war simply burned itself out, or some saint sang the vault shut with her dying breath." He shook his head. "Stories are kind. The truth is that seven people went down into that vault with a working so large it needed every one of them to hold it, and they meant to close the door behind them from the inside. All seven. That was the price. That was always the price. A seal like that doesn't take a lock; it takes a life given freely, held in place forever. Six of them paid it."

"And the seventh?"

Brannoc met his eyes across the fire.

"Aldous Wren walked out of the fens three days later, alone, half-dead, and told the survivors of the mage companies that the others had stayed behind willingly while he carried the final word of the working out into the world. He said someone had to live to guard the secret. He said it was their gift to him." The old soldier's voice flattened. "I was there when he said it. I was a sergeant then, attached to the last company still fighting at the fen's edge. I believed him. We all believed him. We wanted to believe him, because the alternative was unthinkable."

* * *

Rosalind heard himself ask the question from very far away. "How do you know it isn't true?"

"Because I found one of them." Brannoc's jaw worked. "Two years after the war ended. A hedgewitch named Corla Vess, who'd been listed among the dead. She wasn't dead. She was dying — slow, wrong, her magic bleeding out of her like a wound that wouldn't close. The seal was failing around her, see, because it had been built for seven anchors and it only had six. She'd crawled out through some crack in the working, or been pushed, and she spent two years trying to reach anyone who'd listen before her body gave out." He stared into the fire. "She reached me. I was running supply caravans through the marches by then. She told me everything. How they'd stood in the circle of stones. How the working had asked each of them, one by one, to give themselves. And how Aldous —"

He stopped.

"How Aldous what?" Rosalind whispered.

"How Aldous stepped back." The words came out like stones dropped into deep water. "When his turn came. When the Choir opened its mouth for him. He stepped back, and the working staggered, and Corla said the whole vault screamed. Six of them threw themselves into the gap he left — six instead of seven, holding what seven were meant to hold — and the seal closed crooked. Imperfect. Leaking ever since, like a roof missing a tile. And Aldous ran." Brannoc's voice cracked, just once, and he mastered it. "Corla lived long enough to curse his name and long enough to make me swear I'd watch him. So I did. Twenty years, off and on. I watched him settle in Vellamere. Take an apprentice. Disappear. Come back. I watched him teach children how to hide, Rosalind. I always wondered why a man who'd run from the greatest sacrifice magic ever asked would spend his life teaching other people to be small."

The fire popped. A log settled, shedding a constellation of sparks up the chimney.

"He taught me to hide," Rosalind said. His own voice sounded strange to him, thin and young. "That was the first lesson. Before I ever learned a cantrip. *The world will kill you for what you are, so become nothing.* Five years I've lived by that. Five years I've hated it and obeyed it, because he told me it was the only way to survive." He laughed, and the laugh came out broken. "And the whole time — the whole time he was teaching me to survive, he was the one who —"

He couldn't finish it. His hands were shaking. He pressed them flat against his knees and breathed, the way Aldous had taught him, four counts in, four counts out, the little discipline that was supposed to steady a mage's mind before any working. It didn't work. Nothing worked. The room seemed to tilt around the fire's red heart.

Every mile of his flight from Vellamere. Every night market, every fever-charm sold for copper, every year of flinching from his own reflection in shop glass, every friendship conducted in the dark with the lamp of himself turned low — all of it traced back, in a single unbroken line, to a man stepping backward out of a circle of stones while his friends burned.

"I fled my apprenticeship," Rosalind said. "Did you know that? Everyone assumes I was caught doing something, or that there was a scandal. There wasn't. I woke up one morning and realized I couldn't breathe in that house anymore. Couldn't stand being shaped by him. And I told myself it was instinct, some animal wisdom, that I'd felt the trap closing." He looked up. "Was I right? Was there something wrong with me even then, or was I just — seeing him clearly, before I had the words for it?"

Brannoc was quiet for a long moment.

"I think," he said carefully, "that children know more than we give them credit for. And I think Aldous Wren has spent twenty years being very good at making people feel safe right up until the moment he needs them not to be."

"The courier." The thought arrived cold, fully formed, terrible. "The man who died in my room. The map. *The Choir must not sing for her.* If Aldous helped seal the Choir — if he knows what's down there better than anyone alive — then he knows exactly what the Emberstone is worth. To the crown. To the Inquisition. To anyone who wants power over magic itself." Rosalind's stomach turned over. "He sent that courier. Didn't he. This whole trail — the map in his handwriting, leading me through the fens, leading me to the vault — it's not an accident I'm carrying it. It's a summons."

"Or a leash," Brannoc said grimly. "Or bait in a trap whose shape we can't see yet. I don't know. But I'll tell you what I do know." He leaned forward, elbows on his knees, and the firelight made his face look carved out of old wood. "Corla Vess made me swear two things before she died. First, that I'd never let Aldous Wren near the Hollow Choir again. Second —" he hesitated, and something almost gentle moved through the gruffness of his face — "second, that if I ever met someone with his teaching in their hands, I'd judge them apart from him. She said the sin was his. She said the ones who came after shouldn't inherit the shame of it."

Rosalind looked at his own hands. Long fingers. A scholar's fingers. They were trembling.

"I've spent five years believing I was a coward," he said quietly. "Running. Hiding. Selling luck-knots to people who deserved real help and getting charms that were mostly theater. I thought that was my nature. I thought smallness was just — what I was made of." He swallowed. "And now you're telling me the smallness was a curriculum. That I was taught to be a ghost by a man who already was one."

"That's not the same as being innocent of it," Brannoc said. Not cruelly. Honestly, the way soldiers are honest, because they can't afford anything else. "You chose to keep hiding, these five years. Nobody made you. But there's a difference between a chain and a habit, Rosalind, and you're about to learn which one you've been wearing."

* * *

He didn't sleep. He sat by the window of the little loft room with the map spread open on his knees, no longer needing to hide it from Brannoc, and studied the handwriting he knew better than his own.

Aldous's script was unmistakable — the tall ascenders, the peculiar hooked *r*, the way he crossed his *t*s with a single impatient stroke that trailed off like a thought abandoned mid-sentence. Rosalind had copied that hand for years, practicing sigils, transcribing theory, filling notebook after notebook in a style that was half his master's and half his own. Looking at the map now was like looking into a mirror that showed someone else's face wearing his expression.

*The route leads through the fens.* Of course it did. Aldous would want him to come this way — past the villages where the purge had fallen hardest, past the mass graves nobody marked, past the frozen shrines where hedge-witches had once left offerings and now left nothing at all. Aldous would want him to *see*. To arrive at the Choir already angry. Already primed.

*The Choir must not sing for her.*

For her. For the Inquisitor General? For Vane? Rosalind turned the phrase over and over, and it refused to resolve. Unless — unless *she* was someone else entirely. Someone Aldous feared. Someone the map was meant to protect the stone *from*, or deliver it *to*, depending on things Rosalind couldn't yet see.

He pressed his palm flat against the parchment. It was warm. It had been warm since the courier died, faintly, persistently warm, like a body recently vacated. He'd told himself it was imagination. He told himself a lot of things.

Below him, through the floorboards, he heard Brannoc moving — the deliberate sounds of a man cleaning weapons by firelight, checking straps, doing the quiet arithmetic of the road ahead. The old soldier hadn't asked for payment. Hadn't asked for anything except answers, and now he'd given more than he'd taken. Rosalind wondered what Corla Vess's ghost would make of that. Wondered what his own ghost would look like, twenty years from now, if he kept walking roads chosen by other people.

*You're about to learn which one you've been wearing.*

He thought about the flare on the ice road — the moment the magic had come out of him like a shout after years of whispering, wild and bright and *his*. For one instant he had not been hiding. For one instant he had been the largest version of himself that existed, and the snow had bent around him like a congregation.

He wanted to feel that again. That frightened him more than Vane, more than the fens, more than the vault waiting under the marsh with its silent singing stone.

Because Aldous had wanted it too. That was the poison in it. Every ambition Rosalind could name — to stop hiding, to be seen, to take up real power and use it well — was an ambition his master had planted, watered, pruned. How could he trust any desire in his own chest when the gardener had been a traitor?

But then — Corla Vess. Six names in a circle of stones who had given everything and been betrayed by the seventh. Their working still held, crooked and leaking, twenty years on. Whatever else the past contained, it contained *them*: people who had chosen the large thing, the hard thing, the thing that cost. They were his inheritance too. More truly Aldous's than Aldous was.

Rosalind folded the map along its old creases and slid it back into his coat.

"We go faster tomorrow," he said through the floorboards.

A pause. Then Brannoc's voice, dry as tinder: "Been listening, have you?"

"To the wind." Rosalind lay back on the narrow bed and stared at the rafters. "It's changing. Feel it? The pressure's dropping. Snow's coming — real snow, solstice snow."

Another pause, longer.

"Aye," Brannoc said at last, and there was something new in his voice, something that might have been dread. "I feel it. And there's one more thing you should know before we sleep, lad, because you'll want your wits about you come morning." The floor creaked as he climbed the ladder, and his head rose through the hatch, firelit and grave. "Corla told me one thing more about the sealing. About what the six of them did, in the last moments, when they saw Aldous step back and knew the working would fail."

Rosalind sat up. "What?"

"They changed it." Brannoc's eyes were steady and unhappy. "They couldn't hold the full seal with six, so they did something desperate — they bound the Emberstone's song to a condition. A trigger. Something that would wake it, wake the whole Choir, if it was ever disturbed by the wrong hand." He gripped the edge of the hatch. "They bound it to blood, Rosalind. To the bloodline of the one who betrayed them. So that someday, if Aldous Wren or anything of his ever came back to that vault —"

The wind rose outside, keening across the fens, and beneath it — faint, impossible, rising from the frozen earth itself — Rosalind heard, for the first time, a note of song.
