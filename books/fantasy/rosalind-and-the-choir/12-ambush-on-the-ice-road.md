## 12. Ambush on the Ice Road

The causeway appeared out of the fog like a spine of bone laid across the marsh.

Brannoc had been calling it the Ice Road since Mireholt, though it was no road at all — a raised track of packed clay and old flagstones that the fen-folk kept clear in winter, when the ditches froze hard enough to bear a cart. Now, three days north of the wayhouse, frost had welded every stone into a single white ribbon running straight as an arrowshot toward the distant smudge of the Choir hills. On either side, black water showed through gaps in the ice like eyes through a mask.

"We'll make the stones by tomorrow dusk," Brannoc said, breath streaming. "If the weather holds."

The weather did not hold.

Rosalind felt it before he saw it — a wrongness in the air, a pressure behind his ears like the moment before a fever breaks. Five years of hiding had not dulled that sense; if anything, the constant suppression of it had made it sharper, the way a man blindfolded learns to hear. He stopped mid-stride, satchel strap biting into his shoulder, and turned to look back down the long white line they had walked since dawn.

"What is it?" Brannoc asked.

"Someone's coming." Rosalind's mouth was dry. "Behind us. More than one."

Brannoc squinted into the grey. "I don't see—"

"Not with your eyes." The words were out before he could stop them, and he watched them land: the old soldier's face going carefully still, the way a man goes still when a dog he thought was tame shows its teeth. Rosalind forced himself to keep talking, because stopping would be worse. "There's a weight coming up the road. Cold and deliberate. Like — like footsteps inside my skull."

"How many?"

"Four." He swallowed. "Maybe five."

Brannoc unslung the axe from his back with the unhurried motion of a man who had done it ten thousand times. "Then we stop arguing about whether your master taught you anything useful," he said, "and we decide where we fight."

* * *

They chose the narrows, a quarter mile on, where the causeway crossed a deep channel between two drowned alders and the ice on either side was thin as window-glass. It was the best ground available, which was to say it was terrible: no cover, no high ground, nowhere to run except forward or back along a road barely wide enough for two men abreast.

"They won't kill you outright," Rosalind said. His voice sounded strange to him, pitched too high. "Vane wants the map. She wants me breathing, at least until I've told her where the Choir is."

"And me?"

"You're a complication. Complications get simplified."

Brannoc grunted. "Cheerful lad." He tested the ice at the causeway's edge with the butt of his axe; it dimpled but held. "When they come, stay behind me. Whatever else happens, whatever you feel like doing—" he looked at Rosalind then, a long, level look, "—you do what keeps you alive. Understood? Not what keeps *me* alive. You."

"I'm not going to let you—"

"You misunderstand." Brannoc's voice was quiet, and underneath the gruffness there was something almost gentle. "I've fought in two wars and buried more friends than I can name. I know how this arithmetic works. One old soldier against five crown agents is a bad trade, and I'd not have you spend yourself trying to fix it. Your master didn't send me to die for you. He sent me because the last stretch of that road needs a guide who knows it, and because—" he hesitated, and for once the ex-soldier seemed to grope for words, "—because Aldous Hartwell owed a debt to someone, once, and I think paying it to you was the only way left to him."

Rosalind had no answer. The cold pressed in. Somewhere behind them, far down the white ribbon of the road, a crow lifted from a dead tree and flew low over the marsh, and he knew — the knowing arrived whole, without evidence, the way magic always came to him — that the crow had been frightened off by men.

"They're close," he whispered.

* * *

They came out of the fog the way Vane's people must have been trained to: spread wide, silent, closing from both ends of the causeway at once so there would be no choosing which direction to flee. Two ahead, two behind, and a fifth walking the center line with the unhurried gait of a landlord come to collect rent.

The one in front wore no livery, but everything about him said Inquisitorial all the same — the grey travel cloak cut for movement, the short truncheon of black iron at his belt that Rosalind recognized from stories told in whispers: cold-iron, proof against charm and hex, the tool they used on mages who would not come quietly.

"Hold there," the man called, when they were thirty paces apart. His voice carried flat across the ice. "In the name of the Inquisitor General. You'll stand and be searched. You'll come with us to Vellamere. Do this, and no harm comes to anyone today."

Rosalind's heart was slamming so hard he could hear it in his teeth. Behind the speaker, the other agents fanned out onto the frozen margins of the causeway, boots finding the ice with uncanny confidence — and that was wrong, he realized, *that was wrong*, ordinary men did not walk fen-ice like dancers —

They weren't all ordinary. The fifth figure, the one at the rear, moved like water given legs, and when the fog thinned around her face Rosalind saw the brand on her cheekbone: a small circle with a slash through it. A captured mage. A tracker. Vane had written about such things in the registries — hedge-witches taken alive and leashed with oath-collars, their gifts turned against their own kind.

She was looking directly at him. Her expression was not cruel. That was somehow the worst part. She looked at him the way a drowning woman looks at driftwood.

"Last chance," the leader said. "Kneel and be bound."

Brannoc shifted his weight, axe low, and murmured without moving his lips: "On my word, boy, you run for the stones. Don't look back. Don't—"

The tracker raised her hand.

It was nothing dramatic — a flick of two fingers, a word in a language Rosalind hadn't heard spoken aloud in five years — and the world *tilted*. The fog bent toward them like a curtain drawn. The ice under Rosalind's feet woke with a sound like a struck bell, and every hair on his body stood on end as power rolled up the causeway, hunting him, tasting the air for the shape of his blood.

*Find him,* the spell said, in the old tongue. *Show me.*

And Rosalind, who had spent five years being small and dull and invisible, felt himself flare in the tracker's senses like a bonfire in a dark field. There was no hiding now. She gasped — actually gasped, staggering, her hand flying to the collar at her throat as if the thing had burned her — and shouted something to her handler in a cracked voice:

"He's *full*, sir. He's not a hedge-charmer, he's — he's a wellspring, take him *now*—"

Everything happened very fast after that.

The leader drew his cold-iron truncheon and came on at a run. The two flanking agents pulled slingshots and weighted nets from under their cloaks — tools for taking a mage alive, Rosalind understood distantly, tools designed for exactly his size and shape. Brannoc roared his soldier's roar and met the first man head-on, axe ringing off truncheon in a shower of sparks, and the second agent's net whistled through the fog toward Rosalind's shoulders—

—and time went strange.

Later, Rosalind would try to describe what happened next and fail, because the truth was that it did not feel like deciding. It felt like surfacing. All those years of holding his breath — every charm sold for copper that was only knots and misdirection, every fever cooled with willow-bark instead of will, every night lying awake pressing down on the bright thing inside him like a hand over a singing bowl — all of it rose at once, and the hand came away, and the bowl *sang*.

He threw out his arm without knowing why, and the net stopped.

Not slowed. Not deflected. Stopped — hanging in the air a hand's breadth from his chest, its weights trembling, its ropes creaking as if against a gale that touched nothing else. The fog around him bent outward in a perfect ring. Frost bloomed up the length of the causeway in feathered patterns, silver ferns spreading from his boots across the stone, and the black water on either side of the road shivered and went glass-still.

The exhilaration hit first, and it was terrible in its sweetness. This was what he was. This was what he had starved. Power poured up through him from somewhere below thought — from the blood his mother gave him, from the training Aldous had burned into him, from five years of dammed flood — and it felt like the first full breath after drowning, like sunlight after the long winter, like *coming home to a house he'd been told was condemned*. He could feel everything: the heartbeat of every man on the causeway, the slow pulse of the marsh beneath the ice, the tracker's spell unraveling in his presence like thread slipping off a spool. He could have snapped the net to powder. He could have thrown all four agents into the channel with a gesture. He could feel how easy it would be, and that easiness frightened him more than the truncheons did.

Because the horror came right behind it, riding its shoulder.

The tracker screamed.

Not from anything he had done — from what she could see. Through whatever sight her collar granted her, Rosalind must have appeared as what he truly was, and she was on her knees now on the ice, both hands pressed to her branded cheek, weeping: "*What are you? What ARE you?*" And the leader had stopped dead ten paces away, truncheon half-raised, staring at the ring of bent fog and the suspended net and the frost-feathers crawling toward his boots, and on his face was not anger but the particular pale awe of a man watching a story from childhood step out of the firelight.

Witnesses. The word arrived in Rosalind's mind cold and clear, cutting through the song of his own power. *Five witnesses.* Everything he had built — the careful life, the copper charms, the lie of ordinariness — was burning down around him in real time, and there was no version of the next hour in which Sera Vane did not hear the words *wellspring on the ice road* and turn the whole machinery of the Inquisition north.

Brannoc used the stillness. The soldier wrenched free of his grapple, hooked the nearest agent behind the knee, and put him down hard on the flagstones with a crack that echoed off the alders. "BOY!" he bellowed. "The stones! RUN!"

But the second agent had recovered his nerve, and he had a crossbow, and he had decided — sensibly, professionally — that a mage who could stop a net in midair might not be stopped by rope, and that bolts traveled faster than spells. Rosalind saw it happen with that terrible new clarity: the man dropping to one knee, the bow coming up, the quarrel's steel tip catching the grey light — and swinging, not toward Rosalind, but past him. Toward Brannoc. Toward the broad, bellowing, mortal back of the only friend the road had given him.

Time did not go strange this time. Time simply ran out.

Rosalind moved.

He did not run. He *arrived* — one instant at the causeway's center, the next standing between Brannoc and the crossbow, having crossed eight paces in the space between heartbeats, his body carried by power rather than muscle. The quarrel loosed. He caught it.

His bare hand closed around the bolt a foot from Brannoc's spine, and the wood smoked and frosted in his grip, and the force of it drove him back a stumbling step, and it *hurt* — a hot wire of pain up his arm, because power was not free, power was never free, Aldous had taught him that in the first lesson of his life — but he held it. He stood in the middle of the ice road with a crossbow quarrel crushed in his smoking fist and light — actual light, faint gold, like lamplight through honey — bleeding off his shoulders into the fog, and he heard himself speak in a voice that was his own and yet deeper, older, worn smooth like the stones of the Choir itself:

"That's enough."

The word went out across the marsh like a wave. The crossbowman sat down abruptly on the ice as if his legs had been unplugged. The tracker bowed her head to the stone. Even the fog seemed to pause, hanging in the air like held breath.

For three heartbeats, nobody moved.

Then Rosalind opened his fist, and the ruined quarrel dropped, and the light guttered out, and the exhaustion hit him like a wave over a seawall. His knees buckled. Brannoc caught him under the arm, hauling him upright with one iron grip, and the old soldier's voice came rough and shaken beside his ear:

"Now, boy. While they're still remembering how to breathe. *Now.*"

They ran.

Down the white ribbon of the causeway, north, toward the hills where the Hollow Choir slept under its burden of stone and snow. Rosalind's legs were water, his vision swimming, every step paid for in coin he didn't know he had — but Brannoc half-carried him, and the road tilted gently upward, and behind them the shouts began, and then, worse than the shouts, silence.

At the top of the rise, where the causeway climbed toward the black line of the fens' last trees, Rosalind broke his own rule and looked back.

The four agents stood clustered on the ice road far below, tiny as chess pieces. They were not pursuing. They were kneeling — all four of them, even the leader, even the crossbowman — gathered around the tracker, who had risen to her feet and was pointing north. Pointing at him. And even at this distance, even through the fog, Rosalind could feel her voice carrying up the road, pitched to carry, meant for him:

"*The Choir will know its own!*"

Brannoc swore softly. "What does that mean?"

Rosalind shook his head. But the words had gone into him like a splinter, and beneath his breastbone, where the power lived, something answered — a faint, far-off resonance, like a bell heard through miles of earth, like a song waiting for a singer.

The map in his satchel felt suddenly warm against his hip.

Ahead, the snow began to fall — thick, silent, erasing the road behind them — and somewhere south, moving fast through the same storm, a woman with a ledger-full of names was about to learn that the thing she had hunted her whole life had just shown its face on the ice.
