## 13. Taken

Tamsin Ferro had followed a dead man's trail for nine days, and the trail had finally begun to smell like snow and blood.

She crouched at the edge of the ice road where it crossed the frozen causeway above the Blackmere fen, her breath pluming in the grey light, and counted what the ambush had left behind. A boot print half-filled with slush. A scorch mark on the ice that no lamp-oil fire could explain — the ice beneath it melted clean through to black water, then frozen again in jagged petals. And blood. Not much. Enough.

Rosalind had used magic here. She knew it the way she knew weather coming: not by proof but by the ache behind her teeth.

*You lied to me,* she thought, not for the first time, and the thought had lost its anger somewhere around the fourth day and become something quieter and worse — a kind of grief with its sleeves rolled up. *You lied to me for five years, and you did it so well I never once caught you.*

She had caught him now. The scorch mark saw to that. Whatever Rosalind Hartwell was, he was not the shy charm-seller who'd shared her table at the Drowned Bell every market night, who'd laughed at her impressions of the lamplighters' guildmaster, who'd once spent an entire evening teaching her to fold paper boats because she'd said she'd never learned. He was something the city of Vellamere would burn him for.

And she was following him anyway.

That was the part she couldn't explain to herself, so she'd stopped trying. Somewhere between the city walls and the fens, between the wayhouse at Mireholt and the burned-out shrine where she'd found evidence of the purge — lists of names, hedge-witches and herbwives and one eleven-year-old boy marked *rendered* in a clerk's neat hand — her loyalty had stopped being about Rosalind specifically and become about all of them. About the boy named *rendered*. About the fever-charms Rosalind sold for copper to people who'd have died without them. About the whole silent, frightened country of people like him, hiding in plain sight, folding themselves small enough to survive.

The Inquisitors called it treason. Tamsin was beginning to think it was the only honest thing left in Vellamere.

She stood, stamped feeling back into her feet, and looked north along the causeway. The ice road ran straight as a ruled line toward a dark ridge of hills where, according to the smuggler she'd bribed in Mireholt with two months' lamplighter wages, the Hollow Choir lay buried. The smuggler had also told her — after the second coin — that soldiers had passed through two days ago. Inquisitorial soldiers. Grey cloaks, white staves, and a woman leading them with eyes like a ledger.

Vane. It had to be Vane. Tamsin had heard the name whispered in the city's back rooms ever since the courier died: *the Inquisitor General's own hound is off her leash, she's hunting something in the fens.*

Which meant Rosalind wasn't just running from his past anymore. He was running from the most patient hunter in the crown's employ, along a road with no cover, toward a place everyone seemed to want him to reach.

*Or,* said the cold practical voice in her head, the one that kept her alive on rooftops in February, *he isn't running at all. He's being driven.*

She pulled her hood up and went north along the ice.

* * *

She found the second ambush site an hour before dusk, and this time there were bodies.

Three of them, laid out with a carelessness that told her they weren't Rosalind's doing — he'd never been careless about anything in his life. Grey-cloaked agents, Inquisitorial, their staves shattered, their throats opened by someone who knew soldiering. One still gripped a length of chain net weighted with lead. Witch-irons. Tamsin had seen them used once, at a public rendering when she was fourteen, and had vomited behind a barricade while the crowd sang a hymn.

Someone had fought these men off. Not Rosalind — the wounds were blade-work, broad and brutal. The old soldier, then. Brannoc. The name had come up twice in her asking-around: a gruff ex-legion man seen walking with a young clerkish fellow through the fens, asking questions about *her*, whatever that meant.

Tamsin knelt by the nearest body and went through its pockets with hands that only shook a little. She'd stopped apologizing to herself for this somewhere around day five. Papers. A token of office — a tin badge stamped with the eye-and-flame. A strip of dried meat she ate without ceremony because hunger made sentimentality expensive.

And a dispatch case, wax-sealed, addressed in a precise hand: *To the Inquisitor General, by the hand of S. Vane, from the field.*

Her conscience put up a brief, token fight. Her fingers were already breaking the seal.

Most of it was cipher, columns of letters that meant nothing to her. But the last page was plain text, a summary for a superior who evidently didn't trust codes any more than Tamsin did:

*—subject confirmed en route to Choir site. First wave engaged and repelled; losses three. Subject demonstrated active craft on the causeway (see attached witness accounts). Recommend second wave hold at Fenmarch crossing until tracker arrives. Tracker requisition approved. Note: the girl Ferro has reached Mireholt. If she continues north, take her. She is worth more to us breathing than the subject is to us free. V.*

Tamsin read the paragraph twice.

*The girl Ferro has reached Mireholt. If she continues north, take her.*

They knew about her. They'd known since Mireholt — since the innkeeper, probably, or the stable-boy she'd tipped too generously, or the smuggler whose loyalty had lasted exactly as long as her coins. She had walked out of Mireholt feeling clever, feeling ahead of them, and she had been a lit candle moving through a dark room full of open eyes.

*She is worth more to us breathing than the subject is to us free.*

Breathing. As opposed to what? The word sat in her stomach like a swallowed stone. They wanted her as leverage. That was obvious enough. What wasn't obvious — what made her skin crawl in a way the cold couldn't account for — was how precisely Vane understood the shape of the thing. Not information. Leverage. Because anyone who'd read Tamsin right would know she'd trade anything for Rosalind, and anyone who'd read *him* right would know he'd do the same.

Guilt, weaponized. That was the whole strategy. Find the person your quarry loves, and let love do the work of irons.

Tamsin folded the dispatch into her coat, next to her heart, where it felt less like intelligence and more like a diagnosis. Then she looked north at the darkening hills, and made the mistake the dispatch had predicted she would make.

She kept going.

* * *

Night came down over the fens like a lid. She traveled by the stars she knew from rooftop work — the Ploughman, the Sisters, the pale smear of the Mill-river constellation — and by the strange, faint glow that had begun to rise from the hills ahead. Not firelight. Firelight flickered. This light breathed. It swelled and dimmed in long slow pulses, amber-gold, like something enormous sleeping under the snow and dreaming.

The Choir. It had to be. The smuggler hadn't mentioned any glow, which meant either he'd never come this close or the glow was new, and both possibilities frightened her equally.

Near midnight, on a rise above a frozen tarn, she finally saw people — and dropped flat behind a wind-bent alder before her shadow could give her away.

Below, at the mouth of a ravine, maybe forty figures were making camp. Soldiers. Tents, cook-fires, picket lines, the disciplined geometry of a professional force. And apart from the main camp, set slightly uphill in a tent of grey silk that caught the pulsing light and threw it back wrong, a single lantern burned beside a camp table where a woman sat reading, alone, in the open cold, as if winter were a formality she'd declined to observe.

Sera Vane. Tamsin knew her by description and by the stillness. Everything else in the camp moved — sentries pacing, horses stamping, fires spitting — but the woman at the table did not move at all. She read the way a hawk reads a field.

Tamsin's plan, such as it was, had been simple to the point of idiocy: get ahead of the soldiers, find Rosalind, warn him, get him off the mountain before the net closed. Looking at the camp below, she revised the plan to its honest version: get captured on purpose, near Rosalind, at the moment of maximum usefulness, and hope that being leverage gave her enough weight inside the machine to jam it.

It was a terrible plan. It was also the only one that fit the terrain.

She waited for the watch change — lamplighter's habit, reading the rhythms of other people's nights — and slipped downhill along the ravine's eastern wall, where the rock threw a wind-shadow and the snow lay thin. She got within two hundred yards of the picket line. A hundred and fifty. She could hear a soldier complaining about his boots, could smell onions and horse.

Then the light under the hill flared.

Not pulsed — *flared*, a single vast heartbeat of gold that leapt up through the snow, through the ravine walls, through the ground itself, and every soldier in the camp shouted at once, and every shadow on the snow swung like a compass needle finding north. Tamsin felt it in her chest, a pressure like a struck bell heard from inside the bell. For one impossible instant she could *hear* it too — not sound, exactly, but the memory of sound, a chord held so long it had become a place.

And in that instant, every eye in the camp turned toward the hills, and Tamsin did the bravest and stupidest thing of her life.

She ran — not away, but through. Straight across the open ground between the ravine and the treeline beyond, past the pickets, past the tents, using the flare-blindness the way she'd use a passing cart's shadow on a Vellamere street. She was thirty yards from the trees when the light died and a sentry, turning back too soon, saw her.

"Halt! Halt, in the Queen's name!"

She ran. Boots thundered behind her. Something whistled past her ear — a crossbow bolt burying itself in a trunk ahead with a sound like a slammed door. She jinked left, the way she'd dodged cart-traffic since childhood, and the trees took her, black branches raking her hood, snow to her knees, breath tearing.

They caught her at the stream.

Not because she was slow. Because there were six of them and one of her, and because the third man didn't chase her at all — he simply stepped out from behind a boulder she'd already passed and closed a lead-weighted net over her shoulders, and the world became iron links and falling snow and her own voice screaming words she'd be ashamed of later.

They dragged her back down through the trees. She fought the whole way, uselessly, magnificently, biting one man hard enough to draw blood and taking a rifle-butt's kiss across the temple for it. Through the red haze she registered the camp re-forming around her, faces lit orange, and the woman in grey rising from her table at last, unhurried, closing her book.

The soldiers dumped Tamsin in the snow at Vane's feet.

For a long moment the Inquisitor just looked at her. Up close her eyes were lighter than reports claimed — a pale winter grey, almost kind, which was somehow the worst thing about them. There was blood at Tamsin's temple, snow down her collar, a net crushing her arms. Vane took in all of it, and then crouched, unhurried, so their faces were level.

"Well," Vane said. "The girl Ferro."

"You know my name." Tamsin's voice came out steadier than she'd expected. Good. Let her have the steadiness.

"I make it my business to know the names of people who matter to my work." Vane reached out and, with two fingers, lifted the net's edge from Tamsin's shoulder — a small courtesy, or the performance of one. "You've walked nine days through the fens in an open winter to reach a man who lied to you for five years. I'd call that devotion. I'd also call it a waste, but then, I'm told I'm a sentimental woman."

"I'm told you're a lot of things."

"I expect most of them are true." Vane stood, brushing snow from her knee, and gestured, and the soldiers hauled Tamsin upright and marched her toward the silk tent. "Put her by the fire. Give her broth. She'll want her strength — she's going to earn her freedom tomorrow."

Tamsin planted her heels. "I'm not going to tell you anything."

"I know," Vane said, and smiled, and it was the smile of someone watching a lock recognize its key. "That's rather the point. You won't tell me anything. But he will tell me everything, and you're going to be standing there when he does."

* * *

They kept her in the silk tent, unbound except for witch-iron cuffs that weighed nothing and meant everything, and fed her hot barley broth that she ate because refusing food was a luxury for people with better plans. Through the tent's seams she watched the gold pulse under the hills grow stronger as the night deepened, faster now, like a heart climbing toward exertion. Whatever was happening in the Hollow Choir, it was happening tonight.

A little before dawn, the camp woke all at once.

Tamsin heard it before she understood it: hooves, many, hammering up the valley from the south. Shouts. The grey silk of the tent wall glowing with torchlight. A soldier's voice, cracked with something between fear and excitement: *"Inquisitor — riders — it's the second wave, they've brought the tracker—"*

And under the hoofbeats, faint but unmistakable, a new sound. Singing. A single high voice raised in the fen-witch keening style, wordless, wavering, wrong — the sound of a person being made to sing against every grain of their soul.

The tracker. The captured mage Vane had requisitioned. Tamsin pressed her bound hands against her mouth and thought of the shrine, the list of names, the boy marked *rendered*, and understood with perfect clarity what she was looking at: a human instrument, tuned by pain, played by the state.

Vane swept into the tent a minute later, pulling on her gloves, and paused at the sight of Tamsin awake.

"Good morning," she said. "Change of plan. We move on the Choir within the hour, and you're coming with us — at the front, where he can see you." She studied Tamsin's face with that terrible pale attention. "You should know I take no pleasure in this. When this is done, if you're sensible, you'll walk away with your freedom and a story no one will believe. If you're not sensible—" She stopped herself. Adjusted a glove. "Just be sensible, Miss Ferro. I've broken enough people to know I don't enjoy it, and I'd genuinely rather not add you to the count."

"Then don't," Tamsin said. "Use me however you want. But understand one thing, Inquisitor." She leaned forward as far as the chains allowed, and let five years of love and nine days of fury come up into her face where Vane could see all of it. "Whatever you think you're holding over him — it works both ways. He's coming for me. And everything he's been careful about, everything he's hidden, every rule he's ever kept — he's going to stop keeping them the moment he sees what you've done. You're not carrying a hostage. You're carrying a lit match into a room full of oil."

Something moved behind Vane's eyes — not doubt, exactly. Recalculation.

"Then we'd best hope," she said quietly, "that whatever's under that hill loves him less than you do."

Outside, the trumpets sounded the advance, and the whole grey column began to move toward the singing hills, dragging Tamsin with it — toward the pulse of buried gold, toward the storm-light gathering black over the peaks, toward the place where Rosalind Hartwell was about to learn that his friend had traded herself for him, and would have to choose, at last, between the map in his hands and the girl in the Inquisitor's grip.

The snow began to fall harder as they climbed.

It did not stop falling for three days.
