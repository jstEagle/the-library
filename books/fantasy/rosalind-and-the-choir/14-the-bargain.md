## 14. The Bargain

The cell they gave the lamplighter was not a punishment, and Sera Vane had made certain of it. No chains bolted to the wall. A cot with a wool blanket instead of sacking. A window slit high enough to show a bar of grey fen sky. She had signed the requisition herself, in her own careful hand, because she wanted this girl comfortable — and because comfort was a tool like any other, and she refused to pretend otherwise.

She sat across from Tamsin Ferro at a plain table with two cups of tea and waited for the girl to notice that there were two.

"You're not drinking that," Tamsin said at last. Her wrists were unbound; her voice was not. "Is it poisoned?"

"It's chamomile. I find interrogation goes better when no one is shaking." Vane pushed one cup across the scarred wood. "You've been in custody eleven hours. You haven't slept. Your hands are still blue from the causeway water. Drink."

Tamsin looked at the cup the way a person looks at a snare set in plain sight — which, Vane thought, was exactly what it was. But she drank. She was cold and proud and practical, and practical people drank the tea.

"Good." Vane opened the folder in front of her, though she did not need to. Eleven hours of registries and wayhouse ledgers and a dead courier's route reconstructed from toll receipts: she carried all of it behind her eyes, ordered and indexed, the way she carried everything. "Let me tell you what I know, so you understand how little you're actually protecting him from."

"I'm not protecting anyone."

"No," Vane agreed mildly. "You followed a man north through winter marshes without knowing why. That's not protection. That's something else entirely, and we'll come to it." She turned a page she did not read. "Rosalind Hartwell. Apprentice registered under Aldous Wren, guild seal revoked five years ago, presumed fled south. He resurfaced in Vellamere's night market selling luck-knots. He was the last person to see Corvin Ashe alive. Ashe died in his rented room with a wound no knife made. And three days later Hartwell ran — not south, where any sensible fugitive runs, but north, into the fens, along a route that matches, almost exactly, the old pilgrim road to a place that does not appear on any crown map."

She let the silence sit. Tamsin's jaw tightened, but her eyes stayed level, and Vane felt the small unwelcome flicker of respect she always felt for people who could hold a silence.

"You know what's out there," Tamsin said.

"I know what my records say is out there. Which may be nothing. Which may be a hole in the ground full of old stones." Vane folded her hands. "Here is what I suspect, and I will say it plainly because I have found that plainness disarms liars faster than threats. I believe Rosalind Hartwell has magic. I believe he has hidden it for five years, which takes discipline most trained mages never manage. And I believe he is walking toward something that killed better men than either of us will ever be."

"And if he is?"

"Then every hour he walks free, the odds grow that someone dies who didn't choose this war. Not soldiers. Not Inquisitors. Ferry-men. Lamplighters." Vane watched the name land. It landed. "I did not put you in that cell, Miss Ferro. He did, by running. Every mile he puts between himself and the truth, your cell gets smaller."

"That's a very tidy way to blame a man for being hunted."

"It's an accurate one." Vane leaned back. "Now. The bargain. You tell me where he was headed — the place, the name, whatever the map showed him — and you walk out of here tonight. Not released into surveillance. Not paroled. Free. I'll give you papers under a new name if you want them, coin enough to reach the coast, and my personal word — which, whatever else you've heard about the Inquisition, has never once been broken — that no agent of mine touches you again."

Tamsin stared at her. "Just like that."

"Just like that. Because I don't need you, Miss Ferro. I need what you know. There's a difference, and it matters to me which side of it you fall on."

And that was true, and that was the trap, and Vane knew it. She watched the girl weigh it — saw the exact moment the arithmetic went wrong, the moment love overrode sense, the moment Tamsin Ferro decided that a friend in danger outweighed her own freedom. Vane had seen that decision a thousand times. It was the most reliable thing in human nature, and the saddest.

"No," Tamsin said quietly.

Vane exhaled through her nose. "May I ask why?"

"Because you'd keep your word. That's the worst part. You'd let me go and then you'd go get him anyway, and he'd be alone when you found him." Tamsin set down the cup. Her hands had stopped shaking somewhere in the last minute, and Vane noted it with professional displeasure. "So no. Ask me anything else. Ask me about the night market, about the charms he sold, about whether he ever slipped — I'll answer honestly, because lying to you would just slow you down and get more people hurt. But not that."

Vane studied her for a long moment. Twenty years of hunting had taught her to read faces the way clerks read ledgers, and this face told her something she had not budgeted for: the girl was not loyal out of ignorance. She knew what Hartwell likely was. She had followed him anyway, knowing, and knowing, she had chosen him.

*That's how it starts,* Vane thought, and the thought had teeth, because twenty years ago another young woman had chosen the same way — had stood in a burning house in Ashfall and chosen her sister over the evacuation order, and both of them had died when the hedge-witch's working came apart, and Sera Vane had been the one who signed the order that put the evacuation too late. She had spent her life since telling herself the arithmetic was sound. Some nights she even believed it.

"You remind me of someone," she said, before she could stop herself.

"Lucky her."

"She died." Vane stood, gathered the folder. "The offer stands until morning. After that, protocol takes over, and protocol is not gentle. Think about which version of me you want to negotiate with."

At the door she paused. "The tea was clean. For what it's worth."

"It's worth something," Tamsin said. "Not enough. But something."

Vane walked the corridor with her boots ringing on stone and hated, briefly and completely, how much she had liked the girl.

* * *

Three miles east, on the drowned edge of Mereholt Fen where the reeds grew tall as masts and the water lay black under skin-thin ice, Rosalind Hartwell sat across a driftwood fire from a woman with eel-bone beads in her hair and a knife she was cleaning very slowly.

"This is the price," the woman said. "Not coin."

Brannoc shifted beside him. The old soldier had gone quiet the way he went quiet before fights, which Rosalind was learning to read as weather.

"Then what?" Rosalind asked.

"A debt. Ours to collect, later, unnamed." The woman — she had given her name as Sedge, and Brannoc had flinched at it, which Rosalind had also noted — pointed the knife at the map case on his knee. "The Choir doesn't want visitors. The Choir wants *payment*. Everyone who goes down pays something. We take you to the door; what it takes from you inside is between you and it. But our price for the crossing is a favor owed, called in whenever we call it, however we call it."

"That's not a price, that's a leash."

"Yes," Sedge said pleasantly. "It is."

Rosalind looked at the fire. Five years of small careful magic, five years of luck-knots and fever-charms and never once reaching for the thing coiled under his ribs — and now he was bargaining away an unnamed future to smugglers because a dying man had pressed a map into his hands and a friend was sitting in an Inquisitorial cell because of him. Somewhere behind him, Tamsin was drinking chamomile across from a woman who had burned her family's killers' names into law. Ahead of him, the Choir sang to his blood through forty miles of frozen fen, a thread of sound only he could feel, pulling.

He thought about what Aldous would say. *Never sign what you can't read.* Then he thought about the fact that Aldous had taught him that rule and then broken every rule he'd ever taught, and the thought curdled.

"The favor," Rosalind said slowly. "Can I bound it? One favor, one purpose, named at the time of asking — but nothing that harms an innocent. Nothing that serves the Inquisition or works against the magefolk. Sworn on the crossing itself."

Sedge's knife stopped moving. Around the fire, the other fen-folk — four of them, half-hidden in the reeds — had gone very still, and Rosalind understood that he had just done something either very stupid or very interesting.

"You talk like a guild-lawyer," Sedge said.

"I talked like an apprentice for seven years. It sticks."

"And you'd bind us by oath-word? Fen-custom?" Something moved behind her eyes that might have been hunger. "You know oath-word, city boy?"

"I know enough." He knew it from stories, from Aldous's grudging lessons in the old forms — the binding speech that predated guilds and crowns both, the speech the fen-people and hill-clans still kept. He had never spoken it aloud. Speaking it aloud was magic of a kind, the oldest kind, the kind that needed no spark at all, only truth and consequence. His mouth went dry. "One favor. Named at asking. Harm to innocents voids it. That's my terms."

"And if we refuse your terms?"

"Then I find another way to the Choir, and you lose whatever the Choir pays its ferrymen, and we all stand out here in the snow feeling foolish."

Sedge laughed — a short, barking sound, genuine despite herself. She flipped the knife, caught it, sheathed it. "Brannoc. Where'd you dig this one up?"

"He dug himself up," Brannoc rumbled. "I'm just keeping the dirt off him."

"Sedge." One of the reed-shadows spoke — an old man's voice, cracked as river ice. "Ask him the other question."

Sedge's humor drained away. She looked at Rosalind across the fire, and the firelight made her face older and harder. "The old man's right. One more term, and it's not negotiable. If the Choir offers you the stone — if the Emberstone comes to your hand and asks to be taken — you tell us first. Before you touch it. Before you decide anything. You walk back up those stairs and you tell Sedge of Mereholt what it offered, and then you do what you do. That's the price. That's the whole price."

"Why?" Rosalind asked. "What does it matter to you what I—"

"Because twenty years ago a man stood where you're standing and took it without telling anyone," Sedge said, "and the war that followed drowned three fen-villages and salted the ground so nothing grows there still. My mother's village. So yes, city boy. It matters."

The fire popped. Somewhere out in the black water, ice groaned and settled, long and low, almost like singing.

Rosalind thought of the map that would not burn. Of Aldous's handwriting marching across it, sure and elegant, leading him here like a lamb on a string. Of the Emberstone hanging silent in a cathedral of stones, waiting, the way such things wait — patiently, forever, for someone foolish enough to reach.

"One favor," he said. "Named at asking. Harm to innocents voids it. And if the stone comes to my hand, I tell you before I take it. Sworn on the crossing."

He spoke the last words in the old form, and felt them leave him like something physical — felt the fen take hold of them, the water and the ice and the listening dark, and set its seal.

Sedge held out her hand across the fire, palm scarred, grip like a trap closing. "Done," she said. "We cross at first light. Sleep if you can."

But sleep did not come. Rosalind lay on woven reeds under a hide shelter, watching frost form on the inside of the hide, and felt the Choir's song threading up through the frozen ground into his bones — closer now, insistent, patient. And beneath the song, fainter, another sound reached him from far off across the fen: many boots, moving in order. Soldiers. Marching north.

They were not hurrying. They did not need to. They knew exactly where he was going.

Vane had given the girl tea and a choice, and the girl had chosen wrong — or right, depending on whose ledger you read. Either way, the net was drawn now, and everything in the fen — smuggler, soldier, runaway, stone — was caught in it together, converging on the same buried door as the solstice blizzard began to build itself out of the northern dark.
