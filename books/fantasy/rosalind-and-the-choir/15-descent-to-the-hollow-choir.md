## 15. Descent to the Hollow Choir

The fen-witch's boat left him at the foot of a hill that was not on any map Rosalind had ever seen.

That was the first sign he had come to the right place. The second was that the hill should not have been there at all — a smooth green shoulder rising out of the frozen marsh like the back of a sleeping animal, too regular, too deliberate, ringed by nine leaning stones half-swallowed by peat and frost. Maren, the smuggler who had brought him through the last three miles of drowned country, would not take her boat past them.

"Here," she said, shipping the oars. "This is where I stop being paid."

Rosalind looked up at the stones. Snow had begun again — fine, dry snow, the kind that whispered rather than fell — and it did not seem to land on them. He watched for a long moment before he could make himself say it aloud. "It's going around them."

Maren spat over the side. "Everything does." She was sixty if she was a day, brown as old rope, with a voice like gravel poured through a sieve, and in two days of poling through the fens she had spoken perhaps forty words, most of them prices. Now she studied him with pale, watery eyes that missed nothing. "You're the one the map chose."

"It wasn't a choice."

"Never is." She nodded at the hill. "My grandmother's grandmother carried stone up this path when the world was younger, so the story goes. Carried it singing. Said the hill drank the song right out of her by the top, and she came down mute and never spoke another word till the day she died." She fixed him with those milky eyes. "You go up there, you'll hear something. When you do — mind what you answer."

"I don't understand."

"No," Maren agreed. "You won't. Not yet." She dug an oar into the black water. "I'll be at the eel-weirs till the moon turns. After that, you're the fen's problem and not mine."

Then she was gone, the boat sliding back into the reeds like something returning to its den, and Rosalind stood alone on the shore of a silence so complete he could hear his own heart working.

* * *

He climbed.

The path was older than any road he knew — not cut but worn, a pale seam of packed earth winding between the standing stones, each one taller than a man and carved all over with script that hurt to look at directly. Not because it was ugly. Because it moved. He had seen Aldous write wards that crawled across parchment like living things; these were that same hand magnified a thousandfold, letters the size of doorways, still awake after twenty years or two hundred or two thousand. He recognized fragments. A binding-word here. A naming-word there. And once, repeated down the length of the tallest stone like a refrain:

*WE WERE MANY. WE ARE ONE. WE WAIT.*

His breath came out white and ragged. He kept his hands in his pockets, away from the air, because they wanted to move — wanted to shape the old gestures, the ones Aldous had drilled into him until they lived in his wrists. Five years of keeping those hands still, and now the hill itself was asking them to speak.

At the crest, the ground opened.

Not a cave mouth — nothing so honest. It was a seam, a crack in the turf barely wide enough for a man's shoulders, descending into dark, and set into the rock beside it a plate of lead no bigger than a book. Rosalind knelt. His fingers found the edges of a seal pressed into the metal: a circle broken by a single vertical line, and beneath it, in a hand he knew better than his own — small, precise, the letters leaning slightly left —

*Aldous.*

He sat back on his heels and let the cold soak through his knees while the wind combed the grass around him.

*You taught me to hide,* he thought. *You taught me that magic was a thing to be buried alive, smothered in its sleep, starved quiet. You made me ashamed of my own blood. And all along you were keeping the door to this.*

The courier's dying words came back to him — *the Choir must not sing for her* — and beneath them, older, Aldous's voice from a lifetime ago, in the kitchen of a house that no longer existed: *Power is never neutral, boy. It only waits to find out what you are.*

Rosalind broke the seal.

The lead plate swung open on a hinge that had waited twenty years for exactly this, and inside, in Aldous's neat clerk's hand, was a single line:

*If you are reading this, then either you are Rosalind, or you are cleverer than he ever was, which I doubt. Go down. Count the steps. Do not sing until you reach the floor.*

And below that, smaller, almost tender:

*I am sorry for what this will cost you. I was always going to be.*

Rosalind read it four times. Then he folded it into his coat, next to the map, next to the fever-charm Tamsin had pressed on him at the city gate — *so you don't die stupidly*, she'd said, laughing, and he had lied to her with a straight face and walked away — and he put his legs into the dark and began to climb down.

* * *

He counted because Aldous had told him to, and because counting was a rope to hold.

Forty steps, and the light of the sky became a grey coin above him. Sixty, and the walls began to sweat with cold that was not water. Eighty, and the first of the roots appeared — pale threads thicker than his arm, growing *downward*, out of the living earth above and into the dead stone below, as if the whole marsh were lowering its veins into the vault to drink from whatever waited at the bottom.

One hundred and twelve steps, and his boot found level ground.

He stood in absolute darkness and did not move. This was the discipline Aldous had beaten into him — *never enter a room your magic hasn't entered first* — except that his magic had been locked in a box for five years, and opening that box, even a crack, felt like pressing his ear to the door of a cell where something enormous was breathing.

"Light," he whispered, and hated how small his voice sounded.

Nothing happened.

Of course nothing happened. He had spent five years teaching himself not to. The knack didn't live in words; it lived in permission. He closed his eyes. He thought of the night market, the luck-knots, the copper coins, the careful little lies of a life half-lived. He thought of the Inquisitorial clerk's ink-stained fingers on his stall rail. He thought of Tamsin's face when he'd said *it's nothing, Tam, I promise* — the first lie, and how easily it had come, and how much that ease had frightened him.

And underneath all of it, older than any of it, he thought of Aldous's kitchen. Woodsmoke. A kettle singing. His master's hand closing over his eleven-year-old wrist, guiding it through the first shape he'd ever made — a candleflame bending toward his finger like a flower toward sun — and Aldous saying, not unkindly: *There you are. Now we begin the hiding.*

Rosalind opened his eyes in the dark and gave himself permission.

It came up through him like water finding a crack — not the flare he'd loosed on the ice road to save Brannoc, all panic and desperation, but something quieter and far more frightening: a simple warmth in the palm of his hand, and light rising off his skin like heat-shimmer, soft gold, steady, filling the chamber the way dawn fills a valley.

He was standing at the edge of a cathedral.

* * *

There was no other word for it, though no god he'd ever heard preached of deserved it.

The vault was vast beyond the reach of his light — the far walls lost in darkness, the ceiling somewhere above the roots, supported by pillars of living stone that rose in ranks on either side of him like a forest petrified mid-stride. Each pillar was carved, floor to unseen capital, with the same moving script as the stones outside, and between the pillars stood figures. He counted without wanting to. Thirty-one. Human-shaped, human-sized, rendered in some dark mineral that drank his light and gave none back — robed, hooded, each with hands raised, palms outward, facing inward toward the center of the floor.

Toward the Choir.

Rosalind walked between them. His footsteps made no sound. He noticed that the way you notice your own heartbeat — with a distant, animal unease — and kept walking, because the alternative was to turn and run up one hundred and twelve steps and spend the rest of his life knowing he had come this far and flinched.

The thirty-first figure was different. Its face, alone among them, was bare — a woman, young, her expression caught in stone at the exact moment between fear and resolve — and at her feet lay a tablet of the same lead as the seal above. Rosalind knelt. The inscription was fresh compared to everything else, the letters sharp-edged, and there were six names cut into it, and five of them had lines struck through.

*Sealed by the hands of:* — and the names, and after the fifth, a sixth name unstruck, and under it, in smaller characters, as if added later, reluctantly:

*and by the cowardice of the seventh, who lives.*

Aldous. Of course. Brannoc had told him on the ice road, in the flat voice of a man handing over a wound: *Your master ran. The other six stayed to close the door from the inside, and he let them.* Rosalind had raged, then. Here, now, in the presence of the six who had stayed, rage curdled into something colder. He looked up at the woman's stone face and thought: *you died so that this place would stay shut, and the man who walked away used your death to teach a boy to be afraid.*

"I'm not him," he said aloud. His voice fell into the dark and vanished. "Whatever he told you I was. Whatever he's planning. I'm not him."

The vault did not answer.

But the Emberstone did.

* * *

He saw it before he understood what he was seeing — a point of ember-red hanging in the air at the center of the ring of pillars, at the height of a man's heart, unsupported, unmoving, about the size of a fist. It did not glow, exactly. It *held* light the way a coal holds fire: banked, patient, capable. Around it the air itself seemed denser, the way water is denser than air, and the roots from the ceiling reached down toward it and stopped, all of them, a hand's breadth short, as if even the living earth did not dare to touch it.

Rosalind approached it the way you approach a sleeping animal, or a cliff edge, or the truth.

Ten feet away, he stopped. Close enough. Closer than anyone had stood in twenty years.

And it sang.

Not with sound. That was the thing he would try and fail for the rest of his life to explain — it sang in the same register as his own blood. He felt it in his teeth, in the backs of his knees, in the scar tissue of old fears. It was a chord, a single sustained chord made of a hundred voices, and every one of those voices was a mage. He understood that instantly, the way you understand falling. Every witch and hedge-worker and hedge-priest and village charm-mender who had ever lived and died with the gift in their blood — they were all in there. Not their souls, maybe. But their song. The Emberstone was a choir loft, and the whole history of magic was its congregation, and it had been holding that note since the day six people died to make it hold.

*We were many. We are one. We wait.*

To anyone else — to Vane, to the soldiers, to the crown, to every ordinary soul in Vellamere — it would be silent. A pretty red stone in a hole in the ground. That was the design; he could see the design now, written in the pillars, in the thirty-one watchers: the Choir sang only to those with magic in their blood, so that only such hands could ever claim it, and so that the crown could never seize what it could not hear.

But it also meant that the outlawry had been doomed from the start. You could hang every hedge-witch in the kingdom and the song would remain, waiting, patient, in the blood of whatever child was born next with the gift. Magic wasn't a crime that could be punished out of existence. It was a note that had already been sung, and notes, once sung, do not unsing themselves.

Rosalind sank to his knees on the cold stone floor, and the chord swelled gently to meet him, and he wept — not from grief, or not only grief, but from the sheer unbearable intimacy of it. It was like being recognized. Like walking into a strange house and hearing, from another room, your mother's voice calling your name.

In the song he glimpsed things. Fragments, the way you glimpse faces in a fire. A green country burning. Towers coming down in slow majesty. A woman with a charred doll in her hands, standing in a doorway, looking at nothing. Ordinary people. Always, at the edges of every vision, ordinary people paying the bill for mages' ambitions — and that was the memory the stone held closest, that was the verse it repeated, the reason six people had died to seal it: *never again, never again, never again.*

And beneath that verse, fainter, older, almost shy, another one. Not *never again*. Something more like: *but we are still here. We were always still here.*

Rosalind knelt in the dark cathedral with the whole inheritance of his kind hanging three feet from his face, and he understood at last what Aldous had never taught him, what all the hiding had buried: that the question was never whether magic should exist. It existed. It sang. The question — the only question that had ever mattered — was what the singers would choose to do with the song.

He raised his hand.

The Emberstone's light deepened, warmed, leaned toward his palm the way a candleflame had once leaned toward an eleven-year-old's finger in a kitchen full of woodsmoke. The chord gathered itself, all hundred voices, all the dead and the hidden and the outlawed, gathering behind his ribs like a breath drawn to be held or released—

Footsteps.

Above. On the stairs. One hundred and twelve steps, and someone was descending them, unhurried, familiar, with the particular soft tread of a man who had climbed down these stairs before and expected to climb down them again.

Rosalind closed his fist around the stone's attention like snuffing a flame, and turned toward the stair, and the light in his palm burned steady gold as the darkness at the tunnel's mouth took the shape of shoulders, of a head, of a face he had mourned for five years.

"Well done," said Aldous Hartwell, stepping into the light. "You counted the steps. I knew you would."
