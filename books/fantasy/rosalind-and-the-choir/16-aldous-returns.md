## 16. Aldous Returns

The Emberstone hung above the choir of stones like a heart that had forgotten how to beat.

Rosalind stood at the center of the Hollow Choir, his breath clouding in air that should have been warm and was not. The vault was a cathedral without a god — standing stones in concentric rings, each taller than three men, their surfaces carved with script so old it predated the alphabet he'd been taught. Frost furred the stone. And above it all, suspended in nothing, the Emberstone turned slowly on its own axis, shedding a light that was not quite fire and not quite dawn: a deep amber glow that moved across the walls like water.

It did not sing.

He had expected singing. Every story he'd heard as a child — whispered, because even stories about the Choir were dangerous — spoke of a sound. A hum beneath the earth, a chord struck by no hand, a voice in the dark that called mages home like a mother calling children in from the fields. His master Aldous had told him those stories once, in the last year of his apprenticeship, in the low voice Aldous reserved for things that were true.

*It sings only for those who carry the gift,* Aldous had said. *And when you hear it, boy, you will understand why they sealed it away.*

But Rosalind heard nothing. Only silence, vast and mineral, and the small sounds of his own body — heartbeat, breath, the creak of wet leather as he shifted his weight. Five years of hiding had taught him to listen for danger, and the silence itself felt like a held breath. Like the vault was waiting for something from him.

He took one step closer to the stone.

The light brightened.

Not much. A flicker, the way a candle leans when a door opens somewhere in the house. But Rosalind felt it — felt *it*, the way you feel someone watching the back of your neck. The warmth of the glow touched his face, and beneath his skin, in the place where his magic had lived coiled and starved for five years, something stirred. Not rising. Just turning over in its sleep, like a dog hearing its name.

"No," he said aloud, and his voice fell flat into the silence. "No, I didn't come for you."

The stone said nothing, because stones do not speak, but the light held steady on him anyway, patient as sunrise. He thought of the courier dying on his floor with blood between his fingers, pressing the map into Rosalind's hands. *The Choir must not sing for her.* He still didn't know who *she* was. Vane, perhaps. Or someone behind Vane. Someone who wanted what hung above him now.

He had come to destroy it. That had been the decision, made somewhere on the ice road with Brannoc's blood freezing on his sleeve and his own magic roaring out of him like a dam breaking: if the stone could start a war, it would not be left in the ground for anyone to find. Not the crown. Not the Inquisition. Not her. He would unmake it or bury it so deep that even memory lost the way down.

Standing beneath it now, he understood that he had not thought it through. You did not unmake a thing like this. It had ended the last mage war; it had *been* the ending, the full stop written in fire across half a continent. Things that ended wars did not die quietly. They waited.

"I need to know how," he told it, absurdly, conversationally, because five years alone in night markets had made him talk to objects when his nerves got the better of him. "I need to know what you are before I can end you. So just — stay quiet. Stay dark. Let me work."

The light dimmed, almost obediently, and Rosalind laughed — a short, cracked sound with no humor in it — because he knew better than to believe a stone had obeyed him. But his hands were steady as he unwrapped the bundle from inside his coat: Aldous's sealed warnings, the second map, the pages of cipher his master had hidden in the lining of a book Rosalind had carried across half the country without knowing why. He spread them on a flat shelf of stone at the base of the nearest monolith and knelt over them, and began, for the first time since he'd fled this man's house five years ago, to study his master's hand.

Aldous Wren had beautiful handwriting. That was the thing people never understood about him, Rosalind thought — the rumors made him a hedge-wizard, a smuggler of charms, a ghost in Vellamere's underworld, but the truth was that Aldous had been a scholar first, precise as a surgeon, and every letter he wrote was placed like a brick in a wall. Rosalind followed the cipher through its turns, and the turns were familiar, because Aldous had taught him this cipher when he was fourteen, disguised as a game, *because a mind that can hide a thought can hide a life, boy.*

The warnings resolved.

Most of it was warding-structure — the architecture of the seal, layer upon layer, the work of nine mages whose names appeared in a column at the top like a roster of the dead. Rosalind read the names twice. He recognized none of them except one, and that recognition went through him like cold water:

*A. Wren.*

His master had helped seal the Choir. Had stood in this vault twenty years ago with eight others and bound the Emberstone into silence. Brannoc had told him as much on the road — *your master was one of them, and your master walked away, and the others didn't* — but seeing it in Aldous's own hand, dated, signed, annotated in that beautiful brick-by-brick script, made it real in a way secondhand truth never is.

And beneath the seal-work, in smaller writing, cramped as though the pen had been moving fast:

*If you are reading this, then I am dead, and the seal has failed, and you have more courage than sense. Do not touch the stone. Do not listen to it. It will show you what you most want to see and call it memory. It ended the war, yes — but ask yourself what it cost, and who paid, and whether the ones who paid were ever asked. I was not asked. None of us were. We were given a choice between the stone and the fire, and we chose the stone, and I have regretted it every day since in ways I cannot write down.*

*Destroy it if you can. If you cannot, seal it deeper than we did, and then run, and keep running, and do not look back. I am sorry. I was never brave enough to be sorry out loud.*

*I loved you like a son. Forgive me.*

Rosalind read it three times. The third time, his vision blurred, and he wiped his eyes with the back of his wrist and told himself it was the cold.

*Forgive me.* As if forgiveness were a debt a dead man could call in. As if five years of exile — the fever-charms sold for copper, the luck-knots tied with empty hands, the flinch at every boot on every stair, the whole half-lived life — were a thing a paragraph could purchase.

"You're dead," Rosalind said to the page. "You've been dead for five years. I burned your letters. I mourned you."

"Did you?"

The voice came from behind him, from the vault mouth, and it was a voice Rosalind had spent five years failing to forget — dry, unhurried, faintly amused, the voice of a man who had never once raised it and had never once needed to.

Rosalind came off his knees and turned, and every hidden instinct he owned fired at once: the counterspell half-shaped in his mind, the weight shifting toward the dark between the stones, the old reflex of a hunted thing finding its bolt-hole. But there was nowhere to bolt. There was only the vault mouth, forty feet of frozen tunnel, and the figure standing silhouetted against the grey light of the surface world.

Aldous Wren stepped into the Emberstone's glow.

He was thinner than Rosalind remembered. The beard had gone white — all of it, not streaked but *white*, the white of frost or ash — and there were new lines around his eyes, and he walked with a slight hitch in his left leg that hadn't been there before. But the eyes were the same: grey, sharp, kind and merciless in equal measure, the eyes of a man who could tell exactly how much you could bear and would give you precisely that much and not one drop more.

"Hello, Rosalind," his master said. "You've grown into your shoulders. I always said you would."

"You're dead." Rosalind's voice came out flat and strange, a stranger's voice. "I saw the pyre. Tamsin's father was there. Half the fen-folk came. They burned—"

"They burned a body, yes." Aldous limped forward another step, unhurried, hands loose at his sides in the old gesture of harmlessness that Rosalind had learned, long ago, to distrust. "A poor man from Mireholt who'd died of lung-rot and owed me a debt. It was a kindness to him, in its way. His family was paid. His name was spoken over a fine pyre instead of a pauper's ditch." He glanced up at the hanging stone, and the amber light slid across his face. "Death is a useful door, Rosalind. I'd recommend it, if it weren't so hard to walk back through."

Five years. Five years of grief, rationed carefully like everything else in Rosalind's hidden life, kept in a box and opened only at night, and the whole time the man had been alive. Alive and — Rosalind's mind caught up with itself with a lurch — *alive and sending couriers*. The bloodied stranger in his room. The map in Aldous's hand. *The Choir must not sing for her.*

"You sent him." Rosalind's hands curled at his sides. "The courier. Dying on my floor with your map under his coat. That was you."

"He volunteered." Something moved behind Aldous's composure — quickly buried, but Rosalind had been trained by this man to catch quick burials. "He was fen-born. His sister was taken in the second purge. He believed in what we're doing. I told him the risk."

"And when he bled out on my floor, was that in the risk? Did you stand somewhere warm and calculate that too? *The boy will take the map, the boy always takes the map, I trained him to take the map—*" Rosalind heard his own voice climbing and forced it down. The vault drank sound; his anger came back to him flattened, small, swallowed. "You used me. From the grave. You couldn't even let me have your death honestly."

"I let you have your life honestly," Aldous said, and there was steel under the dryness now. "That was the point. If I had come to you — if I had written, if I had sent word through any channel the Inquisition might someday trace — you would have been arrested within the month, and tried, and burned, and everything I spent twenty years building toward would have died with you. Your ignorance was armor, boy. I forged it myself. I'm not proud of the fit, but I don't apologize for the forging."

"You don't apologize for anything. It's in your letter." Rosalind picked up the pages from the stone shelf and held them up, and his hand shook, and he hated that it shook. "*I am never brave enough to be sorry out loud.* You wrote that. Was that true, or was that bait too? Another hook for the boy who takes the map?"

For a long moment Aldous said nothing. The Emberstone turned above them both, slow as a millwheel, and its light lay across the carved script of the standing stones so that the old words seemed to crawl.

"It was true," Aldous said finally. "Every word I have ever written to you was true, Rosalind. That's the discipline. Lies are for strangers. With the people you love, you tell the truth and arrange it." He gestured, an open-handed invitation toward the center of the vault, toward the hanging stone. "So. Truth, arranged. Come and look at what we're doing here, and then judge me."

"We?" Rosalind did not move. "Who is *we*, Master?"

"The ones who remember." Aldous limped past him — close enough that Rosalind caught the smell of woodsmoke and some bitter herb, wormwood maybe, the smell of the old workroom, and it hit him like a fist below the ribs, that smell, five years of nights in a single breath — and stopped beneath the Emberstone, looking up at it the way a man looks at a debt collector's knock. "There were nine of us who sealed this place. Three are dead of age. Two were taken in the purges and burned. One drowned himself rather than be questioned. One I cannot find. That leaves me, and it leaves Merrin Sallow, who you'll meet if this goes well, and it leaves the stone." He turned. "Twenty years ago we buried magic in this hole because we were afraid. Not wise. *Afraid.* We had watched the war eat the world and we wanted it over, and the crown offered us a bargain — the stone for our lives — and we took it, and then we watched them spend the next two decades hunting every hedge-witch and charm-seller and gifted child in the country to make sure the bargain stayed buried. I hid for twenty years, Rosalind. I taught you to hide. And I have decided, in my final years, that hiding was the second-worst thing I ever did."

Rosalind's mouth was dry. "And the worst?"

"Burying the stone instead of using it." Aldous's grey eyes came up and met his, and there it was — the thing Rosalind had been circling since the vault mouth, the arranged truth at the center of all the rest. "The Emberstone answers to will. It ended the last war because nine mages bent it to the will of ending. It can just as easily bend to the will of *beginning*. A demonstration. One night, one storm over Vellamere, one unmistakable sign in the sky that the age of burning witches is over — and every mage in this country comes out of their holes, and the crown negotiates, because crowns negotiate with weather. Rights restored. The purges reversed. An amnesty, written in law, witnessed by a storm no one can deny."

The words landed in Rosalind's chest and sat there, glowing, treacherous. Because he wanted it. That was the horror of it — he wanted it the way a starved man wants bread, instantly and without dignity. Legal magic. A life with the flinch gone out of it. Children learning charms openly in Vellamere instead of in cellars. Himself, walking down his own street under his own name.

"That's blackmail," he said.

"That's leverage." Aldous spread his hands. "Call it what the historians will call it in fifty years, when they're teaching it to children who've never known a purge. I call it the only door left. I have spent twenty years being reasonable, Rosalind. Reasonable men dug the graves. I watched Merrin Sallow's daughter taken at fourteen for a fever-charm that *worked*, that saved her neighbor's baby, and the mother who begged for that charm was made to watch the burning, and the Inquisitor General sent a letter of commendation to the officer afterward. Reasonable is finished. What remains is force, or surrender, and I have not crawled through twenty years of darkness to surrender."

"And the courier's warning." Rosalind's voice was very quiet now. "*The Choir must not sing for her.* Who is she, Master? Who else knows about this place?"

Something crossed Aldous's face — fast, again, again buried — and this time Rosalind caught enough of it to name it. Not guilt. *Fear.*

"Inquisitor Vane has a tracker," Aldous said. "A captured mage, broken and leashed, who can follow the residue of working like a hound follows blood. She's had him since autumn. If she's pointed him at the fen roads—" He stopped himself. Recalibrated. When he spoke again the dryness was back, the lecture-hall calm, and Rosalind realized with a distant, sick clarity that he knew that calm better than he'd ever known the man underneath it. "It doesn't matter. What matters is that we are out of time, and the stone is here, and you are here, and you are the finest natural talent I have ever trained. Stronger than I was at your age. Stronger than any of the nine." He extended his hand, palm up, an offer, an ordination. "Help me lift it, Rosalind. Help me end the long winter — the real one, the one we've lived under for twenty years. Be the heir I made you to be."

The vault was silent. The stone turned. Somewhere far above, muffled by forty feet of rock and frozen earth, the wind changed pitch — the solstice blizzard arriving early, or arriving *called*, and Rosalind found he could not tell the difference anymore.

He looked at his master's open hand.

He looked at the letter on the stone shelf, at *forgive me* written in beautiful brick-by-brick script by a man who had faked his own death and spent a stranger's life like coin.

He looked up at the Emberstone, and the Emberstone, for the first time, seemed to look back.

"You taught me to hide," Rosalind said slowly. "You taught me that a mind that can hide a thought can hide a life. You taught me that lies are for strangers." He did not take the offered hand. "So here is my truth, Master, arranged: I came here to destroy that stone. And I am going to listen to it first — really listen, not the way you listened twenty years ago with the fire at your backs — and then I am going to decide. Not you. Me."

Aldous's hand closed slowly into a fist, and for the first time in Rosalind's memory, his master looked old.

"Then you'll hear the war," Aldous said softly. "And it will change you. It changed all of us. Pray it changes you in the direction you want to go."

Rosalind turned back to the hanging stone, and reached out with the magic he had starved for five years, and the Emberstone began, at last, to sing.

* * *

The song was not sound.

That was the first thing he understood, in the instant before understanding became impossible: the Choir did not sing through the ears. It sang through the blood. It rose in him like water finding an old channel, and the channel went down, and down, and the vault fell away around him like a shed skin—

—and he was standing in a wheat field under a summer sky, and the wheat was burning.

He was standing in a wheat field and the sky was full of wings that were not birds, and a woman beside him was screaming a word he could not hear because there was no hearing here, only knowing, only *memory poured directly into the vessel of him*, and the woman was a mage and the soldiers coming through the burning wheat were ordinary men with pikes and torches and they were afraid, gods, they were so afraid, and their fear was justified, because the day before, a circle of mages had answered a royal insult by pulling the river out of its bed and drowning a garrison town, four thousand souls, farmers and bakers and children, drowned in ten minutes by men who told themselves it was strategy—

—he was in a cellar and a child was hiding him, a girl of maybe twelve, pressing her finger to her lips, while boots crossed the floorboards overhead, and he could feel her gift like a candleflame, tiny and terrified, and he knew — the stone knew, and now he knew — that she would be burned in the square in spring, and that the mages who might have saved her were underground or dead or busy, always busy, with the war, with the great matters, with the fate of nations, while the small people burned—

—he was on a hill with nine figures robed against the wind, and the Emberstone hung between them raw and screaming with power, and the nine were weeping as they bent it, because the price was being shown to them even as they paid it, and the price was *this*, all of this, every field and cellar and child, the whole ledger of the war laid open, and they chose anyway, they sealed it anyway, because the alternative was the fire taking everything, and one of the nine — young, dark-haired, his face unlined, his eyes not yet white — looked directly out of the memory at Rosalind across twenty years and mouthed a single word:

*Run.*

Rosalind tore free.

He came back to himself on his knees on the frozen stone, gasping, his nose bleeding freely, his hands scored with his own fingernails, and the Emberstone blazed above him like a captive sunrise, and the song was still in his blood, still pouring, gentler now, endless, showing him more — harvests saved, fevers broken, the ten thousand small mercies magic had worked in the world before the fear outweighed them — and he understood, kneeling in the dark with his master's shadow falling over him, why it had been outlawed.

And why outlawing it had failed.

Because you could not outlaw a river. You could only dam it, and dams built on fear always, always broke.

"Rosalind." Aldous's voice, urgent now, stripped of its calm. "What did you see? *What did it show you?*"

Rosalind lifted his head. Blood dripped from his chin onto the ancient stone, and where it fell, faintly, impossibly, the carvings began to glow.

"It showed me the bill," he said. "And Master — it isn't finished with us yet."
