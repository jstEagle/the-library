## 17. The Song Beneath

The vault held its breath around him.

Rosalind stood on the last step of the stair, and the Hollow Choir rose above him — a cathedral that no human hand had built, or if hands had built it, they had been hands that thought in centuries. Standing stones in a ring wide as a market square, each one taller than Vellamere's bell tower, their surfaces carved with script so old it predated the alphabet Rosalind had learned his letters in. Between them hung the Emberstone.

It did not float by any trick he could name. There was no chain, no pedestal, no cage of light. It simply hung at the center of the ring, a fist-sized heart of amber and iron and something darker beneath both, turning slowly, as if it had all the time in the world.

And it was singing.

Not aloud. Not in any sound that touched his ears. It sang in the blood — in the thing inside him he had spent five years starving and hiding and denying, the thing that woke now like a man hearing his own name called across a frozen field.

*Come here,* the song said, without words. *You were always coming here.*

Behind him, at the vault's mouth, Aldous's voice came down the stair, thin with distance. "I'll watch the entrance. Vane will come — she'll come faster than either of us wants. Take whatever time you need." A pause, and then, softer, almost gentle: "It will show you things. Let it. That is the only way to claim it."

*Claim it.* As though it were a deed of land. As though it were a purse of coin.

Rosalind did not answer. He climbed down into the ring of stones, his boots loud on the floor — and the floor was wrong, he realized. Not stone. Glass, or something like glass, dark and seamless, and beneath it moved a faint gold light, slow as honey, like veins under skin. The whole vault was alive. The whole vault was listening.

He stopped three paces from the Emberstone.

Up close, it was not beautiful. He had expected beauty — the stories all agreed on beauty, on radiance, on the warm glow of the world's first fire. But the Emberstone looked *used*. Its surface was scarred, pitted, one whole face blackened as if it had been held too long against something burning. Cracks ran through it like the lines in an old woman's palm. It turned in its slow orbit, and the light within it guttered, steadied, guttered again — a candle in a draft, a heart beating with effort.

*You're tired,* Rosalind thought, and was astonished to find the thought carried tenderness in it. *You're so tired.*

The song changed.

*See,* it said. And then it took him.

* * *

There was no falling. One moment he stood in the cold glass cathedral; the next he stood in a wheat field under a summer sky, and the wheat was burning.

He knew — some distant part of him knew — that this was memory, that his body still stood in the Choir below the fens, that none of this could touch him. But the knowing sat far away, like a lamp seen through fog. Here, the heat was real. The smoke was real. The screaming was real.

He saw the mages come over the ridge.

They came like weather. Nine of them — he knew it was nine the way he knew his own heartbeat, the Emberstone counting them for him — robed in the colors of the Concordance, and behind them the sky tore open with light. They were not evil. That was the first thing the memory taught him, and it was the cruelest thing: they were not evil. They were afraid. They had spent eleven years of war being afraid, and fear had burned everything soft out of them, and now they meant to end it the only way left — by unmaking the enemy's army entire, field, camp, baggage train, and all.

Rosalind stood in the wheat among the people who were not mages.

A woman with flour on her forearms, who had run from the mill when the sky began to burn, holding a child who was not hers — she'd snatched it from a neighbor's cart — because her own arms needed something to hold. A boy of perhaps fourteen in a soldier's coat too big for him, standing very straight because an officer had told him to stand very straight. An old man kneeling in the dirt with his hat off, praying to a god whose name Rosalind didn't know, praying with the calm of someone who has already made peace and only wants to say goodbye properly.

The light came down.

Rosalind felt it pass through him like a bell struck inside his ribs. Where it touched, the wheat became glass. The mill became glass. The woman became glass, mid-scream, the borrowed child a smear of white in her arms. The boy stayed standing, very straight, transfigured, his too-big coat gone to crystal. The old man knelt in glass, hat still off, prayer finished or unfinished forever.

Nine mages stood on the ridge and wept, and called it victory.

*This is what I am for,* the Emberstone said, and there was no pride in it. Only exhaustion, vast and geological. *I was made to hold what they could not hold in themselves.*

* * *

The memories came then like floodwater, years pouring through him in hours.

He saw the Emberstone forged — not made but *grown*, coaxed out of the world's deep fire by mages who understood, too late, that power without limit was a wound that never closed. He saw it passed from council to council, army to army, each holder swearing to use it only once more, only in extremity, only to end what could not otherwise be ended. He saw the oaths pile up like sediment. He saw every oath broken.

He saw the last war from both sides — the Concordance mages and the Ashen Compact, two families of the same gift rending each other over a question of law, of succession, of pride dressed as principle. He saw cities emptied not by fire but by fear, whole populations walking away from homes that magic had made unsafe to live in. He saw hedge-witches dragged from their cottages by neighbors they had cured and blessed and midwifed for thirty years, because after enough burning fields, every gift looked like a weapon waiting for its moment.

And then he saw the sealing.

He saw twelve of them — the Twelve Sealers, names half-lost even to the Emberstone's long recollection — carry the stone down into the earth through passages the fens had swallowed since. He saw them build the Choir around it, stone by sung stone, each pillar tuned to a single note of a chord designed to hold the Emberstone silent. He saw them bind themselves into the working, give years of their lives to it, and —

He saw the thirteenth figure at the edge of the circle. Younger than the rest. Clever-eyed. Watching the others pour their strength into the seal and keeping his own strength back, hoarded, hidden, the way a gambler keeps a card up his sleeve.

Rosalind knew him before the memory named him. He had known that clever tilt of the head his whole life, across a workbench, across a candle, across twenty years of lessons in how to be small and safe and unseen.

*Aldous.*

*Yes,* the Emberstone said. *He kept himself apart. He told himself it was prudence. He told himself the others were fools to spend themselves so completely. And when the sealing was done and the others lay empty as spent lamps, he walked out of the dark carrying everything he had withheld, and he lived, and they did not.*

The memory shifted, softened, went gray at the edges — the Emberstone showing him what it had witnessed dimly, through stone and silence, in the years after. He felt the sealers' deaths one by one, like candles going out in a long hall. He felt the fens rise and the roads forget where the vault was. He felt decades pass like weather.

And underneath it all, continuous, patient, the singing. The Emberstone had hung in the dark for twenty years, doing the one thing it existed to do — nothing. Holding power and spending none of it. Waiting for someone to come who would understand what it had seen.

*I am not a weapon,* it said. *I have been used as one. There is a difference, and no one who held me ever survived long enough to learn it.*

* * *

Rosalind came back to himself on his knees on the glass floor, cheeks wet, throat raw. How long he had knelt he could not say. The gold light moved beneath him, unhurried. Above, the Emberstone turned its slow orbit, and its song had gentled, the way a voice gentles when the listener finally stops arguing.

He understood now. Not the way he had understood things before — facts arranged, conclusions drawn, master's lessons recited in the privacy of his skull. This was understanding in the marrow, understanding bought with the weight of a burned wheat field and a woman made of glass with a child in her arms.

Magic had been outlawed because it deserved outlawing. Because nine frightened people on a ridge had decided that their fear licensed them to unmake a thousand innocents, and no law of men had been able to stop them, and so the world had done the only thing left: it had declared the gift itself a crime, root and branch, every charm and cantrip and healing-word guilty by association with the ridge and the glass and the wheat.

And outlawing it had failed — Rosalind saw that now with equal clarity — because you cannot legislate a capacity out of the blood. The purge had not ended magic. It had driven it into cellars and night markets and luck-knots sold for copper, into fever-charms worked by trembling hands in rented rooms, into five-year-old children who would grow up learning to flinch at their own names. Outlawry had not made magic smaller. It had made magic *frightened* — and frightened magic, unschooled, unsworn, ungoverned, was more dangerous than any Concordance army. Every hedge-witch hiding her gift was a small unexploded charge buried in the world. Sooner or later, one of them would be cornered. Sooner or later, one of them would flare.

He had flared himself, on the ice road, four days ago. He knew exactly how it happened.

The answer was not the stone. He saw that too, and it nearly broke him, because the stone was the easy answer — take it, hold it, let its terrible arithmetic settle every question. Aldous wanted the stone to force the crown's hand. Vane wanted the stone destroyed or locked away forever. Both of them wanted a final solution, one act that would close the question permanently.

But the Emberstone had shown him its whole long history, and its history was this: there was no final solution. There was only the next generation, and what they were taught, and whether they were taught to hide or to hold. Power would exist in the blood of ordinary people whether the law acknowledged it or not. The only choice the world ever got to make was between power that answered to something and power that answered to nothing — between the oath and the hiding place.

An oath. Sworn publicly, kept accountably, bound to a council that included the unmagicked — the millers and lamplighters and soldiers, the descendants of everyone who had ever been made of glass. Magic brought up out of the cellars into the light, where it could be seen, questioned, corrected. Not outlawed. Not worshipped. *Answerable.*

It was so simple. It was so hard. It would take a generation, and it would begin with one person standing in front of the Inquisitor General and saying: *here is what I am, and here is what I refuse to do with it.*

Rosalind laughed — a cracked, wet sound in the enormous silence — because he heard his own thought echo back at him in Aldous's voice, from a hundred lessons: *the strongest working is the one you decline to cast.* His master had taught him refusal as survival. Refusal as a wall to hide behind.

Refusal could be a foundation instead.

He put his hand out toward the Emberstone — not touching, never touching; he had learned that much from the memories, that the stone gave itself to no hand but answered to presence, to witness — and he spoke aloud, his voice small and steady in the vault.

"I see you," he said. "I see what you paid for. I'm not going to use you. And I'm not going to bury you again."

The song swelled — not louder, but *fuller*, the way a chord fills when the last voice joins it — and the gold light beneath the glass floor brightened, and every carved word on every standing stone lit at once, and for one impossible moment the Hollow Choir was not a tomb but a choir indeed, twelve notes ringing in harmony, awake.

Then the light guttered.

The stones dimmed, one by one, from the outermost inward — and Rosalind felt the change before he understood it, a wrongness in the chord, a note going flat. Something was pressing on the seal from outside. Something with a great deal of power and no patience at all.

Above, faint through the vault's throat of stairs, came a sound he knew from the ice road: the crackle of ward-light meeting steel. Shouting. Brannoc's roar, wordless and furious.

And threaded through it, cutting clean as a blade through wool, a woman's voice giving orders — calm, precise, terribly certain.

Vane had found the door.

Rosalind rose from the glass floor, and the Emberstone turned toward him in its slow orbit, and its song said, without words, what he already knew:

*Now they come. Show them what you saw.*
