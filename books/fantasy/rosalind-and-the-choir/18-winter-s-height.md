## 18. Winter's Height

The Emberstone woke before Aldous touched it.

Rosalind felt it happen — a change in the air of the vault, like a held breath released after twenty years. The stone hung in its cradle of standing stones, no larger than a fist, and it burned without light. Not fire. Not glow. A kind of *presence*, the way a struck bell keeps ringing in the bones long after the ear gives up on it.

"You feel that," Aldous said. It was not a question. His voice had changed since he'd come down into the vault behind Rosalind — shed the mildness, the teacher's patience, the way a man sheds a coat he has worn too long indoors. What remained was harder and older and terribly familiar. "You were born feeling it. That's why I chose you, boy. That's why I let you run."

"Let me run." Rosalind's breath fogged in air that should not have been cold and was growing colder by the heartbeat. "You sent me into exile. You taught me to be small. You taught me to hide so well I forgot there was anything left to hide."

"I taught you to survive." Aldous crossed the stone floor, his boots ringing against rock that answered him — a faint harmonic hum, rising. "And now you're going to help me finish what I started."

Rosalind looked at the stone and did not step back, though every instinct honed over five years of hiding screamed at him to do exactly that. "I've seen what it remembers," he said quietly. "I stood here while you were still climbing down the fen road. I saw the last war, Aldous. I saw what mages did when they thought their cause was just. Ordinary people burned. Villages. Children who couldn't work a cantrip between them."

"And the crown burned us afterward for twenty years," Aldous said. "Every hedge-witch dragged from her kitchen. Every boy like you taught to flinch at his own name. You think I don't know what the stone remembers? I helped seal this vault. I carried my share of the dead in my arms." His hand rose toward the Emberstone, and the hum deepened, and frost bloomed across the standing stones like white lichen. "The difference between us, Rosalind, is that I stopped being ashamed of wanting to live."

"That's not what this is."

"No?" Aldous smiled, and it was the smile from a hundred lessons, warm as banked coals, and it made Rosalind's stomach turn because he had loved that smile once. "Then stop me."

He closed his hand around the stone.

The Choir sang.

It was not sound — or it was sound the way an avalanche is movement. The resonance went through the floor, through the fens, through the frozen earth itself, and Rosalind felt it leave the vault and climb into the sky above Vellamere, forty miles south, where the winter clouds had been waiting all season for an excuse.

Above them, through miles of stone and peat and iron-grey air, the storm began.

* * *

Sera Vane had commanded men through two border campaigns and one bread riot, and she had never heard weather make soldiers pray before.

The blizzard came down off the northern fens like something with intent. One moment the column was marching through ordinary snowfall, lanterns guttering, Tamsin Ferro riding bound in the supply cart with her eyes fixed on Vane's back as if she could bore a hole through wool and steel; the next, the wind arrived — a wall of it, screaming, carrying ice that hit like thrown gravel and filled the road behind them in minutes.

"Halt the column!" Captain Dree bellowed. "Lanterns! Link arms!"

Vane stood in the stirrups and looked north, where the storm's heart pulsed. There was a rhythm to it. She had spent eleven years learning to read patterns — in registries, in ledgers, in the small evasions of guilty men — and she read this one now with a cold certainty that settled into her chest heavier than her mail. The storm was not spreading. It was *converging*. Every current of wind in the sky bent toward a single point ahead of them, the way iron filings bend toward a lodestone.

Toward the Hollow Choir.

"It's him," she said. "The apprentice. He's reached the vault."

Dree hauled his horse alongside hers, snow crusting his beard. "In this? No army marches in this. We shelter at Mireholt and—"

"We march." She said it flatly, and watched him hear it. "Captain, look at the sky. Whatever is making that storm is drawing power out of the ground itself. If we wait for it to pass, we will be waiting for spring, and by spring whatever comes out of those fens will not be stopped by garrison soldiers at a wayhouse." She turned her horse north. "The girl says the courier gave the map to someone she knows. Someone from the city. If he's opened the vault, then everything the old law feared is loose in the world again, and I will not stand in Mireholt drinking bad ale while it happens."

"And if we freeze on the causeway?"

"Then we freeze moving," Vane said, "which is more than my family got."

She did not say more than that. She never did. But as the column reformed — sixty men, half of them shivering boys from the Vellamere garrison who had signed on for pay and patrol duty, not for this — she rode back along the line to the cart where Tamsin sat wrapped in a soldier's cloak, wrists bound, watching her with an expression that held fear and fury in equal measure.

"You know what's up there," Tamsin said. Her voice was hoarse from the cold. "You can feel it too. Everyone can."

"Everyone can," Vane agreed.

"So why are you riding *toward* it?"

Vane studied her for a moment. The lamplighter's daughter. The friend. The one thread in this whole tangled web who had done nothing wrong except care about the wrong person — and who had, three days ago in an interrogation room that smelled of lamp oil, looked Vane in the eye and lied about knowing nothing, badly, bravely, and then broken and told the truth, which was worse: that she knew everything, that she'd followed him herself, that she would follow him again.

"He'll listen to you," Vane said. "That's why you're here. Not as a hostage. As a voice he trusts."

"And if he doesn't listen?"

"Then I do what the law requires." Vane gathered her reins. "I'm sorry for it either way. That's worth nothing to you, I know. But it's true."

Tamsin laughed — a short, bitter sound swallowed instantly by the wind. "You're sorry. He's sorry. My master's sorry. Everyone in this story is sorry, Inquisitor, and the storm's getting worse." She lifted her bound hands. "Cut these. If you want me to talk him down, I can't do it trussed like festival geese."

Vane considered her. Then drew her knife and cut the cords.

The girl rubbed her wrists and said nothing else, but her eyes, when she turned them north, were wet — and Vane understood, with a discomfort she refused to name, that Tamsin Ferro was not afraid of the storm at all. She was afraid of what Rosalind Hartwell might have become inside it.

The column moved. Behind them, far to the south, the lights of Vellamere flickered under a sky gone black at midday, and the first roofs of the outer wards began, very quietly, to groan under the weight of snow that fell as if the whole winter had been saving itself for one night.

* * *

Aldous raised the storm with the ease of a man tuning an instrument he had built himself.

Rosalind stood at the edge of the stone circle and watched his master work, and understood, finally, the full shape of the betrayal. Not the fake death. Not the courier. Those were tactics. This — the storm, the siege of the sky above a city of ninety thousand souls — this was the *lesson*. The final lesson, delivered at last: *this is what your power is for.*

"You're hurting them," Rosalind said. "People who never did anything. Bakers. Lamplighters." He thought of Tamsin, somewhere south of here, and his chest tightened. "You're doing to them exactly what was done to us."

"I'm doing to them what gets results." Aldous stood at the circle's heart, arms raised, the Emberstone burning in his grip like a coal that would never cool. Frost crawled up the standing stones; the vault's mouth behind them had become a howling throat of wind. "Twenty years of petitions, Rosalind. Twenty years of quiet arguments in quiet rooms. Do you know what the Privy Council calls mages' rights? A *seasonal concern*. Something to raise and shelve. But watch them move when their city freezes. Watch them negotiate when their granaries are buried and their river is ice-locked and their precious law courts are dark." He lowered his arms, breathing hard, alive in a way Rosalind had never seen him. "Fear opens doors that reason has knocked on for a generation."

"And when they open the door," Rosalind said, "what walks through?"

"The future."

"A war."

"A reckoning." Aldous turned, and the light of the stone threw his shadow huge across the vault wall. "There is no version of this that ends gently, boy. I sealed this place to buy time, and I spent the time badly, and now the bill comes due. All I ask—" and here his voice softened, and that was worst of all, because it was the voice from the night he'd found Rosalind crying over his first failed warding, the voice that had said *you are not broken, you are untaught*— "all I ask is that you stand beside me when it does. You've seen the memory in the stone. You know what we lost. Help me win it back."

Rosalind looked at his master — the man who had taught him letters and wards and how to walk through a crowd like a ghost — and felt the last of his childhood go out of him, quietly, like a lamp at dawn.

"You didn't send the courier to give me a choice," he said. "You sent him to give me a *role*. The heir. The sympathetic face. The young mage the council could pardon because he looks harmless." He shook his head slowly. "I'm not your heir, Aldous. I'm your alibi."

For a moment — just a moment — something flickered across the old man's face that might have been hurt.

Then the vault shook, and both of them turned toward the entrance, because through the roar of the storm came another sound, thinner and more terrible:

Horns. Military horns, blown into the wind, close enough to mean the soldiers were already on the causeway.

"They came through the blizzard," Rosalind breathed.

"Of course they did." Aldous smiled again, and this time there was nothing warm in it at all. "I trained the Inquisitor General's predecessors, boy. Fear makes them brave. It always has." He lifted the Emberstone, and the singing rose to a pitch that set Rosalind's teeth aching. "Now. Choose your side. The stones won't hold the door much longer."

* * *

The causeway broke them before the vault did.

Vane lost four men to the ice in the last mile — not to cracks, but to the wind simply lifting them off the road like dry leaves, gone south into the white. After that she ordered the column to rope together, and they came on in a single linked line, leaning into a gale that screamed with what she could no longer pretend was only weather. There were words in it. Or near-words. A pressure at the edge of hearing, like a choir rehearsing behind a cathedral wall.

The vault mouth appeared out of the white all at once — a black arch in a hillside of frozen peat, ringed by standing stones that hummed audibly now, each one rimed in ice that grew even as she watched.

"Form ranks at the stones," she shouted. "No one enters until I give the word!"

They had barely begun to obey when the arch lit from within — a red, sourceless glow — and a man walked out of it.

He was old, silver-haired, unhurried, dressed in travelling clothes thirty years out of fashion, and he stood at the top of the vault steps in the teeth of a hurricane as though it were a summer garden. Vane knew the face from the archives. She had spent three nights with those archives, and the face had looked up at her from a twenty-year-old warrant, yellowed and official: *Aldous Wren, mage of the second rank, presumed dead.*

"Presumed dead," she said aloud, and her hand found her sword.

"Inquisitor General." His voice carried impossibly through the storm, arriving at her ears clean as a letter. "You came quickly. Good. I hoped for someone reasonable."

"Lower the storm."

"When the crown signs terms."

"There are no terms for outlaws."

"There are," Aldous said, "when the outlaw holds your winter in his hand." And he raised the thing he carried — a dark stone, burning without light — and the storm answered him, tightening over the fens, and far to the south Vane heard, or imagined she heard, the bells of Vellamere ringing alarm.

Her soldiers' nerve broke then, or began to; she saw it in the line, men shifting, a boy dropping his pike. Sixty soldiers against a man who wore the weather like a cloak. Eleven years of careful, lawful, patient work, and it had all led here, to arithmetic that could not balance.

Which was when a third voice joined the storm.

"She's right, you know."

Rosalind Hartwell stepped out of the arch beside his master — young, snow-burned, exhausted, standing on the vault steps with his hands empty and open. Vane's breath caught. The courier's map, the clerk's reports, five days of pulling threads through the city's registries — and here was the thread's end, walking toward her through the blizzard with his palms showing.

"There are no terms for outlaws," Rosalind called to her, and his voice cracked on the wind but held. "But there are terms for *citizens*. That's what I've come to offer. Not his bargain. Mine."

"Boy," Aldous said softly, warningly.

"You want to talk terms," Rosalind went on, ignoring him, eyes fixed on Vane, on the soldiers, on the storm he had not asked for and could not yet stop. "Then put down the stone, old man. Because you're holding it wrong. You've been holding it wrong for twenty years."

The Emberstone flared. The standing stones shrieked. And in the heart of the solstice blizzard, with Vellamere drowning in snow to the south and sixty soldiers frozen at their ranks, Rosalind Hartwell took one step forward — and the storm, for the first time, hesitated.

Vane drew her sword anyway.
