## 20. Terms

The blizzard did not stop at the vault mouth. It followed Sera Vane down into the earth like a debt.

Snow came through the broken entrance in long white banners, hissing against the standing stones, and the torches her soldiers carried guttered and spat as though the dark itself were trying to drink them. Ahead, at the heart of the Hollow Choir, two men stood beneath a hanging light that was not fire — a stone the size of a fist, suspended in nothing, burning a slow amber that made every shadow lean toward it.

One of the men was Aldous Wren. She had read his file eleven years ago, when she first took the Inquisitor General's chair: master of the Hartwell boy, presumed dead, presumed loyal to the old outlawry. The other—

The other was a young man with frost in his hair and blood on his lip, standing very still, hands open at his sides, looking at her the way no mage had ever looked at her.

Not with defiance. With *hope*.

"Rosalind Hartwell," Vane said. Her voice carried strangely in the vault; the stones gave it back to her a half-beat late, so that it seemed the Choir itself were repeating her words, testing their weight. "By law of the Crown of Vellamere, I am placing you under arrest for the practice of proscribed arts. You will come with me or you will be brought."

"Captain," said one of her soldiers — Dree, the youngest, snow melting down his face, "the girl we brought—"

"I know where Tamsin Ferro is," Vane said. "She is above, in the custody of Sergeant Ilves, unharmed, because that is how I do things." She said it loudly enough for both men to hear. It mattered to her that they hear it. Whatever else happened in this buried place tonight, she would not become the thing her enemies told stories about.

Aldous Wren laughed. It was a terrible sound, warm and rotten at once, the laugh of a man who had been dead so long he found his own resurrection amusing. "Inquisitor. You've come all this way through my storm to arrest a boy who has just done your work for you. Look around you. Look at what's left of me." He spread his arms, and Vane saw then what the hanging stone's light had hidden: the frost on him was not weather. It had climbed him from the ground up, silvered his beard, stiffened the sleeve of his coat where the fingers had frozen mid-gesture. He had seized the Emberstone and the Emberstone had declined to be seized, and whatever power he'd raised over Vellamere was already dying in his hands.

"The storm," Rosalind said quietly. "It's breaking. He can't hold it. I've unmade his claim on the stone — he can't touch it now without—" He stopped. Swallowed. "He'll tell you otherwise. Don't believe him."

"You *unmade*—" Wren's voice cracked upward, and for one moment the mask of the genial dead man slipped and Vane saw the fury underneath, vast and cold as the fen ice. "Boy, I taught you everything you know about hiding, and this is what you do with it? You hand her the knife? You kneel?"

"I'm not kneeling," Rosalind said. "I'm negotiating."

Vane felt the word land in her chest like a dropped coin.

Because that was the truth she had been refusing since the causeway, since the reports began coming in from Mireholt, since the courier's body had turned up in a rented room in Copperlane with a map sewn into his coat: the world had changed shape while she was hunting its edges. There was a stone here that could end cities. There was a boy who could have taken it and instead stood with empty hands before the woman sent to kill him. And there were soldiers behind her — forty of them, hand-picked, oath-sworn — who had marched all night through a storm raised by a mage, and who were now looking at that same boy with expressions she recognized, because she had spent eleven years teaching herself never to wear them.

They were looking at him like a man, not a plague.

"Hartwell." She stepped forward, boots crunching on ancient grit, and stopped at the edge of the ring of stones. Protocol said ten paces. Protocol said draw steel, name the charge, take him bound. Protocol had been written by frightened men three hundred years ago and had never once been revised by anyone who had actually watched magic burn a city. "You understand what I'm required to do."

"I understand exactly what you're required to do." His voice didn't shake. That surprised her; she had expected fear, and there was some, banked deep, but it was mastered the way a lamplighter masters flame — not extinguished, *held*. "You're required to execute me. Law of Ashfall, section one: any person found practicing spellcraft shall suffer death, and no appeal, petition, or intercession shall avail them. I've known that law since I was nine years old. It's the reason I ran. It's the reason I sold luck-knots in the night market for five years and never once let myself work real magic, even when people were dying of fever two streets over." Something moved across his face — grief, old and worn smooth by handling. "I have obeyed your law better than most of the people who wrote it, Inquisitor. I hid. For five years I hid so well I nearly disappeared. And it bought me nothing except a life too small to hold what I am."

The Choir hummed. Or she imagined it hummed. The amber light pulsed once, slow, like a sleeping heartbeat, and every soldier in the vault shifted.

"So here are my terms," Rosalind said.

*

He spoke them plainly, without rhetoric, and later — much later, in the years when people asked her how it had really happened, whether there had been thunder and visions and a voice out of the stone — Sera Vane would always answer: no. There was a tired young man with a split lip, and he talked to me like a clerk presenting accounts, and it was worse than thunder, because thunder you can dismiss.

His terms were these:

Magic would be restored to lawful practice — not licensed like a poison, not permitted like a vice, but restored, as a craft and an inheritance, to anyone who swore the covenant. The covenant itself he had composed in the vault, in the hours after he had taken the stone's memory into himself; he recited it aloud, and the Choir repeated it after him in that strange half-beat echo, so that it sounded less like a proposal than a verdict already rendered:

*I swear by my own name, which I will not hide: that my art shall serve before it rules; that I will work no compulsion on any mind, and no destruction on any body, save in defense of life, and answer for it openly; that I will teach what I know to any who swear as I have sworn; and that if I break this oath, I yield my power to the council's judgment, willingly or by force.*

"And who judges?" Vane asked. "Who holds this council? Because if the answer is *mages judging mages*, Master Hartwell, then you have written yourself a kingdom with extra steps."

"No." He shook his head. "Mixed. Half sworn practitioners, half unsworn citizens, one seat appointed by the Crown. Abuses answered publicly, sentences passed publicly, records kept publicly — in the open registers, the ones your office burned in the purges. We rebuild them. Every working logged. Every warden accountable." He looked at her directly. "I'm not asking you to trust mages, Inquisitor. I lived underground for five years; I don't trust us either. I'm asking you to build something where nobody has to trust anybody's silence. That's what broke the last time. Not power. Silence. Everyone hiding meant no one answering."

"And the stone?" She nodded at the ember-light hanging in the dark. "Your terms say nothing about the thing that ended the last war. Convenient."

"It says everything about it, if you'd let me finish." No anger in it. Just patience, which was somehow more unnerving. "The Emberstone doesn't get a keeper. It doesn't get a vault, an armory, a royal treasury, or a hero. It gets dissolved."

Wren made a sound like a struck bell.

"You can't," the old mage rasped. Frost crawled up his throat as he spoke, and still he leaned forward, hungry past self-preservation. "Girl — Inquisitor — think. One stone. It turned the tide at Cael Morath. It is the only weapon in the world that answers *both* of us. You want it buried in a king's cellar the day I die, waiting for the next ambitious fool? He wants it scattered because he's young and thinks peace is a place you arrive at. Scatter it and you're defenseless. Keep it and you're a target. Those are the only two futures your law has ever offered anyone."

"He's right that those are the only two futures the old way offers," Rosalind said. "That's why I'm proposing a third. The stone isn't a weapon, Inquisitor. I've *seen* it. I've seen what it remembers. It's a wound that learned to sing. It was made by people who thought power was the answer to power, and everyone who built it died badly, and the war it ended killed ninety thousand ordinary people who never cast a spell in their lives." His voice finally cracked, just slightly, on the number. "I watched its memory last night. I watched a farmwife pull her children into a root cellar while the sky burned, and I understood something your law has never understood: outlawing magic didn't protect people like her. It just guaranteed that when magic came — and it always comes — she'd be alone in the dark with no one sworn to stand between her and it."

Silence, but for the wind worrying the entrance far above.

"You want to dissolve the only counterweight," Vane said slowly, "and replace it with *oaths*."

"With accountability. Yes."

"And if the council fails? If in thirty years some warden swears beautifully and breaks ugly? What then? You'll have disarmed the crown and armed the craft, and there will be nothing left to check it but good intentions and paperwork."

"Then it fails the way every human institution fails," Rosalind said, "in daylight, where people can fight it. Your way fails in cellars, Inquisitor. I grew up in those cellars. So did every hedge-witch you ever burned. Ask yourself how many of them broke bad — and how many just got tired of being hunted and started believing they deserved to hunt back." He glanced at Wren, and the look held history, betrayal, and something almost like pity. "Aldous hid for twenty years. Look what hiding made him."

Wren smiled with blue lips. "Look what freedom is making *him*, madam. Five minutes of negotiation and he's already dictating constitutional law. Give the boy a decade."

"That," Vane said, "is enough out of you." She did not look at Wren again. She found she could not, without her hands wanting to do things that would end the conversation permanently, and she was — gods help her — still trying to conduct this as a negotiation.

*

Here is what Sera Vane had never told anyone, not the Inquisitor General before her, not her chaplain, not the dark at three in the morning:

She did not hate magic.

She hated the *fire*. She hated the specific sound a burning house makes when the flames have gone green at the edges, which is the sound her brother had called "pretty" through the window of the house in Ashfall Row, eleven seconds before the roof came down. She had been nine. Rosalind Hartwell had been nine. Somewhere in the wreckage of that year, on opposite sides of a law neither of them wrote, two nine-year-olds had learned the same lesson in different alphabets: *the world will burn you and call it necessary.*

She had built her whole life on the belief that necessity was a wall you could stand on forever.

And now a runaway apprentice was standing in the holiest terror of her profession, offering to tear the wall down and build, in its place, a thing made of names and witnesses and public record — fragile, human, *auditable* — and the worst part, the part that would keep her awake for months, was that some exhausted chamber of her heart whispered: *this is what I should have built. This is what I would have built, if I hadn't been so busy being afraid.*

"Captain Dree," she said. "Bring her down."

Dree blinked. "Sir?"

"Tamsin Ferro. From the surface. Unbind her first." A muscle worked in her jaw. "If I'm going to weigh testimony, I'll weigh it from the source, not from a sergeant's summary. And if anyone asks, I interrogated her within the full forms of procedure."

"Within the full—" Dree caught her eye and shut up, wisely, and went.

Rosalind had gone very still. "Inquisitor—"

"She lied to my clerks twice, obstructed an official inquiry, aided the flight of a proscribed practitioner, and struck Sergeant Ilves hard enough to loosen a tooth." Vane ticked the charges off on gloved fingers. "By law, that's a flogging and a year's labor. By law." She let the word sit between them, doing its work. "You see my difficulty, Master Hartwell. Every hour I stand in this room, the law and I disagree about more things."

*

Tamsin Ferro came down into the Choir with snow in her red hair and a bruise blooming along her jaw, and she walked like a person entering a temple she intended to argue with. Her eyes found Rosalind first — a whole conversation happened in the space of a breath, guilt and fury and relief braided together — and then found Vane, and narrowed.

"You're the spider," Tamsin said.

"I beg your pardon?"

"In the city. Everyone in the low quarters knows there's a spider in the registry office. Threads everywhere, patient, never rushes." Tamsin planted herself between the soldiers and Rosalind, arms crossed, which by every tactical measure was absurd and which Vane nonetheless found herself respecting enormously. "So. You've got us. Stone, mage, witnesses, the lot. What now?"

"Now," Vane said, "I ask you what you saw. And I warn you that I am very good at telling when people lie, so consider simply telling me the truth, which is faster and hurts less."

"I don't lie well," Tamsin agreed. "Ask him. He's had practice lying to *me*."

"Tamsin—" Rosalind started.

"No." She rounded on him, and her voice cracked across the vault, and the stones echoed it back doubled. "No, you don't get to manage this. Five years, Ros. Five years I lit your lamps and shared my supper and never once asked why you flinched at church bells, and you let me walk into Inquisitorial custody thinking I was saving you, and you *let me*." Her eyes were bright and furious. "You know what I found on the road north? Records. Old purge lists, in a smuggler's strongbox in Mireholt. Four hundred names from this district alone. Herbalists. Midwives. A man hanged for making a charm against cattle-murrain. I grew up next door to that war's aftermath and nobody ever told me it was a *war*. They told me it was justice." She turned back to Vane, chest heaving. "So ask me what I saw, spider. I'll tell you true. I saw your agents put a rope around a fen-witch's neck at the ice road because she refused to scout for you. I saw your sergeant hit a sixteen-year-old girl for crying. And I saw him—" a jerk of her chin at Rosalind "—take a crossbow bolt meant for a stranger and knit the man's chest closed with his bare hands, shaking like a leaf, because that's what he does. That's what they *all* do, the ones in hiding. Small things. Quiet things. For copper. For nothing. While your law calls it abomination."

The vault rang with the echo and then went silent.

Vane stood in that silence and felt eleven years shift on their foundations.

She thought of the registries, the beautiful ordered registries, four hundred names from one district, each one entered by clerks like her younger self, each one ending in a date and a method. She had always read those ledgers as proof of vigilance. Standing here, they read differently: a census of a country's self-mutilation. She thought of her agents' report from the causeway — *subject used proscribed arts to preserve the life of a crown soldier* — filed, per regulation, as a crime. She had signed off on that classification herself, in the field, by lantern light, and her pen had hesitated, and she had told the hesitation it was fatigue.

It had not been fatigue.

"Master Hartwell," she said at last, and heard her own voice come out quieter than she had ever allowed it in uniform. "Your terms. Recite the covenant again. All of it. Slowly."

He did. And this time she listened the way she read registries — line by line, hunting the flaw, the loophole, the soft place where ambition would eventually enter. Compulsion forbidden. Destruction only in defense, and answered openly. Teaching owed to all who swear. Judgment by mixed council, Crown seated among them, records public, wardens answerable. The stone dissolved — and there he paused, and looked at her, and added, unprompted: "Dissolved by joint act. Half the rite performed by sworn practitioners, half by the Crown's own hand. Neither side keeps it. Neither side can steal it. It ends owned by no one, which is the only way it ends safely."

It was, she realized with something close to vertigo, *better drafted than anything her own ministry had produced in a generation.* Because he had written it knowing it would be read by someone hunting flaws. Because he had spent five years thinking like prey, and prey learn the architecture of traps better than any trapper.

"Sergeant Ilves's tooth," she said. "And the witch at the ice road. Those go in the record too. Publicly. My side of the ledger gets audited same as yours, or none of this means anything."

Rosalind blinked. "…Yes. Agreed."

"And Wren stands trial. Open trial, civilian court, mixed jury. No dungeon disappearance, no quiet knife in the night. If we're building this in daylight, it starts with the hardest case in the room."

Above them, faintly, impossibly, the wind was dying. Vane felt the change in her ears first — pressure easing, the long scream of the blizzard sinking toward a whisper — and saw snowfall drift down through the vault mouth no longer sideways but straight, gentle, like the sky exhaling.

Aldous Wren began, very softly, to laugh again. "Oh, you *fools*. You beautiful, doomed fools. Oaths. Paperwork. A council." Frost had reached his eyes now; the laugh came out crackling. "The storm was only the opening argument, children. Do you truly think the Crown's ministers will honor one scrap of this once the stone is dust and the leverage gone? You're signing surrender terms with a government that burns herbalists. Enjoy your daylight while it lasts—"

"Wren." Vane turned to face him fully, and let him see, for the first time in his long hidden life, what eleven years of reading other people's lies had built behind her eyes. "I have spent my career learning exactly how much a person believes their own story. You believe yours completely. That's what makes you dangerous, and it's also what makes you *finished* — because a man who believes his own story can't revise it, and the world," she glanced up at the thinning snow, at the boy beneath the singing stone, at the lamplighter standing guard with folded arms, "the world revises. Take him up. Bind him warm — he's earned trial, not torture. And send a rider ahead to the city." She pulled off one glove, bare-handed, and held it out to Rosalind Hartwell across the ring of ancient stones.

He looked at the hand. At her. "Inquisitor. You should know that if your ministers refuse the terms, I won't run again. And I won't hide again. And there are more of us than your registries ever counted."

"I know," Sera Vane said. "I'm counting on it. Someone has to keep the council honest, and gods help me, Hartwell, it's going to be me." Her bare hand stayed open in the cold air between them. "Do we have terms?"

Far above, the last of the storm was breaking apart over the fens, and through the vault mouth, for the first time in living memory, the Hollow Choir caught a thread of morning light — and the Emberstone's song, which had hung in the dark for twenty years, changed key.

Rosalind took her hand.

"We have terms," he said. "Now we find out if the world can keep them."
