## 22. First Light

The hall had been a granary once, back when Vellamere still stored grain instead of grievances. Tamsin remembered hauling sacks there as a girl, the dust golden in the shafted light, her father's hand heavy on her shoulder. Now the sacks were gone and the dust had settled into the cracks of the floorboards, and someone had scrubbed the Inquisitorial seal off the door with such vigor that the wood beneath had gone pale as a scar.

She stood at the back, near the doors, where she always stood. Old habit. Lamplighters learned early that the edges of a room were where you saw everything.

Twenty-two children sat on benches that smelled of hay and lye soap. They ranged from a boy of five who kept trying to eat his own sleeve to a girl of perhaps twelve who sat with her arms crossed like she'd been dragged here under protest and intended to make everyone pay for it. Between them, the middle ones fidgeted, whispered, kicked each other's heels — ordinary children on an ordinary morning, except that every one of them had been brought by a parent who had spent the last week arguing with themselves about whether this was salvation or suicide.

At the front of the room, Rosalind Hartwell was arranging chalk.

That was all he was doing. Arranging chalk. Three sticks of it, white and yellow and a stub of red worn down to a nub, lined up on the ledge of the slate board with the precision of a man defusing something. He'd been doing it for two minutes. The children watched him with the frank, merciless attention children give to any adult who is visibly afraid of them.

Tamsin's chest did something complicated.

He looked different. Not just the new coat — though the coat was new, deep green wool, Oathbound grey at the collar, cut for him by a tailor who had charged him nothing and told everyone afterward that he'd dressed the first warden of the covenant. It was more that he stood differently. For five years she had known Rosalind as a man built slightly too small for himself, shoulders drawn in, hands always half-curled around something invisible he didn't want anyone to see. Now his hands hung open at his sides, and when he turned to face the room, he turned all the way, no glance flicking first to the windows, the doors, the shadows between.

He still glanced at the doors. She caught it — quick, involuntary, the ghost of the old vigilance. But then his eyes found hers at the back of the hall, and held, and something in his face eased, and he smiled the smile she had been waiting weeks to see again: not the market-stall smile, not the apology-smile, but the real one, crooked and warm, the one that said *you came*.

Of course I came, she thought. You great fool. Where else would I be?

"Good morning," Rosalind said.

A scatter of replies. The sleeve-eater said something unintelligible and triumphant.

"My name is Rosalind Hartwell." He paused after it — she saw the pause, felt its weight, knew what it cost and what it meant. Five years he had gone by *Ros*, by *Hart*, by nothing at all, by a shrug and a change of subject. Five years of a name spoken only in the dark, to one person, quietly, like a prayer you weren't sure was safe. Now he said it aloud, full-voiced, in a public hall, before witnesses, and the roof did not fall in. "I'm going to teach you charms."

The crossed-arms girl raised her hand without uncrossing her arms. "My mum says magic's wicked."

"Your mum's right to be careful," Rosalind said, and Tamsin saw several parents along the side wall — yes, they'd been allowed to stay for the first lesson, hovering like rooks — relax by a visible degree. "Magic *is* wicked, sometimes. So are fire and rivers and other people's dogs. That's why we have rules for them. That's what an oath is. Has anyone here ever sworn an oath?"

Silence. Then the little boy with the sleeve said, "I promised my sister I wouldn't tell about the frog," with the gravity of a man confessing to murder.

"Good," Rosalind said solemnly. "Did you keep it?"

"I did until Tuesday."

"Then you already understand the hardest part of magic better than most grown mages I've known." A ripple of laughter went through the benches, and even the crossed-arms girl's mouth twitched. "Today we're going to do one charm. Just one. And it's so small that if you blinked you'd miss it. But it will be *yours*, and it will be honest, and nobody will ever be able to take it away from you or make you ashamed of it. Can anyone guess what we're doing?"

Hands. Guesses. *Fire? Flying? Turning the teacher into a newt?* — that last from the sleeve boy, hopefully.

"Luck-knots," Rosalind said, and Tamsin laughed out loud, alone, in the back of the hall, before she could stop herself.

Every head turned. Rosalind found her eyes again, grinning now, unrepentant. "Something funny back there?"

"You," she called. "Five years selling luck-knots off a blanket in the night market and telling me they were just knots."

"They *were* just knots," he protested, to the room, to history, to whatever gods kept score of small frauds. "Mostly. Today they won't be. Watch."

* * *

Tamsin had seen him work magic exactly twice before this winter.

The first time had been accidental, years ago — a lantern guttering in the rain on Cordwainer's Row, both of them soaked, and she'd sworn afterward she'd imagined the way the flame steadied, leaned toward his cupped hand like a cat toward a warm lap. He'd denied it so smoothly, so instantly, that she'd believed him, because believing him was easier than asking why he flinched whenever anyone mentioned the purges.

The second time had been on the ice road, through Brannoc's retelling — the flare of blue-white light over the causeway, the agents thrown back, the hidden self surfacing at last in fire and terror. She hadn't been there. She'd been in a cell in the Inquisitory by then, listening to Sera Vane ask gentle questions in a gentle voice, learning that gentleness could be a kind of blade.

And the third time — well. The third time had been the stones themselves, the solstice, the night the blizzard broke. She'd been there for that, standing beside a woman who had come to arrest her friend and stayed to negotiate with him, watching Rosalind stand in a cathedral of buried granite and speak truth aloud until it rang louder than the Emberstone's song. She still dreamed about it. She suspected she always would.

But this — this small thing, this morning thing — this was the magic she liked best.

Rosalind took a length of cord from his pocket, plain brown hemp, and held it up. "Now. The old way — the way I learned, and the way I'll teach you — says a charm is a promise made to something bigger than yourself. You braid your intention into the knot. You don't force it. You *ask*. Everyone pick up your cord."

Twenty-two small hands scrabbled at the baskets that had been set out. Cord everywhere, tangling, a minor war breaking out over a red one. Rosalind waited, patient as stone, until order reassembled itself.

"The charm is simple. We're going to tie a knot that holds warmth. That's all. When your fingers are cold on the walk home — and they will be, it's still winter, whatever the almanac says—" a chuckle from the parents' row, "—you'll untie it, and the warmth you put in this morning will still be there. Small. Honest. Yours. Now watch my hands."

Tamsin drifted forward along the wall, unable to help herself, the lamplighter's curiosity pulling her toward the light as it always had.

His hands moved slowly, deliberately, so the children could follow: loop, cross, draw through. But it wasn't the knot that made the hair rise on her arms. It was the air above his hands. It *warmed*. She could see it, faintly, the way you can see heat rising off cobbles in high summer — a shimmer, a softening, as if the room had exhaled. The nearest candle flames leaned toward him, all together, like a congregation leaning in during the good part of a sermon.

"There's no trick to seeing it," he said quietly. "It isn't hiding anymore. What you feel on your hands right now — that tickle, that warmth — that's magic saying hello. It's been saying hello to some of you your whole lives. You were just taught not to answer."

The silence in the granary-hall was total. Even the sleeve boy had stopped chewing.

Then the crossed-arms girl — Maren, Tamsin would learn her name was Maren, daughter of a cooper on Tanner's Lane — picked up her cord, tied three furious clumsy loops, glared at it, and said, "*Warm up,*" in the tone of someone commanding a mule.

The cord glowed. Faintly, briefly, a blush of ember-light along the fibers — but unmistakably, undeniably, glow.

Maren stared at it. Her arms finally uncrossed. Her whole face opened like a window thrown wide, and she looked up at Rosalind with an expression Tamsin recognized from mirrors, from five years ago, from a night market stall where a young woman had watched a peddler's hands move over knots and thought, *I want to know him. I want to know how he does that.*

"Oh," Maren said. Just that. *Oh.* The sound of a world getting bigger.

The hall erupted. Twenty-two voices at once, cords thrust into the air, a chorus of *warm up* shouted with varying degrees of grammatical confidence. One knot caught fire briefly and was patted out by Rosalind with the calm of a man who had clearly prepared for exactly this. The sleeve boy produced a knot that radiated not warmth but a smell of bread, which nobody could explain and nobody wanted to fix. Parents pressed forward against the wall, torn between terror and tears, and Tamsin watched a grey-bearded cooper — Maren's father — mouth the words *that's my girl* to no one at all.

Rosalind stood in the middle of it, laughing, chalk dust on his cuffs, and Tamsin thought: *this is what he was for. All those years hiding in plain sight, selling luck to strangers. This is what all of it was keeping alive.*

* * *

Afterward, when the children had been collected — Maren leaving last, walking backward out the door so she could keep staring at her knot — the hall emptied into the bright cold morning, and Tamsin found herself alone with him among the scattered hemp ends and one scorched bench.

"You were terrible at the beginning," she said. "The chalk thing. Two minutes arranging chalk."

"I was gathering my courage."

"You were hiding behind stationery."

"It's a very respectable place to hide. I hid behind a market blanket for five years; chalk is practically bold." He started gathering the cords the children had abandoned, winding them into a loose coil, and she watched his hands do it — steady, unhurried, hands that had braided a storm apart six weeks ago and now contentedly tidied string. "You stayed for the whole lesson."

"Someone had to guard the door."

"Tamsin."

"Someone had to," she said, softer, "see it. The first one. Somebody should remember this morning properly, and you were busy."

He looked up at that. Outside the high granary windows, the light was doing the thing it had begun doing lately — the thing the whole city talked about, in markets and bathhouses and the queues outside the council hall, the thing people called *the thaw* though technically no ice had broken yet. It was simply that the light had changed. It came longer, lower, gold instead of pewter, and it lay across the floorboards now in long warm bars, and the dust in it rose and glittered like the hall itself was performing a small honest charm.

"I owe you an apology," Rosalind said. "Several. I've been meaning—"

"Don't." She held up a hand. "If you apologize again I'll start charging interest. You've apologized forty times since the stones. I've counted. I'm a lamplighter; I count things."

"Thirty-eight."

"*Forty.* You apologized twice in your sleep at Brannoc's farm, and both times you also tried to give me the map, which I couldn't accept on account of being asleep." She let the joke sit, then let it go, because underneath it was the true thing and they both knew it, and they had earned the true thing, paid for it in cells and ice roads and lies told to faces they loved. "You lied to me for five years, Ros. I'm not saying it didn't hurt. It did. It hurt like a splinter — small, deep, and in everything I did with that hand. But I know why now. I read the purge rolls in Vane's archive. I saw what happened to the hedge-witches of Marsh End. I understand that the lie was a wall you built around something alive, and walls aren't betrayals, they're just walls, and you were the prisoner inside them same as the rest of us were outside."

"That's very generous."

"It's very accurate. There's a difference." She stepped closer, over the drifts of cord-ends, and took his hands — the gesture she'd been rehearsing for weeks and which turned out, in the doing, to require no rehearsal at all. His fingers were warm. Of course they were. They'd been tying warmth into knots all morning. "So here's my forgiveness, formal, witnessed by the dust and the ghosts of this granary and whatever gods kept score of our small frauds: I forgive you. Fully. Not the partial forgiveness I gave you on the ice road, the one with conditions attached, the one that was really just me being glad you were alive. This is the whole coin. Spent. Done."

His breath went out of him slowly, like a man setting down a load carried so long he'd forgotten its weight. "Thank you," he said — and then, catching himself, "which is not an apology, before you charge me."

"See? Learning." She squeezed his hands. "One condition."

"Name it."

"You stop flinching at your name. I heard it this morning — you said *Rosalind Hartwell* out loud and it nearly cost you your posture. That's done now too. The name's yours. It was always yours. Say it like you own it, because you do, and because somewhere in the fens there's a vault full of stones that sang it back to you, and I refuse to let a piece of granite be braver about your name than you are."

He laughed — really laughed, head back, the sound bouncing off the rafters where swallows would nest come spring — and then, deliberately, watching her the whole time, he said it.

"Rosalind Hartwell."

No flinch. No pause. The words came out level and easy as a lamp being lit, and the light through the windows caught them, and Tamsin thought that in fifty years she would still be able to hear exactly how they sounded.

"Better," she said. "Again."

"Rosalind Hartwell. First Oathbound Warden of the Covenant. Terrible at chalk."

"Getting better at chalk."

* * *

They walked out together into the morning, because there was nothing left in the hall that needed either of them, and some mornings are simply too fine to spend indoors.

Vellamere had changed the way a face changes after fever — thinner in places, marked, but unmistakably alive. The Inquisitorial seals were coming down all over the city, door by door, some pried off officially by council workmen and some chiseled off unofficially by citizens in the night, so that the walls wore a patchwork of pale scars and stubborn paint. On Cordwainer's Row, someone had planted a rowan sapling in the crack where the clerk Aldous's agents had once posted the purge lists. It was maybe two feet tall. People watered it. Nobody discussed it. It was simply understood.

The covenant had been signed eleven days ago in the council hall, and Tamsin had stood in the gallery and watched Rosalind sign it — *Rosalind Hartwell*, full name, no hesitation, ink drying in front of the assembled city — and watched Sera Vane countersign it with the expression of a woman who had marched to a war and arrived to find a treaty. Vane was on the mixed council now, the mage-council, the impossible thing nobody had believed could exist until it did: three mages, three crown appointees, three commoners chosen by lot, and Vane at the center of it like a keel, holding the whole vessel upright by sheer force of conviction. She had voted against the first charter draft, against the second, and for the third, and her arguments had made it stronger, and everyone knew it, and nobody said so where she could hear.

Brannoc had taken a post training the wardens — "somebody has to teach these soft city children which end of a causeway is slippery" — and had reportedly already reduced two recruits to tears and one to laughter, and considered this a fair exchange. Aldous was gone. Walked out of Vellamere the morning after the reckoning, alone, carrying nothing, refusing the escort offered and the pardon implied. Rosalind had watched him go from the gate and had not flinched at that either, though Tamsin had stood beside him and felt the effort of it like heat off a forge. Some winters a man carries inside himself, she'd thought. Some thaws take longer than others. She'd put her hand on his arm and they'd watched the road empty together, and that had been enough.

Now they walked down through the waking streets, past shutter stalls opening, past a fishwife arguing cheerfully with a gull, past the rowan sapling, and the light lay along the rooftops like honey poured slow.

"Same time next week?" Tamsin asked. "The class?"

"And the week after. Forty-two enrolled now. Word travels." He glanced sideways at her, and there was the crooked smile again, but with something new under it, something almost shy. "There's a second thing. The council approved it yesterday — a night class. For adults. Hedge-witches who've been hiding twenty years, cunning-folk, anyone who learned scared and wants to learn honest. First session in two weeks. I'll need..." he hesitated, and she realized he was nervous, actually nervous, the man who had faced down the Emberstone, "...help. Someone who knows the city. Who knows the people. Who's good at counting things."

Tamsin stopped walking.

She looked at him. She looked at the street — the scarred walls, the rowan sapling, the light coming up gold over the granary roofs, the whole ordinary miraculous morning of it — and she felt the last cold splinter of the old winter, the one she hadn't even known she was still carrying, slide out of her chest and melt on the cobbles.

"You're offering me a job," she said.

"I'm offering you a *purpose*. The job comes with terrible pay and a colleague who arranges chalk when he's nervous."

"Two colleagues," she corrected. "You haven't asked yet whether I'd say yes."

"Tamsin Ferro," Rosalind Hartwell said, and did not flinch, "will you help me light them?"

She stuck out her hand, lamplighter-fashion, the old guild grip, palm to palm.

"Thought you'd never ask," she said. "First lamp's always the hardest. After that, it's just walking the route."

Down the street, a bell rang the hour — the old cracked bell of Saint Ellery's, silent through the worst years, rung again now every morning by a sexton who claimed the rope had missed him. The sound rolled out over the city, over the thawing eaves and the opening shutters and the small bright knots of warmth riding home in twenty-two children's pockets, and the light came up another degree, patient and certain, the way it always did, the way it always would.

Winter was over. It was, Tamsin thought, taking Rosalind's arm as they walked down into the shining streets, going to be an ordinary day.

She intended to have as many of them as she could.
