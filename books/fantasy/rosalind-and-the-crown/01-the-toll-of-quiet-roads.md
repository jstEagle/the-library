## 1. The Toll of Quiet Roads

The candles smelled of sanctity, and that was the trouble with them.

Oscar Joubert crouched at the mouth of the Hollow Stair, where the Thornfell passes narrowed to a throat of black granite, and breathed in the cargo behind her: beeswax and myrrh and something older underneath, an incense the temple-chandlers of Vaelmoor had no business making anymore. Consecrated candles. Forty crates of them, wrapped in oilcloth and labeled as tallow for the mining camps, riding on six mules who knew better than to complain.

Sanctity was contraband now. The usurper's edicts had made it so three winters past—no open flame blessed in any god's name, no wax poured over a prayer, nothing that might carry a wish upward where it wasn't wanted. Which meant, of course, that every household from the passes to the coast wanted exactly that, and paid smugglers like Oscar handsomely to bring holiness in through the back door of the world.

She checked the sky through the crack of the gorge. Grey, low, holding its snow. Good. Snow meant empty roads, and empty roads meant her memory could do what it did best: keep her alive.

Because that was Oscar's true cargo, always—the one she never listed on any manifest. Ten years of walking these mountains had carved every path into her mind like a mason's chisel into stone. She knew which ledges held their frost until noon and which shed it by dawn. She knew the scree slope above Kettle Gap that sang a half-tone flat when rain was coming, and the hollow beneath the third waystone on the Pilgrim's Cut where travelers had left offerings for two hundred years—coins once, then bread, then nothing at all, because the gods were dead and everyone knew it. She knew forty-one ways across Thornfell and thirty-nine of them would get you killed if you trusted your eyes instead of your memory, because the mountain moved. Slides, floods, the slow shrug of stone settling into its own weight. The passes rearranged themselves the way a sleeper turns in bed, and only someone who kept every road in her head, updated nightly, walked them safely.

Other guides drew maps. Maps lied; they grew stale the moment the ink dried. Oscar carried nothing but what was in her skull, and she had spent ten years making sure there was nothing else in there worth finding. No letters. No keepsakes. No name anyone used twice. *Joubert* was a coin she'd picked up in Vaelmoor and worn smooth with use. Before that—

Before that was a locked room, and she had thrown away the key so long ago she sometimes wondered whether the door had ever existed at all.

"Joubert." Tam Voss, her hired muscle for this run, came crouching up the trail, breath smoking. He was a big man gone soft around the middle, honest in the specific way of men who were too slow to be anything else. "Mule's favoring its near hind. And there's smoke down-valley. Thin."

"Pilgrims." She said it before she thought about why. The valley below the Hollow Stair held nothing—not a farm, not a shrine, nothing worth burning—but thin smoke meant a small fire, a careful fire, and careful fires belonged either to soldiers or to people hiding from them, and soldiers didn't come here anymore. The usurper's men thought the high passes worthless since the roads closed. "One fire, maybe two. We go wide, east of the falls, and we don't look down."

"And if they see us?"

"They'll see candle-smugglers." She stood, knees cracking in the cold. "Which is a hanging offense. So we won't be seen."

* * *

The east fork was hers alone—she'd found it nine years ago, in her first winter, when a rockslide ate the old trail overnight and she'd spent a starving day climbing goat-paths until the mountain, in a mood she still couldn't explain, simply handed her the way. It climbed through a slot of stone so narrow she had to turn the mules' packs sideways, along a shelf where the drop fell away sheer into blue shadow, past a frozen waterfall hung like a sheet over the cliff's face. Nobody used it. Nobody knew it. When she died—if she ever got the dignity of dying somewhere soft—this road would die with her, and the mountain would close its hand over the shape of it.

That suited her. That was the whole architecture of her life, built stone by stone over ten years: be useful, be unremarkable, be forgettable. A woman with a perfect memory and nothing memorable in it. She'd gotten good at forgetting on purpose, at letting names slide off her like water off wax. There had been another name once. She was almost certain there had been. Some nights, on the edge of sleep, it rose in her like a fish rising toward the surface of dark water—and she'd learned to stop fishing for it, because every time she reached, the water went still and the name sank, and something in her chest ached in a way that felt less like grief than like debt.

The mule stumbled on the shelf. Just once—a slip of a hoof on ice-glazed stone—and the whole train lurched, and Tam swore, and Oscar caught the lead-rope with both hands and hauled, heart slamming, while forty crates of stolen holiness swayed over four hundred feet of nothing.

And her sword spoke.

Not aloud. It never spoke aloud; that was the point of her, the thing she'd buried deeper than the name. But blades have voices for those born to hear them, and hers had been muttering to itself for ten years the way old men mutter in chapels—half-prayer, half-complaint, words she kept carefully untranslated. Now, as the rope burned through her palms and the mule found its footing, the steel rang against the rock wall with a clear bright *note*, and for one unguarded instant she heard it plainly:

*There you are.*

Oscar let go of the rope as if it had bitten her.

"You're iron," she said under her breath, in the smallest voice, the voice she used for things that must not be true. "You're a length of iron with delusions."

The sword said nothing more. Swords rarely did unless spoken to properly, and she had not spoken to one properly in ten years, not since the night she'd sworn she never would again. She pushed the moment down into the same locked room as everything else, shouldered past Tam, and called the train onward.

But her hands wouldn't stop shaking for a mile.

* * *

They heard the prayer before they saw the fire.

It came up the gorge on the still cold air, thin as thread: an old man's voice, cracked and wandering, reciting words Oscar hadn't heard spoken openly since childhood. Not the new liturgies, the safe ones the usurper permitted, all incense and hierarchy and nothing asked. This was the old form. The asking form. *Hear me, keeper of thresholds, opener of the stone door—*

"Mad old pilgrim," Tam muttered. "Praying to nobody."

Oscar held up a hand.

Because the prayer was being answered.

Not by the pilgrim himself—his voice had trailed into wet rattling coughs between phrases, the voice of a man finishing his last climb. Something else was carrying the words onward. The echo, she thought first. But echoes repeat; this *responded*. Each line the old man faltered through came back from the gorge walls a half-beat later, reshaped, completed, as though the stone itself were remembering the liturgy better than he did—as though something inside the mountain had been waiting years for someone to start the song and was picking it up mid-verse.

*—hear me,* said the rock, in a voice like gravel rolled slowly in a wooden bowl. *I hear. I am thin. Speak your need.*

Tam crossed himself with the wrong hand and stared at her. "Joubert. Tell me you heard—"

"I heard it. Wind in the rocks." Even as she said it she hated how fast the lie came, how practiced. Ten years of practice. "Old shrines make strange acoustics."

They crept to the lip of the overlook, and there below them, in the cup of the valley, sat the small careful fire and the small dying man beside it. He was robed as a pilgrim of the old rite—grey wool, ash-marked brow—and he knelt with his hands pressed flat against bare stone, as if trying to hold the mountain's attention by touch. His lips kept moving. The answering voice kept rising out of the ground beneath him, patient and vast and terribly tired.

*A soul for a soul is the old toll,* the stone said. *What will you pay?*

"I've nothing left," the pilgrim whispered, and even at distance Oscar heard it, because the gorge gave his weakness to her like a gift. "Only the asking. Only—I only wanted to know if anyone was still listening."

A silence. Long enough that Oscar's breath froze on her lips.

*Someone is,* said the mountain. *We are waking. We are hungry. Go home, asker. Or stay, and be the first course.*

The old man laughed—a small, astonished, joyful sound—and then coughed, and then didn't laugh anymore. The fire burned on. The answering voice had stopped, but the silence it left behind had a texture to it, a listening quality, the way a room feels after someone stops breathing.

Oscar backed away from the overlook and stood with her spine against cold rock, and made herself think like a smuggler: route, weather, timing, the mules, the delivery. Think like a woman with nothing in her head but roads.

But her hand had found her sword-hilt without her permission.

And the sword was warm. Through glove and wrapping she felt it, a heat like a held coal, and beneath the heat a vibration, faint and rhythmic, like a throat clearing itself to speak. The steel had heard the old liturgy too. The steel had heard the mountain answer. And some deep part of the blade—the part that remembered her, the part she'd starved into silence for a decade—was pressing against the inside of her silence like a child against a locked door.

*Say it,* the warmth seemed to say. *Say the words. They're waking up. It's time.*

"There is no time," Oscar whispered. "They're dead. Everyone knows they're dead."

Above her, far up the gorge where no wind could reach it, a shelf of stone shifted with a sound like a great door testing its hinges.

Tam appeared beside her, grey-faced, leading the mules. "Which road, Joubert? Fastest way off this mountain?"

She looked at the two paths before her. The low road, wide and safe and empty of everything but the ordinary world. And the high cut—her road, her secret—that passed directly above the dead pilgrim's fire, within twenty feet of whatever had answered him.

Her flawless memory told her the low road was correct. Her flawless memory also told her, with the terrible precision she could never switch off, that the answering voice had used the exact cadence of the old toll-prayer, the one from the liturgy of debts, the one she knew word for word without knowing how or why she knew it, the one whose final line was a name.

*Take back what you paid,* the mountain had said to the pilgrim, in closing. *All debts wake eventually.*

"The high road," Oscar heard herself say. "Stay close. Don't speak. And Tam—if you hear anything talking that shouldn't be talking—"

"Yeah?"

"Walk faster."

She led them up into the narrowing dark, forty crates of forbidden prayer swaying behind her, one warm blade whispering at her hip, and somewhere far below the mountain settled around its waking like a cat settling around a meal.
