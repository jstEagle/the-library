## 2. A Woman Called Rosalind

The waystation at Hollowmere sat where three roads forgot themselves—a squat stone inn with a sagging roof, a stable that smelled of wet hay and older lies, and a fire pit where travelers warmed hands they pretended weren't shaking. Oscar had stopped there perhaps forty times in ten years. She knew which floorboard creaked under the third table from the hearth. She knew the innkeeper's wife watered the wine and the innkeeper knew she knew and charged her accordingly. She knew, most importantly, that nobody at Hollowmere asked questions, because asking questions at Hollowmere was how you ended up as one of the pale stains on the cellar floor.

She had delivered the candles. The buyer, a chandler with soft hands and soft eyes, had paid without haggling, which worried her more than haggling would have. Now she sat with her back to the wall, her sword across her knees beneath the table's shadow, and ate a stew she did not taste.

The mountain had been quiet for two days. That was worse than the singing.

She kept turning it over, the way a tongue worries a cracked tooth. The pilgrim dying in the snow, his prayer rising like smoke—and something *answering*, something with a voice like a door opening in a house long empty. And then her blade, humming against her hip, speaking words she hadn't invited: *she is known, she is owed.* She had wrapped the sword in oilcloth that night and told herself it was to protect the steel from salt air. She was a good liar, but she'd been lying to herself so long the craft had gone sloppy.

*You are known. You are owed.*

Oscar Joubert didn't exist. That was the whole architecture of her life—ten years of careful nothing, a name worn like a coat two sizes too large, a face trained into forgettability. Smugglers survived by being no one. She had been so thorough about it that sometimes, in the hour before sleep, she couldn't quite remember who she'd been before. There had been a girl. There had been a name. The girl had traded both away, or had them taken, and what remained was Oscar: competent, invisible, alive.

Except now the stone itself was saying she was *owed*, and debts, in her experience, always came due with interest.

The door banged open on a gust of sleet and a woman in travel-stained grey.

Oscar noticed her the way she noticed everything—peripherally, cataloguing, dismissing. Grey wool cloak, road-burned boots, a satchel slung crosswise. Hands that were wrong for a pilgrim: ink-stained, calloused at the fingertips in the pattern of someone who turned pages by the thousand. A priest's hands, or a clerk's. Her hair was dark going silver at the temples, cropped short and practical, and her eyes—

Her eyes stopped the dismissal cold.

Oscar knew those eyes. Not from meeting them—from *missing* them. It was the specific vertigo of seeing a face half-remembered from a dream, the certainty and the impossibility arriving together. She had seen this woman before. She had never seen this woman before. Both things were true, and the space between them ached like an old break in bad weather.

The woman scanned the room once, unhurried, and found exactly what she was looking for. Of course she did. Oscar sat with her back to the wall because everyone did; the difference was that this stranger looked at the wall behind Oscar first, then the windows, then the two exits, and only then at Oscar herself—with an expression that was not surprise but recognition, and not warm recognition either. Recognition the way you recognize a scar.

She crossed the room and sat down across from Oscar without asking.

"Waystation stew," the woman said. "I've had it twice. Both times I regretted it."

"You've got me confused with someone who wants company."

"No." The woman set her satchel on the table between them, deliberate as a judge laying down a writ. "I don't think I have."

Up close there was something else wrong with her, something Oscar's eye snagged on and couldn't release. She was maybe forty, weathered, competent—but behind her eyes there was a blankness, a smooth place like a wall where a door used to be. People carried their pasts in their faces, all the small accumulated expressions of a lived history. This woman's face was lived-in everywhere except one region, right behind the eyes, and that region was swept clean as a shrine floor.

"I'm looking for a smuggler," the woman said. "One who works the Thornfell passes. Reliable. Discreet. Someone who knows roads that don't appear on any map worth trusting."

"Then you're looking for several people, and none of them are sitting here."

"The candles," the woman said. "Blessed tallow, sold out the back of the chandlery in Vaelmoor's lower quarter, moved through the passes every third week for six years. Never once caught. That's not luck. That's memory. Someone remembers every road, every cache, every debt of favor along the way. Perfectly." She leaned forward slightly. "I need that memory."

"And I need you to find another table."

"My name is Rosalind."

The name landed somewhere in Oscar's chest and detonated quietly. Not a memory—worse. An echo of a memory, the shape of a sound with the substance scraped out. *Rosalind.* She could almost feel the word in her own mouth, could almost taste the context in which she'd last said it, and there was nothing there. A doorway painted on a wall.

"That means nothing to me," Oscar said, and heard her own voice do the thing it did when she lied badly to herself.

"It doesn't. That's rather the point." Rosalind unlaced the satchel. "Ten years ago, we made a bargain, you and I. You don't remember it. I remember making it and can't remember why, which I promise you is worse. I have spent a decade trying to reconstruct a single conversation, and I have recovered—" her voice went flat and careful, "—fragments. Enough to know it mattered. Enough to know it involved you. Not enough to know what I did."

"I think you should leave."

"In a moment." From the satchel she drew a book.

It was not a grand thing. Ledger-sized, bound in grey leather gone soft with handling, its corners rounded by years of pockets and palms. But when Rosalind set it on the wood, the firelight behaved strangely around it—the flames leaning toward it, the shadows gathering under it like water finding a low place. Oscar's skin prickled. She knew that wrongness. She'd felt it in the pass, in the stone, in the answering dark.

"What is that?"

"A ledger." Rosalind's hand rested on the cover without opening it, the way you'd rest a hand on a sleeping animal you didn't entirely trust. "Every soul sold to the under-court beneath Thornfell. Every bargain brokered in the old days, when the gods still answered prayers and the price was always the same. Names, dates, terms. It was kept by the priesthood of the deep shrines." Her mouth twisted. "By me, once. I think. I keep finding my own handwriting in margins I don't remember writing."

"You're mad," Oscar said, without conviction.

"Probably. Madness runs in broken memories." Rosalind opened the book.

The pages were vellum, dense with columns of script in an old liturgical hand, and interspersed among them—newer entries, cruder, in different inks, added over years like barnacles on a hull. The ledger had never stopped being written in. Somewhere beneath the mountain, someone was still keeping accounts.

Rosalind turned pages with a scholar's economy, and Oscar watched despite herself, watched the names march past—names of the dead, names of the missing, names of children marked with a small symbol she didn't recognize and, in her gut, did. Each entry ended the same way: *paid in full* or *payment deferred* or, in a few cases, *held in trust.*

"Held in trust," Rosalind said softly. "That's what they call it when the soul isn't consumed outright. When it's kept. Collateral against a debt still running." She turned one more page, and another, and stopped.

She turned the book around.

There, in the middle of the page, in ink that had faded to brown but was unmistakably a child's uncertain hand guided by an adult's steadier one, was a name.

Not *Oscar.*

The name beneath the entry was a girl's name, one Oscar had not heard spoken aloud in ten years, one she had buried so deep she'd convinced herself it had never belonged to her at all. The letters wavered. Beside them, in the older priestly script: *sold by proxy, age seven, payment deferred until majority. Held in trust. Purpose of sale: survival. Witnessed and brokered by R——*

The rest of the line was smudged, water-damaged, eaten. But the initial hung there like a hook.

*R.*

Oscar became aware that she had stopped breathing. She made herself start again, slowly, the way she'd been taught to steady a hand on a knife edge.

"That's not my name," she said.

"It's the name you were born with."

"People share names."

"Not this one. Not with these terms." Rosalind's voice was gentle now, and gentleness from a stranger was somehow more threatening than a blade. "Age seven. Sold by proxy. Payment deferred until majority—which has come, by my count, four years ago. The debt is due, Oscar. Or whatever your name is. The mountain has started calling in its accounts. You felt it, didn't you? In the passes. The stone answering prayers. Your blade waking up."

"How could you possibly—"

"Because I brokered the sale." Rosalind said it steadily, but her knuckles were white on the ledger's edge. "Or I believe I did. The witness mark is mine—I've compared it to documents I know I wrote. Which means that some version of me, years ago, stood in a shrine and helped sell a child's soul to the hollow throne, and then chose—or was made—to forget it. And that child grew up to become the best smuggler in the Thornfells, and I have spent ten years and most of my mind trying to remember why I ever came looking for her."

The fire popped. Somewhere in the stable a horse stamped. The ordinary sounds of the world went on, indifferent, while Oscar sat very still and felt the floor of her life tilt beneath her.

She wanted to laugh. She wanted to throw the woman out into the sleet. She wanted, most of all, to open her own skull the way you'd open a locked strongbox and finally see what was inside—because that was the horror of it, the true horror, not the ledger or the debt or the hungry mountain. It was that her own past had become a locked door, and someone else was standing in front of it holding a key she couldn't use.

"There's more," Rosalind said.

"Don't."

"You need to hear it."

"*Don't.*"

But Rosalind was already turning the page, and Oscar—God help her, the girl who had traded everything for survival, the woman who remembered every road and no reasons—looked anyway.

Below the entry, in fresher ink, in a hand that was neat and patient and utterly inhuman, someone had added a single line:

*Payment deferred no longer. The account comes due at the next dark of the moon. Let the bearer of the blade be present, or the trust reverts to the court entire.*

Rosalind closed the book. Outside, the sleet had thickened to snow, and far off in the bones of the Thornfells, faint but unmistakable, something in the stone began—very softly—to sing.

And against Oscar's hip, through oiled cloth and scabbard leather, her sword hummed the melody back.
