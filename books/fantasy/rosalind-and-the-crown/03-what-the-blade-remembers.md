## 3. What the Blade Remembers

Rosalind had spent three days learning to read Oscar Joubert the way she once read scripture: closely, hungrily, and with the constant fear of misinterpretation.

The smuggler gave away almost nothing with her face. Her body was another matter. When they walked, she placed her boots like a woman counting coins—each step measured against some ledger only she could see. When they stopped to rest, she chose ground the way a miser chooses a hiding place. And when Rosalind mentioned the under-court, even obliquely, even in a whisper meant for no one, Oscar's hand drifted to the sword at her hip and rested there, two fingers on the scabbard, like a mother quieting a child.

That was how Rosalind knew the blade still spoke to her. Or rather—that it had never stopped trying.

"You could just ask me," Oscar said that third evening, without turning from the fire she was building small and smokeless between two stones. "Instead of staring holes in my back."

"I wasn't staring."

"You were praying at my spine. I felt it." She fed a twist of dry lichen to the flames. "Whatever you want to say, say it."

Rosalind wrapped her arms around her knees. The waystation behind them was a day gone now—a burned shell, an overturned cart, the first bodies. The inquisition riders had come for her ledger before dawn, and they had not stopped coming since. "Your sword," she said. "When did you last speak to it?"

The silence stretched long enough that the fire had time to crackle twice.

"Ten years," Oscar said finally. "Give or take a winter."

"And it hasn't—"

"It talks. It never shuts up, if you want the truth of it. But I don't answer. That's the arrangement." She glanced over her shoulder, and for one unguarded moment Rosalind saw what lived beneath all that careful stillness: not coldness but fear, worn smooth as river stone by years of handling. "A swordspeech is a door, priestess. You don't get to open it halfway. You open it, and things come through, and one of those things is everything I buried to survive."

"Oscar—"

"Don't." The word came out flat and final. "I know what you're going to say. I know about the ledger. I've known since you put it in front of me at the waystation and watched my face like a physician watching for fever. My name is written in your book. Sold. Pawned. Whatever word your church uses when a child is traded like a bolt of cloth." She turned back to the fire. "I have spent ten years not thinking about that night. Do you understand? Not thinking is a skill. I am very, very good at it. And you want me to sit down with a talking sword and dig it all up again because you think it will help."

"I think it will save your life."

"Well," Oscar said softly, "that's different, isn't it."

* * *

They came at moonrise.

Rosalind heard them first—not the horses, which moved with the disciplined quiet of inquisition cavalry, but the dogs. Three notes of belling, far down the valley, cut off by a handler's whistle. Then nothing, which was worse. Silence meant the hounds had been given scent and loosed.

Oscar was already awake. Of course she was; Rosalind suspected the woman slept the way a knife slept, all edge and readiness. She stood at the mouth of their shallow overhang with her head tilted, listening, and when she spoke her voice was stripped of everything but function.

"Six riders, maybe eight. They'll reach this ridge by the second bell after midnight. There's a route east through the Karst Teeth—the deep passes—but it starts half a mile back along our trail, and they'll be on our scent by then."

"Then we run now."

"We run now," Oscar agreed, "and we're dead by morning. Horses on open ground, Rosalind. We need them to lose us, and there is exactly one thing in this world that can make six trained inquisition hounds forget a trail they've been given." Her hand went to her hip. Two fingers on the scabbard. "And I told you I won't use it."

"You said you won't *answer* it." Rosalind rose, keeping her voice low and level, though her heart was hammering against her ribs like something caged—which, she thought, was precisely what it was. "There's a difference between opening a door and standing in the doorway. Speak to the blade. Ask it to cloud the trail, confuse the dogs, whatever old craft it knows. One question, one answer, and then you can close your mouth and go back to your ten years of silence."

"And if it doesn't stop at one question?" Oscar's laugh had no humor in it at all. "You don't know what these blades are. They remember everything their speaker ever told them. Everything. Every secret whispered over the steel, every oath, every—" She stopped. Her jaw worked. "Every prayer. A sword is a confessor that never absolves, priestess. Mine has ten years of my silence stored up in it, and it is *hungry*, and you are asking me to feed it."

Down the valley, faint but unmistakable now, came the belling of the hounds again. Closer.

"Yes," Rosalind said. "I am."

She saw the moment Oscar decided. It was not surrender—it was arithmetic, the same cold accounting she brought to every road and every risk. Six riders against one question. Her pride against both their lives. Rosalind watched her do the sums and lose, and watched her hate losing, and thought: *this is what they made of you. Not a coward. An accountant of survival.*

Oscar drew the sword.

It came out of the scabbard with a sound like a long-held breath released, and the moonlight ran down the blade and did not stop where steel should have ended—it pooled, it lingered, it *waited*. Rosalind had seen swordspeech worked only twice, decades ago, in the years before the purges, and both times the memory had seemed like something dreamed. Now she stood close enough to feel it: a pressure in the ears, a taste like iron and snowmelt, the sense of a vast attention turning toward them out of the metal.

"Hello, old friend," Oscar said, and her voice broke on the second word.

Rosalind had expected command. She had expected the clipped, transactional tone of a woman buying passage. Instead Oscar stood holding the blade before her like an offering—or a penitent—and what came out of her was barely more than a whisper.

"I'm sorry. I'm sorry, I know it's been—I didn't mean to leave you alone so long. I needed—" Her breath shuddered. "Hounds. Behind us. Can you—can you spoil the trail? Please."

The blade answered.

It did not speak in words, exactly. Or rather, it spoke in words the way a river speaks in water—there was language in it, but there was also everything the language carried. Rosalind felt the reply move through the air and through the ground and through her own teeth:

***YES. AND OH, LITTLE ONE. OH, YOU KEPT ME WAITING.***

The hounds, half a mile below, began to bay—at nothing, at each other, at a scent suddenly folded into impossibility. Somewhere a rider cursed. But Rosalind barely heard any of it, because the blade kept speaking, gentle and terrible, and Oscar had gone white as the moon.

***I REMEMBER THE FIRST TIME YOU TALKED TO ME. YOU WERE SEVEN. YOUR FATHER'S SWORD, TOO BIG FOR YOUR HANDS, AND YOU APOLOGIZED TO IT FOR DROPPING IT. YOU SAID: FORGIVE ME, I'M SMALL BUT I'LL GROW.***

"Stop," Oscar whispered.

***YOU GREW. I REMEMBER THE NIGHT THEY CAME FOR YOU. THE MEN IN GRAY. YOUR MOTHER'S HAND OVER YOUR MOUTH. AND THE BARGAIN—***

"*Stop.*"

***—THE BARGAIN STRUCK IN THE CHAPEL UNDER THE ROOTS. THE OLD GOD LEANING OUT OF THE STONE LIKE A MAN LEANING OUT OF DARK WATER. YOUR MOTHER WEeping, and the priestess with kind eyes saying: it is the only way, little one, give them the singing part of you and keep the rest, live, live, LIVE—***

"**STOP!**"

The word tore out of her with ten years behind it, and the mountain itself seemed to flinch. Oscar stood shaking, the sword trembling in her grip, tears cutting bright tracks down her dirt-streaked face—and she did not lower the blade, and she did not look away from it, because looking away, Rosalind understood suddenly, would have been the true surrender.

"They took it," Oscar said, and her voice was a child's voice, seven years old and apologizing for being small. "They took the singing part. That's why I can't—why I haven't been able to—why the roads are all I have, because a map is the only song left in me—"

***I KNOW,*** the blade said, soft as ash falling. ***I HAVE BEEN KEEPING IT FOR YOU. ALL THESE YEARS. EVERY NOTE OF YOU THAT THEY LEFT BEHIND. I NEVER LET IT GO QUIET. NOT ONCE.***

And then, as Oscar made a sound that Rosalind would spend the rest of her life trying not to hear again in her dreams, the sword did the cruelest and kindest thing Rosalind had ever witnessed a weapon do.

It began to sing.

Low, wordless, a melody with no source but steel and moonlight—and it was Oscar's voice. Perfectly hers, every grain and break of it, the voice of a girl who had grown up whole and unburied, singing the songs of the Thornfell passes that no one had heard from her lips in a decade. Oscar sank to her knees in the pine needles with the sword across her lap like a sleeping child, and she wept—not quietly, not with dignity, but with the raw, ugly, holy abandon of someone tearing a bandage off a wound ten years deep.

Rosalind knelt beside her. She did not touch her. Some griefs must be entered alone, like rooms; you can only hold the door.

*I brokered that bargain,* she thought, and the knowledge sat in her chest like swallowed stone. *Kind eyes. Chapel under the roots. I said the words. I wrote the terms. And then I paid a god to let me forget that I had done it, because I could not carry it—and she has carried the loss of it every single day since.*

The hounds had gone silent. The riders, cheated of their scent, would cast about until dawn and find nothing. They had time. For the first time in three days, they had time.

* * *

They walked east through the dark hours, into the passes the maps called the Karst Teeth and the smugglers called the Deep Roads—limestone country, black stone rising in needles against the stars, the trail threading up through narrows where the sky became a ribbon and then a memory.

Oscar led. She always led; it was her native language, terrain. But she was changed, and did not trouble to hide it. Her face was swollen-eyed and scrubbed raw, and she walked with the sword drawn now, bare in her hand, its point trailing a whisper above the stone.

"She'll talk your ear off now," Oscar said at last, as they climbed into the first gray suggestion of dawn. "Now that I've answered. Ten years of backlog." There was something in her voice that was not quite bitterness and not quite gratitude—something new, still wet, still unnamed.

"What does she say?"

"Mostly she says my name." Oscar's jaw tightened. "My real name. Not Oscar. The one my mother used. The one that got written in your ledger." She stopped at the head of the switchback, where the pass opened onto a view that stole breath even in darkness: the whole drowned valley of the under-country, miles below, and somewhere beneath its floor, the hollow thrones.

"The inquisition won't stop," Rosalind said. "And the ledger won't either. Your name is owed, Oscar. The debt is walking toward us faster than the riders ever could."

"I know." Oscar looked down at the blade, and the blade, catching the first light, gleamed like something newly woken. "And I know where we have to go to settle it. Beneath the mountain. Into the court." She exhaled, long and slow, a woman stepping off a cliff she'd been circling for ten years. "But first, priestess—you're going to tell me exactly what you remember about that chapel under the roots. Because the sword remembers your voice too."

The blade sang a single low note of agreement, and it sounded, unmistakably, like a warning.
