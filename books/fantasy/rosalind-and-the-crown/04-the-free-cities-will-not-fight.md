## 4. The Free Cities Will Not Fight

Vaelmoor announced itself the way a rich man announces himself: loudly, and with the expectation of applause.

Oscar saw it first from the shoulder of the last hill before the lake country—a sprawl of white walls and copper roofs catching the afternoon sun, terraced gardens spilling down toward water so still it looked poured rather than fallen. Banners hung from every tower. Not war banners. Market banners. The free cities had always flown their wealth where other places flew their arms, and in ten years of smuggling she had learned to read that difference the way sailors read weather.

"They'll have heard already," Rosalind said beside her. She had pulled her priestess's grey hood up against the wind, though there was little left of her order for the hood to honor. "News travels faster than armies when the news is terrible."

"Then they've had time to prepare."

Rosalind gave her a look—patient, tired, the look of someone who had watched this exact hope die in a hundred different faces. "Time to prepare isn't the same as wanting to fight. Come. We should be inside the walls before dark."

They joined the stream of traffic on the lakeside road: carters, fishwives, a troupe of mummers with their wagon painted like a grinning sun. No one looked twice at two dusty women on foot. That was the old magic, the only kind Oscar had ever trusted—the magic of being no one. She had spent a decade perfecting it. Walk like you're expected somewhere. Carry nothing worth stealing. Meet no one's eyes.

But her sword rode against her back under the travel cloak, and since the deep passes it had not been quiet. It murmured now, low and constant, like a dog scenting something on the wind. *She is close,* it had said that morning, meaning Rosalind, meaning whatever thread of the bargain tied the three of them together. *The debt walks with us.*

Oscar had told it to be silent. It had hummed instead, which was somehow worse.

* * *

The gates of Vaelmoor stood open, because open gates were good for trade, and two spearmen in polished half-armor waved them through without a question. Inside, the city was all noise and color and deliberate cheer. Fishmongers cried their wares. Children ran shrieking through fountains. A crier was reading grain prices from the steps of the customs house, and a crowd gathered around him the way crowds gather around anything spoken aloud—as if words could keep the world ordinary.

"It's beautiful," Rosalind said quietly.

"You think so?"

"I think it's exactly what's at stake." She turned, and in the slanting light Oscar saw how thin she had gotten since the passes, how the stolen years showed in the hollows of her face. "The Council of Nine meets tomorrow morning. I have an old friend among them—or I had one, before my memories were taken. Alderman Corvane. He owes me a debt he doesn't remember owing."

"That seems to be the theme," Oscar said, and did not bother keeping the bitterness out of it.

Rosalind flinched. Good, some small cruel part of Oscar thought. Let it cost her too.

* * *

Alderman Corvane received them that evening in a townhouse that smelled of beeswax and old money, a soft-bodied man with clever eyes and rings on six fingers. He listened to Rosalind's account—the waking gods, the ledger of sold souls, the usurper's inquisition sweeping the mountain provinces—with his hands folded and his face arranged into sympathy.

When she finished, he was quiet for a long moment. Then he said, gently, the way one speaks to the bereaved: "And what would you have Vaelmoor do?"

"Fight," Rosalind said. "Or at least refuse him. Close your gates. Stop paying tribute. If the five free cities stand together—"

"Five cities that haven't agreed on a toll schedule in forty years." Corvane smiled without warmth. "Priestess. Do you know what the usurper's tribute costs us? A tenth of our harvest tariffs. A tenth. For that, his soldiers do not burn our granaries, his inquisitors do not knock down our doors, and our merchants sleep soundly. You're asking me to trade a tenth for everything."

"For your souls," Rosalind said. "He's collecting children, Corvane. Oath-bound children. Souls pawned to the under-court. That's what the tribute has become."

"And how many such children are there in Vaelmoor?" He spread his ringed hands. "A dozen? Two? I grieve for them. Truly. But I will not spend ten thousand lives to ransom a handful from a god we cannot see and an army we cannot beat. Prudence is not cowardice, madam. Prudence is why Vaelmoor still stands while Thornfell burns."

Oscar had been silent through all of it, standing by the door in her smuggler's anonymity, and she found she could not stay silent anymore.

"Thornfell didn't burn because they were foolish," she said.

Corvane looked at her as if noticing furniture that had begun to speak. "And you are?"

"A smuggler. Ten years in those passes. I watched the mountain provinces buy their safety the same way you're buying yours—one concession at a time, each one reasonable, each one small." She stepped forward into the lamplight. "It didn't protect anyone. It just taught him the price of you. And now he's raised it."

"The mountain folk were never ours to protect—"

"No," Oscar agreed. "We weren't. That was the whole point, wasn't it? As long as it happened to someone else, far away, behind mountains—it wasn't a problem. It was geography." Her voice came out flat and cold, and she realized she was talking about herself, about the girl who had been sold, about the decade of silence, and she made herself stop before she said anything true. "Prudence," she finished. "That's a fine word for it."

Corvane's face tightened. Rosalind touched his sleeve. "Tomorrow," she said. "When the herald comes. Just—watch. That's all I ask. Watch what prudence buys."

* * *

The herald arrived at midmorning, and the Council of Nine received him in the Grand Forum, beneath the bronze statue of Vaelmoor's founding merchant holding aloft a set of scales.

Oscar watched from the edge of the crowd, hood up, sword humming against her spine like a struck bell that wouldn't fade. Rosalind stood closer to the front, grey-robed, unhidden—a choice Oscar still didn't understand and couldn't stop watching.

The herald was young. That was the worst of it. A narrow-faced young man in the usurper's black and silver, carrying a scroll case instead of a sword, flanked by four soldiers whose armor was the dull grey of the mountain iron. He did not shout. He read the usurper's demands in a clear, bored voice, the way a man reads a shipping manifest, and somehow that was more frightening than any threat could have been.

"—and whereas the Crown beneath the Mountain has resumed its ancient rights, the free city of Vaelmoor shall render, in addition to the established tribute: all persons bound by oath to the under-court, of whatever age, to be delivered to the Crown's officers within thirty days. Lists of the oath-bound shall be compiled by the city archive and surrendered with the persons themselves. Resistance shall be accounted rebellion. Concealment shall be accounted theft—theft, that is, of property belonging to the Crown."

Property. The word went through the crowd like a cold draft, and Oscar felt it find the place in her chest where her missing soul should have been. Somewhere beneath the mountain, in a hollow court full of hollow gods, there was a cage with her name on it, and this boy in black had just read out loud that she belonged to someone.

The Council's speaker—an old woman with a chain of office and a face like a folded map—rose to answer. "The Council will consider the Crown's request with the seriousness it deserves."

"There is nothing to consider," the herald said. Still bored. Still polite. "There is only compliance or rebellion."

"We are a free city," the speaker said, and even she seemed to hear how the word had gone thin. "We will... respond within the month."

The herald rolled up his scroll. "The Crown accepts your response in advance," he said, and almost smiled. "It always does."

As he turned to go, his gaze swept the crowd—and caught, for half a heartbeat, on Rosalind's grey hood. Something flickered in his young face. Recognition, maybe. Or memory. Then he walked on, and the crowd exhaled around Oscar like a held breath released, and already she could hear it beginning: the murmur of relief dressed up as reason. *A month to negotiate. Perhaps the terms can be softened. Perhaps just the older ones, the ones already half-grown, surely not the little ones—*

Bitter clarity settled over her like ash. This was what invisibility had bought. Not safety—just the privilege of watching it happen to others first, and calling the watching wisdom. She had hidden for ten years, and the hiding had protected no one but herself, and now even that protection was being repossessed, itemized, entered into a ledger in a language of ownership.

Her sword spoke against her back, quiet enough that only she could hear it, and for once she didn't tell it to shut up.

*They will not fight,* it said. *But you might.*

* * *

They left the forum separately, threading through the dispersing crowd. Oscar found Rosalind waiting in the shadow of the customs house, and before either of them spoke, a bell began to ring from the direction of the archive quarter—not the market bell, not the alarm bell, but something harsher, arrhythmic, wrong.

Smoke was rising over the eastern rooftops. Thin, black, greedy.

"The archive," Rosalind breathed. Her hand closed around Oscar's wrist, hard, and her eyes had gone wide and strange, as if something long-buried were clawing its way up through the stolen dark. "Oscar—the lists. The ledger. Everything the herald asked for was kept there—and if it burns, if the record of who was sold and who sold them burns with it—"

She stopped. Her grip tightened.

"I think I kept records there once," she said. "I think I need to see what I wrote."
