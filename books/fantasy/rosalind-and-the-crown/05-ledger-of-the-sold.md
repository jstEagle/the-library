## 5. Ledger of the Sold

The archive of Vaelmoor had burned three days before they reached it, and it was still breathing.

Smoke rose from the collapsed roof in slow gray ribbons, curling through the shattered skylights as though the building were exhaling the last of its long life. Rosalind stood at the threshold with her hood drawn back—there was no one left to hide from here—and felt the heat of the dead place press against her face like a hand.

"They came for the records," Oscar said behind her. Her voice was flat, but Rosalind had learned to hear the tension beneath it, the way a lute string hums before it snaps. "Not for grain. Not for gold. For paper."

"For names." Rosalind stepped over a fallen door, its iron hinges twisted like taffy. "A ledger is a leash. Burn the archive and every soul sold to the under-court becomes untraceable—or rather, traceable only by the ones who hold the copies."

"And you're sure there are copies here."

"I'm sure I would have made copies here." The words came out of her mouth before she could weigh them, and she stopped, blinking at the ash-sifted light. *I would have.* She had said it with the certainty of habit, the certainty of a woman who knew her own methods the way she knew the backs of her hands—and did not know them at all.

Oscar's eyes narrowed. She said nothing. That was worse than a question.

* * *

They worked through the afternoon while the city beyond the walls counted its dead and argued in its council hall about tribute. The archive's lower vaults had survived the fire; whoever had set it had been thorough above and careless below, or perhaps hurried, or perhaps—Rosalind thought, running her fingers along a shelf of fused wax seals—had only wanted to destroy what was catalogued, not what was hidden.

She found the false shelf an hour into the search. Her hands found it before her mind did: a pressure at the third brick, a click she recognized the way one recognizes a staircase in the dark. The stone swung inward on a breath of cold, dry air, and there they were—ledgers bound in gray leather, dozens of them, each spine stamped with the seven-pointed star of the old priesthood.

Rosalind knelt among them and did not move for a long time.

"Rosalind?" Oscar's voice, careful now.

"These are mine." Her own voice sounded far away. "I mean—I know these bindings. I know this cipher. I—" She opened the nearest ledger and the smell of it rose up to meet her, ink and tallow and something faintly sweet, like the incense of a temple she could not picture. The pages were filled with names in a hand that made her stomach turn, because it was beautiful, because it was precise, because it was *hers*. She knew it the way she knew nothing else about herself: absolutely, and without memory.

"Read me something," Oscar said quietly. She had taken up a position near the vault door, sword loose in its scabbard, watching the dark. Always watching. Ten years of hiding had taught her that much.

Rosalind turned pages. Dates. Names. Prices. Each entry recorded a bargain struck between a desperate family and the under-court beneath the mountain—a soul pawned against survival, a child spared fever or famine or the usurper's purges in exchange for a piece of themselves that the hollow gods would keep, and feed on, and grow fat upon. She read entry after entry, and her voice grew steadier and colder, because this was liturgy to her, this was *procedure*, and some part of her that predated the theft of her memories understood every column and clause.

Then she reached a page dated nineteen years past, and stopped.

The name at the top of the entry was *Joubert, O.*

Below it, in her own beautiful hand: *Full surrender deferred. Partial lien accepted—one soul, whole, held in trust until claimant comes of age or claims it. Brokered by R.A., under seal of the Seven-Starred Order. Consideration rendered: the child lives. Witnessed and binding.*

"Read it," Oscar said. "Whatever it is. Read it out loud."

Rosalind read it.

The silence afterward lasted so long that the fire above them finished settling somewhere, a soft collapse like a sigh.

"That's my name," Oscar said at last. It was not a question either. Her hand had gone to her sword hilt—not drawing, just holding, the way a drowning woman holds a rail. "That's my name in your book. In your handwriting. You wrote it down. You—" She stopped. Started again, slower, each word laid down like a stone across ice. "You brokered it. You're the one who sold me."

"Yes," Rosalind said.

"You don't even remember doing it."

"No."

"Then how do you know it was you? How do you know that hand is yours?"

And Rosalind, kneeling in the cold vault with the ledger open on her knees, finally let herself look at the truth she had been circling since the waystation, since the first moment she had seen Oscar Joubert's face and felt the shape of a debt behind her own eyes.

"Because I chose to forget," she said. "There is a rite. The priests of the old order used it when a thing we had done was too heavy to carry and too necessary to undo. You cut the memory out and you leave the skill. The hands remember. The conscience doesn't." She looked up. "I can't recall brokering your soul, Oscar. But I built that cipher. I designed that rite. And when I saw your name in the ledger of souls at the waystation, some part of me already knew whose ledger it was, because it was mine."

* * *

Oscar did not strike her. Rosalind half expected it—the woman had a smuggler's temper and a soldier's economy, and betrayal was the one coin she never forgave. Instead Oscar walked to the far wall and put her back against it and slid down until she was sitting, sword across her knees, staring at nothing.

"My mother," she said finally. "I had the fever when I was six. The whole village had it. Half of us died. I didn't." A short, terrible laugh. "I always thought I was lucky. I thought the gods had simply looked away, the way they'd looked away from everything else in my life, and I'd slipped through the gap. I built my whole life on that. On being the one nobody noticed. The one who got away with it." Her jaw tightened. "I didn't get away with anything. Someone paid for me. Someone signed for me. Like a shipment of candles."

"Your mother sought me out," Rosalind said. The fragments were coming faster now, surfacing like bodies in thawing water—she could see a kitchen, a thin woman with Oscar's same stubborn chin, a candle burning down between them. "Or I sought her out. I don't know which. But I remember—" She caught herself. "No. I remember *that I would have remembered*, once. There's a hole where that night should be, and the hole has edges, and the edges say I was there."

"Does it say why?" Oscar's voice cracked on the word. "Why a priestess of the old order would sell a child's soul to those things under the mountain? Why not just—cure her? Hide her? Anything?"

"Because the old gods were already starving," Rosalind said slowly, feeling her way along the edge of the hole. "And starving gods take payment wherever they find it. If your mother had come begging without a broker—if she'd gone to the mountain alone—they wouldn't have taken a lien. They'd have taken everything. Her, you, your whole line, written into their court forever. A broker knows the law of the bargain. A broker can carve out terms." She pressed her palm flat against the page, against her own handwriting. "I didn't sell you, Oscar. I bought you back. With the only currency they accept. And then I couldn't live with the weight of it, so I performed the rite, and I forgot, and I let you grow up thinking you were lucky."

"Lucky." Oscar said the word like she was spitting out a pit.

"Yes. Lucky." Rosalind closed the ledger. "So now you know what I am. The woman who saved your life and stole your soul and then stole even the guilt from herself, because guilt was more than she could carry. Ask yourself what that makes me. I've been asking it since the waystation and I still don't know. Is a person her memories, or her mercy? Because I'm not sure I have either one intact anymore."

For a long moment the vault was silent except for the distant settling of the burning world above.

Then Oscar laughed—low, exhausted, real. "You want to know the worst part? I spent ten years burying my gift so deep I nearly convinced myself I didn't have it. I erased myself on purpose. And you erased yourself on purpose. We're the same stupid trick performed twice." She pushed herself to her feet. "I'm not forgiving you tonight. Maybe not ever. But I'm not leaving you in a hole with a book full of other people's souls, either. So get up. Bring the ledger. We're going to need every name in it if we're going to make the under-court give them back."

Rosalind rose. Her knees ached. Her hands shook. She gathered the ledgers into her satchel, all of them, every name, every price, every sin committed in her own beautiful hand.

They were climbing back toward the ruined main floor when Oscar stopped abruptly at the foot of the stairs, head cocked, listening. From the streets outside came the sound Rosalind had learned to dread in the weeks since the waystation: hooves. Many of them. And above them, amplified by some art she knew too well, a voice like polished brass announcing itself to the city.

"—by decree of the rightful court beneath the holy mountain, all oath-bound children shall be surrendered within three days—"

Oscar's hand found her sword. Through a broken window, far off across the rooftops, Rosalind could see the usurper's banners coming down the Vaelmoor road, black and silver, and behind them a column of soldiers that did not end.

"They're not waiting for tribute," Oscar breathed. "They're collecting."

Rosalind clutched the satchel of souls against her chest and understood, with terrible clarity, that whatever she and Oscar had just confessed to each other in the dark, the mountain had already heard—and the mountain was coming to collect its due.
