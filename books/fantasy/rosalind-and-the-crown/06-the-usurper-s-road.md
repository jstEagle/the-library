## 6. The Usurper's Road

The first sign was the smoke.

Oscar saw it from the shoulder of the Gray Stair, where the pass narrowed to a spine of rock with a drop on either side deep enough to swallow a wagon whole. Smoke rose from the far valley in three columns—too orderly for accident, too evenly spaced for anything but camps. She had spent ten years reading the mountains the way priests read scripture, and the mountains were telling her the same word over and over: *closed*.

Behind her, the column of refugees stretched back along the trail like a wound that would not close. Two hundred souls, maybe more. Farmers from the Thornfell lowlands, miners whose shafts had gone quiet, two sisters who ran a dye-house in Brindle and still smelled of madder root. A handful of children. An old man carrying a loom shuttle because it was all he had saved. They had come to her at the waystation below Vaelmoor with the eyes of people who had already lost everything once and could not afford to lose it twice.

They had asked her which way was safe.

And she had answered, because Rosalind had looked at her with those patient, terrible eyes and said, *You know a road they don't. You've always known it.*

The Shepherd's Cut. It wasn't on any map. It wasn't on any map because maps were made by people with armies, and armies made roads into throats. The Cut was a smuggler's road, older than the usurper, older than the free cities—a goat-track stitched through the high stone where the mountain folded against itself like a hand closing over something precious. Oscar knew every switchback of it. She knew where the scree shifted after spring melt. She knew the shelf of granite where you could shelter forty people from a storm, and the spring that ran sweet in autumn and bitter after frost. She knew it the way she knew her own heartbeat, because for ten years it had been the only thing she'd trusted enough to know completely.

Now she stood at its mouth, watching the smoke, and understood that knowing it was about to cost her everything.

"They've taken the Kettle Gate." Rosalind came up beside her, breath fogging in the thin air. Her priestess's gray cloak was travel-worn now, hemmed with mud, and the ledger of sold souls hung at her hip in its oilcloth wrapping like a dead weight. "A drover came up from the valley. Soldiers at every crossing. They're asking names."

"Names."

"And children. Any child born under oath-days." Rosalind's voice stayed level, but her hands did not. "The herald's decree has reached even here. Every oath-bound child surrendered to the under-court, in exchange for the passes staying open. The councilors' bargain, spreading like rot."

Oscar said nothing. Below them the refugees waited, huddled against the cold, trusting her. Ten years ago she would have walked away. Ten years ago she had walked away from exactly this—a crowd, a need, a name being spoken too loudly—and she had told herself it was wisdom. Invisibility. Survival. The smuggler's creed: *carry nothing you can't drop*.

She had carried herself like contraband for a decade. And it had protected no one but her.

"We go through the Cut," she said. "It adds four days. There's no water past the second shelf unless I show them where the seeps are. And there's a place—" She stopped. Swallowed. "There's a place where the road narrows and we'll be visible from the valley for half a mile. If soldiers are watching, they'll see us."

"Then they'll see two hundred people who trusted you," Rosalind said quietly. "Yes. I know what you're doing, Oscar. You're spending your secrets."

Oscar turned to look at her. "What?"

"Every secret you kept to stay invisible—you're paying them out one by one to keep these people alive. The road only you know. The water only you can find. That's coin, and you're spending it like a woman who means to be broke by the time this is done." Rosalind smiled, small and sad. "Good. Broke people have nothing left to hide behind."

* * *

The first two days went like a held breath.

Oscar walked the line of the column morning to night, moving between the families, pointing out footholds, calling warnings in a voice gone hoarse. She showed the dye-sisters where the seep water gathered in the shadowed crack of the second shelf, and watched their faces when they understood that no map had ever shown them this—that they were drinking from knowledge that lived in one person's head alone. She taught the old weaver the rhythm of the scree slopes, how to walk when the mountain shrugged. Children began following her between stops, asking questions, and she caught herself answering them, and caught herself not minding.

Her secrets went out of her like coins from a torn purse, and strange things grew in the holes they left.

On the third night, camped beneath the overhang of the great shelf, her sword spoke to her.

Not when she touched it. Not when she drew it. It simply spoke, unprompted, into the cold dark while she sat awake on watch, and the voice was the old voice, the childhood voice, warm as a hearthstone:

*You knew this road before you knew your name.*

Oscar's whole body went rigid. Around her, two hundred refugees slept, breath rising in the frost. "Quiet," she whispered.

*I held your hand down this path when you were six,* the blade said. *Your father carried me then. He let you hold the hilt so you wouldn't cry in the dark. Do you remember?*

"I remember nothing," she hissed. "That's the point. That's the *bargain*."

*A bargain takes. But memory lives in steel as well as flesh.* The sword's voice was gentle, and that gentleness was worse than any hunger. *You gave me your words once, little Oscar. All the words of who you are. I kept them safe. I have been keeping them safe for ten years, waiting for you to ask for them back.*

"I'm not asking."

*No,* the sword agreed, and she felt it hum faintly against her hip, felt the faint vibration of want. *You're not asking yet. But you will. When the soldiers come—and they are coming—I will be hungry for the oath, and you will be hungry for yourself, and the two hungers are the same hunger.* A pause, long as a falling stone. *I only want to give you back to you. That is all swordspeech ever was.*

Oscar sat with her arms around her knees until the stars wheeled, and did not answer, and knew she would have to answer eventually, and hated that she knew.

* * *

The soldiers found them on the fifth day, at the exposed stretch—the half-mile of open slope she had dreaded, red in the late light, no cover taller than a man's knee.

She heard them before she saw them: the ring of hobnails on stone, the creak of leather, the particular silence of men moving in formation. Six of them, maybe eight, coming up the switchbacks from the valley side with crossbows slung and a banner-man carrying the usurper's mark—a crown inverted, points down, drinking from the earth.

"Hold the column," Oscar breathed to Rosalind. "Nobody moves. Nobody runs. Running is a confession."

She stepped out onto the open slope alone, hands visible, empty, the picture of a harmless guide. Behind her she felt two hundred heartbeats holding still.

The sergeant was young. That was the worst part—he had a farm boy's face under the helmet, and he looked at the refugee column with something that might, in a better world, have become pity.

"By decree of the Regent's Voice," he called, "these persons are to be registered. Oath-bound children surrender to the escort. The rest proceed to Vaelmoor for processing."

Processing. The word landed on the slope like a dropped stone.

Oscar walked forward another three paces, putting her body between the crossbows and the column, and did the arithmetic she had spent ten years refusing to do: her secrets, weighed against other people's lives. The road behind them, closed. The road ahead, watched. The Cut was the only way through, and the Cut's survival depended on remaining unknown, and remaining unknown had just stopped being possible, because a farm boy with a crossbow had found her standing on it.

"You're a long way from your garrison," she said.

"We go where the decree goes." His eyes flicked past her to the children. "The oath-bound ones. Names?"

Something in her chest went very quiet and very cold, the way it used to go quiet before she lied to toll-keepers, before she vanished across borders, before she buried her name so deep even she couldn't dig it up. She knew this feeling. This was the feeling of spending her last coin.

"There are no oath-bound children here," she said.

The sergeant frowned. "We'll see. Line them up—"

"You won't." She hadn't meant to say it aloud. It came out flat and final as a door closing. "Because if you take one step toward this column, you'll learn why the Thornfell passes were never conquered. Not by kings. Not by the Regent's Voice. Not by whatever sits under the mountain wearing the gods like stolen coats." Her hand came to rest—not drawing, just resting—on the hilt at her hip. "This road is mine. I know it the way you'll never know anything. And I am telling you, soldier, with all the honesty I have left: turn around."

For a moment nobody breathed. The banner cracked in the wind. The farm boy's face worked through fear and duty and the dawning suspicion that the woman in front of him was not what she appeared, that the column behind her was not fleeing but *led*, that he had walked into a story much older than his decree.

And then her sword woke fully against her palm.

Heat bloomed through the grip—not pain, not quite; more like a pulse, a second heartbeat pressed against her own. And the voice rose through her arm and out into the thin air, audible this time, unmistakable, ringing off the stone for anyone with ears to hear:

*SHE SPEAKS TRUE. THIS ROAD REMEMBERS HER, AND WE REMEMBER THE ROAD.*

The sergeant stumbled backward. One of his soldiers dropped a crossbow. Somewhere behind Oscar, a child in the refugee column gasped—the sound of someone hearing a legend speak.

"The mountain," the banner-man whispered. "The mountain *talked*."

"It talked," Oscar agreed grimly, and felt the truth of the next moment arrive in her chest like a swallowed coal, because she understood suddenly what the sword had done, what it had cost her without asking. Every soldier on this slope had heard it. Every refugee. The secret of swordspeech—the last secret, the deepest one, the one she'd traded her soul to bury—was loose in the world now, and it would run ahead of her to the usurper faster than any rider. There would be no invisibility after this. There never would again.

The sergeant looked at his men. He looked at the mountain. He looked, last of all, at the children.

"Fall back," he said hoarsely. "Fall back to the gate. We report this."

They went, quick and clattering, down the switchbacks like water finding a drain, and the slope was silent except for wind and the slow, shaking exhale of two hundred people.

Rosalind reached her first. "You understand what's happened," she said softly. "They'll bring the inquisition now. Real ones. And they'll bring word to the under-court that a swordspeaker walks the high passes. Everything you built your life on hiding—it's all spent."

"I know." Oscar's hand trembled on the hilt. Beneath her palm, the blade pulsed once, satisfied, patient, warm. *Soon*, it did not say, but meant.

She turned and faced the column. Two hundred faces. The dye-sisters. The weaver with his shuttle. The children who had learned her rhythms, her warnings, her name—because somewhere in the last five days they had started using it, *Oscar*, saying it like it belonged to something, and she had stopped flinching.

"Move," she said, and her voice carried. "We have one day's lead and a hard climb, and I'm going to spend every secret I have left getting you through. All the way through. To the drowned mine below the mountain—because that's where the road ends, and it's where my road began, and there's something down there with my name on it that I intend to take back."

She led them upward into the last light, and behind her, soft as a promise, her sword began—very quietly—to teach her the first words of the oath.
