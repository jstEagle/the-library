## 7. Beneath the Mountain

The drowned mine had a name once. Rosalind could feel it pressing at the edges of her stolen memory like a word spoken underwater—*Kellharrow*, maybe, or something older, something that predated names and needed none because everyone who mattered already knew it. She stood at its mouth with a lantern in one hand and Oscar's wrist in the other, and she listened to the water breathing inside.

It was not a stream's breathing. Streams gurgle, trickle, fall. This rose and fell in long, slow swells, like the chest of an enormous sleeper, and between each swell there came a sound that Rosalind's body recognized before her mind did: a hum, low and sweet, threaded through with voices.

"Souls," she said quietly. "That's what singing sounds like when it isn't in a throat anymore."

Oscar said nothing. She had said very little since Vaelmoor, since the burned archive, since Rosalind had knelt in the ash of her own past and confessed what she had done—the bargain brokered, the memory cut away, the girl named Oscar Joubert sold by inches to save her life. Since then Oscar had looked at her the way one looks at a door that has been locked for ten years: not with hatred, but with the terrible patience of someone deciding whether to open it or burn the house down around it.

Now she looked past Rosalind, into the dark.

"The water's warm," Oscar said. "Mountain water should be cold."

"It's fed from below." Rosalind lifted the lantern. "From *him*. The god of debts keeps his hall heated, the old texts say. Hospitality is a form of interest."

"And you remember this how?" The question wasn't cruel. That was somehow worse. "You burned your own memories out of your head, priestess. How do you know what the old texts say?"

Rosalind turned the lantern handle over in her fingers, buying time the way she'd once bought souls' worth of silence with other people's coin. "Because I wrote some of them," she said. "And because the parts of me I cut away left shapes behind. You know this. You've spent ten years walking around the shape of your own childhood."

Oscar's jaw tightened. But she unslung her pack, checked the oil in her second lantern, and stepped down into the mine.

* * *

They descended through galleries where the water reached their knees, then their waists, then—for one long, screaming stretch of tunnel—their chins. The timber supports were black with age and furred with something pale that grew in perfect spirals, and when Rosalind's light touched it, the spirals turned, slowly, all together, like eyes finding a face. She did not tell Oscar what she thought they were. Some mercies were still hers to give.

The singing grew as they went down. At first it was distant, a congregation heard through walls. Then it was near. Then it was *inside*, vibrating in the water itself, in the bones of the skull, in the fillings of old wounds. Rosalind caught herself humming along and bit her tongue until she tasted copper. The song wanted company. The song had been alone a very long time, and it gathered singers the way a fire gathers moths.

"Don't hum," she whispered, when they paused on a shelf of rock above a flooded stairwell. "Whatever you do, don't match its pitch."

"I know." Oscar's voice was flat. "I've been keeping my mouth shut for ten years. It's the one skill I never lost."

But her hand had gone to the hilt of her sword, and Rosalind saw the blade's faint answering shiver even before Oscar felt it—that thin, hungry tremor of steel that meant swordspeech was waking whether its speaker willed it or not. Below them, in the dark, something vast shifted its weight.

* * *

The last stair opened without warning into a space that had no business existing under a mountain.

Rosalind had prepared herself for a cavern. What she found was a court—a true court, vaulted and pillared, its columns carved from living stone into figures that might have been trees or dancers or weeping women depending on the angle of the light. Water fell somewhere in the far dark, slow and metronomic, counting out seconds that did not belong to any clock above ground. And filling the hall, drifting at every height like snowfall reversed, were lights.

Not lights. Rosalind knew what they were the moment one drifted close enough to show its interior: a small bright shape, curled like a sleeper, turning slowly on its own axis. A soul. Sold, pawned, collected. Thousands of them, tens of thousands, hanging in the vaulted dark like a sky that had been picked apart and hoarded star by star.

And they were singing. Each one with its own voice. Each one with the voice it had carried in life.

Oscar made a sound Rosalind had never heard from her—not a gasp, not a cry, but a small involuntary note, half-swallowed, as if her own throat had been pulled into the choir without permission. Because it had been. Somewhere up in that impossible ceiling, among the drifting lights, was a soul that sang in Oscar Joubert's voice.

"There," Rosalind breathed, before she could stop herself. "There—do you hear it? Second verse, the high line—"

"I hear it." Oscar's face had gone white as bone. "I've heard it my whole life. In dreams. I thought—" Her voice cracked and reassembled itself into smuggler's flatness, the mask snapping down half a beat too late. "I thought I was remembering wrong. I thought I'd forgotten what I sounded like as a child."

"You didn't forget. You were *severed*." Rosalind took her arm. "The bargain took the part of you that could be claimed and hung it up here like a coat in a cloakroom. Everything else walked away. That's why you can't remember making it. That's why your memories have edges like torn paper. You've been living beside yourself for twenty years, Oscar. Half a person, walking very fast, hoping no one would notice."

"And you're the one who hung me up here."

The words landed like a blade laid flat against skin—not cutting yet, but promising. Rosalind did not flinch. She owed better than flinching.

"Yes," she said. "I'll answer for it below, if there is a below. But first we finish what we came for."

* * *

The throne at the hall's far end was not carved. It had grown, the way coral grows, the way scar tissue grows—layer upon layer upon layer, and every layer, when Rosalind's lantern finally gave it light, was faces. Small ones. Pressed into the stone mid-expression, mouths open in the middle of prayers that had been answered too well.

Upon it sat the old gods.

There were seven of them; the texts had always said seven, and Rosalind had assumed the number was poetry. It was inventory. They sat in a row along the dais like effigies on a tomb, robed in stone-colored silk gone grey with dust, and they were enormous—each one tall as three men—and they were *empty*. Their eyes were hollows, deep-carved, and through those hollows Rosalind could see the faint drift of souls passing in and out like breath through a flute. Feeding. The gods did not eat bread or blood. They ate the sold, the sworn-away, the given-up, and they had grown vast and magnificent and utterly vacant on the diet, the way a river runs full and reflects nothing.

At the center, slightly forward of the others, sat the god of debts. He was the only one whose head was bowed, as though listening to accounts, and as Rosalind watched, one massive hand turned slowly, palm up, and a single soul-light drifted down out of the choir and settled into the palm like a bird landing.

It sang. Frightened, high, pleading in a voice that had belonged to someone's daughter. The god's hollow eyes did not change. His fingers closed—not cruelly, not gently, just *completely*—and the singing stopped, and the light went dimmer, and drifted back up into the ceiling to take its place among the hoard.

Rosalind felt awe rise in her chest, ancient and trained, the reflex of a lifetime of liturgy—and then she watched it curdle, sour, turn to ash in her mouth. This was what divinity had become while the world above forgot it. Not mystery. Not mercy. A treasury. A larder. Gods grown fat on everything people had ever surrendered in desperation, sitting enthroned in the dark, holy as a countinghouse and about as loving.

"We shouldn't be seeing this," she whispered. "No one was ever meant to see this. Faith worked best at a distance."

"Faith," Oscar said, and her voice had gone very quiet and very dangerous, the voice she used on men who tried to cheat her crews, "was the distance. That's all it ever was. Rope long enough that you couldn't see the hook."

She drew her sword.

The sound of it rang through the under-court, and every drifting soul-light turned toward her at once—thousands of small bright faces pivoting in the dark like flowers finding sun—and the choir's song broke apart and reformed around a single melody, a single voice, rising from the hoard:

Hers.

The child-voice of Oscar Joubert, twenty years old and unchanged, singing down from the ceiling of the world to the woman standing below with a bare blade in her hand. And the sword answered. Rosalind felt it speak before Oscar could stop it—felt the steel pour words up the girl's arm in a language only two beings in creation shared—and she saw Oscar's face as she listened, saw ten years of practiced numbness crack straight down the middle.

"What does it say?" Rosalind asked softly.

Oscar's lips moved. When her voice came, it was barely there at all.

"It says the debt is due. It says he knows we've come." She swallowed. "It says the god of debts has been waiting twenty years to renegotiate."

On the dais, the center god raised his head. The hollow eyes fixed on them—not on Rosalind, though she had brokered the original sin, but on Oscar, on the sword, on the severed half of a soul still singing overhead—and when he spoke, his voice was the sound of every ledger closing at once.

"*Daughter of the toll-road,*" the god of debts said. "*You are expected.*"

Above them, the choir went silent—all of it, thousands of souls holding one held breath—and in that silence, Oscar's own voice, small and clear and terribly young, called down from the dark:

"*Come and get me.*"
