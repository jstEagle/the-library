## 8. The Bargain Reopened

The under-court did not echo. That was the first thing Oscar understood about it, standing in the drowned dark with water to her knees and her sword drawn and silent at her side: sound went into this place and did not come back. Every footfall, every drip from the mine-shaft above them, every breath she took was swallowed whole, as though the mountain were a mouth that had learned, over centuries, the discipline of keeping its meals.

Before them, the throne.

She had imagined it wrong all her life. In the stories told over smugglers' fires, the court beneath Thornfell was a hall of gold and bone, a king of the old gods crowned in antler and iron. But the throne was only stone—a great rough-hewn seat carved from the living root of the mountain itself—and the god who sat upon it was thin. Thin as a winter branch, thin as the last coin scraped from a beggar's palm. His skin was the gray of ledger paper, and his eyes were hollows, two perfect circles bored through him so that when he looked at you, you saw the dark of the mountain through his head.

He was not alone. Along the walls of the flooded cavern, in niches like the roosts of bats, sat the others—the old gods everyone had buried in prayer and forgotten on purpose. A woman whose hair was riverwater, still moving. A broad-shouldered figure whose chest rose and fell with the slow patience of a glacier. They were not dead. They were *fed*, and what they were fed on hung around them in cages of pale light, each one singing faintly, each one singing with a human voice.

Oscar found hers without looking for it. She would have known that voice anywhere; she had spent ten years refusing to know it. It came from a cage near the god's left hand, small and bright and unbearably familiar, humming a tune her mother used to hum while mending nets—some half-forgotten song of the coast, of salt and tar, of a life that had ended the night a child knelt in a shrine and let a priestess write her name into a book.

Her hands shook. She made them stop.

"Oscar Joubert." The god of debts spoke, and his voice was the sound a quill makes across dry paper. "Or should I say—*Rosalind's bargain, entry forty-one.* You've come a long way down to collect."

"I've come to take back what's mine," Oscar said. Her voice came out flat and hard, the voice she used across bargaining tables in Vaelmoor's back rooms. Good. Let it be that voice. Let there be nothing in it that could be weighed or priced. "You have no claim on me. I was a child. Children can't consent to be sold."

"All debts are inherited," the god said mildly. "That is what makes them debts. Your mother owed. Your grandmother owed. The coast villages owed for their safe passage, for their mild winters, for the storms that turned aside. Someone must pay. It has always been someone." He lifted one gray hand, and the cage nearest him drifted closer, and inside it Oscar's soul pressed against the bars of light like a bird against glass, and sang her own name back at her.

Something in her chest tore along an old seam. She had spent a decade building herself out of practical things—routes, tolls, passwords, the exact weight of a candle crate, the precise number of heartbeats between a sentry's lantern-swing and his turn away. She had built herself into a person with no seams. And now here was the seam, held up in a cage, singing.

Beside her, Rosalind stood very still. Water dripped from the hem of her ruined priestess robes. In the burned archive of Vaelmoor she had found the truth of herself—that she had been the one who brokered this, the priestess of debts who wrote the entries, who chose to cut her own memory out rather than carry the weight of them—and she had confessed it in a whisper on the refugee road, and Oscar had not forgiven her, and had not turned her away either. Both of those facts sat between them now like a third traveler.

"You said a new contract," Oscar said. "Say it, then. I didn't walk through your drowned mines to hear you recite theology."

The god smiled. It was the worst thing she had seen yet, worse than the cages—because it was a merchant's smile, warm and reasonable, the smile of a man who knows he is about to get what he wants and wants you to feel clever about it.

"Direct. Good. Directness saves us both interest." He spread his hands, and between them unrolled a scroll of gray vellum that had not existed a moment before, its lines already written, waiting only for a signature. "Here is my offer, entry forty-one. Your soul—returned. Whole. Untouched by these long years, still singing your mother's songs. You will feel again everything you have paid to keep numb. In exchange: your blades. Both of them—the steel at your side and the older blade, the gift they call swordspeech. Swear them to this court. When the free cities rise, and they will rise, you will stand behind my herald and speak the mountains' obedience into every sword they carry. Betray them. Open their gates. Give me their oaths, and I will give you back yourself."

The words settled over the black water like oil.

Oscar heard them all the way through before she understood them, and then she understood them all at once, and it was almost funny—the neatness of it. Ten years of hiding, ten years of silence, ten years of being no one, and the god beneath the mountain had known exactly what she was worth the entire time. Not her soul. Her soul was collateral. What he wanted was her *voice*. There was only one swordspeaker left in the world, and a swordspeaker could make armies lay down arms or take them up, could turn a city's garrison with a sentence, could do with words what usurpers needed a decade of blood to do.

"You don't want me," she said slowly. "You want the oath."

"I want what the oath can build," the god agreed pleasantly. "Every empire is a debt collection, child. I am simply offering you a position on the winning side. Refuse, and you remain what you are: a smuggler with a hole where her self used to be, who will die in some pass or other, unnamed, unmourned, and your soul will sing in my wall until the mountain cools. Accept, and you go home to yourself. Everyone wins. That is what a good contract is."

*Everyone wins.* Her mother had said that once, haggling over net-mending in the harbor fog. Everyone wins, and someone always lost, and it was never the person saying it.

She thought of Vaelmoor's councilors, weighing tribute against resistance with their soft careful voices, choosing cowardice and calling it prudence. She had despised them. She had walked out of that marble chamber telling herself she was better than they were because at least her cowardice had only ever cost herself.

And here was the god of debts, offering her the same arithmetic with better terms.

"No," she began—

"Yes."

Rosalind stepped forward. The water barely rippled around her legs, and Oscar realized with a lurch that the priestess had been moving before Oscar heard her, that she had seen this coming the way she seemed to see everything—as if some part of her had done this all before, in a life she'd cut out of her own skull.

"Take me instead," Rosalind said. Her voice did not shake. That was somehow worse. "I'm the one who wrote the entries. Entry forty-one is my hand, my ink, my sin. The debt is mine to pay, and I have something your cages are short of." She drew a breath. "A soul that remembers. Mine was taken from me—I know that now—but the fragments came back in the archive, and more come back every day. I remember the shrine. I remember kneeling beside a nine-year-old girl and telling her the bargain was kind. I remember every face I sold. A soul full of memory, full of guilt, full of *weight*—that must feed better than a girl's half-grown song. Take it. Free her. Keep your herald waiting for a swordspeaker who will never come."

"Don't." The word came out of Oscar raw, stripped of all her practiced flatness. "Rosalind—"

"You don't get to refuse for me." Rosalind turned, and her eyes were wet and furious and steady. "I chose to forget what I did to you. That was my escape, and it cost *you* a decade of hiding. I am not making another escape. This is the opposite of an escape. This is the bill coming due." She faced the throne again. "Write it. Strike her entry. Enter mine."

The god of debts looked from one of them to the other, and his hollow eyes gleamed with something that might, in a kinder creature, have been delight.

"Oh," he said softly, "you are both *excellent* value."

And Oscar stood in the black water between the god who owned her past and the woman who had sold it, and understood, with terrible clarity, that this was the true shape of the trap—not the contract, not the cages, but this. Every road out of this place ran through someone else. Take the god's offer and she betrayed the free cities, betrayed the refugees sleeping in the high passes who trusted her routes, betrayed Rosalind most of all. Take Rosalind's sacrifice and she lived the rest of her life as a debt someone else had paid, a woman walking around inside a forgiveness she hadn't earned, which was only another kind of hollow. Refuse them both and the mountain kept her soul forever, singing in the dark.

There was no exit that didn't cost. There never had been. That was the lie she'd been telling herself since she was nine years old and a priestess with kind eyes told her that survival was free.

Her sword hummed against her palm. Faint, insistent, hungry—it had been speaking unprompted for days now, ever since the passes closed, ever since she'd started spending her secrets like coin. It was speaking now, and for the first time she listened past its hunger to the words underneath:

*Not their contract. Yours. Yours. Yours.*

The god unrolled his scroll wider. Rosalind squared her shoulders. The caged souls sang on, dozens of voices, dozens of stolen names, and among them one small bright thread that was hers, singing her mother's mending-song, waiting to be either freed or fed.

"Decide, entry forty-one," said the god of debts, and dipped his quill.
