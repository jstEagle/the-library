## 9. Swordspeech

The god of debts waited, and its patience was the worst thing in the room.

Oscar felt it pressing against her like deep water—the weight of a bargain half-offered, hanging in the hollow air of the under-court. The throne before her was not carved but grown, black stone twisted into the shape of a seated figure whose face had been worn smooth by centuries of worship, or perhaps by centuries of taking. Around it, the old gods sat in their niches like coins left in a dead man's eyes: the god of thresholds with no doors left to guard, the god of harvests with hands like empty granaries, all of them hollow-eyed, all of them fed.

And between them, in a cage of woven shadow that hung from nothing at all, something small was singing.

It sang with Oscar's voice.

She had known it would. She had known since the blade first whispered it to her in the drowned mine, since Rosalind's hand had found hers in the dark and squeezed once—*I am sorry, I am sorry, I did not know what else to do*—but knowing was not the same as hearing. The song was a child's song, wordless, the kind of thing hummed into a pillow so no one else would wake. It was the sound of a girl named not-Oscar, a girl who had been someone else entirely before her mother carried her down into these dark places and paid for her breathing with everything she was.

"Your soul," the god of debts said again, and its voice was the sound of a ledger closing, "for your oath. Swear your blades to us. Speak the names of the free cities' garrisons. Walk back up into the light as our hand, and take this back." The cage swayed gently, though there was no wind. "You have spent ten years being no one, child. We offer you the chance to be someone again. Everyone wants to be someone."

*Everyone wants to be someone.* That was true. That was the whole truth of it, the hook buried so deep in the bait that the fish never felt it going in.

Beside her, Rosalind stepped forward.

"No," Oscar said.

"You don't even remember what you're refusing," Rosalind said, and her voice cracked on it, priestess-trained and steady and failing anyway. "Let me do this. I brokered the first bargain. I wrote your name in their ledger with my own hand—I was older than you, I knew better, and I chose to forget because forgetting was easier than carrying it. Let me carry it now. Take me instead. A soul is a soul; theirs won't care whose—"

"I said *no*."

The word came out of Oscar with ten years behind it, with every road she'd walked alone and every name she hadn't used and every night she'd lain awake in waystation lofts listening to her own heartbeat and wondering what was missing from it. The echo rolled down the under-court and came back changed, and the hollow gods stirred in their niches like moths against glass.

Rosalind stared at her. "Oscar—"

"That's not my name."

The silence that followed was total. Even the caged soul stopped singing.

Oscar looked at the god of debts, and then past it, up at the smooth blank face of the enthroned figure, and she understood suddenly—not with her mind, which had spent a decade making itself small and quick and forgettable, but with something older, something that lived in the marrow and in the memory of steel—that this was the last door. Behind her were the drowned mine and the long dark road and the refugees huddled under the mountain waiting for her to come back or not come back. Ahead was the throne and the ledger and the offered cage. And here, in the space between, was the one payment they had never asked for, because they had never imagined anyone would be fool enough to make it.

They wanted her to swear. They wanted words spoken to them, words of binding, words of debt. Every power beneath the mountain ran on words given freely—that was the whole architecture of it, the whole hunger. Souls sold, oaths sworn, prayers answered. Everything here was bought.

But her grandmother's stories had said something else, something the smugglers' roads had never quite let her forget, something she had kept folded up inside herself all these years like a candle kept cupped against the wind:

*A swordspeaker does not sell. A swordspeaker claims.*

Her sword was loose in its sheath at her hip. It had been whispering to her for days now, hungry, unprompted, and she had told it to wait, and it had waited, because blades are patient in a way people are not.

"Oscar," Rosalind said again, reaching for her arm. "Whatever you're thinking—"

"Keep them talking," Oscar said. "If this goes wrong, get out. Take the mine road. Don't look back."

"Oscar—"

"*Don't look back.*"

She drew the blade.

The sound of it rang through the under-court like a bell through a cathedral, and every hollow god turned its empty eyes toward her at once, and the god of debts leaned forward on its throne of closing ledgers with something that might, in a younger and less hungry thing, have been alarm.

"Child," it said. "There is nothing you can give us that we do not already own."

"You're right," Oscar said. "So I'm not giving you anything."

She lifted the sword until the point stood level with the blank face of the enthroned god, and she planted her feet the way her grandmother had planted hers, decades ago, in a kitchen full of steam and story, teaching a girl the words that girls were not supposed to learn anymore. And Oscar Joubert—who had been Thorn, and Magpie, and the Quiet Woman of the passes, who had spent ten years teaching the world not to see her—took the largest breath of her life.

And spoke.

"I am Rosamund Joubert of the Ashgrove line," she said, and her voice did not shake, though everything else in her did, "*daughter of Maren, granddaughter of Edith, last taught and first forgotten.*"

The words went out of her like heat out of an open forge, and where they passed, the darkness flinched.

"I was sold before I could speak, and I say now that the sale was theft. I was renamed by cowards and I say now that the renaming failed. I walked the quiet roads and I hid the gift and I hid the name, and I say now that hiding was survival, but survival was never the same as living, and I am done confusing the two."

The cage of shadows began to shudder. Inside it, the small voice had gone silent, listening.

"I claim my soul. Not bought. Not bartered. Not traded for oaths or blades or the names of cities. *Claimed.* By the only law older than your ledgers: that what is made of a person cannot be owned by anything that has never been one."

The god of debts rose from its throne, and it was vast, and it was furious, and it was afraid. "You dare unbind—"

"I am not finished." And gods help her, she wasn't. The words were coming up out of her now from somewhere below memory, below the locked door of her childhood, from the place where Rosalind's stolen ritual had sealed them away—and the blade in her hand was singing along, a low harmonic under her voice, steel remembering steel. "I am Rosamund Joubert. I am afraid of deep water and I love the smell of rain on hot stone. I count stairs without meaning to. I have never once in my life liked honey. My mother sang off-key and I would give ten years of silence to hear it again. These things are mine. This name is mine. This soul is mine, and I have come a very long way in the dark to collect what is owed to *me*."

She drove the point of the sword into the floor of the under-court.

"*By my name, I take myself back.*"

The world broke.

Not loudly—that was the terrible, beautiful thing about it. The under-court cracked the way ice cracks on a spring river, a single high note running outward from the blade through the black stone, through the niches, through the thrones, and the cage of shadows burst apart like a breath blown through smoke. The small singing shape fell—and did not fall, because Oscar caught it, caught it with both hands, caught it against her chest, and it poured into her the way water pours into dry ground, and she heard her own voice from inside her own body for the first time in twenty years, and she wept without any shame at all.

Then the other cages began to break.

All around the under-court, in niches and alcoves and vaults she had not seen in the dark, souls began scattering upward like startled birds—a thousand points of light streaming for the surface, homeward, each one trailing the faint sound of its own name. The hollow gods cried out after them, grasping, and their grasping hands closed on nothing, and one by one the old powers dimmed in their seats like lamps running out of oil. The god of debts stood alone amid the ruin of its ledger, pages fluttering loose into the air, every written name lifting off the page and flying free.

"You have unmade us," it said, and there was wonder in it, under the fury. Wonder, and something almost like relief.

"No," Oscar said. She pulled her blade from the stone. "You unmade yourselves, a long time ago. I just stopped paying rent."

The mountain groaned above them. Dust sifted down through the dark, and somewhere far overhead, stone was finding new shapes for itself, and old chains were coming loose.

Rosalind caught her arm as the first tremor shook the floor. Her eyes were wide, wet, blazing. "The exit—the mine road—it won't hold—"

"Then we run," Oscar said, and laughed, actually laughed, wild and cracked and alive, because her chest was *full*, because she could feel all of herself in there, every stolen year accounted for. "Come on, priestess. You owe me a very long explanation, and I intend to collect it in daylight."

They ran. Behind them the under-court collapsed inward on its empty thrones, and ahead the mine road climbed toward air and moonlight and the refugee column camped under the shoulder of the mountain—and ahead of that, Oscar knew with a certainty that lived in her bones alongside her reclaimed name, the usurper's army was waiting.

Let it wait. She had spent ten years learning how to walk unseen.

Now she intended to learn how to be seen.
