## 2. Down Beneath the Mountain

The ink was still wet when midnight came.

Odile had spent the hours between discovery and dark doing what she always did with unbearable things: she worked. She trimmed her pen. She squared the corners of the Ember Court map on her drafting table, weighting them with river stones she'd collected as a girl. She ate half a loaf of bread without tasting it and told herself, in the flat voice she used for impossible terrain, that prophecy books could be forged, that names repeated across centuries, that *Odile Kavanagh* was common enough in Hollowmere — there were three other Kavanaghs within a mile, all of them stubborn, all of them alive.

But the date. The date was tonight's, written down before her grandmother's grandmother had drawn her first breath, and no forgery explained that.

So she worked. And the map — her map, her beautiful, invented map of a court she had built from rumor and candlelight — sat on the table like an open mouth.

At eleven she lit every lamp in the cottage, which was a foolish thing to do and she did it anyway. At quarter past she took down her father's old knife from above the lintel and put it in her belt, though she could not have said what a knife would do against a sentence written a hundred years ago. At half past she stood at the window and looked out at Hollowmere lying quiet under frost, at the chimneys of her neighbors, at the valley walls that rose on every side like cupped hands, holding them all in.

*Magic cannot follow you here.* That was the first thing every child learned. Not *magic does not exist* — children found that out soon enough, from the books, from the peddlers' tales — but *magic cannot follow you here.* The valley was a held breath. A room with the door shut. Whatever burned and glittered and hungered beyond the mountains, it stopped at the ridgeline the way weather stops at glass.

She had built her whole life inside that glass. Careful distances. Maps of places she would never go, drawn so precisely that travelers sometimes wept over them, and Odile would smile and say *I have a good imagination,* and never say: *I have never left. I will never leave. The valley keeps me the way a jar keeps a firefly.*

At five minutes to midnight, the map began to glow.

Not the paper — the ink. Her own lines, the ones she had laid down with such loving patience: the torchlit colonnade, the vaulted hall, the stair descending into warmth. They brightened from sepia to gold to a color she had no name for, the color of the last coal before it goes gray, and the light moved, crawling along her linework like fire finding a wick.

"No," Odile said, to the map, to the book, to whoever had written her name. "No, I didn't agree to anything."

The wet ink of the prophecy, still open on the shelf behind her, answered with a smell — hot iron and beeswax and something older underneath, something like the inside of a mountain.

The clock in the square struck twelve. She heard it through the frost-thickened air, each bell arriving a half-beat after the last, and on the twelfth stroke the light in her map gathered itself into a single point at the heart of the great hall she'd drawn — the place where, in her invention, the Court kept its fire — and pulled.

There was no other word for it. She felt it in her sternum first, then her hands, then the marrow of her, a hook set behind her navel and hauled taut. She grabbed the table edge. The stones slid off the corners of the map. Her lamp went out — all her lamps went out at once, every flame in the cottage leaning toward the map like grass bending under wind, and she understood, in the last half-second of her ordinary life, what fuel was for.

Then the world folded, and Odile Kavanagh fell through her own drawing.

* * *

Falling through a map is not like falling through air. There is no wind. There is only the sensation of passing through layers — cold, then warm, then warm past bearing, then a heat that was not temperature but attention, the feeling of being *looked at* by something vast and patient. She tumbled through darkness shot with veins of ember-light, through stone that parted for her like water, and she was screaming, she was almost sure she was screaming, though the sound went nowhere, swallowed by the mountain's throat.

Then floor. Hard, warm flagstone under her palms, under her knees, the breath knocked out of her in one ugly grunt. Torchlight everywhere, orange and trembling. The smell of smoke and hot metal and, beneath it, faintly, unbearably sweet — like honey left too long near a flame.

Odile pushed herself up on shaking arms and made herself look.

She was standing in the great hall of the Ember Court. *Her* great hall — the one she had drawn. But where her ink had suggested grandeur, the reality was worse, because the reality was *alive*. The columns were not stone but something like cooled lava, black shot through with slow-moving red, and they pulsed. The ceiling arched away into darkness where shapes hung — chandeliers? cages? — dripping light instead of holding it. The floor was warm through her stockings. Everything was warm. The whole court breathed heat like a sleeping animal, and Odile realized with a lurch that this was why the valley bought its deadness: because *this* was what magic was. Not wonder. Warmth. A warmth that wanted things.

The hall was full of people.

They had turned to look at her — dozens of them, hundreds, courtiers in colors she had no names for, garments that shifted like coals breathing. Their faces were beautiful in the way of things grown rather than born, too symmetrical, too smooth, and their eyes caught the torchlight and held it, glowing faintly, each pair of eyes a small banked fire.

At the far end of the hall, on a dais she had never thought to draw, a figure lifted a long horn of black metal and sounded a note that Odile felt in her teeth.

The herald's voice filled the hall the way smoke fills a chimney.

"SOLD ONE," it announced, and the word rolled down the columns and came back doubled, tripled, whispered by mouths that had not opened. "THE SOLD ONE COMES TO CLAIM WHAT WAS PAID FOR. BEHOLD THE WARE ARRIVED AT ITS MARKET."

Silence. Then the court began, softly, to laugh — not cruelly, which would have been bearable, but *knowingly*, the way one laughs at a joke already understood, and Odile understood that they had been waiting for her the way one waits for a delivery. For goods.

Her terror crested — and broke, and what came through on the other side surprised her.

It was anger. Clean, cold, familiar anger, the same anger she'd felt at nine years old when a traveling merchant told her girls couldn't be cartographers, the same anger she'd felt at nineteen when the valley council refused her a travel pass for the fourth time. It was the anger of a woman who had spent her whole life being told where the edges of the world were by people who had never bothered to look.

"I'm not ware," she said. Her voice cracked on the first word and steadied on the second. "I'm not *ware*. I'm a person, and my name is Odile Kavanagh, and whatever someone sold —"

"IT WAS SOLD BEFORE YOU DREW BREATH," the herald said, gentle now, which was infinitely worse. "AND WHAT IS SOLD CANNOT UNSAY ITSELF. RUN, LITTLE LEDGER-ENTRY. THE ARCHIVES ARE THAT WAY. THEY ALWAYS ARE."

And the crowd — the laughing, glowing crowd — parted, opening a corridor toward a door she recognized from her own map: the archive door, the one she'd drawn with a little flourish of scrollwork because she'd imagined archivists as kind.

Odile ran. She hated herself for running and she ran anyway, because between defiance and a hundred burning eyes, even defiance knows to live another minute. Behind her the laughter followed like a tide going out, and ahead the door swung open onto darkness that smelled of dust and vellum and, impossibly, of rain — rain, in this furnace, the ghost of some other climate preserved in paper.

She plunged through, and the door shut behind her with the soft finality of a book closing.

* * *

The archives were endless.

Shelves rose into darkness on every side, taller than any building in Hollowmere, and the aisles between them wound like the streets of a city. Every surface was covered — ledgers, scrolls, folios chained to lecterns, loose pages drifting on some current of warm air like autumn leaves that had forgotten how to land. The rain-smell was stronger here. Odile walked until the sounds of the court faded, until her heartbeat slowed from hammering to merely frantic, and then she stopped in an aisle where the shelves were labeled in a script she almost, almost recognized, and pressed her back against the cold stone, and let herself shake.

"They announced you early." A voice, low and dry, from the shadows between two shelves. "I'd have given you another hour to get your bearings. The Steward likes them frightened. Frightened goods fetch better prices."

Odile spun, fumbling for her father's knife. "Who's there?"

A woman stepped out of the dark — or rather, resolved out of it, as if she had been part of the shadow and had simply decided otherwise. She was perhaps forty, perhaps four hundred; her face had the agelessness of someone who had stopped checking mirrors. Gray-streaked hair pinned up with what appeared to be actual pens. Robes of archivist's brown, patched at both elbows, ink-stained to the wrist. She carried a lantern of green glass and a stack of folios under one arm with the ease of long habit.

Her eyes — ordinary eyes, brown, unlit, human in a way nothing else in this place had been — traveled over Odile's face with an expression Odile couldn't read. Recognition, yes. But more than recognition. Something like grief. Something like guilt.

"You're taller than the portrait," the woman said.

"What portrait?" Odile's knife hand was steady. She was proud of that. "What portrait, who are you, and how do you know —"

"Rosalind. Senior archivist, formerly. Currently something less flattering." She set the folios down on a lectern with a soft thump. "And I know you because remembering is my function. Every soul that passes through this Court, I keep its history. Every sale, every trade, every lien and interest accrued. Four hundred years of ledgers, and I hold all of it." She paused, and her voice went quieter, and stranger. "Everyone's past but mine. I can tell you the name of the chimney-wraith's mother. I cannot tell you mine."

"That's not possible."

"Neither is a valley where magic can't follow," Rosalind said. "Yet here you are, from there, in here." She opened the topmost folio and turned it around.

The page showed a portrait — small, precise, rendered in the same sepia ink Odile used. A young woman with a strong jaw and her hair braided back for work. A drafting pen tucked behind one ear.

It was Odile. Drawn decades before her birth.

Below it, in a careful antique hand: *Kavanagh, Odile of Hollowmere. Soul sold in trust, winter solstice, due for collection.* And beneath that, a line that stopped Odile's breath:

*Sold by: M. Kavanagh.*

Her grandmother's initial. Her grandmother's name.

"She signed it the year your mother was born," Rosalind said gently. "The contract matures tonight. Which means, little cartographer, that you have six days to steal back the only thing in this Court that was ever truly yours — and I am the only one who knows where it's kept."

Odile stared at the page, at her own face wearing her own future, and felt the last careful distance of her life collapse like a bridge cut loose.

"Then draw me the way," she said. "Because I'm not leaving without it."

Something flickered across Rosalind's face — surprise, then a fierce, hungry approval, like a match catching.

"Oh," she said, closing the folio. "I think we're going to get along."
