## 3. The Archivist Who Forgot Herself

The girl collided with me at the mouth of the third gallery, all elbows and terror and the smell of cold mountain air still clinging to her coat, and I caught her by the shoulders before either of us went down among the ledgers.

Up close, she was not what the herald's cry had promised. "The sold one," they had called her, and the Court had turned its torchlit faces toward the doors like flowers toward a sun it did not have. But this was just a girl with ink-stained fingers and a smudge of graphite on her jaw, breathing hard, eyes wide as a creature run to ground.

"You're real," she said. It came out half accusation, half wonder.

"I'm generally accused of less flattering things," I said. "Come. The herald's criers will have your description in every corridor by now, and you do not want to be found standing in an open doorway."

She let me pull her into the stacks — down the narrow aisle where the memory-vellums are kept, the ones that whisper if you press your ear to them, the ones that made my position here possible and my self impossible. I lit no lamp. In the Ember Court, light is a courtesy you extend to others, and I had stopped extending courtesies some decades ago.

"Who are you?" she whispered.

"Rosalind. Archivist of the Third Gallery, Keeper of Patron Histories, formerly—" I paused, because the sentence always ended there, at *formerly*, like a stair that stops mid-air. "Formerly other things."

"You knew my name. When I ran past you, you said it. Odile." She said her own name as though testing whether it fit in this place. "How?"

"Because I filed it." I heard her breath catch. "Every soul that enters this Court passes through the ledger, and every ledger entry passes through my hands. Yours has been waiting for you for a very long time."

* * *

I took her deeper, to the reading alcove behind the map-cabinets, the one place in the Court where the torchlight from the great hall doesn't reach and even the wraiths forget to look. She sat on the floor because there was only one chair and she seemed the sort of person who would rather fold herself small than take what wasn't offered. I noted that. I note everything about everyone; it is the one compensation of my condition.

"Now," I said, "you will tell me how a magic-dead cartographer from Hollowmere walked through a door that hasn't opened in a hundred years."

"A book pulled me. A prophecy. My name was in it — written before I was born, and the ink was still wet."

"Ah." Of course it was. The old prophecies are the Court's cruelest documents, contracts dressed as fate. "And did the book tell you why you were summoned?"

"It told me I was sold." Her chin came up when she said it, defiance arriving like a second heartbeat. "By whom? Sold to whom? For what?"

Such questions. I almost envied her the asking. There was a time — I am told there was a time — when I asked questions like that, before I learned that in the Ember Court every answer has a price and most prices are paid in the currency you can least afford.

"Let me explain how things work here," I said, "and then we will discuss what you owe and what you're owed, which are not the same thing, whatever the Steward's clerks tell you."

I drew a circle in the dust on the floor between us.

"The Ember Court runs on souls. Not metaphorically — literally. A soul is a coin that never tarnishes, never devalues, and can be spent exactly once. The Court mints nothing. It only trades. When a mortal above makes a bargain — for safety, for prosperity, for a child's life, for rain on a dying field — the price is extracted below, and the soul comes here, and it is weighed, and it is entered in the ledger, and it sits in glass until someone spends it."

"On what?"

"Anything. Long life. Power. The undoing of a rival. Most patrons buy small mercies — a harvest, a recovery, one more winter with a dying wife. The Court calls itself the Court of Small Mercies behind closed doors. It is not a kind name. It is an accounting term."

Her hands had gone still in her lap. Cartographer's hands. Hands accustomed to holding still over fine work.

"And yours?" she asked. "You said *formerly*. You said *once*. You talk about souls like someone who's handled them, but you also talk like someone who—"

"Like someone missing one." I smiled, and felt my face make the shape of it without quite remembering what smiling was supposed to feel like. "Yes. You're perceptive. That will be useful or fatal, depending."

I should explain, since she would learn it soon enough anyway. In this Court, memory is the tax levied on those who handle souls too long. An archivist must remember every transaction perfectly — every patron, every price, every clause and codicil — or the ledgers fail and the Court's whole economy collapses into dispute. So the Court takes our payment in advance. It takes us. My own history was the fee for my office: sold off in fragments across a century of clerical service until nothing remained but the work itself.

I remember everything. That is the joke the Court tells about me, though no one laughs where I can hear. I remember the patron who bought a son back from fever in 1811 and paid with his brother's soul. I remember the widow who traded her memories of her husband one by one, thinking she was buying him more years, until she could no longer recall why she'd wanted him alive. I remember the founding bargain of Hollowmere — oh, yes, I know that one intimately, though I did not yet know it concerned the girl sitting in my dust-circle — signed by a woman named Kavanagh, three generations back, the ink pressed deep enough to scar the page.

I remember every patron's face, every seller's trembling signature, every buyer's satisfaction.

I do not remember my own face. There is no mirror in the Third Gallery — I removed them all, or perhaps I never allowed them; I don't remember, which is rather the point. When I pass the polished obsidian of the vault doors, I see a shape, a grey-robed figure, a woman of indeterminate years with careful hands. That is all. I am the Court's memory and I have none of my own. Every morning I wake in my cell above the archives and I know the entire history of everyone who ever bargained here, and I do not know my mother's name, or whether I had a mother, or whether I chose this post or was chosen for it, or whether *Rosalind* is even the name I was born with or merely the name the ledger assigned me, the way one labels a drawer.

"They call me the archivist who forgot herself," I told Odile. "They think it's wit. It's inventory."

* * *

For a long moment she said nothing. Then, quietly, in the voice of someone offering something fragile:

"That's the loneliest thing I've ever heard."

"Do not pity me. Pity is a currency here too, and I won't be bought with it."

"I'm not pitying you. I'm—" She stopped. Started again. "I draw maps. That's what I do. Places no one can reach, realms that exist only in travelers' lies and sailors' fears. I've spent my whole life mapping places I'll never stand in. And you—" she gestured at the shelves around us, at the whispering vellums, at me, "—you're a person no one can reach. Even you can't reach you."

I should have sent her away then. I should have told her the truth every archivist knows: that the sold ones never win, that the prophecies are not promises but traps, that the solstice seals what the solstice seals and the Steward has never lost a negotiation in four hundred years. I should have protected my position, my galleries, my careful grey existence.

Instead I heard myself say: "What are you proposing?"

"Help me get my soul back. Before the solstice. You know these halls, the ledgers, the guards' rotations, the vault. I can't do it alone and I have maybe nine days."

"And what do I get?" I kept my voice dry. "You have nothing to trade. Your soul is already collateral. What could you possibly offer an archivist of the Ember Court?"

She looked at me then — really looked, the way she must look at blank parchment before committing the first line — and said:

"A map."

"A map."

"Of who you used to be." She leaned forward. "Your memories weren't destroyed, were they? They were *sold*. Which means they're in the ledger. Filed. Somewhere in these galleries there's a record of every fragment they took from you — who bought it, when, what it contained. If anyone could reconstruct you from the paper trail, it's me. I'll draw it. All of it. Your history, your face, your name before *Rosalind* if it exists. A map of who you used to be, so you can find your way back."

The dust-circle between us seemed suddenly very loud.

No one had offered me anything in a century. The Court does not give; it trades, and I had nothing left to trade, and so I had simply endured, the way furniture endures. And now this frightened girl with ink on her jaw sat in my alcove offering me the one thing the Ember Court had convinced me was beyond price: myself, returned.

It was hunger, I realized. All these years I had called it diligence, devotion to the archive, love of the work. It was hunger. I had been starving in a room full of food, cataloguing everyone else's meals.

"The records are sealed," I said. My voice came out rougher than I intended. "Patron purchases are confidential. Accessing them requires the Steward's seal."

"Then we'll need to get past the Steward."

"*We.*" I tasted the word. It had been a long time since I'd been part of a *we*. "You understand that if we're caught, they won't merely punish you. They'll unmake me. Whatever fragments of Rosalind remain will be entered as payment, and there will be nothing left to map."

"Then we shouldn't get caught." She said it simply, the way she might say *then we go around the mountain*. "Do we have an agreement?"

I looked at her hand, extended across the dust-circle, ink-stained and steady.

I thought of the vellum in the restricted case — the Hollowmere contract, the Kavanagh signature, the clause I had read ten thousand times and never understood until tonight: *one soul per generation, in perpetuity, against the keeping-out of magic.* I thought of the entry I had filed myself, decades ago, a newborn's soul entered under the name Kavanagh, and how I had wondered, idly, professionally, what became of such entries. Now I knew. She had happened. She had grown up in a valley that ate her future and called it peace.

She deserved better than a warning. Perhaps she deserved the truth. And perhaps — the thought arrived like a struck match — helping her burn her way out of this Court was the closest I would ever come to burning my way out of my own.

"We have an agreement," I said, and shook her hand.

Her grip was warm. I memorized it. It was the first thing in a hundred years I chose to remember.

Then I stood, and brushed the dust from my robes, and led her out of the alcove toward the restricted cases, where the Hollowmere contract waited with its scarred page — and where, I now admitted to myself, we would find the first name on the list of everyone who had ever bought a piece of me.

The first purchase had been made the night I took this office. The buyer's name was the Steward's own.
