## 4. A Ledger of Souls

**Odile**

The forgery took Rosalind three hours and one stolen seal.

"An auditor's writ," she said, pressing warm wax against the parchment, "is the only document in the Court that opens more doors than it closes. Everyone fears an audit. Even the Steward." She blew across the seal — a coiled serpent biting its own tail — and the wax cooled the color of dried blood. "You are now Magister Vellum of the Outer Reaches, come to reconcile the Court's accounts before solstice."

"I can't even write a convincing hand here," Odile said. "Your script has flourishes like frost ferns."

"Then don't write. *Map*." Rosalind tapped the ledger hall's location on Odile's own sketch. "You told me you charted the Sunken Reaches of Ael by candlelight, from rumors alone. This is easier. The ledger hall is real, and you'll be standing in it."

That was the thing about Rosalind: she remembered everything except herself, and she spent her memory like a miser spending gold — carefully, precisely, always with a purpose Odile couldn't yet see.

The auditor's writ got them past the first door. It got them past the second. At the third door, a guard with lanterns for eyes leaned down until his flame-light washed over Odile's face, and she held her breath and thought of contour lines, of elevation marks, of anything steady and measurable, until he waved them through with a grunt that smelled of tallow.

The ledger hall opened around her like the inside of a bell.

Shelves rose into darkness she couldn't find the top of, and every shelf was packed with ledgers — thousands upon thousands of them, bound in leather gone soft as moth wing, their spines labeled in ink that shimmered faintly, as if the words were still deciding what they meant. The air tasted of iron filings and old smoke. Somewhere above, pages turned on their own, a sound like distant rain.

Odile unrolled her blank vellum on a reading table and began to work the way she worked everywhere: quietly, hungrily, with a pencil worn to a stub. Doorways. Shelf rows. The great iron brazier at the hall's center where a clerk fed rejected contracts to a fire that burned green. The stair down to the vault levels, guarded by nothing but a rope of knotted hair.

And the ledgers themselves. She pulled the nearest one open and understood, with a lurch, why the Court called them accounts.

Each page held a name. Beneath each name, a small pressed circle of light — a soul's impression left in the paper like a flower left in a book, faded but unmistakably *there*. Beside it, entries in columns: *acquired*, *appraised*, *held*, *spent*.

Spent. Souls had been spent here the way Hollowmere spent lamp oil.

She found the valley within twenty minutes. She hadn't expected to. She'd told herself the ledgers would be too vast, too old, that Hollowmere was too small and too far to matter to a place like this. But there it was, in a ledger bound in grey leather gone green at the corners: **HOLLOWMERE — PROTECTION CONTRACT, RENEWED PER GENERATION.**

Her pencil stopped moving.

The entries went back two hundred years. Every fifty years or so, a new line in the same careful hand: *one soul received; ward renewed.* And beside each entry, a name. Not the children's names. The *sellers'* names.

Maeve Kavanagh. 1897.

Odile read it four times. Her grandmother's name sat in the column as plainly as a mark on a map, in ink that had dried a century before Odile drew her first coastline. Her hands were very still on the page. Stillness was what she did instead of screaming; she had learned it young, mapping places no one could follow her to.

Below Maeve Kavanagh's name, in smaller script, the appraiser's note:

*Seller states intent: cessation of all magical ingress to Hollowmere vale, in perpetuity, price of one (1) unborn descendant's soul. Ward to be sealed at subject's majority eve. Subject: O. Kavanagh.*

There it was. Not a prophecy. An invoice.

**Rosalind**

Rosalind had distracted Stewards before. It was, in a sense, what she had been made for — though *made* implied someone had bothered to make her, and all she knew for certain was that she had woken in the archives forty years ago with other people's histories stacked inside her head like cordwood and not one splinter belonging to herself.

The Steward of Accounts kept a office above the ledger hall, and the Steward kept rituals, and rituals could be relied upon the way one relied on gravity. At the ninth hour of the evening watch, the Steward took tea brewed from burnt contracts — bitter enough to strip paint — and while drinking it, the Steward liked to be flattered about the orderliness of the Court's books.

Rosalind knocked, entered, curtsied at exactly the depth that said *I am beneath your notice but know my place*, and said: "Steward. Forgive the interruption. I've found a discrepancy in the 1841 transfers, and I thought — who better than you to resolve it?"

The Steward looked up. Its face was long and grey and patient as a filing cabinet, and its eyes were the color of wet ash. "A discrepancy," it repeated, in the tone of a man being offered a gift.

"Two souls entered under the same registry number. One of them is still drawing interest." Rosalind laid the forged transfer slip on the desk — she had prepared it that morning, because a good distraction required paperwork, and paperwork was the one language every bureaucrat in every world spoke fluently.

The Steward was already reaching for its keys.

For the next hour, Rosalind listened. That was the whole art of it: listen while the powerful explain themselves. The Steward explained the registry system. The Steward explained the interest structure. The Steward explained, twice, how *no one else* could have caught such an error, and wasn't it remarkable that an archivist of the lower stacks had the eye for it. Rosalind agreed that it was remarkable. She asked questions. She laughed at the right moments, a small dry sound she'd practiced against the echo of empty archive corridors.

And underneath the listening, she thought about Odile.

She thought about the way the cartographer had looked at the Court's torches on her first night — not with wonder, the way most surface-folk did, but with the flat professional assessment of a woman measuring light sources. She thought about how Odile slept curled against walls, facing the door, a habit Rosalind recognized because she shared it. Two creatures built by hard places, recognizing the architecture in each other.

She had named her price without shame: *draw me a map of who I used to be.* It was the only currency she had left to want. But somewhere between the forged writ and this hour of ash-eyed flattery, the wanting had shifted shape. Now she wanted Odile to get out. Now she wanted the map to be drawn in a free hand.

It was an inconvenient development. Rosalind filed it away to examine later, the way she filed everything.

The Steward finally closed the registry book, satisfied, and Rosalind murmured something about returning the corrected slip to the lower stacks, and curtsied her way out.

* * *

**Odile**

Rosalind found her still standing over the grey ledger, so still that a clerk had draped a dust cloth over her shoulder, mistaking her for furniture.

"You found it," Rosalind said softly.

"My grandmother sold me." The words came out level, which surprised her. "Before I was born. Before my mother was even — " She stopped. Recalculated. "Fifty years. The contract renews every fifty years. There was a girl before me. In 1847. A girl named Sorcha Byrne. And one before her." She turned the ledger toward Rosalind, and her finger traced the column of sellers' names, generation after generation, a lineage of people who had paid for their valley's safety with someone else's child. "They weren't protecting us from magic. They were paying *rent* on its absence. My whole life, the valley taught us magic was poison, that outside was death, that the wards were a blessing. It was a mortgage, Rosalind. And I'm the payment."

Rosalind said nothing. She was good at saying nothing; it was half her profession. She simply stood beside Odile in the vast rustling dark of the ledger hall, close enough that their shoulders touched, and let the silence hold what it needed to hold.

"The worst part," Odile said finally, "is that I understand it. That's the worst part. I've seen the famine years in the old records. I know what the valley was running from. If someone had put a contract in front of *me* — one child, unnamed, unborn, against everyone I loved — " Her voice cracked along a fault line she hadn't mapped. "I would have signed. God help me, I would have signed it too."

"That's how these things survive," Rosalind said. "Not through monsters. Through reasonable people in impossible arithmetic." She touched the ledger page, just below Maeve Kavanagh's name. "Your grandmother didn't sell you out of cruelty. She sold you the way you sell anything when the alternative is everyone starving. It doesn't forgive it. But it explains the shape of the wound."

"The shape of the wound," Odile repeated, and almost laughed. "You talk like a map legend."

"I contain multitudes of other people's phrases. It's one of my features." But her eyes, when Odile met them, were grave. "There's more. Look at the bottom of the entry."

Odile looked. Below the appraiser's note, stamped in wax the same blood-color as the forged seal upstairs, was a final line she had missed:

*Ward expires at subject's retrieval or at winter solstice, whichever comes first. Renewal negotiable with Steward of Accounts.*

"Renewal negotiable," Odile read aloud. Her mouth had gone dry. "They're going to offer me a trade. My soul back, if I sign the next girl's name into this book."

"Now you see the machinery entire," Rosalind said quietly. "The Court doesn't keep souls in glass for storage. It keeps them as *leverage*. Yours isn't treasure, Odile. It's bait on a hook, and the hook is shaped exactly like your own conscience."

Outside, far above, the mountain groaned — a deep tectonic sigh that shivered dust from the shelves. Somewhere in the rafters, pages turned faster, as if the ledgers themselves were growing excited.

Odile closed the grey book gently, the way she closed a map she intended to steal.

"Solstice is nine days away," she said. "Show me where they keep the glass."

Rosalind hesitated — a fractional pause, there and gone, like a flaw in otherwise perfect vellum. Then: "There's something you should know about the vault first. About what it costs to get in." She glanced up at the turning pages, at the listening dark. "Not tonight. Tonight we need allies, and I know exactly two misfits stupid enough to help us."

"Tell me they're armed."

"Better. One of them is a chimney-wraith, and the other makes candles that remember being prayed over." Rosalind allowed herself the ghost of a smile. "In the Ember Court, Odile, that's an army."
