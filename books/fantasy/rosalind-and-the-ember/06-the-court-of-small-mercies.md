## 6. The Court of Small Mercies

There is a particular silence in the Ember Court that I have never trusted. It is not the silence of peace — it is the silence of a held breath, the hush of ten thousand souls in glass jars all listening at once. Tonight it pressed against the archive windows like fog against glass, and Odile stood at my table with her ink-stained fingers curled around a stolen ledger page, and she said, "We can't do this alone."

She was right, of course. She had been right since the moment she fell through her own map and into my arms, all elbows and fury and terror barely leashed. The vault beneath the Steward's hall was warded three ways: by flame, by oath, and by toll. No archivist's keys would open it. No forged seal would pass it. It wanted things from us that we could not give alone.

"We need Brannoc," I said.

Odile frowned. "The one who lives in the walls?"

"The one who *is* the walls," I corrected. "Or the chimneys, rather. Brannoc was a hearth-keeper once, back when the Court still had hearths worth keeping. When the Steward consolidated the flames — when everything warm had to be owned or extinguished — Brannoc refused to sign the register. So they burned his name out of the ledgers and left him with nothing but smoke." I tapped the page in her hand. "But smoke goes where doors don't. And he owes me."

"Everyone here owes you something," Odile said. There was no accusation in it, only wonder, as if she were still cataloguing me — the disgraced archivist whom every patron remembered and who could not remember herself.

"Not everyone," I said. "Some just owe the Court. Those are the ones we need."

* * *

We found Brannoc where I knew he would be: in the flue above the Hall of Small Mercies, coiled in the soot-dark like a question mark made of ash. He did not so much appear as *condense* — first a shimmer of heat-haze, then two eyes like banked coals, then the long grey drift of him, unfolding downward until he filled the chimney throat from brick to brick.

"You bring a stranger to my smoke," he said. His voice was the sound of a draft through a keyhole, thin and whistling. "Bold, little archivist. Or desperate."

"Both," I admitted. "Brannoc, this is Odile Kavanagh. The sold one."

The coal-eyes narrowed. Somewhere far below, a bell tolled the third hour, and the sound climbed the flues like water climbing a straw.

"I know what she is," Brannoc said. "The whole Court knows. The heralds sang it through every vent for a week. *The sold one walks among us; the solstice comes; the ember waits.*" A tendril of him curled toward Odile, testing the air near her face, and she — to her credit — did not flinch. Only her jaw tightened, which I was beginning to understand was how she flinched. "You smell like outside," he told her. "Like rain on stone that never learned magic. Like a valley that paid for its quiet."

"Then you know why I'm here," Odile said.

"I know why you're here. I don't know why *I* should care." He drifted back, gathering himself into the dark. "I stopped caring about the Court's business a century ago. Caring is how they get their hooks in you."

"They already have their hooks in you," I said quietly. "They burned your name, Brannoc. You can't be unmade twice. But you *can* choose what you are instead." I stepped closer to the chimney mouth, soot gritting under my boots. "Help us reach the vault, and when the contracts unravel — if they unravel — your name goes back into the book of the living. Not theirs. Yours. Written by your own hand."

The silence stretched. In it I heard the small sounds of the Court settling around us: the tick of cooling pipes, the distant murmur of patrons counting their jars, the whisper of souls in glass turning over in their sleep.

"You ask a ghost to gamble on fire," Brannoc said at last.

"I ask a man to gamble on being a man again."

A long exhalation of warm ash moved through the flue, almost like laughter. Almost like grief.

"Solstice Eve," he said. "The eastern flues run hot before the vigil. Bring me nothing but your nerve. And, little archivist—" the coal-eyes fixed on me, "—bring wine. If I'm to die a second time, I'll do it drunk."

* * *

The wine required stealing, and the stealing required the candlemaker.

Her name was Wren Halloway, though the Court had struck even that from its rolls; now she was simply *the exile of the Tallow Row*, the woman who made candles that remembered light instead of selling it. Her shop sat crooked at the end of a lane where the torchlight thinned, its windows glowing with a soft amber warmth that no other window in the Court dared imitate. Patrons crossed the street to avoid passing it. Warmth, freely given, offended them.

"She won't see us," I warned Odile as we approached. "Wren doesn't deal in favors. She deals in trades. Everything costs, with her — but fairly. That's more than the Steward ever managed."

"What does she trade in?"

"Light. Memory. Small kindnesses banked against hard winters." I hesitated at the door, my hand on the latch. "And grudges. She has a magnificent collection of grudges."

Inside, the shop smelled of beeswax and cedar smoke, and every surface glittered with candles in every stage of becoming — tapers white as bone, pillars swirled with color like weather seen from above, votives no bigger than thimbles. Wren herself stood at the workbench, a small woman with flour-white hands and eyes the color of struck matches, and she did not look up when we entered.

"Archivist," she said. "You smell like the archives and bad decisions. And you—" now she looked up, at Odile, and her match-strike gaze went strange, "—you smell like a debt coming due."

"We need your help," I said. "And the Steward's cellar wine."

That got her attention. She set down her wick-knife slowly. "The *cellar* wine. The vintages aged over soul-smoke. You want me to help you rob the Steward of his *wine*."

"Among other things."

Wren laughed — a short, sharp sound like a candle catching. "Oh, I like her," she said, jerking her chin at Odile. "The sold one, come to rob the buyer. That's either very brave or very stupid, and in my experience the two wear the same coat." She wiped her hands on her apron, leaving pale streaks. "Why should I burn my fingers on your scheme?"

"Because you remember what it was to make light for people who needed it," I said. "Before the Court decided light was property. Because every candle in this shop is a small rebellion, and you've been burning alone for sixty years." I leaned across the workbench, close enough to see the fine wax scars on her knuckles. "Come with us, and you won't be alone anymore. Whatever happens."

Something flickered behind her eyes — the same thing, I suspected, that flickered behind mine whenever anyone spoke of *before*, the shape of a memory I could not reach. Wren turned away, busied her hands with a taper that needed no tending.

"Cellar wine," she said finally. "For four. And if we're caught, I never met either of you, and I will say so loudly and at length."

"That's the spirit," said Odile, and for the first time since she'd fallen into my life, she smiled.

* * *

We drank the stolen wine that night in the flue above the Hall of Small Mercies, because Brannoc refused to meet anywhere with a floor.

It was Wren who carried the bottles up, wrapped in wax-cloth against the cold, and Brannoc who conjured — if that is the word — a table of hardened soot and heat-shimmer where we could set them. We sat in a circle of dim orange glow, four misfits in the lungs of a palace built on bought breath, and passed the bottles hand to hand.

The wine tasted of summers none of us had lived. It was sweet and smoky both, and underneath the sweetness ran a faint wrongness — the soul-smoke it had been aged over, I supposed, the residue of someone's joy steeped into the vintage. Odile took one sip and grimaced.

"It tastes like being watched."

"That's the vintage," Wren said cheerfully, refilling her cup. "The '31 was aged over a poet's soul. Very sentimental. The Steward drinks it when he wants to feel things without the inconvenience of having any."

Brannoc drifted low over his cup, letting the steam of it curl through him. Wine, I realized, was the closest he came to eating — flavor moving through smoke, sensation without substance. His coal-eyes had softened to a gentler orange, banked-fire warm.

"A toast," he rasped. "To what, I wonder? To failure?"

"To refusal," Odile said.

We looked at her. She turned her cup slowly in her ink-stained fingers, watching the wine move.

"My whole life, Hollowmere taught me that safety was a thing you purchased with someone else. I didn't know that's what it was — I thought the valley was just *quiet*. But it was quiet the way a house is quiet after a bargain in the basement." She lifted her cup. "My grandmother sold me before I was born, and everyone I ever loved drank that wine without knowing what was in it. So — to refusal. To every person in this room who was told they belonged to something, and said no."

"To refusal," Wren echoed, and her match-strike eyes had gone bright.

"To names not yet written," said Brannoc, and drank his smoke.

I raised my cup last, and found, to my surprise, that my throat was tight. I did not remember my mother. I did not remember my own face. I had spent a century being the Court's memory while possessing none of my own, cataloguing other people's joys in a ledger I could not read. And yet here, in a soot-choked flue, with a wraith and an exile and a girl whose soul sat locked in a vault below us, I felt something I had no word for in any of the eleven languages the archives kept.

Belonging, perhaps. Or the rumor of it.

"To us," I said. "Whoever we turn out to be."

We drank. Below us, the Court murmured and counted and slept, unaware that four small rebellions had just joined hands above its head.

* * *

It was past the fourth bell when Wren spread her hands across the soot-table and began, in the manner of a general, to plan.

"The eastern flues converge on the vault corridor here—" her finger traced a line in the ash, "—but they're watched on solstice eve. Every vent in the east wing gets a warden. Except." She smiled, sharp as a wick-trimmed flame. "Except the old candle-flue. My flue. Sealed sixty years ago when they exiled me, and too narrow for anything but smoke and memory. Brannoc can carry you through it."

"And the toll?" Odile asked quietly. "The guardian at the vault door demands a memory. Everyone says so."

The circle went still. The wine-sweetness seemed to curdle in the air.

"Yes," Brannoc said softly. "It takes a memory. Freely given, or it takes one by force — and the ones it takes by force are always the ones you'd least afford to lose."

All eyes came to me, and I understood why. What memories did I have to lose? A century of other people's pasts, and none of my own. I opened my mouth to volunteer — to offer up some patron's wedding day, some stranger's childhood summer, cheap currency I carried by the shelf-load —

"No," Odile said, and her hand closed over mine on the soot-table, ink-stained fingers lacing through mine with a fierceness that startled us both. "Not hers. She's given enough to this place."

"Odile—"

"*No.*" Her voice cracked like a whip, and beneath the crack ran something rawer. "You don't get to pay with the last of yourself. Not for me. Not for anyone. That's the whole point — that's what we're *refusing*."

The words hung in the smoky dark between us, and I felt them land somewhere deeper than argument reaches. Wren looked away, suddenly busy with her cup. Brannoc's coals dimmed, thoughtful.

"Then we'd best hope," Wren said at last, gently, "that whatever the guardian asks of whoever stands before it — it asks kindly."

Above us, through the soot and stone, the first cold wind of the solstice began to move against the mountain, and somewhere far below, in a vault of glass and iron, a small ember waited in the dark, burning with a patience older than any of our names.
