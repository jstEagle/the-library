## 7. Solstice Eve

*Odile*

The Court did not have a night, only a dimming — the torches along the great halls burning lower as the solstice approached, as if the whole mountain were holding its breath. Odile felt it in her teeth. Somewhere above them, past leagues of stone and older bargains, the sun was setting on the last day before her soul was sealed forever.

She crouched with Rosalind and Brannoc in the chimney-wraith's domain: a flue-mouth wide as a cathedral door, breathing warm ash-scented air. Soot lay over everything like black snow. The candlemaker — Wren, who had refused to answer to any other name since they'd shared wine in his exile-workshop — had pressed a stub of grey tallow into each of their palms before they left. *Burn it if you're lost,* he'd said. *The flame will find me before the Steward does.*

"Rules," Brannoc rumbled. He was less a man than a suggestion of one, a tall shape of smoke given shoulders and a voice like a banked fire settling. "You do not speak inside the flues. You do not touch the walls. And you do not," he said, turning the coals of his eyes on Odile, "look down."

"Why not?" Odile asked.

"Because the flues go everywhere, little cartographer. Past the vaults. Past the foundries where souls are smelted into coin. Past places even I don't map." He smiled, and it was a terrible, kind thing. "And you are a mapper. It's in your hands. They itch when there's unmapped ground under you. I've watched you all evening."

Odile curled her fingers closed. He was right. Her hands had been itching since she arrived beneath the mountain, aching to chart corridors that shifted like weather, staircases that forgot themselves halfway down. She had spent her whole life drawing realms she could never reach. Now she stood inside one, and every instinct screamed at her to make it knowable.

Rosalind touched her elbow. "Ready?"

"No," Odile said honestly.

"Good. Ready people hesitate because they think they've earned their safety. You'll move faster scared." Rosalind's face was pale in the flue-light, her archivist's composure stretched thin over something rawer. "Brannoc. Take us up."

The wraith gathered them the way wind gathers leaves — one moment Odile was standing, the next she was pressed against soot-warm stone, rising through the mountain's throat. The flue narrowed and widened around them like a gullet. Heat came in waves, then cold, then a heat that smelled of iron and something sweeter, something that made her think unbidden of her grandmother's kitchen, of honey left too long on the stove.

*Don't look down. Don't look down.*

She looked down.

Far below, through a grate of black iron, she saw them: rows upon rows of glass vessels, each holding a light. Some burned steady gold. Some guttered. One, near the edge of her vision, flickered in a rhythm she knew — the exact cadence of her own heartbeat, which had gone fast and strange ever since the prophecy pulled her under.

Her soul. Down there. A small ember in glass, waiting for midnight tomorrow to be sealed into the Steward's ledger like a coin pressed into wax.

The flue lurched. Brannoc's smoke-hand clamped over her eyes, gentle as a falling curtain.

"I said don't look down."

"I saw it," she whispered. "I saw mine."

"Then hold onto that," he said, "and not the looking."

* * *

*Rosalind*

The flues opened onto a gallery Rosalind knew from a hundred years of cataloguing its contents and could not remember living: the Vault Approach, where the Court kept the things too valuable to burn and too dangerous to spend. Black marble underfoot. Niches cut into the walls, each holding an artifact behind glass — a crown that wept rust, a mirror full of fog, a door that led nowhere and knocked anyway.

And at the end of the gallery, between them and the vault proper, sat the guardian.

It had been a hound once, perhaps, or a person; now it was both and neither, a long low shape of shadow stitched with candleflames where its fur should have been. Each flame was a memory. Rosalind knew this the way she knew everything in the archives — completely, uselessly, without knowing how she came to know it.

"It takes a toll," she murmured. "One memory per soul that passes. It doesn't matter whose. It pulls them out whole, like a thread from cloth, and adds the flame to itself."

"Which memory?" Odile breathed.

"It chooses. That's the cruelty of it. You can't offer up something you want to lose. It takes what it wants."

The guardian raised its head. Its flames leaned toward them like grass toward wind.

"You first," Brannoc said quietly. "Then the girl. I'll pass last — I'm mostly smoke already. There's little left for it to take."

Rosalind stepped forward before fear could argue with her.

Here was the truth she had not told Odile, not fully: she had been paying tolls her entire existence in this Court. Every contract signed, every favor traded, every year survived had cost her a piece of her own past. That was how the disgraced were made here — not exiled but eroded, until nothing remained but function. She remembered every patron, every ledger line, every soul that had passed through the Ember Court's economy. She remembered everyone's history but her own. Her face was a stranger's in the polished obsidian. Her name — *Rosalind*, the archives said, though she couldn't recall anyone giving it to her — was the last furniture in an empty house.

So when the guardian's flames bent toward her, sifting, searching, she found she wasn't afraid.

There was very little left to steal. And whatever it took, she would not feel the absence. That was the one mercy of being hollowed out: you could give freely, because you had already lost the ability to count.

The guardian circled her once, twice. Its flames whispered. Then one of them — a small blue flame near its throat, a memory of some other victim, some other century — detached and drifted toward her instead, hovering before her lips like a moth.

*An exchange,* the guardian seemed to say, in a voice like paper turning pages. *You have so little. Take this back. Give me something newer.*

"What is it?" Odile asked sharply, from behind.

"A memory," Rosalind said slowly. "Someone else's. It's offering me a trade — a borrowed past for a piece of my present."

"Don't," Odile said. "Rosalind, you don't know what it—"

"That's rather the point." Rosalind almost smiled. "I don't know anything about myself. Whatever it takes, I won't miss it. But a memory of someone else — that's a map, Odile. Of who I might have been."

She opened her mouth, and let the guardian choose.

It chose quickly, decisively, the way a surgeon cuts. A cold thread drew out of her — she felt it leave her chest, painless, a held breath released — and she watched it curl into the guardian's flames and vanish. In its place, the small blue memory slid home behind her ribs.

And suddenly Rosalind was standing in a garden she had never seen, in a body she had never worn, younger, laughing, calling across the grass to someone whose name rang in her skull like a struck bell: *Maren. Maren, come see — the swans are dancing—*

The vision folded shut. She staggered. Odile caught her arm.

"What did you give it?"

"I don't know." Rosalind steadied herself. Something was missing — she could feel the hole where it had been, smooth-edged, already healing over with nothing. "That's the trick of it. I can't mourn what I can't name." She looked up, and her eyes were wet, which surprised her more than the loss. "But I received more than I paid. Someone loved me once. Her name was Maren. I danced with swans, or near enough." She laughed, unsteady. "That's more biography than I've owned in a century."

Odile stared at her. "You gave away a piece of yourself and you're *grateful*."

"I'm an archivist, Odile. I hoard. Even grief. Especially grief." She turned to the guardian, which had settled back onto its haunches, one flame richer. "Thank you," she told it formally, absurdly, and walked past.

* * *

*Odile*

The vault door was not locked. That was the worst part.

It stood open at the end of the gallery — a slab of black iron carved with the Court's sigil, a flame within a hand — and beyond it the room breathed cold and golden. Odile crossed the threshold with Rosalind's hand tight in hers and stopped.

Shelves. Miles of shelves, spiraling down into dark, and on every shelf, glass.

Souls. Thousands of them. Each vessel no larger than a lantern, each holding a light that pulsed with the rhythm of the life it had been torn from. Some burned white-hot with rage. Some were nearly out, thin blue threads, the souls of people so old or so broken they'd forgotten they were missing anything. Labels hung from each in copper script: names, dates, prices paid, prices owed.

"There," Rosalind whispered.

Third shelf, second row, marked with a fresh copper tag still bright from the engraver's tool:

*Kavanagh, O. — Hollowmere line. Contract 881. Seals at solstice close.*

Odile lifted the glass from its cradle with both hands, the way you lift a sleeping child, the way you lift the last intact thing in a ruined house. Inside, the ember brightened. Warmth spread up her arms, into her chest, into the hollow behind her sternum she had carried all her life without knowing its name — the flatness, the grayness, the way magic slid off her valley and off her skin like rain off oiled cloth.

*Oh,* she thought. *This is what I was supposed to feel like.*

The ember pulsed. Once. Twice. In time with her heart.

"It knows you," Rosalind said softly.

"It's *me*." Odile's voice cracked. She had mapped coastlines she'd never sailed and mountains she'd never climbed, and none of it — none of it — had prepared her for the weight of a soul the size of a candleflame. "All this time I thought I was careful. Cautious. Cold, Wren says. But I was just — running on less. Everyone in Hollowmere runs on less. We thought it was our nature."

"It was your price," Rosalind said.

Behind them, at the vault door, iron groaned.

Odile turned. The guardian stood in the doorway no longer — in its place, filling the frame, was something far worse: a figure in gray robes with a ledger chained to its wrist, and though she had never seen the Steward's face, she knew the posture. She had seen it in her grandmother's shoulders. In every adult in Hollowmere who had ever said *that's just how things are.*

"Well," the Steward said, in a voice like a closing book. "Contract 881. You've saved me the trouble of delivery."

Odile's hands closed tighter around the glass. The ember flared against her palms — hot, alive, hers.

"Not yet," she said. "It's not sealed yet."

The Steward smiled, and produced a pen.
