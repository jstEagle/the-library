## 8. The Steward's Bargain

The ember sat in its glass like a held breath.

Odile had imagined this moment a hundred ways on the climb through the flues — Brannoc's soot still ghosting her shoulders, Rosalind's hand warm around hers, the guardian's toll paid in a piece of Rosalind that Odile would never be able to give back. In every imagining, she simply took the glass and ran. There was no version of the moment that included the Steward already waiting at the vault's threshold, seated on a chair of black iron as though the room had grown it for him, hands folded, patient as winter.

"You've made good time," he said. "Better than most. The last one who came for her own soul took eleven years. You took nine days."

Odile stopped three paces away. Behind her, she felt Rosalind go very still — the stillness of someone calculating exits and finding none. Somewhere above them, the Court groaned against the solstice wind, and the first flame in its great hearth threw shadows that moved wrong, moving toward things instead of away from them.

"Step aside," Odile said. Her voice came out steadier than she deserved.

"I will." The Steward unfolded his hands. He was not what she'd expected — no horns, no grandeur, just a grey man with kind eyes and ink-stained fingers, like a clerk who had worked one desk too long. That was somehow worse. Monsters were easier to refuse when they looked like monsters. "But first, an accounting. It is solstice eve, cartographer. By dawn, every contract in this Court comes due or renews. Yours is among them."

"My contract expired when I walked out of Hollowmere."

"Did it?" He gestured, gently, at the glass. "Then why does your soul still sit in my vault?"

* * *

He rose, and the room rearranged itself around him without seeming to move — the shadows shifting their allegiance, the torchlight bending. He did not approach the ember. He approached Odile, and stopped just inside the distance where courtesy lived.

"Let me show you what you are," he said. "Not what you fear you are. What you are, precisely, down to the last entry."

He opened his palm, and light gathered there — not fire, but something older, the color of ledger-lines, of ruled paper. And in it Odile saw:

Hollowmere, two hundred years ago. A valley drowning in wild magic — trees that walked, rivers that changed their minds, children born with too many eyes. Her ancestors starving in the gaps between miracles. And then a figure coming down from the mountain: the Steward, younger, or a version of him, offering terms. One soul per generation. One child, named before birth, whose self would be drawn up through the mountain roots and held here, safe, unspent — and in exchange, a valley of quiet. No magic. No miracles. Just weather, and wheat, and ordinary death.

Her grandmother's face swam into the vision — not cruel, not weeping. Tired. Signing. Saying, *let it be my granddaughter's daughter; let me never have to watch.*

"That's not—" Odile's voice cracked. "She didn't sell me. She bought us safety."

"She did both," the Steward said. "That is the nature of every bargain I keep. Both halves are true, always. That is why they hold." He closed his hand, and the vision guttered out. "Your grandmother believed she was sparing you. She believed a soul kept whole in glass was better than a soul scattered by magic. Perhaps she was right. I do not judge. I only keep accounts."

"And now?"

"Now the account renews at dawn." He turned to the glass, to the small ember burning there, patient and orange and unmistakably alive. "Unless."

* * *

The word hung in the cold air of the vault, and Odile hated how her heart leaned toward it.

"Unless," she repeated.

"The valley's protection requires a soul in trust. It has required one for two hundred years. Your line has provided four." He spoke as though reading a shipping manifest. "You were the fourth. The contract names a bloodline, but bloodlines end, cartographer. They thin. They wander. Yours wandered rather thoroughly — you left, after all. The valley will need a new name on its page."

Behind her, Rosalind made a sound — small, involuntary, the sound of someone seeing a trap close from the inside.

"No," Odile said.

"You haven't heard the offer."

"I don't need to. I can see its shape from here."

"See it anyway." The Steward's kind eyes did not blink. "Return to Hollowmere. Renew the contract yourself — you're of age, you're of the line, you'd sign with full knowledge, which is more than your grandmother ever managed. Your soul returns to you tonight. Whole. Warm. You'll feel it settle back into your chest like a coal dropped into a banked fire. And the valley keeps its quiet. The children keep their single set of eyes. The wheat keeps growing. All of it — everything you grieved on the road down here — restored, at the cost of a signature."

"And the new name on the page?"

"A child not yet born," the Steward said. "Named tonight. Bound at birth. As you were."

The silence that followed was total. Even the first flame seemed to lean in.

"There are families in Hollowmere," Odile said slowly, "who would say yes. Who would *want* it. Safety for a price they'd never see paid."

"There always are. There always have been." He spread his ink-stained hands. "I am not the corruption, cartographer. I am the *clerk* of it. The corruption is older than me, and it lives wherever people decide that some child, somewhere, is a price worth paying. I merely keep the books honest. Someone must."

And that was the terrible thing — standing there, exhausted, soul-starved for nine days in a body that felt like a borrowed coat, Odile could see the machinery perfectly. Every gear of it. The valley's fear feeding the bargain; the bargain feeding the Steward's vault; the vault feeding the quiet that fed the fear again. Generation after generation of tired grandmothers signing their grandchildren into glass because the alternative was a world that might hurt them. It wasn't evil. Evil could be fought. This was *bookkeeping*. It would renew itself forever unless someone burned the book.

"You're offering me a choice," she said.

"I am offering you a choice. That is more than most get."

"You're offering me the chance to become the next link in a chain and call it mercy." She took a breath, and it hurt, the way cold air hurts, the way truth hurts. "You want me to look at some unborn child and see what my grandmother saw when she looked at me. A price. A safety deposit. A *thing*."

"I want you to see clearly," said the Steward, "and choose anyway. That is all power ever asks."

* * *

Odile thought of Hollowmere. Not the valley as an idea — the actual place. Mrs. Aldous's kitchen with its blue door. The way frost came off the pond in October like breath on glass. Her own drafting table under the north window, where she had drawn realms she'd never see because drawing them was the closest she could come to leaving. She had built her whole life on careful distance, on maps instead of journeys, and she understood suddenly that the valley had taught her that — a place that kept its children at arm's length from wonder because wonder had teeth.

And she thought of the child. Unnamed, unborn, hypothetical — and therefore easy to spend. That was how it always began. With someone abstract enough not to count.

"No," she said again, and this time it came from somewhere below her ribs, somewhere the Steward's ledgers didn't reach. "No. I won't sign. I won't trade my chains for a smaller pair and call them mine. If the valley wants safety, it can learn to live brave. If it wants quiet, it can plant quieter wheat. But no more children in glass. Not bought with my name. Not bought with anyone's."

The Steward regarded her for a long moment. Something flickered behind the kindness — not anger. Something almost like respect, which frightened her more than anger would have.

"Do you know what happens at dawn, if the contract lapses?" he asked softly. "The valley opens. Every spell it ever refused comes home. Two hundred years of held-back magic, returning all at once, to a people who have forgotten how to survive it. Your mother. Your neighbors. The blue door on Mrs. Aldous's kitchen. Is your principle worth their lives?"

Odile's mouth went dry. Because he was right — that was the horror of him, he was always partly right — and for one black instant she saw it: the flood, the walking trees, the sky remembering it could do anything.

Then she felt Rosalind's hand find hers. Disgraced archivist, memory bleeding away fragment by fragment since the guardian's toll, who had paid a piece of herself without hesitation and never once asked what it was buying. Who remembered everyone's past but her own, and had chosen, with her last sure memories, to stand in a freezing vault beside a stranger.

Some things aren't protected by walls, Odile thought. Some things are chosen, and choosing is the only wall there is.

"Yes," she said. "It is."

The Steward sighed — a long, clerical sigh, the sigh of a man closing one ledger and opening another. "Then we proceed to the final remedy," he said, "and I warn you, cartographer, it is worse than anything I have offered. There is exactly one way to void a contract written in prophecy." His eyes went, deliberately, past her shoulder — to the satchel at Rosalind's hip, where the century-old book rode heavy as a sleeping animal. "And it burns hotter than any fire you have ever known."

Above them, the mountain shuddered. Somewhere far off, a bell began to ring the hours toward midnight, and the first flame stood up taller in its hearth, like something listening.

"It's solstice eve," the Steward said. "Choose quickly."
