## 9. Burning the Book

The Court's first flame did not live in a hearth. It lived in a wound.

Rosalind had known this since her earliest remembered hour — since the day she woke in the archives with no name in her mouth and ink stains on fingers that were not hers. The first flame burned at the bottom of the Foundry's oldest shaft, where the mountain itself had been cracked open like an egg, and it was not gold or orange but the color of the moment before lightning: white with a bruise of violet at its heart. It had burned before there were courts to warm, before there were souls to weigh, before anyone thought to write down what would happen.

Which was, of course, why it could unmake what had been written down.

The book was heavier than it should have been. Prophecies always are; they carry the weight of every life they've pressed flat. Rosalind held it against her chest with both arms as they ran the last corridor, and she felt it breathing against her ribs — a slow, patient pulse, like something sleeping that did not wish to be woken.

*You will burn,* it seemed to say. *You have always been going to burn.*

"Rosalind." Odile's hand closed around her wrist, pulling her out of the book's gravity. The cartographer's knuckles were split from the vault door, her hair escaping its braid in gray-brown threads, and her eyes — gods, her eyes — held the particular ferocity of someone who has already lost everything once tonight and refused to lose it twice. "Stay with me. Whatever it's saying to you, don't listen."

"It isn't saying anything," Rosalind lied.

Odile saw through it. Odile saw through everything; that was her whole terrible gift. She read the world the way she read terrain — every fault line, every place the ground would give way. But she didn't call the lie out. She simply tightened her grip and kept running, and behind them the Steward's voice rolled through the halls like weather.

**THE CONTRACT CANNOT BE BROKEN. THE CONTRACT IS THE COURT.**

"The contract," Rosalind muttered, "is paper. I have spent two hundred years among papers. Paper is only ever waiting for a match."

Brannoc was waiting for them at the shaft's mouth, a smear of soot and grin in the torchlight, his chimney-wraith body more smoke than substance now, stretched thin from carrying them through flues no living thing should fit inside. Behind him stood Wren the candlemaker, holding a stub of tallow like a talisman, and beyond them all — the flame. It roared up out of the wound in the world without heat, without light in any honest sense, a column of white silence that made Rosalind's remaining memories tremble in their shelves.

Because that was the truth she had carried up from the vault and not said aloud, not even to Odile. Especially not to Odile.

The first flame did not burn contracts alone. It burned *bindings*. And Rosalind was bound — thread by thread, memory by memory, stitched into the Court's ledger as thoroughly as any soul in any jar. Her past had been taken as payment long ago; whatever fragments remained were collateral still on deposit. Feed the book to the flame and every contract unraveled — including the one that kept her pieces from scattering entirely.

She had done the arithmetic in the dark of the flue while Brannoc carried her, doing it calmly, the way she'd once catalogued other people's losses: *what remains of me is small enough to lose.* A handful of faces. The smell of the archives, dust and iron. Two hundred years of other people's histories, which had never been hers anyway.

And Odile. She would keep Odile for exactly as long as it took the fire to reach her.

"You're doing it again." Odile had stopped at the shaft's edge. She turned, and the first flame lit half her face white, and she looked — Rosalind thought absurdly, helplessly — like a map of somewhere worth going. "That thing you do. Deciding your part quietly and letting everyone else find out after."

"There's no other way to do it. My parts always end the same way."

"Then let me change the ending. That's what maps are for, Rosalind. Finding another route."

* * *

The Steward arrived the way winter arrives — not running, simply becoming present everywhere at once. One moment the hall was theirs; the next, cold filled it floor to ceiling, and the Steward stood between them and the flame, tall as a doorframe, wearing a face like a ledger page: ruled lines, columns, no room for anything unsanctioned.

**GIVE ME THE BOOK,** it said. Its voice was not loud. It was *total*, arriving inside the ear, inside the teeth. **AND I WILL RETURN WHAT WAS TAKEN FROM YOU BOTH. THE CARTOGRAPHER'S EMBER. YOUR NAME, ARCHIVIST. YOUR FIRST MEMORY. EVERYTHING THE LEDGER HOLDS.**

Rosalind felt Odile sway beside her. Of course she swayed. Her soul was right there, offered back, no burning required, no price except one child in Hollowmere and every generation after.

But Odile's chin came up, and when she spoke her voice did not shake. "You'll forgive me if I've stopped trusting institutions that keep ledgers."

**SENTIMENT,** said the Steward. **AN ASSET LIKE ANY OTHER.**

"No," Rosalind said, stepping forward. "It's the one thing you can't enter in a column. I should know. I've tried."

And she opened the book.

It fought her. Gods, it fought her — pages clamping shut like a jaw, the spine writhing in her grip, and the words lifting off the vellum in black streams that coiled toward her eyes, her ears, her mouth, hungry to be read aloud, to be obeyed, to be *true*. She heard her own voice start to shape syllables that were not hers — *and on the eve of the longest night, the sold one shall—*

"Rosalind!" Odile's hands over hers. Brannoc's smoke pouring around them both, muffling, smothering. Wren shouting something about wax and warding that Rosalind couldn't parse and didn't need to. Four misfits of a court built on ownership, holding one book shut together.

"Together, then," Rosalind gasped. "On three. Like a family."

"We *are*—" Odile began.

"One," said Rosalind, and threw.

* * *

The book hit the flame and the world forgot how to be shaped.

There was no roar. The first flame consumed in silence, and the silence was worse — a great inhale that pulled the air from the hall, from their lungs, from the torches lining the walls, which guttered and died in ranks like soldiers falling. For one heartbeat everything was dark.

Then the contracts began to unravel.

Rosalind felt each one go. They were threads in her, she understood now — had always been threads, silver and binding, and the fire moved along them one by one, singing them apart. Somewhere in the Court, a merchant screamed as three centuries of purchased youth drained out of him and left him only himself. Somewhere a jar shattered and a soul went home to a body that had forgotten expecting it. The ledger-rooms above them were shedding their contents like trees in autumn, and the mountain groaned around it all, its foundations written in obligations that were, page by page, ceasing to exist.

And then the fire reached her.

It did not hurt. That was the cruelest mercy of it. It simply *lifted things out*, gently, the way an archivist lifts a fragile page. Her mother's laugh — gone, and she hadn't known until that instant she still had it. A garden she'd never catalogued, roses gone feral over a broken wall. A name being called across water. The taste of bread shared with someone whose face was already dissolving as she reached for it. Each memory rose off her like a spark, hung white and bright in the flame's column, and scattered upward into a dark that would never give it back.

She heard herself making a sound. Small. She was ashamed of it and let it happen anyway.

"Look at me." Odile was in front of her, both hands on her face, forcing her eyes to focus. The cartographer was crying without any noise at all, tears cutting clean lines through the soot. "Whatever's left — hold onto what's left. Hold onto *now*. Now is real. I'm real. Feel my hands. That's yours. Nobody wrote that. Nobody sold that. That's ours."

"I won't know you," Rosalind whispered. Words were getting harder; they too were entries, and entries were leaving. "I won't know your name. I'll look at you and see a stranger and—"

"Then I'll introduce myself," Odile said fiercely. "As many times as it takes. I'm very good at names. I label everything. Ask Brannoc."

Behind them, Brannoc laughed — wet, cracked, alive.

The last of the bindings parted. Rosalind felt herself come loose from the Court like a tooth from the gum, and the flame drew breath for its final consumption, and she thought, distantly, with the last of her archivist's precision: *so this is how I end, unwritten.*

Then Odile's hand found hers.

Not her face. Her hand. Fingers laced through fingers, gripping hard enough to hurt, anchored in flesh and stubbornness and choice — and the fire paused. Recoiled. Because the flame could unmake what was written, what was owed, what was bound — but it could not touch what was freely given. Odile's grip was not in any ledger. Had never been in any ledger. Was the single uncontracted thing in the entire Court, and she was holding on with both hands.

"Hold onto *now*," Odile repeated, and pulled.

The Court shuddered. Not metaphorically — the stone itself lurched, dust raining from a ceiling that had stood ten thousand years, because the mountain's bones were contracts and the contracts were ash. The first flame flickered, guttered, shrank — a prophecy cannot outlive its own fulfillment, and fulfillment had just walked out of its reach — and in the failing white light Rosalind saw Odile's free hand close around something small and glass, something with a coal of light inside it beating like a second heart.

The ember. Hers. Taken, not traded, in the chaos of the unraveling.

Everything risked. Everything lost. Everything chosen.

The last thing Rosalind knew — the final entry in the archive of herself — was warmth. Odile's hand, the ember's glow bleeding through her palm, Brannoc's smoke wrapping them all as the floor tilted and the world came down. She was falling, and someone was falling with her, and someone was laughing through their tears, and she thought:

*I don't know who I am. But I know whose hand this is.*

Then the dark took the rest, kindly, like snowfall.

* * *

When Rosalind opened her eyes, there was sky.

Gray valley sky, low and soft, smelling of rain and woodsmoke — a sky with no court beneath it, no ledger overhead. She was lying on frost-stiffened grass at the foot of the mountain, and someone had put a coat over her, and someone else — a small woman with soot in her braid and an ember-glass hanging empty at her throat — was watching her with an expression Rosalind did not recognize and could not look away from.

"Hello," the woman said carefully. "My name is Odile Kavanagh. I'm a cartographer. What's yours?"

Rosalind searched herself and found the shelves bare, the ledgers blank, two hundred years gone like sparks on wind.

"I don't know," she said honestly.

"That's alright." Odile smiled, and it was like sunrise over unmapped country. "We'll draw it as we go."

Somewhere above them, faint as a dying echo, the mountain gave one last shudder — and far below, in a hall with no contracts left to keep, something ancient opened eyes that had slept since the world was written, and began, very softly, to laugh.
