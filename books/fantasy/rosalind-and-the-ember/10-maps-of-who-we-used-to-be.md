## 10. Maps of Who We Used to Be

The valley had changed its smell.

That was the first thing I noticed, three days after the mountain stopped humming — or rather, after we finally climbed out of the dark and heard it *not* humming, a silence so complete it rang like a struck bell. Hollowmere had always smelled of ash and cold iron, of hearth-fires banked low and chimneys swept twice weekly because Brannoc's kin in the flues demanded tribute of pine boughs and no one had ever known why. Now there was something underneath all that: green things growing where green things hadn't dared. Moss climbing the north face of the millstone. A wild rose pushing up through a crack in the chapel steps like it had been waiting a hundred years for permission.

Magic was leaking back into the valley. Slowly, cautiously, the way water finds a channel — and this time, nothing was drinking it before it reached us.

I stood at my worktable with the window open to all of it, and I could not draw a straight line.

My hand kept wandering. That was the trouble. For twenty-six years I had been the finest cartographer Hollowmere had ever produced, which is a smaller distinction than it sounds when your valley has exactly one road, two bridges, and a boundary that might as well have been the edge of the world. I drew with precision because precision was all I had; I mapped realms I would never see because imagining them hurt less than wanting them. Every line I'd ever inked had been an act of controlled grief.

Now grief had nowhere to go. The Ember Court was rubble and cooling glass beneath the mountain. The ledger was ash. My soul sat warm behind my sternum like a second heart, and every morning I woke and pressed my palm flat against my chest just to feel it burning, just to be sure the Steward's bargain hadn't been some fever dream I'd wake from back in that torchlit hall, announced again as *the sold one*.

It wasn't a dream. Dreams don't leave you a family.

She was in the kitchen. I could hear her — not moving, exactly, but *being*, the particular presence Rosalind filled a room with, the way she narrated her own actions under her breath because she'd spent centuries surrounded by documents that never answered. She was attempting bread. The candlemaker — Sef, whose name we'd learned only after the burning, who had laughed until she cried when she realized she could finally say it aloud without the Court hearing — was supervising with the air of someone who had made ten thousand candles and exactly zero loaves and considered this a fair exchange of expertise.

"You're kneading it wrong," Sef said through the wall.

"I'm kneading it *enthusiastically*. There's a difference."

"There's flour on the ceiling."

"That's enthusiasm."

I smiled down at the blank sheet of vellum and let the smile stay. That was new too. Before, I had rationed even that — a life built on careful distance doesn't waste warmth on empty rooms. But the room wasn't empty anymore. Nothing was.

* * *

Rosalind came to find me at dusk, flour to her elbows and a smudge of it across one cheekbone like a comet's tail. She stood in the doorway of my workroom — *our* workroom now, though neither of us had said so aloud; we simply redistributed the furniture one afternoon as if it had always been thus — and looked at the abandoned map with the particular expression she reserved for damaged manuscripts. Concerned. Tender. Slightly accusatory.

"It's been staring at you for hours," she said. "You're letting it win."

"It's not a competition."

"Everything is a competition. I worked in an archive full of rival archivists for four hundred years. I know competitions." She drifted closer, and I watched her read the page the way she read everything — hungrily, thoroughly, hunting for what was missing. "You've started the same river four times."

"The Wetherspring." I tapped the ghost-lines. "It used to run gray. Cold, sluggish, half-frozen eight months of the year. It was the first thing I noticed when we came home — it runs *clear* now. Fast. It's a different river, Roz. How do you map a different river?"

She was quiet a moment. Then she pulled the spare stool over and sat, tucking her feet up, which she did only when a conversation mattered. "You map what it *is*, not what it was," she said. "That's all any map ever does. A map isn't a memory, Odile. It's a promise about where things are."

Something turned over in my chest — that ember-place, my own soul, warm as a held stone.

"And if you can't remember what anything was?" I asked gently. Because that was the shape of our evenings now, the question we circled like moths. After the burning, after the Court's first flame ate the prophecy and unraveled every contract it held, Rosalind had come out of the mountain nearly empty. Four hundred years of patrons' histories, court intrigues, stolen moments — gone, scattered like sparks up a chimney. What remained was instinct, skill, and us. Some nights she woke reaching for names that weren't there anymore, and I would lie awake listening to her breathing and hating the fire even as it warmed me.

Rosalind shrugged, but her eyes were serious. "Then someone draws me a new map."

* * *

I worked for six days.

Not on commissions — the valley had sent plenty, giddy neighbors wanting their changed land recorded, the miller wanting his new-clear stream charted, old Tam Vess requesting, with tremendous dignity, a map of his garden "so the beans know where they belong." I did those too, gladly. But the sixth night I cleared everything else away, trimmed a fresh sheet of vellum, and set out my good inks: the sepia for earth, the deep blue-black for water, and a small pot of vermilion I had been saving since I was nineteen years old, bought in a trader's caravan from a woman who swore it was ground ember-stone and charged me accordingly. I'd never found a use for it. You cannot map a realm you'll never see in the color of fire.

I could now.

I began at the center, which is not where maps begin — they begin at edges, boundaries, the safe margins of the known — but this was not that kind of map. At the center I drew the house. Not grandly. Just true. Two floors and a crooked chimney, drawn with the care I'd once lavished on coastlines. Beside the door, a hearth shown in cross-section, flames rendered in vermilion, and above the hearth, in letters small and exact, I wrote two names:

*Odile Kavanagh.*
*Rosalind.*

I sat back and looked at them together on the page, inked side by side in my own hand, and felt something in me settle that had been unmoored since the moment I opened a century-old book and found my own name waiting in wet ink. Fate had written me into its margins once. This was my answer. My hand. My ink. My choosing.

From the house, roads went outward — thin, confident lines. One west to Sef's cottage by the old kiln yard, where the candlemaker had resumed her trade in daylight for the first time in a century, making candles that burned gold and smelled of honey and, she claimed, "kept honest dreams in and dishonest ones out." On her plot I drew her kiln, her drying racks, and a single candle flame, and labeled it *Sef — who chose herself.* She'd cried when I showed her. She'd denied crying immediately, citing flour dust, though there was no flour in my workroom.

Another road ran east and then simply *stopped*, ending in a spiral drawn at the mouth of the mountain's old flue-vent. Brannoc's mark. The chimney-wraith had stayed behind in the deep places when the Court fell — not trapped, he insisted, but *employed*; someone had to mind the roots of the mountain while it healed, and wraiths made of hearth-smoke are nothing if not dutiful. He visited through every chimney in the valley now, materializing in ours most evenings to demand pine boughs and gossip, depositing soot and, occasionally, small treasures: a button lost a hundred years ago, a marble, once an entire spoon. On his spiral I wrote *Brannoc — keeper of the flues, friend,* and I knew he would be insufferably pleased, and I knew he would pretend not to be, and both of these things made my chest ache pleasantly.

The Wetherspring I drew clear and fast, blue-black, running bright from the mountain's shoulder to the valley's far edge — and past the edge, for the first time in my life, I kept drawing. Out beyond Hollowmere, where the map had always ended, I let the rivers wander off the parchment's edge unfinished, trailing toward a margin note in vermilion: *to be continued.*

Because that was the other thing the burning had bought us. The valley's bargain had been one child's soul per generation, collateral against magic, my grandmother's terrible arithmetic of safety. With the contracts undone, the boundary was no longer a wall. It was a door. Caravans would come, someday, from realms I had only ever imagined. And I — Odile Kavanagh, who had mapped worlds she could never touch — was going to walk out and see them, and come home, and draw every mile of it.

But that was tomorrow's map.

* * *

I gave it to her on the seventh evening, at the hearth, with bread (successful, eventually) and Sef's honey-gold candles burning on the mantel and snow beginning outside the window, soft and ordinary, asking nothing of anyone.

Rosalind took the rolled vellum in both hands the way she once held rare manuscripts — reverently, elbows tucked, breath held. She read it in silence for a long time. The fire popped. Somewhere below, water ran quick and clear over stones that had been frozen my whole life.

"This isn't a map of who I was," she said at last, and her voice caught on the middle of it.

"No."

"It's—" She smoothed the vellum across her knees. Her finger found the hearth, the two names, the vermilion flames. "It's a map of who I *am*."

"Some things aren't remembered," I said. "They're rebuilt. Brick by brick. Name by name." I touched the page beside hers, where her name sat in ink that would outlast us both. "You spent four hundred years holding everyone else's history. Consider this yours. And consider it *unfinished* — because you're going to fill in the rest yourself, one day at a time, and when you do, I'll be here to chart it."

Rosalind looked up at me. Firelight moved in her eyes, and for a moment she wore the expression I had seen only once before — at the edge of the vault, on solstice eve, when she'd paid a memory as toll without hesitation, spending a piece of herself for a stranger she'd known three days. That was the whole of her, really. Everything essential had survived the burning, because everything essential had never been memory at all. It was this: the reaching. The choosing. The hand extended in the dark.

"Odile Kavanagh," she said solemnly, "you are the only cartographer I have ever loved, and I remember loving precisely no one else, so the competition is thin, but still. I want it noted in the record."

"Noted," I said, laughing, wiping my eyes with the heel of my hand. "In ink. Permanent."

Outside, the snow fell on a valley that owed nothing to anyone. Inside, the fire — our fire, ordinary and ours — burned steady in the hearth at the center of the map, at the center of the house, at the center of the small, stubborn, self-made country we were drawing together, line by line, year by year, with no fate left in the margins and all the room in the world.
