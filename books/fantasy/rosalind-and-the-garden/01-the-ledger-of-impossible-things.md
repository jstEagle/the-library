## 1. The Ledger of Impossible Things

The trouble with impossible things, Casper Coldharbour had learned across nineteen years and eleven thousand entries, was not that they were impossible. It was that they arrived needing receipts.

Casper sat in the counting room of the guildhouse of Vellum & Thorn with a pot of ink gone cold beside them, a candle burned down to its last honest inch, and before them the night's trade, laid out for audit like a patient on a surgeon's table.

One memory of spring.

Specifically: one memory belonging to a widow named Anthe Merrilow, aged sixty-one, of the tanner's row in Lowmarsh — the memory of an afternoon in her fourteenth year, lying in an orchard with her sister while the apple blossoms came down like slow snow, and neither of them said anything, because there was nothing that needed saying. The client had sold it that evening, through the guild's night-box, for three silver marks and a jar of plum preserves.

Winter prices. Casper had written *winter prices* in the margin twice, underlined once, and then underlined it again because once had felt insufficient and twice felt like shouting, which was against the rules.

The rules were simple. There were only two of them, carved into the lintel above the guildhouse door where every client had to pass beneath them, and they read:

*Everything owed must be written.*

*Nothing spoken aloud.*

People assumed the second rule existed because speech was unreliable, mutable, subject to the soft corruption of the tongue. People were wrong. The second rule existed because words spoken aloud could be *heard*, and some things, once heard, did not forget being heard. The guild had learned this in an age so old that even the ledgers recording the learning had been lost, which Casper considered the single most professionally offensive fact in the history of their profession.

So: everything written. Nothing said. And everything, everything, priced.

Casper turned the page and examined the valuation slip attached to Anthe Merrilow's spring.

The night-assessor — a nervous young man named Petch, who had been with the guild eight months and still flinched when the vault breathed — had valued the memory at three marks on the grounds that it contained no rare elements. No dragonfire. No godsblood. No prophecy, no curse, no name of power. Just blossoms, and a sister, and silence.

Casper dipped the pen.

*Mispriced,* they wrote. *Assessor Petch has valued by content rather than consequence. Note: the seller will spend the rest of her life knowing that something is missing from her fourteenth year and being unable to name it. She will feel it most in orchards. She will avoid orchards. She will tell herself she dislikes apples. This is a wound that compounds interest. Value at twelve marks minimum, and flag the sale for review — the seller may not have understood what she was trading. Consent unverified is consent ungiven.*

They signed it with the small, precise *C.C.* that appeared in eleven thousand other margins, and sat back, and listened to the guildhouse settle around them.

It was a sound like a large animal turning over in its sleep. The Vellum & Thorn guildhouse had been built, rebuilt, added onto, and argued with for six hundred years, and it creaked in a language all its own. Old keepers claimed you could read the building's mood the way sailors read weather. Casper had gotten as far as distinguishing *hungry* from *disapproving*, which covered most evenings.

Through the window, Lowmarsh lay under its first real snow, rooftops hunched white, chimneys smoking like a congregation of very small, very dutiful volcanoes. Somewhere out there was Anthe Merrilow, asleep, lighter by one afternoon and richer by three marks and a jar of preserves she would never be able to eat without crying and not know why.

Casper blew out the candle, relit it — the flame had begun making an argumentative blue color that meant the wick needed trimming — and pulled the next file toward them.

This was the work. This was the whole of the work, and had been since they were nine years old and the previous keeper, a dry stick of a man named Ossory who smelled of pepper and regret, had put a pen in their hand and said, *You have neat handwriting and no family. You'll do.* Nineteen years later, Casper kept the ledgers of the guild that traded in impossible things, and they were good at it — genuinely, deeply good, the kind of good that looked from the outside like nothing more than a person who never made mistakes, which was its own kind of loneliness.

Because here was the thing about keeping accounts of the impossible: nobody thanked you. The clients never saw the margins. The traders resented the audits. The assessors dreaded your red ink. And the guildmaster —

Casper's pen paused over the next file.

The guildmaster, Madam Serapine Vell herself, thirteenth of her name and first of her patience, had stopped by the counting room three nights ago, stood in the doorway for a long moment watching Casper work, and said, "You should take an evening, Coldharbour. The snow is fine this year."

And Casper had said, "There are forty-one files outstanding," which was true, and Madam Vell had looked at them the way one looks at a clock that keeps perfect time in a house with no one left to wake, and had gone away.

Forty-one files. Casper had done thirty-eight.

* * *

File thirty-nine was a trade of weather. A farmer upcountry had purchased one week of rain for his fields, paying with his talent for whistling, which he had never valued and his wife had loved dearly. Standard terms. Casper checked the consent clause, found it properly witnessed, initialed the margin (*note: wife's claim on whistle unrecorded — recommend goodwill payment of one song, to be delivered by trader within the season*), and moved on.

File forty was stranger. Someone had sold their shadow's opinion of them. Casper read the description twice — *the shadow disapproves; seller reports relief* — and priced it fairly, and did not think about what their own shadow thought of them, because that way lay insomnia, and Casper had a schedule.

File forty-one was the one that stopped them.

It was not the trade itself, which was ordinary enough: a young man selling his ability to weep, purchased by someone who collected such things, price fair, consent witnessed. What stopped Casper was the handwriting on the intake slip. It was elegant. It was careful. It was, letter for letter and flourish for flourish, Casper's own.

Except Casper had not written it.

They held the slip to the light. They compared it against their own signature in the margin of file thirty-nine. Identical down to the tiny hesitation in the crossbar of the *h* that Casper had developed at fourteen after a winter fever and had never managed to train away.

Someone in the guildhouse was forging their hand. Or — and this thought arrived uninvited and sat down at the table like a guest who knew where the cups were kept — something was practicing.

Casper set the slip aside, marked the file *hold for keeper's review*, and told themselves firmly that there were forty-one files and one of them being odd was well within the annual allowance for oddness. The guild traded in impossible things. Occasionally the impossible things traded back. This was why the rules existed.

*Everything owed must be written. Nothing spoken aloud.*

Casper wrote the forged slip into the anomaly log, described it precisely, sealed the log with wax and the keeper's signet, and allowed themselves, as a reward, the last of the cold tea.

It tasted like the bottom of a winter. They drank it anyway.

* * *

Near midnight, the guildhouse spoke.

Not creaked — *spoke*. A low bell in the receiving hall, rung once, the tone of it rolling up through the floors and into Casper's chest like a stone dropped down a well. The night-bell meant a client at the door outside of hours. Clients who came at midnight were either desperate, dangerous, or both, and the guild's standing instruction was that the keeper handled intake personally, because desperate people lied best to junior staff.

Casper took the lamp, took the intake ledger, and went down through the halls, past the sleeping cells of the traders, past the door of the vault (which exhaled faintly as they passed, the way it always did, as if it had been holding its breath and had given up), down the stairs to the receiving hall.

Madam Vell was already there, standing in the shadows behind the intake desk, hands folded, silver hair pinned with the severity of a woman who had won arguments with archdukes. She raised one finger: *I'll observe. You'll speak.* — meaning, of course, *you'll write*. Even among themselves, the guild's senior members kept the old discipline loosely, but the desk was the desk, and the desk kept the rules.

Casper opened the door.

The woman on the step was soaked to the skin, hooded, mud to the knees — a traveler, and a fast one, and one who had ridden through snowfall that would have turned back a sensible person. Snow clung to her shoulders and melted as Casper watched. Under the hood, her face was pale and proud and frightened in the specific way of people who have been frightened for a long time and have gotten very good at carrying it.

"Please," the woman began, and then stopped herself, eyes going up to the lintel, reading the carved rules by lamplight. Casper watched her understand them — really understand, the way few clients did — and watched her close her mouth and stand there dripping, a petition with no permitted words.

Casper set the intake ledger on the ledge outside the door, opened it to a fresh page, and wrote:

*You've come to the right door. Write what you need. Take your time. There is a bench; the rain is not going anywhere, and neither are we.*

The woman read it. Something in her face cracked — not broke, cracked, the way ice cracks before it gives — and she took the proffered pen with fingers that shook only slightly, which Casper noted as remarkable, and wrote:

*I need the guild to save my brother. He is dying of something no physician can name. I will pay anything. I mean that literally. I can pay literally anything, and I think you know what I mean by that.*

Casper read it twice. Behind them, in the dark of the hall, he felt rather than saw Madam Vell go very still — the stillness of a hunter, or of a woman hearing an old song she'd hoped was forgotten.

Casper took back the pen and wrote the standard response, the one written perhaps four hundred times a year:

*The guild trades in many things. Life and death are not among them. State plainly what your brother suffers from, and what you offer, and we will determine whether a trade exists.*

The woman bent over the page. Her hood fell back. Snow gathered in her dark hair, and she did not notice. She wrote slowly, choosing each word like a woman crossing a frozen river, testing every step:

*My brother is King Aldous. His crown has begun to speak to him. Last week it began to speak back.*

The pen hovered. Then, beneath it, smaller, the letters pressed deep enough to tear the page:

*And tonight, when I prayed in a ruined chapel for anyone at all to help him — something answered me. It gave me directions. To here.*

Casper did not move. The lamp flame leaned sideways in a draft that had come from nowhere, and somewhere far below them, down past the cellars, past the foundations, past the place where maps stopped pretending to be accurate, the vault exhaled — long and slow and satisfied, like a thing that had waited six hundred years for a knock at the door.

Behind the desk, Madam Vell stepped forward into the lamplight, and smiled, and it was not a smile Casper had ever seen her wear in nineteen years of ledgers.

"Come in out of the snow, Lady Rosalind," said the guildmaster, breaking the oldest rule in the house as though it were a cobweb. "We have been expecting you for a very long time."

Casper looked down at the intake ledger, at the fresh page waiting under their pen, and wrote the only thing the rules allowed:

*Entry one. Everything owed must be written.*

Their hand, for the first time in nineteen years, was not steady.
