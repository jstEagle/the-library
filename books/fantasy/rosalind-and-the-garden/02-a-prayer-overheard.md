## 2. A Prayer Overheard

The chapel had been dead for sixty years, which was precisely why Rosalind had chosen it.

Dead things did not listen. Dead things could not answer. She had learned that much in the last month, and she had learned it the hard way — by hearing her brother's crown whisper back.

She picked her way over fallen roof tiles, past a font full of rainwater and drowned leaves, to the place where the altar had once stood. Someone had carted the altar itself off decades ago; only its shadow remained on the floor, a paler rectangle of stone where the sun fell through the broken roof. Rosalind knelt in that rectangle because it seemed right. If one was going to do something foolish, one ought at least to do it properly.

Her knees found the cold stone. She folded her hands, then unfolded them, then folded them again, because she could not remember how praying actually worked. She had been raised on state prayers — long, formal, addressed to the Concord of Nine by their proper titles, recited by robed men with excellent diction and no discernible belief. Those prayers were performances. This was not supposed to be a performance. This was supposed to be a plea, and she had no idea what a plea looked like when there was no audience to impress.

"Hello," she said finally, to the empty chapel. Then, wincing: "That's not — I'm sorry. I've never done this before."

Silence. A pigeon resettled itself somewhere in the rafters with the offended air of a courtier whose nap had been disturbed.

Rosalind pressed on.

"My name is Rosalind. You probably know that, if you're listening. Everyone knows my family's name." She swallowed. "My brother is Aldous. King Aldous. And something is wrong with him."

There. Said aloud, in a ruin, to nobody. It was the first time she had said it aloud anywhere but to her own reflection, and even the reflection had gotten risky lately, because sometimes when she practiced the words her face in the glass would go strange and still, like a woman listening through a wall.

"It started in the spring," she said. "He began talking in his sleep. Not sleep-talking — I mean he *talks*, in his sleep, in a voice that isn't his. Deeper. Older. Like a man who has been shouting across a very large room for a very long time and has forgotten how to be quiet."

The pigeon cooed. Rosalind took it as encouragement, which told her how desperate she was.

"And now he does it awake. At first only at night, when he thought I couldn't hear. But three days ago —" Her voice caught. She made herself continue, because if she stopped she might never start again. "Three days ago, at the harvest audience, a farmer came to petition him about a boundary dispute. Perfectly ordinary. Aldous was being kind, the way he always is, and then —"

*Then his voice had split.* That was the truth she could not make gentle. One moment her brother had been leaning forward on his throne, saying something mild and fair about hedgerows, and the next moment two voices had come out of his mouth at once, braided together like rope — Aldous's voice, warm and slightly too quick as always, and beneath it another voice, slow as tree rings, saying the same words a half-beat behind, or perhaps a half-beat ahead, she still could not decide which. The farmer had dropped his petition and run. The court had laughed nervously and decided, collectively and instantly, to have heard nothing at all. Courts were very good at that. Courts could out-hear-nothing an avalanche.

"Aldous came to me that night," she whispered. "He held my hands and he said, *Rosie, whatever you hear me say, don't agree to it. Don't sign anything. Don't promise anything. Promise me you won't promise.*" A wet, unlovely laugh escaped her. "He's my brother. He knows me. He knows I'd bargain away my own teeth to help him."

She looked up at the broken roof, at the grey sky beyond it, and made herself say the rest.

"So I'm asking. I don't know who I'm asking. The Concord, if any of you are real. Anything older, if anything older is left. Anyone at all who is *not* the thing in his crown." Her hands tightened until the knuckles ached. "I'm not asking for a miracle. I'm not asking for armies or gold or some prophesied sword. I'm asking for someone to tell me what to do. Because everyone I should be able to trust is either terrified, complicit, or wearing the problem on his head, and I am so tired, and I am so frightened, and I cannot do this alone."

The silence that followed was enormous.

It went on so long that Rosalind began to feel foolish — kneeling in a ruin, confessing royal secrets to pigeons — and she was just gathering herself to stand when the air changed.

She had no better word for it. The air *changed*, the way a room changes when a door opens somewhere far away and a draft finds you. The rainwater in the font shivered without wind. The pale rectangle of sunlight on the floor brightened — not warmer, not brighter in any way she could measure, but *more present*, as though the sun had leaned closer to look.

And then something spoke.

Not aloud. That was the worst of it, she realized later. It never once used her ears. The voice arrived fully formed inside her, the way a memory arrives, or a name suddenly remembered — intimate, inevitable, as though it had always been there and she had only just noticed.

***You asked for anyone at all,*** it said, ***who is not the thing in his crown.***

Rosalind's whole body went rigid. Every sensible instinct she possessed — and she possessed a great many, having survived court since the age of six — screamed at her to run. She stayed kneeling. Partly from courage. Mostly because her legs had stopped taking instructions.

"Yes," she managed.

***Careful,*** said the voice, and there was something in it that might, in a younger god, have been humor. ***That was a wide door you opened. Most pray to someone specific. You prayed to anyone-who-is-not-the-enemy. Do you know how few listeners that excludes?***

"I'm beginning to suspect."

A pause. The light in the rectangle pulsed, slow, patient, like breathing.

***I will tell you what I am, small sister, so that you may refuse me honestly. I am old. Older than the Concord your priests recite. I answered prayers before prayers had names. I was worshipped under nine different titles and forgot most of them myself. Your kind buried me twice, and I let you, because burial is only another kind of sleeping, and I am very good at sleeping.*** The voice softened, or something adjacent to softening. ***But your brother's crown has been ringing like a bell these three months, and bells wake even the dead. So here I am. Awake. Listening.***

"What are you called?" Rosalind asked, because names mattered — Aldous had taught her that, years ago, over a lesson in treaties: *never sign with someone whose true name you don't know, Rosie.*

***Ah.*** Something moved through the ruined chapel then — not wind, exactly, but the sensation of a vast attention settling, the way a great bird lands. ***You ask the dangerous question first. Good. Names are currency where I come from, and I have spent so many. Call me what the first farmers called me: the One Who Waits Beneath. Or call me nothing. I answer to need more readily than to name.***

"That's not reassuring."

***No,*** it agreed, with terrible gentleness. ***It is not meant to be. Now. You asked what to do. I will tell you, and then you will wish you hadn't asked, and then you will do it anyway, because you love him. That is the shape of every prayer ever truly answered.***

Rosalind's mouth had gone dry. "Tell me."

***Your brother is not ill. He is not mad. He is bound. The crown he wears is not a crown — it is a knot, tied around something that should never have been tied, and the thing inside it has begun to speak through the only mouth it can reach. Every day it wears him thinner. Every day it uses his voice to make small promises, small agreements, tiny signatures of consent, because that is how knots tighten — one loop at a time, each one reasonable, each one too small to fight over.*** The light dimmed, fractionally, like a frown. ***Soon the loops will be too many to count. Soon it will not need his voice at all.***

"How do I stop it?" she whispered.

***You cannot. Not alone. Not with swords or prayers or sisterly love, though the last of those will matter more than you yet understand.*** The voice drew breath, though it had no lungs, and the whole chapel seemed to draw breath with it. ***Knots are untied by those who understand binding. There is a guild in this world that trades in impossible things — Vellum & Thorn, they call themselves, though they have called themselves other names in other centuries. They keep ledgers of every debt and bargain ever struck between mortal and otherwise. If anyone alive knows what binds your brother's crown, it is them. Go to them. Kneel, if you must. You seem to have a talent for it.***

Despite everything — despite the terror sitting on her chest like a cat too heavy to move — Rosalind felt a laugh escape her, small and cracked and real.

"You're mocking me."

***I am comforting you,*** said the god. ***Mocking comes later, once we know each other better.***

And then, before she could speak again, before she could ask the hundred questions crowding her throat — *why help me, what will it cost, what do you want* — the presence withdrew. Not vanished. Withdrawn, the way a tide goes out: gradually, reluctantly, leaving the whole chapel feeling emptier than empty, a space shaped exactly like something enormous that had briefly been there.

The sunlight was just sunlight again. The font was just rainwater and leaves. The pigeon, sensibly, had left.

Rosalind knelt alone in the ruin for a long time.

She should have felt hope. Some part of her did — a wild, singing part, the part that had knelt down in the first place, the part that had spent a month watching her brother fade behind his own eyes and had refused, absolutely refused, to hold his funeral while he still breathed. There was a road. There was a guild. There was a *thing to do*, and after weeks of helpless dread, having a thing to do felt almost like being handed bread after a famine.

But underneath the hope, colder and quieter, sat three thoughts she could not shake.

First: the god had known about the crown. Had known details — the loops, the small promises, the thinning. Which meant it had been watching. Which meant it had been watching for some time, and had waited until a desperate woman knelt in a ruin to mention it.

Second: it had asked for nothing. Nothing! Every favor in her experience had a price, and the ones that claimed otherwise were simply pricing you differently. A power that old, answering prayers for free, was either lying or lonely, and she genuinely could not decide which frightened her more.

Third — and this was the thought she carried out of the chapel like a stone in her shoe — it had called her *small sister*. Warmly. Famously. As though it already knew her.

As though, perhaps, it had been listening longer than one prayer.

Rosalind rose on stiff knees, brushed the dust from her skirts, and stood a moment in the pale rectangle of light where the altar used to be. Somewhere beyond the hills lay the capital, and her brother, and the crown that whispered. Somewhere farther lay a guildhouse full of ledgers and impossible things, and whatever price they would ask, which would certainly not be nothing.

"Well," she said aloud, to the empty chapel, because speaking aloud made things true and she needed this to be true. "I suppose I'm going to Vellum & Thorn."

The chapel, being dead, did not answer.

But as she turned to go, climbing over the fallen tiles toward the door and the road and the rest of her life, she passed the rainwater font — and stopped.

The drowned leaves had arranged themselves while her back was turned. Neatly. Deliberately. Five of them, floating in a line upon the water, pointing — she tilted her head, checked against the late sun — east. Toward the lowlands. Toward the river road. Toward, unless she badly missed her guess, the guildhouse of Vellum & Thorn.

Rosalind looked at the leaves for a long moment.

"Thank you," she said carefully. "I think."

Then she pulled her hood up, became a commoner in the space of four practiced breaths, and walked east — terrified, hopeful, and carrying an answer she had never asked for, from a listener she had never meant to summon, who had promised her nothing except that mocking came later.

Which, she reflected as the ruin sank behind her, was somehow the most frightening promise of all.
