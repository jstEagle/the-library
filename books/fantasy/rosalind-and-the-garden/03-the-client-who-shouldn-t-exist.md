## 3. The Client Who Shouldn't Exist

There were three hundred and eleven rules in the Codex of Vellum & Thorn, and Casper Coldharbour had personally written the marginalia for two hundred and ninety of them. Rule the First — *everything owed must be written; nothing spoken aloud* — was not among those they had annotated, because Rule the First needed no annotation. It was the load-bearing wall of the whole house. You did not decorate a load-bearing wall.

So when the woman came in off Tallow Street smelling of rain and desperation and said, "I'd like to make an arrangement that can't be written down," Casper's pen stopped mid-stroke.

They were auditing, at the time, a trade of one memory of spring — a widow's, sold at winter prices, which was technically legal and morally the sort of thing that made Casper want to wash their hands with lye. The entry sat on the desk before them: *One (1) recollection of apricot blossoms, unbracketed, seller's own, condition good, minor weeping at edges.* Winter prices meant the guild paid her half what spring memories fetched, on the theory that people parted with warmth more cheaply when they were cold. It was sound economics. Casper hated it.

"Madam," Casper said, without looking up, "if it can't be written down, it can't be traded here."

"It can be written down," the woman said. "It just can't be *priced*."

Casper looked up then, because that was either ignorance or genius, and both were interesting.

She was dressed as a commoner, which is to say badly — a wool coat gone thin at the elbows, a shawl that had been mended by someone who loved her but could not sew. But she held herself like someone who had spent a lifetime being looked at, and there was mud of an expensive district on her boots. Her eyes were red-rimmed and steady, which is the most dangerous combination a pair of eyes can manage.

"The guild trades in impossible things," she said. "I've brought you one."

Casper set down the pen. "The last client who said that to me wanted to trade his shadow for a second chance at a conversation he'd fumbled thirty years ago. The shadow had opinions about this. It took four hours and a very awkward afternoon."

"I'm not trading," she said. "I'm offering payment. For a service I haven't requested yet." She laid something on the desk between them, and Casper's hand moved before their mind caught up — flat palm over the ledger, the instinctive gesture of a keeper protecting the record.

It was a favor. Not a metaphorical one. An actual favor, wrapped in brown paper like a loaf of bread, and through the paper Casper could feel it the way you feel a struck bell through a door: *something old, something patient, something that remembered when the world was younger and less certain of its own edges.*

"What is it?" Casper asked, hating the hunger in their own voice.

"That's rather the point," the woman said. "I don't entirely know. It answered me when nothing should have been listening anymore. And whatever it is — it owes me now. Or I owe it. The direction of the debt was never entirely clear." A small, tired smile. "Which I understand is your department."

Casper stared at the parcel. Three hundred and eleven rules, and not one of them covered this. There was a rule for trades of years, a rule for trades of names, a rule — added in a hurry after the Incident with the Margrave — forbidding the purchase or sale of anything still breathing inside its wrapping. But a favor from something old? The ledger had no column for it. The ledger had no *alphabet* for it.

"You said you haven't requested the service yet," Casper managed.

"I need the guild to save my brother."

"Your brother is ill?"

"My brother is king," the woman said, "and his crown has started whispering."

The fire in the grate popped. Somewhere upstairs, the guildmaster's floorboard creaked in the particular rhythm that meant she had been listening at the stairwell for some minutes and considered her dignity above pretending otherwise.

"Madam," Casper said carefully, "the guild does not involve itself in crowns. Crowns are debts that compound."

"And yet," she said, "you're still talking to me."

She had him there. Casper glanced at the ledger, at the neat columns where every impossible thing in the city had been reduced to figures and footnotes, and felt — for the first time in nineteen years of meticulous, honorable, suffocating service — the walls of the page pressing back against them.

"Name," Casper said, taking up the pen again. Purely procedural. "For the intake record."

"No."

"No?"

"Not mine. Not yet. If I give you my name, you'll write it down, and written things have weight, and I've recently learned how much weight a name can carry." She drew her shawl tighter. "Call me what you like. The woman with the parcel."

Casper wrote, in careful script: *Client known as 'the woman with the parcel.' Refuses naming. Boots too fine. Eyes worse.* Then, after a moment's hesitation, in smaller letters beneath: *Parcel hums.*

"I'm going to be honest with you," Casper said, capping the ink. "I am going to refuse you. Formally, kindly, and with a pamphlet."

"A pamphlet?"

"'So You Wish to Trade With Vellum & Thorn.' It has illustrations. The guildmaster insisted. I lost the argument about the font."

The woman almost laughed — he saw it start and die, strangled by whatever grief she'd carried in through the rain. "And why do you refuse?"

"Because favors from old things aren't currency, madam. They're hooks. Something that answers prayers when prayers shouldn't be answerable doesn't *give*, it *lends*. And everything it lends comes due." Casper tapped the ledger. "This book exists so that no debt in this city goes unwitnessed. But I cannot witness this one. I can't read its terms. A debt I can't read isn't a debt I can protect anyone from — including you. Especially you."

The woman was quiet a long moment. Rain ticked against the window. Then she said, softly, "You're the first person in three weeks who's tried to refuse me something."

"Is that rare?"

"Where I come from," she said, "nobody refuses royalty anything. That's rather the problem."

Upstairs, the floorboards creaked again — closer now, descending. Casper closed their eyes briefly, the way one does before weather arrives.

"Ah," Casper said. "She's coming."

* * *

Guildmaster Hesperine Thorn descended into the counting room the way fog descends into a valley: gradually, then all at once, then everywhere. She was seventy if she was a day, small and round and upholstered in grey velvet, with spectacles she used as props in negotiations rather than aids to vision, and she had run Vellum & Thorn for longer than Casper had been alive by a comfortable margin.

"Casper, darling, you're hovering over that poor woman like a gargoyle with a fountain pen." She swept past, patted the client's hand as though they were old friends, and settled into the chair across the desk with the sigh of a woman preparing to enjoy herself thoroughly. "Now. I couldn't help overhearing — and I mean that in the most professional sense — that we have a favor from something old on the premises?"

"Guildmaster," Casper said, "we know nothing about its terms—"

"We know it's *old*, dear, which means it predates our codex, which means—" she smiled, and her teeth were very white and very even, "—it falls outside our jurisdiction. Do you know how rarely anything falls outside our jurisdiction? I've waited forty years."

"That's precisely the problem—"

"Casper." She turned the full wattage of her attention on them, and behind the spectacles her eyes were not amused at all. "Who keeps the ledgers?"

Casper's jaw tightened. "I keep the ledgers."

"And whose handwriting is in them?"

"Mine."

"And who," Hesperine said gently, terribly, "taught you that a debt unwritten is a debt ungoverned? We don't get to choose what walks through that door, darling. We only get to choose whether it's *on the books*. Everything else is chaos wearing a nice coat." She turned back to the client, warm as fresh bread. "Now then. Tell me about this brother of yours."

Casper stood very still. This was the shape of the guildmaster's genius, and Casper had always admired it from inside its shelter: the codex was not a moral document, it was a *net*, and Hesperine would net anything that moved. Rules were not there to tell her what to want. They were there to make sure that whatever she wanted got recorded properly afterward.

But watching her lean toward the humming parcel with forty years of appetite in her face, Casper felt the first hairline crack run through the load-bearing wall.

Because the rules had always felt like safety. That was the trick of it. Three hundred and eleven rules, and Casper had hidden inside them like a child under blankets, telling themselves that meticulousness was the same as virtue, that if every debt was witnessed then no one — least of all Casper Coldharbour — could ever be complicit in anything. Write it down, and it's not yours. Record it, and you're innocent. You're just the pen.

The woman with the parcel had walked in and refused to be written down, and suddenly Casper could see the blankets for what they were.

"Terms," Hesperine was saying, producing a fresh folio. "We'll draft terms. A favor owed by an unnamed party to the guild, redeemable once, upon request, in service of the client's stated aim. Vague, admittedly, but vague things can be collateralized, dear, vagueness is just value we haven't itemized yet—"

"Guildmaster." Casper's voice came out quieter than intended, but it stopped the room anyway. Both women looked at them. "Rule Two Hundred and Four. No trade may be accepted whose counterparty cannot be identified. Rule One Hundred and Nineteen. No instrument may be accepted whose redemption conditions are unknowable. Rule Sixty-Three — I *wrote* Rule Sixty-Three — no trade may proceed when the accepting officer believes, in good conscience, that acceptance will cause harm the ledger cannot later remedy." Casper swallowed. "I believe. In good conscience. All three."

Hesperine regarded them over the spectacles for a long, silken moment. The fire popped. The parcel hummed its low, patient note, like a hive dreaming.

"Oh, *darling*," she said at last, and there was real fondness in it, which somehow made it worse. "That's why I keep you. That beautiful, rigid little spine." She turned the folio toward herself and uncapped her own pen — the bad pen, the ceremonial one, the one that meant business. "Fortunately for everyone, Rule One gives final authority over acceptance to the office of the Guildmaster. You may log your objection. In fact, I insist. Objections are wonderful for liability."

Casper logged the objection. Their hand barely shook. It was the small victories.

The contract, when Hesperine finished drafting it, was a masterpiece of elegant nonsense — clauses nested in clauses, definitions referring to definitions, until the words *favor*, *old*, and *owed* had been stretched so far they ceased to mean anything and became pure legal music. The client signed with an X, refusing even that much of herself. Hesperine countersigned. The parcel was placed in the guild's deepest drawer, in a box lined with lead foil, which Casper privately doubted would do anything except annoy it.

Then the woman rose to leave, and paused at the door, and looked back at Casper with those red-rimmed, steady eyes.

"You tried to refuse me," she said. "Thank you. Whatever happens — thank you for that."

"Madam," Casper said, "what's your brother's name? For my own records. Off the books."

She hesitated. Then, so quietly the fire nearly drowned it:

"Ask instead what his crown says at night. When you hear it — and you will hear it — remember that someone tried to refuse you something once, and it didn't help."

The door closed behind her. The rain went on ticking at the window. In the drawer downstairs, faint as a heartbeat heard through a wall, the parcel hummed on.

Casper stood a long while in front of the open ledger, pen poised over the new entry, and found they could not decide what to write. *Favor, one (1), provenance unknown, condition: humming.* It sounded absurd. Worse — it sounded *afraid*, and ledgers were not permitted to be afraid.

In the end they wrote only: *Accepted over keeper's formal objection. May the record show we knew.*

Below that, after a moment, in letters smaller than any they had used in nineteen years of service:

*May the record also show what knowing was worth.*

Upstairs, Hesperine was already pouring herself a celebratory sherry, and Casper climbed the stairs to drink one too, and to stand at the window watching the rain, feeling — distinctly, unmistakably, for the first time — the crack running through the wall, and hearing, very faintly, from somewhere beneath the floorboards, the sound of something old beginning, patiently, to count.
