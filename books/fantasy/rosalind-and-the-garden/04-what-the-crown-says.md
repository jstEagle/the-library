## 4. What the Crown Says

Rosalind had learned three things in her first week back at court under the name "Mistress Wren of Ashford," seamstress to nobody in particular: that the palace laundry ran hotter than any forge and smelled of lye and secrets; that Lord Chancellor Vey's wig was, on close inspection, two wigs stacked for height; and that her brother the king had not laughed — truly laughed — since the winter solstice.

She catalogued these findings the way she'd once catalogued Aldous's childhood crimes against their mother's rosebushes: privately, thoroughly, and with a growing ache beneath the humor.

The third finding was the one that mattered.

* * *

Court audiences were held in the Hall of Amber Light, so named because the windows caught the morning sun and turned everything the color of honey and old promises. Rosalind stood among the petitioners' attendants near the eastern wall, a basket of mending on her hip as camouflage, watching her brother hold court from the throne beneath the canopy of black oak.

He looked well. That was the terrible part. The physicians said his color had returned, his appetite improved, his sleep deep and untroubled. He had grown quieter, yes, more deliberate, but everyone agreed this was maturity settling over him like a well-cut coat. King Aldous, twenty-six years old and four years crowned, had finally become serious.

Rosalind knew better. She had received his letters — the real ones, smuggled out by a kitchen maid who owed her more than money could say — and the letters were wrong. Not the words themselves. The words were Aldous's: dry, self-deprecating, prone to complaining about the quality of the palace cheese with the gravity of a man discussing war. But the handwriting drifted. Some lines pressed hard enough to tear the page. Others trailed off mid-sentence, the ink thinning as though the hand writing them had forgotten what it was doing halfway through.

And once, at the bottom of the last letter, in a script she did not recognize at all — small, precise, patient — someone had written:

*Tell your sister the crown fits better every day.*

She had burned that letter in the guildhouse hearth and watched Casper Coldharbour watch her do it, the ledger-keeper's face giving away nothing except, perhaps, a flicker of something that might have been sympathy if you squinted and believed in miracles.

Now she watched from the wall as the king received the day's petitions.

A delegation of wool merchants first, complaining of tariffs. Aldous heard them out, asked two sharp questions, granted a partial relief. Normal. His voice was his voice — warm, a little rough at the edges, the voice that had read her stories through fevers when they were children.

Then came the matter of the miller of Harrow Fen, accused of shorting the crown's measure.

And Aldous began to argue with himself.

It started subtly. He said, "The evidence seems thin," in his own voice — and then, before the chancellor could respond, he said, "The evidence is sufficient," in a different one.

Rosalind's blood went cold, and then colder, because it wasn't merely different. It was *layered*. As though two voices occupied the same throat, one speaking a half-breath behind the other, like an echo that had arrived early. The second cadence was lower, unhurried, with the particular patience of something that had never once needed to hurry in its existence.

The hall didn't notice. Or rather — Rosalind scanned the faces — most of the hall didn't notice. Courtiers had a genius for not noticing things that endangered their positions. But she caught Lord Vey's eyes flick toward the throne and away again, quick as a card sharp's. And she saw the captain of the guard, Ser Brandt, shift his weight and stare very hard at a point six inches to the left of the king.

So they noticed. They had noticed for some time. And they had decided, collectively, silently, without ever speaking of it, that noticing was as far as they would go.

"The miller will be fined," said the layered voice, both cadences agreeing now, which was somehow worse than the argument. "Half measure taken is half measure owed. Let the ledger be balanced."

*A ledger,* thought Rosalind. *It talks like a ledger.*

The miller was dragged off weeping his thanks, because a fine was survivable and mercy was not, apparently, to be questioned. The next petitioner stepped forward — a widow, by her dress, holding a folded petition in hands stained with garden dirt.

And Aldous smiled.

It was a beautiful smile. It reached his eyes. That was the horror of it: it was a perfect smile, executed flawlessly, and Rosalind had spent twenty years learning the difference between her brother's smiles and performances of smiling. His real ones always arrived crooked, left side first, preceded by a small exhale through the nose, as though laughter had to be let out of a side door. This smile was symmetrical. This smile had been *practiced*, or worse, *calculated*, the way a merchant calculates a price.

"Come forward, goodmother," said the thing wearing her brother. "What is owed to you?"

*Owed.* There it was again. Her skin prickled.

The widow knelt. "Your Majesty, my son was taken by the press-gangs in autumn. I only ask — I only ask to know where he lies. Whether he lives."

Silence moved through the hall like a draft. Press-gangs were the navy's shame, officially discontinued two years ago. Everyone knew they continued anyway. Everyone knew not to ask.

The king tilted his head. And Rosalind watched — actually watched, with the awful clarity of love — as her brother surfaced inside his own body. She couldn't have described how she knew. Something in the set of the shoulders eased. The symmetrical smile faltered into something crooked and human and exhausted.

"Marta?" he said quietly. "Widow Marta of the tannery row?"

The widow looked up, astonished. "You — Your Majesty remembers me?"

"I bought apples from your stall when I was nine and ran off without paying." A breath of almost-laughter, left side first. "I've owed you for seventeen years. Sergeant!" His voice cracked into command, into the old Aldous, the one who'd sworn at his coronation that no subject would ever be a number to him. "Find the muster rolls from the autumn levies. Every name. If her son lives, he comes home with pay owed and an apology from the crown. If he doesn't—" the voice caught, "—she'll have the truth of it, and a pension, and my regret in writing."

Murmurs of approval rippled through the hall. Here was a just king! Here was a king who remembered!

Then the other voice returned, and it did not raise itself. It simply settled into the space behind Aldous's words like sediment into still water.

"How generous," it said, gently, so gently. "But generosity is a debt, little king. And debts are mine to keep."

Aldous's jaw tightened. For a moment — one long, suspended moment — he wrestled visibly, a man arm-wrestling himself across a table. Sweat beaded at his temple. His right hand, resting on the throne's arm, curled into a fist and trembled.

"You will note it," the layered voice continued, pleasant as a clerk, "in the record. All kindnesses noted. All accounts kept current. The kingdom keeps such sloppy books, Majesty. Someone must."

"Yes," Aldous said, through his teeth, and it was impossible to tell which of them had agreed.

The audience moved on. Petition after petition, and Rosalind tracked the pattern now, sick with understanding. Every ruling came wrapped in the language of balance. Every mercy was immediately converted into an entry. The orphan given bread owed labor in spring. The pardoned thief owed service. The forgiven tax owed gratitude, and gratitude, the voice observed mildly, was a form of currency that compounded.

Her brother was being turned into a ledger. Not destroyed — *kept*. Maintained in good order. Audited daily.

*I went to a guild of impossible trades,* she thought, *and begged strangers to save him, and they took the job, and meanwhile the answer was here all along, sitting on a throne, doing arithmetic.*

* * *

The audience ended at noon. The court dispersed in its slow, gossiping eddy, and Rosalind slipped along the eastern wall toward the servants' passage, heart hammering, mind already composing the coded letter she'd send south to the guildhouse.

She made it eleven steps.

" Mistress Wren."

The voice stopped her feet before her mind caught up. It was the king's voice. It was also, unmistakably, not.

She turned. The throne room had emptied except for a handful of guards at the far doors, too distant to hear anything, close enough to see nothing amiss. Aldous stood at the base of the dais steps, alone, the crown catching the amber light. He had never looked less like a king and more like a lamp — something lit from within by a flame that wasn't his.

"You see us," the voice said. Not a question. "You have been seeing us since the second day. You count. You compare. You write everything down in that clever head of yours." A pause, and then, with something horribly like approval: "You would have made an excellent keeper."

Rosalind's mouth was dry. Her basket of mending suddenly weighed as much as a coffin. "Your Majesty," she managed, dropping into a curtsy deep enough to hide her face. "I don't—"

"Do not insult us both with the seamstress." The word *seamstress* was pronounced with delicate distaste, as though tasting a wine gone off. "You kneel in ruined chapels and ask dead gods for favors. You travel with a swordspeaker who has forgotten how to pray. You are the sister who writes letters in lemon juice and sends them by kitchen maids." The head tilted, Aldous's familiar gesture rendered strange by the stillness around it. "We know what you are, Rosalind Sterling."

Hearing her name in that doubled voice was like hearing it spoken over her own grave.

"We are not angry," the voice continued, and it sounded — this was the worst of it — *sincere*. "We admire diligence. We honor contracts. And you, above all people, should understand: nothing in this arrangement is theft. He signed. Read the terms sometime, if you can find them. They are quite fair. Everything is accounted for. Nothing is wasted. Is that not justice? Is that not exactly the world you petitioners always beg for — a world where every debt is paid?"

"It isn't justice," Rosalind said, before fear could stop her tongue. "Justice weighs. It doesn't just collect."

The silence that followed had texture. Weight. Somewhere far above, a raven crossed the amber windows and its shadow slid over the throne like a finger drawn down a page.

"Aldous," she whispered, stepping closer, past sense, past safety. "Aldous, it's me. It's Roz. Look at me."

For a heartbeat, nothing. Then his eyes changed — the focus sharpening, the glassy patience cracking — and it was her brother looking out of his own face, drowning and furious and terrified.

"Roz." Barely air. "Run. Don't come back. Don't pay anything, don't promise anything, don't sign *anything* — it gets you through what you owe, do you understand, it can't touch you unless there's a debt, it can't—"

His hand shot out and gripped her wrist. Hard. Too hard. And the eyes that fixed hers were no longer his at all.

"He forgets himself," the voice said softly. "There is no *unless*, sister-of-the-king. There is always a debt. You were born owing — a life is borrowed, a breath is interest. The only question is to whom the account belongs." The grip loosened, finger by finger, almost tender. "Presently, everything belongs to the crown."

It released her. Stepped back. Became, in the space of a breath, a tired young king rubbing the bridge of his nose, murmuring something about the tedium of audiences, gesturing vaguely for his guards.

Rosalind walked out of the Hall of Amber Light on legs that belonged to someone else. In the corridor beyond, she leaned against cold stone and breathed until the shaking stopped, and then she did what she had been raised to do, what she was apparently born to do, what the thing on the throne had just unknowingly praised her for.

She counted.

One: it spoke of a contract, signed, findable. Two: it feared nothing except unpaid accounts — which meant it could be *owed into weakness*, or perhaps bargained with, or perhaps, if she was very clever and very lucky, *overdrawn*. Three: it knew her name, her methods, her allies. Which meant someone at court was feeding it. Which meant—

Which meant the letter she'd planned to write was no longer enough. Words traveled slowly. Debts collected quickly.

Four: Casper Coldharbour had told her, the night she hired the guild, in that flat ledger-keeper's voice: *Everything owed must be written. Nothing spoken aloud. That is the whole law, and it has kept the world from ending twice.*

The thing in the crown kept ledgers too.

Two kinds of bookkeeping, she thought, pushing off the wall and walking fast toward the servants' stairs. One bound the world together. The other was eating her brother alive.

She needed to know the difference. And she needed Casper Coldharbour in the capital before the bells rang vespers — because she had seen, in the last flicker of her brother's surfacing, where his eyes had darted in warning.

Not to the door. Not to the guards.

To the floor. To the old sealed hatch beneath the dais, older than the palace, that every servant swore had been locked for a hundred years.

The king hadn't been telling her to run.

He'd been telling her where to dig.
