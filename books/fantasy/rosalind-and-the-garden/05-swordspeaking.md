## 5. Swordspeaking

The trouble with roads, Casper had long ago decided, was that they went places.

Ledgers stayed put. Ledgers sat quietly on their shelves in Vellum & Thorn, smelling of beeswax and old decisions, and if a ledger ever tried anything untoward — a page curling, an ink blot where no ink had been spilled — you simply weighted it down with the brass hand-bell and moved on with your life. Roads, by contrast, insisted on winding through countryside full of hedges, ditches, and other people. Casper disliked all three categories equally, but only one of them could hide six armed men.

They were three days out of the guildhouse and two days from the capital, traveling in a party of four: Casper; Rosalind, who was pretending very hard not to be royal; Tam, the guild's mule-driver, who had once been bitten by a contract and never quite trusted paper again; and Wren, the apprentice, who was currently riding point and humming.

Casper knew about the bandits before Wren did.

Not because of any gift. There was nothing mystical in it — just forty years of reading entries, and an ambush is only an entry written in advance. The hedge on the left was too neatly untrimmed for this stretch of road. A woodcutter's cart stood abandoned with its axe still in the block, which no honest woodcutter allows, an axe being worth more than most woodcutters' winters. And the crows — the crows were waiting. Crows that are passing through fly crooked lines. Crows that are waiting sit like creditors.

"Rosalind," Casper said mildly, "when I tell you to get behind the mule, get behind the mule."

She looked up sharply. She was quick — quicker than her brother, perhaps, though Casper kept that observation filed under entries not yet written. "How many?"

"Six. Possibly seven. One in the ditch ahead, two in the hedge, the rest behind us on foot. They've done this before."

"And you can tell that from *crows*?"

"From everything. Everything keeps books, if you know how to read them." Casper reined up and let the silence do some work. "It's the axe that gives it away, mostly. Nobody leaves their axe."

Tam sighed the sigh of a man who had made this exact journey with Casper twice before. "Shall I do the voice?" he asked.

"You may do the voice."

Tam cleared his throat, straightened on the bench of the cart, and addressed the empty road in the flat, bored cadence of guild procedure: "*Attention, persons concealing themselves at roadside. Be advised that the party before you travels under guild seal. Any goods taken from this party will constitute debt, and all debts are recorded. Your names will be entered. Your descendants will be contacted.*"

A pause. Then, from the hedge, a laugh.

Out of it came a big man with a beard like an unpruned orchard and a crossbow held with the easy confidence of someone who had never read a contract in his life. Five more rose around him out of the greenery, and a sixth climbed sheepishly from the ditch, brushing mud off his front as though he'd hoped to be mistaken for scenery.

"Guild seal," the big man said, spitting to one side. "Paper doesn't stop iron, friend."

"No," Casper agreed. "But it does stop me from being merciful on the record." They dismounted slowly, hands visible, the way you approach a frightened animal or a debtor. "Here is what will happen. You will take the toll you've decided we owe — call it a tenth, call it half, I'll write it down either way as *toll, paid under duress* — and we will go our ways, and none of you will be pursued, because honestly, none of you are worth the ink."

"That's a generous offer," Rosalind murmured beside them.

"It's not generosity. It's accounting. Chasing six men across three counties costs the guild eleven crowns a day plus incidentals. Letting them rob us costs—" Casper glanced at Tam's cart "—perhaps nine crowns, plus the mule's dignity. The arithmetic favors patience."

The big man laughed again, and it should have ended there. It would have ended there, in a tidy world. But the young one from the ditch — young enough that his beard was more of an apology than a feature — got excited. Young ones always did. He saw the lady in plain clothes and the fat purse at her belt, and he saw the old clerk talking numbers instead of begging, and something in him decided that today he would matter.

He grabbed Rosalind's wrist.

Three things happened very quickly.

First: Tam stopped humming.

Second: Casper's hand went to the sword at their hip — an old sword, older than Casper's employment, older than the guild itself by some accounts, carried the way a priest carries a relic they privately doubt. It had not been drawn in nineteen years. It had not been *spoken to* in nineteen years.

Third: the young bandit learned what it felt like when a woman who had grown up dodging court daggers broke his grip and put him in the ditch again, harder.

"Enough," said the big man, and the crossbow came up — not at Rosalind, not at Tam, but at Casper, because even bandits understand hierarchy, and the clerk was plainly the dangerous sort. "Old one. Hands wide. Now."

And here is what must be understood about Casper Coldharbour, last oath-bound swordspeaker of an order that had been dust for a generation: they did not want what happened next. They had spent nineteen years arranging their life so that it would never happen next. The oath-craft was not a skill. Skills rust politely. The oath-craft was a vow that lived in the marrow, and vows do not care whether you have kept your end of the bargain quiet. It had been sleeping the way debts sleep — patiently, accruing interest.

The crossbow leveled. Casper's hand closed on the grip.

"*Wake,*" Casper said.

The word was not loud. That was the horror and the beauty of it — the whole art had never been loud. It was a word shaped like a key turning in a lock, and every syllable of it had been sworn by dead men and dead women into the metal of the world, and the world still honored the paperwork.

The blade woke.

Steel sang inside the scabbard — not a ring but a *voice*, a thin bright note that bent upward into something almost like speech, and then Casper drew, and there was no scabbard anymore and no sheathing the moment. Light ran up the edge like dawn deciding to hurry. The air changed pressure. The crows lifted off the hedge all at once, having finished their reading.

What happened after that took four seconds and Casper remembered each of them for years.

The first second: the big bandit's crossbow bolt left the string and met the waking blade midair, and the bolt did not deflect — it *parted*, split lengthwise along the grain of its own making, both halves spinning past Casper's shoulders like startled birds. The oath-craft does not block. It finds the line along which a thing already wants to come apart, and it agrees with the thing.

The second second: Casper moved — faster than a body that age had any right to move, because the body was not doing the moving alone. The sword pulled, gently, the way a dance partner leads. Three strides. The flat of the blade touched the big man's shoulder.

The third second: the big man sat down. There was no other word for it. His legs simply concluded the argument was over. The crossbow clattered onto the road.

The fourth second: Casper stood in the center of six armed men with a lit blade held low and level, and spoke the closing formula — the words every swordspeaker had spoken at the end of every drawing since the order's founding, the words meant to remind everyone present, including the speaker, that this was a last resort dressed in ceremony:

"*By oath drawn, by oath answered, by oath returned to sleep. What was owed is paid. What was broken is bound. Go, and be entered in mercy.*"

The light went out of the steel like a tide withdrawing.

Silence. The specific, ringing silence of a road that has just watched something impossible happen politely.

Then the big man, sitting in the dust, looked up at Casper with the expression of a man revising his entire theology, and said, "What in the nine hells *are* you?"

"A clerk," Casper said, and resheathed the sword.

* *

They rode on within the quarter hour, because Casper insisted on completing the transaction properly — the toll was negotiated down to a single crown and an apology, entered in the pocket ledger with witnesses (Tam and, after some hesitation, the big man himself, who signed with an X and then, visibly moved by the occasion, added a small flourish). The young one from the ditch rubbed his wrist and stared at Casper the whole time with the wide eyes of a boy who would tell this story at every tavern for the rest of his life, growing slightly less accurate with each telling.

"They say the swordspeakers all died at Redford Field," he blurted, as the party mounted up.

"They say a great many things," Casper said. "Most of them are in ledgers somewhere, which makes them technically true."

"What happened at Redford Field?"

Casper paused. Somewhere behind them, the crows resumed their crooked flying. "An ending," they said finally. "Endings are always better documented than beginnings. People sign endings more carefully."

They rode. The road bent north toward the capital, and for a mile nobody talked, which suited Casper fine.

It did not suit the sword.

Anyone else would have heard nothing. The blade rode silent at Casper's hip, cold and ordinary, a piece of well-kept antique steel. But the oath-craft, once woken, leaves a door open in the wielder — that was its price, the reason the order had taught restraint before technique, the reason the order had also, Casper noted with posthumous bitterness, taught neither well enough to survive. Through that door, faint as a voice through a wall, the blade began to talk.

It did not talk the way men talk. It talked the way contracts talk — in pressures, in leanings, in the sense of a sentence waiting to be finished. Casper had spent nineteen years not listening to it. Nineteen years of discipline, of filing the sword under *assets, dormant*, of treating the marrow-vow like a retired employee one does not invite to parties.

Today it had been woken, and woken things ask questions.

*You drew me,* it leaned. *You woke me for six men and a crossbow.*

"For one crossbow," Casper thought back, grimly, keeping their face pleasant for the benefit of Rosalind riding alongside. "There was only one bolt."

*Bolt. Yes. I remember bolts. I have drunk a great many of them.* A pause, in which Casper could feel the blade sorting through its memories the way an auditor sorts through receipts. *Redford Field. I remember Redford Field. There were four hundred of us awake that day, and we drank a war.*

"I know. I was there."

*I know you were there. I have your oath in my steel. That is why I remember you when I forget the others.* The pressure deepened, sharpened, became — unmistakably — a question. Not the question Casper dreaded. Worse. A bookkeeper's question. An *auditor's* question:

***Whose oath binds you now?***

Casper's hand tightened on the reins.

Because that was the wound under nineteen years of careful filing, wasn't it. The order was dead. The masters who had administered the oaths were ash or memory. The grand ledger of the Swordspeakers — the one that held every vow, every binding, every name — had burned at Redford Field along with the men and women whose names it held. Casper's oath remained, sworn into their own bones and into this blade, but the institution that gave it meaning was gone. Which meant, by every rule Casper had spent a lifetime enforcing upon others —

— the oath pointed at nothing. Or worse: it pointed wherever its holder chose. A debt with no creditor. An entry with no counter-signature. The one thing a guild clerk should find unbearable.

*Nobody's,* Casper thought back. *Nobody's. That's what it means to be the last.*

The blade considered this with the long, mineral patience of steel.

*That,* it leaned at last, *is the saddest entry I have ever been shown. An oath that binds no one binds its holder twice over. You wear it like a collar with the leash cut off, and you call the cut leash freedom.* A pause. *I liked the others better. They lied to themselves less beautifully.*

"Hush," Casper muttered aloud.

"Sorry?" said Rosalind.

"Not you. Talking to the inventory."

Rosalind glanced at the plain-scabbarded sword, then at Casper's face, and — being Rosalind, who noticed things the way her brother used to before the crown started noticing for him — she let it go, but Casper saw her file it. Saw the little motion of her eyes, the entry being written behind them. She was learning the craft already, and no one had taught her. It was either admirable or alarming. Casper had not yet decided.

* * *

They camped that night in the lee of a ruined waystation, an old toll-house of the guild's own building, back when roads belonged to institutions instead of whoever held the sharpest entry. Tam cooked. Wren — who had joined them outside the city gates with fresh instructions from the guildmaster and a look on his face that Casper had not yet found the right column for — took first watch, sitting atop the cart with his knees drawn up.

Rosalind settled beside the fire across from Casper, warming her hands, watching the flames with the particular intensity of a woman rehearsing a conversation she hadn't yet decided to have.

"Ask," Casper said.

"What happened back there? Truly. Not the ledger version."

Casper turned the sword across their knees and looked at it. Firelight moved along the fuller like a slow reader's finger.

"The order I belonged to — belong to, the grammar hasn't caught up — we were called swordspeakers because that's literally what we did. We spoke to blades, and blades obeyed, because the speaking was backed by an oath older than any kingdom. Every sword we woke carried a fragment of that oath in it. Wake enough swords, bind enough oaths, and you had..." Casper gestured vaguely. "Order. The kind with a capital O. For three hundred years, wars were short because both sides knew what a swordspeaker's presence meant. It meant the fighting would be brief and the aftermath would be *documented*."

"And then?"

"And then the gods died. Or slept. Or left — the records disagree, and I've read all of them." Casper's thumb traced the guard. "When the divine age ended, the oaths lost their anchor. Our authority came from something higher than kings, and when that something stopped answering... the order lasted another fifty years out of habit. Then Redford Field. Then me."

Rosalind was quiet for a moment. "Your sword asked you something. On the road. I saw your face change."

Casper should have denied it. A good clerk denies everything not in evidence. But the fire was warm, and the day had cracked something open, and there is a limit to how long a person can audit their own heart without filing a finding.

"It asked me whom my oath serves. Now. With everyone who could answer for it dead." Casper looked up. "And I realized I don't know. Which means, by the rules I've enforced my whole life, the oath is void. Which means everything I am is an entry in a ledger whose owner has died, waiting for probate that will never come."

Rosalind poked the fire. Sparks went up, and for a moment her face was all orange and shadow, and she looked — not like a princess in disguise. Like a woman doing sums.

"When I prayed in that chapel," she said, "and something answered — everyone tells me I should be afraid of it. The guildmaster says the gods coming back is a crisis. But do you know what I thought, in that moment? I thought: *finally, someone is keeping track.* Something heard me, something wrote me down, something intends to collect. It's terrible, I know. It's probably exactly the kind of terrible you people warn about." She looked up, firelight in her eyes. "But an account that no one holds — isn't that worse? Isn't that what actually destroys people? Being owed nothing, owing nothing, mattering to no ledger at all?"

Casper didn't answer. The blade, against their knee, leaned very slightly — the faintest pressure, like a colleague nodding across a desk.

Across the camp, Wren shifted on his watch perch, and Casper noticed — not for the first time that evening, but for the first time admitting it — that the boy kept glancing south. Back the way they'd come. Back toward the guildhouse. And that three times since noon he had taken out his own pocket journal and written something quickly, hiding the page with his palm, the way people hide pages when the entry isn't theirs to keep.

Wren had been with the guild six years. Casper had trained him. Casper had *signed* him.

*Everything keeps books,* Casper had told Rosalind that morning. *If you know how to read them.*

And the boy's book, tonight, was reading nervous.

Ahead lay the capital, the whispering crown, and whatever waited in the king's court. But behind them — between them and home — something was already being unwritten, page by hidden page, and Casper Coldharbour, keeper of every debt in three counties, had just realized they might be the only person alive who hadn't read it yet.
