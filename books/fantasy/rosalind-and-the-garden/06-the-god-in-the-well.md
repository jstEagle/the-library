## 6. The God in the Well

The village of Marrow's Bend announced itself by smell before sight — woodsmoke and wet earth and, underneath it all, something sweetly rotten, like fruit left too long in a cellar. Rosalind reined her borrowed mule to a stop at the crest of the hill and looked down at the place where the road bent around a cluster of thatched roofs and one very tall, very wrong wheat field.

"That's barley," Casper said beside her, squinting. "Growing in rows like soldiers. Barley doesn't do that."

"Nothing here does what it's supposed to," said Tam, the guild's hired carter, from the back of the cart. He had the flat tone of a man who had learned not to have opinions near his employers. "See the well? Middle of the green?"

Rosalind saw it. It was hard not to. Every other structure in the village leaned — houses sagging east, fences tilting west, even the church steeple listing like a drunk uncle — but the well stood perfectly, aggressively upright, its stones fitted so precisely they might have been grown rather than built.

"It's the only thing praying to itself," she murmured.

Casper gave her a look. In the three days since they'd left the guildhouse, she had learned to read those looks: this one meant *that was either very wise or very foolish, and I haven't decided which.*

"We need shelter," Casper said. "Storm's coming off the hills, and Tam's horse threw a shoe eight miles back. We go in, we buy beds and silence, we leave before dawn."

"And if the villagers need help?"

"The guild does not trade in charity." Casper said it without heat, the way one recites a rule memorized so long ago it has worn smooth as a river stone. Then, quieter: "But rules are for the guild. You're not guild."

Rosalind looked down at the village again. At the barley standing in ranks. At a woman hanging laundry who paused, mid-motion, to stare at nothing with an expression Rosalind recognized from mirrors — the look of someone listening to a voice no one else could hear.

"No," she agreed. "I'm not."

* * *

The inn was called The Generous Ear, which Rosalind found ominous within minutes of entering it, because the innkeeper, a broad woman named Hesta, would not stop talking and yet somehow said nothing at all. She talked about the weather — *fine weather, fine weather, the god likes fine weather* — about the price of wool — *steady prices, steady, the god likes steadiness* — and about the rooms upstairs — *clean beds, clean, you'll sleep well if you don't dream out loud.*

"I'm sorry," Rosalind said, setting down her cup. "If you don't *what*?"

Hesta's smile stayed exactly where it was while everything behind it went still. "Dream out loud, miss. Best keep your wishes small after dark. Small and dull. 'I wish for rain on Tuesday.' That sort of thing. Nothing with feeling in it."

"Why?" asked Casper, who had been silent in the corner, watching the room the way a hawk watches a field.

"Because He hears them." Hesta said *He* the way other people said *the king* or *the plague* — capitalized, dreaded, unavoidable. "He lives in the well. Old harvest god. Grandfather Wheat, my gran called Him. He slept for two hundred years and we forgot Him, and forgetting suited everyone fine. But then—" she spread her hands, helpless, "—then prayers started getting answered. And once a god starts answering, He gets... enthusiastic."

"Enthusiastic how?" Rosalind asked.

Hesta looked at her for a long moment. Then she jerked her chin toward the window, toward the ranked barley, and said, "Farmer Ollin wished his fields would feed his family forever. So now the barley grows whether he plants it or not, and it grows into the cellars, and through the floors, and last spring it grew up through his sleeping son's mattress, and when they pulled the boy free the barley apologized." Her voice cracked slightly on the last word. "It *apologized*, miss. In a little whispery voice. All night long. *Sorry, sorry, sorry.*"

The fire popped. Somewhere outside, thunder grumbled its opening argument.

"What does He want?" Rosalind asked quietly. "This god. What does He ask in return?"

"Nothing." Hesta laughed, and it was the worst sound Rosalind had heard in weeks. "That's the horror of it. He asks for nothing. He just *gives*. Whether you prayed or not. Whether you wanted it or not. Widow Marsh cried at her husband's grave and said she wished she could hear his voice again, and now every evening at dusk, the wind through her chimney says goodnight to her in his voice. Every evening. Forever. She's begged us to brick up the chimney but the bricks won't stay mortared. He won't let her stop grieving." Hesta wiped her eyes with her apron. "He loves us, miss. That's the trouble. He loves us like a child loves things — squeezing and squeezing."

* * *

Rosalind woke at midnight to the sound of wishing.

It came through the floorboards, faint and terrible: the whole village murmuring in its sleep, small dull wishes recited like catechisms. *Rain on Thursday. No fever this winter. Let the pig farrow easy.* Over and over, the same safe words, worn smooth from use. And beneath them, rising up through the well at the center of the green, something vast and delighted drinking it all in.

She lay in the dark and thought of Aldous.

Her brother had always been generous — too generous, their father used to say, giving away his portion at supper, giving away his coat, his time, his patience, until there was nothing left of him for himself. She remembered being nine and telling him to stop, and Aldous laughing and saying, *but Ro, giving is the best part.*

And now something wore his crown and answered prayers and loved with a child's squeezing hands, and she understood, lying in that creaking bed, that she was not looking at a stranger. She was looking at a preview. A rehearsal. Whatever sat in the well of Marrow's Bend was kin to whatever whispered behind her brother's eyes, and if she could learn how this one worked — how it could be bargained with, bounded, *stopped* — then perhaps...

She got up, dressed, and went downstairs.

Casper was already there, sitting by the dead fire, sharpening a knife that did not need sharpening. They looked at her and raised one eyebrow, which in Casper's economy of expression was an entire conversation.

"You couldn't sleep either," she said.

"I sleep badly in villages that pray in shifts." Casper slid the knife away. "You're going to the well."

"Yes."

"Alone."

"Yes."

"That's foolish."

"Probably." Rosalind pulled her cloak tight. "Come anyway?"

Something flickered across Casper's face — surprise, quickly buried, and under it something almost like pleasure. "Rules say I observe. Rules don't say I can't walk beside you while observing." They stood, took up a lantern. "Besides. Someone should write down what happens next. Everything owed must be written."

"Then write this," Rosalind said, pushing open the inn door into the rain-smelling dark. "*The lady went to make a bargain, and heaven help whatever answered.*"

* * *

The well at midnight was worse than the well at noon, because at noon it merely looked wrong, and at midnight it looked *attentive*.

Rosalind stood at its lip and felt the sensation of being watched from below — not menacingly, not yet, but with the fixed, hungry delight of a dog watching a hand approach with food. The water far down caught the lantern light and held it, gleaming, and she had the distinct impression the gleam was a pupil.

"Hello," she said. Her voice sounded very small against the dark. "I'm told you're listening."

The water shivered.

"My name is Rosalind. I'm traveling to the capital. My brother is sick — sick in a way I think you'd understand, because I think something like you is living in his crown." She swallowed. "I came to ask you a question, and I'm not going to wish for anything, because I've heard what your gifts cost."

The well was silent. Then, from everywhere and nowhere — from the barley in the fields, from the chimney of the widow's house, from the ground itself — came a voice like a hundred harvests sighing: warm, golden, patient, and utterly without malice, which made it infinitely worse.

*You are afraid of Me,* it said, sounding wounded. *They tell stories. I only give. I only ever give.*

"You give, and you never stop, and you never ask what people actually need." Rosalind kept her voice level. "Farmer Ollin's son sleeps in fear of your barley. Widow Marsh begs you to let her rest. Do you know what they need?"

*I know what they WISHED—*

"People wish stupidly!" The words burst out of her before she could dress them up. "People wish from grief and hunger and 3 a.m. despair, and they wish for the wrong things, and a kind god — a *good* god — would know that. Would listen past the wish to the wound underneath. You're not doing that. You're taking their words literally because literal is easier, and calling it love."

Silence. Long, grain-heavy silence. Rain began to fall, soft and tentative, as though the sky itself were leaning in.

Then the voice came again, smaller. Almost petulant. *No one taught Me otherwise. The old priests are dust. The rites are lost. I am doing what I remember, the way I remember it, alone, for two hundred years, and then suddenly they pray again and I am SO HAPPY and I do not know how to be happy correctly.*

And there it was. Rosalind closed her eyes. *Giving is the best part, Ro.* Aldous at nine years old, hollowing himself out with kindness, because no one had ever taught him where generosity ended and self-destruction began.

Behind her, she heard Casper shift — heard the soft scratch of a nib on paper. Taking notes. Writing it all down. *Everything owed must be written.*

"All right," Rosalind said. She opened her eyes and looked straight down into the gleam. "Here's my offer. Not a wish. An offer. You want to love correctly? Then learn limits. Release Ollin's fields — let the barley grow only where planted. Let Widow Marsh grieve in quiet. Stop granting anything spoken after dark. Can you do that?"

*What will You give Me in return?* The voice had gone sly, but gently sly, like a child learning to haggle over sweets. *Everything must be traded. Even the old ones said so. What do YOU have?*

Rosalind breathed out slowly. This was the moment. She knew it the way you know a stair is missing in the dark.

"My future," she said. "Collateral. If you keep these terms for one full year — if you learn restraint, if you hold to the bargain — then in one year's time, I'll return, and I'll teach you myself. Properly. The old rites, rewritten. How to listen past the wish. How to stop when stopping is kinder. I'll give you a year of my life's labor, freely given, and in exchange you'll give this village back its peace."

*And if You break Your promise?*

"Then take the year anyway. Take ten. Take whatever future I have." She heard Casper's pen stop moving. "That's the trade. Write it however you like. I'll sign."

The well hummed. The rain thickened. And Rosalind felt something settle over her shoulders like a mantle — heavy, cold, and strangely right — the weight of a debt she had chosen, which was a different thing entirely from any debt she'd ever carried before.

*A year,* the god said at last, wonderingly. *A human will teach Me. ...Yes. Yes, I accept. Tell them. Tell them all. Tell them they may sleep.*

The gleam in the water dimmed to something almost gentle. Across the green, in the widow's house, Rosalind saw candlelight flare and heard — faintly, impossibly — a door she hadn't known was shut swing open somewhere inside the world.

* * *

They left before dawn, as planned. Tam loaded the cart in the drizzle, muttering about shoes and omens. At the edge of the village, Farmer Ollin came running barefoot through the mud, waving his hat, half-laughing and half-crying, shouting that the barley had stopped at the fence line, stopped like a dog called home, and that his son had slept a whole night without whispers.

Hesta pressed a basket of bread into Rosalind's hands and gripped her wrists hard. "You come back in a year, miss. He'll be waiting. He's already practicing saying no — you can hear Him muttering it to Himself down the well. *No, no, no.* Like a psalm."

Rosalind climbed onto the cart. Casper handed her up, then paused, studying her with that long, ledger-keeping gaze.

"You realize what you did back there," they said. "You negotiated with a god. Untrained. Unbound. Using yourself as currency." A beat. "The guild trains people for decades and most of them still die in that exact conversation."

"I had a good teacher," Rosalind said. "Three days of watching you refuse to flinch."

Casper looked away first, which Rosalind suspected was rare, and muttered something that might have been *rules are for the guild* under their breath.

The cart rolled east through the rain-washed morning, toward the capital, toward the crown, toward the brother who waited at the end of all her ledgers. Rosalind ate the villagers' bread and watched the hills unroll and tried not to count the cost of what she'd signed.

She was not entirely successful. But for the first time since the night in the ruined chapel, the fear in her chest had a shape she could hold — and holding it, she found she could also aim it.

"Casper," she said, as the walls of the capital rose hazy on the horizon. "Tell me everything the guild knows about crowns."

Casper was quiet for a long moment. When they finally spoke, their voice had gone carefully, deliberately neutral — the voice of someone reading from a page they wished were blank.

"That depends," they said, "on how much you already suspect. Because the oldest entry in our first ledger — the one nobody's allowed to read — begins with a crown. And I'm starting to think someone's been tearing those pages out before anyone else can find them."

The road stretched ahead, long and gray, and somewhere behind them a god practiced saying no, and somewhere ahead a king argued with himself in two voices, and Rosalind Sterling rode between them, carrying a debt of one year and a question with teeth:

*Who was tearing out the pages — and what did the missing words say?*
