## 7. Entries Missing

The oldest ledger in the guildhouse of Vellum & Thorn was kept on the third floor, behind a door that only opened for someone who had washed their hands twice and meant it.

This was not a superstition. It was a rule, written into the founding charter by some long-dead keeper who had apparently learned the hard way that people who came straight from handling money also came with money's fingerprints all over them, and old paper remembered what it touched. Casper Coldharbour had been washing their hands and meaning it for eleven years now, and the door had never once failed to open, which was more than could be said for most of the promises Casper had ever heard.

The ledger itself — the *First Ledger*, though no one called it that out loud, because calling it that out loud felt like interrupting a funeral — sat on a lectern of black oak under a glass case that was more ceremonial than protective. It was bound in leather gone the color of dried blood, its corners capped in tarnished silver, and it was so large that opening it required both hands, one knee, and a small private apology.

Casper performed all three.

"Good morning," they said to it, because you did. Not because the ledger answered — it didn't, as far as anyone knew — but because the first rule of the guild was that everything owed must be written, and Casper had privately extended this to mean that everything owed must be *acknowledged*, and they owed this book their entire profession, their entire craft, and most of whatever passed for their soul.

The ledger fell open to the ribbon marker, somewhere in the middle years, where the entries were still written in the beautiful looping hand of Keeper Amaranth Voss: *Received, one debt of gratitude from the widow Hessel, payable in kind. Paid out, one memory of rain, to the farmer Dole, who wept upon receiving it and asked if he might have another.*

Casper smiled at that. Farmer Dole again. The man had come back four times for memories of rain. There was an entry three pages later recording his fifth visit, and beside it, in Voss's hand, a note: *Referred him to the weather. Free of charge. He seemed disappointed.*

That was the thing about the First Ledger that no one told new apprentices. It wasn't really a record of trades. It was a record of *people* — of everything they'd been desperate enough to sell and foolish enough to buy, and of the keepers who'd sat where Casper sat and judged, line by line, what a human life could be broken into and sold for without breaking the human.

Casper turned back toward the beginning, because today was audit day, and audit day meant going back to the roots.

The guild's rules were simple, which is not the same as easy:

*Everything owed must be written.*
*Nothing spoken aloud.*
*No entry may be altered once witnessed.*

Three rules. Eleven years. Casper had never broken any of them, and had watched at least six colleagues break each one at least once, and had developed strong opinions about the relationship between rules and people, opinions which they kept, like everything else, carefully unspoken.

They turned to the earliest pages.

And stopped.

* * *

There is a particular feeling that comes over a person who has spent eleven years among numbers when a number is wrong. It is not alarm, exactly. Alarm is loud. This is quieter — a small cold hand laid flat against the back of the neck, a voice that doesn't speak so much as *notice*.

Page forty-one ended mid-sentence.

Not mid-sentence in the way of a keeper interrupted by fire or invasion or lunch. Mid-sentence in the way of a page *torn out*. The stub of it remained in the binding, ragged-edged, and the sentence on page forty ran to the edge of the leaf and simply — stopped. *And the swordspeaker Ondrell came before the god of the well and spoke to it the word that—*

Gone.

Casper stood very still for a long moment, hands flat on either side of the open book, breathing the way you breathe when you're trying to convince your own body that nothing is wrong.

Then they began, methodically, to count.

The First Ledger was supposed to run to three hundred and twelve leaves. Casper knew this the way they knew their own heartbeat, because they had counted before — every year on audit day, every year the same number, because the first rule of auditing is that you count even the things you are certain of, *especially* the things you are certain of.

They counted.

Two hundred and ninety-six.

Sixteen pages. Sixteen pages missing from the oldest book in the guildhouse, torn out cleanly, close to the spine, by someone who knew how to do it without cracking the binding — which narrowed the field considerably, because there were perhaps four people alive with hands that careful, and Casper was one of them, and they were fairly sure they hadn't done it.

Fairly sure. Which was, they reflected grimly, exactly the sort of certainty a person should never put in a ledger.

* * *

By noon, Casper had done three things.

First, they had closed the ledger, washed their hands twice, and left the third floor walking at exactly their normal pace, because whoever had taken those pages might be watching, and guilt has a walk, and Casper refused to have it.

Second, they had made tea, drunk none of it, and stared out the guildhouse window at the gray street below while the tea went cold and judgmental in its cup.

Third, they had begun to think about what those sixteen pages had held.

Because here was what every apprentice learned in their first month, recited like a catechism: *Once, the gods walked openly, and answered prayers freely, and freely is the problem. A prayer answered is a bargain struck, whether you know the terms or not. The age of gods ended when the last order of swordspeakers spoke the Unbinding Word, and the guild of Vellum & Thorn was founded in the same year, to keep the ledgers clean, so that no god would ever again find a crack in a mortal bargain wide enough to climb through.*

That was the story. That was the whole story, tidy as a balanced column.

But sixteen pages had been torn from the middle of the account, right where the story got to the interesting part — right where a swordspeaker named Ondrell stood in front of a god and opened their mouth to end the world or save it, depending on which side of the ledger you kept your accounts.

Someone in this house did not want that part read.

Casper set down the cold tea and went looking for Wren.

* * *

Wren was Casper's apprentice, seventeen years old, all elbows and earnestness, currently stationed at the front counter pretending to catalog a shipment of bottled lullabies that had arrived from the coast and smelled faintly of salt and other people's mothers.

"How," Casper said, "is the cataloguing."

"Going great," said Wren, who had been holding the same bottle for approximately ten minutes and reading the label with the fixed intensity of a man trying to memorize it before it testified against him. "This one's, um. Lullaby of a fisherman's wife. For a child afraid of storms." He paused. "It's humming."

"Bottled lullabies do hum."

"I know. I just like saying it." He set the bottle down and looked up, and his face did the thing it always did when he was about to ask a question he thought might get him in trouble — a quick tightening around the eyes, like a man checking the exits. "Master Coldharbour, can I ask you something? About the First Ledger?"

The cold hand on the back of Casper's neck returned.

"Ask," they said.

"When you take me up for lessons next month — the history section. The part about the swordspeakers." Wren turned the bottle a quarter-turn, aligning its label with the edge of the counter, which was the closest thing he had to fidgeting. "I tried to read ahead. In the copy downstairs, the one we're allowed to use. The chapter about the last swordspeaker — Ondrell — it just stops. Halfway through. Like somebody lost interest."

"Lost interest," Casper repeated.

"In the middle of the most important thing that ever happened," Wren said. "In the middle of a *sentence*, Master Coldharbour. Who loses interest in the middle of a sentence?"

Nobody, was the answer. Nobody in the history of writing had ever lost interest in the middle of a sentence. Sentences were finished or they were destroyed, and the difference between the two was usually a person with something to hide.

"The copies are made from the original," Casper said carefully. "If the original has a fault, the copies inherit it."

"Mm," said Wren, in the tone of a young man who had grown up with four older sisters and knew exactly what a non-answer sounded like when it was wearing its good coat.

"Wren."

"Yes, Master Coldharbour?"

"Has anyone asked you, recently, about the First Ledger? About the early chapters? Anyone at all?"

The pause before he answered was half a heartbeat too long, and they both heard it.

"No," said Wren.

"Anyone asked you to fetch anything from the third floor? To carry anything up or down? To leave the stairwell door unlocked, even briefly, even for a sensible reason?"

Another pause. Longer.

"No," said Wren again, and this time he met Casper's eyes while he said it, which was either the mark of an honest man or a very quickly improving liar, and Casper genuinely could not tell which, and that frightened them more than the missing pages did.

"Right," Casper said. "Well. If anyone does."

"You'd want to know."

"I'd want to know."

"That's what I thought you'd say," Wren murmured, and went back to his bottles, and Casper walked away wondering why the conversation had felt, at the edges, like a negotiation neither party had admitted to.

* * *

The guildhouse of Vellum & Thorn had forty-one rooms, and Casper had keys to thirty-nine of them, and had always assumed the two exceptions were the vault and the guildmaster's study, because those were the two rooms a person expects to be locked out of.

It was therefore with considerable professional displeasure that Casper discovered, that evening, a fortieth exception.

It happened by accident, the way these things do. A draught, coming down the east stairwell where no draught should be. Casper followed it the way water follows a slope — automatically, quietly, with the candle cupped — to a narrow door set into the wall behind the map cabinet, a door so unremarkable that Casper had walked past it perhaps four thousand times without once seeing it, which is the oldest trick in architecture and the second-oldest trick in accounting.

The door was ajar.

Inside was a room the size of a generous cupboard, lined floor to ceiling with paper. Not ledgers — loose pages, bundles of them, tied with string, labeled in a dozen different hands across what looked like several centuries. And lying on the little desk in the center, squared up neatly, corners aligned, as though their owner had every intention of coming back —

Sixteen pages, removed from a book whose binding scars matched them perfectly.

Casper did not touch them. Eleven years of training screamed *do not touch, do not alter, witness only* — and besides, touching things was how you left fingerprints, and Casper had suddenly become a person who cared very much about fingerprints.

Instead they read. By candlelight, standing, in a room that smelled of dust and tallow and something else underneath, something older — the smell, Casper realized with a slow crawl of the scalp, of a chapel that has been empty a very long time.

The pages were Keeper Voss's hand, the looping beautiful script, and they recorded what the tidy catechism version had sanded smooth.

*The god of oaths did not fall,* wrote Amaranth Voss, a hundred and sixty years dead. *It was bound. The king of that age — dying, childless, terrified of the dark — begged it for a blessing on his crown, and the god, being made of bargains, gave one, and the crown took it, and the taking did not go the way anyone planned. The swordspeaker Ondrell was sent to unbind it. Ondrell refused. Read that again, whoever comes after me. The last swordspeaker looked at the god in the crown and refused to speak the Word — because the Word does not distinguish between the binder and the bound, and speaking it would free the god as surely as it freed the king, and Ondrell judged a loose god worse than a caged one.*

*So the guild did what the swordspeaker would not. We wrote instead of spoke. We bound the whole affair into contract — king, god, crown, kingdom — and we filed it, and we told ourselves the filing was enough.*

*It was not enough. Nothing filed is ever enough. Paper sleeps; it does not die. And the god in the crown has had a hundred and sixty years to read its own contract and look for the loopholes, and I am an old woman now, and I can hear it reading.*

*Whoever keeps this ledger after me: check the pages. Count them. Count them every year. Because a god cannot tear out a page. Only a hand can do that. And there is a hand in this house that has already begun.*

Casper stood in the little room until the candle burned down to a thumbnail of itself, and then, with hands that were almost steady, they did not take the pages, and did not leave them, and did the only thing eleven years of training offered as a compromise: they memorized the shelf, the string, the label, the position of every bundle, so that if a single sheet moved, they would know.

Then they slipped out, closed the door, and stood a long time in the dark hallway listening to the guildhouse breathe around them — the settling timbers, the tick of the great clock, footsteps overhead as someone crossed toward the west rooms.

Someone in this house had torn out sixteen pages.

Someone in this house had lied to Wren, or Wren had lied to them, or both, or neither, and Casper found that they could no longer draw the map of who was safe with any confidence at all. The guildhouse had been home for eleven years, and home had just revealed itself to have a hidden room full of warnings from a dead woman, and the warnings were addressed, specifically, to whoever came after her.

To a keeper.

To, in the end, Casper.

They climbed the stairs to their own narrow room, locked the door, and sat down at their desk, and pulled a fresh sheet of paper toward them, and dipped the pen, and wrote the only entry that seemed to matter:

*Audited the First Ledger this day. Sixteen leaves missing. Cause unknown. Witness: myself, and no one else, which is the problem.*

They signed it, blotted it, folded it away — and then paused, pen hovering, because there was one more line wanting to be written and they had been avoiding it since noon.

At last they wrote it:

*Tomorrow I will ask the guildmaster what she knows. And I find that I am afraid — not of her answer, but of the possibility that she will not be surprised by my question.*

Outside, far off in the direction of the capital, a bell rang once — though Casper checked the clock, and the clock insisted that no bell was due, and that no bell had been due for hours.

Casper blew out the candle and sat in the dark, counting.
