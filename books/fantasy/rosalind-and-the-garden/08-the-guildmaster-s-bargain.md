## 8. The Guildmaster's Bargain

The guildhouse of Vellum & Thorn kept its secrets the way old trees kept rings: layer upon layer, none of them visible from outside, all of them load-bearing.

Casper Coldharbour climbed the stairs to the guildmaster's study with three torn pages in their coat and a question that had been growing teeth since dawn. The stairwell smelled of tallow and old ink, as it had for twenty years, and every creak of the third step sounded — as it always did — like a small voice saying *you again*.

The door at the top was oak, older than the building around it, carved with the guild's motto: *ALL THAT IS OWED IS WRITTEN.* Beneath it, someone long dead had scratched a smaller addition that the guild had never sanded out: *AND ALL THAT IS WRITTEN CAN BE FORGIVEN.* Casper had read those words ten thousand times and never once thought about them properly. Tonight they felt less like graffiti and more like prophecy.

They knocked.

"Enter," said Guildmaster Hesper Vane, "and bring whatever you're carrying in your left pocket, because I can hear it rustling like a guilty conscience."

Casper entered.

Hesper Vane was seventy-one years old and looked like a woman who had been assembled from spare parts of several more formidable women: her grandmother's jaw, some bishop's eyebrows, hands that could have cracked walnuts or hearts with equal efficiency. She sat behind a desk so cluttered it constituted a small civilization, and she was feeding crumbs to a one-eyed crow named Audit who lived in the study and answered to no one.

"You found them," she said, without looking up. It was not a question.

"Pages forty-one through forty-three of the First Ledger." Casper laid them on the desk, smoothing each one flat with the care owed to anything two hundred years old. "Torn out. Recently. The fibers are still pale where they were pulled."

Audit cocked his good eye at the pages and made a sound like a hinge that disapproved of doors.

"And you noticed," Hesper said, "that what's written on them is a list of gods. Living ones. Answering prayers. With dates."

"I noticed," Casper said, "that the most recent date is eleven years ago. Which means someone in this house has known the gods were coming back for eleven years, and has been *trading with them*, and has torn the evidence out of our oldest book like a child hiding a bad report from her mother."

Hesper finally looked up. There was no guilt in her face. There was something worse: patience.

"Sit down, Coldharbour."

"I'd rather stand."

"Of course you would. You'd rather stand in a burning house than sit in a comfortable chair; it's why everyone trusts you and nobody invites you to dinner." She pushed a plate of honeyed figs an inch toward them, an offering Casper had refused perhaps four hundred times. "Sit anyway. This will take a while, and my knees ache watching you loom."

Casper sat. They did not take a fig. This was, they felt, an important moral position, and they held it with the same rigidity they applied to interest rates.

* * *

"Tell me about your first trade," Hesper said.

"That isn't—"

"Humor me. Indulge an old woman. Tell me about your first trade."

Casper stared at her. Then, because refusing Hesper Vane twice in one evening was a thing no apprentice had ever survived, they heard themselves answer.

"A memory of rain," they said slowly. "I was nineteen. A farmer came in during the drought year. He wanted to sell the memory of rain on his wedding day — not the wedding, just the rain, the sound of it on the tent roof while he danced. He needed coin for seed. I priced it fairly. I wrote it down. He signed." They paused. "He came back four years later and bought it back for triple. He wept when he got it home. He said he hadn't realized until it was gone that he'd been dancing in silence all that time."

"And how did that make you feel?"

"It made me feel that the ledger is honest even when the world isn't. That's the whole point of the guild. Everything owed must be written. Nothing spoken aloud. The page doesn't lie, doesn't flatter, doesn't forget. It's the only clean thing in—" Casper stopped.

"In a dirty world. Yes. You've said that in nearly identical words at every guild meeting for fifteen years. We've started keeping score." Hesper leaned back. "Now tell me what happened to the farmer's memory while it sat in our vault."

"It was... stored. Preserved. That's what preservation means."

"Preserved by whom, Coldharbour? Stored *where*?" Her voice didn't rise. It lowered, which was far more alarming. "You are the last swordspeaker alive. You know better than anyone in this building that some things are kept alive by being spoken to. Who do you imagine has been speaking to the vault?"

The room went very quiet. Audit stopped eating. Outside, the wind worried the shutters the way a creditor worries a debtor.

"The vault has always been cold," Casper said. "We assumed it was the stone."

"We let you assume it was the stone." Hesper folded her hands. "Eleven years ago, a prayer was overheard in this city. Not by us — by something older. And that something came here, to the guild that trades in impossible things, and asked to open an account."

Casper's mouth had gone dry. "An account."

"Gods keep ledgers too, you know. Better ones than ours. Theirs go back further." Hesper turned the torn pages over with one blunt finger. "It offered us a trade. Continuity for discretion. Our guildhouse stands while every other institution of the old orders burns, rots, or gets nationalized — in exchange, we hold certain items in trust. Certain memories. Certain prayers. Certain *debts*. And we write nothing of it in any ledger a member might audit."

"You violated the founding rule."

"I *am* the founding rule," Hesper said, "and I have spent eleven years deciding nightly whether I saved this house or damned it, and I will thank you not to look at me like that, because I have seen you look at overdue accounts with more warmth."

Casper stood up after all. The chair scraped. "Who else knows?"

"Three people. Myself. The vault-keeper, who thinks the special holdings are royal deposits. And now you." She met their eyes. "And the gods, obviously. They know everything. It's their single most irritating quality."

"What do they want with a guild of clerks?"

"What does a king want with a treasury?" Hesper spread her hands. "Debts, Coldharbour. Mortal debts. Every promise ever sworn, every bargain ever struck, every little 'I owe you one' whispered between friends — the old god of oaths fed on all of it once, before it was bound away. It's waking up hungry. And it wanted a place to store its food."

The word landed in Casper's chest like a dropped coin. *Bound.*

"The crown," they said quietly. "King Aldous's crown."

Something flickered across Hesper's face — there and gone, quick as a lie. "What about it?"

"The pages. The entries about the binding. Someone tore those too, didn't they? Not just the god-list. The history." Casper's voice was steady, which surprised them, because nothing else was. "You knew the crown was one of yours. One of its."

Hesper was silent for a long moment. Audit hopped onto her shoulder and pressed his scarred head against her ear, and she reached up absently to scratch it, the way she had when Casper was nineteen and terrified of their first audit.

"Yes," she said at last. "I knew."

* * *

The fire had burned low. Neither of them had moved to feed it. Somewhere below, the night clerk was stamping receipts, the sound rising through the floorboards like a slow heartbeat: *all owed, all owed, all owed.*

"Why tear the pages?" Casper asked. "If you were protecting the guild, why damage the one thing that never lies?"

"Because the pages weren't protecting *us*." Hesper's voice roughened. "Coldharbour, when I took this office, the guild had forty members and debts in nine kingdoms and a vault full of things that would end the world if they were ever priced wrong. Within a decade we'd be another ruin with a nice motto. The god's offer was ugly, but it was *arithmetic*. I chose survival. I've never pretended otherwise. But the pages about the binding — those told a story I couldn't bear to have read aloud in my own house."

"Which story?"

"That the last time the gods walked free, it wasn't a war that ended it. It was a contract. A dying king bound the god of oaths into its own blessing — tricked it into crowning him, and the crown became its cage. And the swordspeaker of that age spoke the word that sealed it." She looked at Casper then, fully, and her eyes were very tired. "Your order didn't die out, Coldharbour. Your order was *spent*. Every swordspeaker who learns the forbidden word learns what it costs. The order ended because the oath demanded it end."

Casper had known fragments of this. Every swordspeaker knew fragments, the way children know fragments of the family shame. Hearing it whole, in Hesper's gravel voice, in the room where they had learned their letters, was different. It was like being handed back a memory they'd sold without remembering selling it.

"So the crown whispers," Casper said. "The god strains. And Rosalind's brother is the lock it's rattling."

"Rosalind." Hesper smiled thinly. "Our client who shouldn't exist. Yes. She paid with a favor from something old, and I accepted, and you watched me do it and thought I'd lost my mind or my principles. Perhaps both. But understand what I understood: that favor is the only currency in the world the crowned thing cannot refuse, because it comes from something *older* than oaths. Rosalind didn't stumble into this guild by accident, Coldharbour. Things that old don't send desperate sisters to guildhouses. They send them on purpose."

"To what end?"

"To be unbound. Or to be served. I genuinely don't know which, and that uncertainty has aged me eleven years in eleven months." Hesper rose, joints cracking like green wood, and went to the window. Below, the lamplit streets of the city lay quiet, unaware that their prayers were beginning to be answered by things with teeth. "Which brings me to why I let you find those pages. Why I let you climb those stairs with your righteous little rustling pocket."

"You *let*—"

"Coldharbour. You audited the First Ledger uninvited, decoded a cipher I invented, and cornered me in my own study. If I could have stopped you, I'd have tried. But yes. I left the trail warm." She turned. "Because I am a coward, and cowards need someone braver to make the brave choice for them."

For the first time in twenty years, Casper saw the guildmaster look afraid.

* * *

"Here is the bargain," Hesper said. "There are only two roads now, and I'm too old and too compromised to walk either one cleanly, so I'm handing both to you and washing my hands in front of witnesses. God forgive me. Or god *bill* me. Whichever's listening."

She held up one finger.

"Road one. You stay silent. You put those pages back where you found them — or better, you burn them, and I'll swear you never saw them. The guild keeps its name, its house, its history. *You* keep your name, Coldharbour. Every entry in every ledger bearing it. Twenty years of flawless work. The last swordspeaker, honored and recorded, safe behind our walls while the world outside does whatever the world does." Her voice gentled, which was somehow crueler. "You'd live. Comfortably. Remembered."

"And road two?"

"Road two, you help the Sterling girl unbind her brother. You dig into the binding contract, you break whatever seals need breaking, and you learn — as your predecessor learned — exactly what word ends a god." Hesper's hand trembled slightly as she poured herself wine she didn't drink. "And when you speak it, every record of you goes blank. Not destroyed. *Blank.* As if you were never written. The guild forgets you. The city forgets you. I forget you, Coldharbour, and I have watched you grow from a frightened stick of a child into the finest keeper this house has ever produced, and I am telling you plainly: I would rather lose the guild than lose you, and the choice will erase me either way, and I hate it, and it's yours."

The fire ticked. Audit ruffled his feathers and settled into the shape of a judgment.

Casper looked down at their own hands — ink-stained, sword-callused, steady. Hands that had written ten thousand honest entries. Hands that had spoken one blade awake on a dark road while it asked them, in a voice like a struck bell, *whose oath binds you now?*

They had answered the sword nothing. They found, now, that they had an answer after all.

"No one's," they said quietly. "That's rather the point, isn't it? An oath you can escape by staying comfortable isn't an oath. It's a salary."

"Coldharbour—"

"You're right that it's arithmetic," Casper said. "But you've done the sums wrong. You counted what we'd lose. You never counted what we'd *owe*." They picked up the torn pages and folded them, gently, along their old creases. "A king is dying in a cage built from a broken promise. A sister is paying for him with pieces of herself. And somewhere under this house, a god is eating our vault. Those are entries, Guildmaster. Real ones. And everything owed must be written."

Hesper closed her eyes. When she opened them, she was laughing — a dry, cracked sound, half grief.

"There it is," she said. "Fifteen years of listening to you recite the rules, and I finally understand you actually *believe* them. May every god preserve me from honest clerks." She straightened, and something of the old iron came back into her spine. "Then hear the rest of it, since you've chosen the expensive road. The original binding contract — the dying king's signature, the seal, the terms — it exists. It's real paper, real ink, and it's kept in the deepest vault, behind three doors that only respond to a swordspeaker's voice. I can get you to the second door."

"And the third?"

Hesper looked at the fire for a long moment.

"The third door opens for the guildmaster," she said. "And the guildmaster stays behind to hold it — because the crowned thing already knows you're coming, Coldharbour. It holds debts on half the hunters in this city, and tonight, I suspect, it will call them in."

Outside, far off across the sleeping capital, a bell began to ring.

It was midnight. It was not the midnight bell.
