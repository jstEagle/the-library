## 9. Court of Whispers

The capital had a smell, Rosalind decided, that no one who lived there ever noticed: candlewax and wet stone and the particular sweetness of too many flowers dying in too many vases. She had grown up breathing it. She'd forgotten it in three months away, and now it settled over her like a coat she couldn't take off.

"You're scowling," said Wren, beside her.

"I'm memorizing."

"Same thing, on your face."

They stood in the queue outside the palace's service gate with perhaps two hundred other souls seeking work — a seasonal intake, announced by crier, for seamstresses and launderers and kitchen hands ahead of the Midwinter Court. Rosalind wore a widow's gray and a name she'd practiced until it answered to her spine: *Maren Cobb, of Ashfall Lane.* Wren was her sister, which required no acting at all except the part where she pretended not to be terrified.

"Say your name," Rosalind murmured.

"Maren."

"Yours."

"Tansy Cobb." A pause. "I hate it. It sounds like a vegetable."

"It sounds forgettable. That's the entire point."

The line shuffled forward. Above them, past the walls, the towers of the royal residence caught the morning light, and at the very top — she made herself look, made herself do it every time they passed within sight of it — the crown on the weathervane of St. Aldric's Chapel gleamed gold. A decoration only. The real one was inside, on her brother's head, whispering.

She had not slept properly since the night audience. Since Aldous — *her* Aldous, the boy who'd once traded his dessert for her cough medicine and claimed he wasn't hungry anyway — had surfaced through whatever wore him like a glove and begged her, in a voice cracked down the middle, to cut it from him. Then the other voice had come back and looked at her with her brother's eyes and known her face, and she had run, and run, and kept running until Casper Coldharbour had caught up with her on the road and said, in that dry ledger-keeper's voice, *"Running is a plan with no entries in it. Let's write a better one."*

So here was the better one. It looked like standing in a queue with chilblains forming on her toes.

"Next."

Rosalind stepped up to the table where a palace steward sat flanked by two clerks. He was a soft man with soft eyes and a voice like porridge, and he asked the questions stewards always asked: name, trade, references, health.

"Maren Cobb. Seamstress. Twelve years' trade, last employed by Mistress Halloway of Corrow, dressmaker to the gentry there." Mistress Halloway existed. Rosalind had paid a visit and an outrageous sum, and the woman — who owed three seasons' worth of debts to people who preferred their debts quiet — had agreed to remember employing her fondly.

The steward glanced at her hands. This was the test nobody warned you about. A liar's hands were clean; a worker's hands were scarred. Rosalind had spent two weeks before coming deliberately acquiring needle-scars, pricking her fingers until her fingertips were a constellation of tiny white marks, and working wool until her thumb joints ached in a way that felt honest.

"Hold out your thumbs," the steward said.

She did. He pressed briefly against the calluses, nodded, and wrote something down. "Stitching trial this afternoon. East workroom. If you can set a hem invisible at arm's length, you're hired. Palace pays eleven marks the month, room above the laundry."

"And the hours?"

"The hours are the king's hours." He said it lightly, but something flickered behind the porridge voice — a little joke the staff all knew, the kind that isn't a joke. "Next!"

* * *

The stitching trial took place in a long room full of women bent over fabric like a field of wheat in wind. Rosalind was given a court coat belonging to some lord or other and told to repair the shoulder seam. She worked slowly, competently, invisibly — the way Casper worked a ledger, she realized, and almost smiled. There was more than one way to keep accounts.

Around her, the other seamstresses talked the way workers talk: wages, weather, the price of tallow, whose husband drank. And underneath it, quieter, the way one mentions a draft under a door:

"—did you see Lord Vessery at the levee? Standing when everyone knelt—"

"—the queen mother's chapel's been shut a month. Shut. Her own chapel—"

"—my cousin serves at the evening meal. Says His Majesty talks to himself. Says it's grief, poor man, after the queen—"

"Grief," sniffed an older woman named Bess, who had the forearms of a laundress and the eyes of a hawk. "I've buried a husband and both parents, dearie, and never once argued with myself in two voices about it."

Silence dropped over that corner of the room like a dropped cloth. Then everyone went back to sewing very fast.

Rosalind kept her eyes on her hem and her heart rate steady through sheer force of practice, because this was the thing she had come to learn: who already knew. Who had noticed and said nothing, or noticed and been silenced, or noticed and — worse — agreed.

* * *

By the third week she had a map.

It lived nowhere dangerous — not inked on paper, which could be found, but folded into memory the way Casper had taught her during those cold nights on the road, reciting entries aloud until the guild's methods became her own. *Everything owed must be written,* the guild said. But there was more than one kind of writing.

Lord Vessery: stood at the levee while others knelt, and was not rebuked. Noted.

The Chancellor's undersecretary, a gray man called Pell: seen carrying letters at midnight between the west tower and the king's apartments, letters that never passed through the chancery seal. Noted.

The captain of the night guard, Harrow: rotated the watch so that the king's corridor was always held by the same six men. Six men whose families, Rosalind learned from the gossip of the laundry lines, had each lately come into sudden good fortune. Debts paid. Sisters married off well. A mother's cottage bought free and clear.

*Someone is paying,* she thought, pressing linen flat in the mending room. *And someone is being paid. And it's all written down somewhere, because men like that always write things down — they just don't know they're writing them down for me.*

That was the discovery that frightened her most, in the end: how easy it was. The crowned thing in her brother's head bought loyalty the way merchants buy grain, and the court — that glittering, gossiping, terrified organism — absorbed the transactions without comment, because the alternative was to notice, and noticing was fatal. She watched Lady Ambray laugh at a court function and understood, from the way the woman's laugh stopped half a beat before her eyes did, that Lady Ambray knew exactly what walked around wearing the king, and had decided to be comfortable about it.

Rosalind mended hems and mapped the rot.

* * *

It was Bess who broke first — or rather, Bess who opened, in the way of older women who have survived enough courts to know that silence is also a debt accruing interest.

They were alone in the mending room one evening, unpicking the embroidery from a gown that had belonged, apparently, to a lady who no longer required it. Rosalind didn't ask what had happened to the lady. That was another thing she'd learned: the palace discarded people as casually as gowns, and the seams of both were handled by the same silent women.

"You mend well," Bess said. "Too well. Gentry-trained."

"My mistress was particular."

"Mm." Bess bit her thread. "And your eyes are particular too. You count everything. Doors. Guards. Which of the six night-men takes the east stair." She looked up, unbothered. "I've done forty years in this place, girl. I know what counting looks like."

Rosalind's needle paused. She weighed, in the space of a heartbeat, denial versus truth, and chose a third thing — the thing she'd been learning from watching Casper, who lied constantly but never about anything that mattered.

"What happens," she said quietly, "to the people who go into the king's apartments at night and come out different? Smiling wrong. Agreeing with things they'd have spat at a year ago."

Bess threaded her needle with the unhurried calm of a woman deciding something. "You've met Pell, then."

"Pell is one name on a list I'm building."

"Are you building it to sell it?" Bess's eyes were flat and old. "Because that list has been built before, by cleverer girls than you, and the ones who sold it got rooms in the west tower with locked doors, and the ones who meant it—" she paused— "well. There was a chaplain once. Good man. Started asking why the king's confessions had stopped. They found him at the bottom of the cistern, and everyone agreed he'd slipped, and everyone agreed very quickly, if you follow me."

"I'm not selling it."

"No?"

"No. I'm collecting it." Rosalind put down her needle and met the old woman's gaze straight on, and let a fraction of herself show through Maren Cobb's gray face — just enough. "Something is wrong with the king. Not grief-wrong. Wrong-wrong. And I think you've known it longer than I have, and I think it's eating you alive to know it and hold the linens for it every day."

The silence stretched. Somewhere below them, the great kettles of the laundry boomed softly, like a slow heart.

"There are others," Bess said at last. "Not many. The ones the palace forgot it had. Me. Tally the cook, who counts spoons like your life depends on it and has noticed forty-one spoons gone missing from the king's service since spring — forty-one, girl, taken down to the west tower, and what does a thing in a crown want with silver spoons?" She snorted. "Old Marta, who does for the chapel candles and swears the king's private chapel burns offerings at night when no priest is there. The gate-warden's daughter, who copies the duty rosters and has a memory like flypaper. We're nobody, all of us. Scullions and stitchers and candle-girls. The crown doesn't see us." Her mouth twisted. "That's always been our protection. Might be our purpose, too, if you're the sort who believes in purposes."

"I'm the sort," Rosalind said.

* * *

They met, after that, in the drying loft above the laundry, where the heat rose and the noise of the great copper kettles covered everything. Five of them, then six: Bess; Tally the cook, small and fierce, with flour permanently in her eyebrows; Marta, ancient and half-deaf and sharper than any blade in the armory; Nettie the warden's daughter with her flypaper memory; and a laundry-man called Osk who never spoke at all but communicated, it emerged, entirely through meaningful silences and the occasional devastating raised eyebrow.

Rosalind laid out her map for them in pieces, testing, trusting carefully, and they added to it the way streams add to a river. Tally's missing spoons turned out to matter: silver taken from the king's own service, and Marta knew why — the old rites, the ones for binding oaths, had used vessels of pure silver, and someone was performing old rites in the west tower. Nettie produced, from memory alone, a complete rotation of the night watch going back four months, including the six men and, crucially, the two gaps — the two nights each month when the corridor was cleared entirely and even Harrow's chosen men were sent elsewhere.

"New moon," Rosalind said, when Nettie finished, and her own voice sounded strange to her. "The nights are cleared on the new moon."

"Aye," said Bess. "And the king doesn't sleep those nights. We know because the kitchens send up no tray, and the water-carrier hears him walking. Walking and talking. All night. Two voices."

The drying loft was very warm and smelled of soap and steam, and Rosalind sat among these forgotten people — a princess in a widow's gray whom none of them suspected, though she suspected they might forgive her the lie if they ever learned it — and felt something harden in her chest into a shape she recognized. It wasn't fear anymore. It had been fear on the road, fear in the ruined chapel, fear the night her brother begged her with his own mouth. Fear was for people with nothing to do.

This was resolve. It felt like a needle going through cloth: small, precise, and unstoppable once started.

"Here's what I need," she said. "Not much. The rosters. The chapel times. And on the next new-moon night, one person near the west stairs who can tell me exactly when the corridor empties and exactly when it fills again."

"And what will you be doing?" Bess asked, shrewd.

"Getting into the palace archive," Rosalind said, "to read what my brother's confessions used to say — before they stopped." She had reasoned it out with Casper weeks ago: a man tormented by a voice would confess; the records of those confessions, sealed away in the palace archive, would show when the voice began, and what it asked for, and whether anyone else had heard it too. "And I need one more thing. Someone who can get a message out of the palace to a certain guildhouse, unseen, if I ask urgently."

Osk, silent Osk, raised one eyebrow with such eloquence that everyone laughed, tension cracking like ice.

"That'll be Osk, then," said Tally.

* * *

Two nights later, kneeling to pin a hem in the presence chamber gallery while the court swirled below her like colored smoke, Rosalind saw the thing she had been dreading and preparing for.

Aldous entered at the far end — tall still, golden still, the beloved shape of him — and the crowd bowed like wheat, and she bowed too, head down, heart hammering, watching through the curtain of her own hair.

He moved along the receiving line saying the right things in the right voice. And then he reached Lord Vessery — her map's first entry — and stopped.

For the length of three heartbeats, the king and the lord simply looked at each other, and the whole hall pretended not to notice that nothing was being said. Then Aldous smiled, and it was the wrong smile, the one she'd seen in the night audience, the smile that arrived late at the eyes — and Vessery bowed lower than protocol required, and moved on looking like a man who had been weighed.

And then, as the king turned, his gaze swept the gallery — idle, royal, nothing — and snagged.

On her.

One heartbeat. Two. Her needle kept moving; her face stayed Maren Cobb's pleasant blankness; every skill she had built in nine weeks of gray dresses and counted doors funneled itself into not existing. Below, her brother's borrowed eyes rested on her with an expression she could not read — curiosity, or recognition, or appetite — and the whispers of the court went on around them, oblivious, a thousand threads of talk, and somewhere among them, surely, the crown's own whisper threading through, patient, hungry, keeping its ledgers too.

Then he blinked, and moved on, and the moment closed over like water.

But that night, in the drying loft, Rosalind took out the scrap of paper she allowed herself — one scrap, hidden in a reel of thread — and wrote beneath her map a single new entry, her hand steady, her breath not:

*The thing in the crown has begun to look for me.*

She sealed it into the message Osk would carry at dawn to a certain guildhouse, added the words she'd rehearsed until they didn't shake: *Found allies. Found the new-moon nights. Need Casper. Come soon.*

And beneath it, after a long moment, she wrote the truest line of all, the one that had hardened in her chest in the heat and steam of the drying loft, the one that felt less like fear than like a vow:

*I am done hiding in the seams. Tell them I'm ready to start pulling threads.*
