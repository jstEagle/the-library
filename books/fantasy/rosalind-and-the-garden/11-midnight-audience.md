## 11. Midnight Audience

The seamstress's corridor smelled of tallow and lavender, which was the palace's way of saying it had not been cleaned properly since the old queen died but someone was trying very hard to pretend otherwise.

Rosalind moved through it with a basket of mended shirts balanced on her hip, head bowed, shoulders rounded into the shape of a woman who had spent her whole life bending over other people's clothes. It had taken three weeks to earn this corridor. Three weeks of hemming hems for the chamberlain's wife, of listening to laundry maids complain about their sweethearts, of laughing at the right jokes and never at the wrong ones. Three weeks of being nobody named Marta, with ink-stained fingers she explained away as a habit of marking measurements.

Three weeks of watching the crown.

She had learned its rhythms the way she had once learned Aldous's — his moods arriving like weather off the mountains, his laughter starting in his chest before it reached his face. The crown kept different hours. It was quietest at dawn, when the king took his cold bath and shouted cheerfully at his valet about the state of the world. It grew restless through the afternoon audiences, so that by evening Aldous's voice had developed that second cadence beneath it, the one like a bell rung underwater. And past midnight —

Past midnight, she had been told by a laundress who asked no questions because Rosalind had once unpicked a stubborn stain from the woman's only good dress, the king sometimes walked. Alone. No guards. Through the west gallery, down to the old chapel wing that everyone pretended had been decommissioned, where he would stand in the dark for an hour and then return to bed as though nothing had happened.

The chapel wing. Where she had knelt, months ago, and begged whatever might be listening to save him.

She had come back to answer her own prayer. It seemed only fair that someone should.

* * *

The west gallery at night was a museum of the family's better moments, all of them painted by artists who had been paid to flatter. Rosalind walked slowly past her own childhood: herself at seven, holding a falcon too big for her wrist; Aldous at twelve, already wearing the solemnity he'd been told a crown required; the two of them together under a painted oak, rendered with such tenderness she almost forgave the artist for making her prettier than she'd ever been.

She stopped at that last one. In the painting, Aldous's hand rested on her shoulder, and his expression was the one he wore when he thought no one was looking — the unguarded one, faintly amused, faintly worried, entirely her brother.

*I'll find you in there,* she told the paint. *Under everything else. I have to believe you're still in there.*

The gallery ended at a door she remembered from girlhood — the servants' passage to the chapel vestry, kept unlocked because the sexton was lazy and the king had never cared enough to notice. The lock gave at her touch, and the hinge made a sound like a question she chose not to answer.

The chapel was dark except for one candle left burning before the empty altar niche, because even abandoned gods deserved light, or so the sexton's mother had taught him. Rosalind set down her basket. Under the shirts, wrapped in linen, lay the thing she had carried for three weeks against the day it might matter: a small knife, ceremonial, blunt-nosed, the kind used for cutting thread at royal weddings. She had stolen it from the sewing room and sharpened it herself on the hearthstone, badly, over four nights, hiding the whetstone sounds under coughing fits.

It was not much of a weapon. But if her brother asked her to cut something free of him, she intended to try.

She heard him before she saw him — footsteps in the cloister outside, uneven, the gait of a man walking with someone invisible at his shoulder. Then the chapel door opened, and King Aldous came in out of the moonlight, and Rosalind pressed herself into the shadow behind the vestry screen with her heart hammering so loudly she was certain it would give her away.

He looked terrible. That was the first thing. Court gossip said the king was tired, preoccupied, given to strange pauses; the truth was worse. He had lost weight in the wrong way, as though something were drinking him from the inside. His eyes were rimmed red. His hands shook until he clasped them behind his back, and even then the tremor traveled up into his shoulders.

And the crown — the crown sat on his head like it belonged there more than he did. In the candlelight it did not gleam so much as *listen*.

"Alone," Aldous said aloud, to the empty chapel. "You said I could have tonight alone."

A pause. Rosalind felt the air change pressure, the way it did before summer storms, and understood that the answer had come — silently, or in a register she couldn't hear.

"You always say that." His voice cracked. "You always say that, and then you're there at breakfast, and you're there when I sign things, and you were there when Mother died, you were *there*, whispering what to feel—"

Another pause. Longer. Aldous laughed, and it was the worst sound Rosalind had ever heard, because half of it was her brother and half of it was something using her brother's throat like a borrowed instrument.

"No," he said. "No, I don't want to hear the terms again. I know the terms."

He sank onto the front pew, suddenly, all at once, like a puppet whose strings had been briefly released. He put his face in his hands. And the crown — this was the part Rosalind knew she would remember for the rest of her life — the crown *leaned*. Very slightly, very gently, the way a hand rests on a shoulder. Comforting him. Or keeping him in place. With that thing, she suspected, the gestures were identical.

"I'm sorry," Aldous whispered. To whom, she couldn't tell. Perhaps to himself. Perhaps to the country. Perhaps to the boy in the painting under the oak tree.

Rosalind stepped out from behind the screen.

"Aldous."

He went rigid. For one horrible moment she thought he would call the guard, and she rehearsed her cover story — lost, mending basket, wrong corridor — but then he turned, and she watched something happen in his face. A shutter opening. A man surfacing from deep water.

"...Rosie?"

Her name in his voice. Her childhood name, the one no one at court had ever used, the one only he called her, on the day their father died, holding her hand in the cold hall of succession. She hadn't heard it in a year.

"It's me," she said, and her voice betrayed her completely, wobbling like a child's. "It's me, Aldous."

"How—" He stood up too fast and swayed. "How are you here? They told me you were at Silvermere. They told me—" He stopped. Looked at her properly. Looked at the plain dress, the mended basket, the servant's shawl. Understanding arrived in his expression like frost spreading across a window. "Oh," he said softly. "Oh, Rosie. You came *back*. You shouldn't have come back."

"I came to get you out."

"There is no out." He said it simply, without drama, the way you'd say *the well is dry* or *winter comes early this year*. He crossed to her and caught her hands, and his grip was fever-hot. Up close she could see the fine tremor running through him constantly now, like a plucked string that couldn't stop singing. "Listen to me. Listen. How long do we have? When does it notice?"

"When does what notice?"

"The—" He glanced upward, at nothing, at the crown. "It sleeps shallow. Some nights I get minutes. Tonight it let me walk here alone, which means either it's confident or it's testing me, and I can't tell which anymore, I haven't been able to tell which for months." His words were tumbling now, tripping over each other, a dam breaking after a year of careful silence. "Rosie, I need you to listen, because I don't know how many minutes this is, and I've written things down but it finds the papers, it always finds the papers—"

"Aldous. Breathe."

"Don't tell me to breathe, I'm your *king*—" And then he laughed, wetly, helplessly. "I'm your brother. I don't even know which one I am most days. That's what it does. It doesn't push. It just... leans. Every decision, every thought, it leans a little, and after a while you can't find the line between what you wanted and what it wanted, and you stop looking for the line, because looking is exhausting, and it's so *patient*, Rosie, it has been patient for a hundred years—"

"A hundred years?" Her blood went cold. "Aldous, the crown isn't a hundred years old."

"The crown is just the door." He was gripping her hands hard enough to hurt. "The thing behind it is older. It was bound once — I've seen fragments, in dreams it lets me have, or thinks it lets me have — bound by men with ledgers, bound by *words*, and it has spent a century learning the shape of the keyhole from the inside. Do you understand? It doesn't want to break the binding. It wants someone to *unmake* it properly. It wants to be invited out."

"Then we won't invite it."

"It's already writing the invitation." His voice dropped to a whisper. "In my hand. At night. I wake up and there are documents on my desk with my seal, my signature, and I don't remember signing them. Trade agreements. Land grants. Charters for guilds I've never heard of. It's building something, Rosie, it's building a web of bargains across the whole kingdom, and every bargain is a stitch, and when the last stitch is placed—"

He stopped. Went utterly still.

Rosalind felt it too. The candle flame, which had been leaning toward them all this while, straightened. The pressure in the air changed — not storm-pressured now but *attended*, the specific terrible feeling of being listened to by something that has all the time in creation.

"Aldous," she whispered. "How many minutes was that?"

His eyes were wide and clear and full of terror, and they were *his*, and he said, fast, low, urgent, pouring the words out like a man emptying his pockets before a flood takes them:

"Rosie. Listen. There may not be another night like this. You have to cut it off. Not negotiate, not bargain, not ask nicely — those are its games, it wins every game that has rules — you have to take the blade and cut the crown from my head, and it will hurt, it will hurt *me*, maybe kill me, I don't care, do you hear me? I don't care. Whatever it costs. Whatever it costs *you*. Promise me. Promise me you won't hesitate, promise me you won't try to save us both, because it will offer you that, it will offer you a version where everyone lives and nobody pays, and that version is a lie, it's always a lie—"

"Aldous—"

"*Promise me.*"

And because she was his sister, and because she had knelt in this same chapel and begged a dead god to save him, and because some promises are made before you understand their price, Rosalind said:

"I promise."

He exhaled. His whole body sagged with relief, and for a moment he smiled — really smiled, the unguarded smile from the painting, the one that started in his chest — and he lifted one shaking hand and touched her cheek the way he had when they were children and she'd fallen out of the pear tree.

"You came back for me," he said wonderingly. "Everyone else ran. Everyone sensible ran. And you snuck back in dressed as a *seamstress*, you absolute—"

The smile froze.

Rosalind saw it happen. Saw the clarity drain out of his face like water from a cracked bowl — not gradually, not like sleep coming, but all at once, like a lamp pinched out. His hand dropped from her cheek. His spine straightened. His chin lifted, and the crown settled down onto his brow as though taking its seat.

When he spoke again, the voice was still her brother's voice. That was the horror of it. The same instrument, played by a different musician.

"Well," said the thing wearing the king. "The little sister."

Every hair on Rosalind's body rose. She took one step back, then another, her heel striking the mending basket, sending shirts spilling across the chapel floor like surrender flags.

"That's a lovely disguise," it continued, conversationally, pleasantly, circling her slowly the way she had seen hawks circle the dovecote at Silvermere. "Very thorough. The ink stains were a nice touch — most people forget the hands. You've been here nineteen days. You hemmed Lady Vess's mourning gown beautifully. You laughed at Chancellor Ives's joke about the fishmonger, which was wise, because he tells it to test people." It tilted Aldous's head. "You should know I considered exposing you twice. But you were so *useful*. Servants see everything, and no one guards against a seamstress. I've been reading the court through your eyes for weeks."

"You've been—" Her stomach turned over. "Through my—"

"Oh, don't be dramatic. I don't ride you. I merely... noticed you noticing. You have your grandmother's trick of it, did you know? Seeing the seams in things. She saw them in me, once. That didn't end well for her either." It smiled with Aldous's mouth, and the smile was wrong in a way she couldn't have described to save her life — every muscle correct, the whole assembled wrong. "But here is my problem, little sister. My brother in here"—it tapped the crown with one finger, and Aldous's face flickered, briefly, agonizingly, like a candle guttering—*"has been very quiet since you arrived. Too quiet. Hope is such a disruptive emotion in a host. So I find myself needing to make a decision about you."

Rosalind's hand found the knife in her sleeve. Blunt-nosed. Badly sharpened. Against a god, it was less than a needle against a mountain, and she gripped it anyway.

"What kind of decision?" she managed.

"The kind kings make." It regarded her with Aldous's eyes, and behind them, deep down, in the last lit window of a burning house, she saw her brother — saw him screaming, silent, pounding on the glass. "You made a promise here tonight. I heard it. Promises are *currency*, Rosalind — surely your new friends at the guild have taught you that. You promised to cut the crown from him, whatever it costs. What a generous gift. Because now I hold that promise, and I can call it due whenever I choose, in whatever form inflicts the most—"

The candle went out.

Not blew out — *went out*, all at once, and in the darkness Rosalind heard Aldous gasp, a real gasp, human and ragged, and his voice came thin and desperate through the black:

"Rosie — run — it's fighting itself, I don't know why, something's pulling at it — *run*—"

She ran. Out the vestry door, through the servants' passage, past the flattered paintings, down corridors that seemed longer than they should be, while behind her the chapel filled with a sound like bells ringing underwater and a voice — her brother's voice, both of her brother's voices, layered over each other — cried out words she couldn't parse in a language that made her teeth ache.

She did not stop running until she reached the seamstress's attic, where Casper Coldharbour sat waiting in the dark exactly as arranged, and took one look at her face and put down the ledger they had been pretending to read.

"It knows I'm here," Rosalind said. Her voice sounded far away, someone else's. "Casper — it *knows* me. And it says my promise belongs to it now."

Casper closed the ledger slowly. Outside, far off across the sleeping city, a single bell began to ring — though no bell in the capital rang at this hour, and none of them, Rosalind knew with sick certainty, rang *that note*.

"We need to move tonight," Casper said. "Because if it holds your promise, then it doesn't need to wait for you to keep it. It can keep it *for* you — with any blade, any hand, at any time it chooses." They were already standing, already gathering the satchel, already moving toward the window and the rope beyond it. "And Rosalind — there's only one reason a god would want that crown cut loose ahead of schedule."

The bell rang again. Closer. Wronger.

"It's ready to come out."
