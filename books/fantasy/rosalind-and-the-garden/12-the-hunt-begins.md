## 12. The Hunt Begins

The trouble with being a guild that trades in impossible things, I have often thought, is that eventually the impossible things come looking for you.

I was in the counting room when they arrived — which is to say I was doing what I do best, which is nothing dramatic at all. I was reconciling a ledger entry concerning a woman in Lowmarsh who had sold her talent for growing good onions and had recently written to request a partial refund, on the grounds that her husband now wept whenever he ate soup. The entry read: *One (1) talent, allium-related, exchanged for forty years of guaranteed harvests.* Below it, in my own hand: *Refund denied. Husband's tears not covered under terms.*

There are those who think my work is cold. Those people have never watched a man weep over soup.

The bell above the guildhouse door rang three times. Not the ordinary single chime of a customer entering, nor the double ring of a courier. Three rings meant collectors. Debt-holders come to call in person.

I set down my pen, because a swordspeaker's hands remember things the mind would rather forget, and went to see who owed us enough to be dangerous.

* * *

There were four of them in the entrance hall, standing in the particular way that men stand when they have been paid to be still. They wore no livery, carried no visible weapons, and had the unmistakable look of people who had once been somebody's sons.

I knew two of them.

"Dorn," I said. "And Merrick Salt. Hello."

Dorn had collected debts for Vellum & Thorn for eleven years. He was a broad, patient man who preferred to let contracts do his fighting for him, and he had once spent an entire winter sitting outside a miller's house drinking the miller's tea until the miller agreed to pay what he owed. Merrick had joined more recently. He had a nervous habit of touching his left cuff, where, I happened to know from ledger 4471-C, he kept a knife.

"Coldharbour," Dorn said. His voice was careful, the way a voice gets when it has been rehearsed on a long road. "We're here on collection."

"Whose?"

He didn't answer directly, which was itself an answer. Instead he drew out a folded paper — real paper, not guild vellum, and that detail sat in my stomach like a swallowed stone — and read aloud.

"By order of the Crown, all assets of the house of Velland & Thorne are hereby claimed in settlement of an outstanding obligation, dated—" he squinted "—one hundred and sixty-two years ago."

"Velland & Thorne," I repeated. "Not Vellum & Thorn."

"That's what it says."

"And you've come to collect a debt from a guildhouse that didn't exist when the debt was made, on behalf of a crown that hasn't held this throne for six generations." I looked at their faces. Dorn wouldn't meet my eyes. Merrick touched his cuff twice. "Who gave you this paper?"

"The palace," Dorn said. "A clerk. Pale fellow. Smiled too much."

I knew the smile. Everyone who had walked the palace corridors lately knew it, though most of them couldn't say why it unsettled them. It was the smile of something practicing.

"Dorn," I said gently, "you're holding paper that isn't ours, collecting a debt that isn't real, for a king who isn't a king. Do you understand what I'm telling you?"

"I understand," he said, and his voice cracked just slightly on the second word, "that my contract says I collect, or I default. And you know better than anyone what defaulting means, Coldharbour. You wrote half the defaults yourself."

That was true. That was the worst part. That was entirely, precisely true.

* * *

Here is what the guild does to its own when they fail. We don't take limbs. We don't take lives. We take *standing*. A defaulted collector becomes a name in a red-bordered ledger, and everything they are owed by the world — every kindness, every credit, every small unspoken mercy that lets a person move through life unremarked — is marked *void*. Doors stop opening. Bread stops rising for them. Old friends forget why they liked them. It is not cruelty, exactly. It is arithmetic. And I had performed that arithmetic on men like Dorn without ever once losing sleep over it, because the ledgers balanced, and balanced ledgers were the closest thing to a conscience I had ever been taught to keep.

Now four of our own stood in our entrance hall with false paper in their hands, and behind their eyes was the flat terror of men who had already done the math on what refusal would cost them.

"You could refuse," I said. "You could walk away. I'd tear up your contracts myself. All four."

Merrick laughed — a short, ugly sound with no humor in it. "Tear them up? You can't. You *know* you can't. The master holds the originals. And the master—" he stopped.

"The master signed off on this," I finished for him.

Nobody said anything. The fire crackled in the hall grate with the cheerful indifference of fire.

"Where is she?" I asked.

"Gone," said the youngest of the four, a collector named Pell whose debt I remembered distinctly: his mother's cure, purchased at ruinous interest, the payments structured so that he would owe the guild until he was seventy. "Left yesterday morning. Left *us* the orders. Said if we did this right, our accounts would be cleared." He swallowed. "She said the new holder of the crown pays generously for loyalty."

I felt something shift in my chest — not breaking, exactly, but settling, the way a house settles in cold weather, with a sound you feel more than hear.

Our guildmaster, who had traded with the returned gods for years to buy the guild's survival, had finally found a buyer worth selling to. And she had sold us — the ledgers, the collectors, the whole careful architecture of debt — to the thing wearing Aldous's crown.

"Well," I said, into the silence. "In that case, I'm afraid I'm going to have to ask you to leave."

"We can't do that," Dorn said. "Contract says—"

"Yes, yes. Contract says you collect." I rolled up my sleeves, which is a thing one does in stories, and which I had never once done in actual life, and found it surprisingly satisfying. "Dorn, old friend, I am going to give you the only professional courtesy I have left. Walk out now, and I'll mark your accounts *paid in full* before sundown, in my own hand, and hang the page where the whole guild can see it. Stay, and—" I sighed. "Stay, and I'll have to do the other thing."

"The other thing?" Merrick said.

"Swordspeaking," I said. "It's been a slow decade. I'm actually rather looking forward to it."

* * *

They came at me in the hall, because contracts are simple creatures and theirs said *collect*, and there is no clause in any contract ever drafted that says *unless the debtor seems nice about it*.

I want to be honest about what happened next, because honesty is the last ledger I have left. Four trained collectors against one middle-aged accountant should have gone very badly for the accountant. It went badly for them instead, and here is why: they were fighting a man, and I was speaking to a sword.

The blade was an old one — guild steel, hung above the hall fireplace for longer than I'd been alive, a ceremonial piece nobody had sharpened since the last swordspeaker died. Which is to say, since I stopped being one in any way that counted.

"*Wake,*" I said.

The word is older than the guild, older than the kingdom, older than most of the gods currently pretending to be dead. It went through the hall like a bell struck underwater. The sword came off its brackets into my hand already moving, singing that thin silver note that only oath-bound steel makes, and the collectors — professionals, all of them — did the sensible thing, which was to stop.

Because everyone knows, somewhere underneath, what a spoken blade means. Their grandparents' grandparents' grandparents had known it. There is a reason the age of gods ended, and the reason has a hilt.

"Hold," Dorn gasped, and held up both hands, and — this is the part I will think about for the rest of my life — the others obeyed him, not me. "Coldharbour. Coldharbour, wait. Look at the paper again. Look at the seal."

I looked. I had looked before, but I looked properly now, and saw what I had refused to see: the seal was not the royal seal. It was older. A circle broken by a hand reaching through it. I had seen that mark once before, in the torn pages of the oldest ledger, in the margins of entries about the last time prayers got answered — and I had seen it most recently inked fresh at the bottom of a binding contract described to me by a dying woman holding a door.

The god of oaths. The crowned thing. It hadn't sent hunters.

It had sent *our own paper*.

Every one of these men was bound to the guild by contract. Every contract was now, through the guildmaster's signature, bound upward to the crown. The crowned thing hadn't needed to hire soldiers. It had simply acquired our debts, and debts, as any child of this guild knows, are the most obedient army ever devised.

"They're not attacking the guild," I said slowly. "They're *foreclosing* on it."

"Is that bad?" asked Pell, who was young.

"Pell," I said, lowering the sword, "it's the worst thing I've ever heard, and I audit this place."

* * *

I paid them. Of course I paid them. I sat down right there in the entrance hall with four frightened men watching me, and I wrote, in my own hand, on proper guild vellum: *Account of Dorn of Harrowgate: PAID IN FULL. Account of Merrick Salt: PAID IN FULL.* And so on, down the line, including Pell, whose mother's cure I forgave outright and whose relief made him sit down hard on the floor and put his head between his knees.

"It's not that simple," Dorn warned me, watching me write. "The master countersigned everything to the crown. If you void the contracts—"

"Then the crown comes to collect from *me*," I said. "Good. I'd rather it knew my face."

"You don't have a face to it yet," Merrick muttered. "You're a line item, Coldharbour. We all are. That's the whole point of paper."

He wasn't wrong. That was the horror of it, the clean and elegant horror: the crowned thing was the god of oaths, and oaths are just debts wearing their Sunday clothes. It had spent a hundred and sixty-two years inside a circlet of gold learning exactly how mortals bind themselves, and it had learned well. It didn't need to break the guild. It only needed to inherit us.

I walked the four of them to the door. Dorn paused on the threshold.

"For what it's worth," he said, "I didn't want to come. None of us did. But Coldharbour — the clerk at the palace, the smiling one. He knew my mother's name. Knew what she died of. Knew the exact sum I'd have paid to save her." His jaw worked. "It doesn't threaten you. It just *shows you the bill*. And then you sign."

"I know," I said. "That's how the guild always worked too. We just called it service."

He flinched. I hadn't meant it as a blow, but it landed like one, on both of us.

When they were gone, I stood alone in the entrance hall with the old sword still faintly humming in my grip, and I asked it the question it had asked me once on a road full of bandits, the question I had never managed to answer.

"Whose oath binds me now?"

The sword, being a sword, said nothing. Swords are wiser than people that way.

But I answered myself, standing there in the empty hall, and the answer came out steadier than I expected: *No one's. Not anymore. Not the guildmaster's, not the crown's, not the dead order's.* Because I had spent my whole life believing that a balanced ledger was a kind of virtue, and I had just watched balance itself become a weapon — watched our beautiful, meticulous, fair-minded arithmetic get bought by a god and turned against the very people it was built to protect.

Guilt is a strange fuel. It burns dirty and it burns hot, and it lights exactly as far as you need it to.

I went upstairs to the counting room, took out a fresh page, and began — for the first time in thirty years of service — to write a ledger that was not the guild's. At the top, in letters large enough that no clerk could later claim ambiguity, I wrote:

*Debts owed by the thing in the crown. To be collected in full.*

Underneath, first entry: *One guildmaster, sold. One brother-king, stolen. One apprentice, betrayed.* I paused, pen hovering, thinking of Tam with his ink-stained fingers and his family held hostage by a smiling clerk, and added: *One boy, forgiven — pending his own accounting.*

Then the door below banged open, and Rosalind's voice came up the stairs ahead of her, breathless and furious and more alive than anything else in this whole rotten season: "Casper! Casper, the bells — the bells in the capital have started ringing *wrong*, and Aldous — I found the contract, I know what's in the crown —"

I capped the ink, and picked up the sword, and went down to meet her.
