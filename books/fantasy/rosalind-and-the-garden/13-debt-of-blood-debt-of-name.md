## 13. Debt of Blood, Debt of Name

The alley behind the dyer's shop smelled of vinegar and old secrets, which was fitting, because Rosalind was about to make both.

She had three hunters at her back and one way out ahead of her, and the way out was a wall.

"Seamstress," said the first hunter, and his voice had the flat pleasantness of a man reading terms aloud. "You've been difficult to find. Our principal takes that personally."

Rosalind pressed her spine against the cold brick and did some rapid arithmetic. She had a needle in her sleeve, a thimble, four pins of varying quality, and half a spool of crimson thread she'd been saving for something important. Against three men whose debts were held in ledgers she'd once helped audit from the outside — well. The arithmetic was not encouraging.

"You could run," offered the second hunter, almost kindly. He was young. His hands shook. That frightened her more than the steady ones did; it meant he remembered being a person.

"I could," she agreed. "And you'd catch me, because your contracts don't allow you to be tired or slow or merciful. I've read enough of them." She had, in fact, copied out seventeen pages of guild contract law while posing as a seamstress for the palace's under-steward, who talked in his sleep about interest rates. "So instead I'm going to do something worse than run."

The third hunter tilted his head. "Worse?"

"I'm going to open an account."

She reached into the bodice of her dress — the plain gray one, the disguise that had survived two months of palace laundry rooms and one very suspicious duchess — and drew out the thing she had carried since the ruined chapel. It was small, no bigger than a walnut: a knot of black wood wrapped in thread she had spun herself, on a night when she had been desperate enough to pray to anything that listened.

Something had listened.

*Little borrower,* said the voice, arriving not through her ears but through the marrow of her, the way cold arrives through a floor. *You kept my gift.*

"It's not a gift if there's a price," Rosalind whispered. "That's the whole point of you, isn't it? You're very clear about that. I respect it."

*Flattery,* said the god, *is also a currency. A weak one. Spend carefully.*

The hunters had stopped advancing. They could feel it too — the alley going strange around the edges, the vinegar smell sharpening into something older, like rain on stone that hadn't seen rain in a thousand years. The young hunter took an involuntary step backward and bumped the wall, and where his shoulder touched the brick, moss bloomed.

"Who are you talking to?" the first hunter demanded, and for the first time his flat pleasantness cracked.

"To someone with better terms than yours," Rosalind said. Her heart was hammering, but her hands were steady, and she noticed this about herself with a kind of distant surprise — as though watching a stranger be brave and taking notes. *Good posture under pressure,* she noted. *Remember that later, when you're allowed to fall apart.*

She held up the knot of wood. "I need a favor. Three favors, actually, but let's start with one."

*Name it.*

"The men behind me. Their contracts bind them to take me to their principal. I want them unable to complete the task tonight. Not harmed." She looked at the young hunter's shaking hands. "Not harmed. Just... delayed. Until dawn."

*And what will you pay?*

Here was the part she had rehearsed in front of a cracked mirror in her rented room, back when the mirror still worked and she had still been able to look into it without flinching at what might look back.

"You told me once that everything has a price and everything can be paid. So name it yourself. But know this first—" and here she lifted her chin, because if she was going to bargain with a dead-and-returned god, she was going to do it standing straight, "—I am not desperate anymore. Desperate people accept any price. I am merely inconvenienced. Inconvenienced people negotiate."

There was a silence. Somewhere above them, a cat on the rooftops yowled once and thought better of it.

*I like you,* said the god, sounding genuinely surprised. *The last woman who called me wept the entire time. Very damp negotiation.*

"That does sound tedious."

*For the delay of your hunters until dawn:* the voice grew thoughtful, savoring, *your reflection.*

"My what?"

*Your reflection. All of them. Mirrors, water, polished silver, the shine on a knife. From this moment until the debt is discharged, nothing that shows you to yourself will show you. You will be a gap in every glass. People will find it unsettling. Children may cry.*

Rosalind thought of the cracked mirror. Thought of the duchess, who checked her appearance eleven times a day and would absolutely notice a seamstress with no face in her hand-glass. Thought of Aldous, the last time she'd seen him lucid, saying *you have Mother's eyes* — and how soon she would not be able to check whether that was true.

It was a real price. That was why it was worth paying.

"Done," she said.

*Say it as a bargain, little borrower. Words are walls. Build them properly.*

"I, Rosalind, sister of Aldous, daughter of the Marches, offer my reflection — all reflections, in all surfaces, until this debt is discharged — in exchange for the delay of these three men until dawn, unharmed and unharmed-by-others. Witnessed by the one who listens."

The knot of black wood warmed in her palm like a bird waking.

Then the world *tilted*.

It wasn't pain. It was subtraction. Rosalind felt something lift out of her the way steam lifts off bread — gently, completely — and the three hunters gasped as one, because where her face had been there was now a smooth absence, features present but somehow unreadable, like a portrait viewed through fogged glass.

"What—" the first hunter began.

He didn't finish, because the alley decided they should be elsewhere. Not thrown — *redirected*, politely, firmly, the way a good housekeeper redirects a guest who has wandered into the private wing. One moment the hunters stood before her; the next they stood at the mouth of the alley, facing the wrong direction entirely, arguing about which way they'd been going. By the time they sorted themselves out, they would head east, confidently, toward the river and away from anything Rosalind cared about. They would keep heading east until dawn made them forget why.

The young hunter paused at the corner. He looked back at her — at the gap where her face should be — and something in his expression loosened.

"Thank you," he said, very quietly, and then he turned and let himself be redirected.

Rosalind stood alone in the vinegar-smelling dark, holding a warm knot of wood, owing a god her own face-in-mirrors, and discovered that she was laughing. Quietly, hysterically, the laugh of a woman who has just learned the exact exchange rate between her dignity and her safety.

"Well," she told the empty alley. "At least I won't have to see myself doing this."

* * *

Casper found her an hour later at the safehouse above the cooper's yard, and to their eternal credit, they only stared for four seconds before asking a useful question.

"How many favors?"

"Two so far. Well — one spent, one pending. The reflection bought the delay. There's more coming." Rosalind sat at the deal table with her sleeves rolled up, copying figures. Bookkeeping calmed her. Numbers stayed where you put them, unlike brothers and gods. "The god says I have credit. It says credit is the most dangerous thing it offers."

"It would know," Casper said grimly. They set down a satchel of pilfered vault papers — the binding contract, or the pieces of it they'd recovered — and lowered themselves into the chair across from her with the careful stiffness of someone whose ribs had recently argued with a collector. "Rosalind. Listen to me. Every favor you buy moves us closer to the throne room, yes. But the god isn't lending you strength out of generosity. Old gods don't have generosity. They have *appetites with good manners*."

"I know."

"Do you? Because yesterday you asked me what happens if you run out of things to pay, and I didn't answer you honestly. I'm answering now. What happens is that it starts accepting things you haven't offered. Things you didn't agree to. The contracts get looser the deeper you go, and by the time you notice the terms changing—"

"Then I'll renegotiate," Rosalind said. "I've been reading your order's craft, Casper. You taught me half of it without meaning to. Words are walls. So I'll build faster than it can dig."

Casper looked at her for a long moment. There was something in their face Rosalind had learned to read over these weeks of shared danger and shared bread: the swordspeaker's particular grief, the look of someone watching a person walk willingly toward a door they themselves had sworn never to open.

"You sound like the guildmaster," Casper said finally. It was not a compliment. It was also, Rosalind noticed, not entirely an insult.

"Thank you."

"It wasn't—"

"I know what it wasn't." She smiled, and Casper flinched slightly at the smile, because even now, direct eye contact with her unsettled people. The face was there. The face was fine. It simply refused to be *confirmed* anywhere. Casper had caught their own reflection in the copper kettle that morning and reported, in a carefully neutral voice, that beside Rosalind's reflection there had been "a sort of smudge, shaped like a person, drinking tea."

"It's actually quite freeing," Rosalind said. "Do you know how much of my life I've spent worrying about how I look? As a princess, my face was state property. As a fugitive, it was a wanted poster. Now it belongs to a god, and I belong to myself. There's a joke in there somewhere. I'll find it when I've slept."

"You should sleep."

"After the second favor." She turned the black knot over in her fingers. "Because tomorrow night is the Night of Long Bells — you heard the bells this morning, wrong, all wrong, ringing a carillon nobody ordered — and after that there won't be time for careful bargains. I need to reach Aldous before the crown finishes digesting him. And the route through the servants' stair is watched by six men whose debts your guild holds, which means I need to be somewhere else entirely when they're looking. Which means—"

"Which means you're about to spend something worse than a reflection," Casper said quietly.

Rosalind's hands went still on the ledger.

"Yes," she said.

* * *

The god came at midnight, when the candle had burned down to a thumb's width and the cooper's yard below was silent except for the barrels settling in their racks, creaking like slow ships.

*You wish to spend again,* it said. No wind this time, no chill in the marrow. Just presence, vast and patient, folded down small enough to fit inside a rented room. *Before you ask: yes, I have been watching. Your brother's keeper grows restless. The bells ring wrong. You have less time than you think and more courage than is strictly advisable.*

"Is courage a currency?"

*Everything is a currency. Courage is simply the one you spend without counting.* A pause, dry as old vellum. *Which is why it buys so little.*

"I want to pass unseen past the watchers on the servants' stair. Tomorrow night. For one hour."

*Unseen? Or unnoticed?*

"Is there a difference?"

*A great one. Unseen is a trick of light — cheap, vulgar, and easily dispelled by anyone carrying iron or faith. Unnoticed is a trick of attention. People will see you, speak to you, step aside for you, and remember none of it. You will be the most forgettable woman in the palace. Even your allies will struggle to recall your face an hour after you leave a room.* The god's amusement rippled through the floorboards. *Though given your current arrangement with mirrors, perhaps you're collecting absences.*

Rosalind laughed despite herself, then pressed her lips together. Business. "What's the price?"

*Memories.*

The word landed in the room like a dropped coin.

*Childhood memories,* the god continued, mercilessly precise, the way a good creditor always is. *One per hour of passage. I will choose which. You will not miss them — that is the nature of memories; a missing memory leaves no hole, only a smooth place where you'll believe there was always nothing. You will not know what you lost. You will only know, afterward, that you paid.*

"And if I refuse?"

*Then tomorrow you will be seen, and caught, and your brother will remain a crown's puppet until the crown eats what's left of him. I do not threaten, little borrower. I itemize.*

Rosalind sat with it. Outside, far off, a single bell tolled — hesitated — and tolled again in a note that made the tea in its cup shiver into rings.

Her childhood. The word opened like a chest in her mind, and she looked inside it, deliberately, cataloguing. Mother teaching her letters with a stick in the garden dirt. Aldous falling out of the pear tree and lying in the grass laughing too hard to cry. The smell of the chapel where she'd prayed — no, wait, that was recent. The winter her father let her hold the seal. The song the washerwomen sang. Aldous, Aldous, Aldous — most of her childhood was Aldous, and she understood with sudden cold clarity that the god knew this, and that whatever it chose to take, it would likely take from him.

She could refuse. She could find another way. There was always another way; it would cost other people, other chances, and the clock of the wrong-ringing bells would tick down regardless.

Or she could pay. Knowingly. With her eyes open and her words built like walls.

"Casper says sacrifice is only noble if it's chosen," she said aloud. "Otherwise it's just damage. So I'm choosing. But I have conditions."

*Conditions,* repeated the god. It sounded delighted. *No one has given me conditions in nine hundred years.*

"First: you take memories, but you cannot take the ones I designate. I designate these—" she counted them off on her fingers, voice steady, "—my brother's face as a child. My mother's voice. The moment I decided to come to Vellum & Thorn. Everything else is negotiable."

*Designated exemptions weaken the whole account. Standard rates apply otherwise.*

"Second: when this is done — when the crown is unbound, one way or another — you return what remains returnable. Memories aren't spent like coin. They're pawned. I want the ticket."

A long silence. The candle flame leaned sideways, though there was no draft.

*Nine hundred years,* the god said softly, *and a seamstress in a borrowed dress is drafting terms at me. Do you know what I was, before the crown? Before the binding?*

"Tell me."

*I was the god of oaths. Of promises kept. Of the word that holds.* Something moved in the shadows of the room, vast and weary. *And you, little borrower, are the first mortal in nine centuries to treat my bargains as bargains rather than doom. Do you understand what that does to a creature like me? It makes me* — the pause was long, and when the voice returned it was quieter — *careful. Very well. Your conditions stand. Designated exemptions honored. Returnable memories pledged back upon discharge of the greater debt. Witness it and say it as a bargain.*

Rosalind bowed her head over the knot of black wood. Her heart was pounding, but her hands — her hands were steady. She noticed that about herself again, that watching-stranger-noting-bravery feeling, and thought: *whoever she is, she's doing well.*

"I, Rosalind, sister of Aldous, daughter of the Marches, offer my childhood memories — save those I have designated — one per hour of passage, in exchange for one hour of passing unnoticed through the palace on the Night of Long Bells. Returnable upon discharge, per terms spoken. Witnessed by the one who listens."

*Witnessed,* said the god. *Paid in advance. Two memories, drawn now, for the first two hours' insurance — the night will be long, and I dislike being owed mid-catastrophe.*

"Wait, I didn't agree to advan—"

Subtraction.

It was gentle. That was the horror of it. No pain, no tearing — just a smoothness spreading through her past like ice across a pond. She felt the edges of two somethings dissolve, and she reached for them and found... nothing to reach for. Not emptiness. Worse: completeness. A childhood that had always been exactly this size, exactly these shapes, with two soft places worn smooth as river stones, and no reason at all to think there had ever been anything else.

Except she had written it down. Weeks ago, in the early days, in the margin of her seamstress's account book, she had written: *Remember the pear tree. Remember the song the washerwomen sang. If you ever forget why you're doing this, read the margins.*

She grabbed the book with shaking hands and read her own handwriting like scripture.

*The pear tree. The song.* The words meant nothing. The words meant *everything*, because they proved there had been something, and proof was a wall, and walls were buildable.

"Oh, you clever, cruel old thing," she whispered. "You took the ones I'd have missed most, didn't you? And I'll never even grieve them properly."

*You exempted your brother's face,* the god observed. *But not your brother's laughter. Interesting choice. Was it deliberate?*

Rosalind opened her mouth. Closed it. Searched herself and found, with a lurch, that she could not remember the sound of Aldous laughing. Could remember that he had. Could not remember how it went.

"No," she said hoarsely. "It wasn't deliberate. Take that as a lesson in how well mortals count."

*Mortals never count,* the god agreed, and for the first time there was something in its voice that was not appetite, not amusement — something almost like gentleness, worn thin from disuse. *That is why they need gods. And gods, apparently, need accountants. Sleep now, little borrower. Tomorrow the bells ring wrong, and you will walk through your brother's prison unnoticed, and I will be watching to see what you build with the hours you've bought.*

The presence withdrew like a tide. The candle steadied. In the copper kettle by the hearth, the smudge-shaped absence where her reflection should be seemed, for just a moment, to sit up straighter — a woman squaring her shoulders before a long walk.

Rosalind blew out the candle, lay down on the narrow bed, and did not sleep. Instead she recited, over and over, into the dark, the inventory of what remained:

Mother's voice. The seal in Father's hands. The chapel. The decision. Aldous's face, small and scabbed-kneed and grinning. Aldous's face. *Aldous's face.*

Hold the walls. Build faster than it digs.

Tomorrow, the bells.
