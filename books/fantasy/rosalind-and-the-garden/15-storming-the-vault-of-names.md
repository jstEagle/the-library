## 15. Storming the Vault of Names

The trouble with raiding your own guildhouse, Casper reflected as they crouched in the drainage culvert beneath Vellum & Thorn, was that you knew exactly how well it was defended.

"You built that door," Rosalind whispered beside them. Her breath fogged in the culvert's cold air. "The one at the bottom of the stairs. I've seen it. It has *poetry* on it."

"I wrote the poetry," Casper admitted.

"Is it good poetry?"

"It's *effective* poetry. There's a difference." Casper checked the little lantern's shutter, letting out a blade of light no wider than a finger. "The third stanza is a binding clause. Anyone who reads it aloud without authorization finds themselves unable to stop rhyming for six hours. We lost an auditor that way in my grandfather's time. He rhymed through his own wedding vows. The marriage survived; his dignity did not."

Behind them, Tam huffed a laugh and then smothered it, because Tam had been laughing quietly and guiltily for three days now, ever since Casper had spared him and rewritten his debt in the same ink. He carried their rope, their picks, and a satchel of bread and hard cheese, because Casper had insisted that even a doomed raid deserved provisions. "You can't sign a contract on an empty stomach," they'd said, and Tam had looked at them like they were mad, which was fair.

Three of them, then. Against a guildhouse full of collectors, clerks, and whatever else the guildmaster could summon between here and the Vault of Names.

Casper tried not to think about the guildmaster. They failed, the way they always failed.

*She taught you to hold a pen,* said the small traitorous voice that lived behind Casper's sternum. *She taught you that ink is a promise and promises are sacred. And she has been selling prayers to dead gods for years, and she will stand in that vault doorway and smile at you while she stops you.*

"We go down the culvert," Casper said aloud, because planning was easier than feeling. "It runs under the east wing. It comes up in the counting room, which is empty after second bell — I made sure of that myself, years ago, when I needed somewhere to think. From there, the stair to the vault. One door. One poem."

"And the guildmaster?" Rosalind asked softly.

Casper adjusted the lantern shutter a fraction too long. "The guildmaster," they said, "will be wherever she needs to be."

* * *

The culvert smelled of old rain and older money, which was appropriate; half the guild's wealth had passed through this drain in one form or another, usually disguised as rainwater. Casper counted arches as they went — eleven, twelve, thirteen — and felt the strange vertigo of moving through the bones of a building they had memorized the way other people memorized prayers.

Tam kept pace at their shoulder. He hadn't spoken much since the night Casper had torn up his contract with the crowned thing and written him a new one — a debt owed not to fear but to choice, repayable in acts of his own devising. Tonight he'd repaid a portion of it by picking the padlock on the culvert grate in forty seconds flat.

"My father was a locksmith," he'd said afterward, not quite meeting Casper's eyes. "Before. Before we owed anyone anything."

"He'd be proud of the forty seconds," Casper had said, and Tam had almost smiled.

Now, at the thirteenth arch, Casper stopped and held up a hand. Above them, faint through the stone, came footsteps. Many footsteps. Boots with the particular rhythm of men who walked in step because a contract told them to.

"The hunters," Rosalind breathed.

"On rotation," Casper murmured. "They'll pass over us twice an hour. Which means the guildmaster knows we're coming, or suspects, and hasn't sealed the culvert." They frowned at the dark ahead. "Which means she wants us to come this way."

"That's comforting," said Tam.

"It isn't. But it's *informative*, which is better than comforting. Comforting tells you what you want to hear. Informative tells you where the trap is." Casper started forward again. "My grandmother used to say that."

"Your grandmother sounds like she'd have liked the guildmaster," Rosalind said.

"She *was* the guildmaster. Two generations back. Family business." Casper's jaw tightened. "That's rather the whole problem, isn't it."

The culvert ended at a ladder of iron rungs set into the stone, green with age, and above the rungs, a hatch that opened — Casper listened, counted to sixty, listened again — into silence. The counting room. Their counting room, once, with its high window and its single chair and the view of nothing at all, which had been the point.

They came up into the dark and the smell of paper and tallow, and Casper's chest did something complicated, because the chair was still there, and someone had dusted it.

"She kept your room," Rosalind said quietly, following their gaze.

"She kept an *asset*. Rooms don't mean anything." Casper crossed to the far wall, found the seam their fingers had known since childhood, and pressed the three stones that mattered. The wall sighed open onto the vault stair, and cold air rose up to meet them, air that tasted of iron and candle smoke and, beneath it, something older — the dry mineral smell of very old vellum, vellum that remembered being alive.

"One more thing," Casper whispered, turning back. "When we reach the door. Whatever happens — whatever she says — do not answer her questions. She can't compel speech. Nobody can. That's the whole point of the guild. Everything must be *written*. Words given freely are the only words that bind." They hesitated. "She knows that better than anyone. So she'll try to make you want to answer. That's all she needs. Wanting."

Rosalind met their eyes in the sliver of lantern light. "And you?"

"I've been wanting to ask her questions for weeks," Casper said. "I intend to lose that fight spectacularly."

* * *

The stair descended farther than any stair had a right to descend inside a building whose foundations Casper had personally surveyed. Forty steps. Sixty. A hundred. The candles in the wall niches lit themselves as they passed and gutted themselves out behind, so that they moved inside a bubble of light through an ocean of dark, and the air grew colder and heavier, until Casper felt the weight of it pressing on the ledger-sense they'd carried since birth — the sense that knew where every debt in the world sat, like a lighthouse keeper knows the coastline.

Down here, the debts were *old*. Down here, the debts had teeth.

At the bottom stood the door.

It was black oak banded in lead, tall as two men, and across its face, in gilt letters that caught the candlelight like fish scales, ran the poem. Casper had written it at nineteen, full of their own cleverness, and had never once been able to read it since without wincing at the meter.

*Who knocks at the house of names*
*Knocks at their own;*
*What seeks within these frames*
*Was never loaned.*

"Still bad?" Rosalind murmured.

"Worse than I remembered."

Tam read the third stanza silently, lips moving, and went slightly pale. "You weren't joking about the rhyming."

"I never joke about clauses."

And then, from the shadow beside the door — a shadow that had been empty a moment before, or had perhaps been waiting there since before the candles learned to burn — a voice.

"You always did hate that stanza."

The guildmaster stepped into the light.

She was smaller than Casper remembered, which was absurd, because she had not shrunk in the three weeks since Casper had last stood in her study and been offered the choice that had split their life into before and after. She wore no grandeur. She wore the same gray shawl she'd worn every day of Casper's apprenticeship, and her hands were ink-stained to the second knuckle, and she looked at Casper the way she had looked at them over twenty years of ledgers: patiently, fondly, and with the absolute certainty of a woman who had already read to the end of the page.

"Master Sable," Casper said. Formal. Cold. Their voice only shook a little.

"Casper." His name — *their* name — in her mouth landed like a coin dropped down a well. "You brought the girl. You brought the boy who sold you. And you brought bread and cheese, because of course you did." Her eyes crinkled. "You cannot storm a vault hungry. I taught you that."

"You taught me everything," Casper said. "That's why I'm here. Move away from the door."

"No."

One word. No explanation, no bargain, none of the silver rhetoric she'd used in her study. Just *no*, delivered the way she signed contracts: finally.

"Then we have a problem," Casper said, "because I am the last swordspeaker, and you are—" they searched for it, and hated that they had to search— "*something*, now. Aren't you. Something that trades with things that should be ash. What did they give you, Master Sable? What does it cost to keep a guild alive for thirty years on the goodwill of drowned gods?"

Something flickered across her face — pain, briefly, honestly — and was filed away, the way she filed everything.

"You think I did it for the guild," she said. "That's the kindest version, so I'll let you keep it a moment longer." She folded her hands. "Ask me your question, child. You've been carrying it up and down the country like a stone in your shoe. Ask."

And Casper, who had sworn on the stair not to answer, not to engage, not to give her the wanting she needed — Casper heard their own mouth open.

"Why didn't you tell me? About the pages. About the trade. About *any of it*. Twenty years, and you let me audit a guild that was already sold, and never once—"

"Because you would have done exactly this," she said simply. "Exactly this, but alone, and badly, and you'd have died in a ditch outside the capital holding a contract you couldn't read. I bought you time instead. Time is the only impossible thing I ever managed to trade fairly." She unfolded her hands. "Now. The contract behind this door belongs to the crowned thing, in law if not in spirit. If you take it, the guild falls. Every collector it holds, every debt it carries, every family we protect by holding their paper — all of it comes due. Do you understand what I'm standing in front of? Not a door. A dam."

"Then let it break," Rosalind said, stepping forward, and her voice was the voice Casper had first heard in the guildhouse foyer, desperate and clear. "Let it break, and we'll write new terms. That's what I've learned from all of you — that words can be rewritten. Even yours. *Especially* yours."

The guildmaster looked at her for a long moment. Something moved behind her eyes, some ledger being balanced at speed.

"Yes," she said at last, softly. "Yes, I believe you might." And then, to Casper, with terrible gentleness: "Read the fourth stanza, child. The one nobody's ever had cause to use."

Casper's blood went cold. "There is no fourth stanza."

"There is. I added it the night the crown began to whisper. I've been waiting three weeks for you to come and need it." She stepped aside from the door — aside, deliberately, placing herself not between them and the oak but directly beneath the lintel, in the space where the door would swing. "It opens the way for those who name their purpose aloud. Freely given words, freely opened doors. The oldest rule there is."

"Then say it yourself," Tam said. "Why do *we*—"

"Because the stanza requires a speaker who owes the vault nothing," she said. "And I owe it *everything*. Thirty years of payments. I couldn't open this door if I recited until doomsday." Her smile was small and real and broke Casper's heart in a place they hadn't known had walls. "But you — you clean-handed, stubborn, sentimental creature — you never traded with them. Not once. Not even tonight. Your debt is to *me*, and I forgive it. There. Witnessed. Now the door owes you passage, and I owe the dark a doorkeeper, and both accounts balance."

"Master Sable." Casper's voice cracked. "There are other ways—"

"There are always other ways. That's what I love about you. Read the stanza."

Above them, far up the stair, the first alarm bell began to ring — the deep, wrong bell, the one that meant collectors released. The guildmaster glanced upward without surprise.

"They'll be here in four minutes. The stanza takes one to speak. Go."

Casper stepped forward, and the gilt letters shifted under the candlelight, and there it was — a fourth stanza, in ink so fresh it gleamed, in handwriting Casper knew better than their own:

*For those who come to unmake wrong,*
*Who pay in truth and not in gold:*
*The door swings wide, the way is long,*
*And someone old must hold the fold.*

Their purpose. Aloud. Freely.

Casper opened their mouth, and found, to their horror, that grief had tied every word in knots.

So it was Rosalind who spoke — clear and steady, her hand finding Casper's shoulder — "We come to unmake a wrong. We pay in truth. Open."

The great bolts drew themselves back one by one, each with a sound like a bone setting, and the black oak swung inward on darkness that breathed.

The guildmaster settled herself against the doorframe, gray shawl and ink-stained hands, and looked, for the first time in twenty years, entirely at peace.

"Hold the fold," she said. "Go on. And Casper—" 

Casper turned in the doorway, one foot in the dark, and she smiled at them the way she had smiled at every finished ledger they'd ever handed her.

"When you read what's in there — remember that I was trying, in my clumsy, cowardly, expensive way, to leave you something better than the truth." Her eyes went past them, into the vault, and widened just slightly, as if she could see through the dark to the thing on its pedestal. "Remember that someone has to keep the books. Even the ugly ones. *Especially* the ugly ones."

The first collectors came around the stair's last turn, three of them, contracts glowing faintly through their coats like brands under cloth.

The guildmaster straightened, spread her arms along the doorframe, and began — of all things, in all voices — to rhyme.

"GO," she said, between verses, and the door began to close, and Casper ran into the dark with Rosalind's hand in theirs and Tam's lamp swinging wild, and the last thing they saw was her shawl, and the last thing they heard was her voice, steady as a signature, buying them four minutes with six hours of doggerel.

* * *

The Vault of Names was smaller than a chapel and older than the guildhouse above it, and it held one thing.

On a pedestal of black stone, under glass gone cloudy with centuries, lay a scroll of vellum so old it had gone the color of ivory, and upon it, in a hand that shook with dying, a contract. Casper lifted the glass with hands that did not shake, because shaking was a luxury, and read the opening lines by Tam's lamp.

*In the year the bells rang wrong, I, Alderic, King of Vellamark, being grievously sick unto death...*

"Aldous," Rosalind whispered. "Aldous is named for him. Every king of our line is."

Casper read on. The clauses unrolled like a snake uncoiling — a king bargaining at the edge of death, a god called to witness, a blessing asked for and a binding given, and somewhere in the middle of the third clause, words that made the lamp flame lean sideways though no wind moved.

Casper read them twice. Then a third time, because the first two readings refused to be true.

"Rosalind," they said, and their voice came out very quiet, the voice they used for entries that changed lives. "Your brother's crown. Do you know whose blessing your ancestor asked for, on his deathbed? Whose favor he begged to legitimize his line forever?"

Rosalind shook her head slowly.

Casper turned the scroll toward her, and the lamplight fell across the name, written in a dying king's failing hand, and it was a name Casper had known all their life — had sworn oaths by, had written ten thousand times in ten thousand ledgers, a name every schoolchild in Vellamark could recite.

The god of oaths himself.

And below it, in the same shaking script, the clause that had trapped him: *let the giver of the blessing be bound within the token of the blessing, world without end.*

The god of oaths, imprisoned in the crown he had been tricked into touching.

Casper looked at Rosalind, and Rosalind looked at the scroll, and somewhere far above them, muffled by a hundred feet of stone and one rhyming old woman, the bells of the capital began — very faintly, very wrongly — to ring.
