## 16. The Crown Remembers

The contract was heavier than it looked.

Rosalind had expected that, somehow. Everything in the Vault of Names had been heavy — the air, the silence, the guilt of walking out past the guildmaster's body still holding the door she'd sworn to hold. But this was a different weight. This was paper and ink and wax, and yet when Casper set it on the table of their safehouse — a rented room above a chandler's shop that smelled of tallow and regret — the floorboards creaked beneath it as though it were a man sitting down.

"Generations," Casper said. "It's been down there since before my order had a name."

"And now we read it." Rosalind pulled a chair to the table. Her hands were still shaking from the vault, from the door, from the sound the guildmaster had made when the locks came down behind them — not a scream, just a small, dignified *ah*, like someone remembering they'd left the stove lit. "Casper. Before I touch it. Whatever's in here — whatever it says — we agreed. We agreed we'd tell each other the truth even if it's ugly."

"I remember." Casper sat across from her. In the candlelight their face was pale and set, the face of an accountant about to open a ledger they already knew was wrong. "I've read contracts for thirty years, Rosalind. I have never once been afraid of one until tonight."

She reached out and broke the seal.

* * *

The first thing she noticed was the handwriting. She had expected something grand — a king's hand, all flourishes and self-importance. Instead the letters were cramped and hurried, written by someone whose fingers barely worked, and there were brown stains on the page where drops of blood or tears or both had fallen and been blotted impatiently.

*"To whosoever keeps this record,"* it began, *"I, Alderic the Third, King of Vessany, being in my last illness, do set down what was done in this chamber so that no one may ever again call it justice."*

"Aldous's great-great-grandfather," Rosalind murmured. "The Dying King. The histories say he made a holy covenant on his deathbed. That he bound his line to the gods' protection forever."

"The histories say a great many things," Casper said quietly. "Keep reading."

She kept reading.

*"They brought me a crown,"* the old king had written. *"A thing of iron and blessing, forged by the god of oaths himself — Verath, the First Witness, who hears every promise ever spoken and holds every debt. They told me: let him bless your reign and your kingdom will never break its word. They did not tell me what a god of oaths must be made of. Every promise. Every vow. Every debt ever sworn under heaven, carried in one mind, forever."*

Rosalind looked up. "That's — no wonder he wanted to bless things. If you were made of everyone's promises—"

"You'd want more of them," Casper finished. "You'd *need* more. Keep going."

*"My advisors were afraid,"* the dying king wrote. *"Not of the god. Of me. A king who cannot lie is a king who cannot rule, they said. And Verath, having blessed the crown, could not be unmade — you cannot kill the thing that holds your own promises, for killing him unmakes every oath ever sworn, and the world would come apart at its seams. So instead they tricked him. They asked him to swear the binding himself. To witness his own imprisonment. And because he is what he is — because he cannot refuse a properly spoken oath — he said yes."*

The candle guttered. Neither of them moved to fix it.

*"He is in the crown,"* Alderic wrote. *"He has been in the crown for sixty years, wearing my head like a prison wears a prisoner, and I can hear him trying to be gentle about it. He does not want to rule us. He wants only to collect what is owed — and everything is owed, everything has always been owed, and there is no one left to pay but me. I sign this contract not to bind him further. I sign it so that someday, someone will know what was done, and do better."*

Below that, in a different hand — steadier, colder, the hand of a lawyer — were the terms. Rosalind read them twice and then a third time, because her eyes kept sliding off them the way eyes slide off a bill you don't want to open.

The binding was simple. Elegant, even. She hated how elegant it was.

*The god Verath shall remain within the crown of Vessany until such time as the crown is unmade by word or deed. Should the crown be unmade, the god shall be released entire, with all powers, claims, and collections appertaining thereto.*

"There it is," Casper said. Their voice was very flat, which Rosalind had learned was how they sounded when they were standing at the edge of something high. "Unbind the king, free the god. Free the god, and every prayer anyone ever prayed gets called due. Every bargain struck in desperation. Every 'I'd give anything.' He'll come to collect, and he'll be *right* to, because that's what he is. Not cruel. Just... owed."

* * *

For a long while neither of them spoke. Downstairs, the chandler's dog barked once at nothing and thought better of it.

"So," Rosalind said finally, and heard her own voice come out too bright, the way it did when she was holding something together with her teeth. "Let me make sure I have this right. My brother has spent two years with a god chained to his skull. A god who never asked to be a prison warden. And if we save Aldous—"

"We free the thing that ruined him."

"—and if we *don't* save him—"

"He dies wearing it. And the god goes on collecting through whoever wears the crown next. Which, given the succession, would be our friend Duke Maravel, who I have met, and who once tried to sell the same bridge to three separate merchants." Casper rubbed their eyes. "So. There's no clean ending. There's the ending where we free a god of debts upon a world that owes him everything, and there's the ending where we leave an innocent man in chains forever, and there is no third page."

Rosalind stood up. She hadn't decided to; her legs simply refused to sit still anymore. She walked to the window, where the city lay below in its ordinary evening sprawl — lamplighters working their rounds, a fishwife arguing cheerfully with no one, smoke rising from ten thousand chimneys where ten thousand families were making ten thousand small promises to each other. *Supper soon. Behave yourself. I'll always come back.*

Every one of those, she thought, was in his ledger. Every single one.

"When I prayed in that chapel," she said, "something answered me. I've been assuming it was him. Verath. Reaching through the cracks."

"It fits. You were asking for a miracle on credit. That's his whole trade."

"But what if—" She turned around. "What if it wasn't? What if some of the gods coming back are coming back *because* of him? Because he's straining against the binding, and the cracks go both ways?" She laughed, and it came out ragged. "Casper, I negotiated with a harvest god using my own future as collateral. What if that future is already spent? What if I'm not saving my brother at all? What if I'm just the crack he widened?"

Casper rose too, slowly, the way they did everything — deliberate, unhurried, a person who had learned that haste was where mistakes lived. They crossed the room and put one hand flat on the contract, between her and the terrible elegant terms.

"Then we're back where we started," they said. "Which is: someone has to choose, knowing full well it might be wrong."

"That's not comforting."

"I'm not a comforting person. I'm a bookkeeper." But there was something almost warm underneath it, the way there always was, banked low like coals. "Here's what I know, Rosalind Sterling, and I know it because I have read more contracts than any living soul. Contracts are written by people who are frightened. Frightened people write narrow terms. Narrow terms break. Every binding in history has failed eventually — not because the words were weak, but because the world is wider than the words." They tapped the parchment. "This one has held sixty years. It's a masterpiece. And it's failing *right now*, because a girl knelt in a ruin and prayed, and something answered, and none of this was in the terms."

Rosalind stared at them. "Are you saying what I think you're saying?"

"I'm saying the contract assumes two parties. The crown and the kingdom. It never accounted for a third party who reads fine print for a living, and another who writes her own." Casper almost smiled. Almost. "There's no clean ending in these pages. Fine. Then we stop looking for one in these pages."

* * *

They worked through the night, because that was what they knew how to do — the two of them, the bookkeeper and the sister, spreading the contract out beside scraps of notes and candle stubs and the cold remains of a meat pie Casper had bought from a boy downstairs and forgotten to eat.

Rosalind read the terms aloud, over and over, hunting for the seam. Casper annotated in their precise, upright hand, marking clauses the way a surgeon marks incisions. Somewhere past midnight she caught herself laughing — actually laughing — at the absurdity of clause fourteen, which stipulated that the god could not collect any debt incurred *in good faith*, and she demanded of the empty air how a god of oaths defined good faith, and Casper, deadpan, replied, "Badly. He defines everything badly. Have you *met* him? He's currently wearing a hat."

But under the laughter, the vertigo never quite stopped. It was there every time she looked at the contract and saw her brother's name woven into the terms without his consent — *the crown of Vessany, and its bearer, and his heirs in perpetuity.* Aldous had inherited a prison the way you inherit a nose. He'd been born owing something he never borrowed.

And it was there, worst of all, in the quiet moments, when she thought about the chapel. About the voice that had answered her. *Come to the guildhouse,* it had said. *Bring your grief to the ones who keep accounts.* She had thought she was being helped. Now she wondered if she'd been *collected* — if her prayer had been entered into a ledger somewhere, in a hand far older and colder than Casper's, and the balance was still accruing.

"Casper," she said, near dawn, when the candles had burned down to puddles. "If we do this. If we find a way to unmake the crown without freeing him whole — or free him on terms — what happens to the prayers? All the ones people are saying right now? The little ones. *Let her live. Let me get home. Let it not be cancer, let it not be war, let him love me back.*"

Casper was quiet for a long time.

"Those aren't contracts," they said at last. "Most of them. Most prayers are just... wishes. No consideration offered, no terms accepted. He can't collect on a wish." A pause. "But the ones with prices attached. The bargains. The *I'd give anything*s." They looked at her, and Rosalind saw that Casper Coldharbour, last of the swordspeakers, keeper of impossible ledgers, was afraid — truly afraid, for maybe the first time since she'd known them. "Those he can collect. And after sixty years in an iron hat, Rosalind, I don't think he'll be gentle about it."

Outside, the sky was turning the grey of old pewter. And somewhere across the sleeping city — from the palace on its hill — came a sound.

Bells.

One bell. Deep and slow and wrong, ringing at an hour when no bell should ring, in a tone no bell in Vessany had ever been cast to make. The chandler's dog began to howl. Down in the street, a lamplighter dropped his pole.

Rosalind went cold from the soles of her feet upward. "That's not the cathedral bell."

"No," Casper said. They were already moving, sweeping the contract into its oilcloth, hands steady now that there was finally something to do. "And it's not stopping."

It wasn't. The second bell came a breath after the first, and the third before that one had finished, faster and faster, bells that had hung silent for centuries waking up all over the city, and beneath them — faint, but growing, unmistakable — a sound like a great voice clearing its throat after a long imprisonment, preparing to say a great many names.

Rosalind grabbed her coat. "The terms," she said. "We need new terms. Tonight. Before he finishes counting."

Casper slung the satchel over their shoulder and, for the first time in thirty years of careful, rule-bound life, smiled like a person about to commit a crime.

"Then we'd better start writing."
