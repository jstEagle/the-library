## 17. Rosalind's Ledger

The contract that had bound a god for three hundred years fit on a single page.

Rosalind turned it over twice, looking for the rest of it. The vellum was thin as onion skin, browned at the edges, and the old king's signature sat at the bottom like a spider drowned in ink. Above it ran eleven lines of cramped script. Eleven lines. She had written longer shopping lists.

"This is it?" she said. "This little thing has been strangling my brother's head for a century?"

"Contracts are never about length," Casper said. They were seated across from her at the long table in the guildhouse's back room, the one with the candle-scarred surface and the drawer that stuck. "They're about weight. A single word, placed rightly, can hold more than a thousand placed badly. That's what your brother's predecessor understood — or what whoever wrote this for him understood. Look at line four."

She looked. *The bearer shall carry the blessing until the blessing is paid.* Simple words. Her eyes kept sliding off them, the way water slides off oiled cloth.

"It doesn't want to be read," she said.

"No. It wants to be obeyed. There's a difference, and the difference is everything." Casper drew the page gently from her fingers and set it flat between them, weighting the corners with four smooth stones from the windowsill. "Now. Before you write a single word of your own, you're going to learn why most contracts are lies."

* * *

Casper taught the way they did everything — precisely, patiently, and with an undercurrent of dry humor that Rosalind had come to treasure like coins sewn into a hem.

"The first rule of binding words," they said, tapping the table, "is that a contract cannot create. It can only trade. Every clause must give something and take something, in both directions, or it isn't a contract — it's a command, and commands rot. The old king's scribes didn't understand that. They thought they could trap the god of oaths in his own blessing forever, for nothing, on their side. So the contract has been straining against them for three hundred years, looking for the flaw. And it found one." They touched the crown-shaped shadow of the thing they'd carried out of the vault. "It found a king who would wear it."

"And mine won't have flaws?"

"Yours will have flaws. Everything human does. The trick is to choose your flaws before someone else finds them." Casper pulled a fresh sheet toward themselves and wrote in a quick, sure hand: *The lender lends; the borrower borrows; both know what they hold.* "Read that back."

Rosalind read it. "It sounds like nothing."

"It sounds like nothing because it's honest. Honesty always sounds smaller than a lie. A lie promises the world. An honest clause just says what is." They looked up, and in the lamplight their face was tired and young and very old all at once. "You want to bind a god, a crown, and a kingdom into one agreement. Do you understand what you're actually proposing? Not destruction. Not rescue. Marriage."

Rosalind laughed despite herself. "That's a horrible word for it."

"It's the accurate word for it. You're asking three parties who have spent centuries hurting each other to sit at one table and agree on terms. Gods don't do that. Crowns don't do that. Kingdoms barely do it with themselves." A pause. "But it has been done once. By a woman with a ledger and no army. Which is why I'm going to teach you instead of stopping you."

"You could stop me?"

"I could make it very difficult," Casper said, with the ghost of a smile. "I've decided not to. Partly because you're the first person in thirty years who's asked me to teach rather than to serve. Mostly because when you talked about mercy clauses at the vault door, I realized you understand something the guild forgot." They turned the fresh sheet around so she could read it properly. "Debt is not a weapon. It's a promise that someone believes you'll keep."

* * *

They worked through the night.

Rosalind learned that binding words had a grammar beneath grammar — that *shall* weighed differently than *will*, that *until* was a door and *forever* was a wall, and that walls were how you ended up with a mad king and a straining god. She learned that every party to a contract needed a way out, or they'd claw one out of the walls eventually, and the claw-marks were what people called curses.

"Say your terms aloud," Casper said, somewhere past midnight, when her eyes burned and the candles had burned down to stubs. "Not to me. To the room. Contracts begin as speech. If it sounds wrong in your own mouth, it's wrong on the page."

Rosalind stood. Her legs had gone stiff as broom handles. She felt ridiculous, and then she stopped feeling ridiculous, because the words came up from somewhere below her ribs where she'd been keeping them since the ruined chapel, since the night something old had answered her prayer and she'd run instead of listening.

"The god of oaths shall leave the crown," she said. "And the crown shall become only gold. And the king—" Her voice caught. She steadied it. "The king shall live, diminished if he must, but free, and forgiven for what was done while he was worn."

The room was quiet. Even the stuck drawer seemed to have stopped rattling in the wind.

"Good," Casper said softly. "Now the hard part. What do the god and the kingdom get? Because right now you've written a rescue, and rescues are just theft with better manners."

Rosalind sat back down slowly. "What does a god get?"

"What does any creditor get? Payment. But think carefully, because the god of oaths doesn't want gold. It wants what it was made from. It wants promises kept. Three hundred years trapped in a crown full of broken ones." Casper folded their hands. "If you can figure out what to pay a god that doesn't cost a soul, you'll have done something no keeper of this guild has ever managed."

* * *

She dreamed of the chapel that night, and woke with the answer sitting in her chest like a swallowed coal.

It wasn't clever. That was what frightened her. All night she'd been reaching for cleverness — trades and loopholes, the guild's whole architecture of gotcha — and the answer was none of those things. It was almost embarrassingly simple.

She found Casper in the kitchen at dawn, drinking something gray and calling it tea, and said, "The god gets prayers answered honestly."

Casper lowered the cup. "Explain."

"When it wore the crown, every prayer spoken to the king went into it, and it couldn't answer any of them without twisting through the contract's flaws. That's why the wishes came out cruel. That's why Aldous argues with himself. It's been starving in a room full of food it isn't allowed to touch." She paced the length of the kitchen, hands moving. "So we feed it. Not worship — I'm not asking anyone to kneel. But honest petitions. Small ones. A farmer asks for rain, the god answers with rain, the rain is real, the debt is paid clean on both sides. It gets to be what it was made to be. And the kingdom gets—"

"A god that isn't starving," Casper finished. Their voice had gone strange. "A god with no reason to take. Rosalind. Do you know what you've just invented?"

"A terrible idea?"

"The opposite of a trap." They set down the cup and looked at her with an expression she'd never seen on them before — something between awe and grief. "Every binding in this guild's history is a cage. Yours is a well. Things can be drawn from it, but nothing drowns unless it chooses to stay under." They laughed, short and disbelieving. "I have spent my whole life keeping ledgers of impossible things, and you just wrote one over breakfast."

"It's not written yet." She held out her hand for the pen. "And I want you to check every line. Twice. Then a third time, and then I want you to try to break it, because if you can't, the god certainly can't, and if you can—"

"Then we fix it before it costs your brother anything else." Casper gave her the pen with a small formal bow, entirely serious. "Keeper Sterling."

"Don't call me that yet."

"You signed the guildmaster's succession page last night."

"I signed a draft. Drafts aren't real yet." But she was smiling as she said it, and pulled the fresh vellum toward her, and the pen felt different in her hand than it ever had before — heavier, warmer, like something alive deciding to cooperate.

* * *

Writing it took three days.

The first draft failed because she tried to protect Aldous too completely, and Casper showed her how the clause bent inward on itself, a shield so tight it became a second prison. The second draft failed because she gave the god too much, and Casper read it in silence for a long time and then said, quietly, "This is beautiful, and it would end the world in forty years, and I need you to trust that I know the difference." The third draft she wrote slowly, a line a day, reading each one aloud to the empty room until it sounded like truth instead of hope.

By the fourth morning the contract lay complete on the table between them, and Rosalind stared at it and felt something she hadn't felt since childhood: that the world was a thing she could hold.

*Three parties,* the opening line read. *One agreement, freely entered, severable by none.*

To the god: honest petitions, honestly answered, its name spoken with respect in the kingdom's small prayers, and release from the crown within a day of signing.

To the crown: to become gold again, memoryless, its long servitude acknowledged in a single line of the kingdom's histories — *and it held what no one else could have held* — which was, Casper admitted, the strangest mercy clause ever drafted, and possibly the only one ever addressed to an object.

To the kingdom: a king freed or, if freedom came too late, a council chosen by lot and learning, and a standing office — hers — whose whole duty was to hear debts cried unfair and unmake them.

And to herself, in the final lines, the price. Because there had to be one. She'd argued with Casper about it for a full evening, and lost, and was glad she'd lost. *The author binds herself to the terms herein for her natural life, and should she break them, let the breaking fall on her alone.*

"There," she said, setting down the pen. Her hand was shaking. "It's done."

"It's drafted," Casper corrected, but gently. They read it through once, twice, a third time, lips moving, hunting for the flaw the way a surgeon hunts for the wound. At last they straightened. "I can't break it. I want to be clear about what that means — I have broken every contract this guild has ever shown me, including two that were supposed to be unbreakable. Yours holds." They looked up. "It holds because every party in it can walk away whole. That's the entire secret. That's the secret nobody in three hundred years of keeping ledgers wrote down, because we were too busy counting what people owed to notice what they needed."

Rosalind exhaled a breath she felt she'd been holding since the ruined chapel. "Then we take it to him."

"To the throne room. During the bells." Casper began rolling the vellum with careful hands, and their voice dropped. "There's one thing left, and I'd be a poor teacher if I hid it. When the god reads this — and it will read it, instantly, completely, the moment it's in the same room — it will know it's being offered the first fair deal of its existence. And it will also know exactly who wrote it, and what you cost yourself to get here, and it may decide that the fairest payment of all would be you."

"Let it try to collect," Rosalind said. "I've read my own contract. I know what I'm worth."

Outside, far across the city, the first bell of evening rang — and rang wrong, a note that bent in the air like heat over a road, and did not stop when it should have stopped.

Casper's head came up sharply. "That's early. The bells weren't due until tomorrow night."

Rosalind took the contract from the table and held it against her chest like a shield, like a promise, like a letter she'd finally finished writing.

"Then we're out of time," she said. "Come on. We deliver it tonight."
