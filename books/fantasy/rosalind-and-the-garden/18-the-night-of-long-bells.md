## 18. The Night of Long Bells

**CASPER**

The first bell rang at midnight, and it rang wrong.

Casper had spent thirty years listening to the capital's bells the way a sailor listens to weather — half-consciously, always counting. There were nine of them across the city: the great bronze Vesper at the cathedral, the cracked old Widow in the market district that always came in a half-beat late, the little chapel bells like children trailing their parents. Together they made a sound Casper had stopped hearing years ago, the way you stop hearing your own heartbeat.

Tonight every bell in the city struck at once, and they did not agree.

It wasn't discord, exactly. Discord would have been almost comforting — bells out of tune were bells being bells. This was something else. Each bell was ringing its own midnight, and the midnights did not match. The Vesper tolled deep and slow, solemn as a funeral. The Widow rang bright and quick, almost joyful. Somewhere across the river a bell Casper couldn't even see was ringing *backward*, each swing pulling the sound in before it pushed it out.

"That's not possible," said Wren, the apprentice, standing beside them on the guildhouse steps with his collar up against the cold. He'd been forgiven, reinstated, and assigned to carry things, in roughly that order, and he clutched the satchel of binding-tools now like it might bite him.

"Most of what's happened this week isn't," Casper said.

"No, I mean—" Wren pointed at the backward bell. "Sound doesn't work like that."

"It does tonight." Casper pulled their coat tighter. Above the rooftops, the sky was doing something worse than the bells. Clouds moved in two directions at once, layers sliding against each other like hands rubbing together. And between the clouds, where stars should have been, there were gaps — not empty gaps but *expectant* ones, patches of darkness that felt like held breath.

The god in the crown was straining against its binding, and the world was starting to come apart at the seams of everything anyone had ever promised anything.

"Ledgers," Casper said suddenly.

"What?"

"The ledgers." They turned back toward the guildhouse door. "Every contract we hold. If reality is thinning, if debts are collecting early—"

They didn't finish, because they didn't have to. From inside the guildhouse came a sound Casper had never heard in thirty years of service: the great ledger-room, with its ten thousand bound volumes, was *singing*. A low harmonic hum, page after page vibrating, ink lifting faintly off paper and hanging in the air like iron filings finding a magnet.

Wren made a small noise. "They're waking up."

"They're being *called*." Casper pushed through the doors. "Every debt in this city is about to try to collect itself at once. Get Rosalind. Get everyone. We move for the palace now, before the streets become—"

Before the streets became what, Casper didn't say, because at that moment a ledger on the nearest shelf snapped open by itself, and a name rose off its page in letters of pale fire, and somewhere across the city a man began screaming as three years of unpaid interest arrived all together, all at once, in a form no one had ever specified because no one had ever imagined they'd need to.

* * *

**ROSALIND**

Rosalind was in the guildhouse kitchen when the flour apologized to her.

She had been sitting at the long table with her own ledger open — her ledger, written in her own hand, with terms she understood — trying to review the contract one final time before they went to the palace. Around her, the kitchen had been quietly going strange for an hour. The fire burned green, then blue, then briefly sang a lullaby nobody recognized. The cat, a scarred gray thing called Turnip who belonged to no one and tolerated everyone, had stared at a wall for twenty minutes and then walked directly through it and back again, looking mildly embarrassed.

But the flour was new. She had knocked the jar over reaching for her pen, and the white spill across the table had gathered itself into a shape — a crude little figure, kneeling — and a voice like wind through wheat had said, from everywhere and nowhere, *"We are sorry. You dropped us. That was our fault."*

Rosalind looked at it for a long moment.

"You're a jar of flour," she said.

*"We are also a promise,"* the flour said. *"Someone, somewhere, once promised bread to a hungry child, and promises are coming loose tonight, and we are sorry about the mess."*

"Clean yourself up, then," Rosalind said, because she was tired, and frightened, and her brother was dying in a palace across the city wearing a crown full of god, and honestly a contrite jar of flour was nowhere near the strangest thing she'd negotiated with this month.

The flour swept itself back into the jar. The jar bowed.

That was when Wren burst in, breathless, hair wild. "Rosalind — Casper says now, we go now — the ledgers are—" He stopped, staring at the jar. "Did that flour just bow?"

"We'll discuss it later," Rosalind said, closing her ledger and tucking it against her heart, where she had taken to carrying it the way other people carried knives. "Come on."

Outside, the city was screaming in a hundred different registers. Not just people — the city itself. Cobblestones were settling old grudges. A lamplighter stood frozen beneath his lamp while the flame bent toward him and whispered something that made him weep. In a doorway, a woman argued furiously with her own shadow, which had apparently decided tonight was the night to bring up everything she'd ever done in the dark.

And above it all, the bells, ringing their mismatched midnights, over and over, as though the night itself couldn't decide how long it wanted to be.

"The bells are counting wrong," Wren said, hurrying beside her.

"No," Rosalind said quietly. "I think they're counting *everything*. Every midnight this city has ever promised itself. Listen — under the big bells. Can you hear them? All the little ones?" She could. Beneath the great bronze voices were thousands of tiny chimes, dinner bells and doorbells and the bell on a child's toy, all ringing at once, all the small kept promises of a city of two million souls straining against their hours. "The god is pulling on every oath in the city at once. Everything anyone ever swore. It wants out, and it's using the whole city as a lever."

"And if it gets out?"

Rosalind touched the ledger at her chest. "Then it won't matter what we wrote. So let's make sure it doesn't."

* * *

**CASPER**

The streets between the guildhouse and the palace were a lesson in why the old order had forbidden gods in the first place.

Casper walked point, sword drawn — not spoken awake, just steel, plain and honest — and tried very hard not to look directly at anything that looked back. That was the rule of the night, learned quickly and painfully: *don't meet its eyes.* Because everything had eyes tonight. The gutters had eyes. The doors had opinions. A bridge over the canal demanded a toll from Casper in the name of a ferryman dead four hundred years, and Casper paid it — a copper coin and a murmured "debt acknowledged" — rather than argue metaphysics with masonry.

Behind them came the party: Wren with the satchel, Rosalind with her ledger, and Marta, the ex-collector whom Casper had unbound from her contracts two days ago and who had, to everyone's surprise including her own, chosen to stay. She walked with a crowbar she'd taken from the guild's stores and the expression of a woman who had spent years repossessing impossible things and was not impressed by reality having a tantrum.

A baker's shop window fogged over as they passed, and words appeared in the condensation: *YOU PROMISED TO COME BACK ON TUESDAY.*

"I was seven!" wailed a man Casper vaguely recognized as the shop's owner, rushing out in his nightshirt. "I was seven years old, I didn't know Mrs. Halloway would die!"

*THE DEAD KEEP BETTER RECORDS THAN THE LIVING,* the window wrote, and the door opened politely to let him apologize his way inside to whatever waited there.

"Keep moving," Casper said.

"You're not going to help him?" Rosalind asked.

"He's exactly where he should be — settling his accounts." Casper stepped over a crack in the street that was quietly trying to become a canyon. "Half this city made bargains it never meant to keep. Tonight the bills arrive. That's not cruelty, Rosalind. That's arithmetic." They paused. "Though I'll grant the presentation leaves something to be desired."

Ahead, the Processional Avenue opened toward the palace, and Casper's heart sank, because the avenue was full.

Not with soldiers. With *certainties* — or rather, with the wreckage of them. Reality along the avenue had thinned so far that people's assumptions were visible, lying in the street like shed skins. A merchant knelt over the translucent ghost of a contract, weeping, watching the fine print rewrite itself into something older and hungrier. Two guardsmen stood back to back, swords drawn against a fog that wore the shape of every oath they'd sworn to the crown — and the crown-shaped thing in the fog was looking back at them with interest.

And above the palace, filling the sky where the moon should be, hung the shape of a crown. Enormous. Faint. Made of light the color of old gold and older promises, turning slowly, straining downward, as though the whole night were a lid someone was trying to lift from underneath.

"Oh," said Wren, in a very small voice.

"Yes," said Casper.

"Is that—"

"Yes."

"Then what's holding it *up*?"

"Us," Casper said, and drew breath, and did the last thing the old order ever trained them to do short of the forbidden word itself.

They spoke their sword awake.

* * *

**ROSALIND**

The blade caught fire with silver light and a voice like a struck bell, and Rosalind felt the whole avenue flinch — the fog-things, the ghosts, the grieving street itself — because a swordspeaker's oath was one of the few certainties left in the city that still worked, and hearing one spoken aloud was like striking a match in a drowning dark.

"*Stand*," Casper told the sword, and the sword stood, planted in the cobbles, and its light spread outward in a clean circle that the fog-thing recoiled from.

"Will that hold?" Marta asked.

"Long enough to cross." Casper's face was gray. Speaking oaths cost them; Rosalind had learned to read the price in the lines around their eyes. "The avenue, then the gates, then the hall. Nobody stops. Nobody answers anything that asks you a question — tonight, everything asks questions, and every answer is a bargain. Walk. Don't talk. Don't *promise* anything, not even 'in a minute,' not even 'maybe.' Am I understood?"

They crossed the avenue through that circle of silver light, and the crossing took a year off Rosalind's life in the space of forty yards.

Things pressed against the light from both sides. Her mother's voice, dead eleven years, calling her name from a doorway — and it *sounded* like her mother, and that was the horror, that the thing wearing the voice had gotten it right down to the impatience in the second syllable. A beggar on the steps of a shuttered church grabbed at Rosalind's hem and whispered, "*Bless you, bless you*" — and the blessing stuck to her like a burr, warm and heavy, a gift she hadn't asked for, which she was learning was the most dangerous kind. She had to stop and formally return it, laying her hand on the man's head and saying, "Received and returned, with thanks, owing nothing," before it would let go of her.

"How do you know these forms?" Wren whispered.

"I've been writing contracts for a month," Rosalind said. "Turns out it's all the same craft. Say precisely what you mean. Mean precisely what you say. Leave no room for generosity you didn't choose." She glanced back at the beggar, who was watching her with eyes gone briefly, terribly lucid. "And be kind anyway, wherever kindness doesn't cost a bargain."

At the palace gates, the guards were gone — not fled, *dissolved*, their oaths of service having collected themselves early and marched off toward the throne room without them. The gates stood open. Of course they did. The crowned thing wanted them to come; it had wanted Rosalind since the night it saw her face in the audience hall.

Beyond the gates, the courtyard was full of falling feathers. No birds. Just feathers, drifting down from a sky that had forgotten what they were attached to.

"Last chance to turn around," Casper said quietly.

Rosalind thought of Aldous — of the minute of lucidity, his hand crushing hers, *cut it from me, Rosa, whatever it costs* — and of the private ledger against her heart, and of a garden behind a guildhouse that no one had ever managed to trade away.

"No," she said. "Lead on, Keeper."

Something flickered across Casper's face at the word — the title they'd held for thirty years and might lose forever tomorrow — and then they squared their shoulders, lifted the burning sword, and led them up the palace steps, through doors that breathed open before them, into a throne room where every candle burned upside-down and the bells of the capital rang on and on and on, counting a midnight that refused, tonight, to end.

On the throne, her brother sat upright, eyes open, smiling with a mouth that was only half his own.

"You came," the crown said, in two voices at once. "I *knew* you'd come. Let's discuss terms."

Rosalind put her hand on her ledger, and stepped forward.
