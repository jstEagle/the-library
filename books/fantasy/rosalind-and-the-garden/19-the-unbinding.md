## 19. The Unbinding

The bells had been ringing wrong for six hours.

Not badly — that was the horror of it. They rang *well*, each note perfect and round and patient, like a choir that had all the time in creation. The Long Bells of the capital were supposed to mark the hours. These marked something else. Every stroke peeled a little more certainty off the world, the way you'd peel an apple: slowly, in one unbroken ribbon, until you held the skin in your hand and couldn't remember what it had been wrapped around.

Casper Coldharbour climbed the palace stairs through the sound of it and counted steps, because counting was what they did when the world came apart. Four hundred and eleven from the servants' door to the antechamber. Four hundred and twelve to the throne room doors. Numbers didn't lie. Numbers were the last honest things left in a city where prayers were answering themselves — Casper had passed a baker on the way in whose ovens had filled, unprompted, with bread he'd only *wished* he'd baked, and the man had been weeping into his flour-dusted hands, though whether from joy or terror Casper hadn't stopped to ask.

"Your hands are shaking," said Wren, at their shoulder. The apprentice — former apprentice, forgiven apprentice, apprentice-again-because-Rosalind-had-insisted — was carrying the contract case against his chest like a newborn. "I've never seen your hands shake."

"They're not shaking," Casper said. "They're *remembering*. There's a difference."

It was a lie, and they both knew it, and there was a strange comfort in sharing one. Casper's hands had been steady through ambushes and audits and the guildmaster's confession alike. But those things had been about other people's debts. This was about their own.

The oath sat behind their breastbone like a swallowed coal. It had been there since they were nine years old, since the night the last swordspeaker knelt them down in a cold hall and spoke the binding words over them: *Your voice is a blade. Your word is a debt. You will speak nothing that unmakes.* Sixty years of keeping it. Sixty years of speaking steel awake and never once saying the thing that slept underneath all their craft, the word their order had used exactly once, at the end of the age of gods, when the world had needed pruning so badly it was willing to burn the gardener.

Tonight the garden needed pruning again. And this time, supposedly, someone had found a way to keep the gardener's name in the ground.

*Supposedly.*

"You're doing the face," Wren said.

"What face?"

"The ledger face. The one where you've found an entry that doesn't balance and you're deciding whether to tell the client or quietly fix it yourself."

"I don't have a ledger face."

"You absolutely have a ledger face. Rosalind does an impression."

Casper stopped on the stair. "She does an *impression*?"

"She narrows her eyes and says 'this column is lying to me' in a very serious voice—"

"Wren."

"—and then she taps the page twice, like she's scolding it—"

"*Wren.*"

The boy grinned, and Casper realized he'd done it on purpose — the grin, the nonsense, the whole performance — because a person laughing is a person whose hands have stopped shaking. He'd learned mercy as bookkeeping, but he'd apparently also learned it as misdirection. Casper filed the observation away in the ledger of things Rosalind Sterling had taught them without trying, which was becoming a thick volume.

The throne room doors stood open.

Of course they did. The crowned thing wanted witnesses. Gods returning through cracks in mortal bargains always wanted witnesses; Casper had read enough torn-out pages of the old ledgers to know that much. An unbinding performed before an empty room was just madness. Performed before a court, it was a *story*, and stories were the oldest currency there was.

They walked in side by side, past courtiers pressed to the walls like moths that had decided, collectively, to stop flying toward the light. Some of them were crying. One old chamberlain was holding a candlestick like a mace, which Casper found oddly touching. The floor was slick with wax from a thousand candles burning at noon. Above it all, the bells rang on, patient as tide.

King Aldous sat on his throne, and the crown sat on King Aldous, and neither of them looked well.

He had been a broad, ruddy man once — Rosalind's sketches of him, done from memory in the margins of her contract drafts, showed a brother who laughed with his whole chest. What remained was a frame wearing him. His skin had gone the color of vellum. His eyes moved constantly, tracking something in the air that wasn't there, and his mouth — his mouth was the worst part. It moved in two rhythms at once. When it opened, two voices came out braided together, one deep and human and exhausted, one vast and resonant and *pleased*, the way a landslide might be pleased.

"Sister," said both voices, as Rosalind stepped out from behind Casper.

She had changed clothes three times that day, agonizing over whether the god would recognize the seamstress's grey or the sister's blue, and had finally settled on blue, because — she'd said — if she was going to stand in front of a god and rewrite its contract, she was going to do it as herself. Her hands were steady. Her chin was up. She looked, Casper thought, like every good entry in every honest ledger: exact, legible, and impossible to argue with.

"I brought terms," she said.

"We know." The crowned head tilted. Wax light slid across the circlet, and for a moment Casper saw the metal move — not gleam, *move*, flowing like water finding its level, reshaping itself around a brow it had worn for three generations of kings. "We have watched you write them for nine days. We have watched you cross out four clauses and restore two. We are very fond of the clause about consent."

"It's my favorite too," Rosalind said. "That's why it's non-negotiable."

A sound came out of the king then that took Casper a moment to identify as laughter — the human voice laughing alone, briefly, before the other folded itself back around it like a hand closing.

"You understand," the god said, "what you offer us. We have been *bound*, little author. Bound in blessing, bound in gold, bound to answer the prayers of men who never knew our name while they thanked their saints. Your contract offers us a kingdom's honest worship instead of a crown's stolen obedience. Do you imagine we will simply *sign*?"

"No," Rosalind said. "I imagine you'll read it first. That's rather the point of you."

And there it was — the whole strategy, laid bare in one sentence, and Casper felt the old pride rise in their chest despite everything. You could not trick a god of oaths. You could not bind it by force; the last order that tried had spent everything and bought only a cage shaped like a coronet. But a god of oaths was, before all else, a creature that *kept its word*, and a creature that kept its word could be given a better word to keep. That was the entire art. That had always been the entire art. Casper's order had mistaken swordspeaking for war. Rosalind had understood in nine days what the swordspeakers had missed in three centuries: it was drafting.

The god read.

There was no other way to describe what happened next. The king's body went still, the restless eyes fixed, and the crown rose — actually lifted, a finger's width off his brow, hanging in the candle-smoke like a small golden storm cloud — and the great voice poured through the room reading aloud, clause by clause, in a cadence that made the courtiers flinch and the candles gutter.

*"Article the First: the God of Oaths, hereafter the Signatory, shall receive worship freely given or not at all..."*

*"Article the Fourth: no prayer shall be answered without the pray-er's informed consent to the cost thereof..."*

*"Article the Ninth: upon full execution of this instrument, the binding known as the Crown of Aldous is dissolved, and the Signatory is released into the world entire..."*

At Article Nine, the voice paused.

"And the swordspeaker?" it asked, softly, and the softness was worse than the thunder. "Where is the swordspeaker's part in this elegant document? We smell them, keeper of ledgers. We smell the broken oath waiting behind your teeth like a pulled tooth. Speak your forbidden word, dissolve our prison — yes, yes, that is the design, we have read your design in your sleep. But every word has a price. Who pays yours?"

Casper stepped forward.

"I do," they said. "That's the arrangement. I break my oath, I speak the word, and the word takes my name. Every record. Every memory. Every ledger line that ever bore Casper Coldharbour goes blank, and the world forgets I was ever in it." They heard their own voice come out level and were grateful to it. "That was the price the last speaker paid, and it's the price I agreed to, and it's fair. Debts should fall on the debtor. I taught that rule for forty years."

"Yes," the god murmured. "Yes, we remember. We *invented* that rule." The crown drifted lower, closer to the king's brow, and the human voice underneath gasped — Aldous surfacing, drowning, surfacing. "But your clever sister has added an appendix. Read it to us, author. Read it in your own voice, so we may hear what you think you have done."

Rosalind opened the case. Wren handed her the final page — the appendix, drafted at midnight by candlelight, argued over for two days, rewritten eleven times. She read it clearly, and her voice only shook a little:

"'Appendix the First: Whereas the Unbinding requires the speaking of the Word That Ended Gods, and whereas the Word unmakes the recorded identity of its speaker, let the erasure be *redirected*: let it fall not upon the speaker entire, but upon the binding alone. Let every record of the Crown of Aldous go blank — every contract, every memory, every line in which the binding is written — and let the speaker be spared, per the principle that debts fall on debtors, and the binding, not the speaker, is the debtor herein.'"

Silence. Even the bells seemed to lean in.

Then the god laughed, and this time it was all the vast voice, and the laughter cracked two windows and knocked the old chamberlain's candlestick from his grip.

"Oh, *magnificent*," it said. "Do you know what you have written, girl? You have written a *counter-clause*. A redirect. A little legal culvert to catch a flood and send it somewhere else. And you believe it will hold?"

"I believe," Rosalind said, "that you're going to sign it, and that once you sign it, it holds, because holding is what you *do*. You're the god of oaths. You can't break your own signature. It's the one thing in all the world you can't do."

The stillness that followed lasted long enough for Casper to count their heartbeats. Eleven. Twelve. Thirteen.

"You are either the wisest mortal we have met," the god said at last, "or the most dangerous. We find, at the end of three thousand years, that we cannot tell the difference, and we find that we do not mind." The crown rose fully from the king's brow at last, spinning slowly, shedding light like sparks. "Bring the pen."

Wren brought the pen. Of course Wren brought the pen; he had guarded it the whole climb, a plain steel nib that Rosalind had insisted on because, she said, gods signed grand pens and grand pens wrote grand betrayals, and she wanted this done in something that had never belonged to anyone important. The god signed through the king's hand — Aldous's arm rising as if on strings, the letters dragging themselves across the page in a script that hurt to look at directly, each character a small oath in itself.

When the last letter dried, the crown screamed.

There was no better word for it. The binding knew what was coming — the binding had *always* known, had perhaps been waiting three generations for someone clever enough to free it — and it tore at the contract with everything it had, and the contract held, because a god had signed it, and the god of oaths could not unhold what it had held. But the backlash came down the only channel left open. It came down the Word.

"Now," Rosalind said, and turned to Casper, and her eyes were bright and terrified and certain. "Now, Casper. Say it. Say it and come back to me. Both. Those are the terms."

Casper looked at her. At Wren, white-knuckled on the case. At the king, who in one lucid flicker of his own eyes mouthed *thank you* — or possibly *hurry*, it was hard to tell, and it hardly mattered.

Then Casper broke their oath.

It was easier than sixty years of dread had promised. The coal behind their breastbone cracked open like a knot in green wood, and the word came up the channel of their throat — the word their order had spoken once, the word with no spelling in any living alphabet, the word that meant *be unwritten* — and Casper spoke it into the throne room, into the crown, into the three-hundred-year-old wound between a god and its prison.

The world went white and silent and *blank*.

Casper felt the erasure pass through them like a river through a bridge. It reached for their name — they felt its fingers close on *Casper*, on *Coldharbour*, on sixty years of ledger lines and spoken blades and the cold hall and the kneeling and the oath — and then it caught, snagged, diverted, pouring instead into the narrow channel Rosalind had cut for it, flooding down into the binding, into the crown, into every record of the thing that had imprisoned a god. Casper heard pages going blank somewhere far away — thousands of pages, centuries of pages, the original contract in the vault dissolving into clean paper — and heard the crown's scream thin to a whistle, thin to a whisper, thin to nothing.

The circlet fell. It hit the marble as ordinary gold, dented, dull, small. A dead thing. Just jewelry now, and bad jewelry at that.

The god went with it — up, out, through the roof, through the bells, which rang one final time in a chord so pure that grown soldiers wept — and was gone into the world entire, free, bound to its new terms, carrying its signature like a scar it was proud of.

And King Aldous collapsed.

Rosalind reached him before anyone, catching his head before it struck the throne's step, and Casper got there a half-breath later and saw what she saw: the color coming back into his face even as the strength went out of his body, the two voices gone, only one breath left and it ragged and human and *his*.

"Aldous—" Rosalind's voice broke clean in half.

"Rosie." The king smiled. It was a terrible smile, gray-lipped, but it was entirely his own, and it was aimed at his sister with the whole chest behind it, the laugh she'd drawn in the margins. "You always were... the better negotiator." His hand fumbled, found hers. "Tell the council... I abdicate. Tell them the crown did it. They'll believe that."

"Don't talk. We have physicians—"

"Rosie." He coughed, and the cough had blood in it, and his eyes were clear, clearer than they'd been in years, clear the way a sky is clear after a storm takes the roof off the house. "Three generations. Nobody... nobody ever asked the crown what *it* wanted. You asked. You always ask." His gaze slid sideways, found Casper, and something like recognition moved through them — the king had known them somehow, or the god had, or both. "Keeper. Watch her. She'll rewrite... everything..."

His eyes closed. His chest rose. Fell. Rose again — slower, but *again*, and Rosalind made a sound that was half sob and half fury and pressed her ear to his heart and stayed there, counting, the way Casper counted steps, the way everyone in this strange company counted when the world came apart.

Casper stood in the wreckage of the throne room — the dead crown, the scattered court, the smoke, the silence where the bells had been — and waited to feel erased.

They felt... fine. Tired. Hollowed out, the way you feel after returning something enormous that you'd carried so long you'd forgotten its weight. They flexed their fingers. Steady now. They looked at their hands, at Wren, at the fallen crown, and thought: *it worked. Against every odd, against every torn page and every warning, the girl's counter-clause held.*

Which was when Wren made a small, strangled noise.

"Casper," he said. "Say something. Say your name."

"What? Wren, I'm right here—"

"Say it," he said again, and his face had gone the color of the wax, and he was looking at Casper the way you look at a doorway where someone was standing a moment ago. "Please. I can see you. I can *see* you standing there. But I can't—" His voice cracked. "I can't remember what you're called."

Behind them, from the floor where she knelt with her brother's heart under her ear, Rosalind lifted her head. Her eyes found Casper's, and they were wet and fierce and full of a grief that arrived ahead of her words.

"I can," she said. "I remember. Casper. *Casper Coldharbour.*" She said it like setting a bone. "Only me. The redirect held — it saved you — but it caught wrong, Casper. It caught *almost*. The world forgot you anyway. Everyone. Everything." She rose, leaving the king breathing slow and shallow under a chamberlain's coat, and crossed the ruined floor to take Casper's face in her ink-stained hands.

"But I keep ledgers," she said. "And I am *not* losing you to a clerical error."

* * *

Later — much later, after the physicians, after the council was summoned with the dead crown locked in an iron box, after Wren had cried and been fed brandy and cried again — Casper sat on the steps of the ruined throne and watched Rosalind by candlelight, writing.

She wrote fast, in the shorthand of her own invention, filling page after page: *Casper Coldharbour, who speaks blades awake. Casper Coldharbour, who counts steps. Casper Coldharbour, who does a ledger face and denies it.* Building the record back, entry by entry, from the one memory the erasure had failed to reach.

Casper watched her and felt the strangest thing — not terror, not anymore. Something almost like being born.

Because one question remained, and it sat in Casper's chest quieter than the oath ever had: if the world had already forgotten, if every ledger in Vellum & Thorn now showed a blank where sixty years used to be —

then who, exactly, was going to remember to pay them back?

Rosalind looked up from her page, as if she'd heard it, and smiled through her tears.

"Lucky for you," she said, "you know the new keeper personally."
