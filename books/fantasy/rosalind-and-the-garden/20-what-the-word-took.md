## 20. What the Word Took

The silence after the word was the loudest thing Rosalind had ever heard.

It rang in her teeth, in the hollows of her bones, in the cracked bells of the capital still shuddering from their night of wrongness. The throne room smelled of scorched gold and old incense and something greener underneath — sap, she thought, or spring itself, leaking through the wound the unbinding had torn in the world.

The god was gone. She felt it go the way you feel a great door close three streets away: a pressure lifting off the city, off her chest, off the crown that now lay on the flagstones like a dead beetle, its whispering finally, mercifully stopped.

And Aldous — Aldous was on his knees where the throne used to be dignified, and he was *laughing*, wet-eyed and ragged, and then he wasn't laughing at all.

"Rosie." Her childhood name, in her brother's own voice, with no second cadence underneath it. No echo. No other thing wearing him like a coat. "Rosie, I can't feel my hands."

She was across the floor before she knew she'd moved, gathering him up, and he was lighter than she remembered, as if the god had been carrying part of him for years and had neglected to put it down gently on the way out.

"I'm here," she said, which was a lie in the useful direction, because she was leaving, they both knew she would leave, but not today. Today she was only a sister holding a king who was rapidly becoming neither.

* * *

Aldous did not die. Everyone had braced for him to die — Casper had said so plainly, in their flat ledger-keeper's way, *unbinding this deep usually takes the host with it* — but Aldous woke on the third morning asking for breakfast and complaining about the quality of the palace bread, which was how Rosalind knew he would live, because he had complained about the bread since he was nine years old.

He lived diminished. That was the honest word for it. The physicians called it *frailty of spirit*; Rosalind, who had learned to write truer words than physicians, called it what it was: the god had lived in him so long that when it left, it took some of him along, the way ivy takes bark when you pull it off a wall. He forgot small things first — the names of ambassadors, the words to old songs, which stair in the west wing creaked. Then larger ones. He could no longer read a treaty without his eyes sliding off the clauses. He tired by midday. He wept sometimes at nothing, and was embarrassed, and pretended it was dust.

But he was *him*. He teased her. He remembered the pond behind the orchard and the summer she'd convinced him frogs were good luck if you kissed them, and he made her swear never to tell anyone, and then told everyone himself within the week, because he was still Aldous.

On the twelfth day he summoned the council and set the crown on the table between them instead of on his head, and Rosalind watched her brother abdicate with the same expression he'd worn at nine, apologizing to a frog.

"A kingdom needs a mind that's whole," he said. "Mine has holes in it now. I know exactly where each one is, which is more than most kings can say." A thin ripple of uncomfortable laughter from the councilors. "I'll take the title of Prince-Errant — I've always liked the sound of it — and I will spend my remaining years doing the two things I'm still entirely competent at: planting things, and apologizing."

"Apologizing," said Lady Vess, dry as chalk.

"The crown has a great deal to answer for," Aldous said. "Someone ought to start."

Rosalind stood at the back of the room, in plain clothes, officially nobody, and cried into her sleeve where only one person could see her do it. That person was standing beside her, silent as ever, watching the council with the particular attention of someone counting debts.

Casper Coldharbour. She still said the name in her head every time, deliberately, like pressing a bruise to make sure it still hurt. Because it was going to stop hurting. That was the whole horror of it. It was going to stop.

* * *

She understood what had happened slowly, in pieces, over those first weeks, and each piece arrived like a bill collector.

The first piece: the guards who had fought beside them in the throne room looked straight through Casper. Not rudely — there was nothing left to be rude *to*. When Casper gave an order, the order was heard and obeyed and instantly forgotten, the way you forget a dream instruction. One guard asked Rosalind, in perfect sincerity, why she kept talking to empty air.

The second piece: the guild ledgers. She went with Casper to the guildhouse — half-collapsed, its vault breached, its guildmaster buried under the door she'd held — and watched Casper open the master registry with hands that did not shake, because Casper's hands never shook, and find page after page gone blank. Not torn. Blank. Every entry bearing the name *Casper Coldharbour* had emptied itself of ink, though the paper was untouched. Fifty years of trades, audits, impossible acquisitions, all of it still legible except the one name that had signed it. Where the signature should be: clean vellum, waiting.

"It redirected," Casper said quietly. "Your contract. It caught most of the erasure and spent it on the binding instead." A pause. "Most."

"Don't do that," Rosalind said. "Don't do the arithmetic out loud like it's just numbers."

"It is just numbers. That's what I have instead of comfort." But something in their face softened, the way it always did, the tiniest degree, like a lock turning a quarter-inch.

The third piece came from Tam, the apprentice, who had been forgiven everything and was trying, with the desperate energy of the truly guilty, to earn it back daily. He came running down the guildhouse steps white-faced, holding the recruitment rolls.

"He's not in them," Tam said. "Master Coldharbour — I mean — you're not in them. Any of them. I checked the intake books, the oath registers, the swordspeaking rolls from before the Order fell — I checked the *Order's* records, the ones in the archive under the mountain—" His voice cracked. "There's a gap. Where your training should be, there's a gap shaped like a person, and the archivist wrote in the margin, *one initiate lost to fever, year unknown.*"

"Fever," Casper repeated.

"That's what the record says now. That's what the record has *always* said. It changed history to fit the hole." Tam clutched the roll to his chest. "I remember you. I remember everything. Why do I remember?"

"Because your debt to me was written in your own hand," Casper said. "Self-authored entries resist the erasure. Yours, and hers." They nodded at Rosalind. "Everyone else — the world filed me under *paid in full* and closed the account."

Rosalind looked at the blank pages, and at Casper standing among fifty years of their own vanished handwriting, and felt something in her chest crack along a seam she hadn't known was load-bearing.

"You're still here," she said fiercely. "You're standing right there. The world is wrong, not you."

"Yes," Casper agreed, in the tone of a man noting a discrepancy he intended to audit later, and Rosalind wanted to shake them, and instead she laughed, wetly, because of course. Of course that was what they'd say. The last oath-bound swordspeaker, erased from every record in creation, responding like a clerk who'd found a misfiled invoice.

* * *

The worst of it came in small moments, the way the truest grief always does.

It came when the innkeeper in the capital — who had owed Casper a favor for eleven years, a favor recorded in triplicate — squinted at Casper and said, "Have we met? You've got a familiar sort of elbow."

It came when Casper reached, out of habit, to sign for a delivery of lamp oil, and the pen hovered over the line marked *name*, and hovered, and finally Casper drew a small careful symbol instead — a sword crossed with a quill, the mark of the old order — and the delivery boy frowned at it and said, "What's that, then?" and Casper said, "A signature," and the boy said, "Of *what?*" and Casper said, "Exactly," and paid cash.

It came at night, when Rosalind found Casper sitting in the ruined scriptorium of the guildhouse with a candle, reading aloud from memory — trades, prices, the memory of spring sold at winter rates, the whole impossible inventory of a life — transcribing it all onto fresh pages in fresh ink.

"What are you doing?" she asked, though she knew.

"Rebuilding the ledger," Casper said. "From memory. Mine resists the erasure too — self-authored, all of it. If I write myself back in fast enough, perhaps—"

"Perhaps what?"

Casper's pen stopped. For a long moment the only sound was the candle eating its wick.

"Perhaps nothing," they said at last. "I ran the sums, Rosalind. I always run the sums. What the word took, it keeps. The world's memory of me isn't damaged — it's *spent*. Paid out to unbind a god. There's no clause anywhere that gives it back." They looked up, and their eyes were steady and terrible. "I broke my oath knowing the price. I'd break it again. But I find I would like very much to be *remembered*, and wanting things is new to me, and I don't recommend it."

Rosalind sat down across the table from them and pulled a fresh page toward herself.

"Then we'll want together," she said. "It's less efficient. Everything worth doing is."

* * *

Aldous came to visit the guildhouse in his third month as Prince-Errant, wearing mud on his boots and carrying a sack of bulbs, because he had decided the walled garden behind the guildhouse — the one impossible thing never traded away, the one asset on the guild's books with no price — needed him.

"You're the sister of the woman who saved me," he told Casper solemnly, shaking their hand, and Rosalind saw Casper absorb the sentence the way ground absorbs rain: completely, without visible change, and yet something must have gotten in, because things grew afterward.

"Something like that," Casper said.

"She doesn't talk about you much," Aldous confided, patting the soil around a bulb with the concentration of a man performing surgery. "But she writes in a little book every night. Black cover. She thinks I haven't noticed. I'm a king — I notice *everything*." He paused. "Former king. I notice most things."

That night Rosalind found Casper waiting outside her door, arms folded, wearing the expression of an auditor who has found a discrepancy and intends to be polite about it.

"The black book," Casper said.

"It's private."

"I know what's in it."

"Then you know why it's private."

Casper was quiet for a moment. The corridor lamp guttered. When they spoke again, the flat ledger-voice had a hairline fracture running through it.

"Read it to me," they said. "Once. I want to know what I sound like, while I still can."

So Rosalind sat down on the cold stone step, and opened the book she had written in every night since the throne room, and read to the erased woman the record of her own existence:

*Casper Coldharbour. Keeper of the Ledger of Impossible Things. Last of the swordspeakers. Sold fair, kept every promise, broke one oath to save a king, and never once — not once, in all the years I've known them — let a debt go unwritten or a person go unseen. Stubborn. Precise. Kind in the specific, unglamorous way of people who fix things rather than admire them. Afraid of wanting things. Learning.*

She read it twice. On the second reading, Casper closed their eyes.

"That's a generous entry," they said when she finished. "Inflated valuation."

"Every appraisal is subjective," Rosalind said. "I learned that from a guildmaster."

"And tomorrow?"

"Tomorrow I write it again. And the day after. Every day, Casper. As long as I hold a pen." She closed the book and held it against her chest like a shield, or a heart. "You taught me that a thing written is a thing bound. Well. I am binding you to the world, one day at a time, and the terms are non-negotiable."

For a moment — just a moment — Casper Coldharbour, who did not cry, whose hands did not shake, whose name existed nowhere in creation except in one woman's handwriting and one woman's memory, looked as though they might do all three at once.

Then they stood, and straightened their coat, and said, in a voice that was almost steady:

"The garden needs planning before winter. If you intend to keep writing me into existence, you may as well do it somewhere warm. There's tea in the scriptorium. I counted the leaves this time, so don't—"

"—don't take liberties with the inventory," Rosalind finished, smiling. "I know. I've read your ledgers."

They walked down the corridor together, past the blank pages and the broken vault and the guild that was already, in Rosalind's head, becoming something new — something with forgiveness built into its terms. Behind them the night settled over the capital, and somewhere in the palace her brother slept without whispers, and in her satchel the black book waited for tonight's entry, and tomorrow's, and all the tomorrows after.

Because some debts, she had decided, were meant to be paid forever — gladly, daily, in full — and the name of a friend was the finest currency she would ever spend.
