## 22. The Garden

The wall had been the first thing Casper built, before the beds, before the trellises, before the gate with its latch that stuck in damp weather. A wall of old guild-stone, salvaged brick by brick from the collapsed east wing, mortared with more stubbornness than skill. It stood shoulder-high now, and the roses had claimed most of it, and Casper liked it best in the early morning when the light came over the top and laid a gold seam down the middle of the garden like a ledger line waiting to be filled.

They were filling it now — the line, not the ledger; there were no ledgers here — kneeling in the dirt with their sleeves rolled to the elbow, thinning carrots with a knife that had once spoken and now only cut. That was enough for a knife. That was enough for anyone, Casper thought, and then laughed at themselves, quietly, because it was the sort of thought Rosalind would have written down.

The garden did not care about names. The garden cared about water and patience and whether you got to the slugs before the slugs got to the lettuce. It was, Casper had come to believe, the only honest institution left in Vellum & Thorn's rebuilt house of trade.

The guildhouse rose beyond the wall — taller now, brighter, its windows glazed in real glass instead of oiled parchment. Rosalind's doing. Everything was Rosalind's doing, these days: the new charter pinned behind glass in the entry hall, the two doors marked *Debts* and *Mercy*, the small brass plaque by the front step that read *ALL TRADES REQUIRE CONSENT, INCLUDING OURS*. Pilgrims came from three kingdoms to see it. Some of them wept. Most of them haggled, which Rosalind said proved humanity was not entirely beyond saving.

Casper pulled a weed, considered it, and decided it was a wildflower. The garden could afford one mistake.

A shadow crossed the gold seam of light. Then another. Then a small determined voice at the gate, which stuck in damp weather, and today was damp.

"It's me," said Rosalind, through the wood. "It's always me. You'd think after four years I'd have learned the trick of this latch."

"You pull up, not out."

"I know. I'm testing whether you'll come let me in."

Casper set down the knife, brushed the soil from their hands — an old habit from a life with clean ledgers, though the hands never actually got clean anymore, and they no longer wanted them to — and went to the gate. They lifted the latch. Rosalind Sterling, Keeper of the Guild of Vellum & Thorn, author of the Charter of Consent, terror of predatory lenders in three kingdoms, slipped through looking pleased with herself, carrying a basket covered with a cloth.

"You're supposed to be in negotiations with the Salt Guild," Casper said.

"I was. I am. I will be again at noon." She set the basket on the flat stone by the well — the well was decorative now, but it had history, and Rosalind kept things that had history the way other people kept dogs. "I told them the Keeper required a private audience on matters of guild security."

"Is that what this is?"

Rosalind lifted the cloth. Underneath: a loaf still warm enough to steam in the morning air, a crock of butter, and a jar of honey with a label in her own handwriting — *TAKEN IN TRADE FROM THE BEEKEEPER OF ASH LANE, WHO INSISTED, AND WHOSE CONSENT WAS NOTARIZED.*

"Guild security," Casper said gravely, "has never been better defended."

* * *

They ate breakfast on the flat stone with the sun climbing and the bees going about their own audited business among the beans. Rosalind talked the way she always talked — in currents, eddying from the Salt Guild to her brother to the apprentice intake to a complaint about the new door hinges, doubling back, circling, landing nowhere and everywhere. Casper listened the way they had learned to listen: without keeping score.

That had been the hardest lesson. Not the word. The word had been terrible, but it had been quick — a single syllable spoken in a throne room while reality tore along its seams, and then silence, and then Aldous breathing, and the god gone out of the crown like smoke from a snuffed candle. Quick. Terrible things usually are.

The slow thing was learning to live afterward as a person-shaped absence. Four years on, Casper had made something of a study of it.

People remembered *that* Casper existed — or rather, they remembered that someone must have, the way you remember a chair has been moved even if you can't say where it stood. The records all agreed: the last swordspeaker had unbound the king, and the last swordspeaker's name was — and there the ink simply stopped meaning anything. Not blank. Blank would have been honest. The entries were full of letters that refused to be read, lines that slid off the eye like water off oilcloth. Scholars had written papers about it. One of them had visited the garden, hoping for an interview, and Casper had given him tea and told him he was standing on the answer, and he had looked down at the carrot bed in genuine confusion and gone away unsatisfied.

But Rosalind — Rosalind remembered. That was the miracle nobody had planned. Her private ledger, the second one, the one she kept locked in a drawer she pretended didn't exist, held a name in ordinary ink that stayed put when you looked at it. She wrote it every morning. She had done so for fourteen hundred and some days, and she knew the count exactly, because of course she did.

"The Salt Guild wants a concession," she said, tearing bread. "They want the right to call in debts of salt against debts of grain whenever the exchange rate turns. I told them consent runs both directions or it isn't consent. Old Marrow — you remember Marrow—"

"I don't," Casper said, "but go on."

"—Marrow nearly swallowed his own beard. He said I was making trade impossible. I said no, I'm making trade *possible*, just not for people who want it impossible for everyone else." She buttered her bread with the decisiveness of a woman signing a treaty. "He'll come around. He always comes around. It took him eleven years to admit the old system was theft wearing a nice coat, and he's only been admitting it for six."

"And your brother?"

"Aldous is good." She softened, the way she always did at his name, grief and relief braided so tightly together you couldn't tell which strand was which. "Diminished, the physicians say. I say quieter. He sits on the council and mostly listens, and when he speaks, everyone leans in, because he only says things worth saying now. Last week he proposed a granary law and then voted against his own amendment because a miller's daughter argued him out of it in open session. He laughed. Actually laughed, in the chamber." She looked at her bread. "He doesn't remember much about the year of the crown. He remembers a garden, though. Isn't that strange? Of everything, he remembers a garden. He asked me whose it was."

"What did you tell him?"

"That it belonged to a friend of mine who was very good with growing things." She smiled sideways. "He said that sounded right. He said gardens are how you know a place means to keep living."

Casper turned that over, the way they turned everything over now, slowly, letting the sun get at all sides of it. "He's wiser than the crown ever let him be."

"He was always wise. The crown just shouted over him." Rosalind brushed crumbs from her knees and reached into the basket again, and this time what she drew out was not bread.

It was the ledger.

* * *

Casper knew it on sight, though they had seen it perhaps a dozen times, always offered, always declined. It was smaller than her official volumes, bound in green leather gone soft at the corners, and it had the particular density of a book that is opened every single day. The clasp was plain brass. There was nothing magical about it. That was the whole point, Rosalind had explained once: magic had been tried, in the first months, charms and bindings to hold the name safe against the erasure, and every charm had failed, because the word Casper spoke had been older than charms. What worked was ink. Ordinary ink, reapplied daily, by a hand that refused to forget. A debt of attention, paid forever, by choice.

"No," Casper said, out of habit.

"I haven't asked anything yet."

"You're holding it the way you hold things you're about to ask."

Rosalind laughed. "Four years and you still audit me." But she didn't put it away. She sat with it on her knees, thumb resting on the closed cover, and the garden went quiet around them — not silent, gardens are never silent, but attentive, the way a room goes when something true is about to be said.

"I read it to them, you know," she said. "The apprentices. Once a year, on Unbinding Day. I stand up in the hall and I read the account of the throne room — the bells ringing wrong, the god straining, you walking through collapsing certainties like a person crossing ice — and then I read the name. And every year, for a moment, the whole hall remembers. Every face. Every heart. For the length of a breath, the world knows who saved it." She looked up. "And then it lets go, because that's the shape of the bargain, and I don't cheat bargains, not even the ones I hate. But Casper—" and there it was, the name, spoken freely, the only mouth in the world where it lived unhidden, "—I've started thinking maybe once a year isn't the only lawful reading. Maybe a keeper can interpret her own charter. I did write it."

"You wrote loopholes into it deliberately," Casper said. "I watched you do it."

"I did. Clause nine. *A debt recorded in mercy may be paid in mercy.*" She opened the ledger.

The pages fell to the day's date, because of course they did; she had written the morning's entry before she came, as she did every morning, and the book knew where it lived. And there it was, in her steady, unhurried hand:

*Casper Coldharbour. Last swordspeaker. Who broke an oath to keep a greater one. Who chose to remain forgotten so a king could be merely a man. Who grows carrots.*

Casper read it, and the strange thing — the thing four years had not dulled — was that reading it felt less like being named than like being handed something. A cup of water on a long road. You hadn't realized how dry you were until it touched your hands.

"May I?" Rosalind said, and began, softly, to read aloud.

She read the whole entry. Not just the name — the account beneath it, the running record she had kept since the first day: the garden planted, the wall raised, the latch that stuck, the first carrots, the apprentices who crept over the wall on a dare and were caught and fed instead of scolded, the winter the frost took the peach tree and Casper had grieved it openly and Rosalind had written that down too, because grief deserved recording, that was the entire philosophy of the new guild in one sentence. She read the small things. She had always known the small things were the load-bearing ones.

And while she read, the world remembered.

There was no thunder. No bell. Just the light on the wall changing slightly, the way it does when a cloud moves and you feel warmer without knowing why. The bees kept working. Somewhere beyond the garden wall, in the guildhouse, in the city, in the three kingdoms, people paused mid-task for no reason they could name — a clerk lifting her pen, a sailor turning toward the shore, an old king in a council chamber going still and touching his chest as if hearing a friend's footsteps in another room. For the length of Rosalind's reading, the world knew Casper Coldharbour. Held the name gently, the way you hold something borrowed.

Then she closed the book, and the world let go, and Casper was nameless again — and it was all right. That was the part they could never have predicted, back in the throne room, back in the terror of self-erasure: that namelessness, freely chosen and lovingly counterfeited once a year, was not a wound. It was a door left open. Anyone could walk through it. Rosalind walked through it daily. The garden walked through it constantly, calling them nothing at all, needing nothing at all, which was its own kind of naming.

"Well?" Rosalind said.

"Well what?"

"Clause nine. Interpretation. Am I within my rights as Keeper?"

Casper picked up the knife and went back to the carrot row, kneeling in the warm dirt, and answered without turning around, because some things are easier said to weeds than to faces.

"You're the Keeper. Write it however you like."

* * *

She stayed until the shadows leaned east, and then she gathered her basket and stood, brushing off her skirts, becoming again the woman the three kingdoms argued with and admired and occasionally fled from. At the gate she wrestled the latch, lost, and waited, grinning, until Casper came and lifted it for her.

"Same time next week?" she said.

"I'll have peas by then."

"Peas and clause nine. My favorite harvest." She stepped through, then paused, half-turned, and her voice dropped into the register she used for the things that mattered most, the register under all the currents. "You know it's not charity. What I do. Writing it. Reading it. It's not pity and it isn't payment either. It's just — you gave the world back its tomorrow, and nobody can write that in any ledger that lasts, so I keep trying anyway. Somebody should be keeping your books. You kept everyone else's."

She kissed Casper's cheek, flour-and-honey and ink, and went up the path toward the guildhouse, and the sticking gate swung shut behind her with a click like a clasp closing on a book.

Casper stood a while in the middle of the garden, in the gold seam of light, hands in the dirt, listening to the bees.

Then they knelt, and made a furrow, and opened the little paper packet Rosalind had brought last week — seeds from the palace gardens, Aldous's own request, *for your friend's garden*, he had said, *tell them a king remembers kindness even when he can't remember faces* — and sowed them in rows straight enough to satisfy any auditor alive or dead.

Tomorrow Rosalind would open the green ledger and write the name again, and the day after, and the day after that, for as long as her hand held a pen, and someday — not soon, but someday — an apprentice would inherit the drawer and the duty, because mercy, properly kept, outlives its keeper. The world would forget and forget and forget, and one small book would remember and remember and remember, and between those two facts Casper Coldharbour intended to grow an unreasonable quantity of peas.

The light lay along the wall like a line waiting to be filled. Casper filled it.
