## 2. A Prayer Answered

The client came in just past the eleventh bell, when the fog outside the counting-house had thickened to the consistency of poured milk and the lamps along Vesper Row burned as small and sullen as drowning stars.

Isandro Lockridge saw him first through the grille of the intake window, the way she saw everyone: as a set of particulars to be recorded. Male. Perhaps fifty. Coat of good wool gone thin at the cuffs, which meant money once and less now, or else a man who dressed carefully down because he understood, better than most in the Hollow, what visibility cost. His hands were a mason's hands—broad, scarred at the knuckles with the white seams that lime mortar leaves—and they were shaking, though the night was not cold enough to excuse it.

"Name?" she asked, in the flat clerk's voice she kept for the grille, a voice like a drawer sliding shut.

"Tobias Merren." He said it too loudly, then seemed to hear himself and flinched. In the Hollow you learned to give your name quietly or not at all. Names were currency here, and dangerous coin; the guild paid best for them and worst for those who spent them carelessly.

"Purpose of visit?"

"I want—" He stopped. Started again, softer. "I want to sell something. Something I was told you'd buy."

"We buy memories," Isandro said. "Clean recollections only. No memories of the trade itself, no memories of this building, no memories of any transaction conducted herein. You'll sign the standard waiver. Payment is rendered in guild scrip or, if you prefer, in barter against future services." She recited it the way she recited everything, without inflection, and watched his hands shake harder.

"It's not—not an ordinary one." He leaned closer to the grille, and she caught the smell of him: woodsmoke and chalk dust and under it something sweeter, like incense from a temple that had been closed for thirty years. "They said at the Fog Market there was a clerk who'd know. A woman. They said ask for the ledger-keeper."

*They said,* Isandro thought, *a great deal too much.* But her hand was already moving to the bell beneath the counter, the one that summoned Master Corvane's attention only when an item required appraisal above her station, and she hated herself a little for how quickly it moved.

"The vault is closed to walk-in appraisals after ninth bell," she said, which was true, and also a stalling lie, because the vault was closed to walk-ins entirely and Corvane made exceptions whenever an exception smelled like profit.

"He answers prayers."

The words dropped into the little room like a stone into a well. Isandro heard them fall all the way down and hit nothing.

"I'm sorry?"

"My daughter." The mason's voice cracked on the word and he pressed his lips together until he could speak evenly again, a man accustomed to holding things up while they were still wet. "My daughter was dying. Fever, three weeks. The physicians gave up. The priests—the ones left, the ones who still pretend—I paid them, they sang their songs, and my girl got worse. So on the last night I did what my grandmother taught me before they burned the prayer books. I went out past the tanneries, where the old boundary stones are, and I knelt in the mud, and I prayed. To the old name. The one nobody says."

He was looking at her now with an intensity she found unbearable, the way men look at clerks as though clerks were priests, as though behind every grille there must be someone keeping accounts with heaven.

"And she woke up the next morning asking for breakfast. Asking for *breakfast*, mistress, after three weeks of not knowing my face. And the fever broke by noon, and the physician came back and looked at her and looked at me and said he couldn't account for it, and I—" His breath went ragged. "I remember kneeling there. I remember every second of it. And I'm told that memory might be worth more than anything I've ever owned, and I need money for her convalescence, and so here I am, selling God."

*Here I am, selling God.* Isandro wrote the phrase down later in her private book of phrases, the one she kept locked in her desk among the spare nibs and blotting sand, because it was exactly the kind of sentence the Hollow produced nightly and never noticed producing.

"Wait here," she said, and rang the bell.

* * *

Corvane came down himself, which was rare enough to mean something. Guildmaster of the Hollow Guild was not a title that appeared on any document; officially the counting-house was a chandler's warehouse, officially Corvane was a chandler of middling success with a fondness for ledgers, and officially Isandro Lockridge did not exist at all, since the woman registered as his junior clerk was named something forgettable that changed every few years. Unofficially, Corvane descended into the intake room perhaps four times a year, and each time the air pressure in the room changed, the lamps burning lower and steadier, as though the building itself sat up straighter.

He was a long man, dry as winter paper, with a voice like the closing of a well-made door. He listened to Tobias Merren's story a second time without interrupting, which Isandro had learned was his habit with liars: let them build the whole house, then walk through it and touch every wall.

"Answered prayers," Corvane said at last. He said it the way another man might say *counterfeit coin.*

"Yes, master."

"Mm." He turned to Isandro. "Ledger."

She brought the intake ledger, open to the current page, and the appraisal kit: the silver dish, the tuning fork of blackened iron, the vial of lamp-oil blessed—or rather, un-blessed, scrubbed of blessing, which in the Hollow passed for the same thing. The procedure was older than her employment and probably older than the guild. Memory was weighed the way grain was weighed, by resonance and clarity and the absence of tampering.

Corvane held the mason's temples in his long fingers and drew the memory out the slow, surgical way, the way that left the donor dazed but whole, minus one evening. Isandro watched it settle into the appraisal dish: a curl of light, gray-silver shot through with something darker, coiling and uncoiling like smoke that had opinions.

And she felt it.

She felt it the way she felt weather through the walls of the counting-house, the way she sometimes felt, faintly and absurdly, the griefs she catalogued but never took—a pressure, a texture, against the inside of her skin. Every memory that crossed that dish had a texture. Grief was heavy and wet. Courage was bright-edged, brittle. Love, when they got it, which was rarely, because love held its price—love was warm and clung. She had catalogued thousands. She had never once felt a memory push *back.*

This one pushed. Not hard. The way a sleeping animal pushes, shifting its weight in a dream. The light in the dish bent toward Tobias Merren—toward the man who had lived it—as though it missed him.

"Interesting," Corvane said, in the tone he used for interesting freight charges.

"You feel that, master? It's—it's warm."

"I feel a forgery," Corvane said. "A very good one. Note the date, Lockridge. The man claims this happened nine days ago. Nine days ago we processed eleven lots, none of which contained anything resembling the miraculous, and if a god had answered a prayer within walking distance of this office, do you imagine we wouldn't have felt it? Do you imagine *I* would permit it?"

"No, master."

"The Hollow runs on silence, Lockridge. Silence and mutual pretense. That is the entire architecture of our survival. A god answering prayers is not silence. A god answering prayers is a bonfire in a hayfield." He lifted the tuning fork, struck it against the dish's rim, and held it over the coiling light. The note it produced was wrong—she heard it go wrong, a sweet low tone that bent downward at the end like a sentence trailing off into doubt—and Corvane nodded as though the doubt confirmed him. "Resonance inconsistent with authentic devotional experience. Authentic prayer-memory has a particular signature. Flat. Disappointed. This—" he gestured at the dish "—is theater. Someone sold this man an experience. There are practitioners in the undercity who stitch memories together for the desperate. Cruel trade. Expensive, too."

"But the daughter," Isandro said. "The fever broke."

"Feverish children recover. Grieving fathers attribute." Corvane's mouth did something that was not quite a smile. "It is the oldest accounting error in the world, Lockridge. Men credit heaven with the balances chance provides." He turned back to the mason. "I will tell you what I tell all sellers of miracles. The guild will not purchase counterfeit devotional material. However—" and here his voice softened into the register he used when closing a sale, the register Isandro privately called the mercy-voice, though there was no mercy in it that she had ever been able to locate "—the memory has some incidental value. The craft in it is real even if the content is false. I can offer you forty crowns and removal of the lot."

Tobias Merren stared. "Forty crowns? For the most important night of my life?"

"For a fabricated night," Corvane corrected gently. "Forty crowns is generous for fabrication. Take it, Master Merren. Your daughter needs medicine more than you need a memory of mud and desperation."

Isandro watched the mason's face do the arithmetic that poor faces did constantly, the terrible division of a life into salable parts. He signed. He took the scrip. At the door he stopped, and looked back at Isandro—not at Corvane, at *her*, the nameless clerk behind the grille—and said, "You felt it too. I saw your face. You felt it."

The door closed behind him. The lamps steadied.

* * *

"Enter it as rejected lot, class seven, devotional forgery," Corvane said. "Estimated value nil. Destroy the vessel at end of shift per standard procedure."

"Destroy it," Isandro repeated.

"Of course destroy it. Why would we keep it?"

Why indeed. She thought of the answer she did not say: *Because it moved, master. Because it leaned toward the man who owned it like a plant toward light. Because in nine years of weighing other people's yesterdays I have never once seen a memory miss anyone.* Memories did not miss people. Memories were goods. Goods did not grieve their selling. That was the first law of the trade, unspoken because it needed no speaking: what was taken was taken cleanly, and nothing taken ever reached back.

"Standard procedure," she said aloud. "Yes, master."

Corvane paused at the door to the stairs. He had a way of pausing that made you feel audited. "Lockridge. How long have you kept our books?"

"Nine years, master."

"Nine years. And in nine years have I ever explained a decision twice?"

"No, master."

"Then consider whether your questions tonight were diligence or sentiment, and correct accordingly." The door closed. The lamps burned low and steady. Somewhere above, rain had begun on the roof of the chandler's warehouse, soft as ash.

* * *

She should have destroyed it.

That was the thought that walked home with her through the fog, along Vesper Row, past the shuttered shrine of Saint Ilva-that-wasn't (there had never been a Saint Ilva; the niche was empty and had always been empty; everyone agreed), across the canal bridge where the water moved without sound, up the two flights to her rooms above the button-maker's. She should have destroyed it. Standard procedure existed for reasons. Class-seven lots were destroyed because counterfeit devotional material was unstable—it degraded strangely, leaked, occasionally did things to adjacent lots in storage that appraisers described, in the guarded language of the trade, as *contamination.*

She stood in her narrow room with its single window full of fog, holding the sealed glass vessel Corvane had forgotten to reclaim from her—no, not forgotten; she had simply written it into the destruction log as destroyed, in her own neat clerk's hand, and carried it out under her coat like a stolen loaf, and the enormity of what she had done arrived only now, standing in her own lamplight, nine years of perfect invisibility ending with a theft worth nothing.

*Worth nothing.* She set the vessel on her desk beside the lamp.

Inside, the memory coiled. Gray-silver, threaded with dark, turning slowly without any mechanism she could name. And warm. She could feel the warmth through the glass, faint as sunlight through winter cloth, and she thought: *this is what the others feel like from inside. This is what it is to be the person in the memory instead of the person at the desk.*

Nine years she had catalogued the takings of the Hollow Guild: a widow's grief, a soldier's courage, a bride's certainty, a traitor's shame, whole childhoods decanted like wine. She told herself, every night, the same sentence, until it wore smooth as a river stone: *bookkeeping is not stealing.* Someone must keep the accounts. The accounts are not the crime. She was the ink, not the hand. And every night the sentence held, mostly, the way a dam holds, mostly.

But she had never held a piece of the inventory in her own room before. She had never been alone with it, off the scales, away from the tuning forks and the classification schedules and Corvane's dry door-closing voice. Alone with it, it did not behave like inventory. It behaved like something alive that had been put in a jar.

She uncorked the vessel.

She did not decide to. That was the thing she would return to later, in the sleepless arithmetic of the small hours—that there was no decision, only a reaching, the way you reach toward warmth in a cold room. The memory rose out of the glass like breath in winter and settled over her, and Tobias Merren's night became hers:

Mud. Rain coming down in ropes. The boundary stones leaning out of the earth like broken teeth, and beyond them the fog that marked the edge of everything. His knees in the muck. His hands—her hands, broad, scarred white at the knuckles—pressed together out of habit though he had long ago stopped believing the gesture meant anything. And the prayer, the actual prayer, not words really but a demolition, a tearing down of thirty years of careful disbelief in a single night of terror, and the old name going out of him into the dark like a shout into a well—

And the well *answering.*

Not thunder. Not fire. Nothing a painter could paint. Just warmth, sudden and total, rising out of the ground itself, out of the stones, as though the country beneath the fog had drawn one long breath after decades of held breath, and somewhere far away—or very near; distance stopped meaning anything—a presence turned toward him the way a face turns toward a candle being lit. And a sense—not words, never words, but sense, vast and unhurried and impossibly tender—of having been *heard*. Of having been heard by something that had been listening the whole time, through all the silent years, through all the erased names and empty shrines, waiting with a patience that made the fog look hasty.

Isandro came back to herself sitting on the floor of her own room, the vessel empty in her lap, tears on her face that she had no memory of starting, and her hands—her own small, ink-stained clerk's hands—shaking the way a mason's hands had shaken.

She sat there a long time.

Then, because she was what she was, she reached for a pen.

She wrote it down. Not for the guild ledger—never the guild ledger. Into her private book, the one hidden among the nibs and blotting sand, the book where she kept sentences and small observations and the accumulating secret record of everything about the trade that troubled her. She wrote the date. She wrote the description, precise as any intake entry: *Lot rejected as class-seven forgery. Appraisal findings inconsistent with rejection. Resonance anomalous. Texture unlike any previously catalogued. Warmth persists post-extraction. Memory exhibits orientation toward original owner.*

And then, because the clerk in her could not leave it alone, she added the entry that mattered, the one she would reread forty times in the coming days, the one that would keep her awake:

*Note: authentic devotional memories, per guild classification manual, exhibit flatness. Disappointment. The manual's own authors describe prayer-memory as "the residue of asking an empty room." What I experienced was not the residue of an empty room. Either the manual is wrong, or the room has stopped being empty.*

Either the manual is wrong, or the room has stopped being empty.

She read it back. Outside, the fog pressed against the window, and somewhere out past the tanneries, past the leaning boundary stones, the rain fell on a country that was not on any map, and Isandro Lockridge—nameless clerk, keeper of accounts, thief of nothing and accomplice to everything—blew out her lamp and sat in the dark holding a memory that had been pronounced impossible, feeling its warmth fade slowly from her hands like the last heat of a hand she'd held.

In the morning, she knew, she would log the destruction. In the morning, she would be invisible again. But tonight she sat in the dark and admitted the thing that had been forming in her since a shaking man had said *you felt it too*:

The guild had weighed the memory and found it wanting. The guild was wrong. And if the guild was wrong about this—if the oldest rule of the trade, the flat disappointed silence of heaven, had quietly expired nine days ago in the mud past the tanneries—then everything built on that rule, every ledger line, every classification, every careful pretense that kept the Hollow hollow, was standing on ground that had begun, very softly, to move.

She slept badly, and dreamed of doors.

When she woke, the fog outside her window had burned off early—which it never did—and the light coming through was the color of something beginning.
