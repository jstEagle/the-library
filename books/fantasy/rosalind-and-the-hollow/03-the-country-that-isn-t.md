## 3. The Country That Isn't

The fog came in off the salt marshes at dusk the way it always did, patient as a creditor, and Isandro Lockridge walked home through it with her collar up and her ledger wrapped in oilcloth beneath her coat.

She had learned as a child—though which child, whose childhood, she could no longer quite say—that the fog of the Hollow was not weather. Weather belonged to the world. The fog was a courtesy extended by the world, or perhaps an apology, laid over the streets each evening like a sheet over furniture in a house the owners had stopped visiting. By full dark it stood knee-deep in the gutters and shoulder-deep in the alleys, and anyone who walked abroad in it became, to the eyes of anything beyond the border, no one at all.

Isandro had made a profession of being no one. She was good at it. She was, she suspected on certain nights like this one, very nearly the best there had ever been at it, and that thought sat in her chest like a swallowed stone.

* * *

The Hollow did not appear on maps. This was not a figure of speech, not a boast of smugglers or a poet's flourish. She had once paid three years of her own wages for the privilege of examining a surveyor's atlas of the northern provinces—the genuine article, printed in Vell Marrow with gilt-edged plates—and she had run her finger down every river, every ridge line, every road, and found that where her country should have been there was simply nothing. Not blank space; blank space invited curiosity. Nothing. The cartographer's eye slid off it the way a foot slides off ice. A reader looking at that page would find his attention drifting to the margins, to the decorative compass rose, to a smudge of ink he might fancy was a printing error, and then onward, and he would never know that a nation had been lifted out from under his gaze like a coin from a sleeping man's pocket.

That was what the guild had sold them, or bought for them—Isandro had never decided which verb was honest. A generation ago, when the armies of the Concordat came down out of the passes with their annexation papers and their gallows-carts, the Hollow's rulers had gone to the Guild of Small Thefts and made the only purchase left to them. They had traded away the country's name. Its name on every tongue outside its borders, its name in every archive, its name in the memory of every foreigner who had ever done business in its ports or married one of its daughters or lost a battle against its hill regiments. Gone. And with the name went the borders—not erased but *softened*, drawn inward into fog and mutual pretense, until the Hollow was a secret so complete that even the world's hunger for territory could not fasten onto it.

You cannot conquer a country you cannot remember exists.

It had worked. It was still working. Isandro reminded herself of this most evenings, walking home through fog that smelled of brine and coal smoke and the wet wool of a thousand neighbors: *it worked, we are here, we are alive.* The Concordat's soldiers had marched through the passes and found empty valleys, unclaimed moorland, villages they catalogued as ruins though hearth-smoke rose from them plainly enough if you knew to look. The war ended without a single battle fought inside the Hollow. No mass graves. No burned granaries. Every other nation on the map had a scar somewhere in its history; the Hollow had instead a smooth place, a place where memory should have been, and its people lived inside that smoothness the way oysters live inside a shell grown around an irritant.

* * *

Tonight the streets were busy, because the fog market ran late on the eve of the salt-tide, and Isandro took the long way through it, as she often did, telling herself it was to check on the guild's interests along the route.

The market filled the old tannery square and spilled down the lanes around it, stalls shuttered by day and opened by lamplight, because daylight was the one thing the Hollow could not afford. In daylight, from the high fells, a sharp-eyed surveyor might count chimneys. But under fog and lantern-glow, the Hollow conducted its commerce in the things that could not be owned openly: borrowed years decanted into stoppered vials, first kisses sold by widowers who needed the money more than the memory, courage by the dram, grief by the weight. A woman at the corner of Tanner's Row sold sleep to insomniacs, pouring it out of a brown jug like small beer. Two stalls down, a boy no older than fourteen hawked the memories of a dead man's wedding day, going cheap, going cheap, the groom would never miss it now.

Isandro moved among them with her ledger-sense quietly tallying. The guild took its tithe from the fog market—one memory in ten, assessed by weight and rarity—and she knew the stock better than any living clerk. She knew that the summer of the great drought existed in perhaps forty copies in the city, all of them thinned from overuse, tasting faintly of dust. She knew that nobody in the Hollow had sold a memory of snow in eleven years, because snow had become precious in a way that defied the market; people hoarded winter the way other nations hoarded gold.

"Evening, clerk." Old Maren at the lamp-oil stall nodded to her without looking directly at her face. Nobody in the Hollow looked directly at anybody's face if they could help it. It was not rudeness. It was doctrine, though no one called it doctrine. To be seen too clearly was to be *fixed*, and to be fixed was to be findable, and to be findable was to be remembered, and to be remembered—

Well. Outside the fog, to be remembered was death. Inside it, the habit persisted anyway, worn smooth as a river stone, passed from parents to children with the milk. *Don't stand in doorways. Don't sing loud. Don't tell strangers your name, and don't tell friends twice.*

"And to you," Isandro said, and dropped a clipped copper in Maren's dish, and moved on.

* * *

Here was the truth she carried home every night and set down beside her bed like a knife within reach: the pretense was mutual, and it was total, and it was killing them slowly.

Not with soldiers. There were no soldiers. It killed them the way fog kills a fire—by degrees, by damp, by denial of air. A country that cannot be named cannot be mourned abroad; a country that cannot be mourned abroad cannot be joined; and a country that cannot be joined grows strange to itself, generation by generation, the way a letter recopied a hundred times accumulates errors. Children were born in the Hollow who had never seen the sun rise above an acknowledged horizon. Grandmothers died carrying names of towns that no longer appeared anywhere, not in song, not in treaty, not in the memory of any enemy who might have kept them alive out of hatred. Hatred remembers. It was the absence even of hatred that frightened Isandro sometimes, lying awake: the Hollow was not merely hidden. It was *unmissed*. If every soul in it died tonight, the world would not grow lighter by a single known grief.

And yet.

She turned down Candlemakers' Wynd, where the fog lay deepest, and felt the other truth settle over her like a second coat. The wynd's windows glowed amber. Behind them, families ate supper at tables their grandparents had built, on streets their great-grandparents had paved, speaking a language the Concordat's scholars had never gotten the chance to forbid. Nobody here had been conscripted. Nobody here had watched a granary burn or a temple pulled down for its stones. The wars of the last thirty years had passed over the Hollow like weather over a cellar. The children in those glowing windows were alive—alive, fed, untraumatized, ignorant in the way of hothouse fruit—because their grandparents had agreed, all together, in the terrible arithmetic of that final year, to stop existing.

Belonging and captivity wore the same shape here. Isandro had never found the seam between them.

* * *

At the mouth of Wynd she passed the shrine.

Every quarter of the city had one, or had had one, before the Erasure. Most were bricked up now, their niches empty, their thresholds swept but unused—a peculiar civic habit, sweeping a shrine no one admitted to using. This one still had its god, or the statue of him: a lean figure with a key in one hand and a lamp in the other, standing in a doorway carved straight into the wall, half-in and half-out, belonging fully to neither side of the stone. The god of thresholds. The god of doors, of limens, of the exact moment of passing-through. Her grandmother—someone's grandmother; the memory was hers now, whatever its provenance—had taught her the old greeting as a game: *May the Door-Keeper know your name,* said when crossing any sill, and the answer, whispered: *He kept it while I could not.*

Nobody said it anymore. The niche before the statue held rainwater and a single stub of candle, burnt down to a coin of wax, relit so often by some anonymous hand that the stone beneath was black with soot. Someone still tended it. Someone still, in defiance of every law of survival the Hollow lived by, wanted to be known—even if only by a god.

Isandro paused. She did not light a candle. Lighting a candle was a request, and requests were debts, and debts were records, and records were her whole dangerous trade. But she touched two fingers to the cold doorpost as she passed, an old habit she had never managed to break, and felt—as she always told herself she imagined—a faint warmth, like a hand withdrawn just before contact.

* * *

Her rooms were above a cooper's shop on the far side of Bellows Hill, three flights up, chosen for the view of nothing in particular. She climbed to them through fog that had thickened to soup, let herself in, barred the door, and unwrapped the ledger from its oilcloth with hands that moved through the ritual without needing her attention.

The ledger was not one book. It had not been one book for sixty years. It was a shelf of them, forty-one volumes bound in grey hide, and tonight's takings waited in her satchel to be entered: a widow's grief, weighed at four ounces; a soldier's courage, purchased from a deserter who'd had no further use for it; six assorted childhood summers; one prayer.

She stopped with her pen above the page.

One prayer. The client had sold it that afternoon for a scandalously low price, and Corvane had waved it through as counterfeit without a second glance—prayers were common stock, everyone had prayed sometime, and a memory of praying proved nothing about anything being *answered*. But Isandro had held it, as she held everything that crossed the guild's counter, turning it over in her mind the way an assayer turns a coin, and she had felt something she had never felt in twenty years of handling stolen interior goods.

Texture. The memory had texture where prayers were usually smooth. She could still feel it now, sitting alone in her room with the fog pressing at the shutters: the sensation of words leaving a mouth and *arriving somewhere*, of an attention vast and unhurried settling over a kneeling woman like a shawl laid on her shoulders. Prayers in the ordinary stock were one-sided conversations, hope shaped into sound. This had been received. This had been *answered*.

In the Hollow, gods did not answer. Gods were part of what had been traded away—or rather, part of what the hiding required. A god knows its worshippers. A god calls its people by name. You could not erase a nation's name from the world and keep its gods; the gods would have given the game away in a generation, calling across the borders, tugging at foreign hearts, insisting on remembrance. So the old shrines had been bricked up, the old names gone unspoken, the Door-Keeper and his kin left standing in their empty niches like servants dismissed but never told to leave.

Which meant that either the client's memory was the finest forgery in the history of the trade—

—or something outside the fog had begun, after thirty years of silence, to listen.

Isandro dipped her pen. Wrote: *One prayer, provenance uncertain, assessed counterfeit per Guildmaster's judgment.* Then, below it, in the tiny marginal hand she used for notes no auditor was ever meant to read, she added a second line:

*Does not feel counterfeit.*

Outside, the fog thickened further, and somewhere down on Bellows Hill a church bell that officially did not exist rang the hour that officially was not counted, and Isandro Lockridge sat in her room at the center of a country that wasn't, holding a ledger full of stolen lives, and wondered—for the first time in her careful, invisible life—what would happen to all of them if the world began, after all these years, to remember.

She blew out her lamp. The fog pressed at the glass like a face.

* * *

She dreamed, that night, of doors.

Doors standing open in open fields. Doors in the sides of hills, in the hulls of ships, in the middle of roads, all of them ajar, all of them swinging wider, and through each one came a draft of air from somewhere else—air that smelled of sunlight on real grass, of a world with no fog in it at all. And in the dream she understood that the doors were opening because someone on the far side was calling a name, over and over, patiently, the way you call a child home at dusk—

—and she woke before she heard what the name was, with her heart hammering, and lay in the dark listening to the fog against the shutters, and told herself it was only the salt-tide wind.

But in the morning, when she came down the cooper's stairs into the street, the fog was thinner than she had ever known it. Thin enough to see the rooftops. Thin enough, from the top of Bellows Hill, to see—faintly, impossibly, forty miles away where no road ran and no traveler ever came—a single point of light on the border fells, moving steadily toward the city, like a lantern carried by a man who had never been taught that the Hollow was not there to be found.
