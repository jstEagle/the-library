## 5. Inventory of Impossible Things

The summons came at the hour when the Hollow's fog thinned to a gray gauze and even the guild's lamps seemed embarrassed to be burning.

Isandro was at her desk in the counting room, as she always was before dawn, her pen moving through the night's takings with the small, dry music of commerce: three years of a fisherman's patience with his son, purchased by a childless cooper; a first kiss, sold twice, which was technically against policy but which the second buyer had paid enough to make policy negotiable; half a grief from a woman who wanted only enough of it removed that she could stand at her husband's grave without sitting down in the mud. Ordinary inventory. Ordinary thefts. She wrote them all in her careful clerk's hand, each entry squared and dated, and told herself—as she told herself every morning—that recording a thing was not the same as taking it. That the ledger was a mirror, not a knife. That someone had to keep the accounts, and better her than anyone crueler.

She almost believed it. Belief, like memory, could be purchased in increments.

The knock came once, then the door opened without waiting, which meant Corvane.

Guildmaster Corvane did not walk so much as arrive, the way weather arrives, the way debt arrives. He was a tall man gone slightly soft at the edges, dressed in gray so fine it made other grays apologize, and he had the particular stillness of someone who had spent forty years listening to other people's memories and had never once been asked to share his own. His eyes went to her ledger first, as they always did, then to her face, and Isandro felt the familiar cold slide down her spine—the sensation of being *read*, though she knew he couldn't, not really. Reading required a god's touch or a thief's skill, and Corvane was neither. He was something rarer and worse: a man who had built an empire on other people's souls and kept his own locked somewhere no one had ever found.

"Isandro." Her name, spoken flatly, without warmth. He was one of perhaps four people in the entire country who used it. "Close the book."

She closed the book.

"The vault," he said. "Seal it. Everything."

"Everything?" The word escaped before she could weigh it, and she watched him notice that she'd noticed. "Guildmaster, the vault holds eleven thousand entries. The autumn contracts alone—"

"The autumn contracts will keep." He moved past her desk toward the inner stair, and she fell into step behind him because that was what clerks did; they followed the hand that signed their wages. "We have a contamination problem."

"Contamination," she repeated. In the guild's vocabulary, contamination meant memories that degraded when stored—grief that rotted into something else, joy that soured, courage that curdled to cruelty. It happened perhaps twice a year. It was handled with fire and a form in triplicate.

"Not that kind." Corvane paused at the top of the vault stairs, and for a moment—just a moment—he looked old. Not aged; old the way the country was old, the way secrets were old. "God-touched stock."

Isandro's hand found the banister. The stone under her palm was cold enough to feel deliberate.

"There is no god-touched stock," she said. "There hasn't been since—" Since ever, she almost said, since the country was erased, since the gods went silent and the prayers went up like smoke into a sealed room. Everyone knew the gods were gone. It was the one comfort of living in a nation that officially did not exist: whatever had abandoned them had abandoned everyone equally.

"Entry four-four-one-nine," Corvane said. "Sold to us two nights ago by a client who came in weeping and left smiling. A prayer. Answered. Do you understand what I'm telling you?"

She understood the words. What she did not understand—and what she would spend the rest of that day failing not to think about—was why the words landed in her chest like a bell struck in an empty house.

* * *

The vault of the Hollow Guild lay beneath the counting room, three levels down, behind doors banded in iron and inscribed with wards that had cost the guild more than the building above them. It was not, strictly speaking, a vault for treasure. Treasure could be counted. The vault held the inventory itself: row upon row of glass vessels, each one stoppered and labeled in her own handwriting, each one holding a sliver of somebody's life—a summer, a forgiveness, a face, a fear of drowning carefully decanted so its owner could cross water again. They glowed faintly, the way all stored memory glowed, a pale gold like lamplight through honey. Walking the aisles was walking through the town's whole unlived life, every road not taken shelved and dusted and awaiting purchase.

Corvane stood at the end of aisle nine, where the newest acquisitions waited in their quarantine rack, and Isandro joined him there and looked at entry four-four-one-nine and felt the floor tilt very slightly beneath her professional composure.

Stored memory glowed gold. Everyone knew this. Clerks knew it best of anyone; they spent their lives among the little suns of other people's pasts.

This vessel burned white.

Not bright, exactly—it gave off less light than the others, if anything—but *whiter*, the way snow is brighter than a candle while giving off nothing at all. And it moved. The contents shifted inside the glass like something alive turning in its sleep, and when Isandro leaned close—closer than protocol allowed, closer than she would have admitted to allowing—she saw shapes in it. A kitchen. Rain on a window. Hands folded. A voice saying something over and over, though the vessel held no sound, only the shape of sound, the ghost of a cadence that her bones recognized before her mind did.

*Please. Please. Please.*

"It's counterfeit," Corvante said—Corvane said, she corrected herself; her mind was doing strange things this close to the thing. "It has to be. There are no answered prayers. There have been no answered prayers in sixty years, in any country, anywhere on any map or off it. Whatever this client sold us, it's a forgery. Some hedge-priest's trick, some emotional counterfeit cooked up to pass as the real article." He said it firmly, the way a man says a thing he has decided must be true, and Isandro heard the seam in his voice where the decision had been stitched over doubt.

"And the destruction order?"

"Everything touched by it. The entry itself, the intake records, the client's registration, the clerk's verification notes." He glanced at her. "Your notes."

"My notes say it's authentic."

"I know what your notes say."

"Guildmaster." She kept her voice level, clerk-flat, the voice she used for disputing invoices. "If the stock is counterfeit, there's no need to destroy the documentation. We destroy counterfeits all the time. We don't burn the paperwork unless—" Unless the paperwork proves something true. Unless the paper trail leads somewhere you don't want followed. She didn't finish the sentence. Finishing it would have been a statement, and statements were things she had spent thirty years learning not to make.

Corvane looked at her for a long moment. In the white glow of the vessel his face lost ten years and gained twenty.

"You're a good clerk, Isandro," he said finally. "You're precise. You're discreet. You do not ask questions, and you do not keep copies, and you do not wonder. Those are the reasons you've lasted." He turned back to the shelf. "Seal the vault after the burning. No one enters until I say. And Isandro—if you find yourself wondering, don't. Wondering is how people end up in the inventory instead of behind the desk."

He left her alone with eleven thousand small suns and one impossible star.

* * *

The burning was scheduled for dusk, because fire needed witnesses and witnesses needed schedules. Corvane assembled the senior staff in the vault's antechamber—four clerks, two appraisers, the warden of the seals—and read out the decree in a voice pitched for gravity: contaminated stock, destroyed per the third statute, inventory adjusted accordingly. Isandro stood at the back with her ledger against her chest like a shield and watched the tongs come out, long iron things padded with wool, and watched the white vessel lifted from its rack, and felt something in her rise up against it—not horror, exactly, though there was horror in it too, but something more like *recognition*. Like watching them carry a person toward a fire and knowing the person's name.

Which was absurd. It was a memory. It belonged to a client whose name she knew from the intake form—Maren Voss, widow, weaver, sixty-one years of age—and whose prayer it had been she did not know, because the vessel held the experience, not the context. But she had written the intake form. She had verified the stock, run her thumb along the glass the way she did with every acquisition, checking for the texture of truth. Counterfeit memories had a texture; she'd catalogued it years ago in her private mental taxonomy. Forgeries were smooth. Too smooth, like river stones, like lies worn round by repetition. Real memory had grain. Real memory caught on something.

This had caught on everything. This had caught on *her*, through the glass, through her gloves, through thirty years of careful professional distance, and she had written *authentic* in her notes with a hand that had shaken only slightly, and she had told herself the shaking was the cold of the vault.

The tongs carried the vessel to the brazier. The warden unstoppered it. Memory, released from glass, burns fast and clean and colorless, leaving nothing but a smell like rain on hot stone—

"No," said Isandro Lockridge, nameless clerk of the Hollow Guild, in a voice that came out much louder than she intended.

Every head turned. The warden froze with the vessel tilted over the coals. Corvane, across the chamber, looked at her with an expression she had never seen on him before and would spend weeks decoding: not anger. Interest.

"Clerk," he said.

"The—the seal." Her mind, quick even in panic, snatched at procedure like a drowning woman at driftwood. "Statute three requires the seal-witness to confirm the stock's classification before destruction. I classified it. I should witness it. Properly. At the brazier. Otherwise the record is irregular and the auditors—" There were no auditors. There had never been auditors; the guild answered to no one, that was rather the point of the guild. But Corvane's jaw tightened fractionally, because even empires pretend to have rules, and rules are useful levers for anyone low enough to reach them.

"Witness it, then," he said. "Quickly."

She crossed the chamber. She took the tongs. The vessel sat in the iron cradle warm as a living thing, and up close the white light was not light at all but *movement*, the memory turning over and over like a sleeper who cannot get comfortable, and through the glass she saw the kitchen again, the rain, the folded hands, and now she saw something new: a figure standing in the doorway of the remembered room, tall, indistinct, *present* in the way nothing in stored memory was ever present. Memories did not contain presences. Memories contained the shape of what had been seen. This contained the shape of something that had been *there*.

And the vessel was warm. Not brazier-warm. Hand-warm. Blood-warm.

"Confirmed," she heard herself say. "Classification confirmed. Destroy it."

The warden tipped it over the coals, and the memory went up in a breath of white flame that did not behave like fire—it rose straight, unnaturally straight, like a column, like a pillar, like something standing—and the smell was not rain on stone but bread. Fresh bread. Someone's kitchen, someone's ordinary morning, someone's prayer, and the whole vault full of golden lights flickered once, all together, all eleven thousand of them, like a congregation bowing its head.

Then it was ash, and the staff dispersed, and Corvane dismissed her with a nod, and Isandro walked back up the stairs to her desk with her heart hammering and her face composed and one thought repeating behind her eyes like a prayer of its own:

*I touched it. I touched it and it knew me.*

* * *

She did not sleep that night. Sleep was for people whose hands were clean, and hers had just helped feed a miracle to a brazier, and whatever else she was—thief by proxy, accomplice by habit, coward by long practice—she discovered at three in the morning, sitting upright in her narrow bed above the chandler's shop, that she was not yet the kind of person who could burn the only proof that the gods had spoken and simply go to bed.

Because that was what it had been. She knew it the way she knew good stock from bad, the way she knew a forged signature from a true one: by texture. The memory had been real. The prayer had been *answered*. Something had stood in Maren Voss's kitchen while the rain came down, and the guild had destroyed the only record of it, and Corvane had ordered the vault sealed and the documents burned, and none of it made sense unless—

Unless he was afraid.

Corvane did not destroy valuable things. Corvane did not burn authentic stock, which could be sold for ten times the price of anything counterfeit. Corvane had built the Hollow Guild on the principle that everything human could be bottled, priced, and traded, and here was a thing that refused the bottle, glowed the wrong color, warmed the glass like a living hand—and he had fed it to the fire within a day of acquiring it and ordered every trace of its passage erased.

Men did not do that out of caution. Men did that out of terror.

Isandro got up. She dressed in the dark. She took from her desk drawer the tools of her trade—a fresh ledger, half-size, bound in undyed leather, the kind clerks used for drafts—and she let herself back into the guild through the delivery door whose key hung on the hook it had hung on for thirty years, because no one guarded against the clerk. No one ever guarded against the clerk. Invisibility was not merely her shelter; tonight, for the first time, it was her weapon.

The vault wards recognized her seal. Of course they did; she had inked half of them herself, in the meticulous hand that Corvane trusted precisely because it never wondered. The iron doors swung open onto eleven thousand small suns, and she descended into their honey-colored light carrying her blank book like a stolen thing, which it was, which she was, which she supposed she had always been and had only now stopped pretending otherwise.

She could not copy the prayer-memory. It was ash. That door had closed, and the guilt of it sat in her chest like a swallowed coal.

But the client had sold more than the prayer. Intake forms recorded the full transaction, and Maren Voss, widow, weaver, had come to the guild that night with a bundle of stock to trade against her grief: the prayer, yes, but also the years surrounding it. A winter. A sickness. A vigil kept at a bedside. Small sorrows, ordinary ones, the kind of inventory the guild processed a hundred times a season—except that these had been lived in the same house, under the same roof, in the same days as the impossible thing, and if the impossible thing had changed the air of that house at all, if its presence had left so much as a fingerprint on the neighboring memories—

Aisle six. Rack fourteen. Entries four-four-one-five through four-four-one-eight: the vigil, the sickness, the winter, and one unlabeled vessel that the appraiser had marked *miscellaneous—domestic*.

Isandro lifted the last one, the miscellaneous one, and her breath stopped.

Gold, it should have been. Domestic memory ran gold, always, the color of lamplight and butter and ordinary evenings. This vessel held a color she had no name for—not white, not gold, something between, the color of the sky just before dawn when you cannot swear whether the light is coming or going. And it was warm. Faintly, gently, unmistakably warm, like a stone left near a hearth.

*Miscellaneous. Domestic.* The appraiser hadn't known what he was holding. Perhaps nobody did. Perhaps the answer had soaked into everything in that house the way damp soaks into walls, and the guild was sitting on a vault full of it without knowing, and Corvane had sealed the doors because somewhere in his careful gray heart he suspected exactly that.

Isandro set the vessel on the copying bench, uncapped her pen, and opened the blank book.

Copying memory was not, strictly speaking, possible. Every clerk knew this; it was the guild's founding axiom, the wall the trade was built against. Memory could be removed, stored, transferred—but not duplicated. A thing taken was a thing gone; the ledger recorded the taking, never the keeping. To copy was to steal from the thief, and the guild had laws older than the country-that-wasn't against it.

But the ledger was not only a record. Isandro had suspected this for years, in the wordless way one suspects a thing one cannot afford to know. She had noticed, over decades of entries, that certain pages hummed. That certain vessels resonated against certain inks. That the ledger, bound in its plain leather, sometimes seemed less like a book than like a lock with eleven thousand tumblers, waiting.

She pressed the warm vessel to the page, the way she had seen the transfer-wardens press stock to receiving-glass, and she wrote the entry as she wrote every entry—date, seller, classification, description—and as her pen moved, the words did something words had never done before.

They *took*. The ink sank deeper than ink should sink. The description of a domestic evening, a sickroom, a vigil, a presence in a doorway, wrote itself onto the page with a weight like a keystone settling into an arch, and the little half-size ledger grew warm under her hand, warm as the vessel, warm as a hand, and somewhere far below the level of thought Isandro understood that she had just done the impossible thing, the founding-crime, the theft-from-thieves—

—and that the page had not recorded the memory.

The page had *become* it.

She closed the book quickly, heart slamming, and looked around the vault as if the wards might have noticed, as if the eleven thousand small suns might have turned to look. They glowed on, patient, golden, oblivious—or not oblivious, she could no longer swear to anything, not after tonight, not after the flicker at the burning, the whole vault bowing its head.

She climbed the stairs. She resealed the doors. She walked home through the fog with the stolen book against her ribs, feeling its warmth through her coat like a second heartbeat, and she did not let herself think about what she had done, because thinking would require naming it, and naming it would require deciding whether she was a traitor.

Instead she thought about Maren Voss. Widow, weaver, sixty-one years of age. A woman who had prayed in a rainy kitchen and been *answered*, and had then sold the answer to the Hollow Guild for enough coin to live out her winter, because that was what people in the Hollow did with miracles—they traded them, they spent them, they converted the divine into rent. And tomorrow, Isandro decided, tomorrow she would find the woman. Official guild business: post-destruction follow-up, client interview, standard procedure. She could invent a form for it. She was very good at forms.

The fog closed behind her, thick and gray and hiding everything, the way it had hidden her all her life.

For the first time in thirty years, Isandro Lockridge was grateful for the fog—for the first time, she had something worth hiding.

The little ledger pulsed once against her ribs, warm as a held hand, and did not grow cold all the way home.
