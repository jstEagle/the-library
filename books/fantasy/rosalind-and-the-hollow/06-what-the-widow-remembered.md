## 6. What the Widow Remembered

The entry had been bothering Isandro for three days.

She told herself it was nothing. She was a clerk; clerks were bothered by entries the way sailors were bothered by weather—constantly, professionally, and without any expectation that the weather would change its mind. But this one sat in her mind like a splinter worked under the skin, too small to grip and too deep to ignore.

*Lot 114. One memory, freely sold: a prayer answered. Seller: Maren Ostrevik, widow, age sixty-one. Payment rendered: eleven years of borrowed springtime.*

Eleven years of spring for one answered prayer. The guild's appraisers had valued it low—not because it was worthless but because they'd judged it counterfeit. Answered prayers did not exist. They were the currency of desperate people and dishonest priests, and the Hollow Guild did not deal in fictions. Fictions could not be stored in the vaults beneath Candlemaker Street; fictions curdled there, went sour, leaked into neighboring memories like ink through wet paper. The appraisers had tested Lot 114 twice, found it *true* both times, and then—this was the part Isandro kept returning to—had quietly marked it *suspect* rather than *genuine*, because *genuine* would have required them to believe something impossible, and the guild had survived a generation of erasure precisely by refusing to believe impossible things.

Isandro believed in ledgers. Ledgers did not lie. A ledger might be wrong—a clerk's hand slipping, an appraiser's judgment clouded—but it did not lie, because a ledger was only ever a record of what someone remembered, and memory, whatever else it was, was never a lie. It might be mistaken. It might be stolen. It might be bought and sold and carried away in glass vials by men with soft voices and soft hands. But it was always true somewhere, in some mouth, at some hour, and so Lot 114 was true somewhere, and Isandro wanted to know where.

So on the fourth morning, before the fog had finished burning off the rooftops, she took the ledger's duplicate index down from its shelf, copied the widow's address onto a scrap of paper no larger than a communion wafer, and burned the original over the lamp flame, letting the ash fall into the inkwell where it would never be found.

Bookkeeping was not stealing. But some mornings she wondered what it was.

* * *

The Ostrevik house stood at the ragged eastern edge of the Lamplight Quarter, where the streets gave up pretending to be streets and became alleys, and the alleys gave up pretending to be anything at all. The fog lived thicker here. It pooled in doorways like spilled milk and clung to the walls in grey rags, and the lamps—the quarter took its name from them—burned all day out of stubbornness rather than need, their light going soft and yellow and defeated within three feet of the glass.

Isandro walked with her collar up and her eyes down, which was how everyone in the Hollow walked, and thought about the strangeness of a country you could not leave and could not name. She had been born here—or she assumed she had; her earliest memories began at nine years old, sharp-edged and sudden, like a play whose first act had been cut for time—and in all her thirty-one years she had never once heard anyone say the country's name aloud. Not because it was forbidden. Nothing was forbidden here except being noticed. The name had simply been *sold*, all of it, every syllable traded away to the guild in the great bargain before she was born, and now when people spoke of home they said *here*, and when they spoke of the world beyond the fog they said *out there*, and the silence where the name should have been was so old and so complete that most people had forgotten there was ever a silence at all.

A woman passed her carrying bread, and nodded. Isandro nodded back. Neither of them existed. It was, on most days, a comfortable arrangement.

The Ostrevik door was blue paint gone grey, and it opened before Isandro finished knocking.

"I know why you've come." The woman in the doorway was small and round and fierce-eyed, with flour on her knuckles and a shawl the color of old onions. "You're from the guild. You've come about my mother."

"I'm—" Isandro began, and stopped, because the lie she kept ready behind her teeth (*I'm only a clerk, I'm nobody, I'm just checking the records*) refused to come out. Something about the woman's face made lying feel like kicking a dog. "Yes," she said instead. "About your mother."

"Then you're late." The woman stepped back to let her in. "She's been asking for death for a week. Perhaps you can tell her it's out of stock."

* * *

The sickroom smelled of beeswax and camphor and something underneath both, something warm and mineral, like sun-baked stone. The widow Maren Ostrevik lay in a bed that had clearly been moved to the center of the room—moved there deliberately, Isandro thought, the way you'd move a table to make space for guests—and though she must have weighed less than the bedclothes covering her, she filled the room the way a lit stove fills a kitchen.

Her eyes found Isandro the moment she crossed the threshold, and they were not the eyes of a dying woman. They were too quick. Too amused.

"So," the widow said. Her voice was dry as autumn leaves but it carried. "They sent a girl."

"They sent a clerk," Isandro said.

"A clerk." The widow smiled with too few teeth. "In sixty-one years I have learned that the people who call themselves clerks are the ones holding the pens, and the ones holding the pens are the ones writing the world. Come closer, girl. My daughter will bring tea she'll insist I not drink, and we'll talk while she pretends not to listen."

The daughter—her name was Brigit, Isandro learned, and she moved through the room with the brittle efficiency of someone who had been grieving for months and was furious at grief for taking so long—brought tea and set it down and retreated to the window, where she began furiously mending a shirt that did not appear to need mending.

"You sold us a memory," Isandro said, sitting in the chair beside the bed. "Three weeks ago. A prayer, answered. The appraisers marked it suspect."

"Suspect." The widow laughed, and the laugh turned into coughing, and the coughing bent her double, and Isandro half-rose in alarm before the old woman waved her back down with one twig of a hand. "Suspect. Yes. I suppose a fish would mark water suspect, if it had spent its whole life being told it lived in stone." She settled back against her pillows. "Sit, clerk. I haven't the strength to talk to someone who won't sit."

Isandro sat.

"I was married forty years," the widow said. "To a good man. A dull man, God rest him, good and dull as bread, and I loved him the way you love bread, which is to say completely and without ever once thinking about it. And when he died—two winters past—I discovered that grief is not a feeling. Everyone tells you it's a feeling. It is not. It is a *place*. You live there. You wake up in it. And the house is cold, and the chair across from yours is empty, and there is no one to tell the small things to, and the small things are the whole of a life, clerk, the small things are everything, and I had nowhere in the world to put mine."

Isandro's pen-hand twitched. She was not carrying her pen. She noticed herself reaching for it anyway.

"So I prayed," the widow said simply. "I hadn't prayed since I was a child, since before the erasure, since my grandmother taught me the old words over the kneading trough and told me never to say them where the walls could hear. But grief makes pagans of us all. I knelt by his chair—his *chair*, clerk, do you understand, the ugly green thing with the sprung cushion that I couldn't throw out—and I prayed. Not for him back. I'm not a fool. I prayed—" and here her voice changed, dropped into a register older than her body, a cadence Isandro felt in her sternum like a struck bell— "*Hear me, keeper of the hinge, opener of the way. My house is cold and my hands are full of small things. Send me someone to carry them.*"

The room grew warmer.

It was not dramatic. There was no wind, no light, no trembling of cups. The temperature simply rose—two degrees, perhaps three—as though someone had opened an oven door in a distant room, or as though the afternoon sun had decided, out of season and out of spite, to visit. Isandro felt it on her neck first, then her hands, and she watched the widow's face as the old woman felt it too, and saw there an expression Isandro had no ledger-category for: not joy, not fear, but *recognition*, deep and terrible, the look of a woman hearing a voice she had spent fifty years persuading herself she had imagined.

"He came," the widow whispered. "That night. Or something came. Through the door—he came *through the door*, clerk, though every door in the house was shut. He didn't open it. He was simply on the other side of it, and then he wasn't, and the room was warm the way it's warm right now, and he sat in the green chair."

"What did he look like?" Isandro asked. Her voice came out smaller than she intended.

"Like nothing. Like a man made of the moment when you step over a threshold and haven't yet arrived anywhere—that's what he looked like. That pause. He wore it like a coat." The widow's eyes were shining. "And he didn't say anything. Gods don't, when they're real. The false ones talk and talk. He just sat there, in my dead husband's chair, warming the room, and I talked. All night I talked. Every small thing. The way Aldous hummed when he shaved. The argument we had in '61 about whether to plant beans. How he held his cup with two hands even in summer. I poured out forty years of small things and he *listened*, and when the dawn came he was gone and the chair was empty and the room stayed warm until noon."

"And you sold that," Isandro said. "You sold the memory of it. To us."

The widow looked at her for a long moment, and there was pity in the look, which Isandro found unbearable.

"I sold the *memory*, yes. Because my daughter needed eleven springs she doesn't have, and grief is heavy, and I thought—I was dying anyway, girl, what use has a dead woman for remembering?" The old woman lifted one hand, trembling now, and pressed it flat against her own breastbone. "But here is what your appraisers don't know, and what your guildmaster doesn't know, and what I suspect you are only beginning to know, standing there with your clerk's face and your clerk's hands. The memory was sold. The memory left me. I cannot picture the green chair anymore. I cannot picture *him*. But the warmth—" her hand curled into a fist over her heart— "the warmth stayed. Whatever he touched in me, he didn't take it when the memory went. It's still here. It's the only thing keeping me warm, these last weeks, and I think—" and now her voice did break, finally, beautifully, like ice going out of a river— "I think it's the only thing keeping me alive, and I think it's why I haven't died yet, because I am the last person in this country carrying a piece of him, and I don't think he knows how to leave a house while someone in it still remembers his name."

The room was very warm now. The tea in its untouched cup had begun, very faintly, to steam.

"His name," Isandro said. Her mouth was dry. "What was his name?"

Brigit looked up sharply from her mending. "Mother, don't—"

"Hush." The widow's eyes never left Isandro's. "You came all this way through the fog, little clerk. You burned something to find my address—I can smell the ash on your sleeves, I raised four children, I know the smell of secret paper. You didn't come to check a record. Records don't tremble." She reached out and caught Isandro's wrist, and her grip was astonishingly strong for a dying woman, and her skin was hot, fever-hot, hearth-hot. "You want to know if it's real. So I'll give you what I sold your guild, the part they couldn't put in a vial. Lean close."

Isandro leaned close.

And the widow Maren Ostrevik spoke a god's name.

It was two syllables. It was nothing—no thunder, no Latin weight, no incantation. Two soft syllables, spoken the way you'd speak the name of an old friend met unexpectedly in a market, and Isandro would spend the rest of her life trying to remember exactly what they were and finding that they slipped from memory like water from a closed fist, and yet—

Yet the moment they were spoken, the room *blazed*. Not with light. With warmth. Warmth rolled off the widow's bed in a visible shimmer, and the fog pressing against the windows turned gold at the edges, and the lamp that had burned all day in yellow defeat suddenly stood up straight and bright as a candle at a vigil, and somewhere below them, in the street, a dog began to sing—one long, wavering, unmistakably joyful note—and Isandro felt the warmth enter her own chest and settle there, behind the sternum, in the exact place where the widow's fist had pressed, and she understood with total and terrible clarity that something ancient had just heard itself named in a country that had been erased from every map in the world, and had *turned toward the sound*.

Then it faded. The lamp sank. The dog fell silent. The widow slumped back against her pillows, gray-faced and gasping and grinning, grinning like a girl.

"There," she wheezed. "Now you've heard it too. Now it's in you. You can sell it or keep it, clerk, that's your affair—but you can't unknow it." Her eyes drifted half-closed. "He's coming back, you know. They all are. The old ones. We starved them with forgetting, and now the forgetting is wearing thin, and they're coming home to a country that doesn't remember them, and they are going to be *so hungry*."

"Hungry for what?" Isandro whispered.

The widow's eyes opened again, fully, and fixed on her, and the amusement in them was gone, and what remained was older than the room, older than the house, older than the fog.

"For what we owe them," she said. "For forty years of small things. For names. For *everything we wrote down instead of remembered*, girl. Your ledgers are full of debts." She closed her eyes. "Go home. Check your books. Count what your guild holds that was never truly sold—and count what it holds of *you*."

Isandro stood. Her legs felt borrowed. At the door she paused, and looked back at the small fierce shape in the bed, already sinking toward sleep, still warm, still burning like a banked fire.

"Will you die tonight?" she asked, and hated how young her voice sounded.

"Perhaps," the widow murmured, smiling into her pillow. "Or perhaps I'll hold on until he comes back through the door. He does like doors." A pause. "Clerk. When the hungering starts—and it will start soon, sooner than your masters think—remember which side of the ledger you stood on."

* * *

Isandro walked home through the fog with the god's name dissolving in her memory like sugar in rain, unable to hold it and unable to forget that she had held it, and the warmth behind her sternum did not fade with distance. If anything it strengthened, block by block, until by the time she reached Candlemaker Street she could feel it pulsing gently in time with her heartbeat, patient as a banked coal, and she caught herself thinking, with a clerk's precision and a believer's vertigo:

*The vaults are full of prayers. Three hundred years of them. Every wish anyone ever sold us—and if one widow's warmth could fill a room—*

She stopped in the middle of the street.

Because she had just remembered something she had catalogued a thousand times without ever once *reading*: the vault sub-levels, the sealed shelves, the lots marked *dormant*—thousands upon thousands of memories of prayers, offered up by desperate people across generations, prayers that had never been answered, that the guild had bought cheap as curiosities.

Prayers that had never been answered *yet*.

Behind her, far off in the east, the fog glowed faintly gold, as though the whole horizon had become a door left ajar. Ahead of her, the guildhouse waited with its sealed vaults and its sleeping inventory and its guildmaster who believed, above all things, that gods were bad for business.

Isandro pulled her collar up, put her head down, and walked toward it—toward the ledgers, toward the vault keys, toward the terrible arithmetic waiting in her careful hands—while behind her the light in the fog brightened, and brightened, and did not go out.
