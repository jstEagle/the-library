## 7. The Guild's First Law

The fire in Corvane's study had been lit an hour before he needed it, as all his fires were. Preparation, he had learned across ninety years of keeping the Hollow's books, was the whole difference between mercy and cruelty. A man who waited until the cold arrived to kindle flame was a man who loved drama more than warmth. Corvane had no patience for drama. He had ledgers for that.

He sat now with the oldest volume in the guild's possession open across his knees—a book so old its leather had gone the color of tea left standing, so old that the ink had sunk into the page rather than sitting upon it, as though the words themselves had grown ashamed and tried to burrow out of sight. The Ledger of the First Bargain. No clerk was permitted to touch it. No clerk even knew it existed. Isandro Lockridge, with her flawless hand and her flawless silence, catalogued everything else in the vault down to the last stolen afternoon, but this book lived behind his own breastbone, carried up from the cellar only on nights when the fog pressed hard against the windows and the country outside seemed to strain at its own invisibility like a dog against a chain.

Tonight was such a night.

Outside, somewhere past the crooked lanes and the shuttered market and the ten thousand lives that existed by mutual agreement not to exist, something had changed. He could feel it the way an old man feels weather in his joints—not the fog itself but a pressure beneath it, a warmth where there should be none. Three days ago a client had sold them a memory of a prayer answered. The appraisers had called it counterfeit. Corvane had read the entry himself, twice, and said nothing, because saying nothing was the first law of his office and he had kept that law longer than any man alive.

But he had also gone down into the cellar afterward and stood for a long time before the sealed case where god-touched memories were kept, and he had felt, through three inches of leaded glass and forty years of dust, something on the other side pressing back.

*Not yet*, he told the feeling, as he had told it every night for a generation. *Not while I still hold the pen.*

He turned the first page of the First Bargain and let the past come up around him like water.

* * *

It had begun, as all their catastrophes began, with an army.

Corvane had been young then—younger than Isandro was now, though he had already held the guildmaster's seal, having inherited it from a master who died mid-sentence, pen still wet, the invasion's vanguard already burning farms along the river. He remembered the council chamber of those days: not this comfortable room with its good fire and its view of nothing in particular, but a cellar deeper than the current one, lit by tallow, crowded with the men and women who had run the country's affairs back when the country had affairs to run. Generals whose armies were already ash. Ministers whose ministries existed only in their heads. And at the end of the table, quietest of all of them, the old guildmaster's ghost in the shape of Corvane himself, holding a ledger no one else had thought to bring.

The maps had been laid out on the table. That was what he remembered most—the maps, beautiful and fatal, every road and river and town name rendered in a conqueror's cartographer's loving hand. The enemy did not need to hate them. Hatred took effort. All the enemy needed was the maps, and the maps were everywhere, copied into atlases, embroidered onto tapestries, sung in the verses of marching songs. You cannot burn a country out of the world's memory while the world can still point to where it sleeps.

"We fight," the last general had said. He was missing two fingers and most of his hope. "We fight in the hills. We make them pay for every mile."

"They have already paid," Corvane had heard himself say. His voice had surprised him—steady, reasonable, the voice of a bookkeeper rather than a patriot. "They paid for the river crossing. They paid for the granaries. They will keep paying, because they can afford to, and we cannot afford anything at all." He had looked around the table, at the faces lit gutter-yellow, and asked the question that would define the rest of his life: "What is it they are actually conquering?"

Silence. Then the old minister of records—he had been ancient even then, a man who remembered three kings—had said, slowly, as if handling something fragile: "A name. In the end. They are conquering a name."

"Yes," Corvane said. "So we stop being one."

He had not invented the method. That was the thing he clung to, all these years later, the splinter of absolution he worried at on nights like this: he had not invented it. The guild had traded in names since before anyone now living drew breath. Names were simply memories worn in public, and the guild had always known how to lift a memory without leaving a scar. What he had done was scale it. What he had done was turn a pickpocket's trick into a nation's survival.

The bargain had taken eleven months to arrange. Eleven months of the guild's finest thieves working in shifts, going door to door through a country that believed it was merely enduring a bad winter, lifting—not stealing, never stealing, *purchasing*, always with consent extracted from desperation, which everyone at the table had agreed to call consent—the memory of the country's name. Not the word itself; words could be relearned. The *weight* of it. The way it sat in the chest. The way a mother saying it over a cradle made the child hers and the land theirs and the whole arrangement holy. They took the weight and left the sound, and a sound without weight is just noise, and noise is easily forgotten.

And then the borders. This had been the guildmaster's true masterwork, the part Corvane had executed rather than designed: a fog raised not from water but from forgetting itself, a concealment woven out of every memory they harvested, so that the country did not hide behind the fog but *was* the fog—unmappable because unremembered, unfindable because no one outside could hold the shape of it in their mind long enough to walk toward it. The conquerors' armies had marched along the frontier the following spring and found, by every account that survives in the world's records, nothing. Empty moor. Unclaimed waste. Their cartographers drew a blank space and, being practical men, filled it with a decorative sea monster.

Eleven months. Four hundred thousand names. Corvane had signed the final page himself, and his hand had not shaken, and he had hated himself for that steadiness ever after.

* * *

Here was the arithmetic he performed every night, the nightly mass at which he was both priest and sole congregant:

Four hundred thousand people, erased from the world's knowledge—and therefore erased from the conqueror's lists. No census rolls. No conscription notices. No forced resettlements, no labor levies, no children taken to be raised in the victor's tongue. The empire that had come for them ground through a dozen other nations in the following decades, and Corvane had read the numbers, because reading numbers was his gift and his punishment. He knew exactly what happened to countries that stayed named. He could recite the attrition rates the way other men recited psalms.

Against that: the cost. The Hollow's people woke one morning unable to say what they were, only that they were, together, here. They built new habits in the shape of the old ones without knowing why the shapes fit. They sang songs with a hole in the middle verse. They told their children they had always lived in the fog, and the children believed them, because children believe, and because it was true enough. Was that not a life? Was that not, in fact, a life *saved*?

Mercy, he told the fire. It was mercy. Ugly mercy, mercy with a ledger's cold face, but mercy nonetheless—the mercy of the surgeon who cuts to save, the mercy no patient thanks you for. The alternative had been graves. He had chosen fog over graves, and he would choose it again, and the fact that he had to choose it again soon—that the bargain was fraying, that prayers were being answered, that some warmth was eating through his beautiful concealment like acid through cloth—did not change the arithmetic. It only meant the surgery must be performed again, and deeper this time.

He closed the First Bargain and pressed his palm flat against its cover, the way another man might close a dead friend's eyes.

* * *

There was one entry in the old ledger he permitted himself to reread only once a year, and tonight, apparently, was to be that night, because his hands turned to it without consulting him. Page four hundred and twelve. A single line in his own younger handwriting:

*Item: one (1) childhood, complete, seller: a woman of the Weavers' Row, price: her family's standing within the Hollow, in perpetuity.*

He had processed that purchase personally. He remembered the woman—of course he remembered her; guilt has an excellent memory even when it pretends otherwise. She had come to the guild house with a child asleep against her shoulder, a girl of perhaps five or six, dark-haired, watchful even in sleep, one fist curled around a wooden button as though it were the last small thing in the world she owned. The woman had not wept. That was what he recalled with such terrible clarity—not tears but their absence, a face already emptied, already grieved, doing the arithmetic of mothers in wartime: *they will take my daughter's future or they will take my daughter, and I am offered a third door, and I am too tired to ask what stands behind it.*

"You understand," he had told her, because the law required disclosure and disclosure was the guild's one vanity of honor, "that what you sell is not returnable. The child will grow up without it. She will be—" he had searched for the honest word and failed to find it, then, and failed to find it now— "*lighter*. Than she should be."

"I know what I'm selling," the woman had said. Her voice had been flat as a coin. "I'm buying her a country that can't be conquered. Show me where to sign."

And he had shown her. And she had signed. And the child had slept on, clutching her button, while Corvane lifted her childhood out of her with instruments older than the conquest—lifted it clean, lifted it whole, every scraped knee and lullaby and first snowfall—and sealed it in the vault, where it remained to this day, item four thousand and six, gathering dust beside a widow's grief and a soldier's courage.

He had told himself then what he told himself now: the mother chose. Consent was given. The guild's hands were clean by the only standard that mattered, the standard of the transaction, and whatever heaviness Corvane carried home that night was interest on a debt no contract recognized.

He had not learned, until years later, what became of the child. He had learned it the way he learned everything—by reading a personnel file. The girl had grown up light, exactly as promised. She had grown up precise, invisible, indispensable. She had applied to the guild at fourteen with references no one could verify and a handwriting so perfect it made the senior clerks uneasy, and she had risen through the ranks like water finding its level, and she was now, at this moment, asleep in her narrow bed above the counting room, dreaming whatever a person dreams when the foundation stones of her self have been sold out from under her.

Isandro Lockridge. Item four thousand and six.

Corvane had never told her. There had been moments—a dozen across the years—when the truth had climbed his throat like floodwater, and each time he had swallowed it, and each time he had told himself the same thing: *she is better as she is. She is perfect as she is. The gap in her is the shape of the guild itself, and she fills it without knowing, and knowing would only wound her.* It was, he understood dimly, the same sentence he had spoken about the whole country for forty years. *They are better as they are. Knowing would only wound them.* He was aware, in the small cold room of himself where he kept the things he did not examine, that a man who tells this lie often enough begins to mistake it for a principle.

The fire had burned low. He fed it another log and watched the flames take, and beyond the glass the fog lay against the window like something listening.

* * *

Because here was the truth he had carried alone since the winter of the bargain, the truth that made him rise from his chair now and go to the window and stand looking out at his invisible kingdom:

The fog was not free.

Nothing in the guild's trade was free; that was the second law, taught to every apprentice before they touched a ledger. Every memory taken must be paid for, every debt entered, every balance maintained—and the concealment was the largest transaction the guild had ever conducted, and its payment schedule had never ended. The fog drank. Slowly, patiently, generation after generation, it drank the memories the guild had fed it, and when those ran thin it drank the edges of newer ones, and the guild replenished it the way a farmer feeds a fire—continually, quietly, with pieces of the country itself. Small thefts. A first kiss here. A grief there. Nothing anyone missed, or missed missing. The Hollow's people had been paying rent on their own survival for forty years without receiving a bill, and Corvane was the bill-keeper, and he had always believed the rent fair.

But bargains of that size do not run forever. He had known the term when he signed. He had signed anyway, telling himself the reckoning would fall on some future guildmaster, some future age with better tools or better luck. And now the term was arriving, ahead of schedule, wearing the face of answered prayers.

Gods, he thought, watching the fog shift against the glass. Of course it was gods. The old gods had been part of what was purchased—part of the name, part of the weight, part of the country's public soul. A nation's gods live in its remembering, and the guild had starved them deliberately, carefully, feeding the fog on devotion the way it fed on everything else. For forty years the shrines had stood empty and the prayers had fallen into silence, and Corvane had counted that silence among his accomplishments.

Now something was answering. Which meant something was remembering. Which meant the fog was thinning from the inside, and all his arithmetic—all his beautiful, brutal, merciful arithmetic—was about to be audited by forces that did not keep books.

He returned to his desk and drew a fresh sheet toward him. For a long moment he did not write. Then, in the small exact hand that had signed away a nation's name, he set down the only sentence that mattered:

*The bargain expires. The erasure must be renewed—or completed.*

Completed. He looked at the word for a long time. Renewal meant feeding the fog again, another eleven months of theft, another four hundred thousand small wounds—and it would buy them another forty years, and then another reckoning, forever, an endless tithe of the self. But completion—completion meant finishing what the bargain had started. If the name was the disease, why leave the symptoms? If remembering was the vulnerability, then the cure was not to patch the memory of the country's name but to remove the country's memory of itself altogether. No nation to erase if there was no nationhood left to find. A people who did not know they had ever been one could never be betrayed by that knowledge, never be hunted by it, never die for it. They would be safe the way the unborn are safe.

The fire popped. Somewhere below, in the counting room, a floorboard settled under no one's weight—or under someone's; the guild house was old and full of sounds, and lately full of other things too.

Corvane dipped his pen. He did not tremble. He had stopped waiting for his hands to tremble thirty years ago, and their refusal had become, in its way, the saddest fact of his life.

Tomorrow he would seal the vault. Tomorrow he would order the god-touched memories destroyed, every impossible prayer burned before it could spread its warmth, and the clerks would obey him because they always obeyed him, and Isandro Lockridge would record the destruction in her flawless hand and tell herself, as she told herself every day, that bookkeeping was not stealing, that recording was not complicity, that a clerk who keeps the accounts is not the same as the merchant who sets the prices.

He almost pitied her that comfort. He had bought the same comfort at a far higher price, and he knew precisely what it cost and precisely how little it covered.

He wrote the order. He signed it. He blotted the ink with sand, and the sand hissed softly, like fog retreating, like a country holding its breath, like a god clearing its throat at the edge of a silence forty years deep.

By morning, the vault would be sealed. By evening, the burning would begin.

And somewhere in the sleeping house above the counting room, a clerk with a gap where her childhood should be turned over in her sleep, and dreamed, though she would never remember it, of a door.
