## 8. Veyl Takes a Confession

The cell smelled of tallow and old stone, of the particular dampness that gathers in places where sunlight has never been invited. Magistrate Veyl stood at the door for a long moment before entering, the way he always did, letting the silence do its first work. Silence was a tool. Most men feared it more than pain; they would confess to anything simply to fill it.

The thief sat on the plank bed with his wrists bound in consecrated cord, and he was trying to look bored. He was failing. Sweat had darkened the collar of his shirt despite the cold, and his eyes kept flicking to Veyl's hands—specifically to the rings, three of them, worn on the right hand in the manner of the Office of Inquiry. Ordinary men thought they were decoration. Thieves knew better.

"You've been read your rights," Veyl said. "I'll not repeat them."

"I know my rights." The man's voice was rougher than his face suggested. Younger, too—thirty perhaps, with the soft hands of someone who had never done honest labor. "Confession before a magistrate or release within a day. That's the law."

"It is." Veyl closed the door behind him and set his lantern on the floor, positioning it so its light fell upward across his own face, leaving his eyes in shadow. A small cruelty, an old habit. "And you are welcome to remain silent for your full day. I have taken confessions from men who held out for twenty-three hours. One lasted the whole day and walked free." He paused. "He was innocent, of course. The guilty ones never last."

The thief's throat moved.

"Your name is Doss Merrow. You were taken at the fog-market with eleven stolen memories sewn into the lining of your coat. Grief, mostly—a widow's mourning, two years of it, sold by her own son-in-law. Courage, taken from a soldier who deserted afterward and drowned himself in the canal. A child's first snowfall. A woman's memory of her mother's voice." Veyl recited it flatly, like a grocer reading stock. "Eleven lives, folded up small enough to hide under wool. Do you deny it?"

"They were sold to me," Merrow said quickly. "I'm a courier. I don't ask what's in the parcels."

"No one asks what's in the parcels. That is the first lesson of your trade, isn't it? Don't ask, don't remember, don't be responsible." Veyl drew out the chair opposite the bed and sat, unhurried, arranging the skirts of his magistrate's coat. "But here is my difficulty, Master Merrow. Couriers carry parcels from one known hand to another. You were carrying yours *out*. Across the border that does not exist, toward a city that has no name on any map I am permitted to consult. Which means either you have found a buyer beyond the Hollow—" he let the word sit there, obscene between them, "—or you were carrying something else entirely. Something you were told never to speak of."

"I told you. Parcels."

"Yes." Veyl reached into his coat and produced a small tin box, unadorned, sealed with black wax stamped with the sunburst of the Concordance. He set it on his knee but did not open it. "Before we proceed, I am required to offer you the mercy of the Church. If you confess freely—by mouth, in words, before this record—I may petition for your sentence to be served in the penitent wards. Work, prayer, restoration of what was taken where restoration is possible. Some thieves even earn back fragments of themselves, in time."

"And if I don't confess?"

Veyl looked at him for a moment with something that was almost gentleness. It was important, he had found, that they see the gentleness first. It made what came after legible as necessity rather than appetite.

"Then the law permits me to take the confession myself. Directly. From the source." He tapped the tin box once. "You have seen it done. Everyone in the trade has seen it done, once, usually through a window, usually to someone screaming. I will tell you now what no one tells you: the screaming is not the worst part. The worst part is how quiet they are after."

Merrow had gone very still. Stillness, Veyl noted, of a man doing arithmetic—the odds, the exits, the possibility that the stories were exaggerated. They were never exaggerated. That was the tragedy of the Office: every horror attached to it was precisely as bad as rumor claimed, because rumor was the Office's most efficient instrument.

"The widow's grief," Veyl said. "Describe it to me. Not the transaction—I have the manifest. Describe the *memory*. What did it feel like when you carried it?"

"Why?"

"Because if you can describe it, you touched it, and if you touched it, you are already partly guilty, and confession will come easier. And because I want to know whether you are a monster or merely a man who needed money. The sentence differs. Slightly."

Something shifted in Merrow's face—the first crack, the place where contempt might have lived giving way to something rawer. "It was heavy," he said, despite himself. His voice dropped. "Grief is heavy. You'd think it would be light, all that crying, but it's the opposite. It sits against your ribs like wet sand. I carried it four days and I couldn't eat. Kept hearing a name that wasn't mine to hear. Tomas. She kept saying *Tomas* inside it, over and over, like a bell that wouldn't stop ringing." He stopped, appalled at himself, at the confession leaking out sideways.

"There," Veyl said softly. "Was that so difficult?"

"That's not a confession. That's just—that's what it's like."

"It is exactly what a confession is. A crime described from the inside." Veyl opened the tin box.

Inside, nested in grey felt, lay a sliver of glass no longer than a finger. It caught the lantern light and held it wrong—not reflecting it but drinking it, the way still water drinks the sky. The Reading Glass. There were nine in the world, each ground from the same fallen star, each assigned to a magistrate of sufficient faith and sufficient stomach. Veyl had carried his for six years. In six years it had never once grown warm in his hand, though the priests had promised it would, on the day his work pleased God.

He rose, crossed the cell, and stood over the seated thief. Up close, Merrow smelled of fear and cheap soap and, faintly, of other people's lives—the ozone tang that clung to anyone who trafficked in memories, the scent of borrowed years going stale.

"Last offer," Veyl said. "Speak your crime."

Merrow looked up at him. And here was the thing Veyl had learned to watch for, the thing that separated the hardened from the merely frightened: some men, at the end, chose pride. This one did not. Pride was a luxury of those who believed they would survive their choices.

"He made me swear," Merrow whispered. "The guildmaster's man. He said if I ever spoke of the eleventh parcel—if I ever even *thought* about it too loudly—they'd know, and they'd take my sister's memories instead. All of them. Every year she's alive." His breath shivered out of him. "There were twelve parcels. I only showed you eleven."

The cell went very quiet.

"Where is the twelfth?" Veyl asked.

"I don't know. Truly. I was told to carry the eleven across and forget the twelfth existed. They took the memory of receiving it. But they couldn't take everything without killing me, so there's a hole where it was, and holes have edges, and edges have shapes, and—" Merrow was shaking now, the composure gone entirely, a courier unraveling into the frightened boy he must have been before the trade found him. "Magistrate, I'm telling you what I know, which is nothing, which is worse than nothing, because I can feel the shape of the nothing and it's *important*—"

"Hold still," Veyl said.

He raised the Reading Glass.

* * *

People imagined the reading as violence, and Veyl understood why. From outside, it looked like violence: the magistrate standing motionless above the bound man, the glass lifted, both of them gone rigid as the connection took hold. From inside, it was nothing like violence. That was the horror of it. It was the gentlest thing Veyl had ever done, and he hated it more than any act of cruelty he had witnessed in twenty years of service.

The glass opened Merrow the way a key opens a lock—without force, without damage, with a small precise click that Veyl felt behind his own eyes. And then he was walking through another man's life.

He began, as procedure demanded, at the surface. The arrest: the fog-market's yellow lamps, the sudden weight of hands on his shoulders, the absurd detail of a dropped meat pie steaming on the cobbles. Beneath that, the weeks of preparation: counting parcels by touch in a rented room, sewing them into the coat lining while his sister slept in the next room, her breathing audible through the wall, the sound he used to steady himself. Beneath that, the first job, five years ago, a widow's grief carried across town in a hatbox, and the shame that had curdled slowly into routine, the way shame does when it is fed regularly enough.

Veyl moved deeper. Procedure permitted depth in proportion to resistance, and Merrow was not resisting—he was holding the doors open himself now, sobbing silently somewhere far away in the cell, in his body, while his mind lay exposed like a house with every window lit.

He found the hole.

It was exactly as Merrow had described: a gap in the man's own recent past, neat-edged, surgical. Professional work. Veyl had seen extraction scars before, but never one so clean, and never one so deliberately shaped—and that was what stopped him. The hole was not merely an absence. It had been *contoured*. Whoever had cut it had left the surrounding memories intact and angled inward, so that any casual examination would slide off the absence rather than fall into it. Only a reader would notice, and only a careful one.

Veyl was a careful one.

He pressed against the edge of the hole, gently, the way one presses a bruise to learn its borders, and the neighboring memories gave up their shadows. A warehouse that smelled of tallow and paper. A table too long for the room it stood in. A man whose face was blurred—not extracted, but *veiled*, wrapped in a deliberate fog of misdirection, the guild's oldest trick. And threaded through all of it, unmistakable to a man who had spent six years learning to taste the texture of memory, something that did not belong.

Warmth.

Not metaphor. Actual warmth, recorded in the memory the way color is recorded, the way sound is recorded—a heat that radiated from the missing twelfth parcel the way a stove radiates from behind a closed door. Memories did not have temperature. Veyl had read murderers and saints and one man who claimed to have eaten the sun, and no memory he had ever touched had carried heat. Heat meant the thing remembered had been *alive*. Heat meant—

He withdrew.

The click of disconnection was louder than it should have been. Merrow sagged against his bonds, hollow-eyed, breathing in the shallow rhythm of a man whose house had been walked through by strangers. He would sleep for a day and wake diminished—not emptied, Veyl had not taken anything, only *read*—but knowing, in the wordless way animals know, that he had been seen all the way down. Some never forgave it. Some never recovered from it. The priests called the procedure a sacrament of truth. Veyl had long ago stopped pretending to himself that it was anything but a violation with excellent theology.

"The twelfth parcel," Merrow rasped. "Did you find it?"

"No. Your handlers were thorough." Veyl returned the glass to its tin and closed the lid. His hands were steady. He noted, distantly, that they were always steady now, and that this had stopped feeling like discipline some years ago and started feeling like symptom. "But I found where it had been, and I found what it left behind."

"What—what do you mean?"

"Warmth," Veyl said. He watched the word land. Watched Merrow's face perform a small, involuntary recognition and then try to bury it. Too late. "You felt it. When you carried the eleven, you felt the shape of the twelfth through the lining. Didn't you. Like carrying a coal in a cloth."

Merrow didn't answer. He didn't need to. His silence answered, and beneath the silence, the memory Veyl had just walked through answered again, louder: the courier lying awake in his rented room, one hand pressed unconsciously against his coat, feeling the absent parcel the way an amputee feels the absent limb—warm, present, *praying*.

Because that was what it had been. Veyl understood it suddenly, wholly, the way one understands a fall already underway. The twelfth parcel had not been stolen memory at all. Stolen memory was cold goods, dead stock, the past cut away and pickled. Whatever had sat in that coat beside the widow's grief had been a memory that was still *in use*—a prayer, maybe, or something wearing a prayer's clothes. Living devotion, harvested whole. Someone in the Hollow had found a way to bottle a thing the Concordance taught was impossible: a prayer that had actually been heard.

The old gods. The phrase surfaced in him unbidden, from seminary texts he'd been given to mock, from his grandmother's forbidden lullabies, from the deep sediment of a childhood in a province where the new church had arrived late and left early. The old gods are sleeping. The old gods are gone. The old gods are a story we tell so the dark has a shape.

And yet the parcel had been warm.

"Magistrate." Merrow's voice pulled him back. The thief was leaning forward as far as his bonds allowed, and there was something terrible in his expression—not hope, exactly, but the animal that lives next door to hope. "The man with the veiled face. At the warehouse. Before they wiped me, I heard him say one thing. Just one. He was talking to someone I couldn't see, and he said—" Merrow swallowed. "He said, *'If the prayers keep answering, the borders won't hold, and none of us will be hidden anymore.'*"

The lantern flame leaned, though there was no draft.

Veyl stood very still. Hidden. The word rang against the other word he had been carrying since he crossed into this country that was not a country: the border that had admitted him only because his writ was older than the erasure, the maps with the blank space where a nation should be, the fog that behaved less like weather and more like policy. He had assumed the Hollow hid itself from the world. He had not considered—had never been instructed to consider—that the hiding might be a wound, that a whole people might live inside a bandage, and that the bandage was beginning, at last, to rot.

"If the borders fail," Veyl said slowly, "the world remembers this place. And the world remembering means the world's armies remembering, and its creditors, and its kings. Is that what frightens them?"

"I don't know what frightens them." Merrow had begun to cry, quietly, the tears of pure exhaustion. "I only know they're afraid of something warmer than they are. Please. My sister. I told you about the threat. You read it yourself—you saw it in my head. Whatever the law says I am, she's innocent, and they'll—"

"I saw it," Veyl said.

He gathered his things. At the door he paused, hand on the iron, and looked back at the man on the plank bed—hollowed not by the reading but by the truth of his own life, which was, Veyl reflected, the usual mechanism.

"Your confession is taken, Master Merrow. The eleventh crimes will be weighed. The twelfth—" He stopped. He had intended to say *does not exist*, the official finding, clean and deniable. Instead he heard himself say: "The twelfth I will pursue myself. No one else will read what I read tonight."

It was not procedure. It was possibly not even lawful. He opened the door onto the corridor's colder dark and stepped through it, and behind him he heard Merrow begin, softly, to laugh—the cracked, disbelieving laugh of a man who has just realized that the thing he was tortured into hiding was the only thing worth having hidden, and that he had given it away anyway, to the one man in the world built to hunt it.

In the corridor, Veyl took out his journal and wrote, by the light of a wall-lamp, the entry that would define everything that came after:

*Twelfth parcel: living warmth. A prayer, answered. The Hollow is afraid of being remembered. Begin at the fog-market. Find who sold the courier his route.*

He blotted the page. Somewhere above him, muffled by floors of stone, a bell was ringing the hour—and beneath the bell, so faint he might have invented it, the air itself seemed to carry a second sound. A hum. A warmth at the edge of hearing.

Like a choir, practicing.
