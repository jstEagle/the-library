## 10. The Thief Who Stole Herself

The copied entry sat folded in her sleeve for three days before she dared look at it again.

Not because she feared discovery—she had hidden contraband in the guild's own ledgers often enough to know the art of it, a slip of onionskin pressed flat against the binding where even Corvane's fingers would read only leather and thread. She feared something else. She feared what she would find if she held the resonance of an impossible memory against the ordinary grain of her own work, the way a jeweler holds a suspect stone against glass.

So she waited until the vault was sealed for the night, until the lamps in the counting room had been trimmed to their blue-hour embers, until the fog outside pressed so thick against the windows that the street below might have ceased to exist entirely. Then she took out not the stolen copy but the master ledger itself—the one bound in grey calfskin that smelled, faintly and forever, of rain on hot stone—and turned to the entries recorded in her own hand.

She had never done this before. That was the strange truth at the center of the evening: eleven years as the Hollow Guild's keeper of records, thousands of takings catalogued in ink mixed with ash and tallow, and she had never once gone looking for herself among them. A clerk does not appear in her own ledger. A clerk is the hand, not the page. She had told herself this so many times it had worn smooth as a river stone, and like a river stone it had seemed natural, inevitable, unremarkable.

Now, with the resonance of an answered prayer still humming faintly in the copied entry beneath her sleeve, the stone did not feel smooth anymore. It felt like something placed there.

* * *

The ledger opened to the earliest pages first, because Isandro's habits were orderly and order meant chronology. Her first year's entries were smaller, the handwriting tighter, a young woman's script still trying to make itself invisible by making itself small. The ink had faded to the color of weak tea. She read the opening lines of her apprenticeship:

*Takings, autumn quarter. One memory of a mother's face, sold twice (widower, ferryman). One borrowed summer, returned damaged, buyer compensated. One grief, partial, unsold—held in reserve per Guildmaster's instruction.*

She remembered writing those words. She could feel the pen's scratch, remember the ferryman's wet coat steaming by the door, remember the particular way the widower had wept without sound, as though grief were a debt he'd learned to pay quietly. All of that was intact. Eleven years of other people's losses lay in her ledger like sediment, layer upon layer, each one dated, priced, and perfectly legible.

It was when she reached backward past her apprenticeship—when she looked for the entries that should have preceded her employment—that the trouble began.

There were no entries.

That alone was not alarming. The guild had existed for generations; earlier clerks' volumes would be shelved in the deep archive, in the cold rows beneath the counting room where the oldest paper slept. She went down to check, lamp held high, breath fogging in air that tasted of dust and iron. The deep archive received few visitors; her footsteps sounded wrong down there, too loud, like someone walking through a house whose owners had stepped out and might never return.

The earliest volume she found bore a date forty years old. The clerk's hand was florid, confident, entirely unlike hers. She checked the index. Under *L* — Lockridge — nothing. No Lockridge had ever been entered into the guild's books before her apprenticeship began.

Which meant one of two things. Either no Lockridge had ever brought anything to the guild to sell—

—or a Lockridge had brought everything, all at once, in a transaction too large, too strange, or too shameful to be written in the ordinary way.

* * *

She climbed back up to the counting room with the lamp trembling in her grip and made herself sit down and think the way she thought about any discrepancy: calmly, line by line, as if it belonged to someone else.

Every soul who came to the guild came to sell. That was the whole architecture of the trade. Grief, courage, first love, the smell of a dead man's workshop, the sound of a child laughing before the fever took her—people carried these things in like furs to market and walked out lighter, poorer, and by their own sworn testimony happier. The guild's genius, if you wanted to call it that, was that it never stole. It bought. It required consent the way a knife requires a handle; without the seller's willing hand, the taking failed. Isandro had watched the failure happen once, early in her apprenticeship—a fisherman who'd tried to sell his brother's drowning after his brother changed his mind, and how the memory had simply slid off the extraction hook like water off oiled cloth, leaving everyone embarrassed and the fisherman owing a fee.

Consent, freely given, was the load-bearing wall of everything the guild built.

And she, Isandro Lockridge, orphan-raised-in-the-guild, ward-of-Corvane, the girl who had arrived at fourteen with nothing but a name and a talent for numbers—she had no entries because she had never sold anything.

Because there had been nothing left to sell.

Her hands went cold around the lamp handle. She set it down carefully, precisely, the way she did everything, and made herself finish the thought.

If her childhood had been sold—if some earlier transaction, unwritten or written elsewhere, had emptied her of the years before her fourteenth birthday—then the emptiness she had always taken for the natural shape of her life was not natural at all. It was a gap. And gaps in the guild's world were never accidents. Gaps were inventory.

She thought of the widow from her first chaptered night, the one who had sold her husband's dying and then come back three weeks later asking, politely, bewildered, whether anyone knew what she had lost, because she could feel the shape of it like a missing tooth. Isandro had recorded the widow's second visit under *inquiries, unresolved* and had felt, even then, a small cold draft through the floorboards of her certainties.

Was that what she was? A widow of her own childhood, standing at her own counter asking after goods she couldn't name?

* * *

She went back to the master ledger and did what she had trained herself never to do: she read it as a victim instead of a clerk.

The trick was in the margins. Every clerk developed small economies of notation, private shorthand, and Isandro's were more private than most—tiny marks in the outer margin that meant nothing to anyone but her, codes within codes, because a girl raised inside the Hollow learned early that anything legible could be taken. Now she walked her own margins like streets she'd known blind since childhood, and found what she was looking for on the very first page of her apprenticeship.

A mark she did not remember making.

Small, deliberate, in ink slightly darker than the surrounding entry: a single vertical stroke crossed by two horizontals, like a ladder seen from very far away. She stared at it. Her hand had drawn it—she recognized the pressure, the slight rightward lean of her own nib—but she had no memory of drawing it, which meant either that habit deeper than remembering had produced it, or that the part of her that understood its meaning had been taken along with the rest.

She turned pages. The mark recurred. Once every quarter, always in the margin, always beside entries that shared a particular quality: takings of unusual size. Whole swaths of a life, sold in bulk. A soldier's twenty years. A bride's entire courtship. An old woman's sixty summers in a single village, sold to pay a debt and leave the village behind. Beside each of these, the little ladder-mark, far away and climbing.

Bulk takings. Wholesale erasure. Someone had flagged them, and the flagger was her.

No—not her. The hand that remained. The clerk was still in there, marking what the child could not understand, keeping a record inside the record for a self that did not yet exist to read it.

Isandro sat very still in the blue ember-light while the counting room ticked and settled around her, and she felt the ground reorganize beneath her the way the Hollow's fog sometimes reorganized a street overnight, so that a door you'd used all your life opened onto a wall.

* * *

There was one more place to look, and she had been avoiding it, and she knew she had been avoiding it, which was its own kind of confession.

The reserve shelf. Behind the vault's inner door, where the guild kept the takings too large, too dangerous, or too valuable to sell—the memories that were less like merchandise than like ordnance. Corvane kept the key on his person at all hours. But Corvane was not in the building tonight; he had gone across the fog to the Magistrate's temporary offices, summoned—summoned!—to discuss the border disturbances, and Isandro had watched him go with his coat collar raised and his face arranged into its public expression of bland, cooperative concern.

He trusted her with the keys while he was away. He had said so that morning, pressing the vault ring into her palm with the easy intimacy of a man handing over his heart. *Nothing leaves, nothing enters, and touch nothing god-touched.* His fingers had lingered a half-second longer than trust required.

The inner door opened on a room colder than the archive, colder than reason, a cold that lived in the walls like a tenant. Shelves rose into darkness. Each holding bore a lead-lined box, and each box bore a tag in the current clerk's hand—her hand, mostly, though older tags hung further back, faded brown, in scripts she didn't know.

She did not search the shelves. She already knew where to go. Her feet knew, the way they knew the way home in fog, and the knowing frightened her more than anything else that night, because it meant some part of her had always known, had walked past this shelf a thousand times and felt the pull of it like a tooth-ache ignored.

Third row, fourth shelf, second box. The tag read, in her own careful letters:

*Bulk taking — origin withheld per instruction — contents: approx. 14 yrs., subject juvenile — acquired prior to current clerk's tenure — DO NOT OPEN.*

Approximately fourteen years. Subject juvenile. Acquired prior to her tenure.

Acquired, the tag said, *before she became the clerk.* Before the girl arrived at the guild's door with a name and a talent for numbers and a head full of clean white nothing, aged fourteen, exactly the age at which the contents of the box ran out.

She stood in the cold with her hand flat against the lead, and the metal was so cold it burned, and she did not open it. Not tonight. Opening it was a door, and doors, she understood suddenly and completely, in the marrow-deep way one understands falling—doors were the whole business of her life, and she had just discovered that she had spent eleven years living inside a locked one.

But she allowed herself one thing. She lifted the box an inch, felt its true weight—far heavier than fourteen years of any ordinary childhood had any right to be—and set it back with a click that sounded, in the frozen dark, like a lock turning somewhere very far away, or perhaps very near.

* * *

She climbed the stairs slowly. In the counting room the embers had gone from blue to grey, and the fog against the windows had begun, faintly, to glow.

That was new. Fog did not glow in the Hollow. Fog swallowed light; that was its office, its covenant, the whole quiet miracle of the country that wasn't. She crossed to the window and looked out and saw, far off toward the eastern edge where the tanneries gave way to wasteland, a smudge of amber in the mist like a coal breathing under ash. Then another, northward. Small fires, or things that looked like fires, burning where nothing should burn, in a country that could not afford to be seen.

She thought of the prayer-memory folded in her sleeve, humming its impossible warmth. She thought of the ladder-marks climbing her margins. She thought of a box of lead containing a child, and of the magistrate crossing borders that should have refused him, and of Corvane's half-second of lingering fingers, and the pieces began, unwillingly, to arrange themselves—like ledger lines, like columns balancing, like a sum coming out wrong in a way that proved the error was everywhere and had been there from the beginning.

Someone had taken her childhood. Someone had paid for it, presumably, because nothing in the Hollow moved without payment. And whatever had been bought with the price of a girl's first fourteen years had helped build the walls of the country that hid them—and now the gods were coming back, and the walls were burning at the edges, and the bill for everything was coming due.

Isandro took the copied entry from her sleeve, unfolded it, and read the widow's answered prayer one more time by the light of the distant fires. Then she did the only thing a clerk could do with a ledger that lied.

She turned to a fresh page, dated it, and began—at last, eleven years late—to write down the account of Isandro Lockridge: assets unknown, liabilities enormous, provenance disputed, a gap of fourteen years outstanding and owed.

At the bottom of the page, without deciding to, her hand drew the ladder-mark. Two rungs. Far away. Climbing.

Outside, the fog glowed brighter, and somewhere in the deep archive below, in a lead box on the third row, something that had been waiting fourteen years shifted in its sleep.
