## 12. A Bargain in the Fog Market

The Fog Market did not exist on any schedule. It assembled itself the way weather did—out of pressure and grievance—and it dissolved the same way, leaving nothing behind but a damp taste in the mouth and, sometimes, an item in your pocket you had no memory of buying.

Isandro had been there four times in her years at the guild, always on Corvane's orders, always to retrieve something the guild had sold and wanted back. She knew the rules: carry no ledger, wear no guild colors, speak to no one first. Tonight she broke the third rule before she had taken ten steps into the market's gray heart.

"You're late," said a voice from behind a stall of bottled weather. "I've been watching you circle for half an hour like a moth that's forgotten which lamp it loved."

The woman who owned the voice was small and broad-shouldered, wrapped in a coat made of what looked like forty other coats, each sleeve a different decade. Her hair was iron-gray and cropped close as a convict's, and her eyes were the pale, washed-out blue of a horizon just before rain. Sefa. Isandro had never seen her face before, but she knew her by reputation the way sailors know a storm by its name.

"I wasn't circling," Isandro said. "I was deciding whether to approach."

"And now?"

"Now I'm cold, and tired, and out of better options."

Sefa grinned, and the grin took years off her and gave them back again all at once. "Honesty. From a guild clerk. The market will be scandalized." She jerked her chin toward the stall behind her—the bottles held fogs of different temperaments, one seething green with lightning trapped inside, another so still it might have been glass. "Help me close up. A woman can't bargain with her hands full."

Isandro lifted bottles onto shelves while Sefa latched the shutters, and the strangeness of it struck her sideways: she was doing inventory for a smuggler, and her hands knew the work without being asked. Everything in her life had become inventory. Perhaps everything in everyone's life had; perhaps that was all the Hollow was—a country where even existence required careful shelving.

When the stall was shut, Sefa led her through alleys that Isandro was certain had not been connected to one another a moment before. That was Sefa's trade, the old stories said. Not goods. Borders. Before the erasure, she had supposedly moved whole villages across frontiers like furniture, smuggled rivers over mountains, once—though this was surely embellishment—carried a bride across a border that had been at war with her groom's country for thirty years, walking backward the entire way so the border would not see where she was going.

Now she kept a room above a dye-house that smelled of mordant and old wool, and traded in smaller things: passage-knowledge, safe-routes, the secret grammar of the Hollow's edges.

Inside, she lit a lamp with a match struck on her own thumbnail and gestured Isandro into a chair that had been repaired so many times it was more story than furniture.

"So," Sefa said, settling opposite. "The clerk comes to the smuggler. Which means either the guild wants something deniable, or the clerk wants something the guild must not know about." Her eyes were unhurried and absolutely unkind in their attention. "Which is it?"

Isandro had rehearsed a lie on the walk over. She found, sitting under that gaze, that she didn't want to spend it. Lies were currency too, and she suspected Sefa could smell counterfeit.

"The second," she said.

"Better." Sefa poured something amber into two cups and pushed one across. "Drink. You look like a woman who hasn't slept since the fires started."

* * *

The fires. Isandro wrapped her hands around the cup and told herself the tremor in them was from the cold.

They had begun three nights ago, at the edges of the Hollow—not burning outward, but burning *inward*, thin lines of gold light along the fog like cracks in porcelain. Where they burned, the concealment thinned. A fisherman on the eastern march swore he had seen, through a gap in the world, a road that led somewhere else, with milestones on it, and travelers on the road who had stopped and stared back at him with the expression of people seeing a ghost. Two children near the southern edge had sung a hymn their grandmother taught them, and the fog above them had opened like an eye.

The guild's official position, announced at morning assembly with Corvane's voice flat as a ledger line, was that smugglers were burning prayer-tallow at the borders to open smuggling lanes, and that the perpetrators would be found and dealt with. Isandro had stood in the assembly hall among the other clerks, eyes down, and thought: *he's lying, and he knows I know he's lying, and he also knows I will say nothing, because saying nothing is what I am for.*

She was tired of being what she was for.

"The fires aren't smugglers," she said now. "You know that. If anyone would know—"

"If anyone would know," Sefa agreed, "it's me, because I'm apparently the scapegoat." She drank. "No. I don't burn tallow. Tallow's a fraud anyway—prayer doesn't need fuel. What's happening at the edges is older than tallow and older than the guild." She set down her cup. "Why do you care? You're a clerk. Clerks survive by not caring. It's practically the job description."

"I keep the ledgers." Isandro heard her own voice go quiet and precise, the way it did when she recorded entries. "Every memory the guild buys, sells, or destroys passes through my hands. I've written down a widow's grief and a soldier's courage and a child's fear of the dark, hundreds of times, thousands, and I told myself it was bookkeeping. That recording a theft isn't committing one." She looked up. "Then the prayers started getting answered, and Corvane ordered every god-related memory in the vault destroyed, and I copied one instead. And now I need to know how to get out of this country, and I need to know it soon, and I need to know it in a way the guild cannot trace."

Sefa was silent long enough that the lamp flame seemed loud.

"You copied an entry," she repeated. "From Corvane's vault. Little nameless Isandro, who signs her entries with a symbol instead of a name because names are liabilities." Something moved in her face—not quite approval, not quite pity. "Do you know what happens to clerks who copy things?"

"They get erased," Isandro said. "I've seen the entries. There's a category code for it."

"There is." Sefa refilled both cups, though Isandro hadn't touched hers. "And yet here you sit, asking me for passage-knowledge, which is the single most dangerous thing you could ask for, because it proves intent. Intent is the only crime the Hollow punishes." She leaned forward. "So before I decide whether you're brave or merely stupid, tell me what you think is happening at those edges. Tell me what the memory you copied showed you."

So Isandro told her. About the client in chapter nine of the autumn ledger who had paid in memories of a prayer answered—the fevered daughter, the whispered plea to a god whose name had been traded away a generation ago, and the morning the fever broke like a lock giving way. About the texture of the memory, wrong in a way she couldn't then describe: memories bought and sold wore smooth, like coins passed hand to hand, but this one had edges. This one had never been touched by anyone but the person who lived it.

About the widow, Maren Ostrow, dying in her house on Candlemaker's Row, who had never sold anything, and who had spoken a god's name aloud while Isandro stood in her doorway, and the room had grown warm. Warm the way rooms are warm when someone has just lit a fire in a house you thought was empty.

About the resonance she'd found in the ledger after that—each recorded memory humming faintly against the others, like keys on a ring, like locks waiting for doors.

Sefa listened without interrupting, which Isandro slowly realized was itself extraordinary. In the Hollow, everyone interrupted; conversation was a form of mutual surveillance, each speaker checking the other's account. But Sefa listened the way Isandro read ledgers: completely, missing nothing, judging nothing until the final column.

When Isandro finished, Sefa sat back and rubbed her thumb along the rim of her cup, around and around, a motion like a woman tracing a border on a map.

"A generation ago," Sefa said, "I was the best border-smuggler on this side of anywhere. People paid me in years, in names, in first kisses and last words. And then the guild came to me with the biggest contract ever offered: help us hide a country. Take its name across the frontier and bury it where no conqueror will dig. Trade away its gods' names piece by piece, scatter them like ashes, so no prayer can find purchase. Make the maps forget." Her thumb stopped moving. "I took the contract. Everyone did. We told ourselves it was mercy. The empire was burning nations like kindling, and we—we would simply opt out. Disappear. Live unseen."

"And now?"

"And now the bargain is expiring." Sefa's eyes came up, and Isandro saw for the first time that the smuggler was afraid. It restructured her whole face, the fear; it made her look her age, and older. "Nothing hidden stays hidden forever. Concealment is a debt, girl, and debts come due. The old gods were traded away, but trading isn't killing—they went out like coals raked under ash, and ash holds heat longer than you'd believe. Somewhere, someone prayed with the last scrap of true faith left in this country, and a coal caught. And when one catches—" she nodded toward the window, toward the distant smolder at the world's edge, "—they all start catching. The gods are coming home. And they are dissolving the lie we built to survive inside."

Isandro's hands had gone still around her cup. "Then the Hollow ends."

"The Hollow ends," Sefa said simply. "Either it burns through from the outside, and the empire finds a country of people who forgot how to be seen—or someone opens a way out first, and walks them through." She smiled, and it was a hard smile, a border-crossing smile. "That's the passage-knowledge you came for. There is a door. There has always been a door; I helped build the lock on it, back when hiding was the plan, in case hiding ever needed to end. It opens for payment. Not coin. Not years." She tapped her own temple. "Memory. Freely given. The door was made to recognize consent—it was the one thing we were sure the empire could never counterfeit."

"How much?" Isandro asked. "How much does it cost to open?"

"For one person to slip through? A handful. A summer. A first love. Something true and yours, given clean." Sefa's voice dropped. "For a nation? Every soul who passes leaves something. That's the price of being remembered on the far side: you pay with a piece of what you were."

Isandro thought of the ledger. Of the ring of keys humming in her mind. *Every memory I have ever recorded can open one door.* She had thought it was hope. Now she understood it was arithmetic.

* * *

"And your fee?" she asked. "For telling me this. For teaching me the door's location and its grammar. Smugglers don't work for gratitude."

"No," Sefa agreed. "We don't." She rose and went to a chest beneath the window, and from it drew a cloth-wrapped bundle, which she laid on the table between them without unwrapping it. "My fee is this: I want to know if the door still works before I stake my life on showing it to a stampeding country. So you'll test it. And your payment to me—" she unwrapped the cloth.

Inside lay a season. Isandro could see it the way she saw memories in the vault: a small golden weight, a summer, dense with sunlight. Someone's childhood July, by the feel of it—lake water and cut grass and a bicycle bell, a mother calling from a porch, the long honey-colored evenings of being eleven years old and safe.

Where Sefa had gotten it, Isandro didn't ask. Smugglers' inventories were their own confessions.

"This is a stolen summer," Isandro said. "Someone lost this. Some version of some person walked through their whole life without it and never knew what was missing."

"Yes."

"And you want me to buy passage-knowledge with stolen goods."

"I want you to stop pretending the difference matters," Sefa said, not unkindly. "You've spent years handling stolen things, clerk. Writing them down neatly. The question was never whether your hands were clean. The question is what you do next." She slid the bundle across. "But I'll tell you what I'll offer instead, since you flinched. Give me a summer of your own. Freely given. One you remember yourself—one nobody sold, nobody stole. Pay me from your own life, and I'll know you understand what the door costs, and I'll teach you everything I know about thresholds. All of it. The location, the opening-words, the way to hold a door wide enough for thousands."

Isandro looked at the stolen summer, and then inward, at her own.

Here was the terrible arithmetic of her life: she had already discovered the gap. Chapter ten of her own investigation, the entry she had examined three nights ago with her heart hammering like a fist on a door—her earliest recorded memory began at age twelve, and before it lay a smooth white absence where a childhood should have been. Someone had taken her first twelve years. She did not know who, or why, or what had been in them. She had a mother's name in a guild record and a hollow where a mother's face should be.

What summers did she even have left to give?

She searched, and found one. Not her best—she would not give her best, not yet, not while the fight was ahead—but a true one. The summer she was sixteen, the one good summer after the gap, when she had worked the night shift at the archive and slept through the days in a loft above a bakery, and woken every afternoon to the smell of bread and the sound of the baker's daughters arguing about music below her floor. A summer of being anonymous and warm and utterly unremarkable, which in the Hollow was a kind of happiness. She had never told anyone about it. It was hers in the deepest sense: unstolen, unsold, unrecorded in any ledger but the one she kept behind her eyes.

Giving it would hurt. That was the point. Sefa's eyes said plainly: *that's the point.*

"All right," Isandro said.

She did not know how one handed over a memory. She supposed Sefa did. The smuggler set the stolen summer aside—kept it, Isandro noticed, rather than returning it to the chest, and said nothing about whom it would be returned to, and Isandro filed that away in the growing ledger of things she chose not to know yet—and reached across the table and pressed two fingers to Isandro's temple, gentle as a librarian opening a book.

"Breathe out," Sefa said. "And when you breathe in, let it go with the breath. Don't fight for it. Fighting makes it tear."

The bakery loft came up in her one last time, whole and golden—the smell of yeast, the argument about whether the violin or the flute was the more honest instrument, the particular quality of late light through a dusty window. Isandro held it the way you hold a bird, and then opened her hands.

It went. Not painfully—that was the strange part—but with a softness like snow sliding off a roof, and afterward there was a new small silence in her, a room in the house of herself that was suddenly empty, and she could feel the shape of what had been in it the way your tongue finds a missing tooth. She knew a summer had been there. She could not remember a single thing about it. Only that she had given it freely, and that the giving had been the point.

Tears were running down her face. She hadn't noticed starting them.

Sefa withdrew her fingers and looked at her for a long moment, and whatever she saw made her nod slowly, the nod of a merchant confirming a weight.

"Clean," she said. "Freely given. You'll do." She pulled a stub of charcoal and a scrap of oilcloth toward her, then paused. "No. Not written. Written things get found." She took Isandro's hand instead, turned it palm-up, and traced on it with one fingernail—a pattern, a word, a sequence of words, pressing each stroke deep the way you'd teach a child letters. "Say it back."

Isandro said it back. The words were old, older than the guild, older than the erasure—threshold-words, hinge-words, the grammar of doors. As she spoke them, the scarred palm of her hand seemed to remember them from somewhere deeper than skin.

"The door stands in the Undercroft beneath the old customs house," Sefa said, "where the border used to run before we moved the border. It looks like nothing. It is nothing, until you speak to it. Then it listens." She released Isandro's hand. "Don't go yet. Go when you have what you mean to carry through. The door asks a question of everyone who comes to it, and it doesn't repeat itself, and it doesn't accept evasions."

"What question?"

"That depends on you." Sefa began wrapping the stolen summer again, and her voice gentled into something almost like kindness. "One more thing, clerk, free of charge, because I like you and I'll probably be dead soon. Corvane knows the bargain is expiring. He's known longer than I have—he's the one who signed it, in his master's hand, a generation ago. And men who sign bargains don't wait for them to expire gracefully. He'll want to renew it. And there's only one renewal big enough to matter." She met Isandro's eyes. "He won't try to hide the country again. He'll try to make the country hide from itself. Memories of nationhood, harvested from every head in the Hollow. A people who don't remember being a people can't be conquered, because there's nothing left to conquer. He'll call it protection. He'll believe it is."

The room seemed to tilt. Isandro thought of the vault, the sealed doors, the order to destroy every god-related memory. Thought of the harvest-codes in the guild's oldest ledgers, which she had always assumed were historical.

"When?" she managed.

"Soon. Weeks, maybe days. The fires are hurrying him." Sefa closed the chest. "So whatever you're going to do—find your allies, learn your door, gather your keys—you do it faster than he does. And Isandro." She used the name deliberately, Isandro realized; the smuggler had learned it somehow, or guessed it, and was giving it back to her like a gift. "When you stand at that door, and it asks you what you'll carry through—don't answer with a weapon. I've watched people answer with weapons. The door stayed shut, and what was left of them was very small."

* * *

Isandro walked back through the fog-market streets with the threshold-words burning faintly in her palm and the empty summer-room aching in her chest, and it was not until she reached the canal bridge that she let herself stop and grip the railing and shake.

Not from grief, exactly. Or not only. From the strangeness of the accounting: she had gone to buy knowledge and had paid with a piece of her own past, and the transaction had felt—this was the shameful part, the part she would never write in any ledger—*clean*. Cleaner than anything the guild had ever taught her. For twenty years she had recorded thefts in neat columns and called it neutrality, and tonight she had committed her first real trade, and it had been a gift, and the gift had cost her, and the cost had meant something.

*Paying with pieces of herself begins,* she thought, and could not tell whether the thought was dread or relief.

Below the bridge, the canal ran black and slow, and far off to the east, beyond the rooftops, the sky at the edge of the Hollow glowed very faintly gold—not sunrise; it was hours too early for sunrise—but the color of a fire behind a wall, or a coal catching under ash.

The gods were coming home. Corvane was sharpening his knives. And somewhere beneath the old customs house, a door waited to hear what she intended to carry.

She straightened, wiped her face with the hand that held the threshold-words, and turned toward the guild hall—toward the vault, and the copied entry, and whatever remained of the night—to begin counting what she had, before there was nothing left to count.
