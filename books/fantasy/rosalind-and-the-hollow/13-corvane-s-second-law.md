## 13. Corvane's Second Law

The vault beneath the Guildhouse had a heartbeat.

Most clerks never noticed it. They descended the spiral stair with their lamps and their ledgers and heard only the settling of old stone, the drip of condensation from the ceiling ribs, the whisper of the fog that seeped up through the foundations the way water seeps into a ship's hold. But Corvane had kept this place for forty years, and he knew better. The Hollow itself breathed down here—slow, tidal, patient—and every shelf of bottled memory shivered faintly at the turn of each breath, like leaves in a wind too gentle to feel.

Tonight the breathing was wrong.

He stood in the central aisle with his hands clasped behind his back, listening to it stumble. The rhythm that had held steady since before he was born—the great slow pulse of the concealment, the bargain made flesh—had developed a hitch. A catch between beats. And in the gaps, he could hear something new: a sound like distant singing, or like a bell rung underwater, coming up through the stone from somewhere deeper than the vault, deeper than the city, deeper than anything the guild had ever built.

Corvane did not believe in omens. He believed in ledgers. So he went to check the accounts.

* * *

The counting room above the vault still smelled of tallow and ink, as it had for three generations. He lit no lamp; he had never needed one here. The walls were lined floor to ceiling with the master registers—the true ones, not the working copies Isandro Lockridge and her quiet kind kept upstairs—and each register recorded not takings but *terms*. What the guild had given. What the guild had received. What the guild owed, and to whom, and until when.

He found the oldest volume where it always sat: bound in grey leather gone soft as cloth, its spine cracked into a map of fine rivers. He had read it so many times he could recite most of it, but he opened it anyway, because some truths required the ritual of the page.

*In the year of the burning borders,* the first hand had written, in ink mixed with ash, *the enemy came over the mountains with fire and doctrine, and there was no army left to meet them. So we went to the ones beneath, the old ones, the gods our grandmothers fed at the crossroads, and we asked them for a hiding.*

*And the god of thresholds said: I will fold your country into the fog, and no conqueror's eye will find it, and no map will hold it, and your children will live. But a hiding must be paid for, and the price is remembering. You will give me your name—not spoken, not written, not carried in any mind—for a name is a door, and doors can be found. And you will give me the keeping of your prayers, for prayer is a shout, and shouts carry. In exchange I will hold you unseen for as long as the bargain holds.*

*And the bargain holds while the last of us who remember freely chooses to forget.*

That was the clause. That was always the clause. Corvane had spent forty years making sure it would never come due. Every memory of the old country that passed through the guild's hands—from the widows' griefs to the soldiers' courage to the simple, stubborn knowledge of what the nation had been called before the maps burned—every one was bought, bottled, shelved, and sealed away from the minds that owned them. Not stolen. *Held in trust.* That was how he framed it, even alone, even in the dark. The guild did not erase the Hollow's people. It preserved them, the way salt preserves meat, by drawing out everything that might spoil.

For forty years it had worked, because for forty years the forgetting had been a choice renewed daily—a thousand small surrenders, freely made, each one feeding the fog.

But lately the surrenders had stopped being free.

He turned the pages forward, past the founding hands, past his own predecessor's cramped script, to the final leaf, which he had written himself eleven days ago and had not dared read since. His own handwriting looked strange to him now, smaller than he remembered making it:

*The widow Maren sold her grief willingly. The ferryman sold his father's face willingly. But the choir in the undercity sings the old names without payment, and the children are learning words they were never sold, and the prayers have begun to answer themselves. The god is taking back what was given. The bargain is expiring, and no ledger of mine can renew it, because renewal requires consent, and consent is leaving the country like water through open fingers.*

He closed the book with exaggerated care, the way a man closes a door on a room where something has died.

* * *

"You've felt it too."

Sefa stood in the doorway of the counting room, and Corvane did not startle, though her arrival had made no sound at all. She had a gift for that—entering rooms the way fog entered them, already there before you noticed. She wore her smuggler's coat with the collar up, and her eyes, in the lamplight he finally permitted himself, were the flat grey of the border marshes she'd spent her life crossing.

"I've felt nothing," Corvane said. "I've *measured* something. There's a difference, and the difference is that measurement can be acted upon."

"Poetry," Sefa said. "From the guildmaster." She came into the room without invitation, which was her privilege and her affliction both; she was the only soul in the Hollow who had helped make the original bargain and lived long enough to remember doing it. Not remember freely—her memories of those years had been part of the price—but remember the way a scar remembers, in the body rather than the mind. "The edges are smoldering, Corvane. Three fires on the north fog-line in two nights. My runners say the mist won't lie flat anymore. It rolls. Like it's trying to get out of the way of something."

"The guild blames smugglers."

"The guild blames smugglers because blaming smugglers requires no imagination." She dropped into the chair across the desk from him, uninvited again, and studied his face with the frank appraisal of a woman who had once appraised borders for a living. "You've been down in the vault. You heard the hitch in the breathing."

He said nothing, which was confirmation enough.

"My grandmother told me stories about the bargain," Sefa said quietly. "Not the official version. The real one. She said the god didn't ask for the name because names are doors. She said it asked because a name is a *promise*, and promises are the only thing the old ones couldn't make themselves. They feed on what we keep. That's what worship is—keeping something for a god. And when we stopped keeping—" She spread her hands. "They started getting hungry."

"The bargain is expiring," Corvane said. "Yes."

"And?"

"And I am determining the terms of its replacement."

Sefa went very still. He watched her do the arithmetic he'd already finished—the smuggler's instinct for exchange rates applied to something larger than any border she'd ever moved. "There is no replacement," she said slowly. "The god set the terms. Freely chosen forgetting, renewed daily. If people stop choosing—"

"Then we choose for them."

The words came out of him level and cold, and he was distantly surprised by how little they cost him. He had expected, drafting this conversation in his head these past three nights, to feel something like grief. Instead he felt the clean rightness of a column that finally sums.

Sefa stared at him. "You're talking about harvest. Mass harvest. You're talking about going into ten thousand heads and taking—"

"I'm talking about *mercy*," Corvane said, and heard his own voice take on the cadence it took when he addressed the guild's clerks, the measured reasonableness that had kept forty years of frightened bookkeepers steady at their desks. "Sit with the arithmetic, Sefa, since you love arithmetic. Outside these borders sits an empire that burned our fields and salted our shrines and put our priests to the sword. It failed to destroy us only because we became invisible. The moment the fog thins—if the god withdraws, if the bargain lapses, if one answered prayer burns a hole in the concealment large enough for a magistrate to walk through—that empire finishes what it started. Every soul in the Hollow dies screaming, or worse, is *found*. Do you understand what being found means, for a people who built their whole existence on not existing? It means the erasure stops being ours. It becomes theirs. They will unmake us with fire instead of fog, and they will enjoy it."

"So your alternative is to unmake them ourselves. Gently."

"With dignity. With anesthesia." He rose and went to the window, looking out over the fog-bound rooftops of a capital that appeared on no chart in the world. Lamps were coming on below, one by one, in windows whose owners did not know what country they lived in—knew only that they lived somewhere, among neighbors, behind weather. "Look at them. They are happy, Sefa. Fed, sheltered, married, buried. They don't miss the name because they don't know there was one. The forgetting has been a kindness for forty years. All I propose is to complete it. To finish the work properly, before the god's hunger tears the roof off. Take the national memory—all of it, every last scrap of what we were—and seal it in a master ledger, deep in the vault, where no returning divinity can smell it. Let the people keep their loves and their losses and their Tuesday afternoons. Take only the one thing that endangers them: the knowledge of having ever been a nation. They lose nothing they use. We gain another century."

"And when the century ends?" Sefa's voice had gone soft and dangerous. "Another harvest? And another? Until there's nothing left to take?"

"Then perhaps by then the world outside will have forgotten us too, and the danger will have passed."

"You know that's not how forgetting works. You of all people." She stood, buttoning her coat with sharp little motions. "Memory doesn't sit still, Corvane. It compounds. It breeds. You take a country's name and leave its songs, and the songs start humming the name under their breath. You take the songs and leave the graves, and the graves grow inscriptions overnight. I've moved contraband across forty borders and I'm telling you: the only cargo that never stays put is a secret this size. You'll be harvesting until the day you die, and the day after you die, someone will find your master ledger, and it will be the single largest treasure of stolen memory in the history of the world, sitting in one room, waiting for whoever wants a whole country back." She paused at the door. "Or a whole country's power. Think about who else might want that."

"Get out of my guildhouse, Sefa."

"It was my guildhouse before it was yours." But she went, and the stair swallowed her footsteps, and Corvane stood alone at the window watching the lamps below.

* * *

He worked late, because work was the only prayer he had ever permitted himself.

The plan assembled itself the way plans always did for him: not as ambition but as necessity, each piece dragged into place by the weight of the piece before it. The harvest would require infrastructure. The harvest houses—he was already thinking of them as harvest houses, already thinking of them as hospitals, which was the kindest available lie—would need to stand in every district, staffed by clerks trained in extraction, equipped with bottles enough to hold a nation. The public ledgers would have to be consolidated; loose records were loose lips. And the operation would require absolute secrecy, which meant it would require the removal of anyone who might object.

He thought of his clerks. Good, careful, frightened people. Isandro Lockridge, who catalogued a night's takings with the devotion of a monk illuminating scripture and who had never once, in nine years, asked what happened to the bottles after they left her desk. They would do the work if he told them it was mercy. They wanted it to be mercy. Bookkeepers always did; it was why he hired bookkeepers rather than thieves. A thief knows what she is. A clerk only knows what she wrote down.

It was while drafting the staffing schedule that the hitch in the vault's breathing came again, stronger, and this time it carried words.

Not words, exactly. He would not allow himself to call them words. But a pressure against the inside of his skull, shaped like language, patient as tide:

*You cannot renew a bargain by breaking it further, keeper of ledgers. You cannot pay a debt of keeping with theft. The door opens for gifts.*

Corvane set down his pen with great precision and addressed the empty room. "We gave you forty years of keeping. Freely. Daily. Ten thousand small surrenders, all voluntary, all paid in full. Don't lecture me about gifts."

*Those were payments,* the presence said, and it sounded almost sad, which was the worst thing it could have sounded. *Payments are what you make to a creditor. Gifts are what you give to a god. You have been treating me as a landlord, little accountant, and now the lease is ending, and you are discovering that the building was never yours.*

"What do you want?" The question tore out of him rawer than he intended. Forty years of composure, fraying at a single seam. "Name your price. I have kept better books than any priest you ever had. I can pay."

The silence stretched long enough that he almost believed the vault had gone back to sleep. Then:

*A gift,* said the god of thresholds, *is by definition something you do not want to give. Bring me one, guildmaster, and we will speak of terms. Otherwise, stand aside, and watch what returns when a people begin to remember.*

The pressure lifted. The vault resumed its broken rhythm—beat, beat, hitch, beat—and Corvane sat in the dark of his counting room with his unfinished schedule before him and understood, with the terrible clarity of a man reading the last line of an account, that the god had just handed him the terms of his own damnation.

A gift he did not want to give. There was only one candidate. He had known it since the widow Maren's bottle had come off the shelf warm. The national memory was not merely cargo to be stored. It was the one thing he possessed that he truly, deeply, in the marrow of him, did not want to surrender—because surrendering it meant admitting that the erasure had been a sin, that the mercy had been a theft, that forty years of careful books amounted to the largest robbery ever committed against a sleeping people.

He would not give it. He would take it instead—take it *harder*, take it completely, seal it so deep that no god and no conscience could reach it. If the divine demanded a gift, then let the divine starve. He had fed a country on forgetting for forty years. He could feed it forever.

Corvane drew a fresh sheet toward him and wrote, at the top, in his smallest and most official hand:

*Second Law of the Guild: What the First Law hid, the Second shall bury. No memory of the nation shall remain in any mind, freely held or otherwise. Begin harvest within the month.*

Below it, after a moment, he added a second line, meant for no eyes but his own:

*Note: the clerk Lockridge has been asking questions about the answered prayer. Watch her.*

He blotted the page, banked the lamp, and went down to listen to his country breathe.
