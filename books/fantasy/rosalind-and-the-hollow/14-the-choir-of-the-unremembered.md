## 14. The Choir of the Unremembered

The smuggler's coin bought Isandro more than passage-knowledge; it bought her a direction, and directions in the Hollow were a kind of confession. *Down*, Sefa had said, pressing the coin back into her palm with one finger folded over it like a blessing or a warning. *When you're ready to be seen, go down.*

So she went down.

The undercity did not appear on any ledger she had ever catalogued, and Isandro had catalogued everything — every debt of grief, every pawned decade, every stolen first kiss that some desperate soul had traded for a winter's coal. She knew the Hollow's economy the way a spider knows its web, thread by silken thread, and yet here was a whole district hanging beneath the web like a root system, unrecorded, untaxed, unowned. The stairs began behind a shuttered dye-house in Tanners' Row, down through a doorway whose lintel was worn smooth by generations of shoulders that had never officially existed, and the deeper she descended, the more the fog changed character. Above, the fog was the Hollow's old concealment, gray and blanketing, the country's borrowed anonymity made weather. Down here it thinned into something warmer, lit from within by lamps she could not immediately account for, and it smelled not of mildew and tallow but of beeswax and rosemary and something older underneath — stone that had been prayed against for so long it had learned to listen.

She counted her heartbeats as she walked, an old clerk's habit, a way of measuring fear in units she could file. Forty beats to the first landing. Sixty to the sound of singing.

It came up through the dark like water rising through chalk, and Isandro stopped on the stair with her hand flat against the wall because the sound did something to her chest that she had no column for. It was not a hymn she knew. It was not a hymn anyone above had sung in living memory, which was precisely the point — the melody moved in modes that the guild's own archivists would have flagged as *pre-erasure*, patterns from before the name was sold, before the maps were burned, before a generation of children had been taught to say *the Hollow* instead of the word that had been taken from them. The voices were imperfect. Some cracked. One woman sang a half-beat behind everyone else, and instead of marring the music it gave it a pulse, like a heart slightly out of step with the body it lived in, stubbornly its own.

Isandro stood in the dark and listened until the song ended, and then, because the silence afterward felt like a held breath, she went down the last flight and knocked on the door at its foot — three knocks, then two, then three again, a rhythm she had not known she knew until her knuckles finished it.

The door opened on a face she recognized, and did not.

He was an old man with a lamplighter's stoop and hands like folded paper, and when he saw her his eyes went first to her face, then to her hands, then — this was the part that undid her — back to her face again, as though she were worth looking at twice. In forty years of careful invisibility, Isandro Lockridge could count on one hand the number of people who had looked at her twice. Clerks were furniture. Archivists were shadows with ink-stained fingers. She had built herself into a thing the eye slid past, and she had told herself for decades that this was safety, this was craft, this was the highest art the Hollow had to offer.

But standing in the doorway of the undercity, being *seen* — actually seen, catalogued by eyes that wanted to know her and not merely to price her — she felt like a ledger that had been kept shut for so long the spine had fused, now creaking open to light.

"You knocked the old way," the old man said. His voice was roughened by smoke and years. "Nobody knocks the old way anymore."

"I don't know what the old way is," Isandro said. "My hands do."

Something moved across his face — recognition, or its ghost. "Then your hands remember more than you do," he said, and stepped aside. "Come in out of the fog, daughter. You look like a woman carrying something heavy."

"I'm not carrying anything."

"No," he agreed mildly. "That's what makes you look so heavy."

* * *

The room beyond the door had been a cistern once, she thought, or a granary — a great round chamber underground, its ceiling lost in shadow, its walls hung with hundreds of small cloth banners stitched in faded thread. Lamps burned in niches cut into the stone, and each niche held something: a chipped cup, a child's shoe, a brass key to a door that no longer existed, a sprig of rosemary gone brown with age but replaced often enough that the scent stayed green. People sat in a wide ring on benches and cushions and upturned crates — perhaps sixty of them, old and young, a fishwife with scales still glittering on her knuckles, a boy of maybe ten swinging his feet beside a grandmother whose hands never stopped pleating the hem of her skirt. They had been singing. Now they were looking at her.

Isandro knew how to be invisible. She did not know how to be visible, and the difference turned out to be terrifying.

"I followed the singing," she said, because it was true and truth was the only currency she had left that wasn't stolen. "I didn't mean to intrude."

"Intrude." A woman near the front laughed, low and warm. She was broad-shouldered, gray-braided, with the forearms of someone who had hauled nets or children or both for fifty years. "Child, we've been waiting for an intruder for three generations. Sit down before you fall down. Maren, get her the chair. The good chair."

"The good chair," someone echoed, and there was a ripple of amusement through the ring, gentle, unhurried, the laughter of people who had all the time in the world because time was the one thing no guild had figured out how to steal from them.

They put her in a chair by the lamp-niche with the child's shoe, and the old lamplighter — his name was Odo, she learned, and he had been lighting lamps in the upper city for sixty years while also, all along, doing *this* — pressed a clay cup into her hands. The drink was warm and bitter and sweet all at once, honey and some root she couldn't name, and it tasted like nothing the Hollow above had ever produced. It tasted like somewhere else. Somewhere real.

"What is this place?" she asked.

Odo settled onto the bench beside her with the groan of a man for whom sitting was a full-time occupation. "This," he said, "is the only honest room left in the country."

"The Hollow has no honest rooms?"

"The Hollow has no rooms at all, if you believe the maps." He nodded upward, toward the fog and the streets and the guildhall where Corvane was even now sealing vaults and burning ledgers — though Isandro said nothing of that, not yet, not here. "We live in a country that was erased, girl. Officially. Formally. With signatures and seals and a great deal of money changing hands. And when they erased us, they erased the gods too. Or they tried. You can't erase a god by decree. Gods aren't written down anywhere except in people." He tapped his own chest, once. "So some of us became the writing."

Isandro looked around the ring again, and this time she understood what she was seeing. The banners on the walls weren't decoration. They were records. Each one stitched with names — dozens of names, hundreds, in thread of different ages, some bright, some nearly dissolved. Generations of handwriting rendered in needlework. A ledger, she realized, with a lurch that started in her stomach and climbed all the way to her throat. These people had been keeping a ledger too. Not of thefts. Of *keeps*.

"You've been worshipping them," she said quietly. "All this time. Even after the prayers stopped being answered."

"Especially then." The broad-shouldered woman spoke up again. Her name was Brigit, and she had the air of someone accustomed to speaking for rooms. "Anyone can pray when the harvest comes in easy. Faith is what you do in the silence. Our grandmothers sang to gods who never answered, and their grandmothers before them, and we kept the names alive the way you keep a fire alive in wet wood — small, sheltered, patient. Because we remembered something the guild forgot." She leaned forward, and the lamplight caught the silver at her temples. "A god isn't power. Power is just what leaks out of a god when enough people hold its name. We held the names. We've been holding them for ninety years. And lately—" she exchanged a glance with Odo, and the glance was weighted, careful, "—lately the wood has caught."

* * *

They asked her almost nothing about herself, which was the strangest mercy of the whole night. In the world above, every conversation was an appraisal — who are you, what do you carry, what can you be made to owe. Here they simply made room. The boy with the swinging feet eventually migrated over to stare at her ink-stained fingers, and when she showed him how the clerks' shorthand worked, drawing little hooks and tails in the condensation on her cup, he watched with the naked delight of a child being let in on a secret, and Isandro felt something crack gently inside her chest, some plate of armor she hadn't known was load-bearing.

"You write things down," he said, awed. "Everything?"

"Everything I'm paid to."

"Do you write down the things people want forgotten?"

"Yes."

"And do you keep them?"

She hesitated. "Some of them."

"Then you're like us," he said simply, and went back to his seat, having dismantled her entire identity in four sentences.

Later, when the cups were refilled, Brigit called the ring to order and they sang again, and this time they taught her. The hymn had no title; titles, Odo explained, were a kind of ownership, and these songs belonged to no one. It was a round, or something older than a round, each voice entering at its own moment, weaving, and the words were in the old tongue — the tongue of the erased name — which Isandro discovered she understood without knowing why, the way her knuckles had known the knock. *We were, we are, we will have been.* That was the refrain. Past, present, future perfect: the tense of a people insisting on their own continuity against every official record. She sang it badly. Nobody minded. When her voice cracked on the third repetition, sixty people adjusted around the crack the way water adjusts around a stone, and carried her.

And in the middle of the singing, the room grew warm.

Not lamp-warm. Not body-warm. Warm the way chapter six of her own memory had been warm — the widow's room, the dying woman, the impossible texture of an answered prayer. Isandro felt it climb the back of her neck and pool behind her sternum, and the flame in the nearest lamp-niche stood up straight and tall as if saluting, and the boy stopped swinging his feet, and Brigit closed her eyes, and Odo smiled the smile of a man greeting an old friend across a very long distance.

"They're listening tonight," he murmured to her, without opening his eyes. "They don't always. But they're listening tonight."

"Why?" Isandro whispered. "Why now? After ninety years?"

Odo opened his eyes then and looked at her — that twice-looking, that seeing — and his expression was kind and terrible at once. "Because somebody upstairs has started moving the pieces," he said. "Because the borders are burning. Because the bargain that hid us is coming due, and the ones who signed it think they can sign another one, a bigger one, and erase us twice." He took her hand, paper folds over ink stains, and his grip was stronger than it looked. "You came down those stairs carrying questions, daughter. We heard you coming for days. The gods hear the ledger-keepers loudest — you're the ones who know exactly what was taken."

* * *

She stayed until the lamps burned low. She learned names: Brigit, who had once been a midwife in the upper city until the guild priced birth-memories beyond a laborer's wages and she had come below rather than sell. Odo, who had carried the same rosemary sprig to the shrine-niche every week for forty years, replacing it when it browned, because his mother had told him the god of thresholds liked green growing things at his doors. The boy was Tam, Brigit's grandson, and he had never once been above ground — born in the undercity, raised in the undercity, a citizen of a country that officially did not exist, learning songs for gods who officially had never answered anyone.

"And yet," Isandro said, as the last of the congregation drifted toward the sleeping alcoves, "you're not hiding. Not really. Hiding is what we do up there — pretending not to exist, pretending not to see. This is something else."

"It's keeping," Brigit said. She was banking the lamps, one by one, with a brass snuffer worn smooth as riverstone. "There's a difference. Hiding says *we aren't here*. Keeping says *we are here, and we know what we know, and we will still know it tomorrow*. The guild hides. We keep." She paused at the last lamp, the one beside the child's shoe, and looked at Isandro across the dimming room. "You keep things too. I saw it the moment you walked in. You have a keeper's hands. You've just been using them for thieves."

The words landed harder than any accusation Corvane had ever leveled, because they were accurate. Isandro thought of the copied entry hidden in the lining of her coat, the god-touched memory she had saved from Corvane's bonfire orders. She thought of the ledger memorized line by line behind her eyes. She thought of the gap in her childhood, the smooth blank where a girl should have been, purchased by a desperate mother and filed away by men like her.

"I need to tell you something," she said. "About why the prayers are coming back. About what the guild is planning. You won't like it."

Brigit snuffed the final lamp, and in the sudden near-darkness her voice was calm as deep water. "Child, we buried our country ninety years ago and kept singing at the grave. There is very little left that we won't like. Tell us."

So Isandro told them — the answered prayer, the counterfeit verdict, Veyl crossing a border that should have refused him, the vault sealed, the memories ordered destroyed, the border fires smoldering at the edges of the map. And when she reached the part she had pieced together from Sefa's smuggled knowledge and her own sleepless arithmetic — Corvane's plan, the second erasure, the harvesting of a nation's memory of itself — the room had gone utterly silent, sixty people breathing in the dark like one long-held note.

It was Tam, finally, who broke the silence, in the way of children, which is to say completely and without mercy.

"But they can't take the songs," he said. "Can they? The songs are ours. We give them to each other. Nobody wrote them down, so nobody can burn them."

Isandro opened her mouth to explain about extraction, about harvest houses, about what a determined guildmaster with a master ledger could do to unwritten things — and stopped. Because the boy was right, in the way that mattered. What was freely given could not be stolen. What was shared could not be owned. The choir had survived ninety years of official nonexistence not by hiding but by handing their memory to one another, piece by piece, voice to voice, until it belonged to everyone and therefore to no thief.

The ledger in her head suddenly weighed differently. All those years she had thought of her memorized archive as contraband, as the guild's property smuggled out between her ears. But if she gave it away — if she taught it, sang it, stitched it into banners and rounds and children's condensation-drawings — it would stop being loot and become inheritance.

"Well," said Odo into the darkness, and she could hear the smile in it. "It seems the gods sent us a clerk."

Above them, faint through ninety feet of stone and fog, a bell began to toll — not the guild's hour-bell, but the alarm, ragged and urgent, ringing from the direction of the border quarter. The fires were spreading. And somewhere in the upper city, moving through the smoke with a magistrate's certainty and a believer's patience, a man named Veyl had begun to hear, drifting up from beneath the streets, the sound of singing.
