## 15. Veyl Hears Singing

The song found him before he understood it was a song.

Magistrate Veyl had been walking the Hollow's streets for three days without finding what he came for, and the city had begun to feel like a question asked in a language he almost spoke. The fog here did not behave as fog should. It did not thin with the morning or burn away under lamplight; it simply persisted, gray and patient, wrapping every street in a courtesy of concealment. Doors stood where doors should not. Alleys bent when he was not looking at them directly. He had crossed into a country that officially did not exist, and the country itself seemed determined to keep its word.

He had been sent because prayers were being answered.

That sentence alone should have been enough to steady him. Veyl was a man of the Concordance, sworn to the god of Order—*Aureth, Who Weighs, Who Keeps the Scales True*—and answered prayers were not miracles to be celebrated but errors to be corrected. Doctrine said so plainly: the gods had withdrawn from the world a thousand years ago for reasons inscribed in the Nine Volumes and sealed in the Vault of the Faith at Carreth. Any prayer that received an answer now was either fraud, delusion, or something worse—a crack in the great silence through which older things might seep. His office existed to find cracks. To stand on them until they closed.

And yet.

He stopped at the mouth of an alley that smelled of wet stone and tallow smoke, and listened again.

It was singing. Real singing, many voices braided together, rising from somewhere beneath his feet—the undercity, the smugglers' warrens, the places his informants called *the deep streets*. He could not make out the words. But there was a quality to it that made the back of his neck prickle, a resonance like the hum a wineglass gives just before it decides whether to sing or shatter.

Veyl was forty-one years old and had never once doubted his faith. He wanted that recorded somewhere, even if only in his own mind: that he had walked into this fog with his soul squared away like ledgers in a strongbox, each belief filed and countersigned. He had hollowed eleven memory-thieves in nine years. He had watched men's eyes go blank and felt nothing worse than the satisfaction of a scale coming true. When the Concordance gave him the writ to cross into the Hollow—an act forbidden by treaty, by geography, and by three separate seals—he had gone without hesitation, because Aureth's law did not recognize borders drawn by cowards who had traded their own names for safety.

But he had noticed things in three days. Small things. Things he kept trying to file and finding no drawer for them.

The first: the border had let him through. It should not have. The Hollow's concealment was legendary—whole cartography bureaus had gone mad trying to chart it—and yet the fog had parted for him like a courtier, almost eagerly, as though something on the far side had been expecting him. Or inviting him. He had told himself it was because his writ carried divine sanction. He was no longer entirely sure that explanation was holding weight.

The second: the people. He had expected fear, sullenness, the hunted animal cunning of a nation in hiding. Instead he had found courtesy. A baker had given him bread without being asked and refused payment. Children had stared at him openly, unafraid, the way children stare at anything new. An old man repairing a shutter had nodded to him as though nodding were a thing one simply did, here, to strangers who should not exist in your street. They knew what he was—he could see it in the way conversations hushed around him—and still they did not run.

The third thing was the singing.

He followed it. There was no other honest way to describe what happened; his feet simply went, down a stair he would have sworn had not been there an hour ago, along a corridor whose walls sweated moisture and old candle grease, past a shrine niche where someone had left a bowl of milk and a sprig of rowan—offerings, actual offerings, to gods the Concordance had declared silent for ten centuries. His hand rested on the pommel of his warrant-rod, the instrument of his office, the thing that let him read a mind the way a clerk reads a ledger. He did not draw it. That fact registered somewhere in him like a debt unpaid.

The stair opened into a vaulted chamber that had clearly once been a cistern. Water still pooled in its lowest reaches, black and mirror-still, reflecting dozens of candles that burned in niches cut into the stone. And filling the chamber—perhaps sixty people, perhaps more, standing shoulder to shoulder on the dry ring of paving above the waterline—was the choir.

They were ordinary. That was the first thing that struck him, and it struck harder than any splendor could have. A woman with flour on her forearms. A man missing two fingers, his sleeve pinned neatly at the wrist. Teenagers with chapped hands. Old people leaning on one another. No vestments, no regalia, none of the terrible beauty the Concordance built its cathedrals out of. They sang in rounds, their voices low and sure, and the words—now that he was close enough—were not hymns of petition at all.

They were names.

*Aureth,* sang the baker woman, and Veyl's heart stopped and restarted wrong. *Heshet of the Threshold. Marun Who Remembers. The Quiet Sister. The God Under the Floorboards.* Name after name, old names, pre-Concordance names, names that existed in his seminary texts only as footnotes about heresies—sung not in fear but in the easy familiarity of people greeting neighbors across a fence. They were keeping a roll call. That was what the singing was. Not worship, exactly. Attendance. Sixty souls calling out the names of vanished gods the way a shepherd counts sheep in the dark, making sure nothing has wandered off, making sure everything is still *there.*

And the water was listening.

Veyl saw it happen and would deny seeing it later, in the way of all men witnessing the impossible: first as reflection, then as ripple, then as light. The black pool began to glow from beneath, faintly, warmly, the color of banked coals seen through ash. The ripples moved against the drip of water from the ceiling—inward, converging, as though the pool were a slow eye turning toward the sound. And in each ripple's crest, briefly, he saw faces. Not reflections of the choir. Other faces. Weathered, ancient, half-formed, like figures seen through frosted glass, pressing close to the surface from underneath.

One of them looked at him.

It had no name he recognized. It was neither beautiful nor terrible; it was *attentive*, in a way that made him understand that he had spent his whole life mistaking attention for judgment. The face regarded him from beneath the water with an expression he could only call hopeful, and Veyl—who had broken interrogators twice his size, who had read crimes out of minds until their owners wept—felt his knees soften and put his palm flat against the cold stone wall to stay upright.

*Ask us something,* the face did not say, but said anyway, in the manner of such communications, which bypass the ear entirely and arrive already inside you. *We have waited so long to be asked.*

His doctrine rose up in him like a wall, nine volumes thick. *The gods are silent. The silence is holy. Answers are cracks, and cracks must be closed.* He recited it inwardly, the whole catechism, and heard how it sounded in this place: a child shouting rules into a room where everyone had grown up and gone gentle.

"I don't—" His voice cracked. He cleared his throat. Around him the choir sang on; either they could not see him in the shadows of the stairwell or they had decided, with that unnerving local courtesy, to let him listen. "I don't know what to ask."

The warmth in the pool deepened. The faces gathered closer beneath the surface—not menacingly, the way crowds gather around an accident, but the way family gathers at a door when someone long-absent finally comes up the road. And then, from everywhere and nowhere—from the water, from the candle flames, from the marrow of his own bones—came an answer to the prayer he had not known he was praying.

It was not power. That was the thing his doctrine had promised and the miracle did not deliver: no voice like thunder, no commandments, no revelation of cosmic hierarchy with his own name written in a favored place. What came instead was *recognition*. A vast, unhurried, tender attention settling over him like a coat laid on a shivering boy, and within it, wordlessly, the knowledge of himself seen whole: the orphanage at Carreth where the brothers had taught him that love was disorder; the first thief he had hollowed, a girl of nineteen, and the way she had thanked him afterward, empty-eyed, because the extraction had taken the memory of why she'd stolen and left only the habit of gratitude; the private room in his own house where he kept, behind a false wall, his mother's sewing scissors, which he had not been able to explain to anyone, ever, and had never once been able to throw away.

The presence held all of it and did not flinch. Did not weigh it. Aureth weighed; Aureth kept scales true; every act of Veyl's life had been an entry awaiting audit. This was different. This was a god—or the shape where a god used to be, worn thin by centuries of neglect but still stubbornly, improbably present—simply *knowing* him, the way the choir's song knew its gods: by name, by attendance, without requiring improvement.

The warmth withdrew as slowly as it had come. The pool dimmed to ordinary black. In the candlelight the choir finished their round, and the flour-dusted woman said, in the tone of someone concluding a mundane task, "Marun sends regards," and several people chuckled, and began gathering cloaks and baskets as if they had done nothing more remarkable than attend a market committee.

Veyl stood in the stairwell shadow with his hand still braced on the wall, breathing like a man pulled from deep water.

He should report this. That was the procedure, carved so deeply into him it operated below thought: witness, record, seal, suppress. A mass gathering of pre-Concordance cultists performing genuine thaumaturgy in the capital's forgotten undercity was precisely the crack he had been sent to find, and closing it would mean bringing the full machinery of the Concordance down on these sixty ordinary people—their names entered into suppression rolls, their memories subject to lawful hollowing, the pool drained, the candles extinguished, the songs silenced forever. He could draft the order tonight. He knew the forms by heart. He had signed hundreds like them.

His hand had drifted to the warrant-rod again. He looked down at it now, really looked—at the seal of Aureth set in its pommel, the scales worked in gold, worn smooth by his grip—and for the first time in nine years he thought about what the rod actually did when he pressed it to a man's temple. It did not weigh anything. It took. It reached into another soul and carried pieces of it away in its golden scales, and called the carrying justice.

The scales had never once, in nine years, come back lighter for the person emptied.

"Marun sends regards," he whispered, testing the words, and the candle nearest him leaned toward his breath like a listener.

Above him, muffled by stone, the city bells rang the hour, and beneath the bells, threading up through the fog toward the world where doctrine ruled alone, the choir began the next round of names.

Veyl climbed the stairs slowly. At the top he paused, took out his field journal—the official record, the document that would determine everything that happened next—and uncapped his pen with fingers that were not quite steady.

The entry he wrote was short. It was also, he realized as he blotted it, the first lie he had ever recorded in thirty years of immaculate service:

*Undercity patrol complete. Nothing of significance found.*

He capped the pen. Then, because he was still a magistrate, still an auditor of souls, still unable to leave a ledger unbalanced, he took out a second notebook—one he had bought that morning from a stationer who had smiled at him as if he were a neighbor—and in it, in small private characters, he wrote the truth instead:

*Day 3. Found the singers. Found the pool. Found that I was the one being searched for. The god I serve has never once looked at me. Something under this city did, and asked for nothing, and I do not know what to do with a gift that cannot be filed.*

He sat for a while in the dark at the top of the stairs, listening to the names rise.

Then, distantly, from the direction of the guild quarter, he saw firelight bloom against the fog—orange, wrong, spreading fast along some rooftop line—and heard the first thin cries of alarm. Somewhere below him, the choir faltered mid-name.

Veyl closed both notebooks and started running toward the smoke.
