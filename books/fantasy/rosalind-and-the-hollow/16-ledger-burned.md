## 16. Ledger Burned

The smoke was the first thing wrong.

Isandro smelled it before she saw it—oak-tannin and vellum and something sweeter beneath, like sugar left too long on a stove—and her feet stopped on the stairs of the counting house without her permission, the way a body stops when it hears its own name spoken in a voice that shouldn't know it.

She had come down at dawn to reconcile the week's entries. That was all. A clerk's morning: trim the wick, warm the ink, check the night-takings against the day-book, carry discrepancies up to the master vault for Corvane's seal. She had done it eleven hundred mornings, or near enough that the number itself had become a kind of prayer, and she had expected nothing from this one but the small dry comfort of columns that agreed.

Instead the courtyard was full of fire.

They had built the pyre in the delivery yard where the memory-carts unloaded, a scaffold of iron grating with a trough of coals beneath it, and onto it they were feeding the public ledgers. Not copies. Not the spoiled volumes, the water-damaged ones, the duplicates kept against loss. The ledgers themselves—the great folios bound in grey hide, each one stamped with the guild's sigil, a door with no handle. Isandro knew every one of them by spine. She had shelved them. She had indexed them. For nine years she had pressed her thumb to their clasps each night to confirm the wax seals were unbroken, and now four guildsmen in leather aprons were breaking those seals with knives and feeding the pages into the flames two and three at a time, and Guildmaster Corvane stood at the edge of the heat with his hands folded behind his back, watching, his face as calm as a man supervising the burning of rubbish.

"Isandro." He did not turn. He never needed to; he had a way of knowing where people stood, as though the room reported to him directly. "You're early."

"I came to reconcile the—" Her voice came out thin. She tried again. "Master, those are the public ledgers. The client registers. The takings-books for the last forty years."

"Yes."

"The *records*, sir."

He turned then, and she wished he hadn't. There was nothing cruel in his face. That was always the worst of him—Corvane wore kindness the way other men wore armor, fitted and polished and put on deliberately each morning. "Come here," he said gently. "Look at what we're doing, and tell me if you understand it."

She came because refusing would be a thing remembered, and being remembered had been trained out of her so thoroughly that even now, watching nine years of her own work curl into ash, she obeyed first and thought after.

Up close the fire was louder than she expected. Paper screamed when it burned—not a metaphor; there was an actual sound, a high thin whine as the heat raced through the fibers, as though each page were exhaling everything written on it at once. She watched a column of figures blacken and lift. She could read fragments as they went: *one summer, aged seven, condition excellent* — *grief of a widow, three years' standing, purchased whole* — *the taste of a name, seller unknown*. Entries she had made. Entries she had checked twice. Each one a small theft she had told herself was only bookkeeping, only ink, only the world's sad arithmetic passing through her careful hands.

"You've heard the reports," Corvane said. "The fires on the border. The fog thinning at the north march. The magistrate Veyl walking streets that should have refused him." He picked up a loose page from the cart, held it toward the light as if reading it, then let it fall into the coals. "Every entry in these books is a key, Isandro. I understand that now. Every memory we ever recorded resonates with the doors of this country—resonates, opens, *answers*. The old gods are coming back through our own locks. We built the Hollow's hiding out of traded memories, and every trade left a seam, and something on the other side has begun pulling at the threads."

"So we burn the seams," Isandro said. Her mouth was very dry.

"We burn the keys." He smiled at her, patient, a teacher with a slow pupil. "The memories themselves are already sold, spent, worn into other people's lives. But the records—the precise shape of what was taken, the weight and texture and *address* of each theft—that is ours alone. Destroy the record and the lock has no key cut to fit it. The doors stay shut. The fog stays thick. The country stays safe."

"And the clients?" The question escaped before she could weigh it. "The people who might someday want to buy back what they sold? Their proof of ownership—their only proof—is in those books."

Something moved behind Corvane's eyes, brief and cold as a fish turning under ice. "No one buys anything back anymore," he said. "That is rather the point."

* * *

She stayed through all of it. She told herself it was duty—a clerk witnesses the destruction of records, signs the certificate, notes the date—but the truth was uglier and simpler: she could not make her legs carry her away. She stood in the coal-smell and the drifting flakes of ash, flakes that settled on her sleeves like grey snow, and she watched volume after volume go.

The ledger of the famine year. The ledger with the smugglers' routes Sefa had once sold them, margins dense with her spidery hand. The ledger from the year of the great purchase, when the guild had bought and sold more forgetting in twelve months than in any year since—she knew that one best of all, because she had spent a winter restoring its water-ruined middle sections, lying on her stomach on the counting-house floor with a magnifying lens, coaxing faded ink back into legibility line by line by line.

She had memorized it then. Not on purpose—that was the terrible, wonderful thing. It had simply happened, the way water happens to a sponge. Nine years of restoration work, of cross-checking, of recopying damaged pages, of falling asleep over open folios with her cheek on the margin notes: the ledgers lived in her head. Not perfectly, not every figure—but the architecture of them, the great load-bearing walls of the archive. Which memory had been taken from whom. What it had cost. Where each entry sat, which volume, which page, which hand had written it. The addresses of ten thousand stolen things.

She understood it fully somewhere around midmorning, watching the last of the famine-year ledger collapse into the grate, and the understanding arrived not as a thought but as a physical event—a cold drop straight down through her chest, followed by a heat rising to meet it, terror and something else meeting in the middle and refusing to sort themselves out.

*They are burning the only copy,* said the terror.

*No,* said the other thing, quiet and enormous underneath. *They are burning the second copy.*

She looked at Corvane across the dying fire. He was giving instructions about the vault seals, unhurried, thorough, a man who believed he had just solved his problem completely. And Isandro Zhou, nameless clerk, invisible woman, keeper of other people's stolen pasts, stood in the ash-fall and realized that she was, at this moment, the most dangerous object in the Hollow Country.

The complete ledger was not in the vault. It was not in the fire.

It was wearing a grey clerk's dress and trying to breathe normally.

* * *

That evening she climbed to her room above the counting house and did not light the lamp. She sat in the dark with her hands flat on her knees and took inventory of herself the way she'd take inventory of a shipment: methodically, entry by entry, terrified of what she'd find missing.

The famine ledger. There. All of it—or near enough; the middle sections she had restored herself, and restoration meant reading each damaged line until the reading became remembering. The takings-books, forty years of them, the general shapes and the famous entries. The god-entry—the widow's answered prayer, the one with the impossible texture, the one she had copied out in secret and hidden inside the binding of her own day-book. Still there. Still hers.

Her heart was going hard enough that she could feel it in her ears.

Here was the arithmetic of it, laid out plain as a column of sums: Corvane thought he had destroyed the keys. He had destroyed the *written* keys. But a memory recorded is a key, and a memory *remembered*—held whole in a living mind, rehearsed nightly across nine years of insomnia and devotion—was that not also a record? Was that not perhaps the truest record there was, the original form of the archive, older than ink, older than paper? The guild had spent a century teaching the country that minds could be emptied, that memory was cargo, transferable, burnable. Corvane had burned paper. He had not yet learned that Isandro Zhou was paper.

And the thought arrived on the heels of that one, uninvited, in a voice that sounded uncomfortably like the widow's: *and if the records are keys, and you are the records, then what do the doors do when they notice you?*

She got up and barred the door, which was absurd—no one had knocked on her door in nine years except with laundry or instructions—and then she sat back down and made herself think it through, because panic was a luxury for people who could afford to be replaced.

If the ledger was a ring of keys, then she was carrying the whole ring. If Corvane discovered that—if he ever suspected that his diligent, forgettable clerk had a complete copy of everything he'd burned living behind her eyes—he would not burn her. Burning was waste. He would *harvest* her. He would take the archive out of her head the way the guild took summers out of soldiers, cleanly, professionally, leaving her a husk who couldn't remember why the empty space hurt. Or worse, he wouldn't take the knowledge at all; he'd simply take the knower, quietly, the way the country erased itself, and no one would ever ask after her because no one asked after clerks.

But if she ran, if she hid, if she carried this alone—

She pressed her palms against her eyes until she saw constellations.

There was another possibility, and it frightened her more than Corvane did, because it required believing things she had only glimpsed: that the doors were real, that the resonance was real, that the widow's warm room and the border fires and the god-touched magistrate were all one weather system moving toward her. That the way out of the Hollow was not a road but a *record*—that every memory she had ever catalogued was a door waiting for the right hand. And that the gods were returning precisely because someone, somewhere, had started praying with a memory still intact.

She thought of the copied entry hidden in her day-book. She thought of the dying woman whose memories had never been sold, whose prayer had been *answered*, whose name Isandro had traced through the registers like a thread through dark cloth.

Tomorrow, she decided. Tomorrow she would go back to that thread. Because the ledgers were ash, and the vault would be sealed by week's end, and the only archive left in the Hollow Country was a woman in a grey dress sitting in the dark doing sums—and sums, whatever else they were, always pointed somewhere.

Below her window, in the yard, the last embers of the pyre were still glowing. She watched them through the gap in the shutter for a long time. Then she opened her day-book to the false binding, drew out the copied entry, and read it by the light of the fire she could still smell—the fire that was supposed to have ended everything, and had instead, without Corvane's knowing, handed the whole country's memory to a clerk who had spent nine years learning exactly how to keep it.

Outside, far off toward the northern edge of the city, the sky flickered orange again. Another border fire. Closer than the last.

Isandro closed the book, and began, silently, in the dark, to recite the famine ledger from its first line—checking herself against ruin, page by page, the way a sailor bails water—while somewhere below, in the ember-lit yard, a guildsman kicked apart the ashes of a nation's stolen past and found, of course, nothing at all.
