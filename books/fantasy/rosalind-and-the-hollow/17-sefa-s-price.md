## 17. Sefa's Price

The fog market did not open at any hour. It opened when it felt like it.

Sefa had learned this the way she had learned everything worth knowing in the Hollow: by waiting, and by watching, and by never once letting her face show what either cost her. She sat now on an overturned crate behind her stall—a lean-to of salvaged doorframes lashed together with tarred rope, hung with charms that were mostly brass and mostly lies—and she counted the fog as it came down the alley between the shuttered chandlery and the wall of a house whose address no map had ever carried.

The fog came the way debt comes. Slowly, then all at once, and always owing more than it seemed.

She was sixty-one years old, though her knees insisted on eighty, and she had been trading in borders since before Isandro Lockridge was born. Not in memories—memory was the guild's trade, memory was coin and currency and the slow bleeding of a nation into its own pockets—but in the spaces *between*. Thresholds. Passages. The particular quality of a doorway at dusk. She had sold a smuggler the border between waking and sleep so he could move his cargo through other people's dreams. She had sold a widow the border between grief and forgetting, and had thrown in, free of charge, the warning that the two were not so far apart as anyone liked to believe.

And once—once, forty years ago, to a young guildsman with careful hands and eyes like a man doing arithmetic on his own soul—she had sold a piece of the country's edge itself. A seam in the concealment. A place where the Hollow could be stepped out of, if you knew the price.

Corvane had paid for that seam with a memory Sefa still could not name without her mouth going dry: the smell of her mother's kitchen, gone in an instant, traded for a hole in the world's attention. She had told herself for four decades that it was a fair bargain. She had told herself that everyone who walked through that seam owed her their lives, and that a life was worth more than a kitchen.

Lately, with prayers being answered and fires burning along the border where nothing should burn, she had begun telling herself other things entirely.

The fog thickened. Somewhere down the alley, a lantern lit itself—or was lit; in the fog market the distinction mattered less than it should—and Sefa heard the small, precise footsteps of someone who had learned invisibility so thoroughly that she had made an art of it.

Isandro Lockridge came around the corner carrying a stolen summer in a jar.

* * *

Sefa saw the jar before she saw the girl's face, and her heart did something complicated.

It was good merchandise. That was the first thing, the professional thing, the thing she reached for because reaching for the professional thing was how she had survived every hard moment since her mother's kitchen stopped smelling like anything at all. The summer glowed in its glass like honey held up to a lamp—cut grass, river water warm from a long day, the specific gold light of late afternoon on a porch where somebody loved you. Guild work, obviously. Guild-grade preservation, seamless edges, no rot at the borders of the thing. Whoever had sold it had sold it whole, which meant they had been desperate, or lied to about what selling whole would mean.

And the clerk carrying it had the look of a woman who had already begun paying with pieces of herself and was pretending otherwise.

"You want passage-knowledge," Sefa said, before Isandro had finished sitting down on the crate across from her. No greeting. Greetings in the fog market were negotiations, and Sefa was too tired and too old and too frightened to negotiate anything but the truth tonight.

Isandro set the jar on the plank between them. "I want to know if there's a way out. A real one. For more than one person."

"For how many?"

"All of us."

The word sat in the fog between them. *All of us.* Sefa looked at the girl—at the careful hands, the ledger-keeper's posture, the invisible-woman's clothes worn like armor—and felt forty years of rationalized bargains shift their weight inside her chest like ballast in a listing ship.

"You've been reading things you shouldn't," Sefa said.

"I've been reading everything I shouldn't." Isandro's voice stayed level, but Sefa heard the crack under it, the way you hear ice groan before it gives. "The ledger resonates. Every recorded memory is a key. I copied an entry Corvane ordered destroyed, and when I held the copy against the vault door, the door—" She stopped. Started again, quieter. "The door wanted it."

"Wanted it." Sefa turned the jar slowly on the plank, watching the summer swirl. "Doors don't want, girl. Doors *hunger*. There's a difference, and the difference is the only reason I'm still alive to be rude to you."

"Then tell me what the difference is."

Sefa was quiet for a long moment. Down the alley, the fog moved past the lantern like a congregation leaving early. She thought about all the ways she could answer this question badly. She thought about the seam she had sold Corvane, and what had come through it last week wearing a magistrate's coat and a god's patience. She thought about her mother's kitchen, and how she could no longer remember whether the window faced east or west, and how that absence had grown, over forty years, from a pebble to a stone to the load-bearing wall of everything she was.

"The difference," she said finally, "is consent."

* * *

She told it to her straight, because the girl had earned straightness, and because Sefa had decided—if she was honest with herself, she had decided the moment she'd seen the jar—that she was done dealing in half-truths. Half-truths were how the Hollow had happened in the first place.

"There is a door," Sefa said. "One door. Not a seam, not a smuggler's gap like the one I sold your guildmaster when he was young and I was stupid. A real door. It stands in a shrine nobody tends, in the oldest part of the undercity, and it has stood there since before the erasure, since before the guild, maybe since before the country had a name worth hiding. The old god of thresholds keeps it. Or kept it. Or is it—I don't pretend to know what gods are anymore, not with prayers coming back like weeds through floorboards."

"I've heard the choir sing near it," Isandro said. "The Unremembered. They keep it swept."

"They keep it *fed*, whether they know it or not. Worship is just memory with incense on." Sefa leaned forward. "Here is what I know, and I know it because my grandmother knew it, and her grandmother before her, back to people who watched the door built. The door does not open for force. It does not open for payment. It does not open for cleverness, and it will not open for a guildmaster with a master key and a head full of stolen nation. It opens for a gift. One memory, freely given, from every soul who wants to walk through. Given—not taken, not bought, not extracted while the giver screams. *Given.* The door can tell the difference. The door has been telling the difference since before there were ledgers to record the telling."

Isandro's hands had gone still on her knees. "Freely given. By every soul."

"Every soul who leaves. Those who stay owe nothing. But girl—" and here Sefa's voice did the thing she hated, the old woman's waver, the crack in the merchant's mask— "you have to understand what freely given means, when the person giving knows what they're losing. My mother's kitchen wasn't taken from me. I *handed it over*. I chose it, standing in Corvane's office with my heart going like a rabbit's, and I chose it because I believed the lie that erasure was mercy. That's what makes it a gift. Not that it costs nothing. That it costs everything, and you pay anyway, with your eyes open, for something bigger than yourself."

"And if it's not given freely?" Isandro asked. "If it's taken?"

Sefa sat back. The fog curled around the legs of the stall like something listening.

"Then the door doesn't open," she said. "Worse than that. The door *remembers*. You cannot counterfeit a gift to a threshold-god any more than you can counterfeit love to a mirror. Whatever is forced through that frame comes back out again, wrong. Or doesn't come out at all. There are stories—" she waved a hand at the fog— "old stories, from before the erasure, about a lord who marched three hundred souls to the door at spear-point and took their memories by decree. The door drank the taking and gave back nothing, and those three hundred walked out of the shrine as strangers to each other and to themselves, and their descendants are the ones who can't cross the fog line even today. The door doesn't punish, exactly. It just refuses to lie. And a coerced gift is a lie with tears on it."

Isandro was silent. Sefa watched her do the arithmetic—the clerk's reflex, the same arithmetic Sefa herself had done forty years ago in a guildmaster's office—and waited for the sum.

"One memory per person," Isandro said at last. "From eleven thousand people. Freely. In the middle of a panic, with the borders burning and Veyl hunting us and half the population ready to trade their grandmothers for a guarantee."

"Now you see the shape of it."

"That's not a toll. That's—" Isandro pressed her palms against her eyes. "That's asking a drowning country to vote on which limb to cut off. Some won't give. Some can't. Some will give the wrong thing, or too much, or—"

"Some will give gladly," Sefa said. "That's the part nobody expects. I've seen it. When the choir sings, people weep and *want* to give. There are those in the undercity who've been keeping pieces safe for decades, saving them for exactly this, hoping some door would ask. The old woman on Tanner's Row who's guarded her wedding day like a relic. The ferryman who remembers every prayer his daughter ever said. Girl, the capacity for giving is the one thing Corvane has never managed to tax."

"Corvane." Isandro said the name the way you'd spit out a bad tooth. "You know what he's planning."

"I know what he's *always* planned. Erasure upon erasure. He helped hide this country once and he has spent forty years convincing himself it was kindness." Sefa's hands closed around the edge of the plank until the old wood creaked. "But hiding isn't working anymore—the prayers are burning holes in the concealment faster than the guild can patch them—and a man like Corvane doesn't surrender, he escalates. If the door needs gifts, he'll manufacture gifts. He'll harvest. He'll take the national memory out of eleven thousand heads by force and call it salvation, and he'll bring what he takes to the door in barrels, and the door will refuse him, and when it refuses him he'll try to *force* it, because men like Corvane believe every lock is an insult."

"He'd hollow the whole country to save it."

"He'd hollow the whole country because it's the only tool he has left. Taking is the guild's native language, girl. Giving is yours." Sefa reached out and touched the jar, once, lightly, the way you'd touch a sleeping animal. "Which brings us to your summer."

* * *

They settled the trade in silence, mostly, because the best trades were mostly silence.

The jar of summer sat between them glowing its patient gold, and Sefa found she could not pick it up. Not yet. Her fingers hovered over the glass the way they had hovered over the contract forty years ago, and the same cold question moved through her: *what am I actually buying, and what am I actually spending?*

Because here was the thing Sefa had never said aloud, not to Corvane, not to the smugglers, not to the dark at three in the morning: the passage-knowledge was not hers to sell. Oh, she owned the maps of it, the timings, the names of the dead who had tested the routes. But the knowledge of the door—its nature, its price, its refusal of coercion—had come down to her through four generations of women who had guarded it the way the choir guarded songs, and every one of those women had left the same instruction, written in the same hand in the front of the route-book:

*Sell the paths. Never sell the truth of the door. The truth goes free or it goes nowhere.*

Her grandmother had starved rather than sell it. Her mother had watched her do it and called her a fool, and then, when the erasure came and the family needed a place in the Hollow, her mother had sold the kitchen instead—sold it to the guild, sold it clean, and told Sefa afterward that this was what wisdom looked like: knowing which things were for sale.

Sefa had spent her whole life believing her mother. She was beginning, at sixty-one, with her knees singing and the border on fire, to suspect her grandmother had been right.

So she pushed the jar back across the plank.

Isandro stared at it. "That's not—"

"It's not a sale. Listen to me, girl, because I'm only saying this once and then I'm going to be embarrassed about it forever." Sefa's voice came out rougher than she intended. "The passage-knowledge is yours. Free. All of it—every route, every timing, every trick I know about moving people through impossible space. Take it, memorize it, put it in that head of yours that holds whole ledgers. But the truth of the door comes with it, and the truth of the door is not merchandise. It's a message. You carry it to whoever needs to hear it: the door opens for gifts. Only gifts. And anyone who tells you different—including me, including the guild, including a magistrate with god-light in his eyes—is selling you something that will break."

"Why?" Isandro asked. And it wasn't suspicion in her voice, which Sefa had braced for; it was something worse. It was the genuine bewilderment of a woman who had been invisible so long that kindness read as anomaly. "Why give it away? Everything in this city is a transaction. You taught half of them that."

"Because I sold a seam in the world's skin once, and I told myself it was business, and a magistrate walked through it." Sefa met her eyes. "Because I've been paid in full for forty years and the coin has never once stopped weighing. Because somewhere along the way I forgot which of my mother's lessons was the wise one, and I'd like to figure it out before I die, and helping eleven thousand people leave might balance the kitchen." She laughed, short and unlovely. "Or it might not. That's the trouble with ledgers, girl. They only balance at the end."

Isandro picked up the jar of summer and held it a moment, turning it, watching the gold light move. Then she set it down on Sefa's side of the plank and slid it the rest of the way.

"Keep it," she said. "Not as payment. As—" she hesitated, and Sefa watched her search for a word that didn't exist in any ledger— "as a gift. So you have something to give the door, if you come with us. Something that was given to you freely first."

For a long moment Sefa could not speak at all.

The fog moved. The lantern burned. And in the glass, the summer went on shining—cut grass, warm river, late light on a porch where somebody loved you—patient as a seed, waiting to be planted in whatever ground came after the Hollow.

* * *

She gave Isandro the route-book that night, and the copying took until the small hours: the clerk bent over Sefa's table with pen and ink, transcribing forty years of smuggled geography while Sefa sat across from her drinking tea she didn't taste and talking, because apparently once the dam broke in her, it broke all the way.

She told Isandro about the seam she'd sold Corvane, and how to find it, and why it must never be used again. She told her about the three hundred who had marched to the door at spear-point, and where their descendants lived, and how they flinched from thresholds to this day. She told her about her mother, and the kitchen, and the east-facing window she could no longer be sure of.

And Isandro listened the way clerks listen—completely, cataloguing, every word finding its shelf—and near dawn she looked up from the finished copy and said, quietly:

"Sefa. If Corvane moves first—if he starts harvesting before we can teach people to give—"

"Then we'll have a war over the meaning of a gift," Sefa said. "And wars over meanings are the only kind that matter."

She said it lightly. She did not feel light. Because even as she spoke, even as the ink dried on the copied routes, some old merchant's instinct—the one that had kept her alive through the erasure, through the famine years, through forty years of trading in the spaces between things—was whispering to her that the scales had not finished tipping. That Corvane had ears in the fog market, the way he had ears everywhere. That a clerk walking out of a smuggler's stall at dawn with a stolen book under her coat was a story that would reach the guildmaster's desk before the tea went cold.

That the race had already started, and they were behind.

Sefa stood at her stall's entrance as Isandro vanished into the grey morning, the jar of summer cradled against her chest like a child, and she watched the fog swallow the girl whole.

"Well, Mother," she said to the empty air. "Looks like I finally found the thing that isn't for sale."

Down the alley, the fog began, very softly, to burn.
