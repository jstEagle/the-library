## 18. The Gap in Her Childhood

The archive did not sleep, and neither, lately, did Isandro.

She had told herself she came down for the copied entry—the god-touched prayer she'd smuggled out of the destruction pile, folded now into a false folio labeled *Tallow Renderings, Quarter's End*. That was true. But it was not all of it, and Isandro Lockridge had spent eleven years in the Hollow Guild learning that a ledger never records only what you say it does. Every entry has margins. Every margin has ghosts.

Her ghost lived on the third sub-level, in the row of cabinets marked with the sigil for *Private Acquisitions*—the purchases the guild made from its own clerks and their kin, the quiet trades that kept families fed and places held. She had walked past those cabinets ten thousand times without opening them. A clerk who opens drawers out of curiosity is a clerk who stops being invisible. That was the first law of her survival, older even than the Guild's First Law: *see everything, be seen as nothing.*

But she had found a gap in herself, and gaps do not stay quiet once noticed. They widen. They ask questions in the dark behind your eyes, in the shape of a missing year the way a missing tooth asks questions of the tongue—endlessly, at odd hours, with an ache that has no source because the source has been removed.

She stood before the cabinet with her lamp turned low and her hand flat against the cold iron, and she let herself admit the truth she'd been circling since the night she'd examined her own earliest recorded memory:

Someone had taken her childhood. And someone had been paid for it.

* * *

It had started, properly, three nights ago, in the small hours when the counting-house above was empty and the fog pressed against the windows like something listening.

She had gone looking for nothing in particular—a habit of insomnia, pulling ledgers at random to lull herself with the rhythm of entries—and she had pulled her own file. Clerks' files were supposed to be sealed until death; Corvane kept them under his own key. But keys were Isandro's native language, and locks spoke to her the way other people's mothers spoke to them, gently, revealingly.

The file was thin. That should have warned her. Eleven years of service, hundreds of acquisitions processed through her hands, and her own record held barely a dozen pages: her hiring, her oaths, her wage notations, her evaluations written in Corvane's narrow, patient script—*meticulous; unremarkable; ideal.*

And then the acquisition ledger of her own memory, the one every guild member surrendered in part as surety of loyalty. She had known about that. Everyone knew. You gave the guild a memory as collateral, the way a borrower gives a ring. She had always assumed hers was something small. A taste. An afternoon.

She had read the entry by lamplight, expecting boredom, and found instead a hole wearing the costume of a sentence.

*Acquired: subject's early childhood, ages four through nine. Complete. Condition: pristine. Seller: M— Lockridge (mother). Consideration: permanent residency, two shares, family tier.*

Ages four through nine.

Five years. Not a taste. Not an afternoon. Five whole years, the entire architecture of a self's foundation, lifted out of her like a keel pulled from a hull—and the entry recorded it in the same flat hand, the same ink, the same dispassionate columns she used herself every night for widows' griefs and soldiers' courage. Somewhere in this building was a jar or a vial or a glass cabinet containing five years of Isandro Lockridge, catalogued, priced, and owned by someone else.

She had sat very still for a long time after reading it, the way you sit still when you realize the floor of a room has been painted onto canvas stretched over a pit.

Then she had done the only thing she knew how to do with unbearable information. She had begun to research it.

* * *

Now, three nights later, her fingers found the cabinet's lock and persuaded it, and the drawer slid open with a sound like a long-held breath released.

Inside: folders. Hundreds of them, thin as moth wings, each bearing a name or the absence of one. The Private Acquisitions were the guild's most shameful shelf—not because the trades were cruel, exactly, though some were, but because they were *necessary*, and necessity was the guildmaster's favorite word for things he didn't want looked at too closely. Families bought their places in the Hollow. The country was erased; its people could not pay taxes in coin to a nation that officially did not exist, could not hold deeds, could not inherit. What they had instead was memory—the one currency the erasure hadn't touched, because the erasure ran on forgetting and memory was the only thing left over.

So the guild bought memories the way pawnbrokers buy wedding rings. Gently. Reasonably. With paperwork.

Isandro found the L drawer. Her finger walked down the tabs—Lachance, Lamm, Leery, Lindqvist—and stopped.

LOCKRIDGE, I. — ACQUISITION FILE.

The folder was heavier than the others. She noticed that before she noticed anything else: a physical weight, a thickness, as if the paper itself were reluctant to be so brief about so large a theft.

She carried it back to her desk in the corner where the lamp couldn't be seen from the stairwell, and she opened it, and she made herself read like a clerk. That was her discipline, her armor: when feeling rose, she reached for procedure. Dates. Signatures. Valuations. If she treated her own life as an entry, perhaps it would hurt the way entries hurt—which is to say, not at all.

It did not work.

*Seller:* Marisol Lockridge. Her mother's name in a stranger's handwriting, and beneath it, in her mother's own careful script—the same script Isandro had inherited, the same slight backward slant on the letter *l*—a signature. Dated. Witnessed. *Sold freely and with full understanding of terms.*

Full understanding of terms. Isandro read those words four times. Her mother had understood. Had sat in some room upstairs, in a chair Isandro had probably dusted around a hundred times without knowing its history, and had listened to a guild assessor explain what would be taken, and had signed anyway.

*Consideration:* Permanent residency, two shares, family tier. Waiver of acquisition fees. Annual stipend of flour and oil, ten years.

Flour and oil. Her childhood had purchased bread. Five years of a daughter's dawns and fevers and first words and scraped knees and the smell of her mother's hair and the sound of rain on a roof she could no longer name—all of it traded for flour, oil, and the right to keep existing in a country that wasn't there.

Isandro discovered she was gripping the desk's edge hard enough to whiten her knuckles, and that her breath was coming in the shallow, controlled sips she used when inventorying grief that belonged to someone else. Except it wasn't someone else's. It had never been someone else's. That was the whole horror of it—that she had spent eleven years handling other people's stolen pasts with clean hands and a clear conscience, telling herself *bookkeeping is not stealing*, and all the while the bookkeeping had already been done to her, in this room, at this desk, by hands no cleaner than her own.

* * *

There were appendices. Of course there were; the guild documented everything, even its mercies, especially its mercies. She turned the pages with fingers that no longer felt entirely like her own.

The first appendix was the assessor's valuation note. Clinical. Almost tender, in the way a butcher might be tender arranging cuts.

*Subject's childhood memory is unusually coherent and affect-rich. The seller reports the child experienced no significant trauma in the period offered, which raises market value considerably. Trauma-free early memory is rare among sellers; most who reach us have already spent the sweet years surviving. Recommend full acquisition rather than partial. Note for the residency committee: the seller's husband died in the border purges; she has one dependent child. Approve at family tier with stipend. Compassion cases close quickly and generate goodwill we will need.*

Goodwill we will need. Isandro stared at the phrase until the letters blurred. Her father had died in the border purges—the purges that followed the erasure, when the conquering powers had swept the edges of the Hollow for anyone trying to flee back into the world. She had known that the way you know a fact in a textbook: dated, distant, bloodless. Now it arrived in her chest with its full weight. Her father dead, her mother alone with a small daughter in a country that had been deleted, and the only door to safety was the guild's, and the guild's price was the daughter's own past.

What did you say to that? What did you feel? Fury, yes—but fury at whom? At her mother, who had chosen bread over her child's memories because the alternative was the two of them starving or worse? At Corvane, who had priced a traumatized nation's desperation into his valuation notes and called it compassion? At the world outside the maps, which had made the Hollow necessary? The anger had nowhere clean to land. It scattered, like light through cracked glass, into a dozen smaller angers, each one justified, none of them sufficient.

The second appendix was worse.

It was a receipt of use. The guild did not merely store acquired memories; it sold them, lent them, spent them. And Isandro's childhood—five years, pristine, trauma-free, affect-rich—had been *popular*.

*Sale 1: to client C-114. Purpose: replacement. Client lost own childhood to fire; purchased subject's ages four through six as graft. Note: graft took well. Client writes annually to express gratitude.*

Somewhere out in the fog-bound streets of the Hollow, a person was walking around with the first years of Isandro's life installed behind their eyes. Someone remembered learning to whistle because Isandro had learned to whistle. Someone remembered a dog whose name Isandro could no longer recall—and the dog itself, the actual animal, the wet nose and the bark—existed now only in a stranger's head, in a life that wasn't theirs, attached to a mother who wasn't theirs.

*Sale 2: to client M-207. Purpose: comfort. Terminally ill client requested "a good childhood, any will do." Sold subject's ages six through nine. Client died satisfied, per attending.*

Died satisfied. With her. Inside him. Whatever remained of her sixth birthday, her seventh winter, her ninth summer—it had gone into a dying stranger and been buried with him, unrecoverable, ash in a grave that wasn't hers.

Isandro put the folder down. Her hands were shaking, and she watched them shake with a strange detachment, as if they belonged to one of the hollowed clients she'd seen shuffling out of the purchase rooms, people who had sold away pieces of themselves until too little remained to hold the rest together.

She had always believed she was the guild's exception. The clerk who saw everything and lost nothing. The invisible woman standing safe behind the counter of other people's transactions. And here was the proof, in her mother's backward-slanted hand, that she had been merchandise before she had ever been staff. That the guild had archived her before she'd ever learned to archive. That her invisibility—the thing she'd built her whole self around, the thing she'd told herself was skill and discipline and choice—had been installed in her the way a graft takes, at age nine, when everything before it was cut away.

No wonder she was good at disappearing. She'd been practicing since before she could remember. Literally. Since before she could remember.

* * *

She did not know how long she sat there. Long enough that the lamp burned low and needed trimming; long enough that the fog against the high window went from grey to black. When she finally moved, it was because a sound moved her—a footstep on the stair above, muffled, unhurried. Night staff. Or not.

She closed the folder, returned it to the drawer, relocked the cabinet, and stood in the dark with her heart hammering, listening. The footsteps passed overhead and faded toward the counting-house. Just Vess, probably, the night tally-man, shuffling to his ledgers. Just the building breathing.

But she stayed hidden a moment longer, because something had surfaced in her while she crouched there in the dark, something she needed to look at straight on.

She was angry. Truly, deeply angry—for the first time in eleven years, maybe for the first time ever, if the years before age nine counted as ever, which they didn't, which was the point. And underneath the anger, colder and more dangerous, was a clarity she recognized from her recent nights of rebellion: the same clarity that had made her copy the god-prayer entry instead of burning it. The sense that the ledger was not neutral. That it had never been neutral. That every column she had ever balanced was a wall in a prison, and she had been the warden's favorite clerk because she kept the walls tidy.

Her mother had signed. Freely, with full understanding of terms. Isandro turned that over and over, looking for the traitor in it, and could not find one. That was the cruelest discovery of the night: there was no villain in the story except the arrangement itself. Her mother had done the best available thing with terrible options, and the guild had priced those options, and the world outside the maps had created the need for both. The system had eaten her childhood and called it mercy, and everyone involved had acted reasonably, and the sum of all that reasonableness was a thirty-year-old woman crouching in a dark archive unable to remember her own mother's face from before—

Before.

She stopped.

She could not remember her mother's face from before. From the years of the sale. She remembered her mother now—her hands kneading bread, her voice calling her in from the fog, her slow smile—but those were later memories, age ten and onward, after the transaction. Everything earlier was a smooth blank, a wall painted to look like floor. She had assumed all her life that her early childhood simply hadn't left marks, that she was one of those people whose memory began late. People said that. People comforted themselves with that.

But the file said *complete*. Ages four through nine, complete, pristine. Which meant the memories existed somewhere—or had existed—and the blank inside her wasn't nature. It was surgery.

And if it was surgery, there might be traces. Scars. The guild's own methods left seams; she knew that professionally. Extracted memory left a texture in the mind, a smoothness too perfect, like river stone. And around such smoothness, sometimes, fragments clung—edges that hadn't come free cleanly. A smell. A color. A phrase of song.

She closed her eyes, right there in the dark archive, and did the thing she had trained herself never to do: she reached into her own blankness and felt along its borders.

At first, nothing. The familiar nothing, soft and total, like snowfall.

Then—at the very edge, at the border of the fourth year, where the extraction had begun—a fragment. Not an image. A sensation. Warmth, and the smell of woodsmoke and something baking, and a voice singing low, two syllables repeating, a lullaby in the old language—the language of the country that wasn't, the language the choir sang in the undercity, the language that had survived erasure because it lived in mouths too poor and too stubborn to sell it.

Two syllables. A name, maybe. Her name, maybe—the one her mother had used before the guild's name replaced it, before *Isandro* was assigned like a ration card.

She couldn't hold it. It slipped away even as she grasped, the way dreams slip, leaving only the certainty that it had been there. But certainty was enough. Certainty was everything.

Because it meant the sale hadn't taken everything. It meant something of her had stayed behind, lodged in the seam, too small for the assessors to price. And it meant something else, something that rearranged the whole night's grief into a new shape:

If her childhood could be sold, it could be *bought back*. Somewhere in this vault, or in a client's head, or in a grave outside the city, the five years of Rosalind—whoever Rosalind had been before she became Isandro—still existed. The guild kept meticulous records of its inventory. And Isandro Lockridge was the best clerk the guild had ever employed.

She stood up in the dark. Her legs were steady now. The shaking had stopped, replaced by something harder and quieter, the thing that had been growing in her since the night of the answered prayer: purpose with teeth.

She would find the sales records. She would trace every client who had ever held a piece of her—C-114 with his grafted whistling, M-207 in his grave, and whoever else the appendices named. She would learn what her mother had really paid, and what the guild had really taken, and whether any of it could be returned.

And then she was going to take the stairs up to the third floor, where Guildmaster Corvane kept the master files under his personal seal, and she was going to open the one drawer in this building she had sworn for eleven years never to touch.

The drawer with her mother's name on it.

Because the file had said *seller: M— Lockridge*, abbreviated, anonymized by procedure. But procedure had a weakness, and the weakness was that the guild filed everything, and somewhere in this archive was the complete record of Marisol Lockridge—every trade she'd ever made, every memory she'd ever sold besides her daughter's, every debt, every entry.

Including, Isandro realized as her hand found the banister in the dark, the possibility that her mother hadn't only sold her childhood.

Including the possibility that her mother was still selling.

The lamp guttered. Isandro climbed toward the third floor, into the dark, following the gap in herself all the way up.
