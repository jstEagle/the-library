## 22. The God of Doors

The shrine was not where Sefa had said it would be, which Isandro had come to understand was the smuggler's way of being precise.

"Past the cistern where the fog drinks," Sefa had told her, pressing a stub of chalk into her palm as though handing over something far more dangerous than a writing implement. "There's a stair that goes down twice and comes up once. You'll know the door because it won't be there. That's how you'll find it." She had laughed at Isandro's expression—a dry, cracked sound, like paper folding. "You wanted a god of thresholds, clerk. Did you think he'd make it easy to arrive?"

Now Isandro stood in the drowned quarter of the undercity, where the Hollow's fog pooled thickest and oldest, older than the guild, older perhaps than the erasure itself. The buildings here leaned against each other like tired conspirators. Water ran somewhere beneath the flagstones—she could hear it moving with intent, as if it too were going somewhere forbidden. The air tasted of iron and old prayers.

She had walked past the same archway three times before she admitted what she was doing: circling. Not lost—she could have found her way back to the choir's warren blindfolded—but circling, the way she circled a difficult entry in the ledger, reading it over and over without letting it read *her*.

Because this was different from anything she had done before.

She had stolen memories for the guild. She had copied forbidden entries by candlelight while Corvane slept above her. She had memorized an entire ledger's worth of impossible things—the widow's grief filed under *Seasonal Acquisitions*, the soldier's courage logged as *Bulk Inventory*—all of it held now behind her eyes like a library built inside a burning house. Every one of those acts had been done in secret, in service of something larger than herself, and every one of them she had justified with the same quiet arithmetic: *bookkeeping is not stealing; recording is not taking; I am only the hand that writes.*

But this—walking down a dead street at dusk to ask a question of a god—this could not be filed under anything.

Her hand went, as it always did when frightened, to the ledger-shaped absence at her hip. She still carried a satchel, out of habit, but the ledgers themselves lived in her skull now, in a vault Corvane would burn cities to open. Sometimes she caught herself cataloguing her own fear the way she'd catalogued other people's griefs: *one fear, clerk's variety, moderate condition, provenance: self.*

*Stop,* she told herself. *You didn't come here to inventory yourself.*

She came here because the door out required something she did not yet understand, and because Veyl's face kept surfacing behind her eyes—that terrible honest moment in the harvest house when he had looked at her across the freed memories and asked, not as a magistrate but as a man: *What do you know about my god?*

And because of the shrine.

She found it the way Sefa promised: by the absence. There was a gap between two leaning houses where a building should have been, a doorway-shaped nothing in a wall that otherwise stood intact. No rubble. No scorch marks. Just a clean rectangular absence, as though the world had taken a bite and swallowed.

Isandro stood before it for a long moment, breath fogging, hands cold inside her gloves.

Then she stepped through.

* * *

Inside was outside.

That was her first coherent thought, and it made no sense, so she stopped trying to make sense of it and simply looked. The shrine—if it was a shrine—was a single chamber that seemed to have forgotten its own dimensions. Moonlight fell through a roof that was both ruined and whole, pooling on a floor that was both flooded and dry. Her boots left prints in dust that was also mud that was also marble. The walls bore niches, hundreds of them, and in each niche sat a small object: a key, a coin, a shoe, a letter sealed and yellowed, a child's wooden bird with one wing.

Offerings. Left by hands that had come here asking to pass through something.

At the far end of the chamber, where an altar should have been, there was a door.

It was a plain thing—worse than plain, deliberately unremarkable. Grey wood, iron hinges gone brown with rust, a handle worn smooth by ten thousand palms. It stood free of any wall, framed by nothing, casting no shadow. And it was closed.

"You're late," said a voice.

Isandro did not jump. Years of guild training had burned the flinch out of her, though her heart hammered hard enough that she suspected the god could hear it. She turned toward the sound and found him sitting on the lowest step of a staircase that had not existed a moment ago: an old man in a ferryman's coat, mended at the elbows, with a lantern at his feet that gave off light the color of early morning. His face was unremarkable in the way of faces you've seen a thousand times and never once remembered—porters, gatekeepers, toll-collectors, the man who watched you leave your village and never learned your name.

"You're him," she said. "The threshold-god."

"I'm a door," the old man said mildly. "Doors don't have names. Doors have sides." He patted the step beside him. "Sit, if you like. Standing questions take longer."

She sat. It seemed rude not to. Up close he smelled of rain on hot stone and, faintly, of ink—which startled her more than anything else in this impossible room.

"You've been answering prayers," she said. "In the Hollow. The widow in Lantern Row prayed and you answered, and now the borders are burning and Corvane is harvesting a nation's memory and—" Her voice frayed. She gathered it back. "People are dying of being remembered. Do you know that? Every prayer you answer makes us more visible, and everything visible can be conquered."

The old man listened with his head tilted, the way a door listens to wind.

"Yes," he said finally. "I know."

"Then why?"

"Ah." He reached down and turned the lantern's wick, though the flame needed no adjusting. "That's the wrong first question. Everyone asks why first. Why did you let us be erased. Why did you go silent. Why do you return now, when returning is dangerous. Why, why, why." He shook his head. "Why is a merchant's question. It wants a price. Gods don't deal in why. We deal in *what*." He looked at her then, and his eyes were the grey of a door held halfway open. "So. What will you carry through?"

"What?" Isandro frowned. "Carry where? Through what?"

"There's a door out of the Hollow," the god said. "There has always been exactly one, because a country hidden must have a way of being left, or it becomes a prison, and prisons breed either saints or monsters, usually both. The door opens for gifts. Not bribes—gifts. Things freely given. Your guildmaster will learn this soon, and it will break him, because he has spent a hundred years acquiring and has never once given." He smiled faintly, without humor. "But a gift isn't enough. A door needs a reason to stay open. Otherwise it swings shut behind the last refugee and your country goes back to being a rumor."

"So the door needs—" She thought of Sefa's price, of the passage-knowledge bought with a stolen summer. "Memories. One freely given from everyone who leaves."

"That holds it open. Yes. But someone must carry the country through." He tapped her sternum lightly, one finger, and the touch landed like a stamp on wax. "A ledger of what was lost. A witness who can say: *this happened, these people existed, they loved and feared and sold their children's childhoods to buy shelter, and none of it was nothing.* A weapon can be dropped. A witness cannot. The world beyond the maps will not want you. It erased you once; erasure is easier than guilt. It will try to forget you again within a generation unless something walks out carrying undeniable proof that you were real."

"The ledger," Isandro whispered. "Every memory I recorded. They're keys—they resonate—they can open—"

"They can *test*. Each recorded memory is a small truth, and doors love truths. But hear me, clerk, because this is the part no one ever likes." He leaned close, and up close his ordinary face became very difficult to look at directly, like a page you keep almost-reading. "The door does not open for what you hold. It opens for what you *give*. All of it. Everything you memorized, everything you are, everything you've kept behind your eyes as proof—you'll pour it through the threshold until the last lock turns, and what remains on the far side will be a woman who knows her country's history perfectly and herself hardly at all."

The words went into her like cold water finding cracks.

"And the alternative," she said quietly, "is staying. In a country that's dissolving. With Corvane hollowing it from inside and Veyl hunting whoever carries the answer."

"The alternative is always staying," the god agreed. "Staying is the oldest door there is."

They sat in silence for a moment. Somewhere in the walls, water moved with intent. In the niches, the little wooden bird with one wing caught the morning-colored light and seemed, briefly, to fly.

"My childhood," Isandro said. "Someone took it. I found the record. My mother sold it to buy our place in the Hollow." She had never said it aloud before, and saying it felt like setting down a weight she'd carried so long she'd mistaken it for her spine. "If I give everything to the door—will I give that back? Will I remember her selling me?"

"No." The god's voice was gentle, and gentleness from a god is a terrifying thing. "Given things pass through. They become the door's strength, not yours. On the far side you will know that your mother loved you, because it will be written in the history you carry, but you will not feel it. You'll know your country the way a stranger knows a story they've sworn to tell." He paused. "Unless."

"Unless what?"

"Unless you keep one thing." He raised a finger. "One memory, freely kept—not given, not taken, but *kept*, chosen, held against the current all the way through. One true thing that stays yours. The door will accept it. It must—it was built on the principle that surrender should never be total, or the surrendered become the next conquerors. One kept thing, and the rest given freely." He looked at her. "Choose carefully. Whatever you keep will be the seed the new Isandro grows from. Not your happiest moment—happiness is shallow soil. Not your worst—grief floods. Something in between. Something where you *decided*."

Isandro stared at the closed door at the end of the room.

She thought of the night she had refused Corvane—standing in the vault with his offer hanging in the air like smoke, her own missing childhood held out as bait, and hearing herself say *no* in a voice she hadn't known she owned. She thought of how, afterward, walking out through the guild's corridors with the choir's hooded shapes around her, she had felt—for the first time since childhood, whatever childhood was—like a person rather than a function. Like a hand that had chosen what to write.

"I know what I'll keep," she said.

The god nodded slowly, unsurprised. Gods of thresholds rarely surprise easily.

"Then you understand," he said. "The exit doesn't demand a weapon. It demands a witness. Not a sword carried through the dark, but a lamp. Not an army, but one person who can stand on the far side and refuse to stop saying *we existed.*"

"And the prayers," she said. "The ones you answered. You knew what it would cost us."

"I knew," the god said, "that a country that cannot be remembered cannot be mourned, and a country that cannot be mourned cannot be saved. I answered because someone finally prayed loudly enough to risk it. That is always how gods return—not through power. Through nerve." He rose, joints cracking like a hinge long unused, and picked up his lantern. "Your part comes later. First you must reach the door, and Corvane stands between you and it, drunk now on half a nation's remembering. And the magistrate—" he paused at the top of the stairs that had not existed before, "—is about to make a choice that will decide whether you get there at all."

"Veyl." Her stomach tightened. "What choice?"

The old man smiled, and for one instant his face was every door she had ever walked through, every border, every threshold, every goodbye.

"The one I'm not permitted to spoil," he said. "Go home, Isandro Lockridge. Or rather—go to the warren, wake the choir, and start walking. The door won't wait forever. Doors never do. And take this—" he pressed something into her hand, small and cold and familiar, "—you'll want a key."

She opened her fingers.

In her palm lay a brass ledger-clasp, guild-stamped, the kind that had bound every ledger she'd ever catalogued. She looked up to thank him—

—and was standing alone in the fog-bound street, before a gap in the wall that was only a gap, holding a piece of hardware she had seen ten thousand times and never once, in all her years of careful bookkeeping, thought to wonder about.

Behind her, faint as a prayer, she heard a door close.

She turned toward the choir's warren and began to run.

* * *

The warren was awake when she arrived, and not peacefully.

Candlelight jumped on wet stone. Voices overlapped—Sefa's dry rasp cutting through the panic like a blade through canvas, Mother Ashveil's low hymn-tone trying to anchor it, and underneath both, the sound that had emptied Isandro's stomach on the walk back: distant, rhythmic, coming up through the ground itself.

Drums. No—not drums. Boots. Hundreds of them, marching in the pattern Corvane used for his harvest processions, sweeping district by district through the upper city, gathering the country out of its people's heads.

"He's accelerated," Sefa said when she saw Isandro. The smuggler looked ten years older than she had that morning; there was ash in her hair that hadn't been hers. "Three harvest sites tonight instead of one. The fog's pulling back from the eastern edge—we can *see the far mountains*, Isandro. Nobody alive has seen those mountains. He's not hiding us anymore. He's spending the concealment like coin, buying himself time to finish the great ledger before anyone can reach the door."

"Then we reach it first." Isandro set the brass clasp on the table among the candles, where it gleamed like a small sun. "The god spoke to me. The door out is real. It opens for gifts—one freely given memory from every soul who leaves—and it needs a witness to carry the country through." She looked around the circle of upturned faces: smugglers, choristers, a freed harvest-hand still trembling, old Mother Ashveil with her milky eyes. "I'll carry it. But we need everyone. Every person willing to walk out and give one true thing."

Silence. Then Mother Ashveil began, softly, to sing—and the others joined, and the song was the one from the undercity, the choir of the unremembered, except now it had words Isandro could finally hear clearly:

*We were here. We were here. Carry us. Carry us.*

Sefa studied the clasp a long moment, then nodded once, sharply, a trader sealing a bargain. "Routes. Tonight. I'll get them to the door in strings of twenty—quiet, scattered, the way I move contraband. You—"

A horn split the night. Long, low, guild-made, sounding from the mouth of the warren's only tunnel.

Everyone froze. Sefa's hand went to her knife. "That's a hunt-horn," she breathed. "Someone sold us. Someone—"

Boots in the tunnel. Many. Torches blooming in the dark like terrible flowers. And rising above the panic, cutting through it, a voice Isandro knew better than any scripture:

"Isandro Lockridge." Corvane's voice, amplified by harvested thousands, resonant as a struck vault. "Come out with the ledger in your head, and no one in this room needs to be emptied tonight."

Mother Ashveil's hand found hers in the candle-dark, grip fierce as roots.

"Back wall," the old woman murmured. "Third stone. Sefa's escape. Go, girl. *Witnesses don't die in holes.*"

Isandro looked at the tunnel torchlight, at Sefa already dragging stones aside, at the choir placing themselves between her and the horns like a living hymn—and understood that the god's final warning had already come true.

Somewhere in that torchlit dark, Veyl was making his choice.
