## 23. Corvane's Great Erasure

The harvest house had been a tannery once, back when the Hollow still pretended to have an economy worth the name. Corvane stood in its loft now, among the old curing racks stripped bare and rehung with something no butcher or leatherworker had ever handled: memory, drawn glistening from living minds and wound into spools of pale thread that caught the lamplight like spider silk dipped in milk.

He had chosen the building for its drains. Sentiment was a luxury he had surrendered decades ago, but practicality he kept like a good knife.

Below him, forty of his guildsmen worked the floor in pairs. Each pair attended one seated citizen of the Hollow — a cooper's wife, a ferryman, two brothers who mended nets, an old schoolmaster whose hands would not stop trembling. The extraction itself was gentle work if done properly. A touch at the temple. A question asked softly, the way you'd ask someone to recall where they'd left their keys. *Tell me about the day the maps were burned. Tell me what your mother called this street. Tell me the name of the king we never had.* And the mind, eager to please, offered it up — and the guildsman drew it out through the fingers like drawing thread from a spindle, and coiled it, and labeled it, and hung it on the rack.

Corvane watched the schoolmaster rise from his chair, blink twice at the room as though seeing it for the first time, and ask, politely, whose house this was.

"Yours," said the guildsman assigned to escort him home.

"Oh," said the schoolmaster. "How kind of you to say so."

*There,* Corvane thought, and felt nothing he could name, which was itself a kind of discipline. *That is how it is done. That is how a country is saved.*

He descended the iron stair to walk the floor, because a master who managed by ledger alone deserved the ledgers he got. The lamplight made the memory-threads glow faintly, and as he passed beneath them he could feel them — not hear, exactly; the guild had no word for what it was, though the oldest clerks called it *the hum*. Forty strands of national remembering, each one singing a note too low for ears. Together they made a chord he felt in his teeth. In another month there would be four thousand strands. In three months, every soul in the Hollow.

"You're staring," said Mistress Halden, the senior harvester, without looking up from her labeling. She was seventy and had been pulling memories since before Corvane bought his first ledger. She feared him the way weather fears no one.

"I'm auditing," Corvane said.

"You're staring at the Veyl woman's work." Halden tapped a spool marked in Isandro Lockridge's small, exact hand. "She catalogued these three before she ran. Neatest extraction notes I've seen in thirty years. Shame about her."

"She isn't dead."

"No," Halden agreed placidly. "Shame about that, too, then."

Corvane let the insolence pass, the way he let all things pass that did not obstruct the work. He took the spool from her and turned it in the light. Inside it: a fisherman's memory of standing on the seawall at dawn, thirty years ago, singing a song with no words to it because the words had been traded away before he was born, and knowing — knowing the way you know your own hands — that the song was the last piece of a country that everyone had agreed, gently, kindly, for everyone's sake, to forget.

That was the thing people did not understand about erasure, Corvane thought, winding the spool. Done badly, it was violence. Done well, it was anesthesia. The Hollow had been cut open once, a generation ago, when the empire's armies came over the mountains hungry for a coastline and a harbor and a people too stubborn to kneel. And the guild — his predecessors, his teachers, himself, young then and already keeping books — had offered the only surgery available. *Give us your name,* they had told the crown, the temples, the grandmothers. *Give us your borders, your anthems, your history. We will trade them into the vault, and the empire will find nothing here worth conquering, because there will be nothing here at all. Only fog, and a country that isn't.*

It had worked. It had worked for thirty years. Children had grown up in the Hollow never knowing they were children of anywhere, and the empire's surveyors had charted fog, and the armies had marched past, and everyone had lived.

Everyone had lived. He clung to that arithmetic the way drowning men cling to driftwood, and he knew it was driftwood, and he held on anyway.

And now the gods were coming back, and the arithmetic was burning.

* * *

He found the first border fire report waiting on his desk when he returned to the guildhall that evening, and the second beneath it, and the third pinned under the second's paperweight because his clerks had run out of ways to tell him and had begun simply stacking the news like sandbags against a flood.

The eastern edge of the Hollow, where the fog met the imperial road, had smoldered for two days now. Not fire exactly — the smugglers' reports described it as *lightning caught in wool*, a slow orange glow in the mist where there should have been nothing to glow. Where prayers were being said. Where the choir — Lockridge's choir, the undercity singers, the sentimental fools — had spent generations feeding crumbs of worship to gods the guild had starved, and now those gods, waking, were pressing their palms flat against the inside of the concealment the way a child presses against a window.

Every prayer pushed the fog thinner. Every answered prayer burned a hole in the lie. And through each hole, Corvane knew with the certainty of a man who had read the original contracts, the empire would see in. Not soldiers yet. First the surveyors. Then the cartographers. Then the priests of the imperial faith, who had a doctrine concerning unclaimed lands and the peoples upon them, and the doctrine began with conversion and ended wherever resistance ended.

He had seen the ending once, in a memory the guild had purchased from a dying soldier of the last war. He kept it in the deepest vault, and he looked at it perhaps once a year, the way a physician looks at the disease he fights. A village that had resisted. What the empire's doctrine did with villages that resisted.

So: the choice, laid out on his desk in three reports and a fading contract. Let the concealment fail, and watch the Hollow be remembered by conquerors — remembered as territory, as harvest, as ash. Or complete the erasure. Go deeper than the guild had ever gone. The first bargain had hidden the country's name; the new bargain would hide the country's *self*. Take not just the anthem and the map but the love of the streets, the grief for the dead kings, the stubborn knowledge of being a people. Empty the nation out of the nation, and there would be nothing left for any god to cling to, any prayer to anchor, any empire to covet. Fog, and people who happened to be walking through it.

They would live. That was the whole of his argument, and it needed no other floors built on top of it. They would live, and they would grieve nothing, because grief required a shape and he was removing the shapes.

He had believed it fully, once. He noticed, turning the reports over, that belief now arrived in him the way payments arrived from debtors — late, partial, with excuses attached. He filed the reports anyway.

* * *

The new ledger lay open before him at midnight, and it was beautiful, and he hated it.

He had commissioned it himself from the binders in the Fog Market — Sefa's people, before Sefa's loyalties had shown themselves — bound in grey calfskin, its pages sized to hold a nation. Where the guild's ordinary ledgers recorded transactions, this one would record *contents*: the whole inventory of a country's inner life, itemized, indexed, shelved. He had titled it himself, in his own hand, on the first page, because some things a man must sign to understand:

*Master Ledger of the Hollow. Being a Complete Record of Everything Its People Will No Longer Need to Remember.*

Underneath, after a moment, he had added, smaller: *Entered for safekeeping. To be returned when it is safe.*

He was not certain, anymore, when that would be. He was not certain it was a promise rather than an epitaph. But the smaller line stayed, because striking it out would have meant deciding, and deciding was the one door he could not afford to open tonight.

The entries came up from the harvest houses all night, carried by runners in sealed satchels, and Corvane entered them himself. He trusted no clerk with this book; more to the point, he trusted no clerk to see what he saw in it, page after page, hour after hour, which was not inventory at all.

It was a country, dying in installments, and each installment was lovely.

A baker's memory of her father teaching her to score loaves in the pattern of the old royal crest — entered, indexed, shelved. A stonemason's memory of cutting the name of his village into the lintel of its church, a name that had officially never existed — entered, indexed, shelved. A girl of maybe nine years, harvested early because her family had been flagged as choir-adjacent, and her contribution was the memory of her grandmother's lap and the stories told there, stories of a queen who had bargained with fog, and the girl's own voice preserved somehow in the thread, humming the story-tune under the words — entered, indexed, shelved. Corvane sat with that spool a long time before he could make his pen move.

This was the thing he had not planned for, the flaw in the design no amount of audit could close: that to store a people's remembering, you had to *hold* it. The master ledger did not keep memories inert the way the transaction ledgers kept debts. Memory was alive; it hummed; it wanted witnesses. Every entry he wrote, he wrote from the inside. By the third night he knew the smell of bread scored in a dead king's crest. By the seventh he could sing the wordless song from the seawall. By the tenth he had dreamed, three times, of a queen bargaining with fog, and woken with wet eyes and no idea whose grief he was crying, and had understood with cold clarity that he was becoming the country's archive — that the master ledger was not a book he owned but a book he *was*, and that somewhere in its accumulating pages a self was being assembled that was not his.

He wrote on. What else was there? Retreat meant fire and empire. Halting meant holes in the fog and Veyl finding the choir and the choir finding the gods and the gods finding the empire. There was no version of tomorrow in which the Hollow survived intact; there was only the question of what shape its surviving took, and he had chosen the shape, and a man who chooses must carry.

*We are the mercy,* he told the empty hall, the way his own teacher had told him, thirty years ago, over the first contract. *No one else will be.*

* * *

On the twelfth day, he walked the streets to see the result.

He went without escort, in a plain coat, because he wanted the truth undressed. And the truth walked beside him everywhere, wearing his country's face and none of its history.

At the fountain in Tanner's Square, three women stood talking — he knew two of them by sight, had processed their guild dues for years — and their conversation was the conversation of strangers making polite weather. *Cold morning. Yes, colder than yesterday. Do you know when the market opens? I thought I knew. I thought I used to know everything here.* One of them laughed at herself, a small lost sound, and touched the fountain's rim as though it might remind her why she'd come to it, and it didn't, and she smiled anyway, because the Hollow's people had always been gentle, and gentleness apparently outlasted memory the way a scar outlasts the wound.

In the chandlers' row, a man stood outside his own shop, reading his own sign, mouthing the name of his own trade as though confirming it with a stranger's testimony. *Candles,* he seemed to conclude. *I suppose I make candles.* He went inside. Through the window Corvane watched him stand amid his own wares with the expression of a man left in a room by friends who had promised to return.

Children still played, because play needs no past. That was almost bearable. What was not bearable — what he made himself look at, because he had decided long ago that a man who orders a thing must witness it — was the old ones. An ancient woman on a bench by the fish market, holding a knitted sock, turning it over, turning it over, searching it for a name. She had been harvested three days ago; the sock had not. She held it the way you hold the last object from a dream. Once she looked up at Corvane passing, and for a moment — he would swear this later, to no one, in the privacy of his own guilt — her eyes focused, and she said, "You were in the stories. The man who keeps the book." And then the focus slid away like water off glass, and she went back to turning the sock, and Corvane walked on with his spine straight and his hands very quiet in his pockets.

*They are safe,* he reminded himself, street by street, face by face. *Safe, and unafraid, and free of a history that was killing them.* The fog above the rooftops was thicker than it had been in years — he could see that much working, the concealment drinking deep again now that the prayers had less to grip. Two of the border fires had guttered out entirely since the harvests began. The arithmetic held. The arithmetic always held.

But the city was quiet in a way it had never been quiet, even in the worst years of the pretense. Before, the silence had been a performance — ten thousand people agreeing not to sing, each of them hearing the unsung song anyway, the whole nation humming under its breath. Now the humming had stopped. Now the silence was just silence, and Corvane, who had engineered it, who had signed for it, walked through it and heard what he had made, and what he had made was a country-shaped hole with people in it, going about their days like guests in a house whose owner had died.

He reached the guildhall steps as the lamps were lit, and stopped, because the door was wrong.

Not broken. Not forced. Simply — posted. A single sheet of paper, nailed at eye level, written in the small exact hand he had audited across twenty years of flawless ledgers:

*To Guildmaster Corvane. You are storing what cannot be stored and selling what was never yours. The ledger remembers even when the people can't. Come to the shrine below the tannery and see what your master ledger is dreaming. — A Clerk*

Below the signature, in the same hand, someone had copied out — accurately, entry for entry, index for index — the first page of his master ledger. His title. His smaller line. *To be returned when it is safe.*

Someone had read his promise. Someone was holding it over him like a blade, or like a hand.

Corvane stood on his own steps a long time, while behind him the hollowed city murmured its stranger's courtesies, and the fog thickened overhead like a lid settling closed, and somewhere below the tannery, in a shrine full of people who still remembered being a nation, his own handwriting waited for him in the dark.

He went down to fetch it.
