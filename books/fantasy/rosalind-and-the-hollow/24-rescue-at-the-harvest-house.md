## 24. Rescue at the Harvest House

The harvest house had once been a tannery, and it still smelled of it—brine and lime and something older underneath, the sour ghost of a trade that had died with the country's name. Sefa crouched beside Isandro in the reed-choked ditch behind the building, her breath fogging in the grey air, and pointed at the drainage grate with two fingers.

"Servants' way," she murmured. "Corvane's people think in front doors. Front doors are for magistrates and ledgers. Nobody who steals souls bothers with drains."

Isandro pressed her palm flat against the cold mud and tried to slow her heart. Three nights since the burning of the public ledgers. Two nights since she had stood in the choir's undercity sanctuary and watched ordinary faces—faces that had never seen her, that owed her nothing—decide to shelter her anyway. One night since Sefa had spread a stolen map of the Hollow across a smuggler's table and traced, with a tar-stained finger, the places where Corvane's harvesters worked.

*Harvest houses*, the smugglers called them. There were four. This was the nearest: a low brick sprawl on the fog-eaten edge of the Tanner's Quarter, where the guild's men brought the people whose memories of nationhood were being pulled out like teeth.

"They take them in the evening," Sefa said. "Whole families sometimes. They tell them it's a census. A registration for rations." Her mouth twisted. "People believe it because they want to believe someone is still keeping lists. Someone still knows their names."

"I know their names," Isandro said quietly. "I wrote them down for eleven years."

Sefa looked at her sidelong. In the fog-light her face was all angles and old salt-scars, and her eyes held the particular patience of a woman who had spent decades guarding one memory the way other people guard children. "Then you know what's in there better than the thieves do. Good. That's why I brought you instead of leaving you to hide like a sensible person."

Through the fog came a sound that made Isandro's stomach clench: not screaming, nothing so honest as screaming, but a low, rhythmic hum—the sound of the extraction engines the guild had built from loom-machinery and worse things, machines that sang while they unspooled a life. She had catalogued the takings of those machines for years without ever hearing one run. The ledger only showed the entries. It never showed the hands that shook afterward.

"Two guards at the drain gate inside," Sefa said. "Four walking the perimeter, but they circle wide—they don't like the smell. The harvester-priests work the upper floor. The ones being harvested—" she hesitated, and for the first time since Isandro had known her, the smuggler seemed to search for words, "—the ones being harvested sit in the vats. Chairs, really. Brass chairs with needles. And when it's done they walk out on their own feet, smiling, and they don't know their own street anymore. They don't know why the fog feels like home. They're still *here*, Wren—" she caught herself, corrected, "—Isandro. But they're not *of* here. That's the cruelty of it. Corvane doesn't kill anyone. He just makes them strangers in the place they were born."

Isandro thought of chapter after chapter of neat entries. *One summer, freely sold. One grief, purchased at standard rate.* She had believed, once, that bookkeeping was neutral—that ink did no harm, that recording a theft was different from committing one. She had spent eleven years learning how wrong a careful person could be.

"Then we put them back," she said.

* * *

The drain was exactly as narrow and exactly as foul as Sefa had promised. They came up through a floor hatch into a room stacked with empty brass chairs, each one fitted with its crown of fine needles, each one waiting with the terrible patience of furniture built for suffering. Isandro counted forty-two chairs and felt sick. Forty-two seats meant throughput. Meant schedule. Meant that somewhere above them, a clerk—not unlike her, God forgive them both—was logging the day's appointments.

Sefa moved through the dark like smoke finding cracks. At the stairwell door she paused, pressed her ear to the wood, then drew a short knife and a coil of thin cord from her coat. "Guards change at the bell," she whispered. "We have the length of one bell. After that, every harvester in the building comes down those stairs, and we'd best be gone or be ghosts."

"And if we can't free them all by the bell?"

Sefa's jaw tightened. "Then we free some, and we come back for the rest. That's what smuggling is, girl. You never save everyone. You just refuse to save no one."

They went up.

The harvesting floor was worse than Isandro had imagined, and she had imagined it often enough in the last weeks to flatter herself that nothing could surprise her. Long rows of occupied chairs beneath hanging lamps of green glass. The humming machines, their spools turning slowly, winding onto great glass cylinders the pale substance the guild called *essence* and Isandro had always privately called by its true name: *lives*. Men and women sat in the chairs with their eyes open, and their lips moved, and though no sound came, Isandro understood they were speaking—naming streets, reciting prayers, telling their children's names over and over to whatever part of themselves still listened.

Six harvester-priests in grey robes moved among the chairs with ledger-clips, checking spools, adjusting dials. None of them looked at the faces in the chairs. That was the first thing you learned in the guild, Isandro thought. Never look at the face. Look at the entry.

She looked at the faces.

In the third row sat a baker she knew—knew of, rather; she had entered his quarterly tithe a hundred times. Oskar Venn, whose bread had fed half the Tanner's Quarter, whose wife had died in the year of the bad floods, whose grief Isandro had once catalogued when he sold a season of mourning to pay his rent. He sat in the chair with his hands open on his knees and his lips moving, and Isandro read the shape of the words without hearing them: *Elm Street. Elm Street. My shop is on Elm Street.*

Her fury arrived so suddenly and so completely that it passed through anger into something calm and crystalline, the way deep water is calm. She had carried the copied ledger in her head for days now, memorized in the burning of the archives, eleven years of entries laid end to end like a road. And she had begun, in the choir's sanctuary, to understand what the ledger truly was—not a record of thefts but a ring of keys, every recorded memory resonating like a lock waiting for its door.

She didn't need the whole ledger now. She needed one page.

She closed her eyes and found it: *Oskar Venn, tanner's quarter, autumn of the floods. Sold: one season of grief, standard rate.* The entry rose in her mind with the texture of the paper it had been written on, the scratch of her own pen, the exact weight of the widow's—no, the widower's—sorrow as it had passed through the guild's scales. And around that remembered entry, the air itself began to hum in sympathy, the way a struck string wakes its neighbors.

"What are you doing?" Sefa breathed.

"Bookkeeping," Isandro said.

She walked out from behind the machinery rack into the open floor, and before any priest could cry out, she spoke the entry aloud—every word, every figure, in the flat clear cadence of a guild clerk reading an account. The effect was immediate and strange: the nearest machine shuddered, its spool stuttering, and the glass cylinder mounted above Oskar Venn's chair cracked a fine white line from rim to rim.

The priests turned. One reached for an alarm-cord. Sefa's thrown knife pinned his sleeve to the frame of a loom-machine before his fingers closed, and then she was among them, and Isandro stopped watching her because she had work of her own.

She went to Oskar Venn's chair and took his face in her hands—an old man's stubbled cheeks, wet without his knowing—and she read to him. Not the entry this time, but the memory behind it, reconstructed from everything the ledger held: the flood year, the water in his shop, the coffin he could not afford until he sold his sorrow, the bread he had baked every morning since because his wife had loved the smell of it rising. She read it the way she had once written it down, precisely, completely, holding nothing back.

And the memory went home.

It went home the way water goes into cracked earth. Oskar Venn gasped—a drowning man's gasp—and his hands closed on her wrists with shocking strength, and he said, in a voice scraped raw: "Elm Street. My shop is on Elm Street. Who—" His eyes focused. Filled. "Who are you?"

"A clerk," Isandro said, and her voice broke on the word, and she found she didn't mind. "Formerly."

Then the bell began to ring.

* * *

What followed, Isandro would remember—would carry, when so much else was taken from her—as the first hour of her life in which she was entirely certain of what she was doing.

Sefa cut the alarm-cord's twin and shouted for the freed ones to move, and move they did: Oskar Venn hauling a younger man out of the next chair even before his own legs were steady, a woman named Petra whose harvested memories Isandro restored by shouting her entry across the room like a market-cry, an old couple who had been taken together and who woke in their chairs reaching for each other across the aisle. Each restoration cost Isandro something—a draining at the temple, a momentary greying of the world, as if each returned memory passed through her on its way home—but she paid it gladly, entry after entry, her voice growing hoarse, the ledger in her head opening its pages one by one.

The harvester-priests fled or were trussed with Sefa's cord. The perimeter guards, hearing the bell and the shouting, came running to the front of the building—which was, Isandro realized with a wild respect for the smuggler's cunning, exactly what Sefa wanted. While the guards crowded the front doors, the freed and their rescuers poured out the back, through the chair-room, down into the drain, forty-odd hollowed people made whole again stumbling through filth toward the fog.

Not all of them made it out before the guards circled round. Isandro, last but one at the drain mouth, looked back and saw torchlight flooding the harvesting floor, and heard a voice she knew—cold, carrying, unmistakable—call for order.

Magistrate Veyl stood in the doorway with six soldiers behind him, and his god-touched eyes swept the room and fixed on hers across the whole length of it, and Isandro felt the look like a hand closing around her throat.

He had come to stop them. He had been ordered to stop them. Every law he served, every doctrine he had crossed an impossible border on the strength of, demanded that he seize the thief Isandro Lockridge and the smuggler and the seditionists and drag them to Corvane in chains.

And Magistrate Veyl looked—at the empty chairs, at the cracked glass cylinders with their stolen lives spilled and wasted on the floor, at the old couple clinging to each other in the drain mouth, at Isandro with her mud-stained hands still shaped from holding a stranger's face—and he stepped aside.

He stepped aside. He turned his soldiers with a gesture, turned his own body, gave them the front of the building and his back, and said in a voice pitched to carry no further than the doorway: "The trail leads east. Move."

For one heartbeat his eyes met Isandro's again, and there was something in them she had no entry for—something between shame and hunger, a man looking at evidence against his own faith. Then the torches swept away eastward, and the drain swallowed them all.

* * *

The choir's sanctuary received them with lamp-light and song and hands. Isandro stood dripping at the tunnel's mouth and watched hollowed people become people: Oskar Venn weeping into a bowl of broth because the broth smelled of cardamom and his wife had put cardamom in everything; Petra laughing and then sobbing and then laughing again as her daughter ran to her; the old couple refusing to be parted even to sit down. Around them the choristers moved with the practiced tenderness of people who had waited generations for something to hope for.

Sefa lowered herself onto a bench beside Isandro, produced a flask, and drank deeply before offering it.

"You knew he'd let us go," Isandro said. "At the drain. You planned the route past him."

"I planned the route past the *guards*. What Veyl does is between Veyl and his god." Sefa passed her the flask. "But I've watched that man for weeks, little clerk. A hunter who prays that hard is a hunter looking for a reason to stop. Tonight we gave him one." She glanced sideways. "You gave him one. He looked at you and saw whatever it is his doctrine can't explain."

Isandro drank. The liquor burned, and she welcomed it. Her head ached from the passages—eleven restorations, eleven tolls paid out of her own reserves—and somewhere behind her eyes she could feel small blank spaces forming, soft-edged absences where used-up entries had been. She had known the price when she started. She would count it later.

Oskar Venn approached them, steadied by two choristers, and knelt—actually knelt—in the muck before her. Isandro reached down and hauled him up before he could speak.

"Don't," she said. "Please. I kept the books that robbed you. Tonight only balanced one page."

"The books kept my name," the baker said simply. "That's more than the fog ever did." He gripped her hands. "There are others. The harvest house on Salt Lane—it's bigger. My brother was taken there three days ago. He'll sit in one of those chairs tonight unless—"

"Unless," Isandro agreed.

Sefa groaned theatrically and stood, rolling her shoulders. "Salt Lane has eight guards and a priest-captain and a cellar full of the new machines, the fast ones." She looked at Isandro, and beneath the smuggler's dryness was a fierce, bright thing that might have been joy. "Well, little clerk? How many pages have you got left?"

Isandro closed her eyes and walked the ledger in her head, corridor upon corridor of entries, thousands of doors, and felt the truth of it settle over her like a mantle: she was the archive now. Every theft she had ever recorded was a key, and every key could give something back.

"All of them," she said. "Every page I ever wrote."

From the tunnel mouth behind them came footsteps—quick, uneven, frightened. A chorister boy burst in, breathless, mud to his knees.

"The Salt Lane house," he panted. "They're moving everyone tonight—all of them, carts, going to the vault. Corvane's ordered a great harvesting. They say—" he swallowed— "they say he means to take *everyone*. The whole city. Starting tomorrow."

The song in the sanctuary faltered, note by note, like candles going out.

Isandro looked at Sefa. Sefa looked at the boy. And somewhere far off, beyond the fog, beyond the borders that were already smoldering, Isandro thought she heard—or imagined she heard—a door, very patiently, beginning to open.
