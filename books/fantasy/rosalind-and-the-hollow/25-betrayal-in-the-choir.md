## 25. Betrayal in the Choir

The undercity smelled of wet stone and candle tallow and, faintly, of honey — because the choir kept bees down there, in the old cisterns where no magistrate had ever thought to look. Isandro had learned to love that smell in the weeks since the burning of the ledgers. It was the smell of being seen.

She sat on the third step of the flooded stair with the ledger open across her knees — not the guild's ledger, which was ash now, but the one that lived behind her eyes, the memorized archive of every theft she had ever recorded. Around her, in the great vaulted dark of the cistern, forty people sang.

They were not singing anything grand. That was the point. The choir of the Unremembered had kept their gods alive for six generations not with hymns but with small songs: lullabies, work chants, the counting rhymes children used to skip rope to in a country that no longer appeared on maps. Each song carried a name inside it, folded like a seed. *Old Vessel at the crossing, keep the door. Grey Mother of the fog, keep the breath.* The gods lived in the folds, and as long as someone sang the folds aloud, the gods did not quite die.

Isandro did not have a good voice. She sang anyway, quietly, her finger moving along the ledger-lines in her mind as if she could take dictation from the melody itself. She could not record these songs — they were given freely, never stolen, and there was a difference she understood now down to the marrow of her. But she could hold them. Holding was its own kind of keeping.

"You're flat again," said Sefa beside her, not unkindly. The smuggler sat wrapped in a coat that had once been worth more than a house, mending a net with hands that smelled permanently of tar. "The Grey Mother will think you're mocking her."

"The Grey Mother has heard worse from me."

"True." Sefa bit her thread. "She told me so herself."

Isandro almost smiled. Almost. She had been almost-smiling for three weeks now, ever since the raid on the harvest house, ever since Veyl had looked away at the crucial moment and let forty freed memories pour back into their owners like water finding its level. Three weeks of hiding in the undercity, of moving between choir houses before dawn, of teaching the singers what she knew about doors and keys and the terrible arithmetic of freely given things. Three weeks of feeling, for the first time since childhood — since the gap, since the sold years — something that might have been home.

And underneath it all, the other thing. The thing she had stopped telling even Sefa about.

She was being watched. Not by Corvane's harvesters, who moved through the upper city in their grey coats with their grey instruments, taking memories by force from anyone too slow to run. This watching was smaller. Warmer. Wrong.

It was the way Emeric looked at her.

* * *

Emeric had been in the choir longer than anyone except old Mother Ilsa. He was a broad, gentle man who had been a bridge-warden before the erasure and was now nothing official at all, because his profession had been erased along with everything else. He had two children — twins, nine years old, with their mother's dark curls and their father's habit of listening more than speaking. Their names were Pia and Anselm, and Isandro knew this because Emeric talked about them the way other men talked about saints.

He also sang the loudest. When the choir came to the line about the Old Vessel keeping the door, Emeric's voice carried the name like a man carrying water across a fireline — carefully, desperately, spilling nothing.

So when Isandro noticed him watching her during the singing, she told herself it was devotion. When she noticed him watching her while she ate, she told herself it was curiosity about the woman who had defied the Guildmaster. When she noticed him slip out of the cistern during the second verse and not return until the last, she told herself he had gone to check on his children, which was where Emeric went whenever he could, because the twins were lodged two levels up with a foster-singer named Breda, and a father's heart is a compass that always points the same direction.

She was a bookkeeper. She knew what it meant when the numbers refused to add up no matter how many times you ran them. And still she ran them again, because the alternative sum was unbearable.

* * *

She confronted him on the fourth night, in the corridor of dripping brick between the cistern and the choir's sleeping loft. He was coming back late again, lantern low, and she stepped out of an alcove and said his name, and he flinched the way guilty men flinch.

"Emeric. Where do you go?"

"Nowhere. Rounds. Someone must check the drains."

"In the middle of a hymn?"

He looked at her then, and she saw it plainly at last, the way you see a crack in a wall only when the light crosses it exactly wrong. Not malice. Nothing so clean as malice. What she saw was fear wearing love like a mask it had outgrown — fear so large it had eaten the shape of the man around it, leaving only the father and the terror, fused.

"You've been talking to someone," Isandro said quietly. "Someone from above."

"Don't." His voice cracked. "Don't ask me that. Please don't ask me that, clerk, because I don't want to lie to you."

"Who?"

"They know my children's names." The words came out of him like blood from a wound, fast and unstoppable now that the cut was made. "Do you understand? They came to the house. Not grey coats — a polite man, a soft-spoken man, with a ledger of his own, and he read out Pia's name and Anselm's name and the name of the street where Breda keeps them, and he said — he said the Guildmaster only wants to talk. He said nobody needs to be hollowed. He said—" Emeric's great hands opened and closed, opened and closed, around nothing. "He said the twins would grow up in a quiet country. No fires on the borders. No magistrates. No gods waking up and burning holes in the fog. Just quiet. He said all we have to do is tell him where the woman sleeps."

The drip of water filled the silence between them, patient as centuries.

"And you told him," Isandro said.

Emeric sank against the wet brick as if his bones had been taken out. "Not yet. Not yet. I told him I needed a night to think. One night. He gave me one night because he is *polite*, Isandro, that is the worst of it, he let me keep one whole night of choosing, as if that were mercy—" He looked up at her, and his eyes were the eyes of a drowning man who has just realized the person he must push under to float is someone he loves. "I can't lose them. You don't have children. You can't — no one can understand it who hasn't stood in a room where your whole heart is asleep in two small beds and known that everything you did, every secret you kept, every god you sang to, might be the thing that brings the soldiers to that door—"

"I had a mother," Isandro said. "She sold my childhood to buy our place in the Hollow. So I understand better than you think. And I am telling you, Emeric, as someone who has read every kind of bargain this country makes: it will not stop at your children. There is no version of this where Corvane takes your information, thanks you kindly, and leaves Pia and Anselm in peace. He will take your memory of giving it. Then he will take your memory of why you'd want it back. Then he will come for the choir, and he will need someone inside it, and he already knows you'll trade, and next time the price will be Breda, and after that it will be you."

"I know." A whisper. "Gods keep me, I know."

"Then don't do it."

"I haven't done it!" He pushed off the wall, suddenly fierce, suddenly weeping without any change in his face, tears simply falling from a face that had gone rigid with resolve. "But I will. Tomorrow. Tomorrow at the market bell I will tell him, because if it comes to choosing between a country that was erased before my children were born and my children themselves, I choose my children, and I will burn in whatever hell the Grey Mother keeps for traitors, and I will deserve it, and I will do it anyway."

Isandro had faced Corvane in the vault. She had faced Veyl's handcuffs and the extraction chair and the slow unwriting of everything she was. None of it had hurt the way this plain sentence hurt, spoken by a good man in a dripping corridor.

Because he was right. That was the unbearable part. Put a knife between any parent in the choir and their child, and the choir would sell her out to the last singer. Love was not loyalty. Love was older and hungrier than loyalty, and it did not care about nations or gods or the memory of a world.

"Then go," she said. "Go to your children tonight. All of you — Pia and Anselm and Breda. Get below the salt line, get to Sefa's boat, get anywhere that isn't here. If you run tonight, there's nothing left to sell."

"He'll know I warned you. He'll—"

"He'll do what he does. But he won't get it from you, Emeric. Whatever happens tomorrow, make him take it. Don't hand it over. Make him spend something to get it. That's the only law I ever learned that held." She touched his shoulder, once, the way you touch a door you are closing. "Go."

He went. She heard his boots fade up the stair, faster and faster, a man running toward the only altar he had ever truly served.

Then Isandro stood alone in the dark and did the arithmetic she had been avoiding for four days, and the sum was simple, and it was this: by tomorrow's market bell, Corvane would know where she slept.

* * *

She woke Sefa first.

The smuggler listened without interrupting, which was unlike her, and when Isandro finished, Sefa said only, "How long have you known?"

"Four days. I didn't want it to be true."

"Bookkeepers." Sefa spat into the cistern water, but gently, an old habit of respect toward the drowned. "You people will audit yourselves to death. We move tonight, then. Everyone. The salt line, the boat, the far channel. I've been holding passage-knowledge in reserve for exactly this—"

"No."

Sefa turned. In the lantern light her face was all angles and disbelief. "*No?*"

"If we run, he follows. He always follows — that's what harvesters are for. And the choir can't outrun him carrying the old ones and the children and the shrine-stones. Some of them won't make the boat. And the ones who stay—" Isandro closed her eyes. Behind them, the ledger turned its pages, patient, complete. "Corvane doesn't actually want me, Sefa. He wants what's in my head. Forty years of entries. Every memory the guild ever recorded, memorized, indexed. The only complete copy of the keys in existence. As long as I'm running, he chases me through the dark and takes pieces of everyone in his way. But if I'm standing still somewhere he can reach me—"

"You are not walking into that vault."

"I walked out of it once. I can walk in again." She opened her eyes. "Besides. I'm not going to the vault."

"Then where in the fog are you going?"

"To see a polite man about a ledger of his own." Isandro folded her hands, and for a moment she felt the old clerk's calm settle over her like a familiar coat — the calm of a woman who knows exactly which column every number belongs in. "Emeric said Corvane sent someone soft-spoken. Someone who reads names out of a book and offers quiet countries. That's not a harvester. Harvesters don't negotiate. That's a buyer. And a buyer, Sefa, has to carry his purchase list with him."

Understanding dawned slowly across the smuggler's weathered face, followed immediately by horror. "You want to steal from the Guildmaster's own clerk."

"I want to *read* him. There's a difference. Stealing is taking what isn't yours." Isandro picked up the lantern. Somewhere above them, faint through forty feet of stone, the morning bells of the false city began to count toward market. "What I'm going to do is find out exactly what Corvane thinks he's buying, and how many hours we have, and whether Emeric's children are already on someone's list. Because if they are—" her voice did not shake, but it went very quiet, the quiet of a ledger balancing to zero, "—then the betrayal cost more than he was promised, and I intend to put that in writing."

* * *

The fog market at dawn was a gray confusion of tarps and breath, buyers huddled at stalls of impossible goods, and in the corner near the old customs house — where no customs had been collected in a generation, because the country collecting them officially did not exist — stood a narrow man in a good coat, holding a slim black ledger against his chest the way Emeric held his children's names.

He saw her coming. Of course he did; Corvane's people always saw her coming; that was the whole architecture of the trap. He smiled politely and did not run, and Isandro understood, with the cold clarity of the final column, that she had not come to catch him at all.

She had come because he had been waiting for her. Because the trap had never been for Emeric.

"Madam Lockridge," the soft-spoken man said, opening his ledger to a marked page. "The Guildmaster hoped you'd come. He has a message, and a price, and — forgive me — a deadline." He glanced up at the fading bells, and his smile deepened by one precise degree. "You have until noon."

Behind her, at the mouth of the market, the fog began to burn gold — and through it, unhurried, came the sound of grey boots, dozens of them, closing from every street at once.
