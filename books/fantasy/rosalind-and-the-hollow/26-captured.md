## 26. Captured

They took her in the choir's own sanctuary, which was the cruelest part of it. Isandro had known, from the moment Tam's chair stood empty at evening song—from the way no one would meet her eyes, and the candles had been trimmed too neatly, the way a house is tidied before mourners arrive—that the betrayal was already done. She could have run. Sefa had taught her three ways out of the undercity that did not exist on any map, and her legs were young enough to use them.

She stayed because the oldest woman in the choir, Maren, who had kept the god of thresholds alive with a whispered name through sixty years of silence, took her hand and said, "Whatever they take from you, girl, they cannot take what you gave away freely. That's not how it works. That has never been how it works."

Isandro did not understand her then. She understood it later, in the vault, when understanding was the only thing left to hold.

They came at the hour when the fog thins and even the Hollow pretends to be a real place. Not soldiers—Corvane never wasted soldiers on things he could do with paper. They came as clerks. Four of them in grey coats with the guild's brass pin at the collar, carrying an empty ledger bound in white calfskin, and behind them walked Corvane himself, unhurried, his hands folded at his back like a man arriving to inspect a granary.

"Isandro," he said, in the voice he used for balancing accounts. "You've been careless."

"I've been careful my whole life," she said. "It's the country that's been careless. It let you grow up in it."

Something moved behind his eyes—not anger; she had learned long ago that Corvane did not permit himself anger, because anger was an expense. Something more like regret, entered neatly into a column and totaled.

"The magistrate is closing on us from the east. The borders are burning. In a week—perhaps less—there will be nothing here to protect." He gestured, and the clerks fanned out with the terrible gentleness of men who have done this before. "I am offering you one last chance to do your duty. The ledger you carry in your head. Every entry. You will surrender it, and I will see that you are remembered kindly."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then we will take it anyway, and you will not remember refusing. You will not remember much of anything." He said it without heat, the way he might describe weather. "You have always mistaken bookkeeping for neutrality, Isandro. Let me correct the error while there is still time. There is no neutral column. There never was."

* * *

The extraction room had been the guild's counting hall once, back when the trade was small enough to count by lamplight. Now it held a single chair of black iron, and around it a ring of copper basins filled with something that was not water—it caught the light and held it too long, the way memories do—and above the chair, suspended on wires fine as spider silk, hung the white ledger, open to its first blank page.

They did not bind her. That was the obscenity of it. Corvane simply said, "Sit," in the tone he had used across ten years of ledgers, *initial here, Isandro, witness here, Isandro*, and her body sat, because obedience had been built into her the way mortar is built into a wall.

The first clerk dipped two fingers in the basin and touched them to her temple.

The memory of the widow's grief went first.

Isandro felt it leave the way you feel a tooth leave: a pressure, a crack, a strange lightness where weight had lived so long she had forgotten it was weight. Entry four thousand and eleven. A widow in the Salt Quarter who had sold her husband's dying words because she could not afford both bread and remembering them. Isandro had recorded it in her careful clerk's hand, had noted the texture of it—the way grief smells of cold iron when it is freshly taken—and had told herself, as she told herself every night, *bookkeeping is not stealing.*

Then it was gone. Not the record—the record remained, transcribed onto the white page in ink that wrote itself, curling like smoke settling. What was gone was the knowing. She reached for the memory of writing it down and found a hole shaped exactly like a widow's voice saying *he asked for me at the end, he asked for me,* and the hole had edges, and the edges were sharp.

"That one cost you," Corvane observed, watching her face. "Good. Pain means it's taking properly."

"You're unwriting me," she said. Her voice sounded far away, a stranger's voice. "Entry by entry. You're not extracting a ledger. You're erasing the only person who ever read it honestly."

"I am saving a country," Corvane said, "the only way it can be saved. You'll understand that soon. Or rather—you won't, and neither will anyone else, and that is precisely the mercy of it."

* * *

They worked through the night.

That is the sentence Isandro would have written, if she could still have written anything: *they worked through the night.* But the night stopped being a thing she possessed. Time became a series of extractions with gaps between them, and the gaps grew, and she learned that a person is not a continuous thing at all but a stack of moments, and that you can remove moments from the middle of a life the way you can remove bricks from the middle of a wall, and the wall stands, and calls itself whole, and does not know what it has lost.

The soldier's courage went. Entry nine hundred and six—a young man who sold his bravery before a battle he could not have survived brave, and lived, and hated himself for it ever after. Isandro had recorded the taste of it, tin and thunder. Gone. She felt herself become someone who had never known what courage smelled like.

Her mother's kitchen went. Not a guild entry—her own memory, filed among them because she had memorized the ledger entire and the ledger had grown roots into everything she was. She felt the smell of cardamom lift out of her like steam off a cup, and afterward there was a room in her past with no walls and no table and a shape standing in it that she could not name, and she found she was crying without knowing why, and the clerk noted *subject exhibits residual affect, non-specific,* in the margin beside her mother.

"Residual affect," she repeated, laughing, or trying to. "Write that on my grave. *Non-specific.*"

Corvane leaned close. He had been present for all of it, hour upon hour, and his patience was the most frightening thing in the room—more frightening than the basins, more than the wires. "Do you know what I admire about you?" he said quietly. "You catalogued ten thousand stolen things and never once stole for yourself. I intend to honor that. When this is finished, you will be the cleanest soul in the Hollow. Blank paper. And blank paper, Isandro, can be written on."

"I'd rather be burned," she said.

"Burned paper is still legible at the edges." He straightened. "Continue."

* * *

Somewhere past what should have been dawn—they took her sense of dawn somewhere around the fourth hour—she began to fight. Not with her body; her body belonged to the chair now, had perhaps always belonged to chairs. She fought by holding on.

There is a trick archivists know that no one teaches thieves: to keep a thing, you must stop storing it and start *being* it. So as each clerk's fingers touched her temple, Isandro seized the coming loss and poured herself into it. When they took the Fog Market—entry twelve hundred and forty, the smell of eels and lamp-oil and Sefa's laugh like a knife being sharpened—she became the market for the length of a heartbeat, was the fog and the haggling and the stolen summer she had paid Sefa with, and for one heartbeat the basin steamed and the clerk recoiled, his nose bleeding.

"It's resisting," he said.

"It's a person," Corvane said. "Continue."

But she had learned something in that heartbeat, and she hoarded the learning the way she had once hoarded entries: *what you give, they cannot take.* Maren's voice, from the sanctuary, surfacing through the flood. *Whatever they take, they cannot take what you gave away freely.* She did not understand it yet. She wrapped the not-understanding around herself like a coat and kept giving—giving her attention, her witness, her terrible perfect recall—to every memory as it left, blessing each one out the door like a priestess of some order that had never existed until that night.

The choir's singing went. She held the hymn in her mouth as they pulled it, felt the melody tear free like a root tearing from soil, and in the last instant before it left she sang it—gave it, freely, to the room, to Corvane, to the blank-eyed clerks—and the copper basins rang like bells, all of them, unstruck, and the white ledger's pages fluttered though there was no wind.

Corvane looked at the basins for a long moment.

"Note an anomaly," he said finally. "Freely relinquished material resists transcription. We'll need to develop a method." His pen scratched. Somewhere beneath the ruin of her, Isandro felt a fierce, wordless joy, because he had just admitted it in front of witnesses: *freely given things do not belong to him.* He was harvesting a nation and the nation was slipping through his fingers one gift at a time, and he did not yet know it, and she would not live long enough—or remember long enough—to tell him.

But the door knew. Whatever waited beyond the maps, whatever threshold-god Maren's people had whispered to for sixty years—it knew.

* * *

Near the end, they took her name.

Not Rosalind—she had not been Rosalind since childhood, since the gap, since the sale she had found in the guild records with her own childish handwriting cited as collateral. They took *Isandro.* Entry zero, the first line of the first ledger she had ever kept: *my name, so that I do not forget I am someone.* She had written it at fourteen, the night she signed on with the guild, believing that a person who records everything cannot be erased.

The clerk's fingers touched her temple. She felt the name loosen.

And in the space between heartbeats, she did the last arithmetic available to her. Almost empty now. The ledger nearly transcribed. Corvane standing over her with his columns balanced, his mercy entered, his country saved. One memory left that mattered—one she had guarded through the whole long night, had refused to file, had kept out of the ledger entirely, kept in the place where Maren said given things live.

The moment she refused him. The vault. His offer—partnership, her childhood returned, the nation's identity erased—and her *no*, spoken aloud, the first true word she had ever said in that building.

They could not take it, because she had already given it away—to the choir, to Sefa, to Veyl looking away at the harvest house, to every stranger she had freed. It was not hers anymore. It belonged to everyone she had chosen, and so it sat in her chest like a coal that no basin could quench, and the clerk's fingers slid off it again and again, and the white ledger's final page stayed stubbornly, impossibly blank.

Corvane stared at the page. For the first time in ten years, Isandro saw something in his face that was not accounting.

"What are you keeping?" he demanded. "What is left?"

Isandro opened her mouth. She had almost no self left to speak with—no name, no mother, no market, no hymn—but the coal was warm, and words came out of the warmth:

"The part of me that says no."

The basins rang again, all together, a sound like a struck country. And far above them, muffled through stone and fog and the burning edges of a hidden world, came a second sound—boots, many boots, and a voice she no longer recognized calling a name she no longer answered to, and the vault doors shuddered on their hinges as Magistrate Veyl came down into the dark.
