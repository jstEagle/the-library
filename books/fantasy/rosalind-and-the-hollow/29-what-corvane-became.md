## 29. What Corvane Became

The master ledger drank the country in long swallows.

Corvane felt each one arrive. They came up through the floor of the vault like water finding a crack—not the memories themselves, not yet, but the pressure of them, the weight of forty thousand lives funneling down through the harvest houses and along the smugglers' tunnels and into the great copper basin where his clerks had once counted stolen summers by the pound. He stood at the center of it with his palms flat on the open book and let it pour.

The first grief was a widow's.

He knew it was a widow's because he recognized it—he had catalogued its purchase himself, decades ago, a woman on Candlemaker's Row who sold her husband's death to pay for coal. But this was not the purchased copy, the clean trade-goods version with its edges sanded smooth. This was the original, still rooted where it had grown all these years, and it came into him whole: the cold side of the bed, the unfinished argument from a Tuesday that neither of them had known was their last conversation, the terrible ordinary morning when she reached across the sheets for a body that wasn't there and her hand kept going, kept going, until it found nothing at all.

Corvane gasped. The ledger took another swallow.

A soldier's courage arrived next, and he understood suddenly that courage and terror were the same substance poured into different molds—the same night outside the border forts before the erasure, the same mud, the same boy beside him screaming, and the choice, made over and over, to stand anyway. Then a mother's lullaby in a language the maps no longer admitted existed. Then a baker's pride in bread. Then shame—shame in quantities he had never imagined, whole districts of it, the shame of people who had informed on neighbors to keep their ration of forgetting, who had signed the guild's papers with shaking hands because the alternative was watching their children be unmade.

*This is what I saved them from,* he told himself, and the thought drowned almost immediately, like a man trying to shout instructions underwater.

He had planned for volume. He had not planned for simultaneity.

That was the flaw in the arithmetic, and he saw it now, far too late to matter, with the clarity that always arrives after the decision is irrevocable. A memory removed from a person is a specimen—a pressed flower, a pinned moth. It holds its shape because nothing around it is alive. But Corvane had not taken specimens. He had ordered the harvest of living memory, torn out by the root while still growing in its soil, and roots remember the shape of everything they ever touched. Every grief came trailing its whole history. Every joy carried the specific weather of the day it happened. Forty thousand lives did not stack in him like ledgers in a vault; they *interpenetrated*, grief braided into grief, every widow's cold bed lying down beside every soldier's frozen watch, until there were no separate sorrows anymore, only one vast sorrow wearing ten thousand faces, and he was the country's single mourner, weeping with everyone's eyes at once.

His knees found the stone floor. He did not remember kneeling.

"Master Corvane—" One of the harvest-clerks, young Pellam, at the vault door, his face gray. "The basin's overflowing, sir, the resonance is past every measure—we have to stop the intake—"

"No." His voice came out wrong, cracked down the middle, two voices arguing inside one throat. "Keep it flowing."

"Sir, the readings—people are wandering the streets, they don't know their own doorways—"

"I said *keep it flowing.*"

Pellam fled. Corvane could not blame him. He could feel what his own face must look like, the way the memories showed him: a man with a whole nation moving behind his eyes, visible, like fish under ice.

He had wanted this. That was the terrible thing, the thing he would carry into whatever came after: he had *wanted* it. Not merely the strategy of it—not merely the cold ledger-logic that said a nation's identity, gathered into one vessel, could be erased in a stroke rather than starved out over generations. Some older and hungrier thing in him had wanted to hold it all. Fifty years of keeping accounts for other people's lives. Fifty years of recording griefs he never permitted himself to feel, because a clerk who wept over inventory was a clerk who made mistakes. He had built himself into a perfect instrument of distance and now, drunk on the entire Hollow at once, the distance collapsed, and every feeling he had ever refused came home demanding interest.

He laughed. It hurt to laugh. It hurt to breathe. Everything hurt, because everything was happening.

* * *

Through the flood of it, distantly, he heard the war begin.

The vault doors boomed—something enormous striking them from outside. The threshold-god's people, or what remained of the magistrate's authority turned against him, or both; it hardly mattered. He had accounted for assault. The basin's power was his shield as much as his prize. He pulled the nearest thread of harvested memory and wove it into the air between himself and the doors, the way he had seen the god-touched work their miracles, and found—to his vertiginous horror—that it worked. It worked *beautifully*. A wall of remembered winter sealed the eastern entrance, frost blooming from nothing, because somewhere in the flood was a child's memory of the Freeze Winter, and the child had been afraid, and fear this concentrated could stop an army.

Power. So this was what it felt like from the inside. No wonder the gods had been careless with it.

He rose. The ledger's pages turned themselves now, ink crawling across them faster than any clerk could write, recording not transactions but *everything*—the whole country pouring itself into paper, and the paper drinking it without complaint, and Corvane standing in the eye of it feeling simultaneously like a god and like a man being eaten.

Then a specific memory surfaced, and stopped him mid-stride.

It was his own.

Not a harvest—no one had dared harvest him—but something older, something he had sold to the guild himself in the early years, when he still believed in the cleanliness of the trade and had wanted, once, just once, to know how it felt from the client's side. He had sold the memory of his daughter. She had died in the conquest years, before the erasure, before the guild's great bargain—one fevered week, a name that had meant the world, gone. He had kept a copy for himself afterward, of course. Guildmasters kept copies. And the copy had faded the way copies do, worn smooth by handling, until what remained was less a memory than a caption: *I had a daughter. Her loss shaped me.* A fact about himself rather than a wound.

But the harvest basin held the originals of everything, including the guild's oldest stock—including, somewhere in the deep shelves, the true record of what he had surrendered that day—and the flood found it, and brought it to him, and gave it back.

He was in the sickroom again. He smelled the tallow and the vinegar. Her small hand gripped his finger with a strength that seemed impossible for so failing a body, and she asked him—not frightened, only curious, only four years old and already braver than he would ever be—if he would remember her properly, and he said yes, he promised, he would keep her safe in his remembering, and then, eleven days later, broke the promise in a guild office for reasons that had seemed like mercy at the time: because a grief that size was a weight that would crush the life out of him, and the guild could lift it, and lifting burdens was what the guild was *for*, wasn't it—

Wasn't it?

Corvane stood in the middle of his stolen country with his dead daughter's whole death alive in his chest, and understood.

Mercy was not the lifting. Mercy was the carrying. The widow on Candlemaker's Row reaching across cold sheets for thirty years—unbearable, ruinous, and yet *hers*, woven through everything else she was, the grief and the woman inseparable, and to have cut it out of her would not have healed her but halved her. He had spent fifty years telling himself the guild's bargain was kindness: take the pain, spare the people, hide the country behind fog and mutual pretense so the conquerors' armies would pass over a blank space on the map. Erasure as mercy. He had believed it so thoroughly that he had built an entire nation's survival on the belief, and now, holding forty thousand lives at once, drowning in the truth of them, he saw the shape of his error with a precision no argument could ever have achieved:

You cannot protect people from being who they are.

The country had not survived despite its grief. It had survived *as* its grief, and its stubborn joy, and its shameful compromises, and its secret hymns in the undercity, and every small theft and freely given thing in between. To erase the memory of it—even to save it—was to kill it and call the corpse safe.

And he had begun the killing already. The streets above were full of his own countrymen, wandering, nameless, smiling politely at strangers in their own doorways.

*What have I done.*

The doors gave way.

* * *

They came in through frost and smoke—Sefa with her border-knives, the choir singing something old, the magistrate Veyl walking at the front with his god-light guttering around him like a candle in wind. Corvane raised the basin's power to meet them and felt it answer, vast and eager, and for one bright moment he believed he might actually win: he could feel every prayer ever prayed in the Hollow running through him like current, he could throw winters and fires and forty years of accumulated love at these intruders, he was more than a guildmaster now, he was the country—

That was exactly when it broke him.

Because the power answered with *content*. To throw the memories was to feel them, fully, all of them, and he was already full past any vessel's capacity. When he called up fire he got the arsonist's glee *and* the burned family's screams in the same instant. When he called up the choir's hymn to repel them, the hymn arrived with the funeral it was written for, and the famine it had gotten them through, and the faces of everyone who had ever sung it and died. He swung a weapon made of a nation's soul and the nation's soul went through him like a bell being struck from inside, every note at once, every grief at once, every *everything* at once, and Corvane—who had spent a lifetime believing that feeling was a leak to be patched—came apart at the seams.

He fell among his own ledgers. Around him, the great book lay open, still writing, still drinking, and he watched the ink move and realized with distant, clinical horror that it was not recording anymore. It was *deciding*. A ledger this full stopped being an account and became an actor. He had made a god out of bookkeeping, and gods, he was learning, do not stay owned.

"The basin," he heard himself say—heard his voice come out small, an old man's voice, a clerk's voice. "Someone has to close the basin. If it fills completely, there will be nothing left of anyone. I miscalculated. I—" The words failed him. Fifty years of precise language, and the only honest sentence left was the one he could not finish: *I am sorry.*

Veyl stood over him, sword drawn, and did not strike. The magistrate's face—Corvane read it the way he read everything now, instantly, helplessly, awash in other people's hearts—was not triumph. It was pity. Worse: it was *recognition*. The god-touched man looked down at him and saw, clearly, what Corvane had become: not a tyrant, not even a monster. Just the first casualty. The one man who had tried to carry a whole country alone.

"You understand," Veyl said quietly, "what you've done to them."

"Yes." The word cost him everything he had left.

"And to yourself."

Corvane laughed again—the broken-bell sound—and gestured at the flood raging behind his eyes. "I contain multitudes," he said, "and none of them are me. Do you know what I discovered, Magistrate? At the bottom of all of it?" He pulled himself upright against the basin's rim, the copper warm as skin beneath his hands. "Mercy. Everyone kept asking me where the mercy was in erasure, and I never had an answer I trusted. Now I do." His vision blurred—or perhaps it cleared; he could no longer tell the difference. "Mercy was the carrying. All this time. We were supposed to carry each other. That was the whole of the law, and I turned it into inventory."

The basin shuddered beneath his hands. Deeper in the vault, the master ledger turned a page by itself, and the sound of it was like a door opening somewhere very far away.

Beyond the shattered doors, faintly, through smoke and frost, Corvane heard singing—the choir, advancing—and underneath the singing, unmistakable even through the roaring in his skull, the sound of the Hollow's stolen memories beginning to flow back toward their owners, called home by voices that had never agreed to forget.

He had one act of bookkeeping left in him. One correction to make before the account closed.

With fingers that barely obeyed him, Guildmaster Corvane opened his own coat, drew out the iron key to the harvest relays—the key that could reverse the flow, send every stolen memory back along the lines to the heads and hearts it had been torn from—and held it out to his enemies with both hands, the way one returns something borrowed.

"Take it," he whispered. "Before I want it back."
