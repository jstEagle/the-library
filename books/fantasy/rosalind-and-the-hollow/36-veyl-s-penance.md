## 36. Veyl's Penance

The council met in a granary, because the refugees had nowhere grander and no one thought to pretend otherwise. Grain had been spilled across the floor in the crossing—golden drifts of it, trodden into the cracks between boards—and the smell of it hung in the air like a blessing: something growing, something that would feed people. Veyl stood at the edge of it with his magistrate's sash folded over his arm and waited to be noticed.

He had not slept. He doubted any of them had. Three days since the vault war, two since Corvane came apart into the fog, and the far side of the door was still sorting itself into something like order: tents in the ruins of an abandoned garrison town, cookfires, children who kept asking whether they were allowed to be seen here. The answer, given over and over by exhausted adults, was yes. Yes. You are allowed.

Veyl had spent those three days doing what he knew how to do. He hauled water. He dug latrines. He sat with a woman named Ostra whose son had been harvested so thoroughly that he no longer recognized her face, and he told her, in the flat honest voice he used for confessions, that he did not know if the memories could be given back whole—but that Sefa and Rosalind were trying, and that he would carry the man's body himself if carrying was needed. It was not forgiveness she gave him. It was only the nod of one tired animal acknowledging another. He treasured it anyway, the way a starving man treasures a stone that looks briefly like bread.

Now the council had sent for him.

There were nine of them seated on grain sacks behind a trestle table that some soldier's ghost had left behind: a weaver with burn-scarred forearms, a ferryman, a schoolmistress who had taught in secret for thirty years, old Sefa at the end with her arms crossed and her expression suggesting she had already decided everything and was merely permitting the others their ceremony. And at the center, where a chair had been found, Rosalind Lockridge—or the woman who had been Isandro, though most of them were still learning to say the name—watching him with eyes that held almost nothing of her past and all of her purpose.

He knelt before he was asked to. It seemed correct. His knees found the spilled grain and the hard boards beneath it, and the pain of it was ordinary, human pain, and he was grateful for even that.

"Magistrate Veyl," said the schoolmistress, reading from a scrap of paper as though the words might bite her. "You come before this council under what claim?"

"No claim." His voice sounded strange without the courtroom behind it. He had spoken with the weight of the Concord's law for twenty years, and now the weight had nowhere to rest. "I come to surrender a thing and ask for a judgment. In that order."

"Speak the surrender first," said the ferryman.

Veyl unfolded the sash. It was deep blue, worked with silver thread in the pattern of scales—the Concord's emblem, the balanced blade, the eye that saw all debts. He had been invested with it at nineteen, in a hall of white marble, by men who told him he was becoming an instrument of divine order. He had believed them. Believing them had hollowed eleven people in the Hollow's undercity and signed the harvest orders for hundreds more, and he had called every hour of it faith.

"I, Aldous Veyl, formerly First Magistrate of the Concord's Border Commission, surrender my office, my authority, and the warrant I hold over every soul who crossed from the Hollow." He laid the sash on the trestle table, and the silver scales caught the lamplight and threw it back coldly, the way they always had. "The warrants are void because the law that issued them was built on a lie the Concord told itself: that your country did not exist, and therefore nothing done to you counted. I enforced that lie. I read crimes out of men's minds until there was no man left inside the crime. I hunted a clerk through her own country while her guildmaster stole it from under both of us." He paused. The next part needed to be exact; vagueness was a form of mercy, and he was not here to receive mercy. "And when I understood what I served, I did not stop soon enough. There is a ledger entry for the night I looked away instead of acting, and I have written worse entries than that one myself, in other men's heads, with my own hands."

The granary was very quiet. Somewhere outside, a child laughed—a bright, unguarded sound that seemed to belong to another century—and no one in the room turned toward it, because they were all holding very still around the thing he had just set down among them.

"You're asking us to judge you," the weaver said. Her voice was careful. "A god-touched magistrate. The man who walked through a border that kills, because his god carried him." She looked at her scarred forearms as if consulting them. "We spent a generation being judged by laws written in countries that erased us. Now you want us to write one?"

"Yes."

"Why should we?" It was the ferryman, blunt as a keel-stone. "Why shouldn't we simply give you to the families of the hollowed? Let them decide. That's justice too, isn't it? The kind you never offered."

"It is," Veyl said. "It may be the better kind. I am not here to argue against it. I am here because I have spent my life believing that judgment belongs to the law, and the law belongs to whoever holds the pen, and I held the pen. If I walk away from this room still holding it—if I keep the power to decide what I deserve—then nothing has changed except the direction I point the blade." He lifted his hands, palms open, empty. "I am asking you to take it from me. All of it. Even the parts you'd rather not carry."

Sefa snorted. It was almost fond. "Gods preserve me, he actually means it. I've watched smugglers confess, magistrates confess, once a bishop, and none of them ever meant it. They meant *sentence me lightly*." She leaned forward on her grain sack. "So here's my question, Magistrate, and think carefully, because I sold passage to your hunters once and I know how your kind thinks. When we judge you—what do you want us to say?"

"That is precisely what I cannot tell you."

"Wrong answer."

"It is the only true one," Veyl said. "If I name the sentence I fear, you will weigh it against my fear. If I name the sentence I hope for, you will weigh it against my hope. Either way the judgment bends around me instead of around what I did. Ask the widow Ostra what I took from her son. Ask the choir what years of hiding cost. Ask—" his voice caught, and he let it, because smoothing it over would be another small theft— "ask the ones who didn't come through the door. I can't ask them. That is the shape of my crime. I made people unanswerable."

Rosalind spoke then, for the first time. Her voice was quiet and carried anyway, the way a key turns quietly and opens everything.

"The council will hear testimony," she said. "Not yours, Magistrate. Theirs. Everyone you touched in the Hollow, everyone your warrants reached, anyone who wants to stand in this granary and say what you are. We will sit as long as it takes. Then we will decide—not quickly, and not kindly, and not cruelly either. Kindness and cruelty are both shortcuts, and we've had enough of shortcuts." She folded her hands. "Do you accept that? That we may take weeks? That we may decide nothing you can predict?"

"I accept."

"That we may decide you must stay here, under our eye, for the rest of your life, hauling water and digging earth and sitting with the hollowed men whose minds your Commission emptied?"

"I accept."

"That we may decide you must go back across the border, into the Concord, and testify—to tear open the machine that sent you, knowing what they do to traitors?"

The granary seemed to lean in. Veyl felt the old reflexes rise—the advocate's mind already drafting objections, the magistrate's instinct weighing precedent—and he set them down one by one like tools he would not need again.

"I accept," he said. "I will testify. I know where the harvest orders originate. I know which ministers signed them and which shrines laundered the theology. I know the names of every hollowed man in the Concord's cells, because I put them there. If the council judges that my knowledge serves you best spent as a weapon pointed at my former masters, then I will spend it until there is nothing left of me worth retrieving."

"And if we decide," Sefa said softly, "that knowledge isn't enough? That some things can't be paid in information?"

"Then I will pay in the only coin I have left, which is obedience."

The schoolmistress wrote something down. The weaver watched him with an expression he could not parse and did not try to; parsing expressions had been his trade too, once, and he was finished with trades.

* * *

They began the testimonies that same evening, because Rosalind said delay was a luxury the dead had paid for, and no one argued with her. A brazier was lit. Benches were carried in. Word went through the camp the way fire goes through dry grass, and by full dark the granary was crowded to its walls, and people stood outside in the cold because they wanted to hear.

They came one at a time.

An old man who had hidden in a root cellar for six years after Veyl's first sweep of the fog market, and whose lungs had never recovered the damp. He did not shout. He described the cellar. He described the sound of boots overhead, and how he had learned to breathe in sips, and how he still breathed that way now, on the free side of the world, in clean air, in sips. Then he sat down across from Veyl and looked at him for a long moment and said, "I don't want you dead. Dead men teach nothing. I want you to learn to breathe like me." And Veyl bowed his head and said he would try, and discovered he meant it, and discovered also that meaning it hurt more than any sentence.

A young woman whose brother had been hollowed in the second wave of harvests. She stood with her arms wrapped around herself and told the room what it was like to be spoken to by someone wearing her brother's face and holding none of his love, and halfway through she stopped addressing the council and addressed Veyl directly, leaning close, her voice shaking: "You *read* him. You stood inside his head and you read him like a ledger. Tell me one thing—one true thing—you found there. Give me back one piece of him."

And Veyl closed his eyes and went down into the memory, which he had tried for three days not to enter, and brought up what lay there: "His name was Tam. Before the extraction he was reciting the names of his family to hold onto them—he'd made a list in his mind, mother, sister, the dog, the street he grew up on—and the last thing in him before the hollowing took everything was the dog's name. It was Bristle. He was laughing when he thought it. He thought the dog was a stupid name and he loved it." The young woman made a sound like something breaking gently, and sat down, and later, when the session ended, she did not forgive him, but she nodded to him in passing, the way one traveler nods to another on a long road, and he understood that the nod had cost her something and that it was therefore worth more than absolution.

Testimony after testimony. The ferryman whose boat had been burned on Veyl's warrant. The choir members who had sung underground for decades partly because men like him made the surface a graveyard. A child, seven or eight, who marched up to the bench with tremendous solemnity and announced that the magistrate had frightened her grandmother once, on a bridge, and that this was bad, and that he should be made to apologize, and Veyl knelt and apologized, formally, in the language of the Hollow—which he had learned from interrogation transcripts, and which tasted different in his mouth now, spoken as courtesy instead of as a hunter's tool—and the child considered this gravely and pronounced it sufficient, and the whole granary laughed, and the laughter broke something open in the room that all the grief had only cracked.

It went on for hours. Toward midnight, Ostra came.

She sat across from him with her hollowed son beside her—a big gentle man with a stranger's calm, who smiled politely at the lamps and at the walls and at his own hands. Ostra talked about him. Not about Veyl. About the boy Tam's-neighbor had been, the pranks, the apprenticeship, the way he whistled when he worked. She talked for a long time, rebuilding her son aloud, brick by brick, in front of three hundred witnesses, and then she turned to Veyl and said:

"You're going to help us get him back. Whatever Rosalind and the smuggler are doing with those returned memories—you're going to help. You know how the extraction works from the inside. You know the seams." Her chin trembled and steadied. "That's my judgment. Not your death. Your labor. Every day, until my son knows my face, you work for the mending. And when he knows my face—" her voice finally cracked— "when he looks at me and says *Mother*, then maybe I'll decide what else you owe. But not before. I won't spend my anger cheap."

Veyl found he could not speak for a moment. When he could, he said, "Accepted. Tomorrow morning. I'll be at the harvest house at dawn."

"He rises early," Ostra said, with the ghost of something that was not quite a smile. "He always rose early. You'll see."

* * *

Near dawn, when the granary had emptied and the brazier had burned down to a red heart, Rosalind came and sat across from him. She moved like someone relearning her own body, carefully, as if each gesture had to be chosen fresh—and perhaps it did. He had helped make her nearly empty. He tried to imagine what it was to be a person composed entirely of decision, and could not, and did not insult her by pretending to.

"They'll confirm the judgments tomorrow," she said. "Ostra's terms. The testimony duty. Probably the border crossing, eventually—they'll want the Concord torn open, and you're the knife we have." She studied him. "You could refuse any of it. No one would stop you. You're still stronger than everyone in this camp put together, if strength is what you want."

"It isn't."

"I know. I watched you not use it, at the vault. I remember that much." Something moved behind her eyes, brief and unreachable—the shadow of a memory she had given away, perhaps, or one she had never owned. "I wanted to ask you something. As one archive to another."

"Ask."

"When you read people—back when. The ones you hollowed. Did you ever find a prayer in them? Something they'd prayed for, secretly, that they'd never told anyone?"

Veyl considered lying, found the muscle for it atrophied, and answered. "Always. Under everything. Debts and shames and ledgers of grievance, and underneath all of it, a prayer. Most of them weren't praying to anything in particular anymore. Just—praying. Into the dark. Please let my daughter be safe. Please let it mean something, what I lost." He looked at his hands. "I catalogued them. Without meaning to. I have hundreds of prayers in my head that belonged to other people, and their owners mostly can't remember them anymore. I've been wondering what a man does with that."

Rosalind was silent a long moment. Outside, the sky was going grey at the edges, and the first cookfire smoke rose straight up in the windless cold.

"You bring them to the shrine," she said at last. "When you find it. There's a ruined one past the east ridge—I'm told the god of thresholds answers there, or asks questions, which comes to the same thing. I'm going to ask why the prayers came back." She rose, unhurried, and looked down at him with the terrible gentleness of someone who has given away everything soft in herself and kept only what is necessary. "Bring your hundred stolen prayers, Magistrate. Maybe returning things is the only worship left. Maybe that's what the gods were for all along."

She walked out into the grey light, a woman made entirely of what she had chosen, and Veyl knelt alone in the spilled grain and began, silently, to unpack the first prayer—*please let my daughter be safe*, a cooper's, from the third sweep, eleven years ago—and to wonder, with something that was not yet hope but had stopped being dread, what the god of doors would say to a man arriving with nothing to offer but other people's words.
