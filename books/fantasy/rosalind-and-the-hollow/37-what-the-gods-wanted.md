## 37. What the Gods Wanted

The shrine had no roof.

That was the first thing Rosalind noticed about it, in the way she noticed everything now—slowly, hungrily, like a woman reading a letter from someone she'd loved and lost. The shrine stood on a hill above the settlement where the refugees were learning to be a country again, and it was nothing but four standing stones and an absence above them, open to whatever sky chose to visit. Rain fell straight through the place where a ceiling should have been and pooled in a shallow basin worn into the floor by centuries of exactly this kind of weather. The stones themselves were older than the Hollow, older than its erasure, older perhaps than the maps that had once contained them. Lichen grew on their southern faces in pale continents. Someone had carved doorways into each of them—not pictures of doors, but actual thresholds, deep enough to step into, though they led nowhere except back out again.

She came up the hill most mornings now, before the council convened, before the ledger-work began. She told herself it was to think. She told herself a great many things these days; her mind was a house of nearly empty rooms, and she furnished each one with whatever story fit best.

But this morning she did not come to think. This morning she climbed the wet grass with her coat collar up against the drizzle and her hands empty for the first time in weeks—the new ledger was with Sefa's clerks, being copied—and she stood between the four carved stones and said, aloud, to the doorway that led nowhere:

"I want to ask you something."

The wind moved through the shrine the way wind moves through any ruined place, which is to say indifferently. Rosalind waited anyway. Waiting had become a kind of prayer for her, and she suspected that was not an accident.

* * *

She had been Isandro Lockridge for thirty-nine chapters of a life she could no longer read. That was how she thought of it now, privately, when the disorientation got loud: as chapters. Sefa had explained it to her gently, in the weeks after the crossing, the way one explains a scar to its owner. *You gave away almost everything,* Sefa had said. *The guild took some, Corvane took more, and you fed the rest to the door yourself, handful by handful. What's left is thin. But it's true.*

What was left was this: she knew what she had done. Not remembered, exactly—remembering implied something to remember *with*—but known, the way one knows the shape of one's own hands in the dark. She had kept the ledgers. She had copied the forbidden entry. She had found the choir in the undercity. She had refused Corvane in the vault, refused him again at the harvest houses, and when he had unwritten her memory by memory, she had held the last thing she held—the moment of refusal itself—and carried it to the threshold-god's door, and given even that away so that the door would open wide enough for a nation to walk through.

And then she had woken on the far side, in a country that existed again, carrying almost nothing of herself and all of what mattered.

The council had restored her duties first and her name later—or rather, they were restoring her name now; that work was still underway, still being argued over in long sessions she attended like a guest at someone else's table. They called her Isandro because that was the name in the surviving records, and she answered to it because a person must answer to something. But there was a strangeness in it, wearing a name like a borrowed coat. Sometimes at night she caught herself listening for another name underneath, the way you listen for a second voice in a duet you only half recall.

The people brought her things. That was the other part of her life now, the part that filled the empty rooms faster than any story could. They came to the long low building that had been a tithe-barn in the old days and was now the archive, and they brought scraps—fragments of the memories they had freely given at the door, the small tax of passage every soul had paid to hold it open. A woman who had surrendered her wedding day came with a description of it written out by her daughter, who had kept hers, and asked Rosalind to enter it into the new ledger so that the day would exist somewhere outside one grieving mind. A man who had given away his mother's face brought a charcoal sketch his brother had drawn from his own keeping. A child too young to have given anything brought a pressed flower and no explanation at all, and Rosalind had entered the flower, because the flower had been given freely and that was the only law the new ledger recognized.

*Given freely.* That phrase ran through everything now like a vein of silver through dark stone. It was the lesson of the door. It was the lesson Corvane had died refusing to learn—he had tried to force the threshold open with a whole nation's stolen remembering, and the door had weighed what he carried and found it all taken rather than offered, and it had refused him, and the fog that had hidden them for a generation had taken him back the way the sea takes back a drowned thing. Veyl had seen it happen. Veyl did not speak of it, but he had stopped sleeping with his back to doors, which those who knew him understood to be a kind of testimony.

So the new order—the door-keepers, as they were already calling themselves, though Rosalind flinched a little at the grandeur of it—had one law and one law only: *nothing is written here that was not given.* No purchase. No extraction. No taking. If a memory could not be surrendered willingly, it could not be kept at all, and the keeper's whole art lay in making the giving possible—in sitting with the frightened and the grieving until fear loosened its grip, until grief found its hands, until the memory became a gift instead of a wound being reopened.

It was slow work. It was holy work. Rosalind no longer saw much difference between the two.

* * *

"You've been asking everyone else," said a voice behind her. "I wondered when you'd get around to asking me."

Rosalind turned. The shrine was empty—or it had been. Now, between the second and third standing stone, in the carved doorway that led nowhere, there was a figure. Not standing in it. Being it, somehow—the way a word can be a sentence, the way a threshold is not the room on either side but the moment of passing between them. It had a shape the eye kept losing: tall as the doorway, narrow as the instant of stepping through, and its face was less a face than a suggestion of every face that had ever hesitated at a border, deciding whether to go forward or back.

The threshold-god. The god of doors, of crossings, of the hollow places between one state and another. The god whose name the dying widow had spoken in chapter six of a life Rosalind could no longer read, warming a cold room with a single syllable.

"You're real," Rosalind said, and then felt foolish, because she had freed memories back into their owners' heads with this god's door standing open behind her; she had walked through it herself. But knowing a thing and meeting it were different countries, and she was, after all, a woman who worked in borders.

"As real as a doorway," the god said. Its voice was the sound a latch makes, the sound of hinges, the sound of a key turning in a lock that has waited a long time. "Which is to say: real whenever someone needs to pass, and invisible the rest of the time. You wanted to ask me something."

"Why." The word came out of her rougher than she intended. She knelt—not in worship, she realized, but the way one kneels to speak with a child, or a very old person, or anyone whose dignity deserves level eyes. "Why did the prayers start being answered? After all that time. A generation of silence. The whole country erased, the gods gone quiet, everyone saying the old names like coins that had stopped being currency. And then suddenly—" she gestured vaguely at the air, at the world, at everything that had burned and broken and opened since—"suddenly the prayers worked again. Why then? Why us?"

The god regarded her. Or the doorway regarded her. It was difficult to tell where the attention lived.

"Ask it the way you actually mean it," the god said gently.

Rosalind sat back on her heels. The rain had stopped; light was coming up gray and silver behind the eastern hills, catching the wet grass in a thousand small fires. She made herself say the truer question underneath.

"Why did you abandon us?"

* * *

The silence that followed was not empty. It was the silence of a door held carefully shut while something enormous considers whether to come through it.

"We didn't," the god said at last.

"The records say—"

"The records," the god said, with something that might have been amusement, might have been grief, "were kept by your guild. By men who traded in forgetting and called it mercy. Do you imagine they wrote honestly about us?" It stepped—unfolded?—out of the carved doorway and stood among the stones, and where it stood, the air felt like the moment before knocking. "Listen, clerk. I will tell you what happened, because you are the one person left who will write it down truly, and because you paid more than anyone to hear it.

"When your enemies came for your country, they did not come with armies first. Armies are expensive and gods make them nervous. They came with merchants. With bargains. Your guildmaster's predecessors sold the country's name—that was the first transaction, the founding theft, the one your Corvane spent his whole life defending. A name is a handle. Take the handle and the door cannot be found, let alone opened. So they took it, and hid the whole nation behind the removal, and called it salvation, and in the narrowest sense it was. No army ever marched into the Hollow. No bomb ever fell there. Everyone lived."

"And the price," Rosalind said quietly.

"And the price." The god turned toward the eastern light. "A name is not just a word, little keeper. A name is how a people address the divine. Every prayer begins by naming who is prayed to and who prays. Erase the name and you have not merely hidden the country—you have muffled it. Cut the wire between the bell and the bell-rope. We heard you. Do you understand? All those years, we *heard*—prayers rising out of the fog like voices through a wall, muffled, nameless, arriving without return addresses. And we could not answer, because an answer must go somewhere, must be addressed to someone, and you had all been made unaddressable. It was not abandonment. It was deafness imposed from outside, and the ones who imposed it convinced you it was mercy."

Rosalind's hands had closed into fists without her permission. She looked down at them—these hands that had recorded ten thousand stolen memories, that had copied one forbidden entry, that had memorized a burning ledger, that had fed a door. "Then why did it change? Why did the prayers start getting through?"

"Because of the widow," the god said.

Rosalind went still.

"She was dying, and she was poor, and the guild had bought everything sellable from her years before—her youth, her grudges, her mother's lullabies. But there was one thing they never managed to take, because she never put it up for sale, because it was not for sale at any price: the memory of praying. Of kneeling by her husband's bed in the last winter and calling out to the old gods by their true names—the names the erasure had missed, because she had learned them from her grandmother, who had learned them in secret, in the undercity, from people who had passed them hand to hand in the dark for a hundred years like embers in cupped palms." The god's voice softened to the sound of a door easing open. "She prayed with a name. A real one. An addressed prayer, from an unaddressable place. And it slipped through the gap in the wall like light under a door, and it reached us, and we answered—one small answer, one warmed room, one dying woman comforted—and that answer was a crack. And cracks, clerk, are how gods come home."

"The choir," Rosalind whispered. "They kept the names alive. All that time."

"They kept the *addresses* alive. A hundred generations of smugglers of syllables. And when the widow's answered prayer rippled out—when the guild dismissed it as counterfeit and filed it away, and one careful clerk noticed its impossible texture and could not let it go—the crack widened. Every prayer that followed, every secret worship in the undercity, every refusal to forget, pried it further. Because here is the truth I will give you to carry, the truth you climbed this hill to ask for:

"Gods do not live in temples. Temples are only doors we stand in. Gods live in *being remembered*. Wherever people refuse to forget—refuse at cost, refuse in the dark, refuse when forgetting would be safer and easier and kinder to their children's futures—there the divine has soil to root in. We did not come back because you built us shrines. We came back because you would not let us be erased. Every act of remembering is a prayer, whether it knows it or not. Your ledgers, clerk—your stolen, sold, bartered, hoarded memories—they were never mere inventory. They were a country holding itself in its cupped hands, refusing to drop itself, even when dropping itself was the price of survival."

The light had strengthened. The basin at the shrine's center, Rosalind saw, had begun to steam faintly where the sun touched the pooled rainwater, as if the water remembered warmth.

"So the prayers weren't answered despite the erasure," she said slowly, working it through the way she worked through a difficult entry. "The erasure was the silence. And the remembering—the widow's stubbornness, the choir's songs, the names passed down—that was the answering."

"Now you are doing theology," the god said, and there was definite amusement in it now, the creak of a well-used hinge. "You see why we needed a clerk. Someone has to keep the doctrine honest."

"But then—" Rosalind stopped. Something cold moved through her, the old archivist's instinct catching a discrepancy in the account. "Corvane. He remembered everything. He kept the original bargain, the founding theft, the whole history of the guild in his head. He was the greatest rememberer in the Hollow. Why did the gods never come to *him*?"

The god was quiet for a moment, and in that quiet Rosalind heard the answer forming, and wished she hadn't asked.

"Because he remembered as a hoard, not as a gift," the god said finally. "He kept the country's past locked in vaults the way a miser keeps coin—never to spend, never to share, never to give. Memory that is kept only for power is a dead thing, clerk. It is a specimen in a jar. It preserves the shape of a life without any of the life. The choir sang their fragments to each other in cellars and thereby fed us for a century. Corvane sat atop the complete record of a nation and starved us. Remembering is sacred only when it is *for* something—for someone. Held alone, it is just weight. Given, it becomes grace."

Rosalind thought of the door. Of the memory she had carried to it—the moment of refusal, the one true thing left in her—and of how it had not opened for her *keeping* of it, but for her *giving* of it. The key was always the giving. She had learned that at the threshold and was learning it again here, and suspected she would go on learning it, in one form or another, for as long as she had a self to learn with.

"Is that why you're here now?" she asked. "Talking to me? There are holier people in the settlement. There's a whole choir. There's Veyl, who saw a miracle and changed the entire direction of his life."

"There is a whole country waking up on the far side of erasure," the god agreed. "And they will need many things—councils, granaries, laws, probably a great deal of patience. But they will need one thing above all, and it is the thing you already do." It inclined its suggestion-of-a-head toward the east, toward the settlement, toward the long low building where the new ledger waited under Sefa's clerks' pens. "They will need someone to write it down. Truly. Freely. Nothing taken, everything given. Because I will tell you what happens to countries that come back from the dead, clerk, and it is not always gentle. The world beyond your fog remembers that you vanished. Some of them will be glad to see you. Some of them will want to know where you went and who sent you and what you intend. And some of them—" the hinge-voice dropped low— "some of them will find it very convenient that you were erased once already. Convenient enough to try again. Names can be stolen twice."

The cold in Rosalind's chest spread outward, and then, strangely, resolved into clarity. This was the hook beneath the morning's peace, the thing she had half-known since the first refugee had brought her a scrap of given memory and asked her, with desperate eyes, *write it down so no one can take it again.* The exodus was not an ending. It was a beginning, and beginnings are the most fragile part of any story.

"So keep the ledger," the god said. "Keep it openly, where the whole country can see it. Keep it freely, so that every entry belongs to the one who gave it. Keep it *loudly*, clerk—because a memory shouted in the marketplace is harder to steal than one hidden in a vault. Corvane kept your past in the dark, and the dark ate it. Give yours to the daylight, and the daylight will defend it."

"And you?" Rosalind asked. "Will you stay? Now that the door is open, now that the country has a name again—" she paused on that, on the *again*, on the restoration still being argued over in the council hall below— "will you stay, or will you fade back into thresholds and wait for the next emergency?"

The god smiled, or the doorway did; by now Rosalind could not separate them and no longer wished to.

"I am a god of doors," it said. "Doors do not stay. Doors *remain*. There is a difference, and your people are about to learn it. As long as they cross—from grief to mourning, from exile to home, from one life to the next—I am here. And as long as they remember out loud, I am *audible*." It stepped backward into the carved doorway that led nowhere, and fitted itself into the absence the way a key fits a lock. "One more thing, keeper, before you go down the hill and begin the day's writing."

"Yes?"

"The council argues over the old name. I have heard them through my stones—three nights now, going in circles, each faction certain, none of them sure. They are looking in the wrong place. Names are not recovered from archives." The light behind the god seemed to gather, briefly, like a held breath. "Names are recovered from *keepers*. Ask the one who guarded what she guarded for decades and never told a living soul why. She has been waiting a long time to set it down. Go to her. Today, if you can. Some doors, clerk, have been standing open far longer than anyone has thought to walk through them."

And then the doorway was only a doorway, carved stone and empty air and the smell of rain, and the hillside was ordinary again, and somewhere below, the settlement was waking—smoke from cook-fires, the clang of the bell that marked the council hours, voices calling across the wet grass in a language that had almost been forgotten and was now, syllable by syllable, being spoken back into the world.

Rosalind stood a moment longer between the four stones, letting the words settle into her like rain into dry ground. Then she turned down the hill, moving quickly now, her empty hands suddenly not empty at all but full of purpose, toward the edge of the settlement where a smuggler's wagon stood parked beside a fence, and where Sefa—who had guarded something for decades and never told a living soul why—was drinking tea and watching the sunrise as if she had been waiting all morning for someone to come running.
