## 1. The Ledger of Impossible Things

There were three rules at the Gilded Lantern, and Juniper Vasquez could recite them in their sleep, which was fortunate, because they often did.

*One: the guild trades in impossible things. Two: the ledger remembers what the world forgets. Three: the keeper of the ledger keeps no secrets of their own.*

The third rule was the one that kept Juniper awake on nights like this, though they'd have told you it was the tea.

They sat in the counting room above the vault with a candle burned down to a stub, a pot of blackleaf tea gone bitter as an old grudge, and Volume Nine of the Ledger open before them. The Gilded Lantern's ledgers were not ordinary books. Ordinary books recorded debts of coin. These recorded debts of *strangeness*—and strangeness, properly accounted for, appreciated like fine wine.

Juniper ran a finger down the column and read aloud, softly, the way one reads to a nervous animal.

"*Bottled storm, minor temperment, harvested off the Teeth of Sorrel Bay. Sold to Lady Ammath of the Hill Houses for the purposes of—*" They squinted. "*—atmospheric improvement.*" A snort. "Lady Ammath threw a party, is what she did. Opened that bottle over her garden and gave the whole street a thunderstorm with a two-mile radius." The entry below, in Juniper's own careful hand, noted the return of the empty bottle and a surcharge for "unauthorized weather." The surcharge had been steep. Lady Ammath had paid it without complaint, which told you everything about the Hill Houses.

Next entry. "*Borrowed year, one (1), condition: lightly used.*" That one always made Juniper's chest ache in a way they couldn't quite name. A fisherman from the Saltrows had sold a year of his life to pay his daughter's physician, and ten years later he'd come back to buy it again—not to live it, he'd said, just to know it was still his. He'd sat in this very room, turning the little glass vial in his hands like a man holding a bird he wasn't sure would fly. In the end he'd bought it back and never opened it. Some things you keep just to know they're yours.

Juniper initialed the audit line and moved on.

This was the work, and the work was holy, or near enough that nobody quibbled. Every impossible thing the guild bought, sold, or held passed through the ledger, and every entry passed through Juniper. Not because they were clever—though they were—or because Guild Master Corvane trusted them—though he claimed to—but because of the oath. The oath to Veth, the silent god, god of debts unpaid and words unspoken, whose voice had gone quiet ten years ago mid-sentence and never finished whatever he'd been about to say.

Juniper didn't think about that. Juniper thought about the entries.

"*Name, unspoken, provenance disputed,*" they read. "*Held in trust. Do not shelve beside the mirrors.*" Someone had shelved it beside the mirrors once, back before Juniper's time, and the resulting incident was why Rule Four existed even though officially there were only three rules. Guilds were like families that way. The official rules were never the real ones.

The candle guttered. Outside, the city of Vail went about its midnight business—cart wheels, a drunk singing something mournful and off-key, the distant clatter of the night market striking its stalls. Above it all, the stars. Juniper glanced at the window out of habit and counted constellations without meaning to, the way their old sergeant had drilled into them during the war. *If you can count the stars, the wards are holding. If you can't, run.*

Forty-one visible from this window. The wards were holding. Juniper made a small note in the margin of their mind and moved on.

* * *

The knock came at half past two, which meant trouble, because honest clients came at decent hours and dishonest ones came when they thought no one would see them.

Juniper opened the door to find Tam, the night porter, looking like a man who'd been asked to hold a snake by someone insisting it was a very unusual cat.

"There's a client," Tam said. "Downstairs. Wants a erasure."

"At this hour?"

"He says—" Tam lowered his voice, which was silly, since the stairwell was empty, "—he says the hour *is* the point."

Juniper sighed, set down the tea—they'd regret leaving it, it would be cold and bitterer still—and followed Tam down through the Lantern's bones. The guildhouse had been a temple once, back when temples to Veth were allowed to have roofs, and it still moved like one: long corridors that swallowed sound, stairs worn into shallow smiles by centuries of feet. Lamps burned behind smoked glass, giving everything the color of weak honey.

The client waited in the receiving room, hood up, gloved hands folded. Rich gloves. The kind of rich that wanted to look modest and failed.

"You're the record-keeper," the client said. Not a question. The voice was smooth and ageless and immediately dislikeable, the way a well-bred dog is dislikeable when it looks at you like furniture.

"I'm the ledger-keeper," Juniper said. "Record-keeper makes it sound like I take dictation."

"I require a service. One memory, removed from your vault. Name withheld."

Juniper pulled the requisition book across the table and uncapped the pen. "Erasures are permitted under Article Twelve, provided the memory is the client's own property, freely surrendered, and not collateral against an outstanding debt. You'll state the memory aloud for the record—"

"No."

"—or provide a written description sealed in wax, which I will verify against the vault manifest—"

"No." The gloved hands unfolded, refolded. "You misunderstand. I am not surrendering a memory. I am purchasing the removal of one already in your keeping. An entry. I want it gone—not copied, not archived, *gone*. And I want the fact of its removal gone as well."

Juniper set the pen down.

That was not how it worked. That was so thoroughly not how it worked that there wasn't even a rule against it, because no one had ever been fool enough to ask. The ledger remembered what the world forgot—that was the whole architecture of the place. You couldn't buy a hole in it any more than you could buy a hole in the sky.

"That's not a service we offer," Juniper said carefully.

The client reached into their coat and set a purse on the table.

It was a good purse. It was, in fact, an obscene purse—the kind of purse that had opinions about small countries. It landed with a sound like a body falling downstairs, and gold spilled out across the wood, catching the lamplight, and Juniper—who had audited enough fortunes to price one at a glance—did the arithmetic before they could stop themselves. The sum on the table was more than the guild cleared in a year. Possibly two. The Lantern had been struggling lately; everyone knew it, no one said it. Impossible things were getting harder to find, or harder to believe in, depending on whom you asked.

"The guild is not for sale," Juniper said, and heard how thin it sounded.

"The guild isn't being purchased. One entry is. Think of it as—" the smooth voice considered, "—tidying."

"Which entry?"

"A name is to be withheld."

"Then no transaction occurs." Juniper stood, palms flat on the table, and put the whole of their war-seasoned, ledger-hardened self into the next sentence. "Article Twelve requires disclosure. Without disclosure, there is no verification. Without verification, there is no entry. And without an entry, sir or madam or otherwise, nothing has happened here tonight except that a stranger left gold on my table, which I will now have to report as found property, per Article Three."

A pause. Then, unexpectedly, the client laughed—a dry, papery sound, like a book closing.

"They said you'd be difficult. 'The little clerk with the god's mark,' they said. 'Difficult and devout.'" The hood tilted. "Very well. A compromise, since you're fond of articles. I will give you the date. Only the date. Verify it yourself, if your vault permits such curiosity. If the entry matches, the gold is yours and the page is mine. If it doesn't match, I'll leave and you may keep the gold anyway, as damages to my pride."

Juniper should have refused. Later—much later, in rooms darker than this one—they would lie awake and catalog every moment they should have refused, and this would be the first. But the gold sat there gleaming like a bottled sunrise, and the guild's coffers were down to lint and promises, and Corvane had said only last week, *we cannot afford another quarter like the last*, and Juniper was tired, and the hour was late, and the client's confidence had the specific texture of someone who already knew the answer.

"One date," Juniper said. "Spoken plainly, entered in the record as given."

"Nine years past," the client said, "the third day of the Drowning Month."

And something cold walked up Juniper's spine wearing small wet boots, because Juniper knew dates the way other people knew faces, and the third day of the Drowning Month, nine years past, was—

Was the first day Juniper could remember anything at all.

Everything before it was white. Not darkness—darkness would have been kinder, darkness would at least imply something to be dark *about*. White. A blank page where a childhood should have been. The healers had called it battle-shock; the priests had called it the god's mercy; Juniper had privately called it nothing at all, because thinking about it too hard felt like pressing on a bruise inside the skull. Their life began that morning in a field hospital with a stranger's hand on their forehead and a name in their mouth that wasn't quite theirs, and everything before was unwritten.

And now a stranger in expensive gloves wanted to buy the erasure of an entry dated to the exact day the erasure of *Juniper* began.

"Give me a moment," Juniper heard themselves say, from a great distance. "I'll consult the manifest."

* * *

The vault breathed cold and quiet beneath the guildhouse, three levels down, shelves receding into dark like the ribs of some enormous patient beast. Juniper took a lamp and went alone—Tam offered, and was refused, because some instincts run deeper than politeness.

Volume Nine lived on the ninth shelf, naturally. The ledgers were shelved by number and temper; Volume Nine was a cooperative volume, mostly, except for the storm entries, which sulked. Juniper found the date within four minutes. They knew they would. They knew the volume the way a priest knows scripture, which was, in fairness, close to the literal truth.

Third day of the Drowning Month, nine years past.

The entry was there. Of course it was there. Juniper's own handwriting, neat and certain:

"*Received into containment, one (1) item, nature undisclosed by prior arrangement. Provenance: field of Harrow's Ford. Transferred from military custody. Fee waived—see standing contract, founder's dispensation. Note:*" and here the young-Juniper's hand had pressed harder, the ink deeper, "*note: the item wept all night and asked for its mother.*"

Juniper stood in the cold and read it three times.

They had no memory of writing it. No memory of Harrow's Ford, of a transfer, of a weeping thing asking for its mother. The handwriting was theirs beyond doubt—the particular way they crossed their sevens, the little hook on the *d*. And yet the page felt like a letter from a stranger who knew them intimately, or worse, like their own handwriting on a wall they'd never seen, in a house they'd never entered.

*Fee waived—see standing contract, founder's dispensation.* The founder had died sixty years ago. What dispensation? What standing contract? Juniper knew every article, every rider, every marginal note in the guild's charter, and there was no founder's dispensation. There had never been one.

Unless it had been erased too.

The lamp hissed. Somewhere far above, the drunk had stopped singing, and the silence that replaced him seemed deliberate somehow, attentive, like the vault itself had leaned in to listen.

Juniper looked at the entry for a long time. Then, moving as if underwater, they did the thing the rules demanded and the heart resisted: they closed the volume, returned it to its shelf, and climbed back up toward the light and the waiting gold and the smooth-voiced stranger, carrying the one thought they could not put down, the way you carry a splinter too deep to dig out:

*What did I write, and why don't I remember writing it—and what else in these pages is mine that I've never lived?*

In the receiving room, the client waited exactly where they'd left them, hands folded, patient as debt.

"Well?" the client said.

Juniper sat. Picked up the pen. And heard themselves say the words that would unravel everything, though it would take the whole sky going dark to show them how far the thread ran:

"The entry exists. The transaction is approved."
