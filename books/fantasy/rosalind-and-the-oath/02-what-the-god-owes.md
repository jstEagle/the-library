## 2. What the God Owes

The morning rite took eleven minutes if you did it properly and four minutes if you were honest about it.

Juniper Vasquez was, most mornings, honest about it. They lit the stub of gray candle in the chapel alcove behind the ledger room — the one nobody else used anymore, because the wax was expensive and the god was quiet — and they said the words of the Oath of Attendance in a voice pitched somewhere between reverence and complaint.

"Veth Who Does Not Speak, I speak for you. Veth Who Does Not See, I see for you. Veth Who Owes Nothing, I keep your accounts."

The candle flame stood perfectly still, as it always did. The little alcove smelled of cold stone and old ink and, faintly, of juniper — the plant, not the person; someone decades dead had thought it was funny to plant a hedge of it outside the guild's back door, and the scent had soaked into the mortar ever since.

"You owe me an explanation," Juniper told the flame. "That's what you owe. Item one on a very long list."

The flame did not answer. It never answered. Ten years now, and it had never once answered, and Juniper lit it anyway, every morning, the way a person feeds a cat that ran off years ago just in case it comes back hungry.

They told themselves it was discipline. Discipline was respectable. Discipline was the thing that kept a ledger-keeper's hand steady when a client slid a purse across the table heavy enough to crack the wood. Nobody could fault them for discipline.

It was absolutely not grief. Grief was for people who had lost something. Juniper had merely been *left*.

* * *

Ten years ago, almost to the day — Juniper kept the date in the back of the ledger, small, in pencil, where no auditor would think to look — the god's voice had gone silent mid-sentence.

Juniper remembered the sentence. That was the cruel part. Not that Veth had stopped speaking, but *where* he'd stopped, like a letter cut off by a closing door.

They had been nineteen, and bleeding, and sitting in the mud behind the barricades at Cael Ford with six other soldiers who were mostly children wearing too-large coats, and the war's last night was coming down around them like a roof. And Veth — who had spoken to Juniper since they were nine years old, since the starving winter when a mercenary company had found a feral child in a burned granary and a silent god had found the child right back — Veth had said:

*Little oath-bearer. Listen carefully. There is something I must give you before—*

And then nothing.

Not silence like sleep. Silence like a room where someone had been standing a moment ago and the air hadn't yet figured out what to do about the space they left.

Juniper had waited three days at Cael Ford. Then three weeks. The other soldiers had drifted home or into graves, and Juniper had drifted to the Gilded Lantern, because the Lantern took people with odd skills and no references, and "the last person a dying god talked to" was, it turned out, a marketable oddity even if the god himself had stopped paying out.

They had performed the rites alone ever since. Alone in a chapel built for forty, then alone in a chapel alcove built for one, which was at least more efficient with the candles.

* * *

Presently, the candle sputtered once — a draft from the corridor, nothing more — and Juniper finished the rite and went to work, because the god might be allowed his silences but the ledgers were not.

Tuesday's audit stack sat waiting: three bottled storms due for quality inspection (one had developed a worrying rattle), a contract for two borrowed years (lender deceased, terms transferring to his daughter, who would need to be notified gently), and a name that had never been spoken, held in escrow for a client who paid storage on it quarterly and had never once asked to hear it. Good business, unspoken names. Low maintenance. They kept better than storms did.

Juniper worked through the morning with the particular contentment of a person putting the world in order one line at a time. Debit. Credit. Provenance. Condition. The Gilded Lantern's first rule, chalked over the vault door in the founder's own hand: *We do not sell what we do not understand.* The second rule, smaller, added by some later master with a sense of humor: *Understand it anyway.*

By midday the light through the ledger room's high window had gone honey-colored, and Marrow — no, not yet, that came later, don't rush — by midday the guild was awake around them in its usual way: the clink of the assay room, Brannic arguing with a client about whether a bottled fog counted as weather or memory, someone laughing in the stairwell. A building full of people trading in impossible things, all of them pretending it was ordinary work, all of them, in Juniper's private opinion, lying beautifully.

Juniper liked them. That was the trouble with the Lantern. You came here to be alone with your grief and instead you acquired colleagues.

* * *

Here is how the oath happened, if anyone ever asked, which no one did:

The winter Juniper was nine, the war had already eaten their village, their mother's name, and most of their teeth's enamel, in roughly that order. A mercenary company called the Gray Company found them in a granary that had burned so thoroughly the grain had turned to glass, and the company's sergeant — a woman named Hesk with a voice like a cart on gravel — looked at this feral child gnawing glass-sweetened char and said, "Well. It's got good survival instincts. Feed it."

And while Juniper ate — the first real meal in a season, bread and dripping, eaten so fast they barely tasted it — something else fed them too. Not food. *Attention.* A vast, patient, wordless attention settling over them like a blanket, and then, inside their skull, in a voice like a man speaking very quietly in a very large empty hall:

*You are the only one in this place who can hear me. Do you know why?*

"No," Juniper said, out loud, mouth full, and Hesk gave them a strange look, and the god laughed — a sound like wind deciding to be kind.

*Neither do I,* he said. *Isn't that wonderful? Two mysteries, keeping each other company.*

His name was Veth. He was old the way mountains are old, and tired the way mountains are not, because mountains have never fought a war. He had been the god of the borderlands once — of thresholds, of debts, of things left unsaid — and the empire had spent four hundred years trying to kill him and the neighboring gods had spent those same four hundred years pretending not to notice, and through all of it he had kept one law above all others: he did not lie, and he did not abandon a debt.

"I don't want to be a soldier," nine-year-old Juniper told him, because he asked, directly, what they wanted, which no adult had ever done.

*Then don't be one,* he said. *Be my record-keeper. Every debt I'm owed, every debt I owe. Someone has to keep the books. Gods are terrible at bookkeeping.*

"What do you owe?" Juniper asked. "What does a god owe?"

*Ah,* said Veth, and there was something in his voice then that Juniper would spend thirty years trying to name — something like fear, something like hope, something like a man looking at a door he both needed and dreaded to open. *That is the whole question, little oath-bearer. One day I will tell you what I owe. I promise it. I put it in the books myself.*

He taught them letters on a slate. He taught them numbers in the dirt. He taught them that silence was not emptiness — that a silence could hold a promise the way a sheath holds a knife, ready and waiting and not threatening until drawn. When the Gray Company fell apart after the war's worst year, Veth's quiet counsel kept Juniper alive through two winters and one very bad marriage proposal, and when Juniper finally swore the formal oath at fourteen — kneeling in a ruined chapel with a candle stub, saying the words that bound a mortal to a god's service — it had felt less like signing a contract and more like coming home to a house they'd been living in for years.

*I accept your service,* Veth said, solemn as a funeral. *And I accept my own. Remember that half of any oath is what the greater party owes. Most gods forget. I wrote mine down.*

"Where?"

*In the safest place I know,* he said, and would say no more.

Five years later, mid-sentence, he was gone.

*There is something I must give you before—*

Before *what*?

Ten years, and the sentence still hung there, unfinished, the largest outstanding entry in any ledger Juniper had ever kept. An open liability on the god's side of the page. Juniper was, professionally speaking, obligated to pursue it.

Personally speaking, they were too much of a coward to ask aloud what it meant that he'd promised to give them something and then vanished rather than do it. Because there were only two possibilities, and Juniper circled them nightly like a moth at a lamp: either Veth had been destroyed — taken, killed, unmade — in which case Juniper had failed him at the exact moment he'd needed them; or Veth had *chosen* to stop, had looked at his little oath-bearer, mud-covered and nineteen and trusting, and decided whatever he had to give was too dangerous to hand over.

Abandoned, or a failure. Those were the options. Juniper flipped between them the way other people flipped a coin, except the coin never landed.

So: the rites. Every morning. Eleven minutes, or four. Stubbornness dressed up as devotion, devotion dressed up as routine, routine dressed up as professionalism. If anyone at the guild noticed that the ledger-keeper talked to a candle, they had the grace not to mention it. Brannic had once asked, mildly, why Juniper bothered, since the Silent God hadn't taken prayers in a decade, and Juniper had said, "You keep paying storage on a name nobody's ever going to use," and Brannic had nodded like that settled it, and in a way it did.

You didn't stop honoring a debt just because the other party had gone quiet. Especially when you didn't know whose side the debt was on anymore.

* * *

That evening, Juniper did the second rite — the evening attendance, shorter, mostly bookkeeping. In the old days, when the god spoke, this was when he'd reviewed the day's accounts with them, murmuring corrections, occasionally insisting that mercy be entered as an asset rather than an expense ("It compounds, little one. You'll see."). Now Juniper read the entries aloud anyway. All of them. Even the boring ones. Especially the boring ones, because if Veth was anywhere, listening, he'd want the boring ones; he'd always said the truth of any enterprise lived in its dull pages.

"Bottled storm, third of three, integrity compromised, scheduled for release over open water Thursday. Borrowed years, two, transferred per terms of estate. Unspoken name, storage fee received, condition stable."

The candle burned. The alcove held its cold and its juniper smell.

"And," Juniper said, and paused, because this next part was not in any official book. "Item outstanding, ten years, no movement. 'Something I must give you before.' Before *what*, you great silent mountain of a god. You owed me a sentence. You couldn't finish a *sentence*."

The flame leaned, very slightly, though there was no draft.

Juniper watched it lean, and their heart did something complicated and painful, and they hated themselves for the leap of hope and hated the god for the fall that always followed.

"Draft," they said firmly, to the alcove, to themselves. "Old building."

They snuffed the candle. In the dark, the unfinished sentence sat in its usual place behind their ribs, patient as a creditor.

*There is something I must give you before—*

Before the war ended. Before the empire won. Before—

Juniper stopped in the doorway of the ledger room, one hand on the frame, struck suddenly by a thought that should not have taken ten years to arrive: *before* was a boundary word. Whatever Veth had meant to give, it belonged to the moment the war closed. Which meant it was tied to the ending. Which meant, if anyone ever tried to *un*-end the war — reopen it, reignite it, drag the old borders back out of history's pockets —

Which was absurd. The war was over. Everyone knew the war was over. The treaties were signed, the wards along the border had held quiet for a decade, the stars came up every night in their ancient reliable patterns, and the Gilded Lantern traded its impossible things in a world that was, whatever else you said about it, at peace.

Juniper shook their head at their own fancy, lit the desk lamp, and opened the audit stack again, because work was work and dread was not billable.

Outside the high window, the first stars were coming out. Juniper did not count them. They wished, later, that they had — that they'd marked each one, the way you mark a set of dishes before a dinner party, so you'd know exactly what was missing when the cupboard was found empty.

But that was three nights away, and the candle stubs were stocked, and the ledgers balanced, and Juniper Vasquez went home to their narrow bed above the ledger room and slept the sleep of a person whose accounts are in order, unaware that somewhere below the vault, below the vault below the vault, something that had been silent for ten years was being *handled* — weighed, portioned, priced — and that the price was about to come due.
