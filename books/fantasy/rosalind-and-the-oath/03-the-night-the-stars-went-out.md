## 3. The Night the Stars Went Out

The trouble with a quiet night at the Gilded Lantern, Juniper had learned over ten years of keeping its ledgers, was that it never stayed quiet. It only held its breath.

So when the lamp above the counting desk flickered—once, twice, in a rhythm too regular to be wind—Juniper set down their pen mid-entry and said, to the empty ledger room, "No."

The lamp went out anyway.

Not all at once. The flame shrank to a blue bead, guttered, and steadied itself into something that burned without warmth, like a memory of fire rather than fire itself. Juniper had seen that trick before, once, on the worst night of the war: wardlight bleeding through ordinary flame when the great wards drew breath. It meant the border wards were working hard.

It did not mean they were working *well*.

Juniper rose, took the lamp from its hook—the physical one, oil and wick, the kind that couldn't be lied to—and went out into the corridor. The Gilded Lantern at night was a body at rest: floorboards ticking as they cooled, the cellar breathing its smell of old paper and older wine up through the seams, someone snoring three doors down with the dedication of a man who'd been paid to sleep through anything. Old Tamms, the doorwarden. He could sleep through a siege. He had, once. It was in the ledgers.

"Tamms," Juniper said, rapping on his doorframe.

The snoring hitched, resumed, then stopped entirely. "If it's the ghost of the founder again," Tamms said thickly, "tell him I still say he shorted me on the Kellin job."

"It's not the founder." Juniper held up the lamp. Its light lay wrong against the wall—too pale, too thin, the shadows falling in directions the flame didn't justify. "Look at your window."

Tamms squinted at him, then at the shuttered window, and whatever he saw in Juniper's face made him sit up without another joke. That was the thing about soldiers turned guildsmen: they kept the part of themselves that knew when the jokes were done.

They climbed together to the lantern room at the top of the house—the actual gilded lantern, the one that gave the guild its name, a great caged beacon of wardglass that hadn't been lit since the armistice. It wasn't for lighting roads. It was for reading the sky.

Juniper opened the shutters and looked out, and felt the bottom drop quietly out of the evening.

The stars were going out.

Not being covered—there were no clouds; the air was dry and cold and clean as rinsed glass. They were going out the way candles go out when someone walks past them: in order, one after another, a darkness moving across the sky like a finger drawn over a table of little flames. First the low stars near the eastern horizon, then the higher ones, constellations winking off constellation by constellation—the Shepherd, the Sisters, the long slow river of the Milky Road dimming from its far end toward them like water draining from a bath.

"Oh," said Tamms, very softly. "Oh, that's not weather."

"No," Juniper said. "That's not weather."

* * *

By the time the last star nearest the zenith guttered and died, the whole guild was awake and crowded into the common hall, because Guild Master Corvane had rung the bell himself—three strokes, the old war signal, *shelter and silence*—and Corvane's bells were not suggestions.

He stood at the head of the long table with his hands flat on the wood, and even now, even with the sky gone black as an uninked page, he looked less afraid than *annoyed*, which was somehow worse. Corvane was a narrow man, precise as a ledger line, with grey in his beard arranged as if it had applied for the position. He had run the Gilded Lantern for nine years the way a good clerk runs anything: firmly, frugally, and with the deep conviction that everything, given time, could be filed.

"Inside," he said, though everyone already was. "All of you. Doors barred, shutters closed, no lamps in the front rooms. We don't know what's coming down that sky and we're not going to stand at windows admiring it."

"What is coming down it?" asked Wren, the youngest of the handlers, hugging a bottle of something medicinal she'd liberated on her way upstairs.

"Nothing, with any luck." Corvane's eyes moved around the room, counting heads the way Juniper counted entries. "The border wards have failed. All of them. Every ring, every node, from the Ashline to the sea."

Murmurs. Someone said *impossible*. Someone else said *the empire*, and someone else said *the empire's fifty years dead*, and both were true, which was the sort of arithmetic Juniper hated.

"Failed how?" Juniper asked.

The room quieted. It was not, perhaps, the moment for procedural questions, but Juniper had never found a better one, and Corvane, to his credit, didn't pretend otherwise.

"Cascading," he said. "Ring by ring, inward. The outermost fell first, then each inner ring as the pressure came onto it. Standard collapse pattern." He paused, and something crossed his face—quick, controlled, gone. "Except faster. Much faster. As though the rings weren't resisting at all."

"As though they'd been *opened*," Juniper said.

Corvane looked at them for a long moment. In the lamplight his eyes were the color of wet slate. "Wards break," he said. "That's what wards do. They are walls, and walls fall."

"Yes," Juniper agreed pleasantly. "And doors open. And those are different things, master, and we both know it."

Nobody breathed. Corvane held the look a heartbeat longer, then inclined his head a fraction—an acknowledgment, or a warning; with Corvane the two came in the same wrapping. "Bar the doors," he repeated. "Handler Vasquez, with me. Everyone else: sleep if you can, pray if you must, and touch nothing in the vault."

* * *

What Corvane wanted, it turned out, was a bonfire.

He had the correspondence trays brought up to the yard behind the guildhouse—all of it, six months of letters, contracts, receipts, the daily traffic of a guild that traded in impossible things and therefore wrote an enormous number of extremely compromising notes—and he fed it into the iron brazier piece by piece while Juniper stood in the cold holding a lamp and watching ten years of careful record-keeping go up in smoke.

"You're burning evidence," Juniper said.

"I'm burning *correspondence*," Corvane said. "Evidence is what other people keep. We keep ledgers, and the ledgers stay." He fed in another letter. Juniper caught a glimpse of the seal before it curled—a client's mark, one of the old houses—and then it was ash. "When the sky goes dark, Vasquez, everything anyone has ever written becomes a weapon. Best not to be holding any."

"That's almost wise."

"It's entirely wise. That's why it sounds cold." He glanced over. "You disapprove."

"I *record*," Juniper said. "Disapproval is above my pay grade. But I'll note for the ledger that on the night the stars went out, the guild master burned six months of correspondence within the hour, unprompted, and seemed to have the tray sorted by burn-order already."

Something flickered at the corner of Corvane's mouth. It might have been a smile. It might have been a flinch. "Sorted by sensitivity," he corrected. "Naturally."

"Naturally."

For a while there was only the fire, and the cold, and the terrible velvet blackness overhead where the stars should have been. Without starlight the yard was lit only by the brazier, and the darkness beyond it had a texture to it, a weight, like standing at the edge of deep water at night. Juniper had grown up under open skies full of enemies and had never once been afraid of the dark. This was different. This was a dark with intention behind it.

"The rites," Corvane said suddenly.

Juniper blinked. "Master?"

"You perform the daily rites to Veth. Alone, every dawn, ten years running, even though—" He stopped himself with the delicacy of a man stepping back from a ledge. "Even though the voice went silent. Do you feel anything? Tonight, I mean. Any change."

It was an odd question, and the fact that Corvane asked it told Juniper more than the question itself. Guild masters did not concern themselves with private devotions unless private devotions touched guild business—and Veth's silence, Veth's *anything*, had not been guild business in a decade.

"They're the same as always," Juniper said. "Quiet. I do them anyway."

"Why?"

"Because an oath isn't a transaction." Juniper watched the fire eat another letter. "You don't stop paying a debt because the creditor stopped writing back."

"Hm," said Corvane, in the tone of a man filing something away, and Juniper thought—not for the first time—that if Corvane had been born a century earlier he'd have made a magnificent inquisitor, and that it was fortunate for everyone he'd gone into trade instead.

Mostly fortunate.

* * *

Juniper didn't sleep. Sleep required believing tomorrow would resemble today, and tonight the sky had resigned its position.

Instead they went down to the ledger room, lit every lamp they owned, and pulled the ward-logs.

Every guild kept books, but the Gilded Lantern kept two sets: the trade ledgers, which recorded what was bought and sold, and the ward-logs, which recorded the health of the protections around the vault—the nested wards, keyed and counter-keyed, that made the vault a vault. Juniper had maintained the logs personally for eight years. Not because Corvane ordered it. Because Juniper was oath-bound to Veth, god of silences and settled debts, and the vault's deepest layer was sealed with an oath-lock, and oath-bearers could *feel* oath-work the way sailors feel weather in their teeth.

Ten years ago, Juniper would have felt this coming a week out.

Tonight they'd felt nothing until the lamp flickered. That alone should have terrified them. It nearly got lost, at first, under the larger horror of the numbers.

The ward-logs told the story in cold columns. At moonrise, the outermost border ring had dropped from full strength to zero in under four minutes. No degradation curve. No strain readings. No bleed. A ward under assault *hurt*—the logs filled with tremor-marks, stress-flutter, the scribbled signatures of a structure fighting. These pages were smooth. Clean. The outer ring hadn't fought and died. It had simply, between one hourly reading and the next, ceased to be active.

Then the second ring. Same signature. Then the third. Ring after ring, marching inward toward the city, toward the guild, each one switching off with the tidy finality of a lamp being snuffed by someone walking past—and Juniper sat back from the desk with their heart going hard and slow, because they knew that signature.

Everyone in the trade knew it. You learned it your first year: the difference between a broken ward and a released one. Broken wards left scars in the log—jagged, bleeding, dying loud. Released wards left nothing. A released ward was a door opened from inside, with a key, by someone authorized to use it.

The border wards of the realm had not fallen.

They had been *unlocked*. All of them. In sequence. From within.

And there was exactly one class of key that fit imperial border wards. Not wardwrights' tools—those could raise and mend, but the master keys, the blood-keys, the ones woven into the founding lattice itself—those answered to one thing only. Oath. The old oaths, sworn in the old chapel, in the old words, by the men and women who'd ended the war by swearing themselves to the god who ended it.

Oath-bearers.

Juniper looked down at their own hands in the lamplight. Ordinary hands. Ink-stained, scarred at the knuckles, one finger crooked from a bad mending. Hands that had sworn, at nine years old, starving, half-dead in a trench, an oath to a silent god whose voice had gone quiet mid-sentence ten years ago and never come back.

There were perhaps forty living oath-bearers in the world. Juniper was one. And the wards had been switched off with an oath-key, and the stars had gone out, and somewhere in Juniper's chest, beneath ten years of stubborn, resentful devotion, an old instinct lifted its head like a dog hearing its name called from a great distance.

*Vasquez,* it seemed to say. *You're missing something. You've been missing something for ten years.*

Juniper wrote the entry. That was the discipline; that was the whole of the discipline. *Night of the dark sky. Border wards released, not breached. Key-type: oath-blood. Suspected holders of such keys: see attached list.* The pen scratched. The ink dried. Somewhere above, the guild creaked and settled around its sleeping people, and the darkness outside pressed against the shutters like a palm laid flat against a door, testing.

At the bottom of the page, Juniper hesitated, then added one line more—in cipher, the old field-cipher, the one from the war that only three people alive could read:

*First key on the list is mine. Find out what I've forgotten.*

Because that was the shape of it, once you sat with the numbers long enough. Not just suspicion of others. Suspicion of the log itself, of the smooth pages, of the ten-year gap in Juniper's own memory that began on the exact day the god's voice had died—and the exact day, Juniper realized now, sitting very still in the lamplight, that the earliest entry in their own hand in these ward-logs also began.

As if before that date, someone had turned a page.

As if someone had *excised* one.

Juniper blew out the lamps except one, locked the ward-logs in the oath-box, and stood a moment in the dark with their hand flat on the lid, feeling like a person who has found a door in their own house they've never seen before.

Tomorrow, they decided, they would audit the vault.

Tonight, they would bar the door, and not sleep, and listen—to the guild, to the dark, and to the small stubborn silence inside their own chest where a god's voice used to be.

It had never seemed so much like a wound.

Or so much like a lock.
