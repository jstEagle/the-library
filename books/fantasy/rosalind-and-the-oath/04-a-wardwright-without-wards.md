## 4. A Wardwright Without Wards

The trouble with being dragged anywhere, Sera Iln had long ago decided, was the indignity of the arithmetic. Two men to carry her. One of them sweating through his coat. And all of it because of a signature she hadn't put on anything in eleven years.

"Walk," said the sweating one, who had introduced himself at the tavern as a guild man and then failed to introduce himself as anything gentler than a fist to the back of her ribs.

"I am walking," Sera said. "Badly. It's a style."

"You're drunk."

"I'm *practiced*," she corrected. "There's a difference. A drunk falls down. I have not fallen down once tonight, which you'd know if you were paying attention instead of narrating."

The other one — the quiet one with the lantern and the patient expression of a man who had carried drunks before and expected to again — said nothing at all, which Sera found far more menacing. Men who talked wanted something they could name. Men who stayed silent wanted things that didn't have names yet, and those were the expensive ones.

They hauled her up a street she half-remembered from the war years, when Ashfall had still been called something else and she had still been called *Wardwright First Class, Imperial College of the Veils*, a title so grand it required its own line on correspondence. Now the street was called Lantern Row, because of course it was, and the building at the end of it was called the Gilded Lantern, because guilds had no imagination whatsoever when it came to naming themselves after their own signage.

She knew what the Gilded Lantern was. Everyone in certain circles knew. They traded in impossible things. Bottled storms. Borrowed years. Names that had never been spoken aloud, sold to people who needed them anyway. Sera had spent the war designing wards against exactly the kind of commerce the Lantern conducted, which meant she had spent the war losing arguments with reality.

And now, apparently, her signature was on the border wards. The ones that had failed three nights ago. The ones whose failure had taken every star out of the sky from horizon to horizon, so that the whole world now sat under a ceiling of black like a lid on a pot.

"They forged it," she said, for perhaps the ninth time since the tavern. "I haven't signed anything imperial since the armistice. I burned my seal. I *ate* the ashes, if you must know, on a dare, and they tasted like regret and copper."

"Nobody's accusing you," said the quiet one.

"That's worse," Sera said. "When people accuse you, at least you know what they want."

The doors of the Gilded Lantern opened before they reached them, which told Sera two things: first, that someone inside had been watching the street; second, that whoever it was had decided she was coming in regardless of her opinion on the matter. She appreciated efficiency. She resented it deeply. Both feelings could coexist; she was a professional.

* * *

The interior of the guild was warmer than she expected and more honest-looking than it had any right to be. She had imagined vaults and shadows and men counting coins made of other people's Tuesdays. Instead there were ledgers everywhere — stacked, shelved, chained, some of them clearly older than the empire itself — and a smell of ink and lamp oil and, faintly, of rain that hadn't fallen yet. Impossible things left a residue. She'd always been able to smell it. It was the reason she'd been good at her job and the reason she drank: the world smelled wrong near powerful workings, and wine covered it the way snow covers a grave.

They sat her in a chair that had been positioned, she noticed, with its back to no wall and its view of both doors. Someone here thought tactically. Or someone here had been a soldier.

Then the soldier walked in, and Sera's stomach did something complicated and unwelcome.

She knew the face. Not well — war gave you a thousand faces and let you keep maybe forty — but well enough. Ashfall, the last year. A mercenary company holding a stretch of trench that the imperial engineers had written off, and a supply officer — no, not supply. Records. There had been a person with a ledger who came through the mud like the mud had agreed to part, carrying a book under an oilcloth as though it were a child, and who had looked at Sera — nineteen, shaking, three days without sleep, holding a failing ward-line together with wire and swearing — and said, in a voice flat as a settled account:

*"You're the one keeping us alive. What do you need?"*

Nobody asked wardwrights what they needed. Wardwrights were equipment. But this one had asked, and then had actually gotten the things — copper wire, salt, a crate of candles that turned out to be worth more than the trench — and Sera had lived, and had never learned the person's name, because by the time she went to find them the company had rotated out and the war had moved on to eat somewhere else.

Now the face was older, sharper at the edges, with a scar along the jaw she didn't remember and eyes that held the particular stillness of someone listening to a room even while talking in it.

"Juniper Vasquez," the person said. "Ledger-keeper, second chair. You're Sera Iln."

"Sera Iln," Sera agreed, "who is extremely drunk and would like the record to show that everything I say tonight is provisional."

"The record will show exactly what happened," Juniper said, sitting across from her and setting down a ledger with a soft, terrible finality. "That's rather the point of records."

"Oh, you're one of *those*." Sera slumped back. "A true believer in the book. I knew a quartermaster like you. He could tell you how many nails the Third Legion used in a decade and he cried at inventories. Are you going to cry?"

"No."

"Pity. It humanizes you."

For a moment — a small, treacherous moment — something moved behind Juniper Vasquez's stillness that might, in a less controlled face, have been a smile. Then it was gone, filed away, and Sera understood that she was looking at a person who kept themselves the way they kept their books: entered in full, balanced nightly, nothing left loose on the page.

Someone had hurt this one badly, she thought. Recently or long ago, hard to say. People who balanced that carefully were usually compensating for a column that wouldn't add up.

"Three nights ago," Juniper said, "the border wards failed. All of them. In sequence, inner ring to outer, like someone walking down a staircase switching off lamps."

"I heard. Hard to miss. The sky went out."

"The wards were keyed to oath-bearer blood. That's imperial design. Pre-armistice design. And on the master control lattice of the innermost ring, there is a countersignature." Juniper turned the ledger around and slid it across. "Yours."

Sera looked. Her stomach dropped through the floor, through the cellar, into whatever impossible things the guild kept below the cellar.

It was her signature. Not similar. Not close. It was *hers* — the little hitch in the crossbar of the I, the way the final stroke of the n trailed off like she'd already lost interest in finishing it, which she always had. She had signed ten thousand work orders during the war and every one of them looked exactly like that, including the flaw where the ink pooled in the descender because she pressed too hard on the downstroke, a habit her instructors had beaten at her for four years and never cured.

Except she hadn't signed it. She would stake her remaining liver on it.

"That's mine," she said quietly.

"Good. We agree—"

"It's mine and I didn't write it." She pushed the ledger back with one finger, careful, the way you'd push back a snake. "Do you understand what I'm telling you? This isn't a forgery. Forgeries are *approximations*. This is my hand. Whoever made this either copied a genuine sample with a skill I've never seen outside the College's own authentication cell — which was disbanded, and its master drowned, officially — or—" She stopped.

"Or?" Juniper said.

"Or they took it out of my head." Sera heard her own voice go flat and hated it. "Memory-work. It exists. It's illegal in three jurisdictions and practiced in all of them. If someone pulled the shape of my signature out of my memory, they wouldn't need to forge anything. They'd just need me to have signed something once, in the right context, and lift it whole."

The silence that followed had weight. Somewhere upstairs a clock ticked with the smugness of clocks.

"Why you?" Juniper asked finally. "Of all the wardwrights the empire trained — and there were hundreds — why does your hand open our border?"

"How should I know? Why does anyone do anything? Money, fear, spite, boredom." Sera rubbed her face. Her hands smelled of tavern. "Look. I was Wardwright First Class. I designed three of the seven ward-lattices on that border. If someone wanted a signature that would pass inspection, mine is a reasonable choice. I'm famous, in the narrow sense that certain people hate me. After the armistice I testified about the College's memory-extraction program. Publicly. Loudly. Drunk, the third day, but loudly."

Something flickered across Juniper's face at the words *memory-extraction*. Something quick and involuntary, like a flinch behind glass.

Sera had spent eleven years learning to read people badly on purpose, because reading them well had been her job and she was retired. But she read that flitch anyway, because some skills are like scars: they don't ask your permission to stay.

"You know something about memory-work," she said.

"I know it's illegal in three jurisdictions," Juniper said, "and practiced in all of them."

"That's my line."

"Then we've both been quoting someone wiser." Juniper closed the ledger. "Here is what I can offer. The guild will pay you to investigate the forgery. Not the empire. Us. You'll have access to the ward-lattice records, the transaction history of the border contracts, and whatever protection a guild that trades in impossible things can extend, which is considerable and slightly frightening."

"And in exchange?"

"In exchange, you find out who did this. Not who we should blame. Who *did* it. Those aren't always the same answer, and I've noticed that people who confuse them tend to make very tidy mistakes."

Sera stared at her. Him. Them. She genuinely couldn't tell and suspected that was deliberate, another layer of the ledger-keeper's armor, and she found — annoyingly, inconveniently — that she liked Juniper Vasquez the way you like a locked door with good hinges: with respect, and with an itch to see what's inside.

"What's your rate?" she asked. "I don't come cheap. Well. I come extremely cheap, actually, that's the problem, my rate is currently negotiable down to a warm meal and being allowed to sleep indoors. But historically—"

"We don't pay in coin."

"Of course you don't."

"We pay in kind. Guild currency." Juniper folded their hands on the ledger. "One impossible thing. Anything within our inventory, or anything we can acquire. Bottled storm. Borrowed year. A name never spoken. You name it when the work is done, and we don't haggle."

Sera laughed. It came out rusty, surprised out of her, and she couldn't remember the last time that had happened — laughter arriving before permission, like a bird through a window.

"One impossible thing," she repeated. "You realize what you're offering a disgraced imperial wardwright with a drinking problem and a forged signature on the worst catastrophe in a generation?"

"A dangerous amount of leverage over us, yes."

"No," Sera said. "I mean you're offering me the exact thing I've wanted for eleven years and haven't dared want out loud." She leaned forward, and the lamp caught the old burn scar along her forearm, the one shaped like a branching river, the one she'd gotten pulling a collapsing veil off a hospital full of children while the sky rained cinders. "I want a year back. My last year of the war. The whole of it, unedited, in my own head, where it belongs. Because I have gaps, Ledger-keeper Vasquez. Big ones. Whole seasons gone smooth as river stone, and everyone tells me that's the drink, and maybe it is, but I testified against the College's extraction program and six months later my memory has holes in it shaped exactly like testimony, and I have never once been able to prove it."

The words hung there. She hadn't meant to say all of it. Wine was a poor lock on a cheap door.

Juniper Vasquez had gone very still. Not bored-still. Still the way water goes still over something enormous moving underneath.

"Gaps," Juniper said at last. Their voice had changed — quieter, careful, a hand testing ice. "How long? Your earliest continuous memory. When does it start?"

"What an odd question."

"When does it start?"

Sera frowned, dredging. "Ten years ago. Give or take. Everything before that is... summaries. I know facts about my childhood the way you know facts about a stranger's. I can't *see* any of it." She squinted. "Why? What's yours?"

And there it was again — that flinch behind glass, faster this time, and this time followed by something else. Recognition. The specific, appalled recognition of one liar meeting another's lie and finding it familiar as a family face.

"Mine starts ten years ago too," Juniper said.

The lamp hissed. Upstairs, the clock ticked on, insufferable.

"Well," Sera said softly. "Isn't that a coincidence."

"I don't believe in coincidences. I believe in entries, and someone has been making entries in both of us." Juniper stood, and for the first time Sera saw past the ledger-keeper's composure to the thing underneath it, which was not calm at all but a kind of controlled freefall, a person who had looked into their own book and found pages missing. "You'll take the work?"

"For one impossible thing." Sera raised her empty cup in something between a toast and a surrender. "A year of my own life, returned. And if it turns out somebody stole it — if it turns out somebody stole it from *both* of us — then I want the thief's name entered in full, in your book, in your handwriting, so it stays written."

Juniper's jaw tightened. "That can be arranged."

"And Vasquez?" Sera said, as the ledger-keeper turned toward the door. "Ashfall. Last year of the war. Trench on the river bend. There was a records officer who got me wire and candles when the entire imperial command said the position was unsalvageable. Kept about two hundred people alive for nine days." She paused. "That was you, wasn't it?"

A long moment. The lamp flame leaned and straightened.

"I don't remember," Juniper said quietly. "But it sounds like something I would have done."

* * *

They gave her a room on the third floor with a bed that was honest about being narrow and a window that looked out over Lantern Row, where the starless dark sat on the rooftops like a held breath. Sera sat on the bed with her boots off — a concession to civilization — and a cup of the guild's coffee, which was terrible in a sincere way, and turned the evening over in her mind like a coin she suspected was clipped.

Her signature, perfect, on wards she'd helped design, opening a border she'd helped close. Her memory, holed, starting cleanly ten years ago. A ledger-keeper with the same clean edge in their past, wearing grief like a coat they'd tailored themselves.

Two liars, she thought. Recognizing each other's scars across a table. Because that was the thing about lies — real ones, the load-bearing kind, the kind you built a self on top of — they scarred in patterns. And she'd seen the pattern on Juniper Vasquez tonight as plainly as she'd ever seen a ward-lattice: something cut out, cauterized, and the whole structure rebuilt around the absence so skillfully that only another amputee would notice the balance was wrong.

Somewhere below her, beneath the ledgers and the vaults and whatever impossible things slept in the dark down there, the answer was probably sitting in a file with a ribbon around it.

Sera finished the coffee, grimaced, and lay back.

Tomorrow she'd start with the border contract records. Tonight she'd sleep, if the sky's absence let her, which it might not; she'd spent her whole career putting lights in the dark, and now the dark had won a round, and some animal part of her kept trying to stay awake to guard against it.

Just before sleep took her, one thought surfaced, clear and cold as a struck bell:

*Whoever forged my signature needed a sample of my hand. And the best sample of anyone's hand isn't on paper.*

*It's in their head.*

She was asleep before she could decide whether that thought was a lead or a warning.

Downstairs, unaware of her conclusion, Juniper Vasquez was lighting a lamp over the vault ledgers — and preparing to count what was missing.
