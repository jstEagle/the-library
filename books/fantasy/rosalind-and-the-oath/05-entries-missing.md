## 5. Entries Missing

There is a particular kind of silence that lives in the vault of the Gilded Lantern, and Juniper Vasquez had long ago made peace with it. It was not the silence of emptiness—nothing in that room was empty—but the silence of things being *kept*. Bottled storms murmured on their shelves like sleeping cats. Borrowed years ticked faintly in their lead-lined drawers, each one a little impatient to be spent. A jar near the back held a name that had never been spoken by anyone, and it hummed with the smug contentment of a secret that had never once been ruined by telling.

Juniper loved the vault the way other people loved chapels.

Which was, they reflected, pulling on the white audit gloves, probably a strange thing for an oath-bearer of Veth to admit. The silent god had no use for chapels. He had no use for much of anything anymore, having gone quiet ten years ago mid-sentence, like a man struck dumb at his own dinner table. But devotion, Juniper had learned, was mostly habit wearing a nicer coat, and so they performed the rites anyway: touched two fingers to the ledger's spine, breathed out slow, and said nothing, because Veth had never required words. Only accuracy.

"Right," Juniper said aloud, to the shelves, because talking to oneself was cheaper than drinking and only slightly less dignified. "Let's find out what we're pretending not to know."

The audit was routine. Quarterly, by guild law: every entry verified against its collateral, every collateral weighed against its entry, every discrepancy flagged in red ink and reported to Guild Master Corvane, who would then do absolutely nothing about it, which was itself a kind of tradition.

Juniper began at the beginning, as one does, and for three hours it was comfortingly dull. Three vials of bottled thunderstorm, accounted for; one had developed a leak and was now responsible for the damp patch in the east wall, which explained why the mops kept rusting. Fourteen borrowed years, all present, all ticking. A commission for a duke's second son who wanted to purchase the memory of his first love and had been refused twice on grounds of cruelty—the guild traded in impossible things, but Corvane drew the line somewhere, allegedly.

Then Juniper reached the ledger for the year 1147, and the world tilted.

It was a small thing at first. A page number out of sequence. 1147 ran from folio 201 to folio 268, and between 214 and 215 there was a seam—a whisper-thin ridge where paper met paper that wasn't quite the same age. Juniper ran a gloved thumb along it. Then another. Then, with the slow dread of a person who has stepped on ice and heard it speak, they went looking for more.

There were eleven seams in the 1147 ledger alone.

Eleven pages, excised. Not torn—torn pages leave grief behind them, ragged edges, ink transferred where a hasty hand gripped too hard. These were cut, cleanly, with a blade and a straightedge and all the time in the world. Whoever did it had then sanded the stubs so the remaining pages wouldn't catch, and had rebound the spine so skillfully that only someone who had spent fifteen years learning the exact weight of a ledger's breathing would ever notice.

Someone who was very good at this had done it. Someone trained the way Juniper had been trained.

"No," Juniper said to the shelf of bottled storms, and the nearest storm, sensing mood, flickered sulkily inside its glass.

They pulled the next ledger. More seams. And the next. They worked through the afternoon and into the blue hour when the vault's lamps came up on their own, warded to wake at dusk, and the seams kept coming—not everywhere, but in clusters, decades of them, and always around the same client.

The Chantry of the Silent Star.

Juniper knew the name, of course. Everyone in the trade knew it. A minor religious order, quiet, wealthy in the way old institutions are wealthy—slowly, invisibly, through property and patience. They'd been buying from the guild for as long as the ledgers recorded. Small purchases, mostly. Silences. Unspoken names. Once, memorably, an entire hour that had never happened, purchased in bulk and stored in something that looked like a pickle jar and was most certainly not a pickle jar.

But the entries that remained told a story by their absence. Every transaction with the Chantry sat surrounded by cut pages like a house standing alone in a burned street. Whatever the Chantry had been buying in quantity, someone had decided history didn't need to know.

And here was the thing that made Juniper set down the ledger and press both palms flat against the cold stone floor until the vertigo passed:

Every excision cluster ended with a certification mark. The small, precise glyph in the lower margin that said, in the language of the guild, *this record has been reviewed and found complete.* The auditor's mark.

Juniper's mark.

* * *

There were rules about the auditor's mark. There were rules about everything in the Gilded Lantern, but the mark was sacred even by those standards. It could only be applied by the sitting Keeper of Ledgers, in person, with their own hand. It could not be delegated, forged without death, or applied to a record the keeper had not personally verified. Juniper had put that mark beside perhaps four thousand entries in fifteen years of service, and they remembered maybe half of them individually, but they remembered the *act* of it—the small ceremony of it, the breath before ink.

They did not remember marking anything for the Chantry of the Silent Star.

Which meant one of three things. Either someone had learned to counterfeit the mark perfectly—which was possible, though it would require a skill set that overlapped unsettlingly with Juniper's own—or the entries had been marked before the pages around them were cut, by Juniper, legitimately, and Juniper simply couldn't remember doing it. Or—

Or the third thing. The third thing was the memory erasure from last month. The client who had paid an obscene sum to have *one memory, name withheld* removed from the vault, dated the day before Juniper's earliest recollection began. Juniper had filed that unease away in the drawer where they kept things they weren't ready to think about, between the war and the silence of Veth, and now the drawer was rattling.

They did what any sensible ledger-keeper does when the numbers stop making sense. They went back further.

* * *

The deep archive was below the main vault, down a spiral stair that smelled of candle smoke and older candles, where the founding ledgers lived under lock and ward. Juniper signed the access book—*Vasquez, J., quarterly audit, extended scope*, written in a hand that shook slightly—and carried a lamp into the dark.

The founding ledgers were enormous, vellum-bound things with iron corners, and the first one opened to a page in the founder's own writing: *Let it be recorded that the Lantern exists to hold what cannot be held elsewhere.* Juniper had read it a hundred times. It had always sounded like poetry. Tonight it sounded like a warning they'd been humming past for years.

They worked backward decade by decade, and the pattern held, and worsened. In the early years the Chantry's purchases were logged openly, and Juniper read them with growing coldness in the stomach. Bottled silences by the dozen. A *voice, fragmentary, provenance sealed*—that entry was dated forty years ago, and the price column contained a number followed by so many zeroes that Juniper counted them twice. A *name, true, container required*. Another fragment. Another. Like someone buying a shattered mirror piece by piece and paying fortunes for each shard.

And then, starting thirty years ago, the pages around those entries began to disappear. Cut. Sanded. Certified complete.

By Juniper's mark. By the marks of every keeper before them, going back—Juniper checked, because they had to—going back six keepers. Six generations of auditors, each one certifying the completeness of records that had been gutted around them. Either the Chantry had been forging the guild's most sacred mark for thirty years across six administrations, which strained belief past breaking—

—or every keeper who sat in that chair did the same thing Juniper had apparently done, and then forgot.

Juniper sat down on the archive floor with their back against the founding ledger, which was the closest thing the guild had to an altar, and let the horror arrive properly.

It wasn't the cuts. Cuts could be understood; cuts were commerce, or cowardice, or both. It was the handwriting. Because Juniper had gone back to the surviving Chantry entries, the ones wedged between the excisions, and read the marginalia—the little notes keepers left for their successors, *verify container seal quarterly*, *client pays in old coin, count twice*—and the handwriting was theirs. Not similar. Theirs. The way the *J* hooked. The way the crossbar on the *t* slanted up like it was trying to escape. Juniper had mocked their own handwriting often enough to know it anywhere, the way you know your own laugh.

And the notes described days Juniper had never lived.

*Client visited in person, unaccompanied. High priest wore grey. Discussed "the vessel's temperament." Advised against further handling.*

Juniper had never met a high priest. Had never used the word *temperament* about a container in their life—they said *mood*, everyone said *mood*, Marrow hadn't even arrived yet but still, *temperament* was not in their vocabulary. And yet there it was, in their hand, dated eight years ago, on a night Juniper could account for in perfect detail because it was the night of the harvest flood and they had spent it bailing water out of the receiving room and complaining about it to anyone who would listen.

Two sets of memories, both claiming the same body. One of them documented. One of them missing.

"Oh," Juniper said quietly, to the dark, to the lamp, to whatever was left of Veth to hear it. "Oh, no."

Because there was a fourth possibility, wasn't there? The one they'd been circling like a moth around a flame that had already burned them once. That the pages hadn't just been cut from the ledgers. That the cutting had been done in a place with no paper at all. That someone had taken a knife to Juniper the way they'd taken a knife to folio 214, cleanly, skillfully, sanding the stubs smooth so nothing would catch, so the remaining memories would sit flush against each other and seem whole.

That the vault was not the only thing in the Gilded Lantern with gaps in it.

Juniper thought about the erased entry—the client's obscene payment, *one memory, name withheld*, dated the day before their life supposedly began. Thought about how they'd felt reading it: not curiosity, exactly. Recognition. The specific dread of reading your own name in a list of the dead.

They thought about Veth going silent mid-sentence, ten years ago, and how Juniper had always assumed the god had simply *left*, the way gods did, the way fathers did, the way everyone eventually did if you gave them enough reason.

Had he left? Or had something been taken out of the middle of him, too?

The lamp guttered. Down in the deep archive, with the founding ledgers stacked around them like headstones, Juniper Vasquez laughed—one short, cracked sound with no humor in it, the laugh of a person who has just realized the joke is on them and has always been on them.

"I'm the unreliable one," they told the dark. "Fifteen years keeping everyone else's accounts honest, and I can't reconcile my own books."

The dark, in the manner of the silent god's house, declined to disagree.

* * *

It was nearly dawn when Juniper climbed back up, bleary, ink-stained, and carrying a single sheet of notes they'd allowed themselves—one page, everything they'd found, because if their own head was a compromised archive then paper was safer than memory, and if someone came for the paper, well. Let them leave a seam. Juniper was learning to look for seams.

They emerged into the entrance hall and stopped.

Dawn light was coming through the door in the wrong color—grey where it should have been gold, thin where it should have been warm—and standing in it, dripping rain and shaking, was a child. Young, hooded, temple-robed, with the hollowed-out face of someone who had been crying for a long time and had recently run out of the strength for it.

Behind the child, at the end of the lane, Juniper could see the spire of the Oracle's temple, and see, even at this distance, that the temple doors stood open and dark, like a mouth after the screaming stops.

The child looked up at Juniper with eyes that had seen too much and were now seeing nothing at all, and said, in a voice scraped raw:

"They cast me out. The stars went out and my sight went with them, and they said a blind oracle is just a beggar in nice robes." A swallow. "Please. I don't have anywhere else. I saw—" the child flinched at their own words, as if surprised by them, "—I saw the Lantern. In the last thing I ever saw. I saw the Lantern, and a ledger, and someone writing my name in it."

Juniper looked at the child. Looked at the notes in their own hand, the ones that said *trust nothing, verify everything, especially yourself.*

Guild policy was clear: no sanctuary without sponsorship, no sponsorship without coin, no exceptions. Juniper had enforced that policy for fifteen years.

"Well," Juniper said, stepping aside to let the child in, "the policy can stand in line behind the rest of the things I'm no longer sure about. Come in before you drown. What's your name?"

"Marrow," said the child, and crossed the threshold into the Gilded Lantern, and somewhere far above them, in the starless sky, something that had been holding its breath for ten years exhaled.
