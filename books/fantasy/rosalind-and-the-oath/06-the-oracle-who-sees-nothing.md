## 6. The Oracle Who Sees Nothing

Marrow had been thrown out of better places than this, but never in worse weather.

The rain came down the way it always did now—flat, gray, uninterested in anything. Before the stars went out, Marrow's temple had taught them that rain was the sky remembering the sea, and that every drop carried a little prophecy of its own: where it would land, what it would touch, whose upturned face it would find. Marrow had believed that once. Marrow had believed a great many things before the sky went dark and took their gift with it.

Now they stood in an alley across from a building shaped like nothing so much as a very fat lantern, if lanterns were made of black stone and had a door that looked at you like it knew your debts.

The Gilded Lantern. The guild that traded in impossible things.

Marrow had walked eleven days to get here. Their sandals had given out on day eight. Their last coin had gone on day nine for a bowl of something the vendor had called stew, which was generous of him. Their temple robes—fine white silk, embroidered with constellations that no longer existed anywhere but on their sleeves—were ruined beyond redemption, which felt appropriate.

They were twenty years old, they were blind in the only way that mattered, and they had nowhere left to go except through that door.

"Right," Marrow said to the alley, because talking to yourself was free and the alley wasn't bad company as alleys went. "Worst case, they kill you. Best case, they take you in. Either way you get to stop walking."

The alley did not argue. Marrow appreciated that about it.

* * *

The door of the Gilded Lantern did not have a knocker. It had a small brass mouth, set at chest height, and when Marrow approached it, the mouth said, in a voice like dry paper: *"State your business."*

"I'd like to sell something," Marrow said.

*"What do you have?"*

"A broken oracle."

There was a pause. Somewhere behind the brass, Marrow heard muffled voices, one of them rising in what sounded distinctly like protest. Then the mouth said, *"Wait,"* and clicked shut like a turtle going into its shell.

Marrow waited. The rain waited with them, less politely.

After a while—not long, but long enough for Marrow to count forty-one raindrops hitting their left shoulder, because counting was a habit the temple had beaten into them and habits outlive their reasons—the door opened, and a person stood in the gap, backlit by lamplight, holding a ledger against their chest like a shield.

Marrow knew who this was. Everyone in certain circles knew who this was. Juniper Vasquez, oath-bearer of Veth, keeper of the ledgers, the person whose handwriting was supposedly so precise that three separate governments had tried to hire them away from the guild and all three had received the same reply: *No,* entered into the record in beautiful script.

Up close, Juniper Vasquez looked tired in a way that had layers to it, like sedimentary rock.

"You're the oracle," Juniper said. Not a question.

"I'm the shell of an oracle," Marrow said. "The oracle part stopped working when the stars did. I've come to see if you'll buy the shell."

Something flickered across Juniper's face—too fast for Marrow to name it, though once upon a time they could have named any expression from thirty paces in the dark. "We don't buy people."

"You buy names that were never spoken. You buy borrowed years. You bought a bottled storm last spring—I read about it, before—" Marrow gestured vaguely upward, at the starless sky, the gesture encompassing everything. "A person is just another impossible thing. I am, at minimum, a discount one."

Juniper stared at them. Rain dripped off Marrow's nose. Somewhere inside the guild, someone dropped something ceramic and swore with real feeling.

"That was almost funny," Juniper said finally. "That concerns me. Go away."

"The temple cast me out two weeks ago." Marrow hadn't planned to say that. It came out anyway, the way true things do when you're wet enough. "They said a sightless oracle is a vessel with nothing in it. They said I was taking up space that a working vessel could use. There are nine of us left in the whole order, Keeper Vasquez. Nine. And they still didn't want me."

Juniper's grip shifted on the ledger. "I can't help you."

"No," Marrow agreed. "But you could not help me indoors."

Another pause. This one had a different texture. Marrow had spent their whole life reading textures of silence—it was, in fact, the only thing they were still good at—and this silence had guilt in it, and something older and sadder underneath.

"The guild has policies," Juniper said.

"I know. I've read your published bylaws. Very thorough. Page twelve says no sanctuary without payment or contract."

"Then you know I can't—"

"Page thirteen," Marrow said, "says exceptions may be granted at the discretion of the ledger-keeper on duty, in cases where refusal would result in death, dishonor, or embarrassment to the guild."

Juniper went very still. "You haven't read our bylaws. Those aren't public."

"No," Marrow agreed pleasantly. "But I used to be able to see things that aren't public. Old habits." They held out a hand, palm up, rain pooling in it. "I'm not asking you to break your rules, Keeper. I'm asking you to interpret them generously. It's what they're for."

And there it was—the moment Marrow had walked eleven days for. Not the door opening. The pause before it. The pause where Juniper Vasquez, who had spent ten years doing everything correctly and by the book and according to policy, stood at a threshold and had to decide whether to be a rule or a person.

Marrow knew about that pause. They'd stood in it themselves, once, the night the stars went out. They'd chosen wrong. They were hoping, in some small and desperate way, to watch somebody choose right.

The door opened wider.

"Don't make me regret this," Juniper said.

"Oh, I'll absolutely make you regret it," Marrow said, stepping inside. "But not tonight. Tonight I'm too tired to be interesting."

* * *

The Gilded Lantern's common room smelled of lamp oil, old paper, and someone's attempt at dinner that had ended in surrender. Marrow sat wrapped in a blanket that smelled of cedar and previous owners, holding a bowl of soup that was much better than the steward clearly thought it was, and watched the guild exist around them.

It was strange, seeing a place you'd only ever seen in fragments. Before, visions had come to Marrow sideways and unbidden—a flash of a green door here, a voice saying *the third key opens nothing* there—and they'd spent years assembling the world out of pieces like a child with a dropped mosaic. Now they got the whole picture at once, plain and undramatic, and it was somehow more disorienting than the fragments had ever been.

A woman with burn-scarred hands was arguing with a man about whether a jar on the table contained fog or merely air. A boy no older than fourteen was copying entries in a side ledger, tongue between his teeth. Two mercenaries played cards in the corner with the focused intensity of people for whom the cards mattered enormously and the game not at all.

Nobody looked at Marrow the way the temple had looked at them for two weeks—that flat, assessing look, weighing the vessel, finding it empty. Here, they mostly didn't look at Marrow at all, which was the kindest thing anyone had done for them in months.

Juniper appeared at their elbow with a second blanket and set it down without comment.

"Thank you," Marrow said.

"Don't thank me yet. Guild Master Corvane will hear about this by morning, and he'll want to know why his ledger-keeper violated page twelve."

"Because his ledger-keeper found page thirteen."

Juniper's mouth twitched. It was a small thing, barely a movement, but Marrow caught it the way they used to catch falling stars—with total attention and a private thrill. "Where did you actually learn our bylaws?"

"A vision. Years ago. I saw a page burning in a brazier, and it had your guild's seal on it, and the text was very clear because it was on fire and fire is legible if you look at it right." Marrow shrugged. "Most of my visions were like that. Useless in the moment, useful later, never in the way anyone wanted."

"What happened to them? Your visions?" Juniper asked it carefully, the way you ask someone about a dead relative.

"They went out with the stars." Marrow turned the spoon in the soup. "Do you know what it was like, Keeper? Every night of my life, the sky talked to me. Not in words—in weather, in patterns, in the way light fell. I knew when a ship would sink because the water told me. I knew when a baby would be born because the dark above the mother's house went soft. And then one night the sky just—" They made a fist and opened it. "Stopped. All of it. Like a room where someone had been speaking your whole life, and then you realize the voice was never speaking *to* you, and now it's gone, and you don't even have the comfort of being ignored anymore."

Juniper said nothing. But they sat down across from Marrow, which was answer enough.

"The temple says the silence is holy," Marrow went on. "They say the stars were noise, distraction, the chattering of dead lights, and that Veth has finally drawn the curtain so we can attend to what matters. They held a service. Everyone wept with joy." They looked up. "Did you weep with joy, Keeper Vasquez?"

"No," Juniper said quietly. "I keep rites for a god who doesn't answer. I don't think he's listening either."

"Mm." Marrow ate another spoonful. "You say that like you miss him."

"I say that like I—" Juniper stopped. Recalibrated. "Eat your soup."

"Yes, Keeper."

* * *

The fever came at midnight.

Marrow had known it was coming for two days—it had been building in their bones like weather, the old familiar pressure behind the eyes that had preceded every major vision of their life. They'd hoped, stupidly, that without the gift the fever wouldn't come either. But the body keeps its own ledgers, and Marrow's had apparently decided that ten years of unpaid visions were coming due all at once.

They woke in the narrow bed Juniper had given them—the one in the alcove off the ledger room, because apparently even acts of defiance came with paperwork—and the world was on fire in the old way, the beautiful terrible way, colors pouring through the dark behind their eyelids.

They tried to be quiet about it. They failed.

They heard footsteps, and then Juniper was there with a lamp, kneeling beside the bed, and Marrow grabbed their wrist because the vision was pulling them under and the one thing the temple had taught them about fevers was *don't go under alone.*

"It's happening," Marrow gasped. "I need—I need you to write it down. You're a ledger-keeper. Write it down."

"Write what down?"

"Whatever I say!" The words were already coming, tumbling, and Marrow could feel the difference in them—these weren't their own thoughts, these were the things that lived in the deep water, the things that had survived the drowning of the sky because they were too heavy to float away. "Quickly, before it goes. It always goes."

Juniper moved fast for a tired person—lamp to desk, desk to bedside, pen scratching. "Go."

Marrow closed their eyes and let the deep water have them.

They saw a vault. Not the guild's vault—or yes, the guild's vault, but deeper than deep, below the below, a place that hurt to look at even in vision. They saw chains made of language. They saw a fragment, a shard, a piece of something vast that had been cut into pieces like bread divided among soldiers, and the piece was glowing faintly, and around the glow—

Around the glow, something was feeding.

Not eating the way animals eat. Eating the way rot eats, patiently, from the edges inward. Shapes that might have been hands. A hunger with a ledger of its own, columns and columns of it, each line a bite.

And threaded through it all, thin as wire, a voice. A voice Marrow recognized—not from life, but from the sky itself, from the ten thousand nights the heavens had murmured overhead. The god's voice. Veth's voice. Except it wasn't speaking. It was screaming, very slowly, very quietly, the way things scream when they've been screaming so long the scream has become a kind of silence.

Marrow surfaced enough to speak. Their mouth tasted of copper and starlight.

"The god is not silent," they heard themselves say, in a voice that was theirs and also not. "He is being eaten."

The pen stopped.

"*What?*" Juniper whispered.

"He is being eaten," Marrow repeated, and the vision gave them one more thing—one image, sharp as a knife slipped between ribs: a ring. A signet ring, gold, worn on a soft white hand raised in blessing before a congregation of upturned faces, and on the ring's face a symbol Marrow knew from the burning page of the bylaws, the guild's own mark, and beneath it a star with its points erased—

—and then it was gone. All of it. The fever broke like a wave receding, and Marrow lay in the wet sheets shaking, empty, ordinary, twenty years old and blind again.

Juniper hadn't moved. The lamp flame trembled in the draft from Marrow's shivering.

"Eaten," Juniper said. It wasn't a question. It was a word being tested for weight, and finding it heavy. "By what?"

"I don't know." Marrow pulled the blanket up. Their teeth chattered. "But I saw a ring, Keeper. A guild signet. On the hand of someone who was—" They frowned, chasing the afterimage. "Someone who was leading prayers. To the silence."

Juniper stood very slowly. In the lamplight, their face did something complicated—grief and suspicion and a third thing Marrow couldn't read, something that looked almost like recognition.

"Sleep," Juniper said at last, and their voice was steady in the way of things balanced on an edge. "In the morning, I'm going to ask you everything you remember about that ring. Every detail. And then—" They paused at the alcove's edge, half in shadow.

"And then what?" Marrow asked.

Juniper looked back at them, and for a moment the tired ledger-keeper was gone, and something older looked out—something that had kept rites alone for ten years to a god who never answered, and had just been told why.

"And then," Juniper said, "I think I'm going to need an oracle."
