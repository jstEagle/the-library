## 7. The Chantry of the Silent Star

The Chantry of the Silent Star sat at the top of Candlemaker's Hill like a tooth that had grown in wrong—too white, too straight, too new among the crooked old houses around it. Juniper had walked past it a hundred times in the years since it was built and had never once gone inside. Chantries were for people who had something to say to their gods. Juniper's god wasn't speaking, so there seemed little point.

Now the god was silent everywhere, apparently, and everyone wanted to worship that.

"You're fidgeting," Marrow said.

"I'm adjusting my collar."

"You've adjusted your collar eleven times. I counted." Marrow sat on the low wall across the street, wrapped in a borrowed coat two sizes too big, looking like a scarecrow that had wandered off its post. Their eyes—milk-pale since the stars went out—were fixed on the chantry doors with an expression Juniper couldn't read. "I still think I should come with you."

"Absolutely not. You look like a fever dream."

"I *am* a fever dream. That's practically my job description now."

Juniper crouched down to their level, which put them eye to eye with the oracle's stubborn chin. "Marrow. If this goes badly, someone has to be outside who knows where I went. Sera's still decoding the ward-fragments, and she'll want a witness when she tells me I'm an idiot."

"She already knows you're an idiot. She told me over breakfast."

"*When* did you two have breakfast?"

"This morning. While you were doing your little ledger dances." Marrow smiled, and for a moment they looked less like a cast-off temple child and more like what they probably would have been, in a kinder world: a teenager who stayed up too late and stole bread from the kitchen. "Go on, then. Worship something. Report back."

Juniper straightened their collar a twelfth time, purely out of spite, and crossed the street.

* * *

Inside, the Chantry smelled of beeswax and cold stone, and it was *full*.

That was the first shock. Juniper had expected a handful of grief-stricken faithful, huddled against the dark. Instead the nave was packed shoulder to shoulder—dockworkers in their good coats, merchants' wives, two off-duty city watchmen standing awkwardly by the pillars as if unsure whether guarding a church counted as work. Every face was turned upward toward the ceiling, where a great circular oculus opened onto the sky.

Onto the starless sky.

It was night outside, and through the oculus Juniper could see the black—the vast, total, wrongness-black that had swallowed the stars three weeks ago. Most people in the Lantern district couldn't bear to look at it. They shuttered their windows against it, cursed it, made small superstitious gestures when they caught themselves staring. Here, five hundred people stared at it with their mouths slightly open, the way other congregations looked at saints.

A voice rose from the front—not loud, but pitched to carry, the way water carries in a pipe.

"Brothers. Sisters. The sky has closed its mouth."

Juniper drifted along the back wall, hood up, and found a place beside a pillar where the shadows pooled deep. Old habits. In the war, standing in shadow had kept them alive more often than any sword.

"The philosophers tell us the stars have died," the voice continued. "The astronomers tell us they are hidden. The panic-mongers in the markets tell us the world is ending." A pause, gentle as a hand laid on a shoulder. "I tell you the truth. The sky has not died. It has fallen *silent*. And silence, beloved, is the oldest language there is."

Juniper's skin prickled.

They knew that cadence. Not the man—they hadn't seen his face yet—but the cadence itself. The careful pauses. The way he let silence do half the preaching. It was the rhythm of the daily rites of Veth, the ones Juniper performed alone every dawn in the guild chapel, the ones performed for no congregation because there was no one left to hear them.

Someone up there was speaking Veth's liturgy to a room full of strangers, and calling it something new.

Juniper edged forward along the wall, one pew at a time, head bowed like any humble penitent. The high priest stood on a dais beneath the oculus—a tall man, silver-haired, robed in grey so pale it was nearly white, cut in a style Juniper didn't recognize. He was older than Juniper had guessed from the voice, maybe sixty, with a face like a folded map: all creases, all history. And he preached with his hands open at his sides, palms forward, exactly the posture of an oath-bearer receiving.

"When the stars spoke," he said, "the world listened, and the world bled. Every light in heaven was a lamp over a battlefield. Every constellation a chain of command." His voice dropped, and the whole nave leaned in to catch it. "But now? Now the heavens keep their counsel. Now we live in the first quiet the world has known in ten thousand years. Do not mourn the stars, children. Be grateful. Something up there has finally learned to hold its tongue."

A murmur ran through the crowd—comfort, wonder, hunger. Juniper felt it too, and hated feeling it. Because there was a version of this that was beautiful. There was a version where the silence really was mercy, where the war-god's long shouting had finally stopped, and these people had simply found peace in the quiet the rest of the world called catastrophe.

And then the high priest raised his right hand in blessing, and the candlelight caught the ring on his finger, and every comfortable thought Juniper had been building came down like a badly stacked shelf.

It was a signet ring. Heavy gold, worn smooth with age, stamped with a sigil Juniper knew better than their own signature: a lantern with seven flames, and beneath it, in the old script, the word *VETH-MEMOR*—*Veth remembers.*

There were exactly eleven such rings in the world. Juniper knew this because they kept the inventory. Eleven founder's signets, struck when the Gilded Lantern was chartered, each one keyed to the vault below the vault, each one supposed to pass from guild master to guild master and be buried with the last of them. Ten were accounted for. The eleventh had gone into the ground forty years ago with Aldous Vane, the founder himself, in a coffin sealed under the guild chapel with rites Juniper had personally renewed every year since taking the ledgers.

It should be buried. It was sitting on the finger of a priest who was teaching five hundred people to love the dark.

Juniper did the only sensible thing. They stopped listening to the sermon and started planning how to steal a corpse's ring off a living man's hand.

* * *

The service ended with no hymn—of course it ended with no hymn—and the congregation filed out into the street wearing the soft, emptied faces of people who had been given permission to stop being afraid. Juniper let the current carry them partway to the doors, then slipped sideways through a curtain of grey wool into the cloister corridor marked *INITIATES ONLY*, on the theory that the best place to hide was inside an institution's own certainty that no one would dare.

The corridor smelled of ink and tallow. Doors lined it, most shut, one ajar, spilling lamplight. Juniper pressed close to the wall and listened, which was how they learned two things very quickly.

First: the Chantry of the Silent Star had a scriptorium, and the scriptorium was copying something in enormous quantities.

Second: the two initiates inside were arguing about whether the copies should go to the harbor warehouses or the imperial consulate first.

"—Corvane's man said the warehouses," one was saying. A young voice, nervous.

"Corvane's man says a lot of things. The consul pays in advance." A pause; the scratch of a pen. "Besides, it's not as if anyone reads them. It's the same page four hundred times. *The silence is holy, the silence endures.* My hand cramps writing it."

"It's not the words, idiot, it's the seal. You stamp the seal and the wards read it and—" The young one stopped. Juniper heard a chair scrape. "Did you hear something?"

Juniper was already moving, silent as a debt unpaid, back toward the cloister arch. Behind them, the door opened, footsteps pattered, found nothing, and retreated. But the name rang in Juniper's ears all the way down the corridor, bright and awful as a cracked bell.

*Corvane.*

Guild Master Corvane, who burned correspondence and ordered everyone indoors and told them the wards had failed. Corvane, whose man was directing the distribution of Chantry broadsheets stamped with seals that talked to wards. Corvane, whose guild held the contract on the vault below the vault.

The guild's hands were not clean. That much Juniper had suspected since the excised pages. But this—this was not dirty hands. This was the guild reaching out through a church it had built, feeding a lie to five hundred hungry people, and doing it with the founder's own ring.

Juniper let themselves out through the alms door, walked three streets with a perfectly ordinary gait, and then sat down hard on a loading dock behind a chandler's shop and breathed until their hands stopped shaking.

* * *

"Well?" Marrow was waiting at the wall, and Sera was with them—Sera, smelling of ink and hangover, clutching her ward-fragments in a leather roll under one arm like a duelist's gloves. "You went in gray and you came out grayer. Out with it."

"The Chantry is ours," Juniper said. "Or the guild's. Or—I don't know whose anymore. The high priest wears a founder's signet. The eleventh one. The one buried with Aldous Vane."

Sera went very still. "Buried with him how thoroughly?"

"With full rites. Sealed coffin. Under the guild chapel. I've renewed the seal every year—it's in the ledgers, my own hand, every year for a decade."

"Then either the man dug up a corpse for jewelry," Sera said slowly, "or the corpse never had it, or—" She stopped. Juniper watched her arrive at it, watched her face do the thing faces do when a clever person finds the trapdoor in the floor. "Or the burial was staged. Forty years ago. And the ring has been in use ever since."

"In use for what?" Marrow asked.

"For keys," Juniper said. "Founder's signets key the deep vaults. If the Chantry has one, then the Chantry isn't a client of the guild. It isn't even a partner. It's a *branch office*." They heard their own voice go flat and cold, the ledger-voice, the voice that recorded things without feeling them, and made themselves step past it. "And there's more. They're printing broadsheets by the hundred. Stamped with some kind of seal that 'talks to the wards.' And the man coordinating it is Corvane's."

Silence, on the loading dock, while the chandler's apprentices laughed at something inside and the ordinary evening went on being ordinary around them.

"That's why the wards fell clean," Sera said at last, quietly. "Not broken—*switched*. I told you the forgery was good, but a forgery needs a true key somewhere in the chain. A signet would do it. A signet and a rite." She looked up at Juniper, and there was no accusation in her face yet, only the terrible patience of a woman lining up facts like soldiers. "Juniper. Who else could carry a signet's authority into the border wards?"

"An oath-bearer."

"How many oath-bearers does the guild have left?"

Juniper opened their mouth to say *several*, and found, with a lurch like a stair missing in the dark, that they could not remember a single name besides their own. They could see the chapel where oaths were sworn. They could see the shelf where the oath-registers lived. They could see the register itself, and their own handwriting in it—

—and nothing above it. Nothing below it. Just their name, alone on a page, in a book that should have held dozens.

"Juniper?" Marrow's hand was on their sleeve.

"I can't remember," Juniper said. The words came out small. "I can't remember who took the oath with me. I know there were others. I can almost see them. And it's—" They laughed, and it came out wrong. "It's like the missing pages. It's the same *shape* of empty."

Sera and Marrow exchanged a look over Juniper's head, the kind of look that has a whole conversation in it and decides, for now, to share none of it.

"Then here's what we know," Sera said briskly, in the tone of a woman building a bridge over a drop. "The Chantry is a guild operation. The signet is real and active. The wards were switched off with oath-bearer authority. And the high priest preaches Veth's own liturgy to a crowd that doesn't know it." She rolled up her fragments and tapped them against her palm. "Which means whoever is running this didn't just steal from the guild. They're using the god's own voice—his *words*, his rites—as a tool. And there is exactly one source of Veth's voice in this world that anyone could use."

The loading dock seemed to tilt. Below the vault. The archive behind the ledger room. The founding purpose that was not trade but containment.

"He's awake enough to be *used*," Juniper whispered. "Ten years silent, and I told myself he'd gone out like a lamp. But if his words are moving through the city—if his liturgy is being copied by the hundred—then whatever's left of him down there isn't dead. It's being *spent*."

Marrow made a sound. Small, involuntary, the sound of someone touching a hot pan. Both of them turned.

"What?" Juniper asked. "Marrow. What did you see?"

The oracle's milk-white eyes were wide and fixed on the middle distance, on something none of them could follow. When they spoke, their voice had the distant, borrowed quality it got when the visions moved through them uninvited.

"A room," Marrow said. "Underground. Cold. And a voice saying the same sentence over and over, except each time it's *shorter*. Like a coin worn thin. Like someone is—" Their breath hitched. "Like someone is spending him. Word by word. And he keeps saying it anyway, because it's the only prayer he has left, and nobody down there is listening."

Nobody spoke. Inside the chandler's shop, the apprentices laughed again, warm and oblivious.

Then Marrow blinked, came back to themselves, and looked up at Juniper with an expression of such naked, frightened apology that Juniper's stomach dropped before the oracle said a word.

"There's something else," Marrow said. "In the vision. The man spending him. The one giving the orders." They swallowed. "He had your handwriting, Juniper. On the orders. I don't know how a handwriting looks in a vision, but it was yours. It was *exactly* yours."

The night air went very cold, and somewhere far above them, past the rooftops, the starless sky kept its holy silence—and Juniper understood, with the perfect clarity of a ledger balancing at last, that the next entry they wrote might be their own confession.
