## 8. Borrowed Years

Sera Iln had been sober for exactly four hours, and she already regretted every minute of it.

The problem with sobriety, she had learned over the long gray years since the war, was that it let the thinking in. Drinking was a door you could close. Sober, you sat in a room with your own head like a debtor waiting for collection, and the head always had something to say.

Today the head had plenty of material. She was sitting in the back workroom of the Gilded Lantern—a room she'd been dragged into two nights ago by a mercenary who didn't know how to take *no* for an answer—surrounded by rubbings of the fallen border wards, three cups of tea she hadn't asked for, and one ledger-keeper who kept watching her the way people watch a fire they're not sure is out.

"You've been staring at the same rubbing for an hour," Juniper said.

"I'm not staring. I'm listening."

"To paper."

"Paper talks," Sera said. "If you know how to listen. That's the whole trade, isn't it? You read ledgers. I read wards. Same job, different alphabet." She tapped the rubbing with one finger. "Except this one is lying to me, and I'm trying to figure out why it's bad at it."

Juniper came around the table and looked over her shoulder. Close enough that Sera could smell ink and lamp oil and, faintly, the chapel incense that clung to anyone who'd done the rites as long as Juniper clearly had. Ten years of daily devotions to a god who'd stopped answering. Sera knew what that smelled like. Half the veterans of the Last March did.

"The signature's mine," Sera said. "Or it looks like mine. Here—" she traced the maker's mark at the ward-anchor's base, the little interlocked chevrons that had once meant *certified by the Imperial College of Wardwrights, third rank*, before the College had struck her name from its rolls with more ceremony than they'd ever given her actual work. "That's my hand. The pressure, the slant, the way I break the stroke on the second chevron because my wrist was ruined for six months after Kellarn Bridge and I never unlearned the hitch. Nobody could copy that without studying me for years."

"So it *is* yours."

"It's mine," Sera agreed, "and it's also wrong. Look at the anchoring sequence." She spread three rubbings side by side. "A real ward of this class doesn't fail. It frays. It burns through its fuel in rings, outermost first, and it takes days. Weeks. You get warning like a fever gets warning. But these—" she tapped the inner ring, the one closest to the border stones themselves, "—these went dark all at once. Top to bottom. Which means nobody broke them. They were *switched off*. Cleanly. By someone holding a key."

Juniper had gone very still. Sera had noticed that about them—the stillness. Most people fidgeted when they lied or feared; Juniper simply stopped, like a clock deciding whether to keep time.

"We established that," Juniper said quietly. "Two nights ago."

"We established *that* it happened. We haven't established *how*, because I was drunk and you were being patient with me, which I hate." She pulled a fresh sheet toward her and began sketching, fast, the old shorthand flowing back into her hand like water into a dry channel. "Here's the thing about keys. A ward key isn't a physical object—not for imperial-grade work. It's a *resonance*. A pattern written into something living so it can't be stolen. Blood, usually. Sometimes breath. Once, memorably, a man's shadow, which was a nightmare to maintain and cost the College a fortune in candles."

"And the resonance used here?"

Sera set down her pencil.

"That's the interesting part," she said. "Because I ran the harmonic structure last night when you thought I was sleeping off the drink. And the key that switched off those wards wasn't keyed to any imperial officer. It wasn't keyed to me, even though my signature is all over them—which, incidentally, means whoever built this wanted me to hang for it, and I have *opinions* about that I'll be expressing later, possibly violently." She turned the sketch toward Juniper. "It's keyed to oath-blood. Veth's oath. The specific resonance of someone who swore the god's service and carries his mark in their veins."

The silence stretched.

"There are maybe forty oath-bearers left alive," Juniper said at last. "The war ended ten years ago. Most of the others—"

"Died in the war. Yes. I was there for some of it." Sera kept her voice level, almost gentle, which took effort, because gentleness did not come naturally to her and she distrusted herself whenever she managed it. "So we're looking for a living oath-bearer whose blood could open imperial border wards. Someone with access. Someone with reason. Someone who was—" she watched Juniper's face carefully, "—in a position, ten years ago, to be near enough to the god's voice to leave traces of it in their blood."

"Don't," Juniper said.

"I haven't said anything yet."

"You're about to say something, and I'm asking you not to."

Sera leaned back in her chair. Outside the workroom door, the guild went about its evening: footsteps, the clink of the strongbox, someone laughing too loudly in the taproom because the sky outside was starless and everyone had decided collectively not to mention it. She thought about the war. She thought about Kellarn Bridge, and the field hospital behind it, and the young soldier—barely more than a child—who'd carried dying men on their back for eleven hours and come out of it with eyes like someone had reached inside and rearranged the furniture.

She'd known Juniper Vasquez for one day of that whole war. One day, and a night in a hospital tent, and she had never forgotten them, because some people you meet once and recognize forever. Two liars, she'd thought, when they'd faced each other across this same table two nights ago. Two liars with matching scars.

"What did you do ten years ago?" she asked.

* * *

Juniper didn't answer.

That was the first answer. Sera had spent twenty years reading wards, and wards were just promises made visible—you could tell a broken promise by how it failed, and you could tell a person refusing to speak by everything they didn't do. Juniper did none of the things guilty people did. No flinch, no bluster, no too-quick denial. They simply sat there, hands flat on the table, and looked at Sera with an expression she recognized from mirrors: the look of someone standing at the edge of a memory and finding the bridge out.

"I can't answer that," Juniper said finally.

"You mean won't."

"No." Something moved behind their eyes—something that frightened them, Sera realized, genuinely frightened them, and Juniper Vasquez did not frighten easily. "I mean *can't*. I've been through my own head, Sera. All of it. The year the stars went out, the year before, the year before that. There's a hole. Not a gap where something was torn out—I know what torn-out memories feel like, the guild does enough erasure work that I know the texture. This is different. This is a hole shaped like nothing. Like the memory was never there at all, except that everything around it bends toward where it should be, the way water bends around a stone."

Sera felt the hair rise on her arms. She'd seen erased minds before, in the war—imperial interrogators had been fond of the technique—but she'd never heard one described from the inside, and never by someone so precise.

"When does your earliest memory start?" she asked.

Juniper's jaw tightened. "You've been reading the ledgers."

"I read everything. It's a character flaw." She softened it with a shrug. "The client. The one who paid an obscene sum to erase 'one memory, name withheld.' Dated the day your first memory begins. I did the arithmetic, Juniper. I'm not good at much anymore, but I'm very good at arithmetic."

For a long moment Juniper looked at the rubbings of the fallen wards as if they might offer absolution. Then, quietly: "Ten years ago, the god's voice went silent. Mid-sentence. He was speaking to me—to all the oath-bearers, but to me most clearly, I don't know why—and then he stopped. Everyone assumed he died. Gods die; it happens; the priests wrote elegies. But I kept doing the rites anyway, because—" they stopped, and laughed, a small hollow sound, "—because I'm stubborn, apparently. Because it felt like leaving a door open for someone who might come home late."

"And now?"

"Now the wards fall, keyed to oath-blood, and you sit there looking at me like I'm the answer, and I want to tell you that you're wrong." Juniper met her eyes. "But I can't prove you're wrong. Don't you understand? That's the horror of it. I can't even prove it to *myself*. My own handwriting is on pages describing things I don't remember. My blood might have opened those wards. And there is a hole in my head exactly the size of whatever I did ten years ago, and somebody paid my own guild to put it there."

The lamp guttered. Somewhere in the walls, the old building settled and groaned.

"So we're at an impasse," Sera said. "I think you may have done something terrible. You think you may have done something terrible. Neither of us knows what. And the fate of the border, possibly the god, possibly the world, depends on finding out." She rubbed her face with both hands. "This is the worst contract I've ever taken, and I once warded a privy for a duke who paid me in goats."

Despite everything, Juniper's mouth twitched. "Goats?"

"Eleven goats. What was I going to do, refuse a duke? I ate well that winter and hated myself thoroughly." She stood, gathered the rubbings, squared their edges against the table out of pure habit. "Here's what we do. I keep decoding. If the key was made from your blood, there'll be a residue signature in the anchor matrix—oath-blood leaves traces, like smoke leaves traces. Give me until tomorrow night. And Juniper—" she paused at the door, not turning around, "—if it is you. If it turns out you switched off the wards, or sold something, or did whatever terrible thing that hole in your head is hiding. I need you to tell me. Not the guild. Me. Because I've been the scapegoat of an empire before, and I will burn this whole city down before I let it happen twice."

"And if I can't tell you? If the memory's truly gone?"

Sera looked back at her—one glance over the shoulder, war-honed and tired and honest.

"Then we find whoever took it," she said, "and we take it back."

* * *

She found the residue at midnight.

It was exactly where she'd predicted it would be, buried in the anchor matrix of the innermost ring, and it was worse than she'd predicted, because it wasn't just a match. It was a match with *history* in it. Resonance carried time the way amber carried insects, and this sample was layered: fresh use, ten years old, and beneath that—faint but unmistakable—the bright, raw signature of a brand-new oath. Someone who had sworn Veth's service as a child and bled for it within hours.

A child soldier. Sworn young. Bled young. Carried the god's voice close enough to leave it in their blood.

Sera sat alone in the lamplight with the proof in her hands and understood, with terrible clarity, why Juniper couldn't answer the question. Not wouldn't. Couldn't. Someone had paid to make sure of it.

And whoever that someone was, they had used Juniper's blood to switch off the wards that kept the empire out—or had arranged, ten years ago, for that blood to be available—and either way, the trail didn't end with a mercenary with a hole in their head. It ended with whoever held the knife that cut the memory out.

She was still staring at the readings when the door opened and Juniper came in, drawn by lamplight showing under the door at an hour when no honest ledger-keeper was awake. They looked at Sera's face, and then at the papers, and Sera watched them understand.

"You found it," Juniper said. "In my blood."

"I found it in your blood." Sera pushed the readings across the table. "And I found something else. The resonance is layered. Your oath-signature appears three times—once fresh, once ten years old, and once from when you first swore. Which means your blood has been *used* before. More than once. Whoever keyed these wards has had access to you for a decade, Juniper. This wasn't one theft. It's a subscription."

Juniper stared down at the page. When they spoke, their voice was very quiet.

"The vault," they said. "There are pages missing. Decades of transactions, all around one client. I found it yesterday and I told myself it was routine housekeeping." They looked up, and their eyes were wet and furious and afraid, all at once, and Sera thought: *there it is. The day at Kellarn Bridge. The same eyes.* "Sera. The Chantry of the Silent Star has been a client of this guild longer than I've been alive. And I think—I think whatever they bought from us, they bought with pieces of *me*."

Sera rose, joints cracking, and did the only thing she could think of to do. She put her hand on Juniper's shoulder—the scarred one, the one she remembered bandaging in a hospital tent while a child soldier pretended not to cry—and squeezed once, hard, the way soldiers do instead of saying anything that matters.

"Then tomorrow," she said, "you and I are going to find out what your own guild sold, and to whom, and we're going to do it before whoever holds the knife decides to finish the job."

Above the Gilded Lantern, through the high workroom window, the sky hung black and starless, and somewhere far below the streets, in a vault built to hold impossible things, something that had once been a god's voice waited in the dark—patient, imprisoned, and beginning, very softly, to sing.
