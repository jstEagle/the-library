## 9. The Vault Below the Vault

**Juniper**

The ledger room smelled of ink and old candle smoke, as it always did, and for the first time in ten years of service it smelled like a lie.

I had not slept. Sleep required a certain faith in the world—that it would still be shaped the same way in the morning—and I had misplaced mine somewhere around the third hour, when I'd sat with Sera's question turning over and over like a coin that never landed. *What did you do ten years ago?* I couldn't answer. Which meant the answer was something I had paid to forget, and I was the one who kept the ledgers of everything anyone paid to forget.

There is a particular kind of vertigo in being the record-keeper of your own erasure.

So instead of sleeping, I worked. That was the old answer, the reliable answer: when the world tilts, straighten the shelves. I pulled every ledger from the decade of my missing years and laid them open across the long table, and I read my own handwriting the way you might read letters from a stranger who claimed to know you.

It was my hand. That was the worst of it. Not similar—identical. The same impatient loop on the descenders, the same habit of crossing t's with a single slashing stroke because two strokes wasted time. Whoever had written these entries had been me, down to the smallest laziness.

And they were lying. Or rather, they were *complete*. Every transaction accounted for, every column balanced, every page signed and dated. Ledgers that perfect are never honest. Honest ledgers have smudges.

"You look terrible," said Sera from the doorway.

She looked worse, which was a comfort. She'd slept in the guild's guest room, or lain in it at any rate, and she wore yesterday's wine like a badge of office. She carried her tea in both hands the way soldiers carry anything warm.

"Good morning to you too."

"It's past noon." She came in, squinted at the table buried in open books. "You've been auditing your own decade."

"I've been trying to find where the floor used to be."

"Any luck?"

"Every entry balances." I turned a ledger toward her. "Look. Third month of the war's last year. A payment received from an unnamed client, entered as 'miscellaneous salvage.' The sum is enormous. And here—" I flipped forward, "—the same month, a disbursement of exactly that amount into the vault reserve, marked 'containment maintenance.'"

Sera set down her tea. "Containment."

"That word keeps appearing. Containment maintenance. Containment tithe. Containment contingency, containment survey, containment—" I stopped, because saying it again wasn't making it mean less. "The Gilded Lantern trades in impossible things. Bottled storms. Borrowed years. We don't *contain* anything. We sell things to people who want them contained elsewhere."

"Unless," Sera said slowly, "the trading was always the cover."

We looked at each other across the table, two liars doing what liars do best: recognizing the shape of a bigger lie.

"The founder's wing," she said. "Where's the oldest ledger kept?"

"Behind the ledger room. There's a records closet." I frowned. "It's smaller than it should be."

"What do you mean, smaller than it should be?"

"I mean I've measured this building twice in ten years, because measuring things is what I do when I can't sleep, and the back wall of the closet doesn't match the exterior wall. There's four feet of building that goes nowhere."

Sera smiled, and it was not entirely a nice smile. It was the smile of a woman who had spent her life finding the seams in other people's work. "Four feet," she said, "is enough room for a very important secret or a very disappointing broom cupboard. Let's find out which."

* * *

**Sera**

The closet was a broom cupboard. Of course it was. But above the broom cupboard, behind a shelf of candle ends, was a hatch I would have missed if Juniper hadn't already known the walls were wrong.

"Whoever built this wanted it found by someone who measures," I said, running my fingers along the frame. "That's either sentimental or arrogant."

"Can it be both?"

"In my experience those are the same thing." The lock was old wardwork—not imperial standard, older, the kind they taught before the war made everything standardized and grim. I could feel the pattern under my fingertips like braille. "This isn't locked against us. It's keyed. Look—the sigils are oath-script. Veth's script."

Juniper went very still beside me. In the lamplight I watched them do the thing I'd seen soldiers do before a door they didn't want to open: breathe once, deliberately, like paying a toll.

"I'm an oath-bearer," Juniper said. "If it wants blood—"

"Don't bleed on anything yet. Let me look first." I traced the sequence. It was elegant work, honestly; whoever wrote it had loved the craft. A recognition seal, but gentle—one that asked rather than demanded. "It wants you to say something. An invocation. The daily rite, I think. The opening line."

Juniper's jaw tightened. They'd told me about the rites—performed daily for a god who hadn't spoken in ten years, alone, stubbornly, the way you water a plant someone promised you would bloom. Devotion as grief management. I recognized that too.

"*Veth who listens,*" Juniper said quietly, "*let what is hidden be kept honestly.*"

The hatch clicked.

Inside was a stair, narrow, descending, lit by lamps that burned without oil—which should have been impossible, and after the week we'd had, felt almost rude in its casualness. Cold air rose up around us, smelling of stone and dust and, faintly, of something else. Something like the moment before a storm. Something like a held breath.

"Lamps that burn without fuel," I said. "In a building full of accountants."

"The guild has always been strange about its money," Juniper murmured. "Come on."

The stairs let out into a round chamber, and I will admit—I am a wardwright, I have walked the imperial deep-vaults beneath the Spire, I once helped design a prison for a living hurricane—I stopped on the last step and forgot how legs work.

The room was lined with shelves, and the shelves were lined with ledgers. Hundreds of them. Older than the guild's public founding, older than the war, their spines labeled in a careful antique hand: *Year One. Year Two. Year Three.* And in the center of the room, on a reading table worn smooth by generations of hands, lay an open book with a title page that read:

*The Founding Ledger of the Order of the Kept Voice.*

"Not the Gilded Lantern," Juniper whispered.

"No." My mouth had gone dry. "Not even close."

* * *

**Juniper**

I have read ten thousand entries in my life. I know how to take bad news off a page without flinching. You learn that in this trade: the ledger tells you the truth in columns, and the columns don't care about your feelings.

I flinched.

*The Order of the Kept Voice*, founded in the last year of the Godswar, by twelve signatories whose names had been struck through and replaced with numbers. Its purpose was stated on the first page in language so plain it hurt:

*Let it be recorded that the god Veth, being broken in the final battle, left upon the field a fragment of his voice, which fragment cannot be destroyed, cannot be given away, and cannot be suffered to fall into the hands of kings. Therefore we, being the last who heard him speak, do bind ourselves to keep it: not to trade it, not to use it, not to sell it, but to hold it in silence until such time as silence is no longer needed.*

Below that, the founding purpose of our vaults. Our wards. Our rules about names and memories and things that must never change hands. Every sacred rule of the Gilded Lantern, written first as a *containment protocol*. We were not merchants who sometimes kept secrets. We were jailers who had learned to charge rent.

"They were going to hold it forever," I said. My voice sounded far away. "Twelve people. They built all of this—the whole guild, the whole trade—to hide one prison inside a business."

"And then?" Sera was already three shelves deep, pulling volumes, her drunkard's clumsiness gone entirely. This was her country, old wards and older intentions. "Orders don't stay pure. Read the later ones."

I read the later ones.

The purity lasted ninety years. Then a famine year, and an entry noting that a portion of the voice's *resonance*—not the voice itself, a shedding of it, like bark—had been sold to fund the Order through the winter. Justified in the margins in a shaking hand: *Better a little sold than all lost.* The next century had more famines. More wars. More margins filling up with justifications, each handwriting a little more confident, a little less ashamed.

And then, forty years ago, the handwriting changed to a hand I knew.

Corvane's.

He had inherited the mastership young—he'd told us once, laughing, that the guild had been in his family "since the beginning," and we'd all assumed he meant the trading house. He meant this. He meant the prison. His family had been the wardens of Veth's voice for four hundred years, and somewhere in that inheritance, the shame had drained out of the margins entirely.

I opened the volume from ten years ago. My own decade. My missing years.

The entries were there. All of them. The erasure I had paid for—or someone had paid for, in my name—was recorded in full: *One memory, name withheld, purchased from an oath-bearer of the company Vasquez. Price: the company's debts, discharged. The asset consolidated into the deep seal.*

*The asset.* I sat down on the cold floor, because standing seemed suddenly ambitious.

"That's you," Sera said. Not a question.

"That's me." I turned the page. "Sera. Listen to this. 'The asset has proven unstable since consolidation. The bearer's fragment resists the seal. Recommend periodic harvesting of resonance to quiet it.'" I looked up at her. "Harvesting. He's been *selling pieces of it*. For ten years. Little shipments, disguised as ordinary impossible trades—a bottled storm here, a borrowed year there. Half the miracles we've brokered weren't miracles. They were shavings off a god."

Sera had gone pale in the lamplight. She sank onto the step beside me, and for once she had nothing clever to say, which from Sera was its own kind of testimony.

"The stars," she finally said. "The wards didn't fail because the god woke. They failed because he's been *bled*. Corvane's been thinning the seal's anchor for a decade to make product, and now the seal can't hold what's left, and the sky—" She pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes. "I forged wards for that man's grandfather's contracts. I trained wardwrights who maintain his vaults. I keep finding myself in this story."

"You keep finding yourself in it because you're good," I said. "That's not a crime."

"It feels like one lately."

We sat there together in the lamp-lit dark, under four hundred years of a lie written in beautiful handwriting, and I thought about every rule I had ever enforced. Every client I'd turned away for asking the wrong questions. Every time I'd told some trembling seller of secrets that the Lantern was honorable, that we kept things safe, that the ledgers balanced because we were honest.

The ledgers balanced because we were thieves with excellent penmanship.

"There's one more thing," Sera said quietly. She handed me the current year's volume, open to the newest entries. "Look at the last page."

I looked.

A single fresh entry, in Corvane's hand, dated three days ago—the night the stars went out:

*Buyer confirmed. Empire remittance received. Delivery of the asset to be arranged within the season. Note: the bearer's fragment remains unaccounted for. If located, retrieve before delivery. Without it, the seal cannot be transferred intact.*

The bearer's fragment. Me. Whatever was inside me—the piece of the god's voice I'd sold away, or the piece I'd somehow kept, the piece that made the wards key to my blood—Corvane intended to deliver it north along with everything else.

"He's selling the god," I said. "To the empire. To the empire that broke him in the first place."

"And he needs you to complete the sale." Sera stood, wincing, and offered me her hand. Her grip was steady, whatever the rest of her looked like. "Juniper. We need to get Marrow. We need to get out of this building tonight, before—"

Above us, muffled through four feet of stone and four hundred years of secrecy, the trapdoor slammed shut.

Then came the sound I knew better than my own heartbeat, because I'd heard it at the start of every working day for ten years: the great iron lock of the ledger room, turning.

Someone had locked us in.

From the other side, calm and unhurried, came Guild Master Corvane's voice, softened by stone but perfectly clear:

"Juniper. Since you're down there anyway, do have a look at Volume Twelve. I believe it's time we discussed terms."
