## 11. What Marrow Remembers

Marrow had been at the Gilded Lantern for eleven days when the visions came back.

They knew it was eleven days because Juniper kept a ledger of everything, including guests. Marrow had found their own name in it on the second morning, entered in that careful, upright hand: *One oracle, taken in against policy. Owes: sanctuary. Pays: to be determined.* It was the first time in Marrow's life anyone had written them down as a person rather than a phenomenon.

The visions came back at breakfast.

Not all at once. That was the cruel trick of it—Marrow had spent three years learning to live inside a gift like a house with every door open, wind blowing through every room, and then the stars went out and the doors slammed and for eleven days Marrow had been, for the first time since childhood, *quiet*. They'd almost gotten used to it. They'd started sleeping through the night. They'd laughed twice, properly, once at Sera's impression of Guild Master Corvane's cough (a wet, imperial, disapproving sound) and once at Juniper's face when a client tried to pay for a bottled memory with a chicken.

The chicken was still in the courtyard. Nobody knew what to do with it. It had opinions about Sera.

So of course the sight chose breakfast to return, in fragments, like a dropped plate reassembling itself wrong.

* * *

It began with the smell of tallow candles, which was odd, because the Lantern burned oil lamps. Then the taste of copper. Then—

—a room. A vault room, low-ceilinged, lit by candles guttering in wax pools. A child was there. The child was hiding behind a stack of crates labeled in a script too blurred to read, and the child was not supposed to be there, and the child was Marrow, seven years old, barefoot, temple-raised, lost during a tour of the guild quarter that had gone sideways in a way the temple never explained to the novices.

And there were voices.

"—the whole fragment? You understand what you're offering."

A woman's voice. Dry, unhurried, with an accent from the salt coast. Marrow would learn its owner's name later. For now it was just a voice coming through a doorway left ajar.

"I understand what I'm asking you to do with it," said the other voice. Younger. Rougher. A voice that had been shouting orders over battle-noise until shouting became its natural register. "Contain it. Bury it. I don't care. Just make sure nobody can ever—" A breath. "Nobody can ever use it. Including me."

"And your company?"

"What about them?"

"You said they're dying. Wounded past what surgeons can mend. You said this—" papers rustled, "—this thing of yours healed six soldiers in a field. Miraculously. Permanently."

"It did." The young voice cracked down the middle. "It can heal them again. All of them. My whole company, everyone the war chewed up. It *wants* to. That's the problem. It wants everything. It would pour itself out until there was nothing left, and it wouldn't even notice, because gods don't notice going empty, they just—" The voice stopped. Started again, smaller. "I can't hold it. I'm one person. I'm holding a god's voice in my chest with my hands and my hands are full of blood already. Take it. Please. Before I do something merciful."

Silence. The kind of silence that has a price tag attached.

"Payment terms," said the dry voice at last. "The guild will contain the asset indefinitely. In exchange: your company's debts to us are forgiven, medical care for your wounded is provided at cost, and—" another rustle of paper, "—one additional item. Name withheld. To be erased from your memory upon completion. Standard procedure for high-value containment clients. The less you know, the safer everyone is."

"That's fine," the young voice said, too fast. "Erase whatever you want. Erase all of it."

"You may want to keep something. Clients usually—"

"Erase *all of it*."

The child behind the crates pressed both hands over their mouth. Somewhere above, through a grate, stars should have been visible through the vault's light-shaft, but the child couldn't see any, only candle-smoke, and later—years later, in fever, in the last vision before the sky emptied—the child would remember thinking: *that's strange, there should be stars.*

Then the vision folded up like a letter and Marrow was back at the breakfast table with an egg on their plate and everyone looking at them.

"Marrow?" Juniper said. "You've gone the color of porridge."

"I'm fine," Marrow said, which was the first lie they told at the Gilded Lantern, and therefore, by the guild's own accounting, a debt incurred.

* * *

The trouble with being an oracle whose gift had died was that everyone assumed the quiet meant emptiness. It didn't. It meant Marrow finally had room to think, and thinking turned out to be much worse than seeing.

They spent the day doing chores badly. They swept the same stretch of corridor four times. They watered a plant that turned out to be made of wire, a guild joke Marrow hadn't been in on yet. They fed the chicken, whose name was now, by Sera's decree, Corvane the Lesser, "because it also struts around like it owns the place and contributes nothing."

Juniper found them in the courtyard at dusk, sitting on the step with the chicken regarding them sidelong like a small feathered conscience.

"There you are." Juniper sat down without asking, which was a thing they did, and which Marrow had decided privately was one of the best things about them. "Sera says you swept the hall four times. Either you've developed a religious relationship with that floor or something's wrong."

"The floor and I have an understanding," Marrow said.

"Mm." Juniper looked out at the darkening sky—the starless sky, the sky that had eaten Marrow's gift along with every other oracle's in the world—and said nothing for a while. That was another thing about Juniper. They could be silent *with* you instead of *at* you. "When you're ready to talk, I'll be in the ledger room pretending to audit entries I already audited. It's very soothing. Like counting sheep, if sheep were crimes."

They got up. They paused. They put a hand briefly on top of Marrow's head, an awkward, unpracticed gesture, like someone remembering how the motion worked from a manual.

"Eleven days," Juniper said. "You've been here eleven days and the place is already better. The chicken likes you. That's the real test. Corvane the Lesser hates everyone."

Then they were gone, and Marrow sat in the dark hating how warm their chest felt, because the vision kept replaying behind their eyes, and the voice in it—the rough, cracking, terrified voice—was a voice they'd been listening to for eleven days over breakfast.

* * *

That night Marrow did what oracles do when they can't see: they deduced.

The temple had taught them that visions weren't prophecy, not really. Prophecy was the temple's word for it, dressed in incense. What Marrow actually had was a knack for finding the thread of a thing—the true thread, buried under all the false stitching—and pulling until the garment came apart. Usually the garment was the future. But threads ran both directions.

Fact one: ten years ago, roughly, a mercenary had come to the Gilded Lantern carrying a fragment of a god's voice, and had sold it into containment to save their company.

Fact two: ten years ago, roughly, Veth's voice had gone silent mid-sentence. Every oath-bearer in the world had felt it stop. Marrow had been seven and standing in a vault, though they hadn't known what they were hearing until now—the god's voice, pouring past them through the walls like water through a cracked jar, saying something to somebody, and then *gone*, cut off, contained.

Fact three: the transaction had included an erasure. One memory, name withheld. And the client had said *erase all of it*, in a voice that Marrow now heard every morning asking whether they wanted eggs.

Fact four: Juniper Vasquez had been a soldier in the border wars. Juniper Vasquez had been an oath-bearer of Veth since childhood. Juniper Vasquez's earliest memory, per the ledger entry Marrow definitely had not snooped in on the third night, began ten years ago, the day after the god went silent. Everything before that was blank. Juniper joked about it—*my childhood was so boring my brain deleted it*—but they joked about it the way people joke about scars, quickly, with their eyes somewhere else.

Marrow sat up in the narrow guest bed with the blanket around their shoulders like a cloak and did the arithmetic that children are always doing about adults, the arithmetic that says *this person who feeds you and shields you and calls you "little star" when they think you're asleep is also the person in the vision, and the vision is a crime, or maybe it isn't a crime, or maybe it's the saddest thing you've ever seen.*

Because here was the part Marrow kept circling back to, the part that wouldn't let them simply be horrified: *before I do something merciful.*

The mercenary in the vision hadn't sold the god's voice out of greed. They'd sold it out of terror—terror of their own compassion, terror that if they held it one more hour they'd give it all away and die of generosity. Marrow knew that feeling. Marrow's gift had done the same thing their whole childhood: poured out and poured out until the temple had to feed them honey-water and lay them flat for days. You could love people to death with a thing like that. You could love yourself to death.

But knowing why didn't change what it was. Someone had sold a god. Someone had let the sky go dark—or had they? Was the erasure connected to the stars? Was the containment connected? Marrow pulled the thread and the thread kept branching, and every branch ended either at Juniper's door or at the Chantry's, and Marrow didn't have enough sight left to tell which.

What Marrow had instead was a family. Eleven days old, brand new, smelling of lamp oil and ledger ink. A disgraced wardwright who called them "kid" but checked, every night, that their window latch worked. An oath-keeper who wrote them into a ledger like a promise. A chicken.

And the truth, sitting in Marrow's chest like a swallowed stone, that would break that family open if spoken.

* * *

On the twelfth morning, the visions sharpened.

Marrow woke with candle-wax smell in their nose and the taste of copper again, and this time the fragment played longer. They saw the mercenary's hands on the table—young hands, scarred knuckles, a burn mark across the left wrist shaped like a moth's wing. They saw the contract signed. They saw, most terrible of all, the moment after: the mercenary standing alone in the vault room after the dry-voiced woman left, swaying slightly, one hand pressed flat against their own sternum as if holding a door shut from the inside. Their lips moved. No sound came. But Marrow had spent a childhood reading the mouths of people praying in silent temples, and the shape the mouth made was unmistakable.

*I'm sorry. I'm sorry. I'm sorry.*

Then the fragment showed Marrow something new, something that made them sit bolt upright in bed: the dry-voiced woman turning back at the door, one last look at her client, and saying, "For the record, the payment includes one more term. The asset must never be returned to its bearer. If the bearer comes asking—even begging—it stays. Swear it on your oath."

And the mercenary—Juniper, it was Juniper, it had always been Juniper—had sworn. On Veth. On the god whose voice they carried. Sworn to abandon it forever, in exchange for forgetting they ever had it, in exchange for their company's lives.

*Oh,* Marrow thought, lying in the grey dawn. *Oh, no. Oh, you poor idiot. You sold your god to save your friends and then paid extra to forget you'd ever loved him.*

It was the most Juniper thing imaginable. Marrow had known them eleven days and it was already the most Juniper thing imaginable.

* * *

Breakfast was eggs again. Sera was doing her impression of Corvane's cough for the benefit of the kitchen boy, who was laughing into his apron. Juniper slid a plate in front of Marrow and said, "You look like you slept in a fight," and Marrow said, "Visions," and Juniper's whole body changed—not dramatically, just a settling, a soldier's shift to readiness—and Juniper said, "Of what?"

And Marrow opened their mouth.

And closed it.

Because here was the thing: the truth wasn't ready. The truth was a half-healed wound, and Marrow had watched Sera work enough to know you don't pull a bandage off before the flesh closes, you just end up bleeding and scolding yourself. If Marrow said *ten years ago you sold a god's voice and bought your company's lives and paid to forget it*, then Juniper would either believe them and shatter, or disbelieve them and start doubting the one oracle in the world whose sight might matter, and either way the empire was at the gates, the wards were forged, the guild master was smiling wrong, and the family needed to hold together for approximately one more week.

That was what Marrow told themselves, anyway. Marrow was aware, in some honest cellar of themselves, that it was also possible they simply weren't brave enough yet. Loyalty and cowardice wear the same coat sometimes, and it takes years to learn the difference. Marrow was twelve. They gave themselves until fifteen.

"Not much," Marrow said. "Candles. A room. Voices I couldn't hear properly. It's probably nothing. The gift does that—comes back in junk pieces first, like frost patterns."

Juniper studied them for a long moment. Juniper had a way of looking at people that felt like being audited, gently, line by line. Marrow held still under it and thought, *please don't be able to tell, please, please* —and then hated themselves for hoping an oath-keeper of the god of truth would fail to notice a lie.

"Okay," Juniper said at last, and Marrow's relief lasted exactly as long as it took Juniper to stand, collect the plates, and say, lightly, to the room in general:

"Oh—almost forgot. Sera and I are going into the vault today. The lower stacks. There are some gaps in the ledgers we need to look at." A pause, precisely measured. "Gaps around some old transactions. Ten years old, give or take."

The kitchen boy didn't look up. Sera, nursing her hangover at the far table, did—sharply, over the rim of her cup.

Marrow's heart performed a maneuver that would have concerned a physician.

"So if you remember anything useful," Juniper went on, in that same easy voice, stacking plates with perfect calm, "anything at all—even junk pieces, even frost patterns—you know where to find me."

They left. Sera followed, draining her cup, and at the door she glanced back at Marrow with the expression of a woman who had spent a career reading forged signatures and had just seen a very good one.

Marrow sat alone with the eggs going cold and Corvane the Lesser staring at them through the window with tiny reptilian judgment.

"Well," Marrow said to the chicken. "That could have gone worse."

The chicken continued to stare.

Somewhere below them, under the ledger room, under the vault, under ten years of carefully erased pages, a god's voice waited in the dark—and Marrow was suddenly, absolutely certain, in the way that had nothing to do with sight and everything to do with the truth finally assembling itself, that Juniper was about to find it.

And Marrow had one morning, maybe less, to decide whether to be standing beside them when they did—or to say the words that would break them first.
