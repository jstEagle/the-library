## 12. The Empire Sends Regards

The knock came at dawn, and it was the wrong kind of knock.

Every trade has its grammar. A client knocks twice, soft, apologetic, as though the door might be asleep. A debtor knocks three times with growing confidence between the second and third. A rival doesn't knock at all; they leave a card under the mat and let you find it when you're feeling smug. But the knock that woke me that morning was measured, patient, and struck the wood like a man tapping a barrel to hear how full it was.

I was already in the ledger room—I had not, if I'm honest, slept—and I heard Marrow's bare feet slap down the stairs ahead of me, and then Sera's boots, heavier, slower, the boots of a woman who had learned that most doors are better left unopened.

"Visitors," Marrow said, peering through the shutter slats. Their face did something complicated. "Soldiers. Lots of soldiers. They have a *banner*."

"Nobody brings a banner to buy bottled storms," I said.

"Nobody brings forty men to buy anything," Sera said. She squinted past Marrow's shoulder. "That's an imperial standard. The old one, with both heads on it." She said it flatly, the way you'd say *that's a snake*.

The old empire had been a bedtime story for ten years—a caution we told apprentices about contracts too large to honor. Empires do not die; they nap. And apparently someone had gone and shaken one awake, which felt, given recent events, suspiciously on schedule.

Corvane came down last, dressed, of course, as though he had been up for hours composing himself, which he probably had. He looked at the three of us clustered by the window and something flickered across his face—too quick to name, but I've spent my life reading faces over ledgers, and I'd have entered it as *calculation* with a marginal note: *of what?*

"Stay inside," he said. "All of you. This is guild business."

"It's a legion," Sera said.

"It's a *negotiation*," Corvane corrected, and went to answer his own door.

* * *

I want it recorded—somewhere, anywhere—that I stayed inside for nearly four minutes.

Then I did what any oath-bound keeper of records does when told to stay away from the record being made: I found a window with a good view and opened it exactly wide enough to lie through later.

The street outside the Gilded Lantern had never been grand. It was cobbles and gutter-water and a pie cart whose owner, Hesper, had fed me my first honest meal in this city eleven years ago, when I arrived with a soldier's coat, a soldier's silence, and a soldier's appetite. She'd asked no questions. She'd just slid the plate across and said, "You look like a person who hasn't been asked anything kind lately."

Hesper was out there now, standing in her doorway in her apron with her arms folded, watching forty imperial soldiers arrange themselves in our street like they owned the cobblestones. Beside her, half-hidden behind the pie cart, was Doss—her grandson, sixteen, all elbows and opinions, who swept our steps some mornings for the price of gossip and had once spent an entire afternoon helping me alphabetize a crate of confiscated curses.

An officer dismounted. Not a general—the empire wouldn't waste a general on us yet—but a colonel with a voice trained to carry across parade grounds without effort.

"GUILD MASTER CORVANE." The name bounced off every shuttered window. "BY CONTRACT SEALED IN THE YEAR OF THE ASH TREATY, THE GILDED LANTERN HOLDS IN TRUST ONE ITEM DESIGNATED 'THE ASSET.' THE EMPIRE CALLS IN ITS DEBT."

Beside me in the dark of the stairwell, Sera went very still. "Ash Treaty," she murmured. "That's the armistice. That's—" She looked at me. "Juniper. What asset?"

"I don't know," I said, which was true, and which was also, I was beginning to understand, the most damning thing about me. My whole life was built on not knowing things that were written in my own handwriting.

Down in the street, Corvane stood on our threshold looking small against the banner, and I will grant him this: he didn't flinch. He raised his hands, palms open, the universal gesture of a reasonable man surrounded by unreasonable circumstances.

"The contract is acknowledged," he called back. "But the Lantern honors its debts in full and in order. There are protocols. Authentication. The seal must be verified against the founder's mark, and the founder's mark rests in our vault, which opens only at—" he paused, and I could hear him doing arithmetic, "—moonrise. Come back at moonrise."

A ripple went through the soldiers. The colonel's horse sidestepped.

"You are stalling," the colonel said, without heat, almost admiringly.

"I am *procedural*," Corvane said. "It's the same thing, done politely."

Despite everything—despite the archive below the vault, despite the excised pages, despite the fact that I now suspected Corvane of sins I couldn't yet spell—I laughed. Quietly. Into my sleeve. It was either that or be sick.

* * *

They didn't come back at moonrise. They didn't even wait for breakfast.

What happened next happened fast, the way bad weather happens fast, and afterward everyone who saw it remembered it differently, which is why I am writing it down while my hands still shake, because a record made late is a record made dishonestly.

The colonel sent four soldiers forward to "secure the premises pending authentication." Corvane refused—loudly, correctly, citing guild law older than the empire's current dynasty. The soldiers advanced anyway. And from the crowd that had gathered—and there is always a crowd, drawn by banners the way gulls are drawn by fishing boats—someone threw something.

I never learned who. I hope whoever it was sleeps well.

It was a pie tin. Hesper's tin, heavy tin, thrown hard from short range, and it caught a young soldier across the helmet with a sound like a bell falling downstairs. He went down. His comrades, jumpy and young and very far from home, decided the crowd was an attack, and the crowd—which included Hesper, who had never retreated from anything in her life, and Doss, who was pulling at her sleeve, trying to get her behind the cart—

I watched it from the window. That's the part I keep returning to. I *watched*, because I was obeying orders, because I was keeping myself safe, because I was a coward with a good view. The skirmish lasted perhaps ninety seconds. Soldiers with batons, then with the flats of blades; civilians with fists and fury and the particular courage of people who believe numbers protect them. Numbers don't protect anyone. Numbers are just how many funerals you're scheduling.

When it was over, the colonel bellowed his troops back into formation—he hadn't wanted this, I'll say that much; you could see him doing damage assessment in real time and hating the arithmetic—and the street emptied of everything except dust and overturned carts and the terrible specific quiet that follows violence.

Hesper was sitting against her own doorstep, holding her side, arguing furiously with the neighbor pressing a cloth to her head. She'd live. She'd complain about it for years, which for Hesper amounted to living well.

Doss was not moving.

He was lying in the gutter with his elbows finally still, and a soldier's blade—not the flat, not this time—had found him in the crush, and the neighbor woman was kneeling beside him doing the thing people do, the useless urgent thing, and I was down the stairs and out the door before anyone could tell me to stay inside again.

* * *

I don't remember crossing the street. I remember kneeling on wet cobbles. I remember that his hand was still warm and that he had flour on his knuckles, because he'd been baking before dawn, because he was sixteen and helped his grandmother and swept our steps and laughed at my jokes even when they were bad, especially when they were bad.

I remember thinking, with a clarity that felt like a knife going in sideways: *This is mine. This death is mine.* Because whatever the asset was, whatever sat sealed beneath our vault wearing my handwriting like a stolen coat, the empire had come for *it*, and the soldiers had come because of *it*, and the tin had flown because of *it*, and Doss—who had never in his life held a secret larger than where Hesper hid the good cinnamon—was dead on the stones of a street I walked every day, paying a debt he'd never signed.

Guilt is a strange currency. I had spent my whole life handling other people's guilt—recording it, valuing it, occasionally accepting it as payment. I knew its denominations. I knew it could be laundered, deferred, split into installments. But there is no exchange rate for this kind. You cannot pay it forward. It simply sits in your chest, compounding.

Sera found me there. She didn't say anything clever. She just crouched down next to me in the mess of it, in front of the whole grieving street, and put her hand on my shoulder with the grip of a woman bracing a wall that's about to fall, and said, "Breathe, ledger-keeper. In. Out. The rest can wait."

"He was sixteen," I said.

"I know."

"He swept our steps."

"I know." Her hand tightened. "And the people who sent those soldiers are going to come back at moonrise whether or not Corvane's vault opens, and you need to breathe *now* so you can be useful *then*. Grief keeps. Guilt keeps. Dead boys don't come back, but the living ones upstairs need you functional."

It was brutal and it was exactly right and it was the kindest thing anyone said to me that day.

Marrow appeared on my other side. They were white-faced—they'd seen it from the upstairs window, all of it, their dead gift churning uselessly in their chest—but they knelt and touched Doss's forehead, gently, the way you'd close a book you'd finished.

"He was going to ask someone to teach him letters this winter," Marrow said. "He told me Tuesday. He was embarrassed about it." Their voice cracked clean down the middle. "I would have taught him. I was going to say yes."

We stayed until they took him inside. Then Corvane's bell rang the guild together, and we went, because grief keeps, and guilt keeps, and moonrise was coming.

* * *

The guild hall smelled of tallow and old smoke. Every member we had—thirty-one of us, minus two who'd died in the streets and one, I noticed, whose chair stood empty with a haste about it that suggested packing—filled the benches. Corvane stood at the front, and for the first time since I'd known him, Guild Master Corvane looked tired in a way that wasn't performance.

"They will return at moonrise," he said. "Some of you will want to fight. Don't. We are thirty against a legion's vanguard, and martyrs make poor bookkeepers. Some of you will want to run. Also don't—the empire keeps lists, and running converts you from a witness into a target." He paused. "The contract will be honored. The asset will be delivered."

A murmur ran the benches. Old Fenwick, who'd kept the vault locks since before my time, spoke up: "Master, with respect—what *is* the asset? Half this guild has served twenty years and never seen so much as its entry number."

"That," Corvane said smoothly, "is because the contract predates most of you, and its terms bind the master's office alone." His eyes crossed the room and stopped, briefly, on me. On me, and on Sera beside me, and on Marrow. "Which reminds me. Certain members have been conducting private investigations into guild archives. I remind the assembly that unauthorized research is a breach of charter." Another pause, delicate as a knife laid on silk. "I trust the matter needs no further discussion. For now."

*For now.* The words landed in my stomach like swallowed stones.

Afterward, in the corridor, Sera muttered, "He knows."

"He knows," I agreed.

"And he's going to hand *something* to the empire tonight rather than let them tear the place apart looking for it. Which means whatever's under the vault, he'd rather it be in imperial hands than in ours." She rubbed her eyes. "Or he means to give them something that looks like the asset and keep the real one. Corvane always has a third door."

"Third doors are how you end up with dead boys in gutters," I said, more harshly than I meant.

Sera looked at me for a long moment. "Yes," she said quietly. "They are. So maybe it's time we stopped guessing which door he's using and started opening them ourselves."

Marrow came around the corner then, moving fast, eyes wide, and grabbed my sleeve with both hands—their visions had been coming back in fragments all week, little shards of starless sky, and I knew that grip.

"Juniper." Their voice was shaking. "I saw something. About the contract. About the *asset*." They swallowed. "There's a name attached to it. An old entry, from ten years ago. The night the god went quiet." Their fingers dug into my wrist. "Juniper, the name on the entry is yours."

The bell above the door rang, somewhere far off, though no one had touched it.

Moonrise was six hours away, and suddenly it felt like six heartbeats.
