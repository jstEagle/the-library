## 13. Confession at Low Tide

The tide went out past the pilings of Saltmere's old harbor twice a year, and both times it left behind things the sea had no business keeping: drowned furniture, a wedding dress once, an entire chapel bell that rang for three days before anyone worked up the courage to silence it. The locals called those hours the god's pockets, turned out and shaken.

Juniper had always liked the phrase. Tonight they hated it.

Because Sera Iln had chosen the lowest tide of the season to corner them on the mudflats below the Lantern's seaward wall, and Marrow had come along, wrapped in a borrowed coat two sizes too big, looking like a small solemn heron that had decided to attend an execution.

"You said you wanted to talk," Juniper said, picking their way down the slick stone stairs. "This doesn't look like talking. This looks like an ambush with better scenery."

"It is an ambush with better scenery," Sera agreed. She was sitting on a half-buried mooring post with the ease of a woman who had spent years learning to sit anywhere, in any state, without falling over. Her flask was in her hand. It was, notably, unopened. That was somehow more alarming than if she'd been drunk. "I figured you'd run cleaner out here. Fewer doors."

"I don't run."

"Everyone runs." She tipped her head toward the horizon, where the starless sky met the black water in one seamless band of nothing. "Some of us just do it sitting down."

Marrow stood a little apart, barefoot in the wet sand despite the cold, because Marrow claimed the mud told them things now—fragments, echoes, the sea's version of memory. Their eyes had begun to do the thing again lately: that faint silver flicker, like light under a door. Juniper tried not to watch it. Watching made it worse.

"Ask me, then," Juniper said, and was annoyed to hear their own voice come out flat. "Whatever it is you've been circling for a week. Ask."

Sera looked at Marrow. Marrow looked at the mud. The tide groaned somewhere out in the dark, pulling further back, exposing another length of harbor bed, another decade of sunk things.

"Ten years ago," Sera said. "The last battle of the Vethsworn campaign. You were there. So was I, though we didn't meet properly until after." She turned the flask slowly in her fingers. "The wards that failed last month—the forged ones, the ones with my signature—they were keyed to oath-bearer's blood. Old blood. Given freely, which narrows the list considerably, because nobody gives anything freely to a god anymore. And when I traced the key back through the guild's older ledgers—" she paused, and Juniper watched her decide not to soften it, "—every trail dead-ends at the same date. The same night. And every entry from that night is in your handwriting, Juniper Vasquez. Entries describing transactions you have never once mentioned. Entries you clearly can't remember writing."

"I would remember—"

"Would you?" Sera's voice wasn't cruel. That was the worst part. It was almost gentle, the way you'd speak to a patient whose wound you were about to reopen. "You keep ledgers for a living. You know better than anyone what a clean excision looks like. You've seen enough of them this month to fill a chapel. So tell me honestly, oath-keeper: when you look inward, does your own history have margins? Gaps where the binding should be?"

Juniper opened their mouth to say *of course not*, and found, to their genuine surprise, that they could not.

It was a strange sensation, standing on a mudflat under a starless sky and discovering a hole in yourself shaped exactly like a night. Not emptiness—emptiness would have been easier. It was more like a room in a house you'd lived in all your life that you'd never once noticed, its door flush with the wall, its edges papered over so smoothly that even your grief had learned to walk around it without stumbling.

"There's a night," Juniper heard themselves say. "There's a night I can't find."

Marrow made a small sound. Not quite a word. Juniper turned, and the child—no, not a child anymore, but still young enough that Juniper's chest did something complicated about it—was staring at them with an expression Juniper had only ever seen on people at funerals.

"The mud remembers you," Marrow said quietly. "From before. From the war. You came to the water carrying something heavy, and you set it down, and the water took it, and you asked the water to forget you setting it down." They blinked, and the silver flicker guttered. "That's all I have. It comes in pieces. I'm sorry."

"Don't apologize," Juniper said. "You've given me more than anyone else has. Including me."

* * *

They sat then, the three of them, on the ribs of some wrecked barge the tide had uncovered, because confessions apparently required seating. Sera finally opened the flask, poured a measure into the cap, and handed it to Juniper first, which was such an unprecedented act of generosity that Juniper nearly laughed.

"What was the battle?" Sera asked. "Start wherever the hole starts."

Juniper drank. The liquor was terrible and warm and exactly right.

"It starts with dying men asking a favor," they said. "It usually starts there, in my experience. Everything expensive starts with someone dying and someone else saying yes."

The memory didn't come back all at once. It came back the way the tide came in—creeping, filling low places first, rising by degrees until suddenly Juniper was standing in it.

* * *

The last battle of the Vethsworn campaign had not been fought against the empire. That was the joke nobody survived long enough to appreciate. By the final day, the empire had already signed its armistice; the papers were in a courier's satchel somewhere behind the lines. But gods are slow to hear news, and the men and women of the Ninth Company were dying of wounds received in a war that was, technically, already over.

Juniper remembered the field. Remembered the rain that fell gray and indifferent, the kind of rain that seemed embarrassed to be involved. Remembered holding Corporal Anselm Vay while he bled from a place no tourniquet could reach, his armor full of water and worse, his hands gripping Juniper's collar with the desperate strength of the drowning.

"Pray," Anselm said. "You're the oath-bearer. Pray for us. He listens to you."

"He hasn't listened in months," Juniper had said. "He went quiet. Mid-sentence, ten years ago, and never—"

"Then pray anyway. What else is an oath *for*, if not the times he isn't listening?"

And Juniper—who had been angry at Veth since the silence began, who had performed the daily rites for years out of stubbornness and spite dressed as devotion—knelt in the gray rain among the dying of the Ninth Company and prayed. Not the formal liturgy. Not the careful, contractual language of the guild. They prayed the way a starving child soldier prays: ugly, specific, bargaining, furious, honest.

*They're dying, you silent bastard. They kept your oaths when you wouldn't keep theirs. If you have anything left—anything at all—give it here. Take it out of me if you have to. I'll carry it. Just don't let them die owing you.*

The rain stopped.

Not gradually. It stopped the way a door closes, and in the sudden hush Juniper felt something turn toward them from very far away—a vast attention, weary beyond measuring, like an old soldier hearing his name called across a valley.

And Veth answered.

Not in words. Words were too small. The god poured himself into the prayer like water finding a crack—his voice, the actual voice, the fragment of him that remained after the ancient war had burned everything else away—and it came *through* Juniper, using their body as a channel, their breath as a bellows, and where it passed over the dying of the Ninth Company, they healed.

Anselm Vay sat up. Sergeant Okonwe's lungs refilled. The girl from the supply column, Tesni, whose skull had been caved by a falling beam, opened her eyes and asked, absurdly, whether anyone had seen her hat.

Forty-one people. Juniper counted them later, because counting was what they did, because ledgers were how they held the world together. Forty-one people who should have died, alive and whole and weeping in the rain, while Juniper knelt among them shaking, filled to the brim with a god's voice, feeling it press against the inside of their ribs like a bird too large for its cage.

It hadn't hurt. That was the thing Juniper had never been able to explain to anyone, in the ten years they'd been unable to remember explaining anything. It hadn't hurt at all. It had felt like being *known*. Every corner of themselves lit and seen and forgiven in advance. Veth's presence was a warmth that understood exactly why Juniper had prayed out of anger, and loved them for the honesty of it, and grieved—grieved!—that he had nothing left to give but this, his last coin, his final fragment, spent on forty-one mortals in a war already over.

And Juniper had lain awake that night, healed soldiers snoring around them, holding a god's voice inside their chest like a stolen crown, and thought:

*I cannot do this.*

Not because it hurt. Because it *fit*. Because the voice fit them perfectly, and they knew—with the terrible clarity of the young—that if they kept it, they would never again be an ordinary person leading an ordinary life. They would be a relic. A vessel. A walking argument between gods and empires, and everyone they loved would become leverage, and everyone they hated would become a target, and the weight of it would never, ever be put down, because gods do not take back gifts.

They were twenty-two years old and tired, and they wanted their small life back.

So at dawn they walked to the nearest chapterhouse of the Gilded Lantern—the guild that traded in impossible things—and they sold it.

* * *

"They gave me a fair price," Juniper said aloud, on the mudflat, ten years and a lifetime later. Their voice sounded like someone else's. "That's the part that shames me most. Not the selling. The *price*. A company pension for the Ninth's survivors. Land grants. My own commission transferred out of the line so I'd never see combat again. I sat across a desk from a guild factor and haggled. I haggled over a god's voice like it was a horse with a limp, and I got good terms, and I shook his hand."

Sera said nothing. Marrow had crept closer without seeming to move and was now sitting close enough that their shoulder touched Juniper's arm, a small warm pressure, deliberate.

"And then what?" Sera asked softly.

"And then I paid for the erasure. Same transaction. One memory, name withheld—I withheld my own name from my own sale, that's how much of a coward I was. I couldn't stand to know what I'd done, so I paid extra to make sure I never would. I built myself a room with no door and wallpapered over it, and I moved in next door, and for ten years I wondered why the house felt cold." Juniper laughed, and it came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "I resented him, you know. Veth. All these years of rites and silence, and I resented him for going quiet, for abandoning us, mid-sentence, like we weren't worth finishing the sentence for. And the whole time—" their hands were shaking now, and they let them, "—the whole time, the reason he was silent was that I was *wearing him*. He gave the last of himself through me and I sold the echo of it to a vault, and then I had the gall to mourn his absence. To perform devotion. To light candles for a god I had personally pawned."

The tide had reached its lowest point. The sea lay far off across the exposed flats, a thin silver line under the starless sky, and between here and there lay miles of mud studded with the harbor's drowned history—chains, anchors, the bones of boats, all of it laid bare and gleaming wet in the dark.

"That's why the wards keyed to you," Sera said. It wasn't an accusation anymore. It was just arithmetic. "Your blood carried the oath, and the oath carried... residue. Him. Whatever fragment stayed in you after the sale. Corvane didn't forge those wards alone. He used *you*, or he used what you left behind, and he could only do that because you buried the knowledge so deep even you couldn't warn yourself."

"Yes."

"And the Chantry. The Silent Star. The client who paid to erase 'one memory' last month—the entry dated the day your earliest memory begins—"

"Was me," Juniper said. "Or someone finishing the job. Making sure the room stayed shut." They pressed the heels of their hands against their eyes. "I don't even know anymore which parts of the last ten years were mine."

For a while there was only the sound of the distant water and Marrow breathing beside them, slow and even, the way Marrow breathed when they were being brave on someone else's behalf.

Then Marrow spoke.

"When I was small," they said, "in the temple, before my sight died, I saw a transaction. I told you about visions coming in pieces. This one has been a piece I couldn't fit anywhere, and now—" they took a breath, "—now it fits, and I hate it. A young soldier. Gray rain. Selling something bright. I saw it years ago and I didn't understand it, and when I met you I didn't recognize you, because the vision showed me your *hands*, and your hands were doing ledger-work, and I thought that meant peace." They looked up, and their eyes were wet and steady. "I should have told you sooner. I'm sorry I didn't. I was afraid of what it would cost this—us—if it was true."

"You were right to be afraid," Juniper said. "It might still cost it."

"No," Marrow said, with the flat certainty of the very young. "It won't. I've decided."

Sera snorted—a real laugh, rough and surprised out of her. "Decided. Listen to this one. Decides things, like a person with authority." She stood, joints cracking, and looked down at Juniper with an expression that had traveled a long way from the woman who'd dragged them onto this mudflat for an ambush. "Here's what I know about you, Vasquez. You were twenty-two, exhausted, and holding the last living piece of a god, and every power in the world would have spent you like currency the moment they knew. You hid it the only way available to you. It was a coward's choice and probably the smartest thing anyone's done in this story." She offered a hand. "I've done worse for less. We all have. The question isn't whether you're ashamed. The question is what you do standing in the mess."

Juniper took the hand and let herself—let *themselves*—be pulled upright.

And then it happened: the first full breath in ten years. Juniper felt it go all the way down, past the papered-over room, past the sealed places, into the bottom of themselves, and come back out again, and nothing broke. The confession sat in the open air between the three of them, ugly and complete, and the sky stayed up, and Marrow leaned against their side, and Sera was already talking logistics, because Sera processed grief through planning the way other people processed it through drink.

"We need to get to that vault," she was saying. "Before Corvane sells what's left, before the empire collects, before whatever's waking up finishes waking. And we need your blood freely given—which means you need to re-oath. To him. The god you sold."

Juniper stared at her. "You want me to swear myself, again, to the god whose voice I pawned."

"I want you to swear yourself to the god you *saved forty-one people with*," Sera corrected. "Same event. Different ledger. Pick whichever entry you can live in."

Out across the flats, the sea had begun its long return, the first thin tongue of water sliding up the mud toward them, patient as forgiveness, covering the exposed and sunken things one by one—not erasing them, Juniper noticed. Carrying them. Keeping them.

Somewhere beneath the Gilded Lantern, in a vault below the vault, a fragment of a god who had given everything and been sold for fair terms waited in the dark. And for the first time in ten years, Juniper knew exactly where the debt lay, and to whom, and in what amount.

"Tomorrow," Juniper said. "At dawn. We start with the rite."

Marrow squeezed their arm. Sera smiled like a drawn blade.

And far above them, in the starless sky, something that might have been a single star flickered—once, faintly, like a held breath—before the dark closed over it again.
