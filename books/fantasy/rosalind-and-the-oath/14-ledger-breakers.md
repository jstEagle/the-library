## 14. Ledger-Breakers

Sera Iln had designed wards for emperors. She had warded the Imperial Vault of Seals, the Winter Palace's dream-curtains, and once, in a fit of youthful arrogance, a chicken coop she was certain housed a demon. (It had. But that was another story, and one that ended with her being chased through a beet field by something with too many wings.)

None of it had prepared her for the particular horror of trying to draw a containment schematic on the back of a tavern menu while the tavern's only other patron snored loud enough to rattle the shutters.

"The problem," she said, for the third time, "is not the seal."

"The problem," Juniper said, "is that you keep saying 'the problem' like there's only one."

"There is only one *structural* problem." Sera tapped the menu with her chalk stub. The grease stains made the ward-lines look diseased. "The vault below the vault sits inside a lattice keyed to oath-bearer blood. Corvane didn't build it—the founder did—but he's been maintaining it, tightening it, feeding it guild resources for years. It is, and I say this as a professional compliment, a magnificent cage. And magnificent cages are very difficult to open from the outside."

"So we open it from the inside," Marrow said.

Marrow sat cross-legged on the table itself, because chairs were apparently a suggestion they'd decided to ignore. Their eyes had that new quality Sera was still getting used to—not blind anymore, exactly, but seeing sideways, at things that weren't quite in the room. Sometimes they flinched at nothing. Sometimes they smiled at nothing. Sera preferred the flinching; smiles at nothing were how oracles got you.

"You can't just walk into a sealed vault," Juniper said.

"I can't," Marrow agreed cheerfully. "But you can. You're an oath-bearer. Your blood is already in the lock. It's been in the lock for ten years—it's why you could find the archive at all, why your handwriting shows up on pages you don't remember writing. The cage knows you, Juniper. It's just never been asked to let you *in*."

"Because asking requires—" Juniper stopped.

"Blood," Sera said. "Freely given. Willingly offered. That's the whole architecture of an oath-ward, and before you ask: no, there's no trick around it. I've spent two days looking for one. Tricked blood, coerced blood, stolen blood—none of it takes. The founder built the lattice during the war, when the empire was full of people who'd happily cut an oath-bearer's throat to get at what was underneath. So he made the lock honest. It opens only for someone who *means* it."

The snoring patron rolled over. Somewhere behind the bar, a kettle whistled and nobody attended it.

"And meaning it," Juniper said slowly, "requires re-oathing. To Veth."

"To Veth," Sera confirmed.

Juniper looked at their hands. They had a habit of doing that when the conversation got close to something—turning them over, studying the palms like ledgers of their own history. Sera recognized the habit because she had it too. Wardwrights and ledger-keepers, both in the business of reading marks.

"Ten years," Juniper said. "Ten years I performed his rites alone in that chapel, every dawn, wondering if he'd abandoned me or if I'd abandoned him, and neither answer was bearable so I just... kept doing it. Like watering a dead plant." A short, bitter laugh. "And now you're telling me the way to fix everything is to kneel down and promise him my whole life again. With feeling."

"With feeling," Sera said. "I'm sorry. I know what you want me to say. You want me to say there's a version where you cut your palm out of spite and the lock grudgingly accepts it because technically you showed up. There isn't. I've watched wards reject people for less than spite. A ward keyed to willing blood can taste reluctance the way you can taste ash in milk."

"That's a horrifying sentence."

"I contain multitudes."

Marrow leaned forward, elbows on knees, and for a moment the oracle-gaze fixed on Juniper with uncomfortable precision. "You know what I saw last night? In the fragments?"

"No," said Juniper. "And I already hate it."

"A door. An old door, chapel-colored. And on the other side of it, something enormous and quiet, waiting the way water waits under ice. Not angry. Not even sad, really. Just—" Marrow searched for the word. "*Attentive.* Whatever's in that vault has been paying attention for ten years, Juniper. To you. To your rites. Every dawn you watered the dead plant, something on the other side felt it."

Juniper went very still.

Sera watched them and thought about the war, about the last time she'd seen Juniper Vasquez—at the evacuation of Hallowmere, both of them younger and more broken, passing each other in the smoke without a word because there were no words that either of them could afford. She'd spent ten years assuming the worst of everyone, herself included. It was easier that way. It was also, she was beginning to suspect, lazy.

"Here's what I know about oaths," Sera said, and dragged her stool closer. "I forged my first one at nineteen and broke my most important one at thirty-one, and I have spent the years between learning that an oath isn't a chain. Everyone treats them like chains. Chains, locks, cages—we're all very fond of prison metaphors in this line of work. But an oath is more like..." She gestured vaguely at the tavern, the sticky tables, the snoring drunk. "Like a door you hold open on purpose. Even when it's raining. Even when you'd rather close it. Especially then."

"That's very poetic for someone who smells like gin."

"Gin *is* poetry, distilled." She let the joke sit, then set it down gently. "Juniper. You don't have to forgive him. Not today, maybe not ever. Forgiveness isn't the entry fee here. What I need from you is smaller and meaner than forgiveness. I need a decision."

"A decision."

"Yes. The coldest, most practical kind. The kind you make in a ledger." She tapped the greasy menu again. "Veth is conscious in that vault. He has been imprisoned for a decade while pieces of him were sold off like candle ends. The empire arrives within days to collect the rest. Those are the facts, entered in full. Now—you can resent the god who poured himself into your dying friends, and you'd be right to, because nobody asks consent before performing a miracle. Or you can decide that whatever you feel about him, the fact of him suffering in a cage while Corvane haggles over the price is *unacceptable*, and act on that. Feelings optional. Decision mandatory."

Juniper stared at her.

"That's the most mercenary thing anyone's ever said to me," they said finally. "And I have worked with mercenaries my entire adult life."

"I'm a disgraced imperial functionary. Mercenary is a step up."

They walked back to the Gilded Lantern through streets gone strange with the starless dark. Sera had expected panic—riots, fires, the usual civic response to the sky breaking—but the city had instead gone quiet in a way that frightened her more. People lit candles in windows. Strangers nodded to each other. The whole city was holding its breath, and beneath the breath-holding, Sera could feel the empire gathering at the borders like weather.

At the Lantern's side door, Marrow stopped.

"It's louder tonight," they said. "The fragments. Since the archive." They pressed a hand flat against the brick. "There's a voice under everything now. Under the street. Under the stones. It counts things. Do you hear it counting?"

"I don't hear anything," Sera said.

"It counts the candles in the windows. It counted us walking. It's very patient." Marrow looked up at them both, and their face did something complicated—young and old at once. "I think it's been counting for ten years and nobody taught it any other songs."

Inside, the guild was subdued. Two empty chairs in the common room still wore black ribbon from the funeral—for Tessaly and Old Bram, killed in the skirmish, and every time Sera passed those chairs she saw Juniper's jaw tighten. Guilt, weaponized. Corvane knew exactly what he was doing when he let those deaths happen on his doorstep.

Corvane himself was conspicuously absent, which meant he was watching, which meant they had perhaps a day, maybe two, before he moved against them openly. He'd already erased them in the ledgers—Sera had seen the page, three names struck through with the cold administrative finality of a man who believed he was saving the world. Being erased by a righteous man was worse than being hunted by a greedy one. Greed you could predict.

They gathered in Juniper's rooms above the ledger hall—a cramped space that smelled of ink and dried lavender, with a single window facing the ruined chapel across the yard. Sera spread her materials on the floor: chalk, silver wire, a bowl of clean water, and the schematic she'd redrawn four times on progressively less greasy paper.

"Here's the shape of it," she said. "Tomorrow night. The empire's heralds arrive at dusk to present terms; Corvane will be occupied playing host, and half the guild will be stationed at the doors. That's our window. We go down through the archive, I unpick the outer lattice—which I can do, it's my signature on those wards even if it was forged, the forgery was *good* but it's still my grammar—and then we reach the inner seal. And the inner seal needs you."

"And if my blood isn't willing enough?" Juniper asked. "If I stand there and the words come out and some part of me means them and some part doesn't?"

"Then the lock stays shut, we've burned our one window, and Corvane hands you to the empire personally as a gesture of good faith." Sera said it plainly because sugar-coating tactical realities got people killed. Then, because plainness wasn't the same as cruelty, she added: "So don't do it until you're sure. Not sure you forgive him. Sure you've decided."

Juniper was quiet for a long moment. Outside the window, the ruined chapel stood black against the starless sky, its bell rope swaying in a wind Sera couldn't feel.

"When I was a child soldier," Juniper said at last, "starving, seventeen, watching my company die of wounds that shouldn't have been fatal—I prayed to Veth the way you pray when you've got nothing left to lose. And he came. He actually came. Do you know what that does to a person? Gods aren't supposed to *come*." Their voice stayed level, ledger-flat, which Sera had learned was how Juniper sounded when they were closest to breaking. "He healed them. All of them. And then he was silent, and I spent ten years deciding I'd imagined the whole thing, because the alternative was that I'd been chosen and then discarded."

"You weren't discarded," Marrow said softly.

"I know that *now*. Now I know he poured himself into me and I sold him for the price of my company's lives, and he's been awake in a box ever since, counting candles." Juniper laughed, and it cracked in the middle. "Do you understand the arithmetic? He gave everything. I sold it. And tomorrow I'm supposed to kneel in his ruined chapel and offer him my blood like I'm doing *him* a favor."

"Yes," Sera said.

Juniper looked at her.

"Yes," she repeated. "That's exactly what you're supposed to do, and it's obscene, and you should do it anyway. Because forgiveness—" she held up a hand before Juniper could speak, "—forgiveness, real forgiveness, almost never feels like warmth. It feels like a practical decision made by someone too tired to keep carrying the grudge. The warmth comes later, if it comes at all. Sometimes it doesn't. You do it anyway. That's the whole secret nobody puts in the temple hymns."

The silence stretched. Marrow had closed their eyes, lips moving faintly—counting, maybe, along with something only they could hear.

"Rosalind," Marrow said suddenly.

"What?"

"That's the word under the counting. Under everything. It's been there all week and I couldn't place it because it's not a word, it's a *name*." Marrow opened their eyes, and they were shining faintly, the way lamps shine right before the oil runs low. "It's yours, Juniper. It's the name he gave you. It's been sitting in the vault all this time, saying itself over and over, like a man practicing the one thing they let him keep."

Juniper's hand went to their own chest, an unconscious gesture, pressing against something invisible.

"He remembers my name," they whispered.

"He never forgot it," Marrow said. "Someone paid to make *you* forget *him*. He kept his half."

Sera looked away, suddenly needing to study her schematic very intently, because there were tears in the room and she was a disgrace, not a monster. When she trusted her voice again, she said:

"Tomorrow at dusk, then. Rest. Eat something. Juniper—decide. Not feel. Decide."

Juniper nodded. But as Sera gathered her chalk and wire and turned for the door, they spoke once more, quiet enough that she almost missed it.

"Sera. If I kneel down tomorrow and give my blood freely to a god I've hated for ten years—what does that make me?"

Sera paused in the doorway, silhouetted against the lamplight of the hall.

"Honest," she said. "Finally, horribly, honestly honest. It's the worst. You'll wonder why you waited."

She heard Juniper laugh—small, wet, real—as she walked out.

Downstairs, in the dark of the ledger hall, Sera paused by the great book itself, open to the page where three names had been struck through. She took out her chalk stub and, in the margin, in small precise letters, wrote: *Entries pending.*

Then she went to find somewhere to sleep that wasn't a tavern bench, and to practice, one more time, the unmaking of a perfect cage.

Dusk would come fast. It always did, now, under a sky with no stars to slow it down.
