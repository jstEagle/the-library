## 15. The Rite of Return

The chapel had not been used for worship in ten years, which was fitting, because neither had I.

It sat at the bottom of the Lantern's east stair like a tooth that had gone bad and been left in the mouth anyway—roof half-collapsed, pews stacked with crates of bottled fog that nobody had ever found a buyer for, and the altar still bearing the old inscription: *VETH HEARS.* Someone had scratched out HEARS and carved SLEPT beneath it, sometime during the war, and someone else—I now suspected it had been me, in a different life, in the life I couldn't remember—had scratched out SLEPT and left nothing at all. Just the scar of a word removed.

"Charming," said Sera from the doorway. She leaned on her cane with the air of a woman cataloguing structural failures for a lawsuit. "The roof leaks directly onto the god's altar. That's either blasphemy or excellent plumbing."

"It's honest," I said. "Veth never minded rain."

"You don't know that."

"I know that better than I know most things." I set down the ledger I'd carried down three flights of stairs—the old one, the founding one, its pages soft as cloth from handling—and began clearing the altar. "He liked storms. He told me once that rain was the sky keeping its promises in small installments."

Sera was quiet for a moment. When she spoke again her voice had lost its edge. "That's a strange thing for a silent god to say."

"He wasn't always silent." I lifted a crate of fog off the altar stone and immediately regretted it; the bottle inside had gone cloudy and restless, and something inside it knocked politely, twice, like a guest asking to be let out. "Ten years ago he stopped mid-sentence. Do you understand what that's like? It's not like a door closing. It's like someone reading you a letter and then tearing it out of your hands at the part where it says *and the thing I need you to know is*—"

I stopped. My hands were shaking. The fog-bottle knocked again, sympathetically.

Marrow appeared at my elbow with the particular silence they had perfected, the silence of someone who had spent their childhood being listened *to* rather than listening. They took the crate from me without being asked and set it on the floor.

"You're stalling," Marrow said.

"I'm preparing."

"You've been preparing for two days. You alphabetized the candle drawer. There were four candles."

"They were in *disarray*, Marrow."

They looked at me with those eyes that had seen too much too young, and behind them, faintly, the returning flicker of sight—the oracle-gift coming back star by star as the sky above us stayed black. They didn't say anything else. They didn't have to. That was the trouble with oracles: even broken ones could see straight through you.

* * *

We did it at midnight, because the rite demanded midnight, and because Corvane had locked down the guild at sundown and midnight was when his patrols changed. Sera had mapped their rotation with the grim competence of a woman who had once designed fortresses and now applied the same skills to sneaking around inside one. "Corvane thinks walls are loyalty," she'd said. "Walls are just walls. I built ones that sang, once, and they still fell."

Now she knelt at the altar with chalk and a knife and drew the circle from memory—her memory, the forged wards she'd been blamed for, except this time she drew them true. Watching her work was like watching a musician tune an instrument everyone else had written off as firewood. Her hands shook—not from drink, though there was plenty of that in her history, but from something older. Ten years of being called a traitor by people who had never read her signature closely enough to see it was wrong.

"The circle wants blood," she said, without looking up. "Oath-bearer's blood. Willingly given. That last part isn't poetry, Juniper, it's mechanics. A coerced oath is just a cage with extra steps, and the ward will taste the difference."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then we're three people hiding in a basement while an empire knocks on the roof." She glanced up, and her face did something complicated. "I'm not going to beg you. I watched enough people get begged into oaths during the war to last two lifetimes. But I'll say this: whatever you think Veth did to you—abandoning you, going quiet, making you carry him—he didn't do any of it on purpose. I've read the containment schematics. Whatever's in that vault has been *sealed*, and sealed things don't get a vote."

"That's supposed to comfort me?"

"No," said Sera. "It's supposed to make you angry in a useful direction."

Marrow lit the candles—all four of them, in their proper order, because apparently my fussing had taught them something after all. The light was small and yellow and utterly inadequate against the dark of the world outside, where the stars had been switched off like lamps in a house whose owner had gone to bed. And yet the little flames held. They always held. That was the whole theology of Veth, if you boiled it down: the light doesn't have to be big. It just has to be kept.

I rolled up my sleeve. The old oath-scar ran along my forearm like a river on a map—a thin silver line, the mark of the vow I'd taken at nine years old with a belly full of hunger and a head full of war. I had resented that scar for a decade. I had scrubbed at it. Once, drunk on borrowed courage I'd purchased from our own shelves, I had tried to cut it out entirely, and it had healed before the blood dried, patient as a debt.

"Sera," I said. "When I open the ward—if it works—will I feel him?"

She paused with the knife halfway to the chalk line. "Honestly? I don't know. Nobody's re-oathed to a god who's been pickled in a vault for ten years. This is, and I want you to appreciate the technical precision here, completely unprecedented."

"Wonderful."

"If it helps: the fragment in the vault is small. A voice, not a presence. If he stirs at all, it'll be like—" she searched for the comparison, "—like feeling someone breathing through a wall."

I thought about that. About all the walls between me and everything true: the guild's ledgers, the excised pages, my own edited memory, the vault below the vault. Breathing through walls. I had been doing it my whole life without knowing what I was listening for.

"Do it," I said.

* * *

The knife was guild steel, which meant it cut cleanly and apologized afterward—or so the sales copy in our own catalogue claimed. In practice it hurt exactly as much as any knife, which is to say it hurt honestly, and I bled into the chalk circle and felt the lines take the offering the way dry ground takes rain.

Sera began the words.

I knew them. That was the terrible thing—I had thought the rite would feel foreign, a dead language dug up for an autopsy, but the old vows rose in me like water finding its level. *Veth who hears. Veth who keeps. Veth who was there when the first lie was told, and stayed to write it down.* I had spoken these words at nine years old with mud on my knees and a sergeant's hand on my shoulder, and I had meant them the way children mean things: totally, simply, without knowing the cost.

This time I spoke them the way adults mean things: knowing exactly the cost, and paying anyway.

"I can't promise faith," I said, and my voice came out steadier than I deserved. "I want that entered into the record. I spent ten years being angry at him. I'm not done being angry. Some of it might be at myself, and I haven't finished sorting which is which. But I choose this. Not because I believe. Because it's owed—to him, to the dead of the last battle, to whoever I was before someone paid to erase her. Choice. That's the vow. Take it or don't."

The circle drank the words. The candle flames bent toward the center, all four at once, like flowers turning toward a sun that wasn't in the room.

And then—

Breathing. Through a wall.

It was so faint I almost missed it, a pressure against the inside of my awareness like a palm laid flat against plaster from the other side of a house. Not a voice. Not yet. Just *presence*, the way you know someone stands behind a door before they knock. And threaded through it, faint as a smell from a distant kitchen, something that stopped my heart and restarted it sideways:

Recognition.

He knew me. Whatever was sealed in that vault, however small and starved and imprisoned—it knew me the way you know a song you learned before you could talk. And underneath the recognition, unmistakable even at that whisper-thin distance, was something I had spent ten years refusing to imagine:

Relief.

"Oh," I said. My knees went. Marrow caught my arm. "Oh, no. Oh, *no*. He's awake. Sera, he's been awake—"

"What?"

"The whole time. Ten years. Sealed in the dark, conscious, listening—" The realization came over me like cold water, wave after wave, each one worse. Every rite I'd performed alone in an empty chapel, talking to a god I thought was gone. He'd heard. He'd heard every word and couldn't answer, couldn't move, couldn't even knock, and I'd stood there week after week saying *where were you, why did you leave, how could you go silent mid-sentence*—and he hadn't gone anywhere. He'd been *in the vault*. He'd been in the vault because of me, because I sold him, because I—

"Juniper." Sera's hand closed over mine, hard, calloused, real. "Breathe. What did you feel?"

"He knows me." I was laughing, or crying, or both, the ugly hybrid of the two. "He's been awake this whole time, Sera. All those years I spent cursing him for abandoning me, and he was sealed in a box under our feet, listening to me complain about him like a widow arguing with an empty chair."

Marrow made a small sound. When I looked at them, their eyes were wet, and their gift-flicker was burning steady now, twin points of returned light in the dark chapel.

"That's what I saw," they whispered. "In the fever. I couldn't understand it, but—that's it. The god is not silent. He is being eaten. No. Worse." Their voice cracked. "He's being *kept*. Like a lamp no one will light and no one will throw away."

The circle pulsed once more, gently, and through the new-made bond I felt the fragment press against the wall of its sealing—not struggling, not begging, just pressing, the way a tired person leans against a door. And in that press was a message too large for the thin channel to carry in words, so it arrived as a single image instead:

A child soldier kneeling in the mud of the last battlefield. Rain falling. A voice pouring itself out like a pitcher poured to the brim and past it, past breaking, giving everything because dying soldiers asked and gods are made to answer. And then hands—my hands, younger, terrified—closing around the gift like someone grabbing a live wire, and the terrible arithmetic of a drowning person deciding who else drowns.

*I remember,* I told him, through the wall, not knowing if the words carried. *I remember, and I'm sorry, and I'm coming.*

The pressure against the wall went very still.

Then, faint as a heartbeat heard through a mattress, it pressed back: *Rosalind.*

My name. The name I'd sold away with everything else. The name I wouldn't learn I'd lost until chapter upon chapter later, until the ledger gave up its ghosts—but some part of me, some cellar-deep part that no erasure had reached, heard it and wept.

The candles guttered out, all four at once, and the bond thinned to a thread, then a memory of a thread. Sera collapsed back on her heels, gray-faced, wiping chalk dust and sweat from her brow.

"It'll hold," she said hoarsely. "The re-oath took. Which means I can rebuild the containment wards from the inside—which means we can get into that vault before Corvane sells another piece of him, and before the empire collects." She looked at me, and for the first time since I'd known her, the disgraced wardwright looked something like hopeful. "You did it, ledger-keeper. You swore to a god in a leaky basement surrounded by unsold fog, and it worked. The Chantry would faint."

"Let them," I said. I was still shaking. I expected to shake for a long time. "Sera. There's something you need to know before we go down there. Something he showed me."

"Go on."

I looked at the altar, at the old inscription with its two crossed-out words, and I understood at last what the third word should have been. Not HEARS. Not SLEPT.

WAITED.

"He didn't abandon anyone," I said. "And when we open that vault, we're not stealing guild property." I picked up the knife, cleaned it, and sheathed it with hands that had finally stopped pretending to be steady. "We're breaking a prisoner out. And Corvane—whoever he thinks he is, whatever he thinks he's saving—is the jailer."

Above us, muffled through the ruined roof, a bell began to ring. Not the guild's bell. The gate bell. Three strokes, pause, three strokes—the signal none of us wanted, the one Sera had mapped and dreaded and hoped never to hear.

The empire wasn't knocking on the roof anymore.

It was coming in.
