## 16. Corvane's Ledger

Guild Master Corvane had three rules for reading bad news, and he followed them every morning of his life: read it standing up, read it twice, and never let the tea go cold while you did it. A man who let his tea go cold over bad news was a man who'd already surrendered.

The letter from the empire's Office of Reclamation sat on his desk like something dead that hadn't finished dying. He read it standing up. He read it twice. His tea stayed hot, which was the only victory available to him before breakfast.

*Delivery of the asset to be made within nine days,* it said, in the tidy bureaucratic hand the empire used for everything from tax notices to execution orders. *Failure to comply will be construed as breach of contract, per the terms of the Accord of Ashes.*

Nine days. The empire had waited ten years to come knocking, and now they wanted their god delivered like a crate of bottled storms, with a signature and a receipt.

Corvane set the letter down, picked up his pen, and wrote in the margin of his private ledger — the one no ledger-keeper but himself had ever seen — *They think I am a shopkeeper. Good. Let them.*

Then he went down to make sure his guild was still standing.

* * *

The Gilded Lantern at dawn was Corvane's favorite version of it. The great trading floor still smelled of last night's lamp oil and someone's ill-advised attempt to fry eggs over a brazier meant for sealing wax. Ledgers lay open on their chains. The morning clerks shuffled in with the particular resentment of people whose profession required them to be precise before they were fully awake.

He loved them. Every impossible, mercenary, sharp-eyed one of them. That was the thing nobody understood about Aldous Corvane, and the thing he had never once bothered correcting: they thought he loved the guild the way a miser loves gold. He didn't. He loved it the way a lighthouse keeper loves the lamp. Somebody had to keep it burning, or everything on the dark water died.

His grandfather had taught him that. No — not his grandfather. His great-great-grandmother, though the family tree had been pruned and grafted so many times that only Corvane and two dead archivists knew the founder of the Gilded Lantern shared his blood. Maren Corvane-Vasquez, who had built this place not to trade in impossible things but to cage the most impossible thing of all.

*Containment first,* she had written, in the founding hand he'd copied out a thousand times until his own letters looked like hers. *Trade is how we pay for the walls. Never mistake the trade for the purpose.*

Every guild master since had read those words. Every guild master since had slowly convinced themselves the trade was the point, because the trade was profitable and the purpose was terrifying. Corvane had watched his own predecessor sell off slivers of the seal's maintenance budget to fund an expansion into the name-brokerage market, and had said nothing, because he'd been young and ambitious and telling the truth would have cost him the succession.

He had spent thirty years paying for that silence. He intended to spend the rest of his life paying it forward.

"Master." Pell, the night clerk, appeared at his elbow with the particular hunched posture that meant bad news wearing good news' coat. "The vault wardwright came back positive."

Corvane closed his eyes briefly. "Which wards?"

"The lower archive seals. Someone's been reading them. And—" Pell swallowed. "There's blood on the third door. Oath-bearer's blood. Fresh."

Vasquez. Of course it was Vasquez. Juniper Vasquez, his best ledger-keeper, his most reliable oath-bearer, the person he had privately marked as a successor if the world held together long enough. Corvane had spent years watching the young man audit entries with that ferocious, unblinking honesty, thinking: *there, that's what the guild needs, someone who believes the records matter more than the money.*

It had not occurred to him — and he was honest enough, alone, to admit it stung — that someone who believed the records mattered more than anything might eventually read the wrong ones.

"Thank you, Pell," he said. "Wake the house. Full lockdown. Nobody in or out, and get me the brand-ink."

Pell went pale. "The— Master, the brand-ink? For thieves?"

"For thieves," Corvane agreed, and hated how easily the word fit in his mouth, and hated more that it fit at all.

* * *

The lockdown took less than an hour, because Corvane had rehearsed it for thirty years. Gates barred. Wards raised — the real ones, the ones keyed to his blood and no one else's, not the pretty decorative lattice Sera Iln had once maintained before the empire discarded her. Messengers turned away at the door with polite apologies. The trading floor cleared, clients escorted out under escort, ledgers chained shut.

By midmorning the Gilded Lantern had become, for the first time in its existence, exactly what its founder had built it to be: a fortress pretending to be a shop.

Corvane stood on the gallery overlooking the empty floor and felt the old familiar calm settle over him, the calm that came from doing the thing you were born to do even when it broke your heart.

Because it did break his heart. That was the joke nobody would ever hear him tell. The empire thought he was greedy. Vasquez and the wardwright and the little oracle thought he was a monster hoarding a god's voice for profit. Even his own clerks were whispering by noon, giving him the wide berth usually reserved for clients selling cursed heirlooms.

None of them knew. None of them could know, because knowing was the danger. Maren Corvane-Vasquez had understood that too: *A secret kept by one is a wall. A secret kept by ten is a door.*

The voice of Veth — the last living fragment of a god who had ended a war by pouring himself into a dying soldier's hands — could not be allowed to wake. Not because waking gods were inconvenient. Because waking gods were *hungry*, and the last time Veth had been whole, the war between heaven and the empire had burned a century off the world's history books. Corvane had read the accounts. He'd read them as a boy, in the sealed archive, by candlelight, and he had come up the stairs changed forever, understanding that the cheerful commerce above his head was a roof over a sleeping beast, and that everyone who called the guild corrupt was sleeping soundly *because* of the corruption they despised.

Sedation. Maintenance. Containment. That was the work. Everything else — the bottled storms, the borrowed years, the theater of it — was just the price of keeping the lights on.

And now the empire wanted to buy the beast, and his best ledger-keeper wanted to free it, and both of them thought he was the villain.

Corvane poured himself a cup of tea, found it had gone cold after all, and drank it anyway. Rule three existed to be broken on the worst days. That was how you knew they were the worst days.

* * *

He summoned them to the ledger room at dusk: Vasquez, the wardwright Iln, and the oracle child Marrow, who stood between them like a kitten that had wandered into a knife fight and refused to acknowledge it.

Juniper Vasquez looked tired. Good, some petty corner of Corvane thought, and then, immediately, *no, not good, never good* — but the pettiness was honest, and he let it sit there without feeding it.

"You've been in the lower archive," Corvane said.

"I've been in the lower archive," Juniper agreed. No denial. Corvane respected that, in the way he respected a well-balanced ledger: professionally, distantly, with no warmth attached.

"Do you understand what you found?"

"I understand what the guild was built for," Juniper said. "I understand what you've been selling."

"Selling." Corvane laughed, and heard how ugly it came out. "Sit down. All of you. You're going to listen, because you're going to live in this building for the next nine days whether you like it or not, and I refuse to have you die ignorant."

He told them. Not everything — never everything — but enough. The founding. The containment. The sedative rites performed every generation by a guild master who knew why the guild existed. The empire's original bargain: *keep it buried, keep it secret, and we will leave you alone.* And the offer now sitting on his desk, which was not an offer at all but a leash dressed as a contract.

"They want to wake him," Corvane said. "Weaponize him. Restart the war that killed a million people, because the current emperor's grandfather lost it and pride is hereditary. If I sell them the voice, the world burns. If I destroy the voice—" he paused, and let them see, for one unguarded moment, the shape of the thing he carried, "—I don't know that I can. It has survived everything we've done to contain it. It may survive anything we do to end it. So I do the only arithmetic left. I keep it asleep, I stall the empire, and I hold the walls."

"And the pieces you sold?" Sera Iln asked. Her voice was rough, drink-scoured, but steady. "To fund the walls. To fund the *trade*. How many fragments went out the front door while you played lighthouse keeper?"

Corvane met her eyes. She was the kind of woman who counted things, he realized. She'd have counted already; she was asking to watch him answer.

"Four," he said. "Small ones. Enough to keep the seal maintained through the lean years, when the trade failed and the empire stopped paying its stipend and forty people would have starved." He spread his hands. "You want me to be a monster. I know. It would be simpler for all of us. But monsters don't lose sleep, and I haven't slept properly in thirty years."

Silence. The oracle child, Marrow, tilted their head in that unsettling way of theirs, as if listening to a frequency nobody else could hear. Then they said, softly, "He's telling the truth. Mostly. There's a piece he's not saying."

"There is always a piece I'm not saying," Corvane said. "That's what makes me good at my job."

"And what's your job, exactly?" Juniper asked quietly. "Keeper of the seal? Or keeper of the lie?"

Corvane looked at his best ledger-keeper — at the young man whose handwriting he knew better than his own, whose audits he had trusted with the guild's soul — and felt the grief arrive right on schedule, punctual as a debt collector.

"My job," he said, "is making sure that whatever happens in the next nine days, the seal holds and the world keeps turning. And I'm sorry, truly, for what that costs you. But you should know what it costs me, too."

He opened the master ledger on the desk, dipped the pen in the brand-ink — black, permanent, written into the guild's memory the way brands were written into skin — and entered three names.

*Juniper Vasquez. Struck from the rolls. Thief.*
*Sera Iln. Struck from the rolls. Thief.*
*Marrow. Struck from the rolls. Thief.*

The ink seemed to sink into the page with a hunger that had nothing to do with paper.

"This isn't cruelty," Corvane said, capping the ink. "This is protection. From this moment, officially, you were never members. The empire's agents can search our records from cellar to attic and find no connection between you and the asset. Whatever you do next, you do it as strangers, trespassers, criminals — and whatever happens to you, the guild's hands stay clean enough to keep bargaining." He closed the ledger. "I have erased better people than you for smaller reasons. Consider it the kindest thing I'll do all week."

Marrow was staring at the ledger with an expression Corvane couldn't read — which frightened him more than any accusation could have. Oracles who couldn't see were unsettling enough. Oracles who *almost* saw were worse.

"You're wrong about one thing," Marrow said.

"Only one? I must be improving."

"You think you're holding the walls up." The child's voice was very quiet, and very certain, in the way that only the formerly blind could be certain. "But walls don't hold up what's underneath them. They just hide it. And what's underneath is *awake*, Guild Master. It's been awake longer than you've been alive. Your sedatives aren't putting it to sleep." Marrow smiled, small and terrible. "They're teaching it patience."

The room went cold in a way that had nothing to do with drafts.

Corvane dismissed them — waved a hand, turned away, gave them the back of a man too busy to be shaken — and when the door closed he stood for a long moment over the master ledger, looking at the three names he had struck, feeling the strange hollow ache of a man who has amputated something to save the body and cannot stop checking whether the fingers still hurt.

Then he went down to the vault, alone, as he did every night, and pressed his palm to the outer seal, and felt — as he had felt every night for thirty years — the faint, vast, patient pressure of the thing behind it.

Except tonight, for the first time, the pressure pushed *back*.

Gently. Almost politely. Like a prisoner tapping the wall to say: *I heard everything. I heard the child. And I have been patient so very, very long.*

Corvane snatched his hand back and stood in the dark with his heart hammering, and began, for the first time in his life, to do the arithmetic he had spent thirty years refusing to do.

Nine days. The empire outside. Three thieves inside. And between them, behind the oldest wall in the world, a god learning to wait.

He went back upstairs and wrote a single line in his private ledger, standing up, tea forgotten:

*The walls are thinner than I believed. God help us all — and forgive me, Grandmother, but I no longer believe He can.*
