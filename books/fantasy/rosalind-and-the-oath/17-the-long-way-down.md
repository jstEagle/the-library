## 17. The Long Way Down

Marrow had spent nineteen years being told what the future looked like by people who couldn't see it themselves. Temple elders with their careful diagrams. Priests with their incense and their certainty. *The stars will guide you, little oracle. The stars will speak.*

Then the stars went out, and Marrow's gift went with them—or so everyone believed, including Marrow, right up until three days ago, when they'd woken in a cot in the Gilded Lantern's infirmary and seen the future sitting on the end of the bed like a cat that had decided to adopt them.

It hadn't come back all at once. It came back in pieces, the way a window un-frosts: first edges, then shapes, then—when it wanted to be cruel—the whole terrible picture at once.

Right now, standing at the top of the stairs behind the ledger room, Marrow could see the whole terrible picture.

"Imperial soldiers," they said. "Forty of them. Maybe more. They're coming through the front doors in about ninety seconds, and Corvane is going to let them."

Juniper paused mid-step, one hand on the banister. "You're sure?"

"I'm sure the way you're sure about rain when you're already wet." Marrow swallowed. "He's going to open the doors himself. He thinks if he hands over the vault's lower levels, they'll leave the rest of the guild alone. He's wrong, but he doesn't know he's wrong yet, and I—" Their voice cracked, and they hated it. "I saw him sign something. I saw him write your names in the ledger. All three of us. Under *thieves*."

Sera Iln, leaning against the wall with her wardwright's kit slung over her shoulder like a butcher's bag, snorted. "Well. At least we'll be officially documented. That's more than most thieves get." But her knuckles were white around the strap, and Marrow—who was learning to read people now that the stars had stopped doing it for them—noticed.

"He erased us," Juniper said quietly. "From the guild's own books."

"He erased *me* from the guild's own books years ago," Sera said. "Welcome to the club. Dues are unpaid and the meetings are terrible." She pushed off the wall. "Downstairs. Now. We can grieve our official existence later."

Marrow took a breath and did the thing they'd been dreading since dawn: they reached into the sight and let it pour through them.

The stairwell lit up—not with light, exactly, but with *knowing*. Threads of what-would-be draped over every step like cobwebs made of consequence. Here, a thread that said *if you take the third step quickly, you will not trip*. There, a thicker one that hummed with danger: *in four minutes, something falls from above.*

"Left side of the stairs," Marrow said. "Stay left. In four minutes the ceiling section above the right side comes down—they're breaching the upper floors and the masonry doesn't care whose side anyone's on."

Juniper moved without argument. That was the thing about Juniper Vasquez that Marrow had come to love with a fierce, embarrassing devotion: when Marrow spoke in the voice of the sight, Juniper simply *believed* them. No hedging, no *are you certain*, no temple-trained condescension. Just belief, immediate and total, as if Marrow's word were a ledger entry and ledgers were scripture.

Sera took a little longer. "Four minutes," she muttered. "I could fix that ceiling in four minutes if I had my old tools and both my hands and a reason to care about imperial masonry."

"Sera," Juniper said.

"Yes, fine, left side, moving."

They went down.

* * *

The vault below the vault was not, strictly speaking, supposed to exist. Marrow knew this because Juniper had told them so twice, and because Sera had told them so once at greater volume, and because the sight showed them a corridor ahead that remembered being a secret and resented having visitors.

That was the trouble with dream-locked levels. They weren't locked with keys. They were locked with *stories*—wards woven from narrative, thresholds that required you to believe your way through them. Sera had explained it while sharpening a warding knife that Marrow was fairly sure was not for warding anything.

"The founder built these levels during the god-war," she'd said. "Old imperial craft. You don't pick the lock; you convince the lock you belong here. Which is why they brought me instead of, say, a very determined goat."

"A very determined goat got through the second threshold in 1187," Juniper had said mildly. "It's in the ledger."

"That goat is the finest wardwright this guild ever employed and I will hear nothing against her."

Now, at the first threshold—a doorway that appeared to be a wall until you stopped believing in the wall—Marrow felt the sight flare like a struck match.

The threads here were strange. Not futures. *Memories that hadn't happened yet.* The corridor ahead kept offering them versions of themselves: Marrow the temple oracle, honored and blind; Marrow the cast-out beggar; Marrow dead in a gutter with frost in their eyelashes; Marrow old, impossibly old, sitting in a chair by a window telling a child about the night the stars went out and came back.

"Oh," Marrow whispered. "Oh, it's showing me *myself*."

"It does that," Sera said grimly. "First threshold shows you who you were. Second shows you who you fear you are. Third—" She stopped. Her jaw worked. "Third isn't pleasant. Don't linger on any of them. They're bait. The corridor wants you to stand still and feel things while soldiers shoot at you from upstairs."

Marrow stepped forward into the offered vision of the honored oracle and let it wash over them—the silk robes, the murmured reverence, the elders' faces smoothed with pride—and then said, out loud, in the steadiest voice they owned:

"No thank you. I've seen where that road goes. It ends with me watching a child be dragged out of a temple and doing nothing."

The wall-door shivered, considered, and opened.

Behind them, Sera made a small approving noise. "Nicely done. Most people argue with it for an hour."

"What did yours show you?" Marrow asked, and immediately wished they hadn't, because Sera's face did something complicated and closed, like a shop pulling down its shutters.

"The same thing it always shows me," she said. "Come on."

* * *

The second threshold was a bridge over nothing.

Not darkness—nothing. A gap in the world where the sight refused to reach, and Marrow's stomach dropped just looking at it, the way it dropped looking down from high places except worse, because there was no bottom to imagine. The bridge itself was narrow, iron, and humming faintly, and along its rail hung small brass bells that were not ringing but *wanted* to.

"If a bell rings," Sera said, "the bridge stops existing. Simple. Elegant. Deeply unpleasant. Walk soft, walk slow, and whatever the gap shows you, don't look down."

"What does the gap show?" Juniper asked.

"Whatever makes you look down."

Marrow went first, because the sight was theirs to spend and they'd decided—somewhere between the infirmary cot and the burning of correspondence—that they were done being the person things happened *to*. They stepped onto the bridge and the gap rose up around them like water.

It showed them the temple. It showed them the night they'd been cast out, replayed in loving detail: the high priest's face, pitying and kind; the words *your gift has abandoned you and so have the stars*; the gate closing; the snow. And then it showed them the version where they'd stayed anyway, giftless, sweeping floors forever, safe and small and never cold.

*You could have been safe,* the gap said, in a voice that sounded terribly like their own. *You chose the cold. Look how that's going.*

Marrow's foot slipped. One bell trembled on its hook.

And then Juniper's hand closed around their wrist—warm, calloused, absolutely steady—and pulled them two steps forward without a word.

"I've got you," Juniper said. "Keep walking. The gap lies. It's in the job description."

"You don't know that."

"I keep ledgers for a living, Marrow. Everything lies. The trick is writing down what actually happened afterward." And Juniber smiled—that crooked, tired, real smile—and Marrow laughed, which was possibly insane, crossing a bridge over the end of the world, but laughter came out of them anyway, helpless and bright, and the bells stayed silent, and the far door opened before they even reached it.

Sera crossed last, fast and flat-footed, staring straight ahead, and if her eyes were wet when she stepped off, nobody mentioned it. Found family rules, newly ratified: you didn't mention the wet eyes. You mentioned the bells that didn't ring.

* * *

Above them, the world ended politely, in installments.

Marrow heard it through the stone: the great front doors opening—Corvane's voice, smooth as oiled ledger paper, saying something about *cooperation* and *the guild's long history with the empire*—and then boots. So many boots. The sight flickered upward despite Marrow's best efforts and gave them a glimpse: soldiers in the entry hall, torchlight on black lacquered armor, an imperial captain reading from a scroll while guild members were pressed against the walls. Two of the Lantern's night staff—old Wren, young Oskit—shoved to their knees.

And somewhere below all of it, getting closer with every heartbeat, the sealed fragment of a dying god, waiting.

"They're in," Marrow said. "All of them. Corvane's stalling about the lower levels—buying time, or selling it, I can't tell which. The captain doesn't trust him. The captain has orders to secure 'the asset' tonight." They pressed a hand to their sternum, where the visions sat like swallowed coals. "There's a sergeant named Halven who's going to find the ledger room in six minutes unless someone moves a barrel."

"A barrel?" Sera said.

"There's a hidden catch behind the wine barrel in the corner. If the barrel stays where it is, the catch holds. If someone moves the barrel—"

"Upstairs," Juniper breathed. "Wren and Oskit are still up there."

"Wren and Oskit are going to be fine for eleven minutes," Marrow said, and then, hating the next part: "But *we* won't be. If we go back for them, we lose the seal. If we don't go back—" They couldn't finish it. Eleven minutes was a lifetime and no time at all, and both of the people upstairs had fed them soup when they'd arrived half-dead, and Oskit had taught them a card game, and—

"We're not choosing," Sera said flatly. "We're doing both. Juniper, the third threshold—it needs blood freely given, yes? Yours?"

"Freely given," Juniper confirmed. "Which after everything is either very poetic or very insulting."

"So we do the threshold now, fast, and then I hold the stairwell door while you two go down to the seal. I can fold a ward across that corridor that'll stop forty soldiers for—I don't know. Long enough."

"How long is long enough?" Marrow asked.

Sera smiled, and it was the war-smile, the one Marrow had only seen fragments of, the one that meant she'd already done the arithmetic and didn't like the answer and was going to lie about it. "Long enough. Go."

* * *

The third threshold was a room full of doors, and every door was true.

Marrow understood that immediately, the way they understood weather now—by living inside it. Each door stood open onto a real future. Through one, the trio walked out of the guild at dawn, alive, empty-handed, the god sold to the empire and the sky staying dark forever. Through another, Juniper stood alone before the seal, and the other two lay very still on the stones. Through another, all three of them died on these stairs and the ledger burned and no one ever wrote down what Veth had been.

Through the last door: a page in a ledger, written in Juniper's hand, signed with a name Marrow didn't recognize. *Rosalind.* The page glowed softly, like a lamp behind paper, and through that door Marrow could see stars. Real ones. Coming back.

"Don't," Sera said hoarsely. She was staring at the second door—at herself, lying still—and her hand was shaking. "Don't look at them too long. They're not promises. They're prices."

"They're choices," Marrow said, and found that they meant it. The temple had taught them that the future was a river and oracles were leaves on it. The last weeks had taught them better. The future was a ledger, and somebody had to hold the pen.

Juniper stepped up beside them. "Which one?"

Marrow reached out with the sight, all of it, everything they had left, and held the doors up to the light.

"That one," they said, pointing at the glowing page. "It's the narrowest road and the worst odds and I can't see past the signing. But it's the only door where the stars come back." Their voice shook. "It's also the only one where I can't see whether all three of us are standing there when it happens."

Juniper looked at the door for a long moment. Then at Sera. Then at Marrow.

"Well," they said. "We've never once taken the wide road. Wouldn't want to break tradition now."

They walked through together, all three, shoulder to shoulder, and the false doors slammed shut behind them like a book closing, and the true way down opened—stone steps, ancient and cold, spiraling toward something that Marrow's returning sight rendered as a single enormous thread of gold, frayed almost to nothing, still stubbornly unbroken.

Veth. What was left of him. Waiting.

* * *

The stairwell door boomed behind them, and Sera began to work.

Marrow watched her do it and understood, finally, why the empire had forged her signature rather than hiring her: because what Sera Iln did to a doorway could not be bought. She moved through the wardwright's forms like a dancer, chalk and blood-ink and bare fingers, muttering numbers under her breath, and the corridor entrance thickened with layered light until it looked less like a door and more like the idea of a wall that had decided to become a fact.

"Halven found the barrel," Marrow reported, sick. "Two minutes ago. They're in the ledger room. They've found the stairs."

"Good for Halven," Sera said, not looking up. "Tell him the stairs are closed."

"They're shooting at the ward."

"Tell him that's rude."

The first impact came as a sound like a cathedral clearing its throat. The ward held. The second impact bent it, light rippling like pond water, and Sera swore creatively and drove another layer into place.

"Go," she said. "Both of you. Down. Now."

"We're not leaving you," Marrow said.

"You are absolutely leaving me, because I am extremely good at this and you are extremely not good at holding doors, and every second you stand there arguing, Juniper gets closer to being the only one of us who can finish this." She glanced back, and for one unguarded moment the war-smile was gone and it was just Sera, tired and scared and ferociously fond. "Besides. Somebody has to survive to complain about how you two did things. I volunteer."

Juniper gripped her forearm, hard, warrior-fashion. "Hold the door, Iln."

"Hold the door," she agreed. "Go be impossible."

Marrow went. And the last thing they saw, turning at the spiral's first bend, was Sera Iln standing alone in the lamplight with her back to the door and her hands full of light, singing some filthy marching song from the war at the top of her voice, daring the empire to try.

* * *

The lower levels were older than the guild, older than the empire, maybe older than the idea of levels. The stones sweated. The air tasted of iron and incense and something else underneath—something like the moment before a storm, stretched thin over ten years.

And the sight, Marrow's poor battered sight, blazed.

They saw the corridor ahead collapse in one future and hold in another, and called out the turn that mattered. They saw a pressure plate disguised as a loose flagstone and stepped over it, dragging Juniper with them. They saw, briefly and horribly, the containment chamber itself: a round room, a seal like a wound in the air, and something inside it that had been conscious for ten years, listening to footsteps overhead, waiting to learn whether anyone was coming to help or to sell.

"He knows we're coming," Marrow whispered. "Juniper—he's been *awake* this whole time. He heard everything. Every rite you did alone. Every—" Their throat closed. "He heard you keep the oath even after he went quiet. He wants you to know that. He's trying to say it and the seal keeps eating the words, but I can see the shape of them."

Juniper stopped walking. For a moment they were utterly still, the way people are still when something enormous lands on them gently.

"Tell him," they said at last, roughly, "that I'm coming. And tell him—" A breath. "Tell him I'm sorry it took ten years."

Above them, muffled by a hundred feet of stone, the sound of Sera's ward taking a hit rolled down the stairs like far-off thunder. Then another. Closer together now.

Then a sound Marrow would carry for the rest of their life, however long the sight allowed it to be: the specific crack of a ward failing, followed by silence, followed by Sera's voice—breathless, defiant, and no longer singing—shouting words that echoed down the dark:

*"JUNIPER! RUN!"*

Marrow ran.
