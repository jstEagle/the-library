## 18. The Voice in the Dark

The vault below the vault had a door that was not a door.

Sera had explained it twice, once sober and once less so, and both times the explanation had ended with her saying, "It's a lock that thinks it's a wall. Don't argue with it. It's had ten years to get smug." Marrow had touched the stone and gone pale and said only, "He's still talking," which was either comforting or the worst thing anyone had ever said to me, depending on the hour.

Now Sera was upstairs holding a corridor against imperial soldiers with a wardwright's rig cobbled from lamp oil, copper wire, and what I sincerely hoped was not her own blood, and Marrow was holding open a vision like someone holding open a heavy door for a very slow funeral procession, and I was alone in the dark with a god who had been silent for ten years.

Alone is a strong word. The dark itself felt occupied.

I set my lantern down on the floor. Old habit — you don't carry fire into an archive. Then I knelt, because my knees decided before my pride did, and put my palm flat against the seal.

It was warm.

Not hot. Warm the way a hand is warm. The way a forehead is warm when someone has been sick a long time and is finally, finally sleeping properly.

"Hello," I said.

My voice came out smaller than I meant it to. Ten years of rites said into empty air, ten years of *Veth who keeps the count, Veth who hears the unspoken*, all of it performed to a silence I had assumed was absence — and now here I was, saying hello like a merchant at a counter, like this was a transaction we could conduct politely.

The warmth under my palm shifted.

And then — not in my ears, because gods are not so crude as ears — I heard him.

*Juniper.*

One word. My name — not Rosalind, not the name I'd sold, just *Juniper*, the name I'd made out of whatever was left — spoken the way you'd speak the name of someone you'd been waiting for through a long illness.

I did not cry. I want that entered in the record. My eyes were simply responding to the dust, of which there was a great deal, in a vault that had been sealed for centuries, and if my voice cracked it was because the dust was very fine and got everywhere.

"You're awake," I managed. "You've been awake. All this time."

*Yes.*

"How long?"

*Since your war.* A pause, and I felt him choose his words with the care of a ledger-keeper entering a figure he knows will be audited. *Since the last battle. Since I poured what remained of me into a dying girl's hands because she asked me to save her friends, and she was so frightened of what she carried that she sold it to be locked away, and I let her, because I was tired, and because her fear was the first honest thing anyone had offered me in an age.*

"That's not—" I stopped. Started again. "That's not how I remember it."

*No,* he said gently. *You remember it as abandonment. You have spent ten years performing rites to a god you believed left you mid-sentence. I have spent ten years listening to them from inside a jar.* Something moved through the seal that I can only describe as wry. *You light far too many candles, by the way. The wax drips into the mechanism. Sera will complain about it later.*

Despite everything — the soldiers overhead, Sera bleeding somewhere above me, the empire at the doors — I laughed. It came out wet and ragged, but it was a laugh.

"They told me you were silent."

*I am contained, not mute. There is a difference. Your guild master has always confused the two. Silence is a choice. Containment is a sentence.* The warmth pulsed, faintly, like a heartbeat deciding whether to bother. *Though I confess I have not had much to say. One thinks a great deal, in a jar. Mostly about candle wax.*

"I'm sorry." The words fell out of me before I could weigh them, before I could enter them in any ledger. "I'm sorry I sold you. I was twenty-two years old and everyone I loved was dying and you were *heavy*, you were so heavy, and I thought—"

*I know what you thought.*

"—I thought if I put you down somewhere safe, I could pick you up later. When I was stronger."

*And were you?*

"No," I said honestly. "I got worse. I got very good at ledgers and very bad at people, and I resented you for a decade for a thing I did to myself."

The warmth held steady. If a god can be patient, Veth was being patient the way stone is patient — not waiting for something, just *being*, without hurry, without demand.

*May I tell you why I let you sell me?*

"You could have stopped me."

*I could have burned through your hands and taken your company's souls as payment for the healing. That is what gods of the old war would have done. I chose instead to go quietly into a box, because the war was over, Juniper. Truly over. And a god whose war is over has exactly one duty left.*

My mouth had gone dry. "Which is?"

*To die well.*

The word landed in the vault like a coin dropped down a well — I heard it falling long after it should have stopped.

"The empire doesn't think so," I said. "They came for you tonight. They want to wake you up. Put you back together. Corvane says the stars went out because you're waking anyway, that you have to be sedated, kept—"

*Corvane is half right, which is more dangerous than being wrong.* The warmth cooled by a degree, and I felt, for the first time, something beneath the gentleness: exhaustion. Deep, geological exhaustion, the kind that gets into the bones of mountains. *The stars went out because I stirred in my sleep. Not waking. Dreaming. Even a fragment dreams, and my dreams pull at the sky the way a sleeper pulls at blankets. The empire looked up, saw darkness, and concluded their old weapon was winding itself. They are coming to reignite a war that ended because both sides ran out of everything except hatred, and hatred, unlike everything else, regenerates.*

"If they resurrect you—"

*They will not resurrect me. Resurrection implies I wish to return. What they will do is pour what remains of me into a vessel — a soldier, a saint, a very ambitious priest — and aim it at their enemies. And I will be conscious, Juniper. As I have been conscious in this vault. Aware of every prayer screamed at me to stop, every city burning in my name, every child taught that the silence in the sky is holy while I scream inside it.* The warmth trembled. *I know what containment feels like from the inside. I would rather end than be aimed.*

I sat back on my heels. My knees ached. Somewhere above us, muffled through centuries of stone, something exploded, and dust sifted down through the lantern light like grey snow.

"Sera's holding them off," I said, mostly to myself. "Marrow's holding the visions open. They're up there spending themselves on getting me to this room, and you're telling me the whole rescue is pointless because what you want isn't freedom."

*Is it pointless,* Veth asked, *to bring water to a man dying of thirst, if what he asks for instead is a kind word?*

"Don't be clever with me. I'm a guild ledger-keeper. I invented being clever as a defense mechanism."

That earned me another pulse of wryness, fainter now. He was fading as he spoke, I realized. Not dangerously — not yet — but the way a candle gutters when it's finally allowed to burn down instead of being pinched out. Every word cost him something he had been hoarding for ten years.

*Then let me be plain.* And he was. The gentleness stayed, but underneath it his voice became what it must have been at the war's beginning, when priests wrote down his decrees in letters tall as doors: *There is a true name. My mother-tongue named me before the first altar, before the first soldier, before the first debt. Spoken aloud, it unbinds me — not into power, but into ending. Gods do not die as mortals die. We dissolve into what we governed. When I end, the silence ends with me. The stars relight. The wards root themselves again. The empire's weapon becomes ash, and the ancient war stays buried where I sent it.*

"And the catch," I said, because there is always a catch; I keep the ledgers, I know how contracts work. "There's a catch."

*The name, once spoken, is known. Anyone who hears it — any agent, any scholar, any ambitious heir with a grudge and a decade to spend — could speak it again someday. Not to free a willing god. To summon what's left. Names of dead gods are dangerous things, Juniper. They are hooks, and the dark remembers its shape.*

"So if I say it, I'm handing the empire the key to your corpse."

*If you write it nowhere and tell no one, you are handing yourself the key, and trusting one mortal to guard it for as long as mortals last.* A pause. *I would not ask it of you. I have watched you guard other people's secrets for ten years and it has cost you nearly everything soft in you. But I am asking, because you came down here, and because in ten years of listening through a jar, yours were the only rites I ever wanted to hear.*

I pressed my forehead against the seal. It was warm as a sleeping animal. Warm as a friend's shoulder.

"Why me?" I whispered. "Why did you pour yourself into *me*? There were priests there. Devout ones. People who'd spent their whole lives ready to carry you."

*Because the priests prayed for victory,* Veth said, *and you prayed for your friends. I have never been a god of victory. I am the god of what is owed, and what is kept, and what is spoken when speaking costs. You asked small. Small prayers are honest ones.*

Above us, the explosion-sounds were getting closer. I thought of Sera, swearing magnificently at a door. Of Marrow, white-faced, holding a vision open with their bare hands because family was on the other side of it. Of two guild members already dead in the street because of secrets I'd kept, debts I'd hidden, a crime I'd committed at twenty-two and paid for by making everyone else pay interest.

Terrible clarity, Marrow had called it once, describing a vision. Now I understood the term. Everything was simple. That was the terrible part. For ten years I had lived inside a knot of resentment and shame and half-remembered grief, and the knot cut through in one stroke: *he never left you. You locked him up. Let him out.*

"There's a battle happening over our heads," I said. "Corvane will try something desperate before he lets the empire take you — he's a believer, and believers with keys are worse than thieves. And the moment I speak your name, I become the most dangerous person in this building, possibly in this hemisphere."

*Yes.*

"And you're asking anyway."

*I am asking anyway.*

I laughed again — that same wet, broken sound — and rested my hand flat on the seal one more time.

"Then hold on," I told him. "Hold on a little longer. Because I'm not doing this with an empire at the door and Corvane in the halls and my family bleeding upstairs. When I free you, it's going to be clean. No witnesses, no soldiers, no one left alive who wants to aim you at anything. You've waited ten years in a jar. You can wait one more hour for a decent death."

The warmth under my palm went very still.

Then, softly, with something that in a mortal I would have called gratitude and in a god I could only call *recognition*:

*Rosalind.*

My breath caught. The name I'd sold. The name I'd clawed back a piece of, enough to sign a confession I hadn't written yet.

*That is how I will know you,* he said. *When you come back to speak it — come back as her.*

I stood. My legs shook. I picked up the lantern, and at the top of the stairs, where the dream-locked levels gave way to smoke and shouting and the sound of Sera's wards singing themselves apart, I heard Corvane's voice ringing through the guild hall, high and cracking with conviction:

*"If I cannot keep it, no one will have it — begin the transfer!"*

The floor dropped out of the world, and I took the stairs three at a time.
