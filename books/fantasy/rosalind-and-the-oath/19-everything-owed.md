## 19. Everything Owed

### Corvane

Guild Master Aldous Corvane had spent his whole life learning that the world did not end in fire. It ended in paperwork.

The empire's soldiers came through the front doors of the Gilded Lantern at the hour of evening audit, and they came with a writ bearing the imperial seal and a colonel who smelled of oiled leather and patience. Behind them came the sound of the street: boots, shouting, the wet thud of a battering ram against the neighboring tenements where guild families lived.

"Delivery of the asset," the colonel said, "per contract dated the founding year. You will surrender it, or you will be recorded as in default."

Recorded as in default. As if the empire were merely another client, another line in a ledger. Corvane almost admired it. He had built his life on the principle that everything was a transaction; it was bracing to meet someone who believed it harder than he did.

"The asset is not for sale," Corvane said. "It never was."

"Then your guild dies tonight, and we take it anyway."

Corvane looked at the man for a long moment. Then he looked past him, through the tall windows, at the starless sky pressing down on the city like a held breath, and he made his decision — the one he had been making, in one form or another, since he was a boy reading his grandmother's private ledgers by candlelight.

*Containment above all. Containment at any cost.*

"You may search," he said pleasantly. "You will find nothing."

He was lying, of course. But then, lying was simply bookkeeping done in advance.

### Sera

Sera Iln had been drunk for most of a decade, and she could say this for drink: it taught you exactly how much pain you could work through while thinking about something else.

She was thinking about something else now. She was thinking about ward-geometry, which was either the most beautiful language ever devised or the cruelest, depending on how many of its sentences ended in your own name being struck from the rolls.

"They're in the vault levels," Juniper said, crouched beside her behind the overturned ledger-desk. Above them, the ceiling shuddered. Dust sifted down like gray snow. Somewhere upstairs, Marrow was screaming — not in fear, Sera realized, but in *work*, the way a smith grunts at the anvil. The oracle was holding open a vision of the vault's dream-locked corridors, projecting it into all their heads like a lamp lit in a cellar, and holding a thing like that open was tearing the child apart from the inside.

"How long can they keep it up?" Sera asked.

"As long as they can," Juniper said, which was the answer of someone who loved somebody and couldn't do arithmetic about it.

Sera pressed her palm flat against the floor. Beneath the flagstones, beneath the sub-basement, beneath the vault below the vault, the containment wards still hummed — the old ones, the true ones, the ones keyed to an oath-bearer's freely given blood. Her forged wards had been switched off from within. These could not be switched off. They could only be *collapsed*, deliberately, catastrophically, by someone standing inside their geometry.

Which meant someone had to stand inside their geometry.

"I need your blood again," she said. "Freely given. And I need you to go down there and give it to the seal itself, because when these wards come down, they come down all at once, and whatever's holding the voice has to already be open when they do."

"And you?"

"I'll be holding the door." She grinned at Juniper, and knew it was the grin that had gotten her thrown out of the Imperial Wardwrights' College, the one that meant *I have already decided and arguing will only waste your blood*. "Somebody has to make sure the empire doesn't follow you down. Consider it my payment. One impossible thing: me, sober, useful, and on time."

Juniper's jaw worked. Ten years of resentment and three weeks of alliance moved across their face like weather.

"Sera—"

"Don't." She took the knife from her belt and offered it hilt-first. "Every debt comes due sometime, Kestrel's ghost. Tonight everybody pays. Go pay yours."

### Marrow

Marrow had never known that seeing could hurt.

At the temple they had taught that visions arrived like rain: gentle, seasonal, a gift. Nobody told you what it was like to hold a vision open with your bare hands while it fought you, while the future writhed like a netted fish, while every second you kept the corridor-of-the-vault visible in three minds at once was a second the future tried to take back.

They sat in the chapel ruins with their legs folded and their eyes rolled white and their fingers bleeding where they'd dug their nails into their palms, and they *held*.

In the vision, Juniper ran. In the vision, Sera stood in a doorway with her arms spread and light crawling over her skin like frost forming in reverse. In the vision, far above, Corvane descended the stairs with a ledger chained to his wrist and something terrible and hopeful in his face.

And underneath all of it, in the dark under the dark, Marrow felt *him*. The fragment. The voice. Not silent after all — never silent — singing a note so low and so patient that the world had mistaken it for quiet.

*He is being eaten,* Marrow had said once, in fever. Now, holding the vision open, they understood the rest of it: *and he is letting himself be eaten, because he thinks it is what he owes.*

"No," Marrow whispered, to the god, to the future, to nobody. "Debts go both ways. That's the whole point of them."

The vision buckled. Marrow bent their spine against it and held.

* * *

### Juniper

The vault below the vault was colder than memory.

Juniper went down the dream-locked stairs with Marrow's borrowed sight painting the way — corridors that folded wrong, doors that opened onto last winter, a hallway made entirely of the smell of rain. Twice the dream-stuff reached for them, offering comfortable lies: *you never sold anything, you never betrayed anyone, turn back and be innocent.* Twice they walked through it, and each time it cost something small and irreplaceable, like paying a toll in teeth.

The seal waited at the bottom.

It was smaller than they expected. A circle of old wardwright's script on the floor, a lantern burning with no oil, and in the center of it — nothing visible at all. Just a pressure, like the moment before a storm speaks, like a room where someone very large is holding very still.

Juniper knelt at the edge of the circle and drew Sera's knife across their palm.

"Veth," they said. Their voice cracked on the second syllable, and they let it. "I'm not here to free you yet. I'm here to open the door before the walls come down. Sera's collapsing the wards. If I don't unseal you first, you'll be buried under them, and the empire will dig you out, and everything — the war, the dying, all of it — starts again."

The pressure shifted. It did not speak — the fragment had no voice left to speak with, that was the whole horror of it — but Juniper felt it attend to them, the way a starving animal attends to a hand.

"I know," Juniper said quietly. "I know you've been awake in there. I know what I did. I sold you. I sold *you*—" and here the words nearly failed, because ten years of resentment curdled all at once into ten years of shame, "—because I was afraid, and I dressed it up as duty, and I have been keeping your books ever since as if penance were the same thing as apology. It isn't. I'm sorry. I am going to say your name. Tonight. Even if it kills the quiet. Even if it costs everything."

Blood fell into the circle's groove. The old script drank it and began, slowly, gratefully, to glow.

Above them, muffled through forty feet of stone, the first ward collapsed with a sound like the sky clearing its throat.

### Sera

The door held for eleven minutes, which Sera would later maintain was a personal best.

Imperial sappers had brought a portable breach-engine, a nasty piece of imperial engineering that chewed through ordinary wards like a goat through laundry. Sera met it with everything she had: layered countersigns, mirrored geometries, a trick involving the door's own hinges that she'd invented at nineteen and gotten formally reprimanded for. The corridor filled with light and noise and the smell of ozone and hot stone.

Somewhere around minute six, a splinter of counter-force got through and opened a line of fire along her left side. She noted it the way you note weather, tied the bleeding arm to her body with her belt, and kept working right-handed.

Minute nine: the engine's operators, frustrated, fed it more power. The door began to sing.

Minute eleven: from deep below, through the stone, through the wards, through her own aching bones, Sera felt the seal open — felt it the way a lock feels its key — and she smiled, spat blood on the flagstones, and spoke the collapse-word.

The wards around the vault did not break. They *fell*, all at once, inward, like a cathedral kneeling, and the shockwave went up through the guildhouse and out into the street and knocked the breach-engine and everyone attached to it flat on their backs.

Then Sera sat down rather abruptly against the ruined door, because her legs had filed a formal complaint, and watched the dust settle, and thought, with the giddy clarity of the badly wounded: *Well. That's done. Now somebody had better hurry.*

### Corvane

Corvane heard the wards fall and understood, with the terrible speed of a man who has read enough ledgers to know a final entry when he sees one, that he was out of time.

The empire's soldiers were regrouping in the street. His guild — his *grandmother's* guild, his family's sacred burden for four generations — was being pried open like an oyster. And somewhere below, Kestrel Kavanagh, oath-breaker, thief in his books, was opening the seal.

There was one transaction left. There was always one transaction left.

He unchained the founder's ledger from his wrist and descended the stairs two at a time, an old man moving with the desperate grace of conviction, and he burst into the seal-chamber just as the circle's glow reached full brightness and the air itself seemed to lean toward the empty center of the room.

"Kavanagh." His voice echoed strangely, as if the chamber had grown larger to hold what was coming. "Step away from it."

"No," Juniper said. Simply. Almost kindly.

"You don't understand what you're doing. Containment saved the world once. My grandmother bled for it. Four generations of my family gave their lives to keeping that voice asleep, and you would hand it to the empire, or worse, loose it—"

"I would let him *die*, Corvane." Juniper rose, blood-slicked hand open at their side. "That's all he wants. That's all he's ever wanted. Your family didn't save the world. They took a dying god hostage and called the ransom peace."

For a moment — one long, swaying moment — Corvane wavered. He was seventy-one years old. His knees hurt. Every person he had ever loved was dead, and the only inheritance they'd left him was a prison and a lie, and some tired, buried part of him looked at Juniper Vasquez and saw the truth standing there in bloody, stubborn flesh.

Then the ceiling shook, the empire hammered at his doors, and fear — old, loyal, familial fear — won.

"There is another way," Corvane said, and stepped into the circle. "A transfer rite. The founder designed it as a failsafe. The voice passes to a living vessel. To *me*. The empire gets nothing. The seal stays strong. The world stays safe." He was crying, he realized distantly, without embarrassment. "I am the last of the line. It should have been me all along."

"That's not a failsafe," Juniper said, horror dawning. "That's a *mouth*. You'd be feeding yourself to—"

"Everything owed," Corvane said, "must be paid by someone," and he began the founder's words.

Below them both, patient as winter, the fragment of Veth's voice turned its attention upon the old man — and Juniper saw, with perfect clarity, that it would accept him. That it would pour into him like water into a cup, and fill him, and use him, and that the empire would not even have to dig.

Unless someone spoke first.

Juniper closed their eyes, found the name — the name Veth had whispered to a starving child soldier a lifetime ago, the name they had sold and stolen back and carried like a coal in the chest — and opened their mouth.

* * *

### Marrow

Far above, in the ruined chapel, Marrow felt the moment arrive the way you feel a wave lift beneath a boat.

The vision they had been holding open for — hours? years? their whole life? — finally, mercifully, began to close. But as it folded shut, it showed them one last frame, one last glimpse down the long corridor of what might be:

Juniper, speaking. A name like a key turning. An old man falling to his knees, emptied. Stars — *stars* — relighting over the city one by one, like lamps lit in windows after a long war.

And beside that frame, dimmer, the other future: silence, forever, and a god wearing a dead man's face.

Marrow let go of the vision. They let go of everything. They put their bleeding hands together in the dark and did the only thing left that a seer could do.

They believed.

"Hurry," they whispered, to Juniper, to the stars, to the dawn. "Hurry, hurry, *hurry*—"

And below, in the cold heart of the vault, Juniper Vasquez drew the breath that would change the shape of the sky —

— and spoke.
