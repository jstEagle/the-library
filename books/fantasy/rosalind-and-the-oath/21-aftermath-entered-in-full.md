## 21. Aftermath, Entered in Full

Sera Iln had died twice in her life—once at the Battle of Redwater Ford, briefly and embarrassingly, and once more just now, she was fairly certain, when the wards came down around the vault and the whole world rang like a struck bell.

She woke on her back on the vault stairs with Marrow's small hands pressed against her ribs and Juniper's face above hers, gray as old ash, saying her name in the flat careful voice of someone who has decided not to cry yet.

"Don't," Sera croaked. "Don't say anything sentimental. I'm too tired to be embarrassed properly."

"You're bleeding from somewhere I can't find," Juniper said.

"Then it's a mystery. I've always wanted to die of a mystery. Very literary." She tried to sit up, discovered that her body had filed a formal objection, and settled for turning her head. The great seal chamber beyond the broken door was dark now—not the hungry dark of containment, but an ordinary dark, the kind that simply meant no one had lit a lamp. Somewhere in it lay a god who was not there anymore.

"It worked?" she asked, because she needed to hear it said by someone whose ledger-keeping she trusted.

Juniper's jaw worked. "He's gone. Gently. He thanked me." A pause. "The stars are back."

Sera looked past them all, up through the shattered skylight of the lower vault, to where the ceiling opened onto the night—and there they were. Stars. Thousands of them, indifferent and bright, as if they'd only stepped out for ten years and expected everyone to have kept the place tidy.

"Huh," she said, and then, because she was a wardwright and could not help herself: "They're slightly out of alignment. The whole firmament. Someone will have to recalibrate."

Marrow made a sound that was half laugh, half sob, and pressed their forehead to Sera's shoulder. "You're going to fix the sky."

"I'm going to *complain* about the sky. Fixing it is a different contract."

* * *

She did not, in fact, die of the mystery. She died a little more over the following three days, in the comfortable way of wounds going septic, until a guild physicker with hands like a jeweler found the splinter of wardstone lodged under her shoulder blade—a shard of the door she'd held, driven deep when the collapse took it. He removed it, packed the wound with something that smelled like a swamp's funeral, and told her she would live if she stopped being heroic for a fortnight.

"I've never been heroic in my life," Sera said. "I've been *paid*. It's entirely different."

"Mm," said the physicker, who had clearly read her file.

So she convalesced in a room on the third floor of the Gilded Lantern, propped on pillows, with a window that looked out over a city learning how to look up again. That was the thing nobody had warned her about: people had forgotten stars were supposed to exist. On the first night back, the whole street below her window emptied into the square, and strangers stood shoulder to shoulder in the cold, pointing upward, some of them weeping, one old woman laughing so hard she had to sit down on a mounting block. Children who had never seen a star asked their parents what the lights were called, and the parents—who also didn't know, not really, not anymore—invented names on the spot, and those invented names spread street by street, and by the end of the month half the constellations over Vell's Crossing would bear names no astronomer had ever written down.

"The empire named them once," Sera remarked to Marrow, who sat cross-legged on the floor doing whatever it was Marrow did now, which seemed to involve staring at nothing with an expression of mild interest, like a cat watching weather.

"The empire can have its old ones back," Marrow said. "These are ours."

Their sight had come back with the stars, but changed. They described it, when they could be persuaded to describe it at all, as less like a flood and more like a tide—visions arriving on a schedule, mostly, predictable enough to keep a little chalkboard schedule by their bed. *Tuesday: rain, a letter, someone with kind eyes lying.* They had taken to writing the smaller visions down and pinning them to a corkboard in the kitchen, where the guild members consulted them with the same casual faith they'd once given the weather report. It should have been absurd. It was, instead, weirdly comforting. Sera had watched a hardened smuggler check the board three times before a run, and when Marrow's Tuesday entry said *the bridge holds*, he'd gone whistling.

"How much do you see now?" Sera asked. "Honestly."

Morrow considered. "Enough to know you're going to ask Juniper something difficult this week and chicken out of it twice first."

"I hate oracles."

"No, you don't. You brought me soup yesterday and pretended it was left over."

"It *was* left over."

"It was made *for* me and then left over on purpose." But they said it softly, and went back to watching the weather inside their head, and Sera, who had spent forty years perfecting the art of needing no one, let the warmth sit in her chest without arguing with it.

* * *

Corvane surrendered on the fourth day.

Sera insisted on being carried down to witness it, over the physicker's objections and in a chair, which she directed two very embarrassed guild porters to hold aloft like a palanquin until Juniper threatened to put her back to bed. From this throne she watched Guild Master Aldous Corvane—founder's blood, keeper of the seal, the man who had branded them thieves in his ledgers—walk into the great hall of the Gilded Lantern and set his signet ring on the master's table with a click that echoed like a nail in a coffin.

He looked, Sera thought, like a building after a fire. Still standing. Nothing left inside.

The empire's soldiers had withdrawn two days prior—not defeated, exactly, but leaderless. Their commanding general had been one of the Chantry's quiet faithful, a man who had believed with his whole career that Veth would wake and the old war would resume and the empire would finally finish what it started. When the god died—not fell, not slept, *died*, gently, finished—the general had reportedly stood on the guild steps for an hour, staring at the returned stars, and then given the order to march home without a word to anyone. An army built around a resurrection has nowhere to go when the dead stay dead. Sera almost pitied him. Almost. She remembered the door, and the wound, and decided pity could wait a decade or two.

Corvane's surrender was not the theatrical sort. He did not weep or rage. He sat across from Juniper at the master's table—an empty chair between them where the guild's law required a neutral party, and Marrow, uninvited, had installed themselves in it with a cup of tea—and he spoke quietly, like a man reading his own eulogy.

"I was nine years old," he said, "when my grandmother showed me the seal. She told me the world above us was a roof, and the guild were the pillars holding it up, and that someday it would be my turn to stand under the weight. I believed her my whole life. I believe it still. I think—" his voice caught, the first crack in the masonry— "I think I was wrong. But I don't know how to be wrong about the only true thing I ever knew."

"You kept a god prisoner for a hundred years because your grandmother told you to," Sera said from her chair, because someone had to.

"He was *suffering*," Corvane snapped, and then, horribly, he was crying—not loudly, just leaking, a man whose grief had gotten ahead of his dignity. "Do you think I didn't know? Do you think the founder's records don't say what containment does to a conscious mind? I read those entries every year of my life. I told myself it was mercy. Sedation. Rest. And then the empire came with its contracts and I told myself it was leverage, protection, and every year the story got bigger and the god got quieter, and I—" He stopped. Wiped his face with the heel of his hand like a tired laborer. "I am done telling myself stories. Take the guild. Take the ledgers. I'll sign whatever entry you write about me. I only ask that it be accurate."

Juniper was silent for a long moment. Then: "It will be. All of it. Mine too."

Corvane looked at them—at the oath-bearer whose memory he'd helped sell, whose god he'd helped cage—and something in his face folded. "You're kinder than I deserve."

"Yes," said Juniper. "I've decided to be. It's very inconvenient."

* * *

The vote happened a week later, in the great hall, and Sera attended it armed with a cane she barely needed and fully intended to use as a gavel.

There were, it turned out, rules. Of course there were rules; it was the Gilded Lantern, an institution that had once fined a member six coppers for returning a borrowed year eleven days late. The guild charter specified that a new master must be chosen by majority vote of all sworn members, and that the candidates must be nominated, seconded, and given the opportunity to refuse—which, the charter noted with what Sera could only assume was centuries of accumulated exasperation, *had never once happened, so stop trying*.

Juniper Vasquez was nominated by acclamation before anyone even finished asking.

It was, Sera had to admit, a sight. The smugglers and wardwrights and memory-brokers and one reformed imperial defector, all of them standing, all of them shouting Juniper's name—or rather, since most of them still didn't know any name for the person but Vasquez, shouting *Vasquez* with the particular fervor of people who had nearly died together and found they liked each other. Marrow was standing on a bench. Sera herself was on her feet, cane raised, bellowing, feeling her stitches pull and not caring a copper bit.

Juniper stood at the front of the hall looking like a person who had been handed a live grenade with a bow on it.

"No," they said.

The hall went quiet enough to hear the candles.

"No," Juniper said again, more steadily. "Thank you. Genuinely. But no. I sold a god's voice to save my company and lied about it for ten years. I am not the person who should hold this institution. Find someone with cleaner hands."

"Nobody here has clean hands," called the smuggler from the back. "That's why we work here."

"Then find someone whose dirt is older and better aired than mine."

It might have ended there. It probably should have. But Marrow, still on their bench, said in the mild carrying voice they used for prophecy: "Ask them what they'd do instead."

And someone did—old Tessin, the physicker, arms crossed: "Well, Vasquez? If not you, then what?"

Juniper looked around the hall. Sera watched them do it—watched them take in the corkboard of Marrow's small prophecies, the scorch marks from the skirmish, the corner where two dead friends' memorial candles burned, the whole battered beloved mess of the place—and saw the exact moment the refusal turned over in their hands and became something else.

"I'd change what we are," Juniper said slowly. "Not the master. The *guild*. We trade in impossible things. Bottled storms. Borrowed years. Names. Memories." Their voice roughened. "We bought a piece of a dying god and locked it in a basement, and we called it commerce. We erased a child soldier's worst day and called it service. I know, because I signed both entries. So here is my condition. If I take the chair—I take it only if the Lantern stops trading. We become keepers. What comes through our doors that cannot belong to anyone—a storm, a year, a voice, a name—we hold it in trust. We guard it. We give it back when its owner comes, and if no owner ever comes, we keep it safe anyway, forever, and we write down everything, honestly, in full, so that no one ever has to buy back their own past again."

Silence. Sera found she was holding her breath along with everyone else.

Tessin spoke first. "Half our income walks out the door with that."

"Half our income walked out the door when the stars went black and the wards fell," Sera said, leaning on her cane. "We survived. We're good at surviving. We may as well survive *decently*."

"There will be clients who hate it," someone said. "Empires. Chantry remnants. People who want what we used to sell."

"Good," said Juniper. "Let them hate us honestly. It's a novelty."

They voted again. This time the acclamation was louder, and when it ended, Juniper Vasquez stood in the master's place, looking stunned and slightly seasick, and said, "Right. First order of business: someone get Sera Iln off her feet before her stitches go, and second—"

They paused. And smiled, small and real.

"—second, somebody teach me where the master keeps the tea, because I have absolutely no idea how any of this works."

* * *

Evening found the three of them on the guildhouse roof, which was technically forbidden and had therefore been Sera's favorite spot since her first week of employment. Marrow had brought blankets. Juniper had brought tea, having located it after only twenty minutes and two false cupboards. Sera had brought herself, slowly, complaining, and had been handed the best cushion without discussion, which was the closest thing to tenderness she knew how to accept.

Below them, Vell's Crossing glittered under its new-old sky. Above them, the misaligned stars burned on, waiting for someone to give them names.

"Sera," Marrow said, in the tone they used when a vision had been sitting on them all day. "Tuesday's coming."

"Mm."

"You're going to ask Juniper about the name. Rosalind. You've been circling it for a week like a moth at glass."

Sera stared at the sky. Beside her, Juniper went very still, teacup halfway to their mouth.

"I wasn't going to pry," Sera said at last. "Whatever Veth gave you, whatever you sold—it's yours. Ledgers have taught me that some entries aren't meant to be read aloud."

"I know." Juniper set the cup down. For a while there was only the wind and the distant sound of the city relearning its constellations. Then, quietly: "But I'm going to write it down. All of it. Tomorrow. First entry in a fresh book—the whole truth, mine included, signed with the name I gave away. If the guild is going to keep memory honestly, it should start with mine."

Marrow smiled and pulled the blanket higher. "Then Tuesday can wait."

"Tuesday can wait," Sera agreed, and leaned back, and let the misaligned stars spin slowly overhead—out of true, imperfect, unnamed, and theirs.
