## 22. Rosalind and the Oath

The new ledger arrived on a Tuesday, which felt right. Tuesdays were for beginnings at the Gilded Lantern—had been, once, before the guild forgot what it was for. Sera said she'd ordered it from the bindery in Lowmarsh. Marrow swore they'd made it themselves out of smuggled starlight and spite. Corvane, in the weeks since his surrender, had taken to leaving small anonymous gifts around the guild like a cat leaving dead birds: an inkwell here, a case of quills there, a jar of the good sealing wax he'd hoarded for twenty years. Nobody thanked him. Everybody used them.

The ledger was heavy as a sleeping child and bound in grey leather that had never been dyed, because Juniper had specified undyed hide. *Let it take its color from what's written in it,* they'd told the binder. The binder had charged extra for the philosophy.

Juniper sat alone in the ledger room on the first quiet evening in a season, turned to the first page, and found their hand would not move.

It wasn't fear. They knew fear intimately by now; they'd shared a bed with it for ten years. This was something older and more ordinary—the particular paralysis of anyone who has ever faced a blank page knowing that whatever they wrote first would define everything after. Juniper had entered ten thousand transactions in ledgers like this one. Bottled storms. Borrowed years. A widow's grief, purchased at discount. A king's second chance, sold at ruinous markup. Every entry had followed the same iron rule drilled into them as a child soldier with ink-stained fingers: *Record what happened. Not what you wish had happened. Not what you can bear to have happened.*

They had broken that rule exactly once. It had cost a god his voice and Juniper themselves their name.

*Well,* they thought, dipping the quill. *No time like the present to stop.*

---

The guild had changed more than the ledgers. That was Marrow's doing, mostly. The young oracle had appointed themselves Minister of Atmosphere—a title nobody had voted on and nobody dared revoke—and had hung the common room with lanterns of every color except gold, because "gold is a lie we tell about light," which was either profound or nonsense depending on how much of Sera's plum brandy you'd had. The vault below still held impossible things, but now behind wards keyed to three bloods instead of one, and nothing left the building without an entry, a witness, and a reason.

Sera had rebuilt the border wards herself, signature and all, and this time her name on them meant what it said. She'd stood beneath the relit stars the night they kindled back into being—one by one, like lamps in windows of a house everyone had thought empty—and she hadn't said anything at all, just gripped Juniper's shoulder hard enough to leave a bruise they treasured.

And Veth was gone. Properly gone, the way rain is gone after a drought breaks—not absent but finished, folded into the world he'd spent four thousand years holding together. The chapel roof still leaked. The god who could have fixed it had chosen, instead, to die honestly, and Juniper honored that choice weekly by climbing up there with tar and hammer and doing the work themselves.

That was the whole of the new faith, if you could call it one: the god is dead; the work remains; do the work.

Juniper dipped the quill again. And began.

---

*Entry the First. Entered in full, per the new charter, by the hand of the keeper thereof.*

*This ledger records the life and death of Veth, called the Silent God, called the Listener, called in the old empire's tongue Ashkarath-who-Waits, and it is written so that no one—no guild, no empire, no priest, no frightened child—may ever again trade in what belongs to the dead.*

Then Juniper stopped, because the next part was the part.

They had rehearsed it, in the way you rehearse a confession you intend never to make aloud: in the bath, on the stairs, at three in the morning staring at the ceiling while Marrow's cat—acquired from nowhere, owned by no one, fed by everyone—sat on their chest and judged them. Ten years of rehearsal. And now the pen hovered over the page and all the rehearsals evaporated, and what remained was just the truth, plain and ugly and finally, blessedly simple.

*In the last year of the Border War, at the battle of Cindermarsh, I was dying among other dying soldiers, and we called on Veth for mercy. He answered. He poured what remained of his voice into me—not to save the world, though perhaps he thought of that too, but because someone was crying his name and he had never once refused to listen. I carried him out of that field inside my own skull, and he spoke to me for six days. He told me stories. He taught me a song about rivers. On the sixth day he gave me a name—not my birth name, which I barely remembered, but a true one, a gift-name, the kind gods give when they mean to keep you.*

*Rosalind.*

*I was nineteen years old and terrified. The voice of a god is not a comfort. It is a sun burning behind your ribs, and every oath-bearer in the company could feel it, and I knew—I was certain—that if the empire learned what I carried, they would crack me open like an egg. So I did the arithmetic of a frightened child. I brought the voice to the Gilded Lantern and I sold it. I signed the transaction myself, in my own hand, under the name Juniper Vasquez, and I paid extra to have my memory of it erased, because I could not bear to be the person who had done it.*

*I have since learned that the person who does a thing and the person who cannot bear the thing are the same person. There is no erasing that. There is only entering it in the ledger.*

Juniper's eyes blurred. They let them. The old rules said a keeper's entries must be legible and dry. The new rules—Marrow's rules, ratified over Sera's objections and Corvane's sulky silence—said entries must be *true*, and truth, Marrow argued, got damp sometimes.

---

Footsteps on the stairs. Juniper didn't look up; there were only three people whose tread they knew that well, and two of them had been eavesdropping from the landing long enough that the third stair's creak had worn smooth.

"You're writing it," Sera said. Not a question. She came down anyway, wine bottle in one hand, three cups hooked on her fingers, because Sera Iln did nothing without provisions. "Corvane's going to have an aneurysm. An honest ledger. In *his* guild."

"It's not his guild anymore."

"It'll always be a little bit his guild, the way a scar is always a little bit the knife." She set the cups down, poured without asking, and read over Juniper's shoulder the way she did everything—too close, uninvited, and entirely without apology. When she reached the part about Cindermarsh, she went very still. "You were at Cindermarsh," she said quietly. "I pulled bodies out of that mud for a week. I might have pulled *you* out."

"You did." Juniper hadn't known until the memories came back, but they knew now. "Red cloak, bad knee, screaming at a medic. You told me I was too stupid to die."

"I stand by it." Sera sat down across from them. For a long moment she just looked at the page, at the words *I sold it* sitting there in wet ink, undeniable, permanent. Then she raised her cup. "To the worst transaction ever recorded in this building."

"And to the keeper who finally balanced it."

They drank. Above them, through the patched roof, the stars were out in force—Veth's old regiment, Marrow called them, mustered one last time.

---

Marrow arrived an hour later, as Marrow arrived everywhere: suddenly, sideways, and mid-sentence. "—so the temple sent a *letter*, an actual letter, asking if I'd reconsider returning now that my sight is 'stabilized,' and I'm going to frame it and use it as a dartboard, don't try to stop me—" They skidded to a halt at the sight of the open ledger, and their face did something complicated. "Oh. You're doing it tonight."

"Tonight."

"Can I—" Marrow hovered. "Can I see? Just the part where you say his name?"

Juniper turned the book toward them. There it was, further down the page, where the entry reached the night at the seal:

*He asked me for death the way a tired traveler asks for a door. And to open that door I had to speak his true name—the name that was the last thing left of him, the secret empires would have bled continents for. So I spoke it. Once, aloud, into the dark where only he could hear. And then I wrote it here, on this page, and burned the page, so that the name now lives in exactly one place: my memory. I am the door, and I am the lock, and I am the keeper of the key, and I will carry it until I die, and when I die it dies with me, and that is not a burden. I have carried burdens. This is a promise.*

"He liked it," Marrow said softly. "When you spoke it. I wasn't there, but I dreamed it after, the way you dream weather. He liked the sound of it. He said—" Marrow frowned, reaching. "He said it sounded like his mother saying dinner was ready."

Juniper laughed, wet and startled. "That's not dignified."

"He was a god, not a statue." Marrow curled into the chair beside Sera, stole her cup, and looked at the entry's final lines, the ones Juniper had saved for last:

*The guild that bought my crime no longer trades in impossible things. We keep them. We witness them. We enter them in full. And I sign this first entry not with the name I was born to nor the name I hid behind, but with the name I was given and sold away and have clawed back out of the dark, interest paid in full:*

*Rosalind Vasquez-Kavanagh, Keeper of the Gilded Lantern, formerly the silent partner of a silent god.*

*(Kavanagh is Sera's surname. She doesn't know yet. Someone should probably tell her.)*

"I know," Sera said, reading upside down. "I've known for a week. You talk in your sleep, Rosalind."

---

Later—much later, the candles guttered, the wine gone, Marrow asleep in the good chair with the cat standing guard on their chest—Juniper blotted the final line and closed the cover, and felt the click of the clasp travel up through their palm like a small heartbeat.

Somewhere downstairs, a knock at the guild door. Three knocks, then two, then one—the old signal, retired a decade ago, dusted off by whoever stood outside in the cold. Sera's hand went to her knife out of pure habit. Marrow opened one eye.

Juniper rose, took the lamp, and went down to answer it, because that was the job now: you kept the door, you kept the record, you kept the faith however it arrived.

On the step, hooded against the drizzle, stood a young woman Juniper had never seen, holding out a small clay jar that hummed faintly, like a bottled storm deciding whether to behave.

"I was told," she said, "this is where you bring things that shouldn't exist anymore."

Juniper looked at the jar. Looked at her. And smiled, tired and true.

"Come in," they said. "Tell us everything. We'll write it down."
