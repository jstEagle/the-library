## 2. A Prayer Answered Collect

The entry was open when Rafael came down to the counting floor at dawn.

She knew it before she reached her desk. She felt it the way a sailor feels a change in wind—not a sound, not a sight, but a pressure against the skin of things. Ledger Nine, folio forty-one, line seventeen: *Favor of Ashen-Vey, god of the salt road, one (1) petition, status CLOSED.* The word *CLOSED* had been written in her own hand eleven years ago, in guild black, with the double-strike that meant finality. It was still there. But beneath it, in an ink she did not recognize—a brown so old it had gone almost gold—new letters were surfacing like drowned bodies rising slowly through water.

*ANSWERED.*

Rafael stood over the ledger for a long moment without touching it. Around her, the counting floor woke into its morning order: clerks unshuttering windows, the smell of tallow and cold tea, someone two desks away arguing softly with a column of figures as though it were a stubborn child. All of it ordinary. None of it touched the small golden word on her page.

She pressed her thumb flat beside the entry, careful not to smudge it. The paper was warm. Not sun-through-the-window warm. Warm like a hand that has just let go.

"Okafor." Supervisor Brann's voice, behind her, dry as dust in a hinge. "You're staring at folio forty-one."

"It opened itself, sir."

"Ledgers don't open themselves."

"This one did."

He came around the desk and looked down at the line. Rafael watched his face do something complicated and quick—the flicker of a man seeing a rat cross a clean kitchen—and then settle back into professional blankness. He took out his spectacles, put them on, took them off again.

"Date of original petition," he said.

"Forty-one years ago. Petitioner: Tomas Ferrow, ferryman, Salt Road crossing. Prayer logged under the standard tithe. Favor granted by—" she checked the provenance column, though she knew it by heart, "—Ashen-Vey. Status closed upon the god's recorded death. Year of the Border Treaties."

"And now?"

"Now it says answered."

Brann rubbed the bridge of his nose. "Ferrow is dead," he said. "Or moved. Or both."

"He's neither, sir. I looked him up last night after I noticed the ink was wet." She had told herself it was diligence. It had been something closer to hunger. "Tomas Ferrow, aged seventy-three, still ferries the Salt Road crossing. He never stopped paying his tithe. Forty-one years, never missed a season, even after we marked the account dormant."

Brann was quiet for a moment. Somewhere across the floor, a clerk laughed at something; the sound seemed to come from another country.

"Send for him," Brann said finally. "Quietly. And Okafor—when he comes, you copy the transaction. Nothing else. You don't annotate. You don't interpret. You write what happened and you close the line again, and this time you use the good wax." He paused at the edge of her desk. "And if anyone from the upper floors asks, the entry was always open. Bookkeeping error. Yours."

The injustice of it sat in her chest all morning, small and hot, like a swallowed coal. *Yours.* Eleven years of double-struck closures, and the first impossible thing the ledgers ever did would be entered against her name. She copied out the summons with steady hands anyway. That was the discipline. That was the whole of the discipline: your hands stay steady while everything else burns.

* * *

Tomas Ferrow came at midday, and he came afraid.

Not of the Guild—or not only of the Guild. He stood in the receiving room wringing a wool cap in fists gone white, a big man made stooped by decades of rope-work, salt-cracked hands, a face weathered down to its essential kindness. He kept looking at the walls as if they might lean in and listen.

"Mistress scribe," he said, when Rafael bade him sit. "I want you to understand. I didn't ask for it back. I know how it works. I paid my tithe every season because that's what you do—you pay, and the prayer stays where you left it, and nobody bothers anybody." His voice cracked. "But three nights ago, on the crossing, in the fog, my boat went onto the pilings. Split like kindling. And I'm seventy-three years old, mistress, and the water was black and freezing, and I thought, well. This is the far shore, then."

He turned his hands over. They were unmarked. Smooth, even—as though the river had declined, politely, to touch him.

"And then I was standing on the dock," he said. "Dry. Whole. And the fog was full of warmth, like standing near a hearth, and something said my name. Not my given name. My *mother's* name for me, the one only she used, and she's been dead thirty years." He looked up, and his eyes were wet and terrified and shining. "It said, *Tomaso. Your account stands open. We are settling.*"

The receiving room was very quiet. Rafael became aware that she had stopped breathing.

"What did you do?" she asked.

"What any honest man does when a dead god pays his debts." He managed something like a laugh. "I came here. To make it right. Because whatever's come loose down in those cellars of yours, mistress—it isn't mine, and I don't want it, and I'd rather owe the Guild than owe *that*."

Rafael wrote none of this down. Brann had said nothing else, no annotation, no interpretation—but her pen hovered over the transaction form and she understood suddenly, with a lurch, that the form itself was the trap. There was a box for *nature of miracle*. There was a box for *disposition*. There was no box for *a man heard his mother's love in the mouth of a murdered god*. The ledger could hold the fact and not the truth, and it had been doing so for forty-one years, and so had she.

They settled him by afternoon. Two hundred crowns—generous, Brann called it, for a dormant account—and a lifetime waiver of tithes, and a signature on a form that transferred all claims, present and future, arising from the favor of Ashen-Vey, unto the Ledger Guild of Vess, its heirs and assigns forever. Ferrow read it twice. His lips moved. Then he signed, because men who have nearly drowned will sign anything that promises dry land, and because he believed, God help him, that the Guild wanted the paperwork rather than the thing itself.

The Guild wanted the thing itself.

* * *

They brought it up from the cellar at dusk, in a jar.

Rafael was ordered to witness the transfer—standard procedure, two signatures, chain of custody—and so she stood in the vault antechamber while a senior acquirer named Mistress Vole set the jar on the lead-lined table and lifted away the cloth.

It was a preserving jar, the plain kind used for pickled eels, glass gone cloudy with age. Inside there was no eel. Inside there was warmth. That was the only word for it, and Rafael hated how inadequate it was and how exactly right: a soft amber glow, dense as honey, that moved when you looked away and was still when you looked back. And it hummed. Low, below hearing, felt in the teeth and the breastbone—a note like the one a struck bell holds after the sound has officially ended.

Vole handled it with tongs lined in wool, the way one handles a sleeping animal or a live coal. "Forty years in the dark and it still answers collect," she said, with the satisfaction of a fisher landing a prize. "Do you know how many of these went flat when the old gods died? Most of them. This one waited." She glanced at Rafael. "You're the scribe on the source account?"

"I am."

"Then mark it received. Grade one. Unspent." Vole smiled, and it was a terrible smile, bright and professional. "Grade ones are rare now. The cellar will be glad of it."

Rafael signed. Her hand did not shake, which she would think about later, in the dark, with something like shame. The jar hummed against the table, and the hum passed up through the wood, through the floor, through the soles of her boots, and settled somewhere behind her ribs like a second heartbeat that was not hers.

As Vole carried it toward the stair, the light inside the jar flared once—brief, deliberate, unmistakable—and Rafael would have sworn, would swear it still, that the warmth turned toward her. That it looked at her the way Ferrow's story had looked at her. That it knew the name on the account. That it knew the hand.

Then the door shut, and the antechamber was only a room again, cold and lit by tallow, smelling faintly of brine.

* * *

She copied the transaction that night, alone at her desk while the rest of the floor slept.

*Received into inventory: one (1) favor, grade one, unspent, provenance Ashen-Vey (deceased), claimant T. Ferrow, consideration 200 crowns and waiver, witness R. Okafor.*

Simple words. Words she had written ten thousand times in ten thousand permutations, words that meant nothing more than grain received or tolls collected. She dipped her pen in guild black and set it to the line.

The ink moved.

Not smeared. Not bled. *Moved*—it crawled along the fibers of the page like something waking, like blood returning to a numb limb, and for one vertiginous instant Rafael saw that the words she was writing were not entirely hers, that the ledger was reading her even as she read it, that the page was a throat and her pen was a tongue and somewhere below the counting floors, below the vaults, below the treaties and the taxes and the tidy double-struck closures, something vast and patient was tasting each word she wrote and remembering.

She pulled the pen away. The ink stilled. The entry sat there, complete, correct, damning: a god's answered prayer, bought for two hundred crowns, filed between a shipment of candle wax and a widow's defaulted loan.

Rafael sat in the lamplight until the candle guttered, and she understood what she had spent eleven years not understanding. She had thought herself a keeper of accounts. She was not a keeper. She was a gear—cut true, oiled well, turning faithfully inside a machine whose purpose she had never once asked, because asking would stop the wages, and stopping the wages would stop the food, and the debt-bond around her neck was long enough to reach the gallows but short enough to reach the pantry.

Two hundred crowns. A man's terror signed away. A god's warmth in a pickle jar, humming in the dark below, waiting.

She blew out the lamp. In the black, her own handwriting seemed to breathe on the page.

And on folio forty-one, beneath the entry she had just closed—in that same old gold-brown ink, patient as tide—one new word surfaced before her eyes:

*NEXT.*
