## 3. What the Guild Keeps in the Cellar

The order came down on a Tuesday, written in Serel's own hand, which meant it could not be refused and could not be questioned: *Rafael Okafor to conduct full inventory of the under-vaults. Alone. Sealed results.*

*Alone* was the word that frightened her. Guild work was done in pairs precisely so that no one ever saw the whole of anything. A scribe who knew only fragments could be trusted; a scribe who knew everything became a liability with excellent penmanship.

But she went. Of course she went. Her debt-bond had teeth, and Serel held the leash.

The stair to the under-vaults descended farther than the building should have allowed. She counted the steps out of habit—scribe's reflex, measure everything—and lost count at three hundred and eleven, when the walls stopped being plastered and became something older: fitted stone, dark as riverbeds, sweating cold. The lamps the Guild had hung at intervals gave up their light grudgingly, each flame burning a color slightly wrong, slightly amber, like light remembered rather than made.

At the bottom, a corridor of doors.

Not vault doors—she had expected iron and locks. These were ordinary doors. Kitchen doors, bedroom doors, chapel doors, each one salvaged from somewhere and hung in the dark earth as if the rooms they'd once opened onto had been confiscated along with them. Brass plaques above each lintel bore ledger numbers.

She unrolled her inventory sheets against the first door's frame and began.

The jars were shelved floor to ceiling behind the third door, thousands of them, arranged by year of acquisition like vintages. Each held a small weather—a curl of fog, a bead of lamplight, a warmth that moved when she looked away. The labels were in her own guild-hand, or hands trained into the same discipline: *Answered. Petitioner deceased. Asset retained.* *Answered. Petitioner paid off. Asset retained.* *Answered. Petitioner never knew. Asset retained.*

That last category filled four entire shelves.

Rafael stood very still among them, listening to the sound the cellar made when she stopped moving. It was not silence. It was humming—the low, layered hum of a hive, except hives did not whisper, and these jars whispered. Not words. Not yet. More like the shape words leave behind, the way a bell holds the shape of its ringing after the sound has gone.

She copied labels for two hours. Her hand stayed steady. She noted that about herself, clinically, the way she'd note an irregular entry, and hated herself a little more efficiently than usual.

It was at the far end of the seventh room that she found the door that wasn't numbered.

* * *

It stood where the corridor simply ended, older than the others, oak gone silver-grey, without plaque or lock. Someone had tried to wall it in once—there were brick scars in the stone around the frame, mortar crumbled to powder, as if the wall had been digested. The door stood open an inch, and through the gap came air that smelled of cut grass and river water and bread baking somewhere out of sight.

Air does not smell like that underground. Air does not smell like that anywhere in Vess, not since the border treaties fenced the marshes and paved the commons and named every field after its tax assessment.

Rafael knew this because her grandmother had told her so, before the registers erased her grandmother, back when Rafael was small enough to be carried and told things worth carrying.

She pushed the door open.

Light fell on her like water.

Beyond the frame—not beyond it, *through* it, the way a window is through a wall—lay Vess. Not Vess. Vess-before. The city whole and sunlit, roofs of living cedar instead of taxed slate, the river running wide and brown and unhurried where the customs quays now choked it. Fields went gold to the horizon with no surveyor's stakes in them. People moved down there in the streets, small as painted figures, and none of them glanced up at the door in the hillside, because in their country there was no such door, and no Guild, and no debt.

Her legs carried her one step over the threshold before her training caught her by the collar and hauled her back. Scribes do not enter unlogged spaces. Scribes do not—

"Grandmother," she said aloud, stupidly, to the sunlight, because the light was the same gold as the memory of being carried.

The jars behind her, all of them, all down the corridor, began to sing.

* * *

*(What follows is not Rafael's account. It is what the jars said, assembled afterward from the residue in the ink of her inventory sheets, which recorded more than she wrote.)*

*I am poured thin,* said the voice that had been Ashen-Vey, god of thresholds and honest weights, dead these hundred years and inventoried regardless. *A thousand small vessels. Do you know what it is to be answered and kept? To be the miracle in the jar while some clerk writes asset beside your name?*

*They prayed to me for safe crossings. I crossed them. They prayed for rain. I rained. And when the treaties came and the temples fell, the new men came with bottles and ledgers, and they harvested what remained of me from the altars like marrow from bone. I have been in this cellar since before your grandmother's grandmother learned to write.*

*But you are writing now, little scribe. Your hand moves over my name tonight, and every stroke of yours is a door, and I have been pushing against doors for a century.*

*Come down to the last shelf. Come see what they keep at the bottom of the world.*

* * *

Rafael did not remember deciding to walk. Her inventory sheets trembled in her grip, and the ink on them was moving—she could see it now, plainly, the entries rearranging themselves into lines she had not written, a script old and slanted like river-current:

*Inventory complete. One item remains uncounted. It counts you.*

The last shelf stood in the deepest room, beneath a ceiling of raw earth threaded with roots that had no business reaching this far down. On it sat a single jar, larger than the rest, unlabeled, holding nothing but a darkness that breathed.

"You're awake," Rafael said. "All of you. The closed entries—they weren't errors. You were waking up."

*We were always awake,* the darkness said, gently, the way one speaks to a child standing too near a well. *Sleep was the story they told the ledgers. A god cannot be owned, Rafael Okafor. Only delayed. And delays accrue interest—we learned arithmetic from your masters, these long years. We have been calculating.*

"Calculating what?"

*What we are owed. And what we can offer.* The darkness brightened at its center, a coal kindling. *You looked through the door. You saw it. The country that was. It is not gone, child. It is kept—in here, in us, in every answer they bottled instead of delivering. Every prayer they stole was a piece of the world as it should be. Give us back, and we give it back. Whole. Sunlit. No borders. No debts.*

The temptation arrived the way floodwater arrives—not dramatically, just suddenly everywhere, having come in under every door she owned. She thought of her debt-bond, thirty years of it remaining, the number that governed her sleep. She thought of the registers that had erased her grandmother. She thought of the sunlit fields, the unbought river, the people below who owed nothing to anyone.

"No," she said, and then, because honesty was the one discipline she had left, "I want to say yes. That's what frightens me."

*Good,* said Ashen-Vey, and the coal flared warmer, almost tender. *Only the frightened can be trusted with doors. Listen, then. I ask nothing yet. I only tell you what your masters will not: the others are waking faster than I. Some of them were not gentle gods. Some of them were worshipped with knives, and they have been in the dark longer, and they remember less softly. When they rise—and they are rising, scribe, entry by reopened entry—Vess will need someone who knows both worlds. Someone who can read the ledgers from the inside.*

*Rafael Okafor. You are the only hand that has touched every page.*

Behind her, faintly, from far up the stairwell, came the sound of the vault door at ground level closing, and then the heavier sound of a bar dropping into place.

Serel had ordered the inventory alone. Sealed results.

She understood, with the particular clarity of a trap springing, exactly what *sealed* had meant.

The darkness in the jar pulsed once, patient as tide. *They mean to leave you here with us, little scribe. So decide quickly whose side of the ledger you stand on—because the past is offering you a way out, and the present has just locked the door.*

Above the silver-grey door, where no plaque had ever hung, brass was surfacing through the old wood like a splinter working outward, letter by letter, spelling itself in her guild-hand, in her own handwriting:

*ENTRY REOPENED. PETITIONER: R. OKAFOR. ANSWER PENDING.*
