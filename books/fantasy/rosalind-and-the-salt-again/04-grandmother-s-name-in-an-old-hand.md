## 4. Grandmother's Name in an Old Hand

The provenance archive sat beneath the counting floor, three levels down, where the air tasted of cold stone and older ink. Rafael had been granted access twice in her nine years of bond—once to copy a shipping manifest, once to verify a seal—and both times a Guild warden had stood at her shoulder, breathing through his nose like a man counting someone else's money.

Tonight there was no warden. Tonight there was only the empty corridor above, the cellar door still humming faintly behind her thoughts, and the ledger she'd stolen from her own desk: the founding folio, *Provenance of Miracles, Years One Through Twelve*, its spine cracked like an old man's knuckles.

She told herself it was routine. An audit of the audit. A scribe checking a scribe. But her hands knew better; they had been shaking since the vaults, since the door that opened onto sunlit Vess-before-Vess, since the jars had whispered in a voice like warm honey poured over gravel: *Ashen-Vey. Ashen-Vey. We remember.*

The archive was one long room, and the lamps burned low and blue—the Guild economized even on light. She set the folio on a reading table scarred by two centuries of elbows and opened it to the first entry.

*Item the First. The Founding Acquisition.*

The hand was beautiful. That was the thing that struck her first, before the words even resolved—how lovely the script was, how patient, each letter drawn with the care of a man who believed he was doing holy work. Scribes always wrote their worst crimes in their best hands.

*Acquired from the Temple of the Salt Tide, Vess-by-the-Marsh, in the year of the Border Treaties. One (1) miracle, unclassified, harvested from the person of the presiding priestess during the rite of the Turning Year. Harvested under Writ of Salvage, signed Serel Oduvai, Guildmaster-Designate. Subject consented, per attached testimony.*

Rafael read the last line four times.

*Subject consented.*

Her grandmother had been a priestess. Not of Ashen-Vey—of nothing so grand as that. Of the Salt Tide, the little god of the marsh country, the one who kept the dikes honest and the fish plentiful and asked for nothing but that someone remember his name at the turning of the year. Mama had sung his litany as a lullaby without knowing she was singing. *Salt keeps, salt gives, salt takes back what the river forgives.* Rafael had thought it was nonsense syllables, the way children's songs are.

The presiding priestess at the Turning Year, the year of the Border Treaties.

There were eleven priestesses of the Salt Tide in the marsh country in that generation. Rafael knew because she'd spent one sleepless night, years ago, copying a census of religious orders for a tax assessment, and the number had lodged in her like a splinter. Eleven women. And only one of them had been her grandmother, though no record anywhere in Vess said so, because her grandmother's name had been struck from every register the year Rafael was born—a debt-collection erasure, the Guild called it, wiping a debtor's line clean of honorifics. Rafael had grown up thinking her grandmother had done something shameful.

She turned the page with fingers that did not feel like hers.

*Attached testimony:* a single sheet, copied in the same beautiful hand. It was not the priestess's testimony. It was the harvester's.

*The subject was informed that the rite would proceed as normal. She was not informed of the extraction. Note: extraction from a willing celebrant yields a stronger vintage; extraction from an unwilling one yields more, but spoils faster. We chose abundance over quality, per Guildmaster-Designate's instruction. The subject experienced the event as a failure of faith. Recommend follow-up in ten years to harvest the grief, which compounds.*

*Recommend follow-up in ten years to harvest the grief, which compounds.*

Rafael sat very still in the blue lamplight. Somewhere above her, the counting floor creaked as clerks worked the late entries, pens scratching like insects in a wall.

They hadn't just taken the miracle. They had taken the *aftermath*. They had come back, decade after decade, to drink down what the theft left behind—the shame, the doubt, the slow erosion of a woman's belief in her own calling—and they had logged it all as yield. Her grandmother had spent forty years feeling her faith die and calling it her own failing, and every ounce of that dying had gone into a jar in a cellar, entered into inventory, taxed, traded, *compounded*.

And Rafael—

Rafael pulled her own bond-contract from her coat. She knew it by heart; she had copied it out as a girl, when the Guild first took her, because copying things made them real and real things could be survived. *Debt of inheritance, incurred by the estate of [name struck], transferred to issue, per the Erasure Statutes.* She had always assumed the struck name hid some commercial ruin. A defaulted loan. A smuggled cargo.

She laid the contract beside the folio and compared the account numbers.

The founding acquisition carried a ledger designation: *V-1*. Every interest payment on V-1, every dividend, every reinvested yield, flowed into a holding account. And the holding account's number was the same number that appeared, in the bond-contract, as the source of her family's debt.

Not a loan. Not a ruin. The debt was the theft itself, inverted. They had taken her grandmother's divinity, sold it, invested it, and then billed the family for the privilege—as if the miracle had been a loan advanced to the priestess, as if the woman had *borrowed* her own holiness and died owing it back with interest.

Nine years. Nine years of copying prayers into asset columns, of balancing accounts that balanced because the other side of every entry was a person emptied out. She had thought she was surviving the machine. She had been the machine's handwriting.

The lamp guttered. In the silence Rafael heard, or imagined she heard, the faint hum rising through the stone from the cellar below—the jars, the answers, the bottled tide of everything the Guild had skimmed off a country's soul. And beneath the hum, almost too low to catch, a voice that was not the jars' voice at all. Older. Drier. Like wind over salt marsh.

*Salt keeps,* it said. *Salt gives.*

Her grandmother's lullaby. Her grandmother's god. The little god of the dikes, whose name had been harvested along with everything else, whose entry must sit somewhere in this folio among the founding acquisitions, closed and dormant and presumed dead.

*Salt takes back what the river forgives.*

Rafael looked down at the folio, at the beautiful criminal hand, and found that her grief had changed shape while she wasn't watching. It had cooled, and hardened, and taken an edge. This was not despair. Despair was a debt; she knew debts; she knew how they pressed you flat and made your days small. This was something else. This was an *entry awaiting posting*, and she was the only hand in Vess qualified to post it.

She turned to the last page of the founding folio, where the closing certifications lived, and there it was—the thing she had half-known she would find, the way you know a storm is coming from the taste of the air.

A second signature, beneath Serel Oduvai's. A witness signature, small and neat, in a hand she recognized because she saw it every day of her working life, aged now but unmistakable: the current Guildmaster, Serel, signing himself as witness to his own father's crime, thirty years before he rose to oversee it.

He had known. He had always known. And he had let her—Okafor, the granddaughter, the heir of the erased priestess, the living collateral on the Guild's founding sin—copy his ledgers for nine years, pen in hand, close enough to touch the truth and never see it.

Or never be allowed to see it. Or perhaps—her blood went cold at the thought—perhaps he had been waiting to see if she would find it herself, the way you wait to see whether a seed will come up.

The lamp went out.

In the dark, the voice from below came clearer, patient as water finding a crack. *You are the last entry, little scribe. The one they forgot to close.*

Rafael gathered the folio and her contract against her chest, and climbed toward the lamplight of the counting floor, where Guildmaster Serel was waiting by her desk with two cups of tea and an expression of terrible kindness.

"Rafael," he said gently. "We need to talk about your grandmother."
