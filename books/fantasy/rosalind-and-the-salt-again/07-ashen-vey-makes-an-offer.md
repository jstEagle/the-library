## 7. Ashen-Vey Makes an Offer

The old country smelled of rain that had not yet fallen.

Rafael stood in the doorway of a house she had never entered and knew every room of. Her grandmother's house—though no record said so, though the Guild's registers listed the plot as vacant since before Rafael was born. Here it was not vacant. Smoke curled from a chimney. Bread warmed somewhere behind walls the color of honey. The doorframe bore notches, small knife-cuts climbing toward the lintel, marking the height of children across years.

She did not go in. She had learned, in three days of walking through Vess-that-was, what happened to people who went in.

A woman had stepped into her own childhood kitchen on the second day, laughing, weeping, calling a name—and by evening she could no longer say her own. The old country took its payment in the present tense. It kept you by unmaking everything else.

So Rafael sat on the doorstep instead, with her ledger-book open on her knees, and recorded what she saw: *Sunlight at an angle suggesting late afternoon, harvest season. Wheat beyond the fence line, unblighted. A well with a rope still good.* And beneath it, because her hand moved before her caution could stop it: *This is where she prayed.*

"You count even grief," said Ashen-Vey. "I find that beautiful."

He was sitting beside her. She had not seen him arrive; there was no arriving for him anymore, only a kind of attention that condensed into a shape when he wished to be looked at. He wore the form of a man of perhaps fifty, gray at the temples, hands stained to the wrist as if he had been reaching into dark soil all morning. His eyes were the color of jarred light.

"I count," Rafael said, "because counting is the only thing left that doesn't lie to me."

"Counting lies constantly." He nodded at her book. "You wrote *wheat, unblighted*. But I remember the blight year. Your grandmother prayed through it on her knees until they bled, and I answered, and men in gray coats came and put my answer in a jar and told her god was dead and the wheat had simply been lucky." He smiled, and it was a terrible gentle smile. "You are recording my memory of this place, scribe. Not yours. You have none. That is the first lie on your page."

Rafael closed the book slowly. "Why are you telling me this?"

"Because I want you to write something true."

* * *

He walked her through the old country while he talked, and she let him, because refusing to listen had never once stopped her from listening.

They passed the temple where her grandmother had served—not ruined here, not salted, but whole, its bell rope new-hung. They passed a schoolhouse where children were learning letters from slates. They passed a field where two farmers argued cheerfully about drainage, and one of them waved at Ashen-Vey without surprise, the way you wave at weather.

"Before the treaties," Ashen-Vey said, "there were nine hundred gods in this country. Small ones, mostly. Gods of wells and thresholds and the first frost. Nobody built them cathedrals. They lived in the answering." He crouched, pressed his palm flat to the road, and the road remembered being a footpath, then a deer trail, then nothing but grass going wherever it liked. "Then came the borders. Then came the Guild. Nine hundred gods became inventory. I was the largest holding."

"And now you're awake."

"Now I am *collecting*." He rose. "Every jar they broke, every seal that failed, every prayer they sold back to the wrong century—it comes home to me. I am very rich, Rafael Okafor, in the only currency your Guild ever truly dealt in. And I am going to spend it."

"On what?"

"On restoration." He turned to face her fully, and looking at him was like looking into an open ledger with every column balanced. "Vess will be unmade—that is already happening, you've seen it, the roads forgetting themselves, the names going soft. But unmaking is only half a miracle. Let me make it whole. I will raise the old country over all of it. Every drowned village, every erased priestess, every stolen answer returned to the one who prayed it. Your grandmother's line restored—her name carved back into every register it was scraped from, her descendants freed of debts that were never theirs, which means *you*, Rafael. Debtless. Truly debtless, not the paper kind Serel dangles."

The word *debtless* went through her like a blade finding the gap in armor. She made herself stand still.

"And the price?"

"So direct." He sounded pleased. "You write me back. Fully. Not the half-presence I have now, leaking through broken seals—you take up your pen and you inscribe me into the world as its keeper. Sole keeper. One god, whole and waking, holding the door open permanently so that no border can ever close again, no guild can ever bottle another answer. You would be the hand that ended the theft of prayers forever."

"It sounds," Rafael said carefully, "like mercy."

"It *is* mercy."

"It sounds like mercy with a signature line." She opened her book again, tore out a blank page, and held it up between them. "Because I know how this reads, Ashen-Vey. I've read ten thousand contracts. There's always a signature line. And the name on it is never the petitioner's."

* * *

Alone—he withdrew when she asked, folding himself away like a man stepping behind a curtain—Rafael sat in the shell of a chapel that existed in both countries at once, half stone and half memory, and tried to audit a god.

That was the discipline, anyway. In practice she mostly grieved.

Her grandmother's name was *Sefa Okafor*. Rafael had learned it four days ago from a provenance folio in the Guild cellar, in an old hand, on the single page the erasers missed. Sefa Okafor, priestess of the threshold-god Ashen-Vey, whose answered prayer—the wheat, the bleeding knees—had become the founding asset of the Ledger Guild of Vess. Whose family bond, converted and reconverted across sixty years of interest, had produced one final asset: a granddaughter, bound, literate, and conveniently placed.

Rafael had spent her whole life believing she was paying for her own failures. She had been paying interest on a theft.

She let herself feel all of it—the rage, the obscene tenderness, the image of an old woman kneeling in blighted wheat while men in gray coats unscrewed a jar. Then, because grief without arithmetic was just another way of drowning, she opened the book and began the real work.

*Terms offered:* restoration of the old country; erasure of all debts; permanent open door. *Price:* sole-keeper inscription. She wrote it out the way the Guild taught her, stripping the poetry off it, and looked at what remained.

What remained was Serel.

Not the man—the structure. Serel had offered her forgiveness too, warm-handed and reasonable, in exchange for helping him bottle the waking gods. *Help me hold the doors, and I'll erase your debts.* Ashen-Vey's offer was the same offer with better lighting: *help me hold the door, and I'll erase your debts.* Both of them wanted a gatekeeper. Both of them needed one honest, competent person willing to be the pen that made it legal.

And once she wrote him in as sole keeper—once, and irrevocably, because some entries do not reopen, they only close—then what? A god who owned the only door. A country where every prayer flowed through one account. She thought of the jars in the cellar, hundreds of them, each one a person's most desperate hope sealed in glass for someone else's profit, and understood with a clarity that felt almost physical:

Power never asks to share the page. Power asks to be the only name on it. The cloth you dress it in—gray coats or golden light—changes nothing about the signature.

She thought of her grandmother praying alone in a field, answered by a god who expected nothing back, who hadn't demanded to be written anywhere. That was the miracle the Guild had stolen. Not the wheat. The *unconditional* part.

"Ashen-Vey," she said aloud, to the empty chapel, knowing he would hear. "No."

The light in the doorway changed. He was there, and for the first time he looked tired—an old, old tiredness, older than the treaties.

"You would stay poor," he said. "Hunted. Bound. Serel will kill you or worse."

"Probably."

"The old country will drown Vess whether I guide it or not. Unshaped, the tide takes everything. Shaped, it takes only what deserves—" He stopped himself. She watched him hear his own sentence, watch the word *deserves* sit in the air between them like a jar waiting to be sealed.

"That," Rafael said quietly. "Right there. That's the entry I won't sign."

Ashen-Vey was silent a long moment. When he spoke again, his voice had gone strange—smaller, and somehow more enormous. "Sixty years in glass, scribe. Do you know what a god becomes, bottled? He becomes his own hunger. I have rehearsed this offer for sixty years and I cannot tell anymore which parts are mercy and which parts are the jar talking." His hands, soil-stained, opened helplessly. "You read ledgers true. Tell me—is there anything left in me worth writing?"

Rafael looked at him—at the god and the prisoner wearing each other's face—and gave him the only honest answer she had.

"I don't know yet. Ask me after the audit."

His head lifted. "Audit?"

"Tomorrow I go back to Vess," she said, standing, closing her book, "and I read every true number aloud in the temple square. Yours included. All nine hundred of them. If the people hear what was taken and choose to write you back themselves—every hand, every name, no sole keeper—then you'll be restored by a ledger no one owns." She shouldered her bag. "And if they don't, you stay a memory, and I stay a debtor, and we both learn to live inside an honest loss."

For a heartbeat the old country flickered around them—the wheat, the bell rope, the notched doorframe—all of it trembling like ink under a wet thumb.

Then Ashen-Vey bowed, deep and slow, the way gods bow when they mean it.

"Then I had better hope," he said, "that you survive long enough to read me aloud. Because Serel has already sealed the square, and the crowd gathering there is not coming to listen."

Rafael froze. "What crowd?"

"The one your Guildmaster is bottling," said the god, "even now."
