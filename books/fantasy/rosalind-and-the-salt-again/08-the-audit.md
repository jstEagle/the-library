## 8. The Audit

Rafael came back to Vess the way water comes back to a dry riverbed: quietly, and then all at once.

She had meant to stay in the old country. She had meant to let the tide take her, to stand in the sunlit fields of a Vess that no longer existed and simply stop being a debt with legs. But she had stood at the edge of Ashen-Vey's offer for three days, and on the third day she had understood something that rearranged her spine: every door out of Vess was a door someone else had built, and every builder wanted rent. Serel wanted her signature. The god wanted her pen. The old country wanted her silence.

There was one thing left that no one owned yet, because no one thought it was worth owning.

The truth, read aloud.

She walked out of the cellar vault at dawn, past the sleeping jars, up through the Guild's underhalls where the clerks were just lighting their lamps, and no one stopped her. That was the first miracle of the day—that they saw her ink-stained cuffs and her ledger-satchel and assumed she belonged, because in Vess only two kinds of people moved freely before sunrise: thieves and accountants, and she had spent eleven years being both.

By the time anyone thought to raise an alarm, she was standing on the steps of the temple square with the true ledgers open across her knees.

* * *

The temple square of Vess was built to hold ten thousand petitioners, though it rarely held more than a few hundred anymore; the queues had thinned as the Guild moved its business indoors, behind doors, behind fees. Rafael climbed to the base of the Weighing Statue—the bronze goddess of accounts whose scales had been gilded over by Serel's grandfather—and set the first ledger down on the statue's plinth like a woman setting down a stone she has carried across a desert.

Her hands shook. She let them.

"Good morning," she said, and her voice cracked on the second word, so she started again. "My name is Rafael Okafor, scribe third class, debt-bond number 4471. I am in violation of my bond by speaking without guild sanction. I am adding this speech to my own account." A breath. "I will be reading from the master ledgers of the Ledger Guild of Vess. Not the copies you sign. The originals."

A fishwife looked up from her baskets. Two lamplighters paused on their ladders. A child stopped chasing pigeons.

Rafael opened the ledger to the first page she had marked, and read.

"Entry one. Harvested prayer, petitioner Amara Okafor, priestess of the salt-shrine at Greywater, dated forty-one years ago. Answer recorded: rain after drought, three seasons' worth. Disposition of asset: transferred to private reserve, Guildmaster's discretion. Petitioner's name struck from all registers." She looked up. "Amara Okafor was my grandmother. Her answer is still in a jar in your cellar. It still hums. I have held it."

The square was very quiet. Somewhere a shutter banged.

"Entry two," she said, and turned the page. "Forgiven debt of Alderman Corvus, in exchange for his vote on the Border Tolls Act. Entry three. Forgiven debt of Magistrate Ilse, in exchange for her ruling in the matter of the drowned ferry—ruling that found no fault where there were nine dead." Page. "Entry four. Prayer harvested from a dying man who believed he was praying to a living god. He was praying to us. We answered, because answering is cheap when you own the bottling works."

She kept reading. That was the discipline of it, the scribe's discipline she had spent eleven years perfecting: do not editorialize, do not weep, do not stop. Read the line. Turn the page. Read the next line. The ledgers were four hundred pages long and she had chosen sixty entries, but sixty was enough, sixty was a flood, and by the twentieth entry the square was not quiet anymore.

Because the crowd had begun to answer back.

It started small—a woman near the fountain saying, *that was my brother's ferry,* in the flat voice of someone touching an old scar. Then a man shouting about a harvest that never came despite his family's tithes. Then an old soldier: *they took my pension, they said the fund failed, the fund didn't fail, look, it's right there, entry thirty-one—*

"Entry thirty-one," Rafael confirmed, and read it aloud, and the soldier sat down on the cobbles like a man whose legs had been unstrung.

This was the thing Serel had never understood, she thought, reading on while the crowd grew and grew—spilling in from the market rows, from the docks, faces appearing at upper windows, children hoisted onto shoulders. He thought the ledgers were power because they were secret. But a ledger is a list of grievances with good handwriting, and grievances do not expire. They compound. Every entry she read landed somewhere in the crowd like a key finding its lock: *that was mine. That was my mother's. That was the year we starved.* The Guild had spent forty years writing down everything it stole, in beautiful copperplate, with dates, and now Rafael was giving the dates back to the people who had lived them.

Words were the last lawful weapon left in Vess. The courts were bought; the temples were bought; the borders themselves were bought. But a crowd could still hear, and hearing, remember, and remembering, refuse.

She was halfway through the provenance records—the section she had saved, the way you save the worst wound for the surgeon—when the Guild's doors opened across the square.

* * *

They came out in formation, which told her everything: clerks first, then enforcers in grey, then Serel himself, walking unhurried, his hands folded, his face arranged into an expression of gentle sorrow that Rafael had once mistaken for kindness and now recognized as inventory-taking. He was counting the crowd. She watched him do it. Even now. Especially now.

"Sister Okafor," he called, and the square hushed, because his voice was trained for exactly this, warm as a hearth in a cold room. "You are ill. You've been away, among—unwholesome influences. Come inside. There is tea. Whatever you think you've found in those old books, we can discuss it properly, with counsel, with care."

"There is nothing to discuss," Rafael said, and did not lower the ledger. "There is only to read. Listen." And she read the next entry, louder: "Provenance record, founding miracle of the Ledger Guild of Vess. Source: Amara Okafor, priestess, harvested without consent during the rite of the salt-shrine. Authorization signature—" she lifted the page toward the crowd, toward the light, so the great looping letters caught the morning sun— "*Serel Vane, acting guildmaster.*"

The name went through the square like a crack through ice.

Serel's expression did not change, which was itself an answer. "Forged," he said mildly. "She forged my hand. Grief does strange things to the bonded. You all know how debts press on a person—"

"Do we ever," said the fishwife, and the laugh that went around the square was ugly and wonderful.

"The signature matches forty years of documents," Rafael said. "I copied them for six years. I know his hand better than my own. Check it. Check all of it. That is what an audit *is*—you don't believe me, you verify. Bring your own eyes. Bring your own grievances and find them in these pages, because they are here, every one, indexed and dated and signed." She turned another page. "Entry fifty-two. The debt-bond system itself. Do you want to know why your grandfather's loan never closed? Here is the clause. Interest accrues on interest. It was designed never to close. It was designed to be inherited. Some of you are here today paying on bonds your grandparents signed, and the original principal was—" she checked, though she knew it by heart, had wept over it in the old country by jarlight— "already paid. Twice over. In most cases."

A sound rose from the crowd then that Rafael had never heard in Vess before—not a riot-sound, not yet, something older and stranger. It was the sound of ten thousand private arithmetic problems finishing at once. Every person in that square had carried a number for years, a balance that never shrank, a shame that must somehow be their own fault, and now the numbers were being read back to them with the fraud laid bare beside each one, and the sound they made was the sound of shame turning inside out into fury.

"Enough," Serel said, and for the first time the warmth dropped out of his voice, and what was underneath was the voice he used on the jars in the cellar, patient and proprietary. "Seal the square."

The enforcers moved. And the crowd—Rafael would remember this until she died—did not run. The fishwife stepped forward. The old soldier stood up off the cobbles. Lamplighters came down their ladders and stood with their brass poles planted like pikes. They made a ring around the statue, around Rafael, around the open books, with nothing in their hands but market-day tools and forty years of receipts they suddenly understood.

"You can't arrest all of us," the fishwife said. "We're not in any of your books."

"Oh, you are," Rafael said quietly, still reading, turning pages, because the audit did not stop for swords—that was the whole point of an audit, it continued until it was finished or the reader was. "Entry sixty. Final entry. Dated yesterday." She raised her voice over the grinding of the Guild's great doors beginning to swing shut across the square, over the first shouts, over Serel calling her name in a voice that had finally, finally lost its patience. "Disposition of remaining assets: all bottled answers, all harvested prayers, all outstanding debts. Authorized by: pending. Witness required."

She looked up from the page and found Serel's eyes across the sea of heads, and held them.

"I need a witness," she called to the crowd, "with clean hands and a clear grievance. Who will sign?"

And from the back of the square, pushing through the ring of lamplighters and fishermen, came a tall figure in a coat the color of woodsmoke, whom half the crowd instinctively bowed to and none of them could say why—Ashen-Vey, risen god of the salt country, wearing the body of an ordinary man and holding out one hand for the pen.

"Me," he said. "I've been waiting forty years to witness something honestly."

Behind him, the Guild's doors slammed shut, and every bell in Vess began to ring at once—not the hour, Rafael realized, gripping the ledger, not any hour. Serel was ringing the alarm that sealed the city, the one that locked the gates and woke the enforcers and started the fires in the archive, where the copies were kept.

He was going to burn the evidence and bottle the crowd, and he was starting with the bells.

"Audit continues," Rafael said, to no one, to everyone, and turned the page.
