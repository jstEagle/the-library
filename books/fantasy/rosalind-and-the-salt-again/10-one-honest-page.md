## 10. One Honest Page

The school had been a countinghouse once. Rafael liked that about it—the way the old brass slots in the counter still gaped like mouths waiting for coins, and how she'd filled them instead with chalk stubs, ink pots, a jar of honey sticks for the children who cried on their first day. The building remembered what it was. She let it remember, the way she let Vess remember, and did not try to make either of them into something they had never been.

Morning came through windows that no longer matched their frames, because glass was scarce and neighbors were generous and nobody kept accounts of who owed whom a pane. Outside, the square where she had read the ledgers aloud now hosted a vegetable market on thirds-days and a marriage on most others. The temple still stood at its head—no one had pulled it down—but its doors stood open all day and all night, and people wandered in to argue, to sleep, to leave flowers for gods whose names they were still relearning. There was no tithe. There was no tax. There was, Rafael had discovered, an astonishing amount of arguing about drainage, which she considered proof that freedom was working.

She swept the floor before the children came. This was ritual now, though she would not have used that word. The ash from the master ledger had never fully left the world; it settled in fine grey film on sills and lintels, on the rims of cups, in the creases of her knuckles. Some said it was the ledger's ghost. Some said it was the borders, ground so fine they could finally go everywhere. Rafael only knew that when she breathed it in, she felt nothing but the ordinary ache of dust, and that this too seemed like a kind of answer.

* * *

They arrived the way children always arrive: loudly, in clumps, dragging smaller siblings by the wrist.

"Write my name first," demanded a girl named Tavi, who had lost two teeth that week and spoke with a whistle. "Before anyone. You promised."

"I promised you could go first *today*," Rafael said. "Yesterday's first doesn't carry over. That's the whole point of days."

Tavi considered this with the gravity of a guild auditor and found it acceptable.

Rafael set out the slates. Real paper was rationed—not by law, there was no law, but by common sense—and so the children learned on slate and chalk until their letters stopped looking like birds that had landed badly. Only when a child could write their name clean, without lifting the chalk in fear, did Rafael give them paper. One page. Their name on it, and anything else they wanted the world to keep.

It had taken her three years to understand why this mattered more than teaching them sums or scripture or the histories. A name written by your own hand was the one entry no one else could open, amend, or collect against. She had spent her life inside other people's books. These children would spend theirs holding pens that answered to no one.

"Teacher," said a small boy called Fen, tugging her sleeve. He was new, from the marsh country east of the old salt line, where the roads had gone back to water and stayed there. "My mam says you burned up all the debts. Is that true? Did you burn up mine?"

"You didn't have any," Rafael said gently.

"Everyone had some. Mam says even babies owed for being born."

"Not anymore." She crouched to his height. "You were born owing nothing. That's the truth your mam is still learning to believe. It takes longer to unlearn a debt than to burn one."

Fen frowned, unconvinced, and went to his slate. But at midday, when the others ran out to the square, he stayed behind and wrote his name over and over until the slate was white with it, like he was making sure the fact would hold.

Rafael watched him and thought of a cellar full of humming jars, and of a god who had offered her everything if she would only make him the sole signature on the world. Ashen-Vey had chosen, in the last burning moment, to help rather than rule—because she had written him one honest page, and honest pages, it turned out, were contagious. Where he had gone afterward, no one knew. Into the tide with the rest of them, back into the country that existed now as memory and choice. Sometimes, on evenings when the light came low and gold across the marshes, Rafael thought she saw a shape standing in the shallows, patient as a heron. She never waved. She never needed to. Some accounts, once closed honestly, stay closed.

* * *

In the afternoon she taught the older ones provenance—which was what she called history now, because *history* sounded like something done to you, and *provenance* sounded like something you could check.

"Every story has a hand that wrote it," she told them. "Ask who held the pen. Ask what they left out. Ask what they were paid." She tapped the slate. "I once copied a ledger that said a dead god's favor was worth four hundred crowns. The god was not dead. The favor was stolen. And the person who paid for all of it—" she paused, as she always paused here, "—was a priestess whose name had been erased from every register but one."

"What was her name?" Tavi asked. She asked every time. It was her favorite part, Rafael suspected, because it was the part where the erased thing came back.

"Sefeya Okafor," Rafael said. "My grandmother."

The children wrote it down. They always wrote it down. Somewhere in Vess there were now perhaps sixty slates and forty pages bearing Sefeya Okafor's name in childish hands, and Rafael thought that this was a better monument than any register—a name restored not by decree but by repetition, passed along like bread, owned by everyone who carried it.

That was the inheritance. Not power kept. Power given away, page by page, until it belonged to whoever needed it.

* * *

Evening. The children gone home to suppers that no tithe had thinned. Rafael sat at the old countinghouse counter with a plain new notebook before her—the last of a batch bought from a papermaker in the hill towns who traded for lessons rather than coin. Its pages were unruled, unnumbered, unblessed. No guild stamp. No watermark of ownership. Just paper, waiting.

She had been saving the final page for three years without quite admitting it.

Now she turned to it. The paper was rough and warm under her palm, and outside the window the marsh light was doing its gold trick, and somewhere far off a bell rang the hour in a tower that no longer collected anything from anyone.

She dipped her pen.

First, the grandmother. *Sefeya Okafor,* in a careful hand, the letters given room to breathe. The priestess whose prayer had founded a theft. Whose divinity had been decanted into jars and sold by the crown-weight. Whose granddaughter had spent twenty years paying interest on the ruin of her own bloodline without knowing it. Rafael wrote the name and felt the old grief move through her—not the sharp grief of the audit, not the burning grief of the ledger fire, but a settled grief, the kind that has finished its work and stays on as company.

Then, beneath it, her own.

*Rafael Okafor.*

No debt-bond number after it. No guild seal. No entry code, no interest schedule, no line reading *balance outstanding.* Just the name, whole, on a page that belonged to no institution, that would be inherited by no power, that asked nothing of the world except to be true.

She looked at the two names together for a long time. Grandmother and granddaughter, stacked like generations should be, with nothing between them but blank space—and the blank space, she realized, was the point. Room. That was what she had bought with everything: room for what came next.

Outside, the first stars were coming up over the salt marshes, and down in the square someone was singing, badly and joyfully, a song about the burning of the books. The words were wrong. The tune was catching. In a year it would be unrecognizable, and that was right too.

Rafael capped her pen, closed the notebook, and set it on the shelf beside the others—each one ending the same way, each one a door that opened onto nothing but tomorrow.

Then she blew out the lamp, and in the dark the school sat quiet, keeping its accounts of nothing, owing nothing, waiting for morning and the sound of small feet, and names not yet written, arriving.
