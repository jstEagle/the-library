## 1. The Weight of Borrowed Years

The merchant's house smelled of camphor and oranges.

Yara noticed the oranges first—six of them in a blue bowl on the sideboard, their skins gone soft at the stem, perfuming the whole sickroom with a sweetness that had nothing to do with the man dying in the bed. Everything else in the room was about endings: the darkened windows, the basin of reddened water, the physician dozing upright in a chair with his coat over him like a shroud he'd tried on early.

But the oranges were about living. Someone had bought them recently. Someone still hoped.

"Miss Galloway." The physician startled awake and stood too fast, knocking his knee against the chair arm. "You came. Good. Good." He said it twice, as though once might not survive the crossing between them. "He asked for you specifically. Well—he asked for *the guild*. But I thought—" He wrung his hands, a man who had spent thirty years learning what bodies could do and still had no instrument for this particular failing. "I thought you should know he wants the good years. Not the whole life. Just the good ones."

"They're all his," Yara said. "I only take what he gives."

She set her case down on the floor beside the bed and opened it with the small ceremony she always performed, though no one ever watched closely enough to appreciate it. Inside, nested in wool like eggs, sat her vials: empty ones for carrying away, and one stoppered vial of clear syrup that was nothing but sugar and saltwater and a drop of tincture of willow, which did nothing at all except make the mouth go pleasantly numb. The placebo was her own invention. Clients liked to feel something happen. It made the leaving easier.

Tobias Merren lay propped against four pillows, a big man gone small inside his skin, the kind of dying that took months to finish its sentence. His eyes found her and brightened with an effort that cost him visibly.

"You're younger than I pictured," he rasped.

"I'm exactly as old as I look."

"That's a strange thing to say." He tried to laugh; it turned into a cough that went on long enough for the physician to step forward, then back when Merren waved him off. "My daughter said you'd come. Said the guild does this. Takes the—the heavy years off a man before the end so he can go light." His hand crept across the blanket toward her, stopped halfway, as if the distance between them were a country he could no longer afford to cross. "But I don't want the heavy ones taken. I want to keep those. They're mine. The bad years made me too."

Yara waited. She had learned that clients always arrived at it themselves if you let the silence do its work. Silence was a tool like any other in her case, and she kept it oiled.

"It's my wife," he said finally. "Maren. Dead eleven years now, gods keep her. And our girl, Liss, drowned at nine. Fever, not water—everyone assumes water because of the name, isn't that something? Liss. We named her after the river because Maren loved it so." His voice had gone somewhere far away, and Yara recognized the terrain: he was already walking through the decade without her help. "Those ten years. From when we married until Liss died. That's the best of me. Every day of it. And I'm going to die in three weeks, maybe four, and I can't bear—" His jaw worked. "I can't bear to be in here alone with them. With all that joy and nobody left who was there for it. You understand? Joy needs witnesses. That's the whole trick of it. That's what nobody tells you."

"I understand," Yara said, and it was even true, in the way a map understands a coastline.

"So take them. All ten years. Give them to someone who'll feel them fresh. And give me—" he gestured vaguely at the case, at the vials, at the whole gray apparatus of the guild, "—give me quiet. A head with nothing shouting in it. I'll pay whatever the rate is. I'll pay double."

"The rate is the rate," Yara said. "The guild doesn't haggle over grief."

That made him laugh properly, a big broken-bellows sound. "No, I don't suppose it does. You people are the only honest merchants left in this city."

*If only you knew*, Yara thought, and knelt beside the bed, and began.

* * *

The taking was simple, which was the first lie about it. Everyone imagined it must be dramatic—a struggle, a scream, some visible rending. In truth it required nothing more than touch and attention and a trained stillness that Yara had been practicing since before she could remember practicing anything.

She laid two fingers against Merren's temple, just below the hairline where the skull went thin, and closed her eyes, and reached.

His mind opened to her the way a house opens when the owner falls asleep in a chair by the door: not locked, just unattended. She moved through it with the courtesy of a thief who has done this ten thousand times. Past the front rooms of the present—the camphor smell, the physician's snoring, the ache in his bones that had become so constant it registered as furniture. Down the corridor of the near past. Deeper, where the air changed, where the memories grew warmer the way cellars grow colder, until she found it: the decade, whole and gleaming, a stretch of riverbank in the landscape of him.

She had taken thousands of memories. She knew all the shapes they came in. Grief was wet and heavy and clung. Fear was sharp-edged and bit the tongue of anyone who handled it carelessly. Shame was slippery, always trying to hide itself behind other things. But joy—joy was the strangest cargo of all. Joy was *light*. Not weightless, exactly, but buoyant, the way driftwood is heavy out of water and nothing in your hands once it floats. Carrying a decade of happiness felt less like lifting a stone than like holding a bird that trusted you.

This one was a good one. She felt its quality immediately, the way a jeweler feels a true stone. A wedding under a rain-slicked awning with everyone laughing because the tent had leaked. A baby's fist closing around a thumb with shocking strength. Liss learning to walk along the riverbank, arms out, shrieking with triumph at every step. Maren singing off-key in the kitchen with flour on her cheekbone. A hundred ordinary evenings that had seemed unremarkable while they happened and were now, from the far side of loss, incandescent.

Yara gathered it the way she'd been taught: gently, completely, leaving no ragged edges. A memory torn out leaves a wound; a memory lifted whole leaves only smoothness, a polished absence the mind slides over without catching. She coaxed the decade loose from its moorings—one anchor at a time, the wedding last, because anchors remember best—and felt it come free and rise through the channels of him into her.

It passed through her chest on its way to the vial.

That was the part no one warned you about, the part the guild never put in its manuals. Extraction wasn't a bucket on a rope. The memory had to travel *through* the extractor, brief as a swallow's shadow, and for that half-second Yara felt all of it at once: the wedding, the river, the small hot weight of a sleeping child against a shoulder, flour-dusted laughter, ten years of being loved and loving in return, compressed into a single instant of unbearable brightness.

Then it was in the vial, sealed in salt-glass, glowing faintly amber like a lamp seen through fog, and Yara was herself again, kneeling on a stranger's floor, feeling precisely nothing.

That was the trade. That was always the trade. For half a second she got to know what a whole life felt like, and then it was gone, and the nothing rushed back in behind it like water finding its level. Some extractors couldn't bear it. They became addicts of other people's weddings, other people's children, stealing and re-stealing, tasting and tasting, until the guild retired them to the coast houses where they sat looking at the sea with faces like unsent letters.

Yara had never been tempted. She told herself it was discipline. She was careful not to examine the alternative explanation too closely—that the nothing in her ran very deep, deeper than training, deeper than choice, a well that had been dug in her long ago by hands she didn't remember—and that one taste of borrowed joy was a cup dipped in an ocean compared to the drought underneath. If she started, she would never stop. So she didn't start. That was all. That was the whole architecture of her virtue: a dam built on top of a hole.

"There," she said aloud, and her voice came out level, professional, faintly kind. She was good at that voice. She'd had practice. "It's done."

Merren blinked up at her. Something had gone out of his face—not pain, not peace, but a certain crowdedness, as though a room full of furniture had been cleared overnight and he kept turning to look at the clean floors. He touched his own temple where her fingers had been.

"I feel—" He frowned, searching. "Lighter. Is that terrible? I feel lighter and I can't remember why I wanted to be heavy."

"That's normal. It settles in a day or two." She took the placebo vial from the case. "Drink this. Slowly. It helps the edges knit."

He drank. His face relaxed around the numbness, grateful for any sensation that could be named. "And the years? Where do they go? Do you—you know—" He couldn't say it. *Do you sell them? Does someone wear my wedding?*

"They go to the archive," Yara said. "They're kept safe. Preserved." All technically true. The guild was scrupulous about technical truth; it was the only scripture they had. What happened after preservation—the ledgers, the buyers, the prices paid in coin and favors by people who wanted to feel brave or bereaved or young for an evening—was a country Merren would never visit, and Yara delivered him to its border with a smile she had practiced in mirrors until it looked born rather than built.

"Good," he murmured. "Good. Keep them safe. They were—" He stopped. Started again, slower, like a man walking on ice. "They were good years. Weren't they? I can't quite—I know they were. I wrote it down somewhere. I remember writing it down."

"You wrote it down," Yara agreed, and hated how easily the lie came, how little it cost, how the nothing swallowed even the guilt before it could finish forming. She counted out the coins into his palm—guild policy, payment on delivery, the ledger balanced both ways—and his fingers closed around the metal like a child's around a charm.

"Will you come back?" he asked. "Before the end?"

"If you send for me."

"No. I mean—" His eyes were wet and clear and terribly honest. "Will *you* come back? You. The girl with the case. It gets quiet in here, and I think I'd like a witness. Even for the plain days. Even for nothing much."

Yara stood, and closed her case, and heard herself say, "Of course," in that same level, professional, faintly kind voice, knowing she would not come back, knowing she had said *of course* to perhaps two hundred dying strangers across nine years and returned to none of them, because returning meant becoming a person in someone's story, and Yara Galloway had worked very hard to remain a marginal note.

Outside, the evening had come on violet and cold. The oranges in the bowl glowed in the sickroom window like small captive suns. She did not look back.

* * *

The guildhall stood on Cindermason Street like a tooth that had grown too large for the jaw: black stone, narrow windows, a door of salt-iron that no lockpick in the city could argue with. To the street it presented the face of a countinghouse, which is what the Ledger Guild called itself in its public moods—appraisers, notaries, keepers of accounts. Half true. The guild did keep accounts. It simply declined to specify what currency.

Yara showed her seal to the door-warden, a hollow-eyed man named Bracke who had sold the guild his own childhood at nineteen to pay a debt and now remembered nothing of why he'd needed the money, only that the owing had felt enormous and the price had felt fair. He nodded her through with the flat courtesy of one blank page acknowledging another.

Inside, the halls went deep. Everyone knew the guildhall had cellars; almost no one knew the cellars had floors beneath them, and floors beneath those, descending in the dark like the roots of a tree planted upside down. The Archive occupied most of them. Yara had walked its length perhaps four hundred times and had never once seen the end of it, and suspected, in her more tired hours, that it did not have one—that the Archive grew the way a coral grows, fed on everything the city forgot on purpose.

The extraction wing's filing room was a cavern of shelves rising out of lamplight, row upon row upon row, each shelf ranked with vials in wooden cradles, each vial a small imprisoned weather. Ten thousand memories. Fifty thousand. No one counted anymore; the count had stopped meaning anything around the time Yara joined. Amber ones held joy, mostly. Blue-gray held grief. The fear-vials had a shimmer to them, a restless glitter, like ice over moving water, and junior clerks were forbidden to shelve those alone.

Yara signed the intake book—*Merren, Tobias, dec., 1 item, decade-term, quality: high*—and carried the vial to its place.

Quality: high. She'd written it without thinking, and then thought about it anyway. Somewhere in this city tonight, a buyer would pay a month's wages to sit in a guild parlor and wear Tobias Merren's wedding, his daughter's first steps, his wife's off-key singing. They'd feel it fresh, exactly as he'd wished. They'd leave weeping and grateful and lighter themselves, having rented a soul the way one rents a room. And Merren would die in three weeks in a room full of oranges, unable to remember why he'd wanted to be heavy, waiting for a witness who was already filing him away.

Yara slid the vial into its cradle. It settled among its neighbors with a small glass click, indistinguishable within a minute, one amber grain in an ocean of amber grain. She had filed her ten-thousandth memory two years ago—or so a clerk had estimated—and she had felt nothing then either, and had noted the nothing with a kind of clinical interest, the way one notes a symptom that hasn't yet decided to become a disease.

She stood a moment in the lamplight, surrounded by the shelved lives of the city, and listened to the Archive breathe. It did breathe. Old extractors swore to it: a vast slow tidal sound, vials warming and cooling, memories settling against their glass like sleeping animals. On bad nights Yara thought she could hear individual voices in it, singing, arguing, calling names.

Tonight, beneath the tide, she thought she heard something else. Fainter. Lower. Almost like a bell rung underwater, once, from very far down—deeper than any shelf she'd ever walked.

She stood very still.

"Lady Galloway." A clerk at the end of the row, breathless, holding a lantern too high. "Guildmaster Corvane asks for you. Tonight. He says—" The boy swallowed. "He says it's about the Prophecy Vault. He says you're the only one he trusts with it."

Down in the dark, the bell-note faded, or seemed to.

Yara closed the intake book and followed the lantern toward the stairs, and did not look back at the shelves, and told herself the small cold feeling at the base of her skull was only the cellar draft.
