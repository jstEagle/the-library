## 2. Ledgers of Impossible Things

The summons came at the hour when the guildhall smelled most of tallow and old paper, when the junior clerks had gone home to their suppers and only the lamplighters moved through the stacks like patient ghosts.

Yara was still at her desk in the Receiving Room, cataloguing the merchant's decade—*one joy, ten years' measure, gently taken, donor compensated*—when Corvane's page appeared at her elbow, silent as spilled ink.

"Guildmaster wants you," the boy said. "Third landing. Now."

She capped the vial first. That was the discipline they had ground into her from the beginning: never leave a memory uncapped, never leave a thought unfinished, never let anything escape its proper container. The vial went into its felt-lined slot in the transfer case, the case into the iron cabinet, the key back around her neck where it lived against her sternum like a second, colder heart.

Then she climbed.

Corvane's office occupied the third landing of the guildhall's central tower, a room she had entered perhaps forty times in eleven years and never once without the sensation of walking into a mouth. It was not an unkind room. That was the trouble with it. There were cushions on the chairs, and a fire that was always lit but never roaring, and a decanter of plum wine he offered to everyone and which no one, as far as she knew, had ever accepted. Kindness, in the guild, was architecture. You learned to read the walls before you trusted the warmth.

"Yara." He did not look up from his writing at first. He never did. He liked you to stand there a moment, feeling the weight of being waited for. "Sit."

She sat. The cushion exhaled dust and lavender.

"You filed the Galloway transaction?"

"The Vessin merchant. Yes. Clean extraction. He'll sleep tonight thinking he gave the money away out of generosity." She kept her voice level, professional. "He wept a little during. They usually do when it's happiness. The body remembers even when the mind agrees to sell."

"Mm." Corvane set down his pen. He was a long man, dry as a winter reed, with white hair cropped close to a skull that seemed built for thinking unpleasant thoughts efficiently. His eyes were the pale grey of ledger paper, and they had the same quality: everything written on them could be erased. "You've been with us eleven years now."

"Eleven years in the spring."

"And in all that time, you've never once been given vault duty. Not the deep vaults. Not the restricted shelves." He folded his hands. "Do you know why?"

Because I'm good at taking and terrible at keeping, she almost said. Because I file other people's pasts all day and have never once asked for my own back. Because you like me exactly where I am: useful and hollow.

"I assumed my aptitude lay elsewhere," she said instead.

"Your aptitude," Corvane said, "lies everywhere. Which is precisely the problem." He rose, crossed to the window, and looked out over the rooftops of Vessin, where the evening lamps were coming up one by one like a slow infection of gold. "There has been a complaint. An audit finding. Nothing you've done—it concerns records older than your employment. But the Prophecy Vault must be inventoried, every folio counted, every seal verified, and it must be done by someone with no stake in what the inventory finds." He turned. "It must be done by someone who feels nothing about what she reads."

The words landed in her chest and sat there, cold and small, like a coin dropped down a well.

"You want me because I'm empty," she said.

"I want you because you're *thorough*," Corvane said mildly. "The emptiness is incidental. Though I won't pretend it isn't useful. Sentimental auditors find sentimental errors." He came back to the desk and drew a ring of keys from the drawer—not the guild's common keys, dull brass things, but a single black iron key on a chain, old enough that its teeth had been worn soft by centuries of turning. "Three days. Count everything. Touch nothing with bare hands. And Yara—" he held the key out across the desk between them, "—if you find something that frightens you, you bring it to me. Not to your friends. Not to your diary. To me. Can I trust you with that?"

Every gift is a leash. The thought arrived unbidden, from nowhere, in no voice she recognized, and was gone before she could examine it.

"Of course, Guildmaster," she said, and took the key.

Its weight surprised her. Iron should be heavy; this felt heavier than iron, as if weight were a thing it remembered having more of.

* * *

The Prophecy Vault sat beneath the guildhall's oldest wing, below the water line, below the wine cellars, below even the sealed archive where the guild kept the memories it had decided no one should ever taste again. You reached it by a stair that spiraled down through stone so old the masons' marks had eroded into suggestions, and the air changed as you descended—the way air changes near the sea, or near grief. Salt and dust and something underneath both, faintly sweet, like flowers rotting in a locked room.

The door was black oak banded in lead, and it had not been opened, according to the log beside it, in nine years.

Yara wrote her name in the log—*Yara Galloway, auditor, by order of Corvane*—and noticed, as she always noticed, how strange her own signature looked. Neat, correct, lifeless. A hand that had been taught to write before it had anything to say.

The key turned with a sound like a bone setting.

Lamplight found a long room, low-ceilinged, lined floor to ceiling with pigeonhole shelves, and in each pigeonhole a document case of oilcloth and wax. Thousands of them. Tens of thousands. Prophecies were the guild's oldest trade, older than memory-theft itself: before people learned to sell their pasts, they had sold their futures, and the guild had bought greedily and indiscriminately for four hundred years. Every crackpot vision, every fever-dream scrawled on bark, every genuine article of impossible foresight—all of it came here, to be catalogued, cross-referenced, and forgotten.

Her instructions were simple. Count. Verify seals. Log discrepancies.

She began at the north wall and worked methodically, and for two hours the work was exactly as deadening as she'd expected. A prophecy that the river would run backward (it hadn't). A prophecy of a comet shaped like a fish (unverified). A prophecy concerning the love life of a baker's daughter in Marrowford, purchased from the girl herself at fourteen and rendered spectacularly wrong by her marriage at fifteen. The guild had paid good coin for all of it. Hope, Yara reflected, stamping another seal *verified*, was the one commodity people would always oversell.

It was near midnight, on the third shelf of the west wall, in a case whose wax seal bore a date two years before her birth, that she found the folio with no title.

Not an untitled folio—a deliberately unmarked one. No catalogue number. No provenance entry. Just a strip of vellum pasted inside the oilcloth bearing a single word in Corvane's handwriting, which she knew as well as her own:

*HOLD.*

She should have logged it as a discrepancy and moved on. That was the procedure. That was what a thorough auditor with no stake in what she read would do.

She broke the wax.

Inside was a single sheet of prophecy-paper, brittle as autumn leaf, covered in a cramped hand that shook toward the ends of lines—the writing of someone old, or ill, or frightened. Above the text, in faded ink, someone had sketched a woman's portrait: head and shoulders, quick strokes, the kind of drawing done from life and done fast, before the subject could change her mind.

Yara read the prophecy three times. The language was archaic, formal, the dialect of the coastal temples a century gone. But the guild trained its thieves in old tongues—you couldn't steal a memory safely if you couldn't understand what you were stealing—and translation came to her the way salt comes to seawater.

*When the thirteenth turn completes its circle, and the moon stands full above the drowned chapel, the Salt shall wake in its vessel.*

*The vessel will be a woman, and she will not know what she carries.*

*She will be known by the broken collarbone, healed crooked, on the left side.*

*On that night the heirs of both armies come to her, and the god's memory chooses, and the world begins again—or ends.*

Below it, in a different hand, newer, sharper, someone had added a date.

Tonight's date.

Yara stood very still in the cold of the vault, and the lamp flame leaned in a draft that had no source, and she made herself do the thing she was trained to do: observe, catalogue, feel nothing.

She observed that her left collarbone had been broken once. She knew this the way she knew all facts about herself—from documents, from others' reports, from the medical file the guild kept on every thief. *Fracture, clavicle, left; healed with deformity; pre-employment injury, origin unknown.* Origin unknown. Eleven years she had carried that phrase without once wondering what lay behind it, because wondering was a door and she had been taught that doors were expensive.

She observed that the sketch—the quick, confident strokes of a woman's face, sharp chin, dark hair pulled back, eyes that the artist had bothered to make tired—was, allowing for the difference in age and the crudeness of the medium, her own face.

She observed that her hands were shaking, and catalogued this too, and felt nothing, and the nothing was the loudest thing she had ever heard.

* * *

She did not remember climbing the stairs. She was aware, in the disconnected way of someone moving through a dream of work, of the log book again—her pen hovering over the entry column—and of writing, in her neat correct lifeless hand: *West wall, third shelf, case 4471: folio present, seal intact.* A lie. Her first lie to the guild in eleven years, and it flowed from her pen as smoothly as truth, which told her something about herself she was not ready to examine.

The folio was inside her coat, against her ribs, over the crooked collarbone.

Above ground, the guildhall slept. Lamplighters had snuffed the corridor flames down to embers behind glass. Somewhere a clerk coughed in his bunk. Rain had begun against the high windows, soft as fingers testing a lock.

Yara walked to her cell—the guild called them cells, a joke no one laughed at anymore—and barred the door, and lit her own lamp, and took out the prophecy paper with hands that would not quite obey her.

*The vessel will be a woman, and she will not know what she carries.*

She pressed her palm flat against her chest, over the old badly-healed bone, as if she might feel something there—heat, hum, the stirring of a century-deep sleep. There was nothing. There was only Yara Galloway, twenty-six years old, owner of a name, a trade, a scar of unknown origin, and a childhood sold before she was old enough to hold it.

But.

But there had been that voice, in Corvane's office. *Every gift is a leash.* Words in no voice she owned, arriving from nowhere, meaning nothing and refusing to mean nothing.

And there was the fact—which she had never called a fact until tonight—that Yara Galloway could walk into a stranger's mind and lift a decade out of it without leaving a mark, a talent so far beyond guild training that her instructors had gone quiet when they watched her work. She had always assumed it was aptitude. People assumed things about themselves all the time. It was cheaper than knowing.

*She will not know what she carries.*

Outside, the rain thickened. Down in the vault beneath her feet, ten thousand false futures moldered in the dark, and somewhere among them, she now understood, the guild had kept one true thing, marked HOLD, dated for tonight, drawn with her own face.

Someone had known. Someone had put her in front of it—Corvane, with his rehearsed kindness and his worn-black key, sending the empty girl to count prophecies because she wouldn't feel what she read. A punishment disguised as trust. Or a test disguised as a punishment. Or—

Or a trap disguised as both, sprung years ago, on a night she couldn't remember, in a city she couldn't name, by people she had never met.

Yara folded the prophecy paper small and hid it where she hid everything: in the hollow of herself, in the blank pages where a life should have been.

Tomorrow, she told herself, she would go back to the vault and finish the audit and log every discrepancy honestly and give Corvane the folio, as instructed, as promised.

Tomorrow.

But tonight she sat awake until the rain stopped, one hand curled over her crooked collarbone, listening to the sound of the sea that wasn't there—and was.
