## 3. Thirteenth Turn

The moon charts lived in the tower room at the top of the guildhall, a round chamber with a domed ceiling painted to look like the night sky as it had been on the night the guild was founded—every star in its proper place, every constellation named in gold leaf that had dulled to the color of old honey. Yara had been up here perhaps six times in her nine years of service, and each time she'd felt like an insect pinned inside a clockwork orrery, some small mechanism turning beneath wheels too vast to see.

Tonight she climbed the spiral stair with the prophecy page folded against her ribs, and the feeling was worse than ever, because now she suspected the wheels were turning *around* her specifically.

She lit the lamps one by one. The charts unrolled across the great table in overlapping sheets of vellum gone soft as cloth with age, inked by hands long dead: lunar tables, tidal tables, the slow precessions of the thirteenth moon—the hidden one, the one that only completed its circuit once a century, the one the common almanacs pretended did not exist because its existence made nonsense of every calendar the city relied upon.

Yara spread the prophecy page flat beside them and weighted its corners with brass astrolabes.

*When the thirteenth turn comes full,* she read again, *the Salt shall wake in the vessel prepared for it.*

Vessel. She turned her wrist over in the lamplight, looked at the blue tracery of veins, the small crooked scar at her collarbone where the bone had knotted wrong from some childhood break she could not remember sustaining. A vessel. A cup. Something made to be filled.

"Right," she said aloud, because the room was empty and saying it aloud made it smaller. "Let's find out when."

She began with the current year. The guild's master chart—the one Corvane consulted before signing anything of consequence, though he would deny believing in it—showed the thirteenth moon's position as a faint silver thread winding through the outer ring of the wheel. Yara traced it with a fingertip, counting backward through the years the way she counted backward through other people's memories: methodically, without sentiment, looking for the seam.

The thread spiraled inward. Each century it came closer to center. And at the center—

At the center was a date.

Not a day, exactly. The thirteenth turn did not complete on a single marked square of any calendar; it completed in the gap between two of them, in the twenty-six days when the hidden moon crossed the last arc of its circuit and slipped behind the sun's blind spot, invisible even to those who knew to look. The old cartographers had shaded this span in gray wash and written across it, in a hand so small she had to bend close:

*The Unnumbered Days.*

Twenty-six of them. And according to the count she ran three times, checking herself like a clerk who distrusted her own arithmetic, tonight was the fourth.

Twenty-two days remained.

Yara straightened up. Her back cracked. Somewhere below her, the guildhall went about its evening business—the clink of the coin-room scales, the muffled coughing of the archivists, the hiss of the gaslamps being lit along the Processional outside. All of it ordinary. All of it continuing as if the world were not, by her count, twenty-two days from whatever happened when a dead god's memory woke inside a woman who had never asked to carry it.

She thought about what waking might mean. The priestess's words from the drowned chapel still echoed—*able to remake whoever holds it*—and Yara turned the phrase over like a coin of uncertain mint. Remake how? Remake whom? If the Salt woke in her, would she simply stop being Yara Galloway and become something else wearing her skin? Or would it be slower than that, a tide rising in her blood, a voice getting louder, until one morning she answered to a different name?

Her reflection flickered in the dark window glass. Just the lamp behind her, she told herself. Just the flame moving.

*Twenty-two days.* Enough time to run. People ran from the guild all the time; they were called ex-thieves, and they were called corpses, but they ran. Yara knew the coastal roads, knew which ferrymen took no ledger and no questions, knew the mountain passes where the enforcers' grey cloaks stood out against the snow like bruises. She had spent nine years making herself competent at everything except belonging, and running was a skill like any other. You needed money—you could lift memories for coin anywhere, the trade flourished in every port. You needed papers—

Papers.

The thought arrived quietly, the way the worst thoughts always did, sliding in sideways while she was busy with something else. Her forged identity papers. The set she'd paid a forger in Lowmarsh three years' savings for: the merchant's daughter from Vell, the widow with property in the southern isles, the whole stitched-together life that existed nowhere except in documents good enough to survive inspection. They were kept, per guild regulation, in the strongroom under her registered seal. Every thief's escape kit was. It was the guild's way of pretending to trust you while holding your future hostage.

She should check them. There was no reason to check them; there was every reason not to, because if they were fine she'd have climbed four flights of stairs for nothing, and if they weren't—

If they weren't fine, then someone had anticipated her, and she wanted another hour of not knowing that.

She checked them anyway. Of course she checked them. That was the whole architecture of her: curiosity like a hook behind the sternum, hauling her toward every thing she feared.

* * *

The strongroom smelled of cold iron and lamp oil. The keeper—a heavy man named Brannoc who had been old when Yara arrived and seemed determined to outlast the building itself—looked up from his ledgers with the expression of a man interrupted during his favorite part of the evening, which was doing nothing.

"Galloway." He said her name the way he said everyone's name, as though reading it off an inventory. "Late for a withdrawal."

"Not a withdrawal. An inspection. My own file."

"Inspections need a countersignature."

"From Guildmaster Corvane?" She kept her voice bored, professional, the voice of a woman asking for a routine thing. "It's my own papers, Brannoc. Regulation Nine. I'm entitled to verify their condition quarterly."

He grunted, which was either agreement or indigestion—he produced the same sound for both—and hauled himself up to fetch the ledger of seals. Yara waited, hands loose at her sides, pulse ticking somewhere behind her teeth. Around her the strongroom shelves rose into shadow, thousands of drawers, thousands of sealed fates: wills, deeds, confessions, identities. The guild did not merely steal memories. It stole the paperwork of being a person, and rented it back.

Brannoc ran a thick finger down the page. Stopped.

"Huh," he said.

Yara's stomach dropped through four floors and into the cellar. "Huh?"

"Your file's flagged." He turned the ledger around so she could see, and there it was: her name, her seal number, and beside them a mark in red wax—a closed circle with a line through it, the guild's sign for *recalled*. Beneath it, a date. Four days ago.

"Recalled," she repeated. "By whose order?"

"Corvane's office." Brannoc shrugged, unbothered, a man describing weather. "Quiet recall. Means the papers come to me, get verified, get resealed. Standard when there's—" He paused, and she watched him decide how much to say to her, watched him arrive at the same conclusion any careful man would. "Standard procedure," he finished. "You'll have them back within the week."

"Within the week," Yara said. Twenty-two days until the thirteenth turn completed. A week to return her papers—if they were returned at all. "And where are they now?"

"Verification office. Second floor." He frowned at her, and something almost like kindness moved through the wrinkles of his face. "You look pale, girl. Sit down before you fall down."

"I'm fine." She was already walking. "Thank you, Brannoc."

"Mm." As she reached the door: "Galloway. The recall order—it wasn't just your papers."

She stopped. Did not turn around. Turning around would show him her face, and her face was currently doing something she did not want witnessed.

"There were eleven recalls that day," Brannoc said. "All thieves. All field agents. All yours and mine and the rest of us who work outside these walls." He tapped the ledger with one blunt nail. "Funny thing, a quiet recall on eleven sets of papers the same afternoon. Can't think what you'd all be planning to do that anyone would want to prevent."

"No," Yara said. "Neither can I."

* * *

The verification office on the second floor was locked, but locks had never been more than a polite suggestion to Yara, and the corridor beyond it was empty at this hour, the clerks gone home to their suppers. She let herself in with two picks and a hairpin and stood for a moment among the sleeping shelves, listening to the building breathe.

Eleven sets of papers. Recalled the same day. Not hers specifically—or not hers *only*—which meant this was not about the prophecy page she'd found, not about anything she had done. This was broader. Preemptive. Someone had looked at the whole roster of guild thieves and thought: *in twenty-six days, we may need these people unable to leave.*

Or: *we may need these people unable to run.*

She found her file in the third drawer where the G's lived, and it was worse than recalled. The folder was open on the desk—not misfiled, not shoved in hastily, but *open*, squared neatly with the desk's edge, as though whoever had last handled it expected it to be found this way. Inside, her forged identity lay where it always lay. But on top of it, weighted with a paper knife, was a single sheet of guild letterhead bearing one line in Corvane's narrow, elegant hand:

*Stay close, child. The coming weeks will require all of us.*

No signature. No seal. He didn't need one; nobody else in the guildhall wrote letters that sounded like a father's hand on a shoulder.

Yara stood very still. The gaslight hissed. Down in the street a cart rattled past, and somewhere a dog began barking at nothing, and the ordinary world went on insisting on itself while she understood, with the slow cold certainty of water finding a crack, that she had never once in her life been unobserved.

He had raised her. He had *found* her, he said, a child with no history, and given her a trade and a bed and a name, and she had spent nine years being grateful in the specific wordless way of the indebted. And all that time he had kept her papers in his strongroom like a bird's clipped feathers in a drawer, waiting for the season when flight would matter.

*The vessel prepared for it.* Prepared. The word sat differently now. Vessels were not found. Vessels were made—chosen, shaped, kept close, kept *safe*, kept unable to leave.

She thought of the prophecy vault assignment. A punishment disguised as trust, she'd called it, when Corvane handed her the key with that mild smile. But punishments disguised as trust were also trust disguised as bait. He had sent her into that vault the way you send a dog into a field where something is buried. To sniff. To dig. To learn what she was carrying before she learned it herself—or perhaps precisely so that she would learn it herself, because a vessel that understood its contents might behave differently than one kept ignorant, and Corvane had always preferred to work with informed instruments. Cleaner that way. Fewer broken vessels.

*Stay close, child.*

Yara put the note back exactly as she'd found it, squared the folder, closed the drawer, and relocked the office behind her. Her hands were steady. She noticed this with a kind of distant professional approval, the part of her that lifted dying men's happiest decades without flinching observing the rest of her the way a handler observes a useful animal.

Then she walked down four flights of stairs, out the side door, into the streets, and did not stop walking until the guildhall was three districts behind her and the harbor smell had replaced the smell of cold iron.

She had no papers. Eleven thieves had no papers. In twenty-two days the thirteenth turn would complete, and the Salt would wake in the vessel prepared for it, and the vessel was her, and the man who had prepared her had just asked her, gently, in writing, to stay close.

Yara stood at the harbor rail where the black water slapped the pilings, and she did the arithmetic of her life. Money: enough for a week, if she slept rough. Skills: theft, evasion, the lifting of memory—useless against whatever was coming, probably useless against Corvane, possibly useless against the thing inside her own ribs. Allies: none. Family: a fiction signed by the man hunting her.

And yet.

She turned her wrist over again in the light of the harbor lamps, that old habit, looking at the veins as if the answer might be written there in blue. Under her breastbone, faint as a tide pulling at sand, something shifted. Not pain. Not voice. Just attention—the sensation of being regarded from inside.

Twenty-two days. She had been a thief for nine years, and the first rule of theft was the oldest rule there was: *never let them close the trap all the way. Always leave yourself one door, even a bad one. Even a door on fire.*

Somewhere out there was a drowned chapel, and a song, and a woman who had sung a name into burning air a lifetime ago. The memory-vial in the guild archive held an hour of it. Sefra had offered her more.

Yara pushed off the rail and walked toward the archive district, toward the one door left, the bad one, the one on fire.

Behind her, unseen, a figure in a grey cloak noted the direction of her going, and turned to make report.
