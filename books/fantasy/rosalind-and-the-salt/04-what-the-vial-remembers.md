## 4. What the Vial Remembers

The vault of confiscated memories had a smell Yara never got used to: cold glass and old paper and, beneath it, something faintly sweet, like fruit going soft in a locked room. Ten thousand vials sat in their cedar racks, each one labeled in the Guild's careful hand—*name, date, extraction, classification*—each one holding somebody's yesterdays in suspension, pale as moonlight through fog.

She had filed memories here for eleven years. She had never once opened one.

Guild law was simple on this point, as it was simple on most points: *A taster who tastes becomes a thief twice.* Extraction without license was theft; consumption without license was theft of theft, the one crime Corvane's ledgers treated as unforgivable. She had watched an apprentice named Pell weep as they escorted him out—not because he'd lost his post, but because they'd poured his own confiscated vials down the drain in front of him, all four of them, everything he'd ever tried to keep.

Yara stood in the aisle between racks 400 and 401 with her lantern turned low and told herself she wasn't going to do it.

The lie lasted about as long as it took to find the drawer.

Her name was on the label in her own handwriting, which was the strangest part. *GALLOWAY, YARA. EXTRACTED: UNKNOWN DATE, PRE-ACQUISITION. PROVENANCE: WAR SALVAGE, CITY OF VESS. CLASSIFICATION: SEALED — GUILDMASTER'S ORDER.* The vial itself was older than any others in the drawer, its glass clouded, its wax seal stamped twice with Corvane's signet. Someone had handled it often enough to wear the wax smooth at the edges. Someone had held it up to the light, year after year, and put it back.

She wondered, with a cold turning in her stomach, whether that someone had been Corvane. Whether he had tasted her childhood the way she was about to. Whether he knew what she had been before he'd made her what she was.

The prophecy page was folded into her sleeve. She could feel its brittle corner pressing her wrist like a small accusing finger. *On the thirteenth turn of the salt moon, the drowned god remembers itself.* And beside the words, that sketch—a woman's profile, a crooked line at the collarbone like a bird's broken wing.

Yara unclasped the vial. The wax cracked with a sound no louder than a knuckle.

* * *

There was a ritual to tasting, and every thief learned it in their first year, though most never needed it for anything but quality-checking merchant stock. One drop. Never more—the memory would take you whole otherwise, would pour itself into you like water into a dry cloth and leave you unsure which of you was the vessel. One drop, held under the tongue, eyes closed, breath slow. Count to ten. Let it rise.

She let the drop fall from the glass stopper onto her tongue.

Salt. That was first, always first with old extractions—the preservative brine, the taste of the sea where the Guild sourced its fixing salts. She almost laughed. Of course. Of course it tasted of salt. Everything about her life lately tasted of salt.

Then the counting began, and the memory rose.

* * *

Heat.

Not lantern-heat, not summer-heat. Heat with a voice in it, a roaring, a crackle like the world being torn open along its seams. She was small—she felt her own smallness the way you feel a coat two sizes too large—and she was running, or being carried, the jolt and bounce of it, arms tight around her. Smoke lay over everything in yellow ropes. Somewhere close, timber groaned and gave, and a shower of sparks went up like furious fireflies.

She couldn't see faces. Memory didn't work that way at one drop—it gave sensation, not sight, impression, not fact. But she could smell it: burning pitch, burning wool, burning hair, and underneath it all the sea, near and enormous, breathing against stone.

Someone was singing.

The song came through the smoke the way light comes through shutters, in bars, in pieces. A woman's voice, low, roughened by smoke or by crying, singing something with a rhythm like rowing, like rocking, like waves. Yara strained toward it with her whole borrowed body, this small child's hunger for the sound, and caught—

—a name.

The song had a name in it. The woman sang the name the way you sing a prayer, the way you sing a warning, over and over, folding it into the melody so it couldn't be separated out, couldn't be lost even if everything else burned.

The name was hers.

*Yara. Yara. Little salt-pearl, little tide-born, Yara.*

She surfaced gasping, back in the vault, back in her own tall body, the lantern flame steady and indifferent. Her cheeks were wet. She touched her face and looked at her wet fingers as if they belonged to a stranger, which, in a sense, they did—they were the fingers of a woman who had just heard her mother's voice for the first time in her remembered life, and the woman did not know what to do with that.

One drop. She had taken one drop, and it had given her thirty seconds.

Thirty seconds of a burning city and a song.

* * *

She should have stopped there. She knew that the way you know the edge of a roof in the dark—instinctively, bodily. Guild law said one drop. Guild law said one drop because the Guild understood what Yara was only now beginning to understand: that memory is not information. It is appetite. You do not read a memory; you eat it, and eating wakes hunger, and hunger does not count drops.

She counted to ten again. She let another fall.

* * *

Water this time. Cold water, dark water, the slap of it against a hull. She was in a boat—she could feel the narrowness of it, the way her small body was wedged between crates or sacks, wrapped in something wool and damp. Oars creaked somewhere above her, rhythmic, exhausted. A man's voice said, low, "Not yet. Not till we're clear," to someone else, and the someone else answered in a whisper Yara couldn't parse, except that it was frightened, and except that the fear had a shape she recognized, because it was the shape of her own fear whenever she thought of the Prophecy Vault now.

Then light—grey dawn light, thin as skimmed milk—and hands lifting her, passing her over the side of the boat like cargo, like contraband. Which was what she was. She understood that suddenly, wholly, in the child's wordless way and the adult's precise way at once: she had been sold, or traded, or smuggled, passed hand to hand across black water while her city burned behind her, and the wool blanket smelled of salt and someone else's sweat, and the last thing the memory gave her was the receding sound of the oars, and the woman's song faint on the wind behind them, still singing the name, still singing it, as if the name could be thrown like a rope across the water and catch on something, anything, before the dark took it.

*Yara. Yara.*

The memory ended. The vault was very quiet.

Yara stood with both hands flat on the cedar rack, breathing like a woman who had been underwater. The vial sat in its drawer, three-quarters full, glowing faintly, the way all memories glowed if you looked long enough—pale gold, sea-colored, alive.

Two drops. Sixty seconds of a life. She was twenty-six years old and she owned perhaps a minute of her own past, and it was the worst minute imaginable, fire and flight and sale, and she wanted the rest of it more than she had ever wanted anything.

That was the trap. She saw it clearly, even through the wanting—that was the only thing that let her see it. The Guild hadn't sealed this vial to protect her. They'd sealed it because a thief who knows what she lost will do anything to steal it back. Hunger was a leash. Corvane had taught her that himself, in his gentle voice, in his ledger-perfect kindnesses: *want nothing, Yara, and nothing can be held over you.* He had raised her empty on purpose. He had kept her childhood in a drawer like bait on a hook, and tonight, because of a prophecy and a sketch and a date, she had swallowed the hook whole.

And still. Still.

Her hand was already reaching for the stopper.

* * *

The third drop broke the rules of drops entirely. Perhaps because the memory was hers—truly hers, keyed to her blood and bone the way no merchant's decade could ever be—or perhaps because the Salt, whatever it was, whatever slept in the marrow of her, recognized its own. But the moment it touched her tongue, the vault fell away like a stage set struck by stagehands, and she was *in* it, wholly, drowning in it:

Fire again, but closer now, inside a room. A kitchen? A chapel? Stone walls, a low ceiling, a table dragged against a door. The woman was there—not seen, but *felt*, her presence filling the room the way heat fills a kiln. She was pressing something into cloth. Into swaddling cloth. Yara felt the bundle against her own chest, felt the weight of it, dense and strange, warm as a second heartbeat, and heard the woman's voice not singing now but speaking, rapid, urgent, half-prayer and half-instruction, words tumbling too fast for the child's ears to hold but landing in the adult mind like coins in a well:

*"—keep it, keep it hidden, they must never find it, it sleeps, it only sleeps, and when it wakes—"*

The rest was lost. The memory shuddered, tore like wet paper, and in the tear Yara glimpsed—for one impossible instant, one frame of a picture that should not exist—a face. The woman's face. Younger than Yara had imagined, older than Yara had hoped, smoke-streaked, tear-cut, and looking directly at her. Looking at her across twenty-six years and a vial of glass, with an expression Yara knew intimately because she wore it herself every day in the guildhall mirrors:

the expression of someone leaving something precious somewhere safe, and knowing it isn't.

Then white. Then salt. Then nothing.

* * *

She came back to herself on the floor of the vault. She didn't remember falling. The lantern had guttered low; the racks rose around her like the walls of a canyon; somewhere a clock in the hall above was striking the hour, and she counted the strokes with her cheek against cold stone—eleven, twelve—and realized she had been gone for hours, though the memory had held minutes at most.

Three drops. Three drops and the law was broken past mending, and worse—worse than the law—she had seen the face. The Guild's extraction records said memory could not be recovered whole from a single taste. Three drops should have given her fragments. Instead she had gotten a face, a voice, a bundle pressed into swaddling cloth, and a sentence cut off at its hinge: *and when it wakes—*

When it wakes.

The prophecy's words rearranged themselves in her mind, unbidden, inevitable: *on the thirteenth turn of the salt moon, the drowned god remembers itself.* Twenty-six days. She had checked the charts herself, three times, hoping to be wrong.

Her mother had pressed something into her swaddling clothes while a city burned. Something that slept. Something that must never be found. And the Guild had pulled her from the water, erased her, sealed her past in wax and cedar—and kept her. Kept her the way you keep a strongbox. Kept her the way you keep a fuse.

Yara got to her feet slowly, joints aching as though she'd run a great distance. She resealed the vial with a bead of wax from the kit in her apron pocket, pressed her thumbprint into it beside Corvane's worn signet—let him find it, let him know, some small defiant animal in her chest wanted him to know—and slid it back into the drawer.

Then she stood in the aisle a long moment, listening to her own heartbeat, which sounded different now. Louder. As if something under it had shifted in its sleep and settled closer to the surface.

She wanted the rest of the vial. God help her, she wanted it the way the drowned want air—took all her craft, all her eleven years of practiced emptiness, to cap the hunger, to fold it small and put it away in the place where she kept everything she refused to feel. But it didn't go away. Nothing she had ever stolen had taught her how to steal from herself.

And as she doused the lantern and made for the stair, moving through the dark vault with the ease of eleven years' habit, one thought kept pace with her like a shadow slightly too long for her body:

The woman in the burning room had sung her name like a rope thrown across black water.

And somewhere out in the sleeping city, in a memory-den that smelled of opium and stolen summers, a stranger was asking questions about a girl with a crooked collarbone scar.

Yara paused at the top of the stair, hand on the rail, and listened.

Below, far below, at the guildhall's street door, someone was knocking.
