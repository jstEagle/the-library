## 6. Corvane's Kindness

The summons came at the hour when the guildhall's lamps were trimmed low and the corridors smelled of tallow and old paper, which was to say: the hour Corvane liked best for kindness.

Yara knew this because she had filed enough memories to know how men like him worked. Grief kept its own ledger, and so did guilt, and both were heaviest in the dark hours before dawn, when the mind went soft as wax and took the imprint of whatever pressed against it. A summons at midday meant business. A summons at the dead turn of night, delivered by a junior clerk who wouldn't meet her eyes—that meant something else entirely.

She dressed carefully. Not her working clothes, with their many pockets and their seams reinforced for running, but the grey dress she'd been given three years ago on her naming day, the one with the collar that sat crooked over her scar no matter how she arranged it. If he wanted to perform a father tonight, she would give him a daughter to perform it at.

The thought arrived fully formed and cold, and she stood a moment in front of the small mirror in her cell, surprised by it. *Give him a daughter to perform it at.* When had she started thinking of Corvane's warmth as a performance?

She knew when. She had known since the Prophecy Vault, since the sketch of a woman with her own crooked collarbone, since the date written in faded ink that matched tonight's moon chart exactly. Since she had begun, very quietly, in the privacy of her own skull, to audit him the way she audited everything else.

* * *

His study sat at the top of the guildhall, above the vaults, above the ledgers, above the thousands of vials racked in their cedar cabinets like a library of stolen lives. You reached it by a spiral stair worn hollow in the center of each step—worn, Yara had always assumed, by generations of guildmasters climbing toward responsibility.

Tonight, climbing it, she thought: *or worn by generations of orphans being summoned.*

The door was open. It was always open. That was part of it too—the studied informality, the message that Guildmaster Corvane had nothing to hide from his people. She had believed that once. Belief, she was learning, was just a memory you hadn't yet checked against the record.

"Yara." He looked up from his desk with genuine-seeming pleasure, lamplight pooling in the deep lines around his eyes. He was not an old man, but he had arranged himself to look like one over the years—silver threaded deliberately through his beard, shoulders allowed a scholarly stoop. Everything about Corvane was arranged. She saw it now the way a thief sees the false bottom of a merchant's strongbox: not because it was hidden well, but because she finally knew what to look for. "Sit. Sit. I've got tea going cold and no one to drink it with."

There was one cup on the desk. One. Placed there before she arrived, so that he could gesture at it and say *I've been expecting you* without saying it. She sat.

"You've finished the Vault audit," he said. "Three weeks early."

"The pages catalog themselves, mostly. They're just paper."

"Just paper." He smiled, and poured her tea from a pot she hadn't seen him signal for—a servant behind the screen, then, another small machinery of impression. "Nothing in this building is just anything, Yara. That's the first lesson I ever taught you, if you recall. You were seven. You'd found a ledger open on my desk and asked why we wrote down what people remembered if the memory itself was already in the vial." He leaned back. "Do you remember that?"

*No,* she almost said. *I don't remember being seven. I don't remember anything before the fever ward you claim to have pulled me from.* But she drank the tea instead, and let the silence do the work, and watched him fill it the way he always filled silences—with himself.

"You said," he prompted gently, "that the vial holds the body of a memory but the ledger holds its soul. The weight, the worth, the name of who it belonged to. Without the ledger, a memory is just salt water with delusions." He laughed at his own joke, warmly, waiting for her to laugh too.

She smiled. It cost her nothing; she had practiced smiling into emptiness for twenty years.

"I've been thinking about that lesson," she said. "About names. Who gets them in the ledger, and who doesn't."

Something moved behind his eyes—not guilt, exactly. Guilt was a raw thing, and Corvane's inner workings had been sanded smooth by decades of use. It was more like a man hearing a floorboard creak in a house he'd built himself, and knowing precisely which nail had loosened.

"Ah," he said softly. "You found your entry."

* * *

The fire cracked. Somewhere below them, the guildhall settled into its bones, and the sound of a clerk's pen scratched faintly up through the floor like a beetle in a wall.

"I didn't go looking," Yara said. Which was true. She had gone looking for a prophecy and found herself instead, which was worse. "I was auditing. Your assignment."

"My assignment," he agreed. "And what did the ledger say?"

"It said I wasn't found." She set down the cup. Her hand was steady; she made sure of it, made a small craft of it, the way she made a craft of every feeling she couldn't afford to show. "It says I was purchased. From the survivors of Vell. A baby, bought for forty marks and a pardon. And it says who signed for me."

She let his name hang in the room between them. Corvane did not flinch. She gave him credit for that, even as she noted it: *no flinch. Rehearsed.*

"Yes," he said simply. "I signed."

"You told me my parents died in the fever wards. That you found me among the bodies, too young to even be sick myself, and that the guild took me in out of—" she searched for the word he had always used, and found it, and hated how easily it came, "—out of decency."

"And so I did find you." He spread his hands, palms up, an old man's gesture of helpless honesty. "In a manner of speaking. Yara, sit with what those years were. The war had just ended. The Salt Wars, they called them, though no one fighting them understood what they were fighting over. Two armies, a dead god, and a coastline of towns burned to the waterline. Vell was the last of them. The survivors had nothing. Some of them had less than nothing—they had children they couldn't feed and enemies coming to ask questions about where the god's remains had gone." His voice dropped, went gentle, went *kind*. "I bought a child from people who were selling. Is that theft? Or is it the only mercy available in a market where everything's already for sale?"

"You could have left a ledger entry saying so."

"The Ledger Guild does not advertise its purchases. Even merciful ones." He rose, crossed to the window, and looked out at the city—the real city, the one below the guildhall's hill, where the lamps of the memory-dens burned green and the poor sold their happiest hours for bread. "Every gift has a price visible only to the giver, Yara. That's not corruption. That's just arithmetic."

"Then let's do the arithmetic," she said. "What did I cost? Forty marks?"

He turned back to her, and for a moment—for one unguarded half-second—the performance slipped, and what looked out through his face was not a father and not a mentor but an accountant confronting a sum that refused to balance. Then it was smoothed away, and he was kind again, and she understood that the slip had perhaps also been part of the design. A man who showed you his weariness was a man you trusted.

"What do you want it to have cost?" he asked quietly.

* * *

She should have left then. She knew it the way a thief knows the moment a mark's attention drifts to the wrong pocket—the moment to move is *now*, before the trap finishes closing. But her legs stayed folded under her on the chair, and the tea cooled in her hands, and some starved animal in her chest, the same one that had broken guild law to taste a single drop of her own confiscated childhood, wanted to hear the rest. Wanted to hear him say *I loved you*, even knowing now that love, in Corvane's mouth, was a line item.

"You raised me," she said. "Twenty years. You taught me letters and ledgers and how to lift a decade out of a dying man's head without leaving a scar. You gave me a trade, a name, a room of my own. Why?"

"Because you were alone, and so was I."

"That's not an answer. That's a poem."

He laughed—genuinely, she thought, the first true thing all night—and came back to the desk, but he didn't sit. He stood over her, and the lamplight put his shadow across her knees like a hand.

"All right. Arithmetic." He held up one finger. "One: the guild needed thieves, and the best thieves are made, not hired. A child raised inside the craft has no accent of the outside world, no family to sell her out, no debts but ours. I have built this guild's next century on children like you. Don't look wounded—it's the most honest thing I've said all night, and you deserve honesty tonight of all nights." A second finger. "Two: you were not an ordinary purchase. The sellers in Vell weren't just clearing their mouths to feed. They were passing on a burden. Something had been given to them during the war—something they were afraid of, something the armies on both sides had died trying to hold—and they pressed it into the smallest, most deniable vessel they could find. A baby. Wrapped in swaddling cloth, wrapped in a story, wrapped in forty marks of silence."

The fire popped. Yara became aware of her own heartbeat, slow and enormous, filling her ears like tide.

"They hid it inside her?" she said. "Inside a child?"

"Inside a blank." His eyes came up to hers, and there it was at last, the thing beneath all the kindness, surfacing like a drowned chapel rising with the tide. "A child whose memory would be taken before it could take root. You were the safest vault in the world, Yara, because you didn't know you were a vault. We erased your childhood ourselves—your first years, the years that mattered—and the Salt settled into the space we made. Quiet. Weightless. For twenty years it has slept in you like a stone at the bottom of a well, and the guild has slept easy knowing that no heir, no army, no prophet could ever find it."

"*We* erased," she repeated. Each word came out flat and separate, coins counted onto a table. "You're telling me you did this to me yourself. The blankness. The—"

"The mercy," he said, "of not carrying what you were carrying."

She stood. The chair scraped. Her body was doing things without consulting her—she noted distantly that her hands had curled, that her breath had gone shallow, that grief and fury had arrived together wearing each other's clothes. Twenty years of feeling nothing where a life should be, and here was the invoice: *the nothing was installed. The emptiness was craftsmanship.*

"You took my childhood to make room for a god's corpse," she said.

"To keep it from men who would have burned ten cities to get it." He didn't raise his voice. He never raised his voice; that was the horror of him. "You think I don't know what I took? I have carried the ledger of it for twenty years, girl. Every year on your naming day I gave you a gift, and every year I watched you open it with those careful empty eyes, and I knew—I *knew*—that I was the reason they were empty. Do you imagine that was easy?"

"Yes," Yara said. "I imagine it was exactly as easy as everything else you've ever done. Because you're good at it."

The words landed. She saw them land. And still he recovered, still he turned the wound into leverage, because that was the deepest lesson he had ever taught her without meaning to: *everything can be spent.*

"Then believe this instead," he said, and now his voice did change, dropping to something rawer, whether crafted or not she could no longer tell, and the not-being-able-to-tell was its own kind of wound. "The prophecy you found. The date. Tonight begins the thirteenth turn, and when the moon completes it, the Salt wakes. It will wake *in you*. And when it wakes, every heir, every zealot, every remnant of both armies will smell it on the wind and come here—to this hall, to these ledgers, to the children sleeping under this roof. The guild survives the last war because we buried it. If it surfaces now, in a thief's body, in a guildhall full of orphans—" He stopped. Made himself breathe. "Give it to me, Yara. Let me carry it. Let me be the vault. I'm asking as the man who fed you, taught you, named you. I'm asking as your father in every way that matters."

*In every way that matters.* She turned the phrase over and found the flaw in it immediately, the way she'd been trained to, the way he'd trained her: *matters according to whom.*

"No," she said.

"Yara—"

"You rehearsed this." Her voice came out quiet, and that surprised her; she had expected shouting. "The tea. The one cup. The story about finding me. You've done this speech before, haven't you—in front of a mirror, or a clerk, or the dark. *Let me be the vault.* You've been waiting twenty years for the Salt to wake so you wouldn't have to steal it from me. So I'd *give* it. Because a gift doesn't go in the ledger, does it? A gift leaves no entry. No witness. No proof that the guildmaster of the Ledger Guild is holding a dead god's memory in his two clean hands."

He was silent for a long moment. The fire burned. Downstairs, the pen scratched on, recording someone else's life.

"You sound," he said at last, "exactly like me."

"I know," Yara said. "That's the worst thing you ever gave me."

She walked to the door. Her legs worked now; the starved animal in her chest had gotten what it came for, and it had learned, finally, the taste of ash. At the threshold she stopped, because some last stubborn scrap of the girl who had believed in him for twenty years made her turn.

"The woman in Vell," she said. "My mother. Did she sing? When she gave me up—did she sing something?"

Something crossed his face then that no rehearsal could have put there. Pity, maybe. Or fear.

"Go home, Yara," he said gently. "Sleep. In the morning, we'll speak again, and you'll feel differently."

"In the morning," she said, "you'll have enforcers at my door. I've read enough ledgers to know how your kindness ends."

She went down the spiral stair, past the vaults, past the racks of vials where ten thousand lives slept in salt water, out into the cold where the city's green dens glowed and the thirteenth moon was three weeks from full. Behind her, high in the guildhall, a lamp went out in the guildmaster's study—deliberately, she was sure. A man putting out his light so no one could watch what he did next.

She had until morning. Perhaps less.

And somewhere south, past the docks, past the salt marshes, a melody she had tasted once in a stolen drop of her own blood was waiting in a drowned chapel for her to come and learn the rest of the song.
