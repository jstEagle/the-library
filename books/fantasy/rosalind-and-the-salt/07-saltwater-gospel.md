## 7. Saltwater Gospel

The song did not leave her.

Yara had expected it to fade the way stolen memories faded—like breath on glass, there and then only a smear. But three nights after she had broken guild law and tasted her own confiscated childhood, the melody still lived behind her teeth. It rose when she washed her face. It rose when she counted coin in the dark of her rented room above the chandler's shop. It rose, most treacherously, when Corvane passed her in the guildhall corridor and smiled his rehearsed smile, and she had to bite the inside of her cheek to keep from humming it aloud.

It was a woman's voice, that song. Low and salt-roughened, the kind of voice that came from singing over wind. The words were gone—or had never been words at all, only vowels worn smooth by water—but the shape of it was unmistakable. It was a lullaby. It was a naming-song. It was sung *to* someone, and Yara could not shake the certainty, bone-deep and irrational, that the someone was her.

So she followed it.

Not the way one follows a sound through streets. There was no direction to it, no left or right. It was more like following a current felt through the hull of a boat—a pull in the blood, faint but insistent, strongest near the harbor and stronger still on the water. She hired a fishing skiff from a man who asked no questions because she paid in questions answered: she lifted from him, gently, without permission, the memory of where he'd once found a school of silverfin beyond the shoals, and traded it back for passage. He never knew what he'd given. That was the craft of it. That was always the craft of it.

She told herself it was investigation. Due diligence. A guild thief auditing an anomaly before the anomaly became a liability. She told herself a great many things as the skiff slid past the breakwater and out along the drowned coast, where the old city of Vhalen Mora lay beneath the green water like a sleeping animal—the spires of it visible on calm days, the bell towers leaning, the whole quarter swallowed a century ago when the sea had come in over the walls and no one, in any ledger she had ever filed, had written down why.

The pull strengthened as the sun went down. By full dark it was less a song than a hand at the back of her neck, guiding her, and the skiff bumped against something solid in the black water.

Stone steps. They rose out of the sea at an angle, slick with weed, ascending toward a shape that resolved slowly as her eyes adjusted: a chapel roof, collapsed on one side like a broken eggshell, its remaining arches black against the stars. A drowned chapel, half-raised from the water on a shelf of rock, as if the sea had taken the city and then, in some mood of mercy or carelessness, set this one piece back down.

Yara tied the skiff and climbed.

Her boots found each step by feel. Salt crusted the stone in white rime, and where she touched the wall to steady herself, the stone hummed—not vibration exactly, but something closer to recognition. Like touching a struck bell that remembered being struck.

"You're late," said a voice from inside the ruin. "Or early. With this moon, it's hard to say which."

* * *

The priestess sat in what had been the apse, wrapped in a robe that might once have been white and was now the color of driftwood. She was old the way sea-cliffs are old—not frail so much as weathered down to essentials. Her eyes were closed and had the sunken, settled look of eyes that had been closed for many years.

"I heard you coming," the woman said, "from two miles out. Not your boat. You. You sing in your sleep, girl. Did you know that? Everyone does. Most people sing their own songs. Yours isn't yours."

Yara stopped at the edge of the light—a small oil lamp, burning with the flat yellow flame of cheap tallow—and made herself breathe. "You know what I am."

"A thief." The priestess said it without heat, the way one says *tall* or *left-handed*. "A Ledger Guild thief, if I'm any judge, and I am. You walk like someone carrying vials. Careful of them. Afraid to spill." She tilted her head. "But that's not why you've come, is it? You didn't come to steal from a blind woman in a ruin. You came because something in you wouldn't stop singing."

Yara's throat tightened. "A song. A woman singing. I don't know the words."

"Of course you don't. The words were drowned with everything else." The priestess patted the stone beside her, an oddly domestic gesture in all that desolation. "Sit. My name is Mother Ossa, though titles sink faster than cities. And you are going to want to sit down for what comes next. Everyone does."

Yara did not sit. She stood with her back to the broken arch and the sea wind at her shoulders and said, "Tell me what the song is."

Ossa sighed, and the sigh had the patience of someone who had waited a very long time and could wait a little longer. "It's a gospel," she said. "The last one. Everything else we sang burned or rotted or was bought up by your Guild and locked in vaults. But a song lives in people, and people are harder to burn than paper. My order kept it. Mouth to ear, mother to daughter, for a hundred years. We sang it to the water every dusk." Her lips moved then, silently, shaping sounds; and Yara felt the melody rise in her own chest to meet it, unbidden, a harmony answering across a century.

"Stop," Yara whispered. "How do I know it?"

"Because it was sung *over* you." Ossa turned her blind face toward her, and though the eyes saw nothing, Yara had the terrible sensation of being seen entirely. "It's a naming-song. We sang names into the water so the dead god would have something to hold onto. So it wouldn't forget itself. A god that forgets itself doesn't die, child. It just... disperses. Becomes weather. Becomes tide. Becomes the forgetting between one thought and the next." She folded her hands. "And someone, a hundred years ago, decided that was too merciful. Someone decided a god's memory should be kept. Bottled. Owned."

The word *bottled* landed in Yara like a hook finding flesh.

"The Salt," she said.

Ossa's eyebrows rose. "You know the name."

"I know the name." Her voice sounded far away to herself. "I know it from prophecy vaults and heirloom vaults and the ledgers of two families who've been sharpening knives over it for a hundred years. I know it the way a courier knows the contents of a package she's not meant to open." She laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "What is it? Truly. Not the stories. The truth."

"The truth is that memory has weight." Ossa spoke slowly now, choosing each word like a woman laying stones across a stream. "Your Guild knows this better than anyone—they make their living off the weight of other people's pasts. Every memory ever held by any mind leaves a residue. Grief, joy, terror—it settles, like silt. And when a god dies—a real one, a thing that *was* its memory, that had no self apart from what it remembered—the residue doesn't scatter. It crystallizes." She reached down beside her chair and lifted something: a shallow bowl of seawater, which she set in her lap. "We call it Salt because that's what it looks like. White grains. Coarse. Beautiful, if you can bear to look at it. And it remembers everything its god ever was. Every prayer it received. Every war fought in its name. Both armies. Both lies."

"Both—" Yara started.

"There were never two truths, girl. There was one truth and two armies, and each army carried half the lie and called it the whole." Ossa's voice hardened, the first edge she'd shown. "The heirs who come sniffing after the Salt—ash-blood and wave-blood, sword-families and song-families—each believes their ancestors were wronged. Each believes the Salt will vindicate them. And here is the horror of it, the thing my order spent a century praying would never come to pass: they're both right. The Salt can do that. It can remake whoever holds it. Give them their grievance made holy. Give them their victory, retroactive and eternal. It is a dead god's memory, and a memory that size doesn't sit in a person. It *grows* to fit them, and then it grows past them, and then there is no person anymore. Only the memory, wearing them like a coat."

The lamp flame bent in the wind off the sea. Somewhere below, water slapped the steps in a slow, patient rhythm, like a heart deciding whether to keep beating.

"Why are you telling me this?" Yara asked. "You don't know me."

"No," Ossa agreed. "But I know the song. And the song has been getting louder in you since you walked up those stairs—I can hear it in your breathing now, the way your chest wants to hum. Which tells me the Salt isn't near you." She paused, and the pause stretched until Yara thought she would scream. "It tells me the Salt is *in* you."

* * *

Afterward, Yara could never reconstruct the next few minutes properly. Memory, her own trade, her own craft, betrayed her—there were gaps where understanding should have been, moments her mind refused to file. She remembered sitting down finally, because her legs simply stopped negotiating. She remembered the bowl of seawater in the priestess's lap, and how Ossa dipped two fingers into it and flicked droplets toward her almost casually, the way one flicks water at a fevered brow.

She remembered the taste.

Salt, yes—but under the salt, everything. A burning city seen from very high up, from the height a god sees from, with the strange compassion of altitude. Two armies converging like hands closing around something small. A temple full of people singing while the roof came down, still singing, choosing the song over the screaming. And underneath it all, threaded through it like wire through wax: exhaustion. Not malice. Not hunger. Just a vast, ancient tiredness, the tiredness of a thing that had been remembered too loudly and too violently and wanted, more than anything, to be put down.

The vision lasted perhaps four seconds. It took something from her that took days to grow back—she couldn't afterward have said what, only that she felt lighter in a way that frightened her, as if a room in her had been emptied and she hadn't known the room existed.

"It touched you," Ossa said quietly. "Through the water. It's curious about you. It's been curious about you for a long time, I think." She wrung her hands in her lap. "Who carried you, child? Who gave you to the world?"

"My mother," Yara heard herself say. "In a burning city. She pressed something into my swaddle. I've seen it—one hour of it, returned to me against every law I live by. She was singing. The song."

Ossa nodded slowly, and tears ran freely down the old woman's face, unchecked, as if she'd long ago stopped bothering with them. "Then you're the vessel. The last vessel. The Guild hid it in the one place no thief would ever look—in a thief. In a child with no past, whose past they owned outright." She laughed softly, horribly. "Do you see the elegance of it? They erased your childhood so thoroughly that even you believed you had none. And all the while, the god's memory slept in the hole they'd made. You aren't a carrier, girl. You're a reliquary. They built you into one."

Yara looked at her hands. Thief's hands, scarred at the knuckles, steady as stone. Hands that had lifted ten thousand memories from ten thousand heads and felt nothing, nothing, nothing where the joy should sit—because there was no room for anything. Because the room was occupied.

"How do I get it out?" she asked. Her voice was very calm. Calm the way the sea is calm an hour before it takes a ship apart.

"That depends on what 'out' means." Ossa leaned forward. "There are those who'll tell you to surrender it—to hand it to an heir and let history resume its argument. There are those who'll tell you to destroy it, though I'll save you time: it cannot be destroyed. Memory that size doesn't die twice. And there will be a voice—you've heard it already, haven't you? In dreams?" Yara flinched, and Ossa nodded as if she'd expected the flinch. "There will be a voice asking only to be remembered. And it won't lie to you. That's the worst of it. Gods' memories don't need to lie. Being remembered is enough for them. Being remembered is *everything*."

"And you?" Yara asked. "What does your order say? What does the gospel say?"

Ossa was quiet for a long moment. The lamp guttered; she fed it a twist of wick without looking. "The gospel says the sea forgets," she said at last. "The sea takes everything eventually—cities, armies, names, griefs—and it holds none of it. That's not cruelty. That's the only mercy large enough to hold a god." She reached out and, finding Yara's wrist with surprising accuracy, gripped it with fingers strong as kelp rope. "Whatever you choose, choose it soon. The moon is turning. Thirteenth turn, the old charts say. When it completes, the Salt wakes fully, and it will not ask your permission again. Until then, it sleeps in you like tide in a cave—going out, coming in. And lately, child—" her grip tightened— "lately the tide has been coming in farther each night."

Yara pulled her wrist free and stood. The ruined chapel loomed around her, arches open to the sky, the drowned city breathing beneath the floor. She thought of Corvane's rehearsed kindness. Of forged papers recalled before she knew to run. Of a baby purchased from the war's last survivors, entered in a ledger in a dead man's careful hand. Of a mother singing a name into fire.

Every gift in her life had been a leash. Every blank space, a cage.

"Thank you," she said, and meant it, and hated how small it sounded.

"Come back at dusk tomorrow," Ossa said. "I'll teach you the whole song. All the verses. If you must carry it, you should at least carry it *sung*." She smiled then, and her face in the lamplight was suddenly, startlingly young—an echo of some girl she had been a lifetime ago, singing to the water. "No one should carry a god in silence."

Yara climbed down the sea steps with the song rising in her chest like water in a lock, higher and higher, and she was halfway to the skiff before she understood what the cold feeling at the back of her neck was.

It wasn't the wind.

Lanterns. Three of them, moving along the cliff road above the drowned coast, in the formation the Guild taught its enforcers—spread wide, silent, cutting off the landward path. And on the water behind her, barely visible against the starlight, the low sleek shape of a guild cutter, oars muffled, sliding toward the chapel steps.

They had followed her. Or they had known where she would go before she did.

Behind her, in the ruin, Mother Ossa began to sing.
