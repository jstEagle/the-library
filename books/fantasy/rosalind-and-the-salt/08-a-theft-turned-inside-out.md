## 8. A Theft Turned Inside Out

The chapel had a smell Yara would carry for the rest of her life, though she did not yet know how short that life of carrying things would prove to be. Salt and stone and something older underneath—candle tallow gone rancid in the heat, and beneath even that, the green rot-sweetness of water that had stood too long in the dark.

She came back at dusk on the third day because she could not help it. That was the truth of it, ugly as it was: she told herself she returned for answers, for doctrine, for the practical business of understanding what slept inside her ribs, but the real reason was smaller and more human than any of that. The priestess had known the song. And when the old woman hummed it—half-voice, cracked as riverbed clay—something behind Yara's sternum had turned over like a sleeper disturbed, and she had wanted, with an appetite that frightened her more than Corvane's enforcers ever could, to hear it again.

So she descended the drowned steps again, hem soaked to the knee, lantern held high enough to catch the waterline marks on the walls. Seven floods the chapel had survived, according to the tide-rings painted in faded ochre up the pillars, each ring labeled by some patient dead hand. The eighth flood had never come; the sea had simply decided, a century ago, to stay.

The priestess was waiting. She seemed always to be waiting, seated on her driftwood stool before the altar with her milky eyes turned toward the door, hands folded in her lap like two pale crabs at rest.

"You walk louder than you think," the old woman said. "Grief has a heavy heel."

"I'm not grieving."

"No." A smile, toothless and unhurried. "You're hungry. It sounds similar, from in here." She tapped her own temple with one knuckle. "Come. Sit. The water's warm tonight. It means the god is dreaming."

Yara did not sit. She stood at the edge of the candlelight with her lantern swinging gently, and she asked the question she had carried up from the depths of three sleepless nights. "You said the Salt can remake whoever holds it. I need to know what that means. Precisely. Not prophecy-language. Plain words."

"Plain words." The priestess laughed softly, a sound like sand shifting. "You guild-thieves and your plain words. Very well." She raised her face, and though those clouded eyes could not see, Yara felt them find her anyway, the way a hand finds a wall in the dark. "A memory is not a picture, child. It is not a thing you look at. It is a weight you have learned to carry, and the shape of your shoulders is made by what you've carried. The god's memory is the heaviest weight there is. Whoever holds it will be *reshaped* around it. Their griefs will become its griefs. Their loves will bend toward its loves. They will wake one morning and reach for their own name and find the god's instead, worn smooth as a riverstone from all that remembering."

"And if they refuse it?"

"Then it waits. Memory is patient. It outlasts everything except forgetting."

Yara's hand drifted, without her permission, to the place below her breastbone where the Salt sat—or where she imagined it sat, a grain of impossible weight, a splinter of dead divinity lodged in the meat of her. "And me? What am I, to it?"

The priestess was quiet a long moment. Water dripped somewhere in the dark nave, counting seconds neither of them wanted counted.

"You are its cradle," she said at last, gently, the way one delivers a hard diagnosis. "And cradles are not asked. Cradles are *made*."

* * *

She stayed after that. She told herself it was strategy—learn the chapel's approaches, learn the priestess's routines, prepare for the raid that surely must come, because Corvane did not let assets wander and blind women who sang dead gods' songs did not go unnoticed in a city of ledgers. But strategy did not explain why she found herself hauling fresh water down the drowned stairs for the old woman, or mending the fraying hem of the altar cloth with stitches learned from no one, or sitting through the long blue hours of evening while the priestess talked.

The old woman talked the way the sea moved—in slow masses, without apparent effort, covering enormous distances. Her name was Mother Vess. She had been born sighted, she said, and lost her eyes to a fever at nineteen, and had spent sixty years since learning that blindness was only a different kind of ledger. "I keep accounts of voices," she said. "Of footfalls. Of the way breathing changes when a person lies. You'd be amazed what people think they hide."

"What do my accounts say?" Yara asked, and hated herself a little for asking.

Mother Vess tilted her head, listening to her the way other people looked. "That you were emptied young. That someone poured you out and used the vessel. That you have been so quiet inside yourself for so long that you mistake the silence for peace." A pause. The candleflame leaned. "And that lately, there is something else in there. Something vast, turning over in its sleep, murmuring in a language made of everyone's yesterdays."

"It talks to you?"

"It talks to *you*. I only hear the echo coming off you, the way you hear surf from a cliff top." The old woman reached out and, with startling precision, took Yara's wrist. Her fingers were cool and dry as paper. "It sings to you at night. Don't be ashamed. Gods sing lullabies too. They learned it from us, after all—we taught them everything they know about longing."

Yara should have pulled her arm away. Instead she sat very still, feeling the papery grip, feeling—for the first time since the vial, since the burning city, since the woman singing her name—the sensation of being *known* without being catalogued. The Guild knew her as entry numbers and vial weights. Dain knew her as a key. Sefra's people, wherever they waited, knew her as a throne. But this blind woman in a drowned chapel held her wrist and knew her as a tired girl who had never once been tired *for herself*.

"When they come," Yara said quietly, "and they will come—you should not be here."

"Child, I have nowhere else to be. This is where the sea put me." Mother Vess released her wrist and smiled into the middle distance. "Besides. I have been waiting sixty years for the god's cradle to walk through my door. Do you imagine I'll leave now, during the interesting part?"

* * *

They came on the fifth night, at slack tide, when the water in the nave lay flat as poured glass and every sound carried.

Yara heard them first—not footsteps but the absence of them, a wrongness in the dripping, a held breath in the dark above the seventh flood-line. Then lantern-light bloomed at the head of the stairs, harsh and white, guild-lanterns with their shuttered beams, and shadows came down the steps in the practiced stagger of men trained to move through flooded places: six of them, then eight, wading hip-deep with their blades wrapped in cloth so they wouldn't glint.

She knew the lead one. Brannoc. He had trained her in extraction technique when she was fourteen—how to hold a subject's temples, how to breathe in rhythm with them until their memories rose like silt in still water. He had kind eyes and terrible hands, and he had once told her, patting her shoulder after her first successful theft, *you're a natural, girl. Born empty. Easiest vessel I ever filled.*

"Yara Galloway," Brannoc called across the water, his voice flattened by the low ceiling into something almost gentle. "Guildmaster's compliments. Come home. No one needs to be hurt."

Behind her, Mother Vess had risen from her stool. The old woman stood between Yara and the enforcers with her arms slightly out, palms open, a frail white gate against the dark.

"She's under my protection," the priestess said. "This is consecrated ground."

"This is a flooded basement, mother." Brannoc waded closer. His wrapped blade trailed in the water like a sleeping eel. "Consecration expired with the god. Everything did. Now step aside, and we'll only take the girl's memories of the last week. She won't even miss them. Clean work. Painless. You know how we do it."

Yara felt it then—the old reflex rising, the professional calm sliding over her like a second skin. Assess exits: the seaward arch, submerged, passable if she dove. Assess threats: eight, blades, formation wide enough to cut off the arch. Assess assets—

Her eyes fell on Mother Vess's thin back, on the knobbed spine beneath the faded robe, on the sixty years of accounts kept in that paper-skulled head. Voices. Footfalls. The way breathing changes when a person lies.

Sixty years of memories. Every hymn. Every flood. Every pilgrim. The song itself, whole and unbroken, in the mind of the last person alive who knew it.

And the thought arrived fully formed, cold and professional and utterly horrifying in its clarity: *if I take her memories of me, they cannot follow me through her. If I take her memories of the last five days, the trail dies here.*

No. Worse. The thought went further, because thoughts like hers always went further, trained further, guild-further: *if I take her memories of me entirely, she cannot tell them my face, my voice, my name. She becomes a wall with nothing written on it.*

"Last chance, Yara." Brannoc was close now, close enough that she could see the water wringing his cloak, the lamplight carving his kind eyes hollow. "The Guildmaster remembers who pulled you from nothing. He expects loyalty."

*Every gift in my life was a leash.* She had thought that in Corvane's office, and here was the leash's end, wet rope in a drowned church.

"I'm sorry," Yara said. She did not know which of them she said it to.

She moved past Mother Vess—brushed the old woman's shoulder, an apology disguised as a stumble—and her hands came up and found the priestess's temples with the tenderness of long practice, thumbs settling into the hollows beneath the ears, fingers light as moth-feet on the papery skin.

Mother Vess stiffened. For one heartbeat the old woman understood everything—that the girl she had fed and taught and listened to for five nights was doing the guild's work in reverse, that the cradle of a dead god was also, still, after everything, a thief.

"Oh," Mother Vess breathed. Not angry. Almost relieved. "*Oh*, child. So that's what you are."

Yara breathed in rhythm with her. In through the nose. Out through the mouth. She felt the old woman's pulse against her fingertips, quick and trusting even now, and she sank into the familiar dark behind the eyes where memories floated like bodies in deep water, and she gathered them.

Five days. Five days of herself: her first descent, her lantern trembling on the flood-marks, her voice asking plain-word questions. The evening she'd mended the altar cloth. The night she'd sat with her wrist in the old woman's grip while the god murmured offshore. Herself laughing—she hadn't known she still could laugh—at some story about a pilgrim and a stolen goose. Herself asleep in the pew, mouth open, young, unguarded, while the priestess listened to her breathe and kept the account.

She took all of it. She rolled it up like a net full of fish, silver and struggling, and she pulled.

The extraction was clean. That was the obscenity of it—it was *clean*. Twenty years of training saw to that. No tearing, no bleeding at the edges, no fragments left behind to fester. Mother Vess swayed; Yara caught her, lowered her gently onto the driftwood stool, and pressed the stoppered vial of shining days into the inner pocket of her coat, where it lay against her heart like a coal.

The old woman blinked. Straightened. Looked directly at Yara—with those milky, seeing-nothing eyes—and smiled the polite, faintly puzzled smile one gives a stranger in a market.

"Forgive me," Mother Vess said pleasantly. "Are you lost? The chapel's closed, dear. Has been for years. But you're welcome to shelter, if the tide's against you."

Brannoc stopped wading. Behind him, the enforcers exchanged looks over their wrapped blades.

"Mother," Brannoc said slowly, "the girl standing beside you. Describe her."

"What girl?"

"The one *touching you*. Right now."

Mother Vess turned her face toward Yara with perfect courtesy and perfect blankness, and Yara watched herself become invisible in real time—watched the old woman's attention slide off her like rain off oilcloth. There was no flicker of recognition. No account of her voice, her footfall, her lying breath. Five days had been subtracted from sixty years and the sum closed over the wound without a scar.

"There's no one beside me," the priestess said, kindly. "There's never anyone beside me. There hasn't been anyone in years." And then, softer, to the air, to the water, to no one—to the shape of a girl she could no longer remember missing—"But I seem to have mislaid something. Isn't that strange? I feel like I've set something down and forgotten where. Like a song I can't finish humming."

She began to hum. Half-voice, cracked clay. The melody climbed three notes and stalled, circling, searching for the phrase that a thief had carried away in a vial against her heart.

Yara's legs nearly gave. She put a hand on the pillar—on the ochre flood-ring marked by some patient dead hand—and held herself upright by the memory of strangers while the song broke apart in the old woman's mouth.

"That's enough," Brannoc said quietly, and there was something in his voice that might have been nausea. He had trained her. He knew exactly what he was looking at. "Gods, girl. That's enough. Come home."

"Tell the Guildmaster," Yara said, and her voice came out steady, which was its own horror—twenty years of training again, competence laid over carnage like a cloth over a face, "that the asset is no longer retrievable. Tell him the cradle walked out of the nursery."

She backed toward the seaward arch. The enforcers shifted; Brannoc raised a hand and, to her lasting surprise, held them. Perhaps it was the priestess's blank smile. Perhaps it was the look on Yara's face. Perhaps even kind-eyed men who fill empty vessels eventually tire of the filling.

She dove through the arch into black water, and the sea closed over her like a page turning.

* * *

She surfaced a mile down the coast, in the lee of a collapsed breakwater, and vomited seawater and something that was not seawater—something that tasted of candle tallow and salt and sixty years of patience.

The vial against her heart was warm. Warmer than body-warm. It pulsed faintly, like a throat swallowing, and when she held it up in the moonlight she saw the memories inside moving on their own: five days of herself, swimming in circles, looking for their owner.

*I stole myself,* she thought. *Out of another person. I am a memory now. I live in a vial in the mind of a blind woman who smiles at strangers.*

She thought of Sefra restoring the beggar's memories—mercy wearing its bright face. She thought of Dain's vault of waiting weapons. She thought of Corvane's rehearsed kindness, of leashes and gifts, of a baby purchased from the war's last survivors and entered neatly into a ledger.

Everyone wanted to give her back to herself. Everyone wanted to own the giving.

And she had just done the same thing, in miniature, in a drowned chapel: taken a self away to save her own. The power didn't care whose hands it sat in. It only cared about the taking. That was the gospel of the Salt, and she had sung it fluently, cleanly, painlessly, leaving an old woman humming a broken song to the tide.

Yara pressed her forehead against the wet stone of the breakwater and let the waves say what she could not.

When she finally lifted her head, the vial had gone still. And far up the coast road, where the cliffs cut the moonlight, a figure stood watching her—tall, patient, wrapped in a traveler's cloak, making no move to approach. Even at that distance, even in the dark, she knew the posture. She had seen it in a vault full of century-old weapons.

Dain had found her. Or Dain had followed her. With him, she suspected, the two had always been the same thing.

He raised one hand—not a wave, not a summons. Just a sign that he was there, and waiting, and that whatever she had done in the chapel, he already knew.

The vial pulsed once against her heart, like a knock.

Yara got to her feet and started walking—not toward him, not away from him, but along the cliff path where the two directions happened, for the moment, to be the same.
