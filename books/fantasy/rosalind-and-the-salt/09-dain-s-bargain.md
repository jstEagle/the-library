## 9. Dain's Bargain

The enforcers came at dusk, and I learned something about Yara Galloway that the guild's files would never have told me: she runs like someone who has never once been chased.

Not badly — that was the strange part. She ran *well*, fast and low through the fish-market lanes of Saltreach, vaulting crates with a thief's economy. But there was no cunning in it, no history. A hunted person learns the shape of their own city the way a lover learns a body: which alleys dead-end, which doors stick, which rooftops hold under a running weight. Yara ran like a woman reading a map in a storm. She was fast because she was young and frightened, not because she knew where she was going.

I followed her from the chapel roof, three streets back, watching the enforcers close like a hand around a coin.

There were four of them. Guild grey, hoods up, faces wrapped against memory-scrim — the gauze they breathe through when they mean to take a thief's past whole. The two in front carried ledger-irons, the long tongs the guild uses to pin a skull still while the extraction needle does its work. I had seen them used once, when I was nine, on a den-thief who'd skimmed from Corvane's private collection. They had left him standing in the street afterward, blinking, polite, asking everyone he met if they knew his name.

The priestess's chapel was burning behind them. That was my fault as much as theirs; I had led them there by being seen asking after a blind woman who sang old songs. Guilt is an efficient fuel. It got me over the market roof before I had finished deciding to move.

* * *

Yara made her mistake at the tannery cut, where the lane forks around the rendering sheds. She went left, toward the water stairs, which would have been right if the tide were out. The tide was in. The stairs ended in black water, and behind her the enforcers fanned out with the unhurried confidence of men who have done this a hundred times and been paid for every one.

"Thief," said the lead one, and his voice was almost gentle. "Guildmaster wants only what's yours to give."

I have thought about that sentence since. *What's yours to give.* As though anything inside a person could be given without dying a little.

Yara backed to the edge of the stairs. I saw her weigh the water — thieves can swim, but wet clothes are loud, and loud things get remembered — and then she did something I did not expect. She reached into her coat and drew out a vial, small and dark, and held it up between two fingers like a gambler showing a card she hadn't looked at yet.

"Come closer," she said, "and I'll drop it."

The enforcers stopped. Even from the shed roof I felt the change in them, the way a room changes when money enters it. Whatever was in that vial mattered more than she was.

It bought her four seconds. Four seconds is nothing, unless you are me, arriving along the rendering-shed roofline with a boat-hook and a bad temper.

I will not pretend it was elegant. I took the nearest enforcer across the shoulders with the hook's blunt end and let his partner's surprise do the rest of the work; men trained to take memories are rarely trained to take blows. The other two turned toward the noise, which was the entire point, and Yara — quick as a card returning to a deck — went past them rather than away from them, through the gap their bodies had opened, and I dropped from the roof into the lane beside her and we ran together into the maze of drying-racks and salt-stink behind the sheds.

She didn't thank me. I noticed that. She matched my pace stride for stride and spent no breath on words, and when we finally stopped — deep in the warren where the racks hang so thick the moon comes down in stripes — she put her back to a post and looked at me the way you look at a door you didn't open.

"You," she said. "From the den."

"Dain." I kept my hands visible. Boat-hook lowered, palms out. Everything about me arranged to say *tool, not trap.* "You're welcome, incidentally."

"I didn't ask to be saved."

"No," I agreed. "You asked a blind woman to sing you a song about a dead god, in a city where the guild pays silver for exactly that kind of question. You asked loudly. This is what loud costs here." I nodded back the way we'd come, toward the glow of the burning chapel staining the low clouds orange. "They'll pull whatever's left of her memories out of the ashes and read them like tea leaves. Yours will be all over them. By morning every enforcer in Saltreach will know your face better than their mothers'."

Something moved behind her eyes — grief, maybe, or its efficient cousin, guilt — and was filed away. I had seen that look before, on collectors counting losses. It told me more than her questions did.

"Who are you?" she said. "Truly. Not the den version."

"The den version is true enough. Heir of Ash, some of them call it, when they're drunk and feeling poetic. My family held the eastern line in the Godswar. We were the ones who—" I stopped, because the rest of the sentence has eleven versions and I have never found one that doesn't sound like either bragging or begging. "We were there at the killing. That's the part that matters. Come with me and I'll show you the rest. What's left of it."

"Why would I?"

"Because your face is known now, and mine isn't. Because you have nowhere to sleep tonight that the guild hasn't already priced. And because—" I hesitated, and chose honesty, which I almost never do, and which is why I remember doing it. "Because I've been looking for you since before you knew you were lost. And I'd rather you came knowing that than find out later and call it a trick."

She studied me for a long moment, moon-striped, vial still in her hand. Then she put it away and said, "If this is a trap, I'll steal something from you that you'll miss."

"Everyone says that," I told her. "Almost no one can."

* * *

My family's house stands on the cliff road above the drowned quarter, behind gates that haven't opened for strangers in a hundred years. It looks like ruin from the road — and it is ruin, honestly, the wings gone soft with salt-rot, the gardens gone to gull-grass — but the heart of it is stone older than the guild, older than the city, and the doors of the undercroft are ash-oak banded in iron, and they answer to blood.

Literally. That is not a figure of speech. I cut my palm on the lock's tooth and pressed it flat, and the bolts went back one by one with a sound like slow knuckles cracking, and I watched Yara watch them, and I saw her do the arithmetic every thief does: *what would it cost to pick this?* The answer, which she arrived at visibly, was *everything, and then more.*

The stair went down farther than the house stood tall. Lamps woke as we passed — oil-fed, century-old, tended by a cousin who draws wages for a job that consists mostly of silence. At the bottom, the vault opened out of the dark like the inside of a bell.

I have shown it to no one living. I want that understood. Whatever else I am — and I am several things, few of them clean — I brought her down those stairs because there was no other way to say what needed saying, and I am a man who trusts objects more than sentences.

The weapons hung in ranks along the walls, and they were beautiful the way wounds are beautiful.

Spears first: forty of them, heads of grey metal that is not steel and was never ore, each one quenched, according to the plaques beneath, in water that had touched the god's drowning. Bows whose strings were woven from the hair of women who had sung the god's name until the singing killed them. Armor plates etched with hymns in the old script, worn inward against the skin so that the wearer fought inside a prayer. And at the far end, alone on a stand of black glass, the thing my family had built last, when hope was thin and the war nearly lost:

A key.

That is the only word for it. A blade-shaped absence, a sword forged backward, its edge ground to a geometry that hurt to look at directly. The smiths of my line had made it from the same instructions that made the Salt itself — the crystallized memory of the god we killed — on the theory that what remembers can also be made to forget. Touch the key to the Salt, the founding writ said, and the memory unbinds. Not destroyed. *Unwritten.* Scattered into whatever sea holds the world's forgetting.

One hundred years it had waited on its stand. One hundred years of my family dusting it, oiling its case, training sons and daughters in the hymn-scripts, and telling each generation the same story: *when it wakes, we finish what we started.*

"When it wakes," I said aloud, because Yara had gone very still in front of the key, "is tonight. Or near enough. You've read the prophecy. Don't lie to me about that; the priestess sang it to you, and the guild burned her chapel for it."

"How do you know what I've—"

"Because I was in the den when you asked about her. Because I've had watchers on the Prophecy Vault's outer corridor since spring. Because, Yara—" and here I made my second honest mistake of the evening, or my first honest act, depending on how you keep such ledgers, "—because the Salt is in you. Has been since you were a child. I don't know how it was done, and I'm sorry for it, whatever it cost you. But everything that's happened since the date appeared in that vault has been the world arranging itself around what sleeps in your bones. The guild knows. Sefra's people know. And now you know."

She turned. In the lamp-light her face was pale and set, and I braced for the knife or the denial, and instead she asked the question I had dreaded most:

"And you? What do *you* want?"

So I told her. All of it, standing among my family's beautiful wounds.

I told her that my great-grandmother had driven the spear herself and had wept while she did it, and had spent her last thirty years trying to build a weapon that could undo the deed. I told him — told *her* — that the Ash line had spent a century preparing for a resurrection none of us believed in, the way soldiers polish boots for a war that ended. I told her that when the prophecy surfaced and pointed at a guild thief with a crooked collarbone scar, my cousins had argued for taking her — taking *her*, the way you'd argue over a strongbox — and that I had argued for something else, and won the argument narrowly, and had never been sure whether I'd won it out of wisdom or vanity.

"I want it destroyed," I said. "Unwritten. Scattered. I swear it on the key itself, on the ash in our name, on whatever you hold sacred, which I suspect is nothing, which makes the oath easier not harder. I don't want a god's memory enthroned. I don't want my family's war finished. Wars should stay finished. I want the whole cursed inheritance to end with my generation, quietly, and I want to be the last heir of anything."

The lamps hissed. Somewhere above us, wind moved through the ruined house like a resident who had never left.

Yara walked the ranks slowly. She looked at the spears and did not touch them. She looked at the armor and did not touch it. At the key she stopped, and her hand rose — I saw the tendons shift — and hovered a finger's width from the black glass, and did not land.

"It's warm," she said quietly. "The air near it. Like standing near a hearth."

"Keyed to the Salt," I said. "It's been waiting for what you carry. A century of waiting makes things warm."

She took her hand back. When she faced me again, something in her expression had rearranged itself — not trust; I want to be clear that it was not trust — but a kind of professional appraisal, the look of a thief deciding whether a lock is worth her time.

"If I believe you," she said, "and I'm not saying I do — then understand what you're offering. You're offering to help me destroy the only piece of myself anyone ever thought worth hiding. Do you know what the guild erased to make room for it? I don't. That's the point. I don't know what I am underneath it. And you're asking me to dig the thing out and burn it, and go on being a hole shaped like a person."

"Yes," I said. "That's what I'm asking."

"And if I refuse? If I decide I'd rather know what I am?"

"Then I'll have failed," I said, "and my family's weapons will matter again, and I'll spend whatever time remains trying to stop you without hurting you, and probably fail at that too. I'm not pretending I hold the high ground, Yara. I'm holding a hook and a hundred years of guilt. It's what I have."

For a long moment she said nothing. Then, unexpectedly, she laughed — short, dry, real — and it did something to the lamplight, or to me.

"A thief and an heir," she said. "Both of us sworn to end an inheritance. Neither of us trusting the other further than the next doorway."

"That's usually how the durable ones are built," I said. "Trust is a decoration. Usefulness is load-bearing."

She looked at the key one more time. And I want to record, because it matters, because everything that came after grew from this moment like a crack grows from a flaw in glass — I want to record that she did not agree. She did not swear anything, or reach for my hand, or give me the pretty scene my cousins would have staged.

She said: "There's another heir. Sefra. She found me first, before the chapel. She offered me my childhood back. Every hour of it, whole." Her eyes stayed on the key. "And the worst part, Dain — the part I need you to understand before you swear anything else to me — is that I'm still thinking about saying yes."

The lamps hissed. The key sat warm on its black glass, patient as only a weapon can be.

Above us, faint through a hundred years of stone, we both heard it: bells. Not the harbor bells, which ring in pairs. These rang in thirteens.

Somewhere in the city, the count had begun.
