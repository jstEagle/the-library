## 10. The Ledger of Her Name

The guildhall slept the way old men sleep: lightly, and with one hand already reaching for a weapon.

Yara had known its rhythms since she was small enough to be carried through these corridors—Corvane's boots at dawn, the scribes' coughs at midmorning, the long dead hush after supper when even the candles seemed to hold their breath. She knew which floorboards sang. She knew that the night watch drank chicory instead of wine because Corvane believed a dull mind was a safe mind. She knew all of it, and she had never once asked herself why a guildmaster would teach a foundling every seam in his house.

She asked herself now, standing in the cold mouth of the sublevel stair with a scribe's grey cloak over her shoulders and a stolen ring of keys biting into her palm.

Because he trusted you, said the voice that still sounded like gratitude.

Because he owned you, said the newer voice, the one born in the Prophecy Vault with a sketch of her own crooked collarbone staring up from a century-old page.

She went down.

* * *

The deep archive was older than the guildhall above it, older than the city's cobbled streets, maybe older than the harbor wall. The stairs descended through three kinds of dark: lamplight, then memory-lantern light—the blue-green glow of vials racked along the walls like a cellar of drowned stars—and finally no light at all, only the pressure of ten thousand stored lives humming faintly against her skin. Yara felt them the way you feel weather coming. Griefs mostly. A few joys, gone sour in their glass. Somewhere to her left, an entire wedding day sat shelved between a drowning and a betrayal, and none of it made a sound, and all of it made a sound, if you listened with the part of yourself the guild had spent twenty years teaching her to ignore.

Her lamp threw her shadow huge against the racks. She looked, for a moment, like someone taller than herself. Someone with room inside her.

The registry hall lay at the bottom of the stair: a round chamber walled floor to ceiling in ledgers, their spines cracked and re-cracked, their leather gone soft as old skin. Acquisition records. Provenance ledgers. The guild kept meticulous accounts—it was the first thing they taught every thief. *A memory without provenance is worthless. A memory with provenance is money.* Yara had copied that maxim onto her apprentice slate at nine years old, in careful letters, beside a drawing of a vial that Corvane had pinned to his office wall and called *promising.*

She had thought, until very recently, that promising meant loved.

The ledgers were organized by decade of acquisition. Her childhood would have been entered—she did the arithmetic as she walked the shelves, fingers trailing the spines—twenty-two years ago. Give or take. Give or take being the whole problem; she didn't know her own birthday. Corvane celebrated it on the feast of Saint Aldery, because that was the day he'd *found* her, he said, half-frozen on the guildhall steps, wrapped in a blanket that smelled of woodsmoke and salt.

He had told that story so many times it had worn smooth as a river stone. He told it when he promoted her. He told it when she came back bleeding from a lift gone wrong. He told it last month, gently, when she'd asked him—only once, only idly—whether anything was known about her mother.

*A blanket and a name tag stitched at the hem,* he'd said, and touched her cheek with two fingers, the way he did when he wanted her to stop asking. *You were left, Yara. Left is a kind of luck. It means no one was looking for you.*

She found the ledger before she was ready to find it.

*Acquisitions, Year of the Ash Winter.* The spine crumbled slightly under her thumb, shedding brown flakes that drifted down like the season itself. She set her lamp on the reading stand, wetted her finger, and turned pages filled with Corvane's younger handwriting—tighter than now, hungrier, each entry a small act of appetite.

*Vial 441: grief, maternal, deep-strata. Vendor: field agent Hesk. Price: 3 crowns.*
*Vial 442: courage, battlefield-grade. Vendor: Hesk. Price: 6 crowns.*
*Vial 443: lullaby, fragmentary. Vendor: Hesk. Price: 1 crown.*

Lullaby. Fragmentary. One crown.

Her hand stopped moving on its own, the way a body flinches from a flame before the mind agrees to feel the burn. She stared at the word. Lullaby. She had no lullaby. She had checked, over the years, in the small dishonest ways thieves check things—humming nonsense to herself in the dark, waiting to see if any tune rose to meet her. Nothing ever had. Her childhood was a vial in this cellar somewhere, sealed and catalogued and priced, and the song that should have lived in her bones had been decanted into glass and sold for less than a good pair of boots.

Turn the page, she told herself. You came for the entry. Turn the page.

She turned the page.

And there it was—not where she expected it, not among the acquisitions at all. It was written across the top margin of the next leaf in red ink, red like a wound that hadn't decided to heal:

*Special acquisition. See bound record, Shelf Thirteen.*

Shelf Thirteen held the ledgers the apprentices were warned about in whispers: the ones with chains on their spines, the ones containing names of clients who paid the guild to forget they had ever been clients. Yara had assumed the warnings were about blackmail material. Politicians' sins. Merchant princes' crimes.

The chain lock yielded to the third key she tried. Of course it did. Of course the ring she'd stolen from the night clerk's desk opened everything in this house. Why wouldn't it? She had been given the run of the place since she could walk. Every door in the guildhall had always opened for her, and she had always read that as welcome.

It read differently now. It read like a man who keeps his livestock where he can reach it.

The bound record was smaller than the others, black leather, unmarked. She opened it under the lamp and the first page nearly made her laugh—a client list, exactly as suspected, dukes and magistrates and one archbishop, each with the memories they'd purchased listed in neat columns. Sins by the pound. She flipped past them, past years of them, hunting the date. Ash Winter. Twenty-two years ago.

Near the back, the handwriting changed. Newer ink. Corvane's mature hand, the one she knew from her apprenticeship evaluations, from her promotion papers, from the note he'd left on her pillow this morning saying *be careful today, little wren.*

*Item: one female infant, approx. 8 months, acquired from survivors of Vessengard. Condition: healthy. Memory strata intact but unstable—exposure to Salt residue noted. Recommend full extraction at earliest safe age.*

*Disposition: retained. Not for sale.*

*Retained* — and beneath it, the signature. Not a stamp. Not a clerk's mark. His actual hand, the loops of the C she had traced a hundred times on documents he'd asked her to witness, the little flourish on the final e that she'd once, at twelve, practiced copying because she admired it.

Corvane. Signed. Dated three days after the fall of Vessengard.

Yara stood very still in the round room full of dead voices, and let the words arrange themselves into meaning, because they resisted it. They resisted it the way a bone resists setting.

Not found. Not left on the steps in a blanket. *Purchased.* There was a price column. She made herself look. *Price: waived — consideration rendered in kind.* And below that, in smaller script, almost casual, the way a man notes a delivery of paper or ink:

*Survivors delivered per contract. Six heads. Guild obligation discharged.*

Six heads. The war's last survivors of Vessengard—the ones who had carried the god's memory out of the burning city, the ones the priestess's stories spoke of in a whisper, the ones who were supposed to have scattered like ash. They hadn't scattered. They had come to the guild, or been brought, and they had traded something, and the something had included a baby. A baby with Salt residue in her blood and a lullaby worth one crown.

She had not been rescued. She had been *acquired.* Inventory. An item marked *retained, not for sale*—and she understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, what that phrase meant in a memory-thief's ledger. Not for sale meant too valuable. Not for sale meant the guild had looked at an infant carrying a dead god's memory and decided, the way a miser decides about a coin of rare minting, to keep it.

To keep it. To raise it. To teach it every seam of the house and every rhythm of the watch and every way a person can be emptied out and refilled—and to wait twenty-two years for a date written in a vault to come due.

The lamp guttered. Or her hands shook. She couldn't have said which.

* * *

She did not remember climbing the stairs. That frightened her more than anything else—the gaps, the missing minutes, her body moving through the guildhall while her mind stayed down in the dark with the black book. She surfaced in the scribes' corridor with the ledger still under her cloak, pressed flat against her ribs like a second heartbeat. She should have left it. Taking it was madness; its absence would be noticed by morning; Corvane would know within the hour who had taken it, because who else could walk into Shelf Thirteen as if the chains were garlands?

But her hands would not open. Her arms had closed around the book the way a drowning woman closes around driftwood, and some animal part of her had decided that whatever happened next, this—the proof, the ink, the signature—was not staying in that house of soft leather and soft voices.

She let herself into her own room through the window, out of habit, though there was no one to hide from yet. The city lay beyond the glass, gray-blue with the hour before dawn, the harbor breathing its slow salt breath. On her desk sat the things of her life: her knife roll. Her forged papers—recalled, she remembered, quietly recalled weeks ago by someone who had anticipated her running before she'd known she wanted to. A dried flower Sefra had—no. That wasn't hers. That was the beggar-woman's joy, restored, that Yara had watched bloom on a stranger's face like watching sunlight through another person's window.

Everything on the desk was borrowed. Everything in her head was borrowed or bought or blank. Even her name—

Even her name. She opened the black ledger to the entry again, there in her own room, and read it a third time, slower, the way you read a ransom note. And this time she saw what grief had blurred past on the first two readings, in the corner of the disposition line, in ink so faded it might have been an afterthought:

*Name assigned: Yara. (Subject's mother, per vendor, used same. Retain for handler familiarity.)*

Retain for handler familiarity.

They had kept her mother's name the way you keep a dog's call-whistle. So the handler could summon it. So the item would come when called. Corvane had not named her out of tenderness. He had named her out of efficiency—he had taken the one word her mother had presumably breathed over her, the last inheritance of a burned city, and filed it in a ledger as a handling instruction.

Yara sat down on the floor. Not the bed; the floor, with her back against the desk leg, the ledger open in her lap, because the bed was Corvane's gift, the room was Corvane's gift, the trade was Corvane's gift, and the floor was the only honest surface left.

She had believed she was grieving. For an hour, walking back through the sleeping streets, she had thought the hot weight behind her eyes was grief—for the child on the steps, for the blanket story, for the version of herself who had been found rather than bought. But sitting here with the ink drying its old blood in front of her, she recognized the feeling for what it actually was, and it was worse than grief, and simpler.

It was gratitude, dying.

That was the thing no one warned you about—that gratitude could die, that it had a pulse, that it could be murdered by a single line of red ink, and that when it went it took everything built on top of it down with it. Twenty years of warmth toward the man who fed her and taught her and touched her cheek and called her little wren: all of it had been load-bearing, all of it had held up the roof of her life, and now the walls were coming down and underneath them there was nothing. No self. No history. Just a purchase order and a price that had been waived because six other people had paid it in the only currency they had left.

She thought of the priestess, smiling at a stranger. She thought of the merchant's happiest decade in its vial, filed among thousands. She thought of how carefully she had learned to feel nothing where joy should sit, and understood suddenly that this too had been the design. They hadn't just hidden the Salt in her. They had hollowed her first, patiently, lovingly, year by year, so that when the god's memory woke inside her there would be nothing in the vessel to fight it. Nothing to say *no.* A blank page accepts any handwriting.

Except, she thought, looking down at the ledger in her lap, that pages could steal pens.

Somewhere across the city, a bell tolled the hour—one stroke, thin and cold. Dawn was close. In a few hours the night clerk would find his ring missing, and the shelf would be found bare, and Corvane would come to her with his rehearsed kindness and his two fingers to her cheek, and he would ask her—gently, reasonably, for the guild's survival—to give back what had never been his to give.

Let him come, she thought, and was surprised by the steadiness of it. Let him bring his soft voice and his worn-smooth stories. She had something now that she'd never had before, better than forged papers, better than a knife: she had his handwriting. She had proof that every kindness was a leash, and she intended to hold the leash-end herself.

She closed the ledger. As she did, a loose slip of paper slid from the back binding—thin, yellowed, folded twice, tucked there by some clerk a lifetime ago and forgotten. She almost left it. Then she unfolded it, because she was a thief, and thieves are curious past the point of survival.

It was a bill of sale. Older than the ledger entry. Written in a hand she didn't know, cramped and desperate, the letters leaning like people running:

*Received of the Guild of Ledgers, in payment for the child and passage out of Vessengard: safe conduct for six persons, as agreed. The Salt goes with the girl. May your god forgive what ours could not.*

Six persons. Safe conduct. As agreed.

So the survivors had sold her—or saved her, the words were twins—and the guild had promised them passage, and the ledger downstairs said *six heads, obligation discharged,* and Yara sat on the floor of her purchased life doing the arithmetic of that discharge while the sky outside went from gray to the color of an old scar.

Six had gone in. How many had walked out?

She was still holding the slip when she heard it: footsteps in the corridor above, unhurried, familiar, stopping at her door. A pause. Then a soft knock, and the voice she had loved for twenty years, gentle as a hand on the back of the neck.

"Yara? Little wren. I know you're awake."

On the desk, beside the dying candle, her window stood open to the east—and through it, far off across the rooftops, a single figure watched the guildhall from the tower of the old customs house, still as a heron, waiting for morning.

Waiting for her.
