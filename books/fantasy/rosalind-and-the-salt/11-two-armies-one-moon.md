## 11. Two Armies, One Moon

Sefra had learned to read a city by its beggars.

Ashfall taught her that, in the years after the purges, when the worshippers of the Drowned God were hunted from temple to gutter and she learned to sleep in doorways with her mother's hymnal sewn into the lining of her coat. A city's beggars were its memory. Where they gathered told you where the warmth was. What they asked for told you what the city had taken from them.

The beggar at the mouth of Tanner's Row had been asking for nothing for three days now. That was what caught her attention—not his hunger but his stillness, the way he sat with his palms upturned on his knees like a man waiting for rain that had stopped falling years ago. His eyes were wrong. Not empty—Yara Galloway's kind of empty, the guild-made blankness Sefra had been tracking through six cities—but *full* in some terrible way, like a cup filled past the brim, trembling at the edge of spilling and never allowed to.

Someone had stolen this man's memories and left him the wanting.

Sefra crouched across from him, careful to keep her hands visible, the way you'd approach any wounded thing. "What's your name?"

He looked at her. It took a long time, as if her face were a page he had to sound out letter by letter.

"They took it," he said finally. "I keep reaching for it. There's a shape where it should be." He touched his chest, then his throat, then his chest again, patient as tide. "A name-shaped hole."

"Who took it?"

"The coin people." He said it without bitterness, which was somehow worse. "They gave me bread. Warm bread. I remember the bread." He smiled, and the smile was the most broken thing Sefra had seen since Ashfall burned. "I can't remember why I needed a name when there was warm bread."

She sat down beside him in the filth of the gutter because some things required it, required the lowering, the shared ground. Her grandmother had done this. Had knelt in the ashes of the last chapel and sung to people whose minds the guild had gutted, not because the song returned anything, but because it told them: *you are not alone in the hollow.*

But Sefra's grandmother could only sing. Sefra had inherited more than songs.

"I'm going to touch your hand," she said. "If you want me to stop, say so. Loudly."

He nodded, that same terrible patience.

His skin was cold and gritty, tanner's lime worked so deep into the creases of his palm he'd never wash it out. She closed her eyes and did what her blood demanded, what the old rites called *opening the sluice*—letting the memory of the god move through her the way it had moved through eleven generations of her line, thinned each time, diluted like a relic cut with base metal, but never gone. Never entirely gone.

She felt him before she felt anything else: the man-shaped absence, the wound in the world where a life used to be. And beneath it, faint as a footprint in wet sand, the residue. The guild thought theft left nothing behind. The guild had never understood memory. Memory was not a thing you carried; it was a thing the world remembered *through* you, and the world did not forget easily. It only waited.

*Come back,* Sefra said—to the residue, to the shape, to whatever of this man still clung to the edges of himself. *You were poured out, but the vessel remembers its own contours. Come home.*

The beggar gasped.

It came back the way water comes back to a dry riverbed—all at once, in a flood, carving channels that had been waiting under the dust. She felt it pour into him: a woman's laugh, salt-stung and young; a boat with a green-painted prow; a daughter, a daughter, a small fierce girl with lime dust in her hair; his own name arriving last, the keystone, the word that made all the other words fit—

"*Ollen,*" the beggar said, and began to weep. "*My name is Ollen. I have a daughter. Marta. Marta, she'll be—how long, how long has it—*"

"Three days since the theft," Sefra said quietly. "I don't know how long since the rest."

He clutched her wrist with both hands, and his tears fell on their joined skin, warm as blessing. "Who *are* you?"

"No one important," said the heir of the god's murderers' enemies, and helped an old tanner to his feet.

* * *

She watched him stagger toward the harbor district, shouting his daughter's name to anyone who would listen, and she let herself feel it—the rightness, the *purpose*, the thing the priests had promised her bloodline all her life. This was what the Salt was *for*. Not power. Restoration. The god had died holding the memory of everything, every prayer ever whispered to it, every grief entrusted to it, and its crystallized remembrance was not a weapon no matter what the heirs of ash believed, not a treasure no matter what Corvane's ledger-keepers hoarded it as. It was a *reservoir*. And the world was dying of thirst.

Her contact had sent word that morning, chalked on the underside of a bridge arch in the old script the guild couldn't read: *The carrier is in Grayharbor. Alone. Watchful. Wounded.* And beneath it, the detail that had made Sefra's heart turn over like a boat in a squall: *She visited the drowned chapel. She heard the song.*

So Yara Galloway knew something now. Knew enough to be frightened, or curious, or both. Sefra had spent two weeks assembling the woman from fragments—guild records bought at ruinous price, testimony from a memory-den keeper whose silence cost more than the records, the report of a priestess who had looked into the thief's face and wept.

*A girl with a crooked collarbone scar and nothing behind her eyes. Polite. Careful. Watches exits. Bought soup for a starving child without being asked, then denied doing it.*

And carrying, unknowing, unwaking, the single most sacred object in the history of Sefra's faith.

Tonight she would meet her.

* * *

She found Yara Galloway exactly where the pattern of her said she'd be: high ground with two exits, a rooftop above the fish-market where the chimney smoke hid her silhouette and the tar smell masked her scent from dogs. Sefra didn't sneak. Sneaking would read as threat. She climbed the far side openly, boots loud on the rungs, and stood at the roof's edge with her hands loose at her sides until the thief turned, knife already in her hand, body coiled.

"You're hard to find," Sefra said. "That's a compliment."

"You found me anyway." Yara's voice was flat, watchful. Up close she was smaller than the reports suggested, sharper-boned, with the particular pallor of someone who hadn't slept properly in weeks. Her eyes went over Sefra the way a locksmith eyes a lock—hinges, pins, weaknesses. "Dain send you?"

"Dain." Sefra tasted the name with distaste she didn't bother hiding. "No. He and I are... opposite ends of the same grave. I'm here on my own account."

"Which is?"

"May I sit?" Sefra gestured at the rooftop's low parapet. "I've been walking since dawn, and if we're going to distrust each other, I'd rather do it comfortably."

Something flickered at the corner of Yara's mouth—not quite humor, but the ghost of where humor lived. She didn't lower the knife, but she turned slightly, granting the roof's other half. Permission of a kind.

Sefra sat on the parapet, deliberately exposed, deliberately unthreatening, and looked out over Grayharbor's sprawl: the market's dying lamps, the masts of the night-boats, the far dark line of the sea. Above it all, the moon hung three-quarters full and climbing toward its impossible thirteenth turn.

"You saw the moon charts," she said. "In the Prophecy Vault. Don't look surprised—I know what Corvane assigned you, and I know why. He wanted you to find that date. He wanted you frightened and rooted, a scared animal stays in its burrow. But you're not only frightened, are you? You went looking. You always go looking. It's the one thing about you that isn't guild-trained."

"You've had me investigated."

"I've had you *remembered*," Sefra said. "There's a difference. My family kept records too—different records. Records the guild burned twice and failed both times, because my grandmothers memorized them and sang them into their children. Would you like to hear what our records say about you?"

The knife shifted. "I'd like you to get to the point before I decide you're a problem."

"The point." Sefra turned to face her fully. "The point is that I know what's inside you. Not a metaphor. Not a suspicion. My line has guarded the prophecy of the Thirteenth Turn for a hundred years, and the prophecy says the Salt wakes in a carrier marked before her first breath, and I have spent my whole life learning to feel the god's memory the way you'd feel weather coming. When you walked into that memory-den fourteen days ago, every hair on my arms stood up. You are carrying something older than every nation on this coast, Yara Galloway. Something that was a *god*, once, before two armies made it a casualty."

"And you want it."

"Yes," Sefra said simply, because lies were for people with weaker positions. "I want it enthroned. I want what was stolen from my people restored—not the Salt itself, not the power, though I won't pretend the power means nothing. The *recognition*. My ancestors were slaughtered for worshipping it. My grandmother died in a cell singing its name. The world owes my dead a truth, and the truth is sitting inside a thief who doesn't even know her own childhood."

Yara flinched. Small, fast, controlled—but Sefra had been raised among survivors of atrocities, and she knew every species of flinch. That one was grief.

"You know about my childhood," Yara said.

"I know it wasn't lost. It was *taken*. Purchased, filed, vialled. I know a woman sang a name over a burning city while she pressed something precious into her daughter's swaddling clothes." Sefra paused. "I know the name she sang was yours."

The silence stretched long enough that somewhere below, a drunk began singing a harvest song badly and was shushed by his companions.

"What do you want from me?" Yara asked at last, and her voice had changed—lower, rougher, the flatness cracking to show the hunger underneath. Sefra recognized it. She'd heard it in her own voice, once, in a chapel in Ashfall, asking a dying priestess to tell her one true thing about her mother.

"Nothing tonight," Sefra said. "Only to show you something."

* * *

The beggar was easy to find again—he hadn't gone far, just to the mouth of the fish-market, where he sat on an overturned crate telling his story to anyone who'd listen, and people *were* listening, because joy like his was rarer than coin. His daughter had come. Sefra had watched the reunion from a distance: a broad-shouldered woman in an apron who had struck him once, hard, across the face, and then held him and sobbed into his lime-crusted neck.

Now Sefra led Yara down to the market's edge, keeping to the shadows, and nodded toward the little knot of people around Ollen.

"Three days ago that man had no name, no daughter, no past. The Guild of Ledgers emptied him and sold the contents, and left him sitting in a gutter reaching for a shape that wasn't there anymore." She stepped forward, into the lamplight, and felt Yara follow despite herself. "Watch."

Ollen saw her and rose, pressing his hand to his chest. "The lady. The lady who—" He couldn't finish. His daughter looked between them, bewildered, and Sefra smiled at her gently.

"Your father was robbed," she said. "I gave him back what I could."

Then, because mercy that hides teaches nothing, she took Ollen's hand in front of them all, and opened the sluice wider than she had before, and let the market see.

It was not dramatic. That was the point. No light, no thunder—just an old man straightening, just his face filling in like a sketch being inked, just him turning to his daughter and saying, in a voice gone suddenly precise and present, "*Marta, your mother's loom—the green one—you were going to sell it, don't, don't ever, she'd have hated that*"—and Marta going white, because their mother had been dead nine years and no living soul knew about the loom.

The crowd murmured. Someone dropped to their knees, which embarrassed Sefra more than she could say. She raised Ollen's hand and her own together, the way the old priests had done, and said, loud enough to carry:

"This is what was stolen from all of us. Not gold. *This.*"

* * *

On the walk back toward the rooftops, Yara said nothing for four streets. Sefra let the silence work. Temptation, her grandmother had taught her, was not a matter of argument. Arguments hardened resistance. You showed a person the door and then you *waited*, and you made sure the door was real.

"He'll remember tonight forever," Yara said finally. "What you did. Him and everyone who watched."

"Yes."

"It doesn't drain you? Cost you?"

"Some. Less than you'd think." Sefra glanced sideways. "The god's memory moves through me; it isn't mine to spend. I'm a channel, not a well. Which is more than I can say for your guild—they dammed you up at birth and called the reservoir a vault."

"That's what this is." Yara stopped walking. They stood in the mouth of an alley that smelled of brine and rotting kelp, the sea audible beyond the warehouses, breathing against the shore. "The demonstration. The sermon. You're selling."

"I'm offering."

"You want the Salt. You said so. Everything else is packaging."

"Yes," Sefra agreed. "And here is the offer, plainly, so you can weigh it honestly." She turned to face the thief fully, and let herself speak the words she had rehearsed for two weeks and meant completely. "When the thirteenth turn completes—when the Salt rises in you whether you will it or not—I can give you your childhood. Whole. Every year of it. The burning city and the singing and the swaddle and your mother's face. Not an hour, not a fragment, not a taste from a stolen vial. All of it, restored clean, the way Ollen got his daughter's name. The guild made you a blank page. I can make you a book again."

Yara's breath went out of her slowly, like air from a punctured hull.

"You know about the vial," she whispered. "You know I—"

"I know you broke guild law and drank your own past from a thimble, and it nearly unmade you, and you went back for more and couldn't make yourself." Sefra softened her voice. "I know what a drop does. Imagine the ocean."

For a moment—one long, tide-slow moment—Sefra watched the temptation take hold, watched it move behind Yara's eyes like weather, and felt no triumph at all. Only the old grief of her line, the grief of people who had learned that the truest cruelties arrive dressed as gifts.

"And what does it cost," Yara said, "the ocean."

"Everything you are now," Sefra said honestly. "The woman who built herself out of nothing would be built over. Some of her would survive—memory layers, it doesn't erase, it *burrows*. But you'd wake a different creature. A daughter of a drowned city instead of a blank of the Ledger Guild." She held out her hand, palm up, the way Ollen had sat in the gutter with his palms open. "I won't pretend that's a small price. I'll only tell you what my grandmother told me: a person can live with almost any past. It's the *absence* that kills. Ask yourself which of your selves is the wound—the one they erased, or the one you grew in the erasure."

The sea breathed. Somewhere above them, on the rooftops, the moon slid free of a cloud and laid a pale road across the harbor water, straight as a ledger-line.

Yara looked at the offered hand and did not take it.

"When the thirteenth turn comes," she said slowly, "and the Salt rises in me—and you're certain it will rise—what happens to everyone standing close?"

Sefra hesitated one heartbeat too long.

"That's what I thought," Yara said, and stepped back into the alley's dark. "Find me when you have an answer that isn't a hymn."

She was gone between one lamp and the next, silent as spilled salt, and Sefra stood alone in the brine-smelling dark with her palm still open, hearing her grandmother's voice in the wash of the tide: *Mercy is a door, child. But doors swing both ways, and the girl who refuses a gift may yet refuse a crown—and what will our faith be worth then?*

Above the harbor, the moon climbed another degree toward fullness, and beneath Sefra's feet, faint as a second pulse, something old turned over in its sleep and began, very softly, to hum.
