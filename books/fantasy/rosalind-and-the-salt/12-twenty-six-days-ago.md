## 12. Twenty-Six Days Ago

Sefra's hands were warm.

That was the first thing Yara noticed, and the last ordinary thing. Everyone else who had ever touched her in this city of ledgers and borrowed years had hands like coin—cool, weighed, exchanged. But Sefra took Yara's wrists the way a midwife takes them, or a sister, thumbs finding the pulse points as if she'd done it ten thousand times to ten thousand frightened people, and said, "It will feel like drowning in reverse."

"I've stolen memories," Yara said. "I know what they weigh going in."

"Going in is theft." Sefra's eyes were the grey-green of sea glass, and they did not blink enough. "This is restitution. It is not the same animal at all. A returned memory doesn't settle into you like something new. It remembers where it lived. It goes home angry."

They sat in the back room of a dyer's shop that Sefra's people kept above the tidal flats, where bolts of cloth hung from the rafters like sleeping bats and the air tasted of mordant and brine. Outside, the tide was coming in over the flats with its long wet whisper, and somewhere below the floorboards the drowned chapel waited under thirty feet of green water, patient as everything else in this story.

Yara had told herself she would refuse. She had rehearsed the refusal on the walk over, polished it, made it sharp: *No. I don't want it. Whatever I was, whoever burned—I built myself out of the blank space they left, and I'm not giving that up for an hour of someone else's grief.*

But then Sefra had unstoppered the little clay phial—not guild glass, nothing so clinical—and the smell had reached Yara before any ritual did. Salt water. Woodsmoke underneath. And beneath even that, faint as a name called across a harbor, something sweet and scorched, like honey left too long near a fire.

Her body knew the smell before her mind could object. Her body leaned toward it like a plant toward a window.

"One hour," Sefra said now, folding Yara's fingers around the phial. "That is all I can give safely, and all I will give without your asking twice. The rest of you is still yours until you say otherwise. Drink it, or put it down. Either way, I will think no less of you." A pause, and then, with terrible gentleness: "But I will know what you chose, and so will you."

*Every gift in my life was a leash.* Corvane's kindness, rehearsed as a play. Dain's protection, bladed underneath. She had learned to look for the hook in every offered hand, and here was another one—mercy wearing mercy's face, exactly as she'd feared.

Except.

Except that Sefra wasn't asking for anything. Not yet. The phial sat in Yara's palm, small and warm as a bird's egg, and the only price written on it was the truth.

"I want to know why I'm afraid of the color of certain fires," Yara heard herself say. "I want to know why I can't sleep when the tide goes out. I want—" Her voice cracked and she hated it, hated the hunger showing through like bone through torn skin. "I want to stop being a question."

"Then drink," Sefra said, "and let the question answer. It won't be kind. Truths that have waited twenty-six years rarely are."

Yara drank.

* * *

It began with cold.

Not the cold of the room—the cold of water closing over her head, of being very small and very much underwater, held, actually *held*, cradled against a chest that rose and fell in great deliberate breaths, a woman swimming with a child in her arms through water lit orange from above. Because above there was fire. Above, the city was burning, and the woman was swimming *under* the burning, through the flooded lower streets of a place Yara had never seen and recognized instantly, the way you recognize your own face in a mirror even when the mirror is cracked.

The salt stung her infant eyes. She remembered that. She remembered *being* that—small, furious, alive, screaming soundlessly into her mother's collarbone while the world turned to cinders overhead and her mother swam on, strong and calm, singing.

Singing.

A song in a language Yara didn't know and somehow knew completely, every syllable landing in her like a key into a lock that had been waiting. The song had a name in it. The name was hers. Not *Yara*—that came later, that was Corvane's coin, his leash, his little forged label—but the true one, the first one, sung low and steady against the roar of a dying city: a name that meant, in the grammar of that lost tongue, *the tide keeps what the fire cannot.*

Her mother's face swam into focus above her—young, so young, younger than Yara was now, with ash in her black hair and blood drying at her temple in a line like a seam—and her expression was not terror. That was the worst of it. It was *resolve*, the fierce settled resolve of someone who has already grieved everything except the task in front of her, and the task was this: swim, sing, carry.

Carry what?

The memory tilted, and Yara saw her mother's hands—one arm holding her, the other pressed flat against her own chest, and beneath the palm, beneath the soaked wool of her dress, something was glowing. Faintly. Rhythmically. Like a heartbeat that wasn't hers, or like light trapped in ice.

*They're coming,* said a man's voice, from somewhere behind them in the flooded dark. Another swimmer, another shape. *Maren, they're coming, we can't outrun—*

*We don't outrun,* her mother said, and her voice in the memory was the voice of the song, the same low tide-rhythm. *We choose who carries it. That was always the whole war, Aldric. Both armies thought it was about winning. It was never about winning. It was about who holds it after.*

And then the water shook. Not waves—something deeper, a concussion through the stone itself, and far above, through the burning surface of the flooded street, Yara saw—or the memory saw—a shape against the smoke. Vast. Falling. The god coming down out of its own sky like a lamp dropped from a great height, and the light of it going out in stages, and the screaming—

She remembered the screaming. Twenty-six years old and the screaming was new, was happening *now*, was pouring into ears that had never been allowed to hear it, and it was not the scream of an army or a city. It was the scream of a mind. An entire mind, a mind the size of a coastline, being killed in pieces, and each piece screamed differently, and some of the pieces screamed *her mother's name*—

"Maren," Yara said aloud, in the dyer's back room, in the present, in her own adult body shaking on a stool with Sefra's hands still on her wrists. "Her name was Maren."

But the memory wasn't finished. It gathered itself for the last of the hour, the way a wave gathers before the shore, and gave her the ending:

A cellar. Dry, at last, candlelit, the sounds of the burning muffled to a heartbeat by stone. Her mother kneeling, unwrapping the swaddle, and Yara—baby-Yara, three weeks old, furious and wet-eyed—watched her own mother lift the glowing thing from her own chest and hold it in both cupped hands like water she couldn't spill.

Up close, the Salt was beautiful. That was the trap of it; she understood the trap instantly, viscerally, the way you understand a blade is beautiful right up until it opens you. Crystals like frost grown inside amber, and moving in them—not light, *memory*, whole seasons of memory, centuries of it, a god's long slow thoughts about rain and worship and the specific blue of evening over its own sea. And threaded through all of it, wound in like a dark thread through bright cloth, the war. Two armies. One lie. She saw the lie for half a second—saw whose order had actually burned the eastern quarter, saw which side had broken the truce, saw that both banners had been raised by men who knew—and then her mother bent close, and the glow lit her face from below like a mask of gold, and she spoke directly into baby-Yara's eyes, into the eyes Yara was looking out of now across twenty-six years:

"They will come looking for a vessel. So I make you not a vessel. I make you a *door that has forgotten it is a door.* Sleep, little tide. Forget me. Forgetting me is the safest thing anyone will ever do for you."

Yara came back to herself on the floor.

She didn't remember falling off the stool. She was on the boards of the dyer's back room, curled around her own middle like something trying to keep from spilling, and she was making a sound she had never made before, a low continuous animal keening, and her face was soaked, and Sefra was beside her saying her name—not the true name, mercifully, just *Yara, Yara, breathe*—and outside the tide was going out, and she could hear it, the long withdrawing hiss, and now she knew *why* she had never been able to sleep when the tide went out, because the tide going out sounded like the water leaving the flooded streets, sounded like the moment the fire came down to meet them, sounded like the exact acoustic shape of the worst hour of her life repeating itself forever at the edge of every night.

*Maren.* Her mother had a face. Her mother had ash in her hair and blood in a seam at her temple and a voice like low tide and a name, and Yara had spent twenty-six years not knowing any of it, and now she knew all of it, all at once, permanently, irreversibly, and knowing hurt exactly as much as promised. Exactly. To the drop. Sefra hadn't lied about a single thing, and that was somehow the cruelest part—that the temptation had been honest.

She got her knees under her. She stood up too fast and the room tilted and she caught a hanging bolt of indigo cloth and hung onto it, breathing, while dye-stiff fabric cooled her burning cheek.

"You knew," she said. Her voice came out scraped raw. "You knew what was in that hour. You've given this before. You've watched people come back from it."

"Yes." Sefra hadn't moved from the stool. Her hands rested open on her knees, empty, deliberately empty, and her sea-glass eyes were wet—which surprised Yara more than anything else, that the heir of the worshippers could cry. "My grandmother carried memories of that night too. Fragments. I grew up on the edges of it. I have never once thought of it as a gift, Yara. I think of it as a debt. Your mother trusted our people once. We failed her. This was the smallest installment of what we owe."

"Don't." Yara's fury arrived all at once, fully formed, twenty-six years overdue, and it needed somewhere to go and Sefra was simply *there*. "Don't frame it as debt. Don't make it noble. You gave me that hour because you want the Salt, and the hour was the hook, and I *took the hook*, I drank it like a starving—"

"Yes," Sefra said quietly. "All of that is true. And the hour was also yours, and you were also starving, and I would rather you hate me with your whole history intact than love me as a blank page." She rose, slowly, keeping her hands visible, a diplomat's choreography. "Both things live in the same room. You of all people should know that. You've filed ten thousand vials. Nothing in them is only one thing."

Yara wanted to hit her. She wanted it with a purity she'd never felt, a clean white wanting, and the wanting scared her more than the memory had, because it wasn't hers either—it was older, it came up from the same deep place the Salt slept in, the god's rage or her mother's, inherited anger, anger as inheritance, and if she let it move her hand then Dain was right about her and Sefra was right about her and everyone who had ever called her a vessel would win.

She let go of the cloth. She stepped back. She made her hands be empty.

"The man in the water," she said. "Aldric. He survived?"

"Some of his line did." Something moved behind Sefra's composure, quick and guarded. "Why?"

"Because he saw the lie." Yara's voice steadied as she found the shape of it, the one solid thing in the flood. "In the memory. Half a second—who broke the truce, who burned the eastern quarter. I saw it. It's *in me now*, Sefra, woven into the same hour you gave me, and if your family has spent a century building a religion around that night, I promise you the truth I'm carrying does not match your hymns."

For the first time since Yara had met her, Sefra looked afraid.

"You should rest," Sefra said. "Before you decide what to do with—"

"There's a knock at the street door," Yara said, because there was: three knocks, spaced wrong, too even, the rhythm of someone announcing themselves rather than asking entry. Guild rhythm. Or army rhythm. In this city those had always been the same handwriting.

Through the memory of smoke, absurdly clear, her mother's voice: *they will come looking for a vessel.*

Yara wiped her face with both hands, stood straight, and discovered that fury, properly aimed, was the steadiest ground she had ever stood on.

"Well," she said. "Let's see who came for the door."
