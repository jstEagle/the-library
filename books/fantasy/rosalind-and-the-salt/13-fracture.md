## 13. Fracture

Dain found the letter before he understood it was a letter.

It lay where Yara had left her coat draped over the chair in their rented room above the chandler's shop—a slip of paper, cheap and grey, folded once. He almost didn't open it. He had made a private vow, somewhere in the long weeks of shared roads and shared watches, not to go through her things. It was a strange courtesy between thieves, but they were both thieves, and courtesies were the only currency that never devalued.

But the paper smelled wrong.

Not of ink, not of the tallow smoke that clung to everything in the chandlery district. It smelled of myrrh and brine, of candle-wax burned in a closed room, of something older than either of them. He knew that smell. His family's vault beneath the ash-hills smelled of it—the century-old air that leaked out when he turned the great wheel, carrying the ghost of incense his great-grandfather's soldiers had burned on the night they killed a god.

He opened it with hands that had gone cold without his permission.

The handwriting inside was not Yara's. It slanted the other way, letters tall and narrow as spear-shafts, ink the brownish red of dried wine. *You have seen what she offers,* it read. *I offer more. Come alone. The beggar by the Tallowgate will show you the way.*

No signature. No name. But Dain had grown up on stories of the god's worshippers, the ones who had scattered rather than die, who had kept the faith alive like an ember cupped in two palms for a hundred years. He knew the shape of their hand even if he'd never seen it. Sefra. The heir of the other army. The one whose existence he had hoped was a rumor right up until the night she wasn't.

He sat down slowly on the edge of the bed, the letter trembling in his grip, and did the arithmetic every soldier's child learns early: *when*. When had this come? Not today—he'd been with her since dawn, haggling over passage on a grain barge, watching her lie beautifully to a ship's master about cargo manifests. Yesterday? The day before? How many days had she known, and how many nights had she lain three feet from him in the dark, breathing evenly, pretending sleep, while she decided whether to sell him to the last living remnant of the people his family had broken?

*Come alone.* Had she gone?

He thought of her face across the breakfast table that morning, the way she'd looked at him—too long, too carefully, the way you look at someone you are measuring for a coffin or a crown. He had told himself it was nothing. He had told himself a lot of things lately.

Outside, the gulls were screaming over the fish-market, and the light was going the color of old pewter, and somewhere in the city a bell rang the hour with a crack in its voice.

Yara came back at dusk.

She came in wet from a rain that had started and stopped twice, shaking droplets from her hair, and she was talking before the door shut behind her—"The barge leaves on the morning tide, I got us berths, the master wanted double but I convinced him we're spice-factors out of—" and then she saw the letter in his hand, unfolded, and stopped as though she'd walked into glass.

For a moment neither of them moved. Rain ticked against the window. Downstairs, the chandler's boy was singing tunelessly to himself over the rendering vats.

"Where did you get that?" Yara said quietly.

"It was in your coat." He stood. "Which is a question I'll allow you to answer after you answer mine. Which is: how long?"

"How long what?"

"Don't." His voice cracked on it, and he hated that, hated the boyish crack in it, hated that she could hear how much this cost him. "Don't do the thing where you make me feel stupid for asking. How long have you known about her? About Sefra?"

Yara set down her satchel. She did it carefully, the way she did everything, and he realized with a fresh wave of fury that she was buying time, arranging her face, doing to him right now exactly what she did to merchants and guildsmen—managing the mark. Except he wasn't a mark. He was supposed to be—

What? He didn't finish the thought. That was the trouble. Neither of them had ever finished it.

"She found me," Yara said. "Four days ago."

"Four days." He laughed, and there was no humor in it, only a kind of stunned pain. "Four days. We've slept in the same room for four days. We've planned our route for four days. And you've been sitting on the fact that the other heir to the Salt has been making you offers behind my back."

"She hasn't been behind your back. She's been behind *yours*, Dain. Your family's vault is full of weapons keyed to the thing sleeping in my head. You think I don't know what happens if you change your mind about destroying it? You think I don't lie awake doing that arithmetic?" She stepped closer, and her voice dropped low and hard. "I met her because I wanted to see her face when she lied to me. That's all. That's tradecraft, and you know it is."

"And did you see it? Her face when she lied?"

A pause. One heartbeat too long.

"That's what I thought," Dain said.

"She didn't lie," Yara said, and now her voice had gone strange, quieter, which was worse than shouting. "That's the problem. She offered to give me back my childhood. An hour of it, to start. My mother's face. My own name spoken by someone who loved me. And she could do it, Dain. She put her hand on a beggar's head in the Tallowgate and gave him back twenty years the guild took, and I watched him weep, and it was real. Every word of it was real. Can you offer me anything like that? Can your family's rusted arsenal give me back one minute of who I was?"

"No," Dain said. "I can offer you the truth, which is that everything she gives you will have a hook in it. Mercy with a hook. That's how worshippers work—that's how *gods* work. They don't take. They give, and give, and give, until you owe them your whole soul and call it gratitude."

"You sound like Corvane."

He flinched as if she'd struck him. The name landed between them and lay there, ugly, and he watched her realize what she'd said, watched her decide not to take it back.

"My family killed a god," he said, very softly. "Whatever else is true, whatever lies my bloodline told itself to sleep at night—we ended it. We ended the war. And now the war wants to start again inside the one person I—" He stopped.

"The one person you what?"

Silence. The rain filled it. Somewhere below, the chandler's boy had stopped singing.

"The one person you what, Dain?" Yara asked again, and her eyes were bright and terrible. "Say it. Say the word. Because if you can't say it, then maybe there's nothing here to betray, and then this letter doesn't matter, and none of it matters, and we're just two heirs circling a corpse waiting to see which of us gets hungry first."

"I trusted you," he said, because he couldn't say the other thing, because the other thing would be a rope around both their necks, and he had watched enough people hang to know better. "And you let me walk into the Tallowgate district blind. If her people had decided to take you—if they'd decided to take *me*—"

"They didn't."

"This time."

"Yes. This time." She dragged a hand through her damp hair, and for a moment she looked so tired, so young, so much like the orphan the ledger said she was, that his anger wavered. "Dain. Listen to me. I went to weigh her. Nothing more. I needed to know if she was another leash, and she is, she absolutely is, but she's a leash made of true things, and that makes her more dangerous than Corvane ever was. I came back. Doesn't that count?"

"Coming back isn't trust. Coming back is just returning."

The words were out before he could catch them, cruel and precise, and he watched them land. Watched her go still in that particular way she had—not soft stillness but the stillness of a blade being sheathed, decision folding down into silence.

"All right," Yara said.

"All right what?"

"All right." She picked up her satchel. "You want to keep score, keep score. But count honestly, Dain. Count the vault full of weapons your family built to hold a dead god's power. Count the letter your grandfather wrote ordering the chapel burned with everyone inside. Count it all, and then tell me which of us inherited the betrayal." She paused at the door, hand on the latch, and did not look back. "The barge leaves on the morning tide. There's a berth for you if you want it. I won't ask twice."

The door closed behind her with a click far too gentle for what it ended.

Dain stood alone in the lamplight, the letter still in his fist, creased now where his grip had crushed it. Slowly he opened his hand and smoothed the paper flat against his thigh, again and again, a soldier's pointless ritual, straightening what could not be straightened.

Then he noticed the second fold. Inner. The part he hadn't read.

He opened it with numb fingers.

*If he follows you,* Sefra had written, in that tall spear-slanted hand, *tell him the vault remembers him. Tell him the weapons already know his name.*

* * *

Yara walked the wet streets for hours without deciding to.

Her feet chose the route, down through the chandlery terraces, past the tar-works where the air tasted of smoke and salt, along the sea-wall where the water slapped stone in a rhythm almost like speech. She walked because walking was the one thing that had never betrayed her. Legs didn't lie. Legs just carried you away.

*Coming back is just returning.*

She turned the sentence over and over, looking for the wound in it, and found instead that it was true, and that was the wound. What had she expected—that coming back through his door would mean something? Trust was a ledger entry, and ledgers could be forged. She had spent her whole life proof against that truth and tonight it cut her anyway, which meant some part of her had stopped keeping books. Some part of her had believed.

Foolish. Dangerous. The Salt was waking inside her; she could feel it the way you feel weather changing, a pressure behind the eyes, a taste of deep water at the back of the throat. She could not afford belief. Belief was a door, and doors opened from both sides.

At the mouth of the harbor, an old mirror leaned against a wall outside a glazier's shop, cracked diagonally, set out with the rubbish for morning collection. Yara stopped in front of it without meaning to. Habit. Thieves check reflections; it's how you spot the watcher behind you.

The woman in the glass was rain-soaked and hollow-eyed, and she was Yara, and she was not.

It happened between one heartbeat and the next. The face flickered—like a candle guttering, like a page turning—and for half a breath the reflection was older. Grey threaded through dark hair. Lines carved deep around the mouth, the kind of lines that come from decades of speaking words that mattered. Eyes that had seen a war from above and never stopped seeing it. The stranger looked out at Yara with an expression of vast, exhausted tenderness, the way you look at a child asleep in a burning house, and her lips moved.

No sound came. But Yara heard it anyway, felt it in the salt-water hollow of her skull, in the place where the drowned chapel's song had lodged and never left:

*Let me carry it awhile. You look so tired.*

Yara stumbled back off the curb. A cart rattled past; the driver cursed her. When she dared look again, the mirror held only a rain-soaked thief with a crooked collarbone scar and no business standing in the street at midnight staring at garbage.

But her hands were shaking, and the taste of the sea wouldn't leave her tongue, and somewhere beneath her breastbone something enormous turned over in its sleep and settled closer to the surface.

*You look so tired.*

"I'm not tired," she said aloud, to no one, to the rain, to the thing wearing her face from the inside. "I'm not anything. That's the point of me."

The night gave no answer. But as she turned toward the harbor, toward the barge, toward the berth she'd bought for two and might now sail alone, Yara caught the smell of myrrh and brine drifting on the wind from the Tallowgate—and beneath it, faint and patient, the sound of a woman singing a name.

Hers.

Sefra had said *come back anytime.* The priestess's song had said it too. And now, worst of all, the thing in the mirror had learned to say it in her own voice.

One night remained before the tide turned. Twenty-six days, the charts had said, and the charts did not lie—but standing at the sea-wall with the water black and waiting below her, Yara understood suddenly that the counting had never been hers to do. Something in her had begun counting on its own.

She pressed her hand flat against her sternum, feeling the slow, tidal pull beneath the skin, and whispered the only prayer she knew, the thief's prayer, the guild's first lesson:

*Mine stays mine.*

Deep inside, patient as the sea, something answered: *Nothing stays. Everything returns to the water. Even you, little ledger. Especially you.*
