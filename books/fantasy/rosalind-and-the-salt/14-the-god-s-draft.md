## 14. The God's Draft

Sleep took Yara the way the tide takes a sandcastle: gradually, then all at once.

She had meant to stay awake. She had lit three candles, wedged a chair beneath the door handle of her rented room, and set Dain's knife on the table where she could see it—a black sliver of iron that drank the candlelight instead of reflecting it. She had told herself she would not close her eyes until dawn, because for two nights now her dreams had been wrong, thick with salt and the smell of smoke that wasn't hers, and because Sefra's restored hour still lived in her head like a coal someone had buried but not extinguished: her mother's hands, the burning city, the weight pressed into her swaddle with a blessing she could not pronounce.

But sleep is patient, and thieves know better than anyone that you cannot guard against what is already inside the house.

She dreamed first of standing.

That was all, at first—just the sensation of being upright somewhere vast, on a floor that was not a floor, under a sky that was not a sky. The dark around her was not empty. It was *occupied*. It pressed against her skin like deep water, warm and enormous and breathing, and when she turned—and turning took a long time, as though her body were a ship coming about—the dark turned with her, attentive.

*You are small,* said a voice that did not come from any direction, or rather came from every direction at once, the way heat comes from a fire. It was not a cruel voice. That was the first thing Yara understood, and it unsettled her more than cruelty would have. It was a tired voice. It was the voice of someone who has been carrying something up a very long stair and has finally, finally reached the landing.

*Yes,* Yara said, and was surprised to hear her own voice work here, rough with sleep. *And you're not.*

A sound moved through the dark that might have been laughter, if laughter could be worn smooth by centuries. *Small things say that word like an accusation. I have heard it said as a prayer, as a curse, as a battle cry. Never before as a fact offered politely across a room.*

"Where are we?"

*Nowhere. Everywhere your kind keeps me. In ledgers. In songs no one remembers the words to. In the salt cellars of old houses. In the oath soldiers swear without knowing why their grandfathers' grandfathers chose those particular words.* The dark shifted, gathered. *But mostly, tonight, we are in you. Forgive the crowding. I have been folded very small for a hundred years, and you are the first room I've had with a window.*

Yara looked down and found she had hands—her own hands, the ink-stained knuckles, the burn scar on her left thumb from her first week filing vials. She looked up and found stars. They hung above her in their thousands, but they were arranged wrong, too dense near the horizon, and as her eyes adjusted she understood that they were not stars. They were fires. A coastline of them, curving away into distance on both sides, and below each fire a city, and between the cities a land torn open like a seam ripped out of cloth.

"Oh," she said quietly.

*Yes,* the god said—or the memory of the god; she could no longer feel a difference, and suspected there was none left to feel. *I want to show you something. Not to convince you. I am past convincing anyone. But you have been handed my story in pieces, by people who each kept the pieces they liked. Let me give you the whole cloth, once, and let it be heavy in your hands.*

* * *

The war began, and it began twice.

The first time, Yara saw it from the ground—from the height of a woman walking a road with grain in baskets on her back, dust gold in the evening light, singing. Yara knew the song before she heard it. She had tasted it in a vial drop in a locked archive room: salt water and smoke and a name sung low. Her own throat tightened around a melody she'd never learned.

*This is how it started,* the tired voice said, gentle as a hand on the shoulder. *With a harvest song. Your mother's grandmother sang it, and hers before her. We were a small thing, then. A god of the tide-line, of the place where water gives back what it takes. Fishermen's luck. Safe returns. The naming of children so the sea would know them and be slow to collect its debts.*

The woman on the road walked toward a city built of white stone and green copper roofs, and the city rang bells for her, and Yara felt—borrowed, secondhand, but real—the warmth of being expected somewhere. Of belonging to a place that knew her name.

*Then come the other ones,* the voice said. *Come see how it looked from their side.*

The world tilted, sickeningly, and Yara stood on a hill among men in grey wool, looking down at the same coast, the same white city—but from here the bells sounded different. From here the green copper roofs gleamed like scales. From here, an officer was pointing at the temples along the shore and saying words that made her blood run cold with their familiarity, because she had read them in guild archives, in heirloom letters, in Dain's family vault inscriptions: *they hoard the divine. They claim the tide answers to them alone. They will not share the shore.*

*A lie?* Yara asked.

*Half a one,* the god said, and there was something in the tired voice like an old wound being touched. *Lies that last are always half true. Yes, the priests claimed me jealously. Yes, they charged for blessings I never priced. And yes, the other army wanted what the temples held—not the god, mind you, gods are heavy luggage—but the *authority*. The right to say who the sea belongs to. Each side saw truly through one eye and blindly through the other, and each eye fed a different hunger, and the two hungers marched toward the same beach.*

She watched it happen. That was the worst of it—there was no skipping, no mercy of montage. She stood in the surf line as the grey wool came down the hills at dawn, and she stood in the white towers as the bells changed from festival peals to warning, and she stood—this was unbearable—in a granary with a boy of maybe nine years who was hiding between sacks while outside men argued over him in two languages, both sides insisting he belonged to them, both meaning it kindly, both willing to kill for the kindness.

*I tried to stop it,* the god said, and for the first time the tiredness cracked and something raw showed underneath, something that in a human face Yara would have called shame. *Understand what I was. Small. Local. I could quiet a storm in one bay. I could not stop an army on a road. So I did the only thing left to a small god. I let myself be taken.*

* * *

The scene changed, and now Yara stood in a temple that was already half rubble, watching through eyes that were not her own—wider, older, seeing heat and intention as much as light. Soldiers filled the sanctuary. Priests lay where they had fallen. And in the center, on a stone that hummed, a shape of white crystal sat in a basin, catching torchlight, throwing it back in slow moving rainbows.

Her chest ached. She recognized it the way you recognize your own handwriting.

*They came for the authority,* the god said, from inside its own memory. *And I gave them me instead. Do you understand the difference? Authority can be inherited. It passes hand to hand, crown to crown, and every hand that holds it believes it holds a right. But a *memory*—a memory must be carried. It seeps into whoever carries it. It changes the flavor of their thoughts. I thought: let them take the burden. Let every heir who reaches for my power find instead a hundred years of grief, and let the grief slow them down.*

The crystal rose from the basin. Hands lifted it—Dain's ancestors, grey wool and grim faces, reverent despite themselves. Yara felt the moment of contact from the god's side: the sudden crushing intimacy of a human mind, all its small fears and large loves pouring in, the soldier carrying the crystal weeping without knowing why, dreaming that night of harvest songs.

*It almost worked,* the god said softly. *For three generations, it worked. The heirs kept me locked away because being near me hurt. Grief is a fine cage, when the prisoner builds it. But cages rust. Heirs forget why the vault is cold. And then someone decided the grief was a message—that I was *asking* for something—and began to wonder what.*

*And your worshippers?* Yara asked. The question scraped going down. *Sefra's line. They wanted you free.*

*They wanted me *back*.* The word landed with weight. *Do not mistake them for gentler hands, child. Love can grip as hard as greed. Sefra's ancestors sang my songs while they sharpened knives. They believed the world had been broken since I fell silent—tides gone strange, fish gone scarce, deaths unluckier than they should be—and that restoring me would restore the old order, the old certainties, the old names. What is faith, at the far end, but a demand that the past return?*

*And what do you want?*

The dark went very still around her. Even the distant burning coastline seemed to hold its breath.

*To be remembered,* the god said. *Only that. You must believe me—I know how it sounds. Every tyrant says his appetite is modest. Every hungry thing calls its hunger holy. But listen:* and the voice bent close, not threatening, pleading, which was somehow worse, *I do not ask to live. I do not ask for thrones or temples or the tide-line back. I ask that when I end, someone knows what ended. A hundred years I have been a rumor, a weapon, a treasure, a prophecy. Not one soul in a century has simply *mourned* me. Is that so much? To be grieved by name, once, before the forgetting takes me?*

Yara stood in the warm dark with her own hands shaking and understood, with terrible clarity, that this was the trap—not the offer, but the sympathy. Because the grief was genuine. She could feel it, the way she felt everything she stole: whole, unfiltered, true. A small dead god, tired beyond measuring, asking only for a mourner. And every instinct she had—the instincts of a thief, of a girl raised on ledgers, who knew that the most valuable things are the ones people don't know they're giving away—every instinct screamed that this was exactly how the Salt would make itself loved.

"You're asking me to open the door," she said slowly. "Not to a king. To a mourner. But the door doesn't know the difference. Whatever walks through it, walks through *whole*."

*Yes,* the god admitted, without flinching. *I cannot enter partially. Memory does not knock and leave its boots at the threshold. If you mourn me, you carry me. If you carry me, you become me—at least enough. Enough to matter. Enough to be fought over again.* A long pause, and then, quieter: *I tell you this honestly because I am too tired to lie well anymore, and because honesty is the last luxury of the dying. I would rather be refused knowingly than accepted blind. But I will not pretend I hope for refusal.*

* * *

The dream loosened after that, the way a fist opens finger by finger.

The coastline of fires dimmed. The warm dark receded to a comfortable distance, no longer pressing. Yara found herself standing on what was unmistakably now a shore—grey sand, grey water, the honest grey of early morning—and the god's presence settled behind her like an old dog lying down at the end of a long walk.

*One more thing,* it said. *Since you were brave enough to ask what I want, I will be brave enough to show you what I fear.*

The sea before her parted—not dramatically, not with walls of water, but the way a crowd parts, reluctantly, showing what everyone had been pretending not to look at. On the wet sand lay a ledger. Just a ledger, guild-issue, cracked leather, swollen with damp. She knew without touching it whose entries filled it: every theft she'd ever made, filed in her own neat hand. And beside it lay a second book, identical, except that every page was blank except one line on the first page, written in handwriting she was only now beginning to recognize as her own:

*What power wants is to be wanted.*

*The heirs think they want me,* the god said. *Corvane thinks he wants me contained. You think you want to be rid of me. But watch the ledger, child. Every plan anyone makes ends the same way—with someone's hand on the crystal. Mine included. Especially mine. Even my mourning requires a carrier. Even my ending requires a witness. I have spent a century learning that there is no version of this story where I am not held. There is only the choosing of hands.*

"And if no hand chooses?"

The tired voice was silent for so long she thought the dream might be ending. When it spoke again, it sounded almost young—startled, the way the very old are startled by genuinely new ideas.

*Then,* it said slowly, tasting the possibility like a foreign word, *I suppose I would be weather. Tide. The thing that happens to everyone and belongs to no one. I had not... I did not know that was a door.*

"It is," Yara said. "It's the only one I'm looking at."

* * *

She woke with salt drying stiff on her cheeks and did not remember crying.

Grey dawn light came through the shutters in slats. The candles had drowned themselves in their own wax. The knife lay untouched on the table, and beneath her window the city was waking—cart wheels, a fishmonger's cry, the ordinary machinery of a morning that did not know what it held.

Yara sat up and pressed her palms against her eyes until sparks bloomed, trying to hold the dream's shape. Already it was fraying at the edges, the way dreams do—but the center held. The war seen from above. Two armies, one lie cut in half and shared between them. The boy in the granary. The soldier weeping over a crystal he didn't understand. *Let them take the burden.*

And underneath it all, worn smooth as sea glass: *to be remembered, only that.*

She got up, splashed water on her face from the basin, and caught her reflection flickering—just at the edge of vision, just for a heartbeat—an older face laid over hers like a watermark, patient and grieving and vast. She did not gasp. She was past gasping. She looked at it directly until it faded, and spoke aloud to the empty room, to the thing wearing her like a borrowed coat:

"I heard you. All of it. And I'm not done deciding."

But even as she said it, her eyes went to the window—to the east, where beyond the rooftops the guildhall's spire caught the first sun, and where, she now understood with a clarity that felt like cold water, Corvane would be waking too. Kind, rehearsed Corvane, who had bought a baby and called it rescue, who had spent twenty years feeding her just enough self to keep her useful and no more. He had sent her to the Prophecy Vault. He had recalled her papers. Every leash had been measured to fit.

And today, if the tremor running through the streets was anything more than morning noise—if the crowds gathering at the market gates with torches unlit and voices rising were what she suddenly feared they were—then the leash was about to be pulled from the other end.

Down in the square, someone began to shout a name. It was not hers.

It was Corvane's.
