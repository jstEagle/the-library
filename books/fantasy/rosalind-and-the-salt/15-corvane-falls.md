## 15. Corvane Falls

The bells began at dusk, and they were wrong.

Guildhall bells rang in patterns—quarter-hours, shift-changes, the soft triple note that meant a ledger had closed. These bells were being struck by hand, hard and arrhythmic, someone hammering out a warning with no grammar to it. Yara stood at her window on the fourth floor of the dormitory wing and watched torchlight crawl up the Processional like a wound reopening.

She had known this was coming. She had told herself she'd known it, ever since Sefra's followers first appeared in the fish markets wearing grey scarves and asking gentle questions of the guild's debtors. Ever since the beggar outside the Tallow Gate had wept in the street, suddenly remembering his daughter's face after thirty blank years, and the crowd around him had gone quiet in a way that quiet never goes in cities—attentive, hungry, dangerous.

Memory was the guild's coin. And Sefra had just made it rain.

Yara dressed quickly, in dark clothes, and did the thing she had done ten thousand times without thinking: she checked her sleeves for vials. Her fingers found the two she carried now—the priestess's song, saved from the chapel raid, and one more she could not bring herself to name even privately. Then she went down into the burning house of her childhood.

* * *

The great hall smelled of smoke and old paper, which was its ordinary smell, though tonight the paper smell had an edge of panic to it. Clerks ran the galleries with armloads of ledgers, feeding them into the iron stove that usually held only the guild's ceremonial fire. Corvane's order, she heard someone gasp—burn the debt records, burn the purchase records, burn—

Burn the evidence.

Yara stopped on the stairs and watched a junior clerk feed pages into the flames. She recognized the format of the topmost sheet even at this distance: the long columns, the buyer's signature block, the small cold notation of price per unit. She had audited hundreds of those entries. She had filed them. She had believed, in the way you believe the floor will hold, that the entries recorded orphans *found*.

The crowd reached the doors before the stove reached the bottom of the crate.

They did not break them down. That was the terrible part, Yara thought later—they didn't have to. The doors opened from inside, because half the guild's own staff had decided, sometime in the last week, whose side the truth was on. The grey-scarved people came in not as invaders but as auditors, carrying their own ledgers: broadsheets printed on cheap paper, lists of names, dates, prices. Someone had copied the purchase records before Corvane thought to burn them. Someone had always been copying.

"Yara Galloway." A woman in a grey scarf stepped into her path on the gallery. Not Sefra—Sefra would come later, or wouldn't; this was one of the rank and file, a laundress by her hands, a mother by her voice. "You're the thief. The one who takes memories."

"I take what people sell," Yara said, and heard how the sentence sounded in this room, tonight, and could not finish believing it herself.

"My son sold three years to your guild. To pay my lung-fever debts." The woman's eyes were dry, which was worse than weeping. "He doesn't remember I taught him his letters. He stands in my kitchen like a polite stranger. Where is it? Where do you keep him?"

"In the vaults." Yara's voice came out level. Competence, her oldest armor. "Sealed, indexed, intact. Nothing decays in storage. He can have every year back."

"And who pays for that?" the laundress asked softly. "Who pays the guild's fee to give a man his own mother's face?"

Yara had no answer. She had never once, in all her years of lifting decades from dying merchants and desperate fathers, been asked where the memory went afterward. People assumed it was destroyed. It was stored. Catalogued. Priced. Somewhere below her feet, in the dark, three years of a boy learning his letters sat in a vial worth more than his mother's house.

The crowd surged past them toward the master's dais, and the laundress let her go, which felt less like mercy than like being set down among things too heavy to carry.

* * *

Corvane stood on the dais with his hands open at his sides, the way he stood when he was about to be reasonable.

Yara had seen that posture a hundred times. Over contract disputes, over enforcer discipline, over the year she'd begged him to tell her anything about her origins and he had folded his hands exactly so and said, *Yara, some questions cost more than the answers are worth, and I say that because I love you.* She had thought it was wisdom. Standing in the gallery now, watching the same gesture fail against a crowd of four hundred people holding printed lists, she saw it for what it had always been: a technique. Kindness, rehearsed until it shone.

"You are owed," Corvane was saying, and his voice carried the way it always carried, warm as a banked fire. "Every one of you is owed, and I will not insult you by denying it. Yes—I bought children. Yes, I signed those ledgers. I did it in a city full of war orphans starving in doorways, when no orphanage would take a child marked by the Salt War, when the temples turned them away because their dreams frightened the other foundlings. I gave them beds. I gave them trade. I gave them—" his eyes found Yara in the gallery, deliberately, a man spending his last coin, "—I gave them names."

"He gave us numbers," someone shouted. "Check the ledgers! G-114 through G-131! We were stock!"

"The ledgers also record every meal—"

"The ledgers record the *price*!"

It went fast after that. Yara had watched crowds before, from the safe height of guild business, and knew they moved like weather—slowly, then all at once. The front ranks pressed the dais steps. Enforcers looked to Corvane for orders and received only that open-handed reasonableness, and one by one they stepped aside, because enforcers were guild-raised too, and most of them had numbers instead of names somewhere in their earliest files.

They did not kill him. Yara kept waiting for the moment the crowd became a mob, and it never quite came, and somehow that was worse. They stripped his chains of office. They took his rings—each one a signet of some archive or annex, each one leaving a pale groove in flesh that had worn it for forty years. They took his coat. An old clerk, a man Yara had seen bowing to Corvane for two decades, spat on the dais boards in front of him, precisely, like a man completing a transaction.

And then they left him there, in his shirtsleeves, amid the smoke-smell and the scattered ash of burned ledgers, and went looking for the vaults.

Corvane stood alone for a long moment. Then he climbed down from the dais—not fleeing, walking, unhurried, a man crossing his own entrance hall—and came to the foot of the gallery stairs where Yara stood.

Up close he looked smaller. That was the thing no one warned you about tyrants: the flesh gave out before the power did. His hands, bare of rings, seemed suddenly like an old man's hands.

"They'll open the vaults," he said quietly. "Everything we kept. Every debt, every purchase, every theft dressed as charity. It will all be read aloud in the square within a month." He smiled, and it was almost the real smile, the one from the winter nights when he'd brought her sweet tea and taught her cipher-work by candlelight. "I'm not telling you to save me, Yara. I'm telling you what comes next, because you're the only one here who understands ledgers. When everything is known, nothing is forgivable. You've spent your life among memories—you know what remembering does. It doesn't heal. It *prices*."

"You're asking me to steal their anger," Yara said. "Lift it out of them. Make them forget what you did."

"I'm asking you to remember me kindly." His voice cracked on *kindly*, just slightly, a flaw in the glaze. "That's all. One mind. Yours. You're the finest thief the guild ever trained—if anyone can choose what to keep, it's you. Keep the tea. Keep the ciphers. Keep the man who found a frozen child on the Salt Road and named her after his own dead mother." He laughed, short and awful. "I chose that name for you deliberately, you know. Rosalind means *one who grieves beautifully*. I wanted—"

"I know what it means." She had looked it up herself, years ago, in a stolen hour, hoping it was a clue to a family. It was only another of his possessions.

"Then keep that," he said. "Keep something. Everyone else will remember me correctly. I need one person—one—to remember me *wrong*, kindly wrong, the way you remember the dead you loved. Do this and I'll tell you where your original entry is filed. The true one. The one under the forged finding-record. I bought you too, Yara. I know exactly what I paid."

The hall roared behind him—the vault doors, opening. Torchlight and shouting and the sound of ten thousand small glass deaths being carried toward daylight.

And Yara, who had spent her whole life taking things from other people's heads, reached out and did the one theft she had sworn never to perform: she reached into her own.

Not with a vial. With attention. She went down through her own remembered Corvanes—the tea, the ciphers, the hand on her shoulder after her first successful lift, saying *you did well, child*—and she looked at each one honestly, the way she'd been unable to look at the prophecy, the way she'd been unable to look at her own file. She found the kindness in every memory, and she found the leash attached to it, and she understood that they had never been separate things. There had never been a kind Corvane and a cruel Corvane. There had been a man who purchased children and raised them as assets, and the purchasing and the raising were the same act, performed with the same hands, warmed by the same hearth.

She could not take the kindness out without taking the man.

So she kept it. All of it. The tea and the ciphers and the shoulder-pat and the frozen child on the Salt Road—and she held all of it in her mind at once, together with the ledgers, the numbers, the price per unit, and she let the two truths fuse into one object, one memory of one man, complete and correct and unbearable.

"Yes," she said aloud. "I'll remember you kindly."

His face eased, and that ease was the worst thing she had ever caused, because she hadn't lied. That was the corruption of it, she realized—that pity had gotten inside her the way the Salt got inside her, quietly, through an unlocked door. She pitied him. Under the fury, under the grief, some traitorous chamber of her heart had already forgiven him, the way you forgive weather, the way you forgive a fever that raised you as much as it burned you. He had built that forgiveness into her the way he'd built everything else—patiently, over years, as an investment.

"Now," he said, steadying himself on the newel post, "my entry. Your entry. Sub-vault nine, under the false floor of the finding-records, sealed with my personal cipher. Take it and run, girl. Whatever Sefra promised you, whatever the god whispers—it's all in that file, and none of it is what any of them think."

Behind him, the crowd came back up out of the vault stairwells carrying vials by the hundred, cradling them like infants, weeping over labels. The laundress was somewhere down there, buying back her son's letters with nothing, because tonight nothing was finally the right price.

Corvane walked past Yara toward the service door, small and ringless in his shirtsleeves, and paused beside her the way he used to pause at her dormitory door on winter nights.

"You did well, child," he said.

She hated him. She hated him with a completeness that should have burned the pity out of her, and it did not, and she understood then that this was the last thing he had stolen: not her childhood, not her name, but her ability to hate him cleanly. Even her hatred came pre-mixed with love, like everything else he'd ever given her.

She went down into the vaults to find sub-vault nine, and the true file, and whatever waited beneath the false floor of her own beginning—while above her, the guildhall bells rang on, struck by strangers' hands, keeping no time the guild had ever taught.
