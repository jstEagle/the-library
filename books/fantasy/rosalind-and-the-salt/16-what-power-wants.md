## 16. What Power Wants

The guildhall burned for three days, and on the fourth morning the smoke finally thinned enough to show the sky, and the sky was the color of a bruise going yellow at the edges—dawn coming anyway, indifferent as arithmetic.

Yara stood in the archive's ruins with ash sifting down around her like gray snow that had forgotten which way was up. The great ledgers had not burned. That was the joke of it, the ugly punchline she kept turning over: paper soaked in salt-water tanning for a century does not burn easily. The guild had built its vaults to survive fire because fire was what thieves used. No one had ever planned for the people.

Shelves lay toppled in rows like felled trees. Vials glittered among the wreckage by the thousand, each one holding someone's wedding day, someone's mother's voice, someone's last afternoon before a fever took them. The enforcers were gone—scattered, or dead, or wearing plain coats now and walking quickly with their eyes down. The city outside had that particular hush of a place between rulers, the held breath before the next hand closes.

And inside her, the Salt sang.

It had been singing since Corvane fell. Not words yet, not quite—a pressure behind her sternum like tide against a sea wall, rising a little more each hour, patient in the way only very old things are patient. When she closed her eyes she could feel it mapping her, learning the shape of her want the way water learns the shape of a cup.

*You don't have any wants,* it seemed to say, gently, terribly. *That's the saddest thing about you. Let me give you one.*

She pressed her palm flat against a fallen shelf until the splinters bit. Pain helped. Pain was hers, at least. Everything else felt borrowed now—even her grief for Corvane, even her hatred of him, both of them tangled together like rope that had been knotted too long to separate. She had remembered him kindly when he begged her to, standing in the mob with his fine coat torn and his hands raised, and she had done it deliberately, a final theft in reverse: she had given him a good memory of himself in her head, and she would carry it forever, and she hated him for making her carry it, and she hated herself for the small warm pity that had risen in her throat like bile.

*He bought you,* she reminded herself. *A ledger entry. Ink and coin.*

*Yes,* said the Salt, mild as milk. *And who taught you to read?*

She walked out through the broken doors into the city that no longer had a guild, and the Salt hummed its tide-song all the way down the stairs.

* * *

Dain found her at midday, or perhaps she found him; afterward she could never be sure which of them had been walking toward the other. He was waiting under the awning of a shuttered chandlery, out of the ash-fall, dressed plainer than she'd ever seen him—no family signet, no blade at his hip, just a man in a gray coat with tired eyes and a week of stubble. The charm was still there, but worn thin, like gilt rubbed off a frame.

"You heard," he said.

"Everyone heard. Your family vault is a smoking hole in a hillside."

"Sefra's people." He said it without heat, which frightened Yara more than rage would have. "They didn't take anything. They just burned it. Weapons keyed to the Salt—weapons that could *hold* it—and they burned them rather than let them exist. Do you understand what that means?"

"It means she's not planning to destroy it."

"It means she's planning to *use* it, and she doesn't want anyone able to stop her after." Dain looked at his own hands as if checking they were still empty. "I came to tell you I'm done. My whole line spent a century keeping those weapons oiled for this night, and I watched them burn in an hour, and do you know what I felt?" He laughed, short and awful. "*Relief.* And then I felt the other thing underneath the relief, the thing I've been carrying since I first cornered you in that memory-den. I wanted them. Even knowing what they'd make me. Some part of me has wanted them since I was a boy hearing the war-songs."

Yara studied him. Under the Salt's rising hum, her perception had changed—she noticed things now the way a ledger notices entries, everything legible, nothing hidden. She saw the tremor in his left hand he couldn't still. She saw that he had come alone, truly alone, no watchers on the rooftops. And she saw, beneath all of it, the want sitting in him like a coal that had never gone out: not greed exactly. *Significance.* A hundred years of his family being the ones who killed a god, and none of them ever getting to hold what they'd won.

"Dain," she said carefully. "What do you actually want? Right now, today, if the Salt weren't part of it."

He opened his mouth. Closed it. And the silence stretched long enough that she understood something was happening to him in real time—the Salt reaching through her, or through the air itself, offering him the answer to a question he'd never been allowed to ask.

Because then he said, wonderingly, "My father's approval. That's it. That's the whole rotten thing. If I brought the Salt home—even to destroy it, even *especially* to destroy it—I'd finally be worth the name." His jaw tightened. "Gods. It's been my father this entire time. A hundred years of prophecy and it comes down to a dead man's disappointment."

"That's what it does," Yara said quietly. "It finds the smallest want and builds a cathedral on it."

"Is that what it's showing you?"

She almost lied. The lie rose ready-made—that the Salt showed her nothing, that she was a locked door. But the tide inside her chest pushed up against her ribs, and she was so tired, and there was something obscene about lying to a man who had just handed her his worst truth.

"It shows me my childhood," she said. "Whole. Every hour of it. All I have to do is keep it instead of letting it go. It knows that's the one thing I can't refuse forever."

"And can you?"

"I don't know." The honesty came out of her like blood from a cut. "Ask me tomorrow."

Dain nodded slowly. Then he did the thing she would think about for days afterward: he turned out his pockets, laid his knife on the cobbles between them, and stepped back from it.

"I'm not giving you my oath," he said. "Oaths are just wants wearing uniforms. I'm telling you where I'll be tonight—the old chapel road, the drowned church. Sefra summoned us both. You got the letter too; don't pretend you didn't." He picked up his knife, hesitated, and left it on the stones after all. "If I reach for it—if I reach for *you*—I want you to know I came here first and put the blade down. Whatever happens after, remember that part. Remember that I tried."

He walked away into the ash-light, and Yara stood a long moment looking at the knife on the cobbles, and the Salt murmured inside her, soft as a lover: *He'll pick it up again. They always do. Shall I show you how it ends?*

"No," Yara said aloud, to a god, in an empty street.

The Salt laughed—not cruelly. That was the worst part. It laughed the way a grandmother laughs at a child insisting she'll never grow old.

* * *

Sefra came at dusk, and she did not summon Yara anywhere. She simply arrived, the way weather arrives, sitting on the low wall of the ash-gray fountain in the square below Yara's rented room, feeding crumbs to pigeons that had no business trusting her and trusted her completely.

Yara came down because refusing would have been its own kind of answer.

"You look terrible," Sefra said pleasantly. She was beautiful the way a chapel is beautiful—symmetry intended to produce a feeling in you whether you consented to it or not. Her dark hair was bound back with white cord, the color of her faith, and her hands were stained to the wrist with ink. "You're not sleeping. Of course you're not. It gets louder at night, doesn't it? When the mind's defenses go down. Like a tide coming in over mudflats."

"How many beggars have you 'restored' this week?"

"Eleven." No flinch. "Word spreads. People bring me their mothers now, their brothers, the ones the guild hollowed out decades ago. I sit with them and I pour back what was taken, and they weep, and some of them curse me because the memories hurt worse than the absence did—" she smiled, and it was a real smile, which made it worse, "—and every single one of them chooses the pain. Every time. Ask yourself why, thief. Ask yourself why people will pay agony for a self."

"Because they had a self to lose. I didn't."

"No," Sefra agreed, and her gentleness was the gentlest cruelty Yara had ever felt applied to her skin. "You didn't. Which is why you of all people should understand what I'm offering. Not power. Not a throne. I'm offering you the hour your mother died. The song. The burning city. The moment she put the god's memory into your swaddle and told you—do you even know what she told you? Because I do. Our order kept records too, Yara. We wrote down what the survivors said."

Yara's heart was doing something painful and arrhythmic. "Then say it."

"She said: *carry it until it wakes, and then it will choose.*" Sefra spread her ink-stained hands. "Not you. It. The memory chooses. That's the heresy at the center of everything—both armies thought they owned a god's remembrance, and the truth is the remembrance owns whoever carries it, and it has been choosing for a century, and it chose a child with no self, because a blank page takes ink best."

The tide inside Yara surged, and for one vertiginous second she was not standing in the square at all—she was above it, above the city, seeing the streets as veins, the people as cells, the whole war as a single body turning over in sleep—and then it passed, leaving her gripping the fountain's rim with wet stone biting her palms.

"What does it want?" she whispered.

"To be remembered," Sefra said. "That's all. That's always been all. And here is the terrible mercy of it: whoever holds it will remember it *forever*, perfectly, and will become the vessel of everything it was—every prayer, every battle, every lie both sides told. Immortality of a kind. Sainthood of a kind. My ancestors called it enthronement. Yours called it theft. Both were wrong. It's neither. It's just—" she searched for the word and found it with visible reluctance, "—*lonely*. A dead god's loneliness, looking for a house."

Behind Sefra, the pigeons had gone still, all their small heads cocked toward Yara as if listening to the same tide.

"The letter," Yara managed. "Tomorrow night. The chapel."

"The thirteenth turn completes. The moon fills for the thirteenth time this year—one extra fullness, one night the calendars never accounted for, except ours." Sefra stood, brushed ash from her white-corded hair, and looked at Yara with an expression that was almost, horribly, kind. "Come armed or come empty-handed, it changes nothing. Come willing or unwilling, it changes nothing. But come, thief. Because it has already chosen, and we both know whose body it's living in."

She walked away, and the pigeons rose all at once, a gray explosion against the bruised sky, wheeling once over Yara's head like a thrown handful of ash before scattering.

* * *

She did not sleep. She sat in her rented room with a candle burning down and the Salt singing its tide-song, and somewhere past midnight she stopped resisting and let it speak plainly, because she was too tired to fight and because a part of her—the part that had tasted her own confiscated childhood vial and wept—wanted to hear it.

It did not tempt her. That was the revelation, the cold clean horror of it. It didn't need to.

It simply showed her the two of them—Dain and Sefra—as they would stand tomorrow night in the drowned chapel, and it showed her what each of them would be offered, and it showed her, with the dispassionate clarity of a ledger, what each of them would do.

Dain, offered his father's voice saying *well done*: he would kneel.

Sefra, offered her god enthroned and her order vindicated across a hundred years of ridicule: she would kneel faster.

And Yara, offered her mother's arms and the burning city and the song—offered the whole stolen country of her childhood, border to border—

She caught her reflection in the black window glass and it flickered, the way it had been flickering for days: a woman older than her, eyes like deep water, mouth moving around a word. Not her face. The carrier before her, maybe. Or the god itself, wearing the shape of everyone who had ever carried it, one after another, down the century, like coats handed down through a poor family.

And there, in the flicker, in the space between her own face and the older one, Yara saw it at last—saw the thing that had been true since the first page of the first ledger and that no one, in a hundred years of prophecy and war and inheritance, had been willing to write down:

There was no heir. There had never been an heir. The prophecy didn't name a person; it named a *container*. Both armies hadn't fought over a god—they had fought over a *memory*, and the memory had let them fight, because a memory that is fought over is a memory that is never forgotten, and being forgotten was the one thing the Salt feared more than anything. The war wasn't about the Salt. The war *was* the Salt. Its survival strategy. Its long, patient, drowning refusal to die. Every heir who reached for it fed it another century. Every army that bled for it kept it warm.

It wasn't asking to be remembered.

It was asking to be *kept*—and it had arranged the whole world into a machine for keeping it.

Yara sat very still while the candle guttered, and the tide inside her chest rose and rose, and she understood that it knew she understood, and that it was not afraid, because what could a blank-page girl possibly do against a thing that had outlived its own death?

She picked up her pen.

She pulled a scrap of ledger paper from the ruins in her bag—guild stock, salt-tanned, nearly unburnable—and she began, in a hand that shook only at first, to write. Not a record. Not a report. A plan. A plan no one had agreed to, that no one would agree to, that depended on no one agreeing to it.

*To whoever I am tomorrow,* she wrote at the top, and then stopped, because the candle had gone out, and the tide was singing, and outside the window the thirteenth moon was already swelling toward its fullness like a wound filling with light.

One night remained.
