## 17. The Twelfth Turn

The moon came up wrong.

Yara had watched it rise every night for twenty-six nights, and she knew its face the way a child knows a mother's—by the flaw in it, the shadow-smudge on its lower rim that the tide-tables called the Drowned Eye. Tonight that eye was open wider than it should have been, silver bleeding into silver, as though something behind the moon had leaned closer to look at the world below. The thirteenth turn was nearly complete. One night remained.

She stood in the bell tower of the drowned chapel with the sea sighing through the broken windows below her feet, and she counted what she had left.

Not much. A knife with a chipped handle. A vial of stolen decades she no longer wanted to sell. A name that three different people wanted for three different reasons. And promises—she had promises. She turned them over now like coins from a dead man's purse, testing each one's weight, and found that every single one of them rang false at the bottom.

* * *

Dain's promise had come first, two nights ago, in his family vault beneath the ash-hills where the war had ended without ending. He had stood among the racks of salt-keyed weapons—a hundred years of heirlooms waiting for a hand worth serving—and he had put his palm flat against an empty case and sworn it.

"I want it destroyed," he'd said. "Not used. Not sold. Not enshrined. Burned out of the world so my children never inherit the wanting of it." His eyes had been steady, and Yara had believed him, which frightened her more than any lie would have. "You have my word, Yara. Whatever happens under the thirteenth moon, the Salt dies."

But promises are ledgers too, and Yara had learned to read the fine print written in what people did not say. *Whatever happens.* Not: whatever you choose. Not: whatever it costs you. Dain's promise had a shape, and the shape was a pair of hands closing around a thing that could not be held without being changed by it. Destroying the Salt meant taking it first. Taking it first meant holding it. And everything she had learned—from Corvane, from Sefra, from the god's tired voice in her dreams—said that nothing held the Salt. The Salt held you.

She had asked him, before they climbed back into the grey morning: "And if destroying it requires someone to carry it to the fire? If it can't be burned where it sleeps?"

He hadn't answered. He had looked at her the way men look at maps when the route runs through country they'd rather not name.

That was the fine print. Every promise ends with someone holding the Salt. She just hadn't yet admitted whose hands the promises all pointed toward.

* * *

Sefra's promise had come wrapped in mercy, which made it heavier.

The beggar on the shrine steps still haunted her—the old man who had wept in the street when Sefra poured forty stolen years back into him like water into a cracked pot. He had known his wife's name again. He had known his own. And then he had looked at Yara with those wet, reborn eyes and said, *you're the vessel, aren't you, girl? You're the cup,* and Yara had understood that Sefra's gift had not been charity at all. It had been a demonstration. A merchant showing her wares.

"Your childhood," Sefra had said, in the chapel's drowned nave, her voice soft over the lap of black water. "Whole. Every hour of it. Your mother's face. The city before the burning. I can give you back the woman who sang your name." She had reached out—not touching, never touching, Sefra understood consent the way a fisherman understands nets—and her hand had hovered near Yara's cheek like warmth offered across a table. "All I ask is what the god asks. To be remembered. Let the Salt wake. Let it be enthroned again in living memory, and you will never be empty another day of your life."

And Yara had wanted it. That was the obscenity of it. Standing there in the cold and the salt-smell, she had wanted it with a hunger that felt like drowning in reverse—like air, like water after twenty years of thirst she hadn't even known she carried. Sefra had seen the wanting and smiled, gently, because Sefra was kind, and kindness was the sharpest tool in her armory.

But Yara had read the fine print there too. *Let it be enthroned.* Enthroned in whom? A throne needs a body. A remembered god needs a mouth, a face, a will. And Sefra's whole lineage—the worshippers, the faithful, the army that had loved the god enough to die for it—had been waiting a century for a vessel worthy of the crown. Yara looked down at her own crooked collarbone scar and understood that Sefra's promise ended the same place Dain's did: with Yara holding the Salt. Holding it forever. Wearing it.

Every promise ends with someone holding the Salt. And every someone was her.

* * *

Even Corvane's ghost of a promise pointed here. He was dust and rumor now, torn down by the orphans he'd purchased and the city he'd bled, but his last words to her still sat in her chest like a swallowed stone: *remember me kindly.* And she did, gods help her, she did—some traitorous corner of her heart kept a small warm ledger entry for the man who had fed her and taught her and lied to her from the cradle onward. Pity, she had learned, was the last corruption. It worked in you slowest and deepest, and it never fully washed out.

His promise had been the oldest one: *I will keep you safe.* Given to a bought baby in a guildhall nursery, renewed year after year with tutors and warm coats and careful erasures. And its fine print was the finest of all, because keeping her safe had meant keeping the Salt safe, and keeping the Salt safe had meant keeping it inside her, and so the promise had always been a leash with a child on the end of it. Even Corvane's love, such as it was, ended with Yara holding the Salt.

She laughed aloud in the bell tower, a short ugly sound that the wind took and shredded. Twenty-six days of running, and she had run in a circle so tight it might have been drawn with a compass. Three heirs, three armies' worth of wanting, and every road converged on the same crossroads: her.

Unless she stopped being a road.

* * *

She went down into the chapel nave, wading shin-deep through water the color of tarnished mirrors, and lit the stub of candle she allowed herself. The blind priestess's chair still sat by the altar, empty now, facing the door out of habit even though its owner would never see another door. Yara had stolen the old woman's memories of her to buy her escape, and the theft still ached in a way that ordinary thefts never had. Somewhere in the priestess's head there was a Yara-shaped hole, smooth-edged, healed over, forgotten. She had done that. She had unmade herself in another person's remembering to save her own skin, and the priestess had smiled at her afterward like a stranger, because that was exactly what she was.

That was the cost her power charged, and she had paid it so many times she'd stopped counting. But tonight she counted. Tonight she needed the arithmetic exact.

She spread her materials on the altar stone: a fresh ledger book, taken from the ruined guildhall; a pen; ink in a stoppered jar that sloshed like the sea outside. And around the book she laid out the other things—Dain's token, a sliver of ash-iron worn smooth by his thumb; Sefra's ribbon, blue as deep water; the vial of her own confiscated childhood, its drop of burning city and singing mother still sealed behind glass. Her whole life, arranged as evidence.

Then she began to write.

*Ledger of Yara Galloway, thief, vessel, unwritten woman. Entry the last.*

She wrote the way she'd been trained to write, because the training was the only inheritance she'd ever actually been given, and she refused to be ashamed of it. Item: one prophecy, naming tonight. Item: one Salt, crystallized memory of a dead god, currently resident in the body of the undersigned. Item: two heirs, one of ash and one of water, both sworn to ends that require her surrender. Item: one guild, broken but not buried, its enforcers scattered like sparks that remember how to burn.

Item: the problem.

She wrote it plainly, because plainness was a kind of courage: *Every promise ends with someone holding the Salt. Therefore the only plan that serves no one's inheritance is a plan in which no one holds it—including me.*

Her pen paused above the page. Below her, the sea moved through the chapel's drowned aisles, patient as a creditor.

There was a place the guild taught its thieves about in their second year, and then taught them never to think about again. The sea of collective forgetting. Not the ocean—though the ocean bordered it, and perhaps was its shore. The place where memories went when they were not stored, not sold, not mourned. Every erased childhood, every guild-vanished decade, every name scratched from a ledger: the thieves were told it simply dispersed, that forgetting was a spreading-out, a dilution, harmless as sugar in water. But the old texts—the ones Corvane thought were locked away well enough—said otherwise. They said the sea received. They said nothing given to it was ever held by anyone again, and nothing given to it could ever be taken back, and that this was either the most terrible thing in the world or the most merciful, depending entirely on what you were giving.

A dead god's memory, poured into that sea, would not be destroyed. Destruction was impossible; the priestess had been clear about that, and the god's dream-voice had confirmed it with a weariness that sounded centuries deep. But it would be *unclaimed*. Scattered past all gathering. No heir could inherit it. No throne could hold it. No army could march beneath it. It would become weather. Tide. The taste of salt on the wind that no one would ever think to worship, because no one would ever know it had once been a god.

And the price—

She wrote the price down because she made herself. *The passage must be opened from inside. The carrier does not pour the Salt out; the carrier lets it pass through. What passes through takes its toll. The toll is everything.*

Everything. Not just the god's memory. Hers. All of it—the borrowed competence, the forged names, the twenty years of blankness and the fragments she'd clawed back. The Salt would take its channel with it when it went, scouring her clean the way floodwater scours a riverbed. She would not die. She suspected death would be simpler. She would simply cease, in every ledger and every mind, including her own. Corvane's records would show no purchase. Dain's memory would hold no alliance. Sefra's beautiful mercy would have no object. Even the priestess's healed-over hole would close completely, seamless, as though no one had ever stood in that nave hearing a song.

No grave. No mourner. No heir. Unclaimed, unmourned, gone.

Her hand was shaking. She watched it shake with a strange detachment, as though it belonged to one of the merchants she'd robbed, some dying man trading his happiest decade for coin. *Feeling nothing where joy should sit.* She had spent her whole life being the empty one, the blank page, the vessel—and now, at the end, she finally understood that the emptiness had never been her wound. It had been her armor. The guild had hollowed her out to make room for the god, yes—but the hollowness also meant she had less to lose than anyone alive. Less self to grieve. Less name to mourn.

They had built her for this. Corvane, the armies, the prophecy itself—they had all conspired, across a century, to produce a person who could carry the Salt and might, at the crucial hour, be empty enough to let it go. And the final cruelty, the one that almost made her admire them, was that they had never needed to order her to do it. They had only needed to make sure that everyone else's plans required her surrender, and trust that a thief, cornered, would invent her own exit.

Well. She had always been better at theft than anyone gave her credit for. And tonight she intended to steal the one thing no one had thought to guard: the ending.

* * *

She wrote until the candle guttered low, and what she produced was not a confession and not a prayer. It was instructions. A ledger addressed to a woman who would not exist by morning, in a hand that would recognize itself and nothing else—because she had learned, watching the priestess smile at a stranger, exactly how much a person could survive without, and how little. She sealed the pages against the damp. She set the childhood vial beside them, then hesitated, and took one drop of the priestess's song—the melody, only the melody, salvaged from the chapel's stones—from the small flask she'd decanted weeks ago without letting herself ask why, and added it to the vial. One thread of music through all that dark. A single coin left in a dead man's purse.

Then she climbed back up to the bell tower, because there was one more thing to do before dawn, and it was the hardest item in the ledger.

She had to gather her allies' promises one final time—not to test them, but to spend them. Dain would come at moonrise tomorrow, ash-iron in his fist, sworn to destruction. Sefra would come with her choir of the restored, singing mercy. The enforcers would come because broken guilds bite hardest when dying. And Yara would stand between all of it, at the chapel of the thirteenth moon, and give each of them exactly what they had promised her—protection, childhood, remembrance—in the only currency that couldn't be inherited: absence.

Below her, far out past the harbor bar, the sea of collective forgetting breathed against the edge of the world. She could almost hear it tonight. Almost hear, beneath the ordinary wash of waves, a deeper sound—vast, unhurried, patient as a ledger awaiting its final entry.

The moon leaned closer. The Drowned Eye stood wide open.

One night remains, she wrote, and blotted the page. Let it find me finished.

She blew out the candle and began, in the dark, to hum.
