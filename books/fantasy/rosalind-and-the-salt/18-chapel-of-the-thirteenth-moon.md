## 18. Chapel of the Thirteenth Moon

The tide came in singing.

Yara felt it before she heard it—a pull low in her chest, like a hook set behind her ribs, drawing her down the cliff path toward the water. The moon overhead was wrong. Everyone on the coast had said so for weeks without knowing why: too full, too white, hanging in a sky that seemed to lean away from it. The thirteenth turn. The last night of a century's arithmetic.

Below her, half-drowned and black against the phosphorescent shallows, stood the chapel.

She had come here once before, following a song she'd stolen from her own confiscated childhood. Then it had been ruin—arches broken like snapped ribs, seaweed draped over the saints' niches, the bell tower leaning at an angle of surrender. Now the water around it glowed faintly, and Yara understood that the glow was not reflection. It was attention. The sea itself had turned its face toward this place, the way a crowd turns toward a door when someone important is about to enter.

And the important one was her.

*Come*, said something inside her, in a voice that was not quite hers and not quite the god's but some tide-marked thing between them. *It is time to be remembered.*

"Not yet," Yara said aloud, and her breath fogged strangely in the warm night air, as if winter had followed her down the path. "You wait your turn."

The Salt laughed. She felt it as a shiver along her skin, a taste of brine at the back of her throat. It had been laughing more often lately. Waking the way a sleeper wakes—not all at once, but in stages: first the breathing changes, then the eyes open, then the hands begin to move.

Inside her chest, behind her sternum, something opened its hands.

* * *

She waded in to her knees before she saw that she was not the first.

Two figures stood in the flooded nave, where the pews had rotted to lace and the altar stone rose from the shallows like a drowned animal's back. Lanterns hung from hooks rusted into the arches, burning with a light that did not flicker even when the water moved beneath them. Someone had prepared this place. Someone had been preparing for a hundred years.

Dain stood on the left, at the foot of the aisle, dressed not in his usual traveler's leathers but in ash-grey armor worked with sigils she now recognized—the marks of the army that had killed the god. The Ash Host, the priestess had called them, before Yara stole the memory of herself from the priestess's mind and left her smiling at strangers. The armor was old. Older than Dain. It fit him anyway, the way inherited things always fit whether they suit you or not.

Sefra stood on the right, barefoot in the shallows, wearing white gone grey at the hem. Her hair was loose, and in the lantern light it moved as though underwater even above the waterline. Around her wrists she wore rings of salt-crystal that caught the light and broke it into colors Yara had no names for.

Between them, on the altar, lay an open space. A hollow worn into the stone, shaped like nothing, shaped like everything, shaped—Yara realized with a lurch—like the exact dimensions of the ache behind her own breastbone.

They had made a cradle for what slept inside her.

"You came," Sefra said. Not surprised. Pleased. That was worse.

"I carried her," Dain said, and there was no charm in his voice tonight, none of the memory-den warmth, the blade-under-silk ease. He sounded like a man reading charges. "From the guildhall. Through the marsh road. Past three of Corvane's enforcers who will never report anything again." His hand rested on the hilt of a sword whose blade was clear as glass. "I kept my word."

"You kept your *bargain*," Yara said. "There's a difference. I've spent my whole life learning it."

"Yara." He stepped forward, and the water parted around his boots with a sound like a held breath. "Listen to me. What's inside you has been whispering to you for weeks. I know. I've watched you talk to empty rooms. Whatever it's promised you—"

"It hasn't promised me anything." Her voice cracked on the truth of it. "That's the horror of it, Dain. It doesn't want. It only *remembers*. And remembering hurts less than forgetting, so I keep listening."

"She understands," Sefra said softly. "Better than you ever did, heir of ash. You think it's a weapon to be broken. She knows it's a wound to be tended."

"I know it's a god," Yara said, "and gods are just wars that learned to speak."

The words left her mouth and she did not recognize them as hers—and then she did, because they were not hers, they were old, they were the oldest sentence in the world, and the Salt had put them on her tongue the way a musician puts a reed to a mouthpiece.

Both heirs went still.

* * *

The tide rose while they argued. Yara felt each inch of it as pressure against the walls of herself, the way rising water presses against a hull. The chapel filled. The lanterns climbed higher on their hooks or perhaps the floor sank lower; the geometry of the place had stopped obeying rules. The altar remained dry. Of course it did. Everything here arranged itself around the hollow in the stone.

"The prophecy says tonight," Dain was saying. "It says the Salt wakes under the thirteenth turn, and it says—" he faltered, and for the first time since she'd known him, Dain of the Ash Host looked young. "It says a carrier. A vessel. It doesn't say the vessel gets a vote."

"No," Yara agreed. "Prophecies rarely do. I've read enough ledgers to know how inheritance works. The dead write the terms. The living sign them without reading."

"My family read," he said. "A hundred years of reading. Do you know what my vault looks like? Weapons keyed to this thing, waiting. My grandfather's grandfather died believing he'd failed his children by not finishing the war. Every meal of my childhood was eaten off the table of a grudge." His glass blade caught the light. "I don't want to use it, Yara. I want it *gone*. Broken past mending. I want my family free."

"And I want it *home*," Sefra said, and her voice was gentle, and gentle was the most dangerous register it owned. "Not used. Not broken. Remembered. It was ours before it was anyone's enemy—it sang over our fields, it taught us the names of tides. Your army cut its memory out of the world and salted the ground where it fell, and now you stand there calling destruction mercy." She turned to Yara, and her eyes were wet, and Yara believed every word she was about to say, which was exactly what made them poison. "Give it back to us and we will give you back yourself. Every year Corvane stole. Your mother's face. The song. All of it, whole, unbroken. You have spent your life as a blank page, Yara Galloway. Let us write you full."

"There it is," Yara whispered.

"What?"

"The offer. Both of you. Dain offers oblivion and calls it freedom. You offer restoration and call it love. And both of you—" she pressed a hand flat against her sternum, against the thing turning over inside her like a sleeper rolling toward morning, "—both of you end up holding it. That's the whole ledger. That's every entry. Protection, restoration, destruction, mercy—every promise ends the same way. With one of you standing where I'm standing, holding what I'm holding, deciding for everyone who didn't get asked."

The Salt stirred. Not laughing now. Listening.

* * *

It began to rise out of her like tide.

There was no pain, which frightened her more than pain would have. There was simply a loosening, a lifting, as though the ocean had reached up through the flooded nave and found the seam of her and begun, gently, patiently, to unmake it. Light came through her skin—not from her, *through* her, the way moonlight comes through fog. She looked down at her own hands and watched them go translucent at the edges, watch the lantern flames shine through her fingers as if she were already becoming the thing she carried.

She could feel the memories leaving. Not hers—she had almost none left to lose—but the god's, the stolen centuries, the crystallized grief of a murdered divinity packed into the marrow of a purchased child. They rose through her like bubbles through deep water, and each one surfaced somewhere in the chapel as an image written in light:

A city on a green coast, singing.

An army coming over the hills at dawn, ash-grey, carrying glass.

A god kneeling in its own temple, offering its throat, saying *remember me* instead of *spare me*—

—and the army, hearing it, cutting anyway, because a dead god can be forgotten but a living one must be obeyed, and obedience was the one prayer the Ash Host would not answer.

The images bloomed and faded, bloomed and faded, and both heirs watched them with expressions Yara would carry to whatever grave waited for her: Dain weeping without knowing he wept, his inheritance showing him the crime at its root; Sefra reaching toward the light with her whole body, her face lit with a devotion so total it looked, from certain angles, exactly like hunger.

"Look," Sefra breathed. "Look what they took from us."

"Look what we were," Dain said hoarsely. "*We.* One city. Before."

"Yes," said the Salt, through Yara's mouth, in a voice like water finding the lowest place. "Before."

And then it spoke to each of them alone, and Yara, still half-herself, still anchored by some stubborn knot of refusal at her center, heard both whispers at once and finally, finally understood.

To Dain it said: *Break me, and be free of the vault forever. Be the last of your line, the one who ended it. No more grudge at the table. No more glass in the dark.*

To Sefra it said: *Take me home, and never again kneel. Enthroned in me, you will hear every prayer on the coast. Your people will never be orphaned again.*

And to Yara, quietly, in the voice of the woman from the burning city, the voice from the vial, the voice that had sung her name across a century of dark: *And you, little ledger. I have nothing to offer you. You are the only one here I cannot buy. That is why I chose you.*

*Why?* Yara asked, inside, where no one else could hear.

*Because everyone else wants me to be something,* the god said, tired beyond all measure, tired the way only the murdered are tired. *A weapon. A crown. A wound. A warning. You are the first soul in a hundred years who might let me simply end.*

The light through her skin brightened. The tide reached the tops of the arches. The lanterns burned on, impossibly, under the water.

On the altar, the hollow began to glow.

* * *

"Yara." Dain's voice, ragged. He had drawn the glass sword without seeming to decide to; it pointed at the light pouring out of her chest, and his arm shook. "Whatever it's telling you—it lies. It's a god. Lying is what they do."

"It hasn't lied to me once," Yara said. "That's the worst discovery of my life. Everyone else has. Corvane. The guild. You, Dain—you swore you wanted to destroy it, and you do, and you also want to be the man who destroyed it, and those are two different hungers wearing one coat." She turned to Sefra. "And you'd give me back my mother's face, and then I'd spend eternity watching you wear my god like a dress."

Sefra's devotion curdled, just slightly, at the edges. "You ungrateful little thief—"

"Yes," Yara said. "That too. I steal things. It's the one trade I know." She spread her arms in the rising water, in the light, in the middle of a hundred years of other people's grief. "So here is my counteroffer. The only one I have."

The Salt rose higher. She could feel it now as a second heartbeat, vast and slow, pressing outward against the walls of her, asking permission the way the sea asks permission of the shore—by simply arriving.

"Both of you put down your inheritances," Yara said. "Or watch me do something no prophecy accounted for."

Dain's blade lowered an inch. Sefra's salt-rings flared white.

And the god inside her, feeling the shape of what she intended, did the last thing Yara expected.

It began, very softly, to sing.

* * *

The song was the song. The one from the vial. The one the blind priestess had recognized with tears running into the wrinkles of her smile. The one Yara had chosen—or would choose, or had always chosen—to keep, out of everything, when everything else was taken from her.

The water rose. The heirs stepped forward, both of them, unable to help it, drawn by a melody older than their quarrel, older than their armies, older than the lie the two sides had told themselves until the lie grew bones and a name. The light poured out of Yara like tide out of a broken harbor wall, and the hollow in the altar stone filled with radiance, and the whole drowned chapel rang like a struck bell, and every soul in it—thief, heir, murderer's child, martyr's child—stood in the presence of the last memory of a god, offered freely to whichever hand reached first.

Dain's hand moved.

Sefra's hand moved.

And Yara Galloway, carrier, vessel, purchased child, blank page, opened herself completely—and stepped backward off the edge of the world.

The last thing she saw, in the instant before the sea of collective forgetting rose up to meet her, was both heirs grasping at light that was no longer there—two hands closing on empty water, two inheritances arriving a moment too late at an altar that had already gone dark.

The last thing she heard was her own voice, or the god's, or theirs, indistinguishable now, saying:

*Unclaimed.*
