## 19. Unclaimed

The water came up to her knees before she stopped walking.

Yara stood in the flooded nave of the drowned chapel, and the thirteenth moon poured through the broken roof like something molten, laying a silver road across the black surface of the sea. The tide had risen all day, as the priestess had said it would, until the chapel was less a building than a ribcage full of ocean—arches for ribs, altar for heart, and her standing in the hollow of it, feeling the Salt stir beneath her sternum like a second pulse.

Behind her, at the chapel's drowned threshold, Dain stood hip-deep in the shallows with his family's blade drawn—not pointed at anyone, just held, the way a man holds a thing when he has forgotten what else his hands are for. To her left, balanced on a fallen pillar with the grace of someone who had never once doubted her own body, Sefra waited with her palms open and empty.

Empty hands. Yara almost laughed. Of everything they had shown her—the vault of waiting weapons, the beggar made whole, the mercy worn like jewelry—it was the empty hands that frightened her most. Empty hands meant confidence. Empty hands meant *you will come to me*.

"Yara." Sefra's voice carried over the water without effort, low and warm, the voice of a mother calling a child home from the shore at dusk. "It doesn't have to hurt. That's the lie your guild taught you—that everything worth having costs. The god's memory isn't a wound. It's a *home*. Let me show you how to open the door."

"She's right that it doesn't have to hurt," Dain said, and even now, even here, there was charm in it, worn smooth as sea glass. "But she'll tell you it can be shared, and that's the other lie. It can't. I've read every account my family kept for a hundred years. A vessel holds one god or none." He waded a step closer, water dragging at his legs like reluctant hands. "Give it to me and I'll end it. Not use it—*end* it. You have my oath."

"You had my oath too," Sefra said mildly, "about the beggar on Tanner's Row. And yet here we are."

"That was necessity—"

"Everything is necessity," Sefra said, "right up until it's appetite."

The Salt rose inside Yara like a tide answering a moon she could not see, and for a moment she let it rise, let it fill her throat with light, because she wanted to hear what it would say. It always said the same thing, in its tired, enormous voice: *remember me*. Not *take me*. Not *use me*. Just—*remember*. A dead god's last request, repeated so long it had curdled into a hunger, into a war, into two bloodlines sharpening themselves against each other across a century of waiting.

She had heard it clearly three nights ago, in the dream that was not quite a dream: the war from above, the two armies marching under one moon, the single lie both sides had needed to believe—that memory could be owned. That anything could be.

Dain's people believed the Salt was a weapon to be destroyed by the only ones strong enough to hold it. Sefra's people believed it was a crown to be restored by the only ones worthy to wear it. And between those two beliefs, a hundred years of graves.

And Corvane—poor, pitiful, torn-down Corvane—had believed it was inventory.

Everyone who had ever touched the god's memory had believed it belonged to them.

"Yara." Sefra again, softer now, and Yara felt the pull of it in her chest like a hook set behind the breastbone. This was the woman who had given her back an hour of her mother's voice, who had knelt in the ash of a burning city in memory and held her hand while she wept. Whatever else Sefra was, the temptation she offered was not a mask. It was real. That was the horror of it. "I know what you're considering. I've seen that look before, on others who carried fragments. You think if you refuse us both, you win. But refusal isn't escape, girl. The Salt has waited a hundred years. It can wait inside you forever. You will never be alone in your own skull again. Is that the freedom you're drowning yourself for?"

"It's not drowning," Yara said. Her voice came out steadier than she expected. "It's letting go."

She looked down at the water around her legs. Black, patient, fathomless. The priestess had called it the sea of collective forgetting, and Yara had thought it a metaphor—a pretty name for the place where unclaimed memories went when no ledger recorded them. But standing here, knee-deep in it, she understood it was not a metaphor at all. Every dropped name, every forgotten face, every grief that had faded from sharpness into weather—it was all here, dissolved into the salt water, holding nothing and refusing nothing. The sea did not keep. The sea did not spend. The sea only received.

The guild had taught her that a memory was a commodity: extracted, vialled, filed, traded, spent. Dain's line had taught that it was a burden to be borne or broken. Sefra's line had taught that it was a birthright to be seized. Three doctrines, one error.

A memory was not a thing you held.

It was a thing that held you—for a while—and then, if you were brave, you opened your hand.

"I'm going to do something," Yara said, "and neither of you is going to like it."

"Don't." Dain's charm cracked clean down the middle, and underneath it was just a man, young and afraid, standing in cold water with a sword he had inherited from people who had died believing in this night. "Whatever you're thinking—there's no third option. There never was. That's the whole shape of prophecy. Two hands reach, one hand closes."

"There's a third option," Yara said. "There always was. It's just that no one with power ever wanted it."

She closed her eyes.

Opening herself was not like opening a door. It was like being a door—like realizing she had been one all along, hinged and swinging, built to let something pass through and never to keep it. She found the place where the Salt lived, that hot bright weight beneath her sternum that had been growing louder for twenty-six days, and instead of clenching against it, the way she had clenched every night since the first drop of her childhood vial, she did the hardest thing her thief's hands had ever done.

She unlocked everything.

Every internal seal the guild had installed. Every wall she had built herself, brick by stolen brick, to keep the god's memory separate from the hollow where her childhood used to be. Every lock she had ever picked in another person's mind had taught her the shape of locks; now she picked her own, all of them, in a single breath.

The Salt came up through her like dawn coming up through a window—sudden, total, indifferent to what it illuminated.

She saw the war entire. Not the version in Dain's vault, not the version in Sefra's songs—the true one, the tired one, the one the dead god had watched from above while its worshippers and its killers marched under the same moon believing different lies about the same night. She saw the god die not in triumph or tragedy but in confusion, calling out to both armies in a language each side translated into victory. She saw the priests gather its last remembering into crystal. She saw a century of hands reach for that crystal, and every hand want something, and the wanting be the only corruption the Salt had ever suffered.

*Remember me,* it said, one last time, from inside her own chest.

"No," Yara said gently, the way you speak to a child asking for a thing that would hurt them. "Not like that. Not as a crown. Not as a weapon. Not as a name carved somewhere permanent. I'll remember you the way the sea remembers rain—by taking you in and letting you go."

And she opened.

* * *

It did not feel like dying. She had expected dying—had made her peace with it on the walk down through the tidal flats, composing small farewells in her head and discarding them all as dishonest. But this was not an ending. It was a *transit*. The Salt moved through her the way a wave moves through a channel, and she was only the channel, and channels are not diminished by what passes through them—they are merely shaped by it, briefly, and then the shape fills with ordinary water.

Light poured out of her mouth, her eyes, the scarred crook of her collarbone, streaming upward through the broken roof toward the thirteenth moon and then bending, impossibly, downward, into the black sea, and where it touched the water the sea lit from within like a lantern of frosted glass, and Yara felt every memory the god had ever been pass out through her skin like heat leaving a cooling stone.

She felt it leave, and she felt what it took with it.

That was the price, and she paid it knowingly, and knowing did not make it gentle. Her mother's song went first—she had only just gotten it back, one hour of it, and she felt it dissolve like sugar in warm water, and she wept without stopping the outflow, because stopping was the one thing she must not do. Then the burning city. Then the guildhall, the ledgers, the smell of vial-wax and ink. Then Corvane's rehearsed kindness, which she released with something close to relief. Then Dain's laugh in the memory-den. Then Sefra's open hands. Then the priestess's blind eyes finding her face. Then—

Then it reached for her name, and she let it go too.

*Yara.* The word peeled away from her like bark from a birch, and for one vertiginous moment she was no one at all, a doorway with no house attached, standing in silver water under a silver moon, and it was the freest she had ever been and the most terrified, and both feelings were already going, going, softening at their edges, dissolving into the vast patient dark that received everything and kept nothing.

On the pillar, Sefra lunged.

Yara saw it through the last of her clearing sight—the worshipper's heir moving faster than belief, hands outstretched, reaching into the stream of departing light as though she could cup it, dam it, drink it. For an instant the light pooled in her palms, and Yara felt Sefra's whole soul strain toward it, a century of inherited longing concentrated into ten fingers.

The sea took it back.

The light ran through Sefra's grasping hands like water through a net, and Sefra cried out—not in pain but in *bereavement*, the sound of a woman watching a birthright evaporate—and fell to her knees on the pillar, and the sea lapped at her shins, and gave her nothing. Not cruelty. The sea does not punish. It simply does not keep.

Dain did not lunge. That was the thing Yara would have remembered, if she could have remembered anything: he stood in the shallows with his ancestral blade half-raised and then, slowly, lowered it, point-first into the water, and did not reach at all. Whether it was defeat or understanding or simple exhaustion, the light did not pause to ask. It streamed past him, around him, through the space where his reaching hands might have been, and went down into the dark, and kept going, deeper than any vault, wider than any ledger, into the sea of collective forgetting where nothing is owned and therefore nothing can be fought over.

The last of it left her like a long exhale.

The moonlight went ordinary. The water around her ankles was just water, cold and salt and alive with small unseen motions. Somewhere beyond the broken roof, a nightbird started up its one dry note, and the tide continued its ancient bookkeeping, coming in, going out, keeping nothing.

Yara swayed.

Her knees found the submerged altar stone, and she sat down hard on it, gasping, and the gasp itself felt strange—unaccompanied, unhaunted, a breath belonging to exactly one person and no one else. Inside her chest, where the second pulse had beaten for twenty-six days, there was silence. A silence so complete it rang.

She tried to think of her mother and found a shape where the thought should be, a hollow pressed into her like a fossil in soft stone—the outline of a love, with nothing left inside it. She tried to think of the guildhall and got only the word *guildhall*, a label on an empty drawer. She reached for Dain's face and Sefra's voice and the priestess's song and found, one after another, beautifully, terribly, nothing.

Both heirs were still there. She could see them dimly, at the edge of what remained of her attention—Sefra kneeling in the ruins of her inheritance, Dain standing very still with water dripping off his blade—but their names slid off her like rain off oiled cloth. Strangers. Witnesses to something she had done, apparently, for reasons written nowhere she could find.

The tide was rising. It seemed important to stand.

She got up, wobbling, and began to wade toward the shore, and the water gave way before her step by step, generous with nothing, keeping nothing, and behind her in the drowned chapel the two heirs of the two armies stood in the ruins of a hundred years of wanting, and neither of them followed, because there was nothing left to follow.

Except—

At the chapel steps, half-submerged, her bare foot struck something that was not stone. She bent, the motion automatic, a thief's reflex surviving the theft of everything else, and lifted from the water a small book, swollen and salt-stiff, its cover dark with brine. A ledger. Her fingers knew ledgers the way her lungs knew air.

On the first page, in handwriting that trembled but did not hesitate—in handwriting that felt, distantly, like her own—two words had been written and blotted against the wet:

*To me.*

The tide pulled at her ankles, patient as everything, and somewhere above the broken roof the thirteenth moon began, at last, to set.
