## 20. Blank Pages

She woke to the sound of water.

Not the sea itself—the sea was somewhere below the chapel steps, breathing in its slow way—but water dripping from stone onto stone, a patient metronome counting out a morning she did not recognize. The sky above her was the grey of unwritten paper. Gulls argued over something far away. Her cheek was pressed against cold granite, and when she lifted her head, the world tilted and righted itself like a boat finding its keel.

Her name was Yara.

That was all.

She sat with this fact for a long time, the way one sits with a single coin in an empty room, turning it over, testing its weight against nothing. *Yara.* It fit her mouth. It came when she reached for it, unhesitating, the way a heartbeat comes. Everything else—everything—was a door painted on a wall. She could see the door. She could even feel the handle's shape under her fingers, a smooth absence where a life should have been. But when she pushed, there was only plaster, and behind the plaster, sea-salt dark stone, and behind that, nothing at all.

She tried to remember waking up. She tried to remember yesterday. She tried to remember whether she had ever seen the sun rise before this one, whether she had ever been cold before this cold, whether the ache in her knees was new or old or hers at all. The questions went down into her like stones into a well that had no bottom—or rather, a well that had been emptied so thoroughly that even the walls had forgotten they were walls.

*Yara.*

She said it aloud, just to hear it exist. Her voice cracked on it, dry as driftwood. There was salt on her lips—not tears, she thought, though she couldn't be sure; she had no memory of crying to compare it against—and salt in her hair, which hung in stiff ropes past her shoulders. Salt crusted the backs of her hands in pale whorls, like frost, like lace, like the ghost of writing.

When she looked at her palms too long, they seemed to shimmer.

She looked away.

The chapel steps rose behind her, slick and black with weed, ascending out of the drowned dark toward the broken doorway where, presumably, everything had happened. She felt no urge to go back up. The building exhaled a silence that her body understood before her mind did: *you have already given it everything you had.* Her legs shook when she tried to stand, and she stayed sitting, hugging her knees, watching the tide withdraw from the rocks below with the unhurried confidence of something that has done this ten thousand times and will do it ten thousand more.

Somewhere in her chest, where a person might keep their childhood, there was a hollow. Not painful, exactly. Pain required edges, and the hollow had none. It was more like the feeling of reaching for a stair in the dark and finding floor—a small lurch, over and over, every time she reached for anything. Her mother's face: floor. The city where she'd been born: floor. Whether she liked honey or hated it, whether she had ever danced, whether anyone had ever braided this hair: floor, floor, floor.

But grief was there. That was the strange mercy and the strange cruelty of it. She grieved without knowing for what, the way a house grieves after a fire—smoke-stained, structurally sound, missing rooms. She wept, actually, suddenly and without warning, great heaving sobs that tasted of brine, and she had absolutely no idea what she was crying about, and the not-knowing made it worse, because the sorrow was clearly *about* something. It had a shape. It pointed somewhere, out past the horizon of what she could see, and whatever lived at the end of that pointing finger was gone.

She cried until there was nothing left, which did not take long, because apparently she was empty in more ways than one.

* * *

The ledger was under her hand.

She discovered it the way you discover a bruise—only when you press. She had been sitting with her palm flat on the step beside her, and the leather cover was cool and familiar under her fingers, worn soft at the corners, and some part of her knew that texture the way her mouth knew her own name. She picked it up.

It was a guild ledger. She knew that much the way she knew her name: instantly, without evidence. The binding, the stitching, the faint smell of tallow and iron-gall ink—her hands recognized all of it and reported back to a mind that had nothing to do with the report. She turned it over. On the back cover, in ink gone brown with age or salt, someone had written two words in a small, careful, slanting hand:

*To me.*

Her handwriting. She understood that immediately, the way you understand your own reflection. The *T* crossed low. The *e* closed tight, like a fist. Whoever had written those words had written them slowly, deliberately, the way people write things they need to survive being forgotten.

Yara opened the front cover.

The first page was dated twenty-six days ago. The handwriting was hers from the very first line, but younger somehow—quicker, cockier, the loops showing off. She read:

*If you are reading this and you don't know why, then it worked. Don't panic. Panic is loud and loud things get found. Read to the end before you decide anything.*

Her breath fogged in the morning air. She read on.

*I am going to tell you who you are, because by the time you read this, I may not be able to. My name is Yara Galloway. I steal memories for the Ledger Guild—I stole them for eleven years, and I was good at it, and I told myself that didn't make me a monster because I always traded fair. I don't know if that's true anymore. I don't know much anymore. That's rather the point.*

*Here is what happened tonight, while I can still write it down:*

And then, page after page, in handwriting that deteriorated as the entries went on—from quick to careful to shaky to a scrawl that dug through the paper in places—the story of the last twenty-six days. A prophecy vault and a date circled in brittle ink. A vial of her own childhood, tasted illegally, full of salt water and a burning city and a woman singing her name. A man called Dain with a knife under his charm and a vault full of weapons keyed to her blood. A woman called Sefra whose mercy was a kind of crown offered sideways. A guildmaster named Corvane who had bought her as a baby and called it saving her. A drowned chapel. A blind priestess whose memories Yara had stolen to escape, leaving the old woman smiling kindly at a stranger. A god's memory, tired and enormous, sleeping inside her like the sea sleeps inside a wave.

And then the last pages, written in a hand so cramped it was nearly illegible, letters leaning like masts in a storm:

*They all want it. Dain wants to destroy it, but his hands are his family's hands, and his family held it once. Sefra wants to enthrone it, and she'll call that freedom. Corvane wanted to sell it, and he called that survival. Even the god only wants to be remembered, and I understand that better than anyone alive, because remembering is all I ever took from other people and never kept for myself.*

*Every road ends with someone holding the Salt. Every promise ends with a hand closing around it. So I am taking the only road that ends with no one holding anything—including me.*

*If you are reading this, the Salt is gone. Scattered into the forgetting, unclaimed, unmourned. And it took its price on the way out, because memory does not travel light. It took my childhood, which was never really mine. It took Corvane, whom I pitied and hate myself for pitying. It took the priestess's song, which I loved. It took Dain's face and Sefra's voice and the taste of stolen joy and the weight of eleven years of other people's yesterdays. It took everything except my name, because I asked it to leave me that. One word. Enough to sign my own ledger with.*

*You will grieve without knowing what for. Let yourself. The grief is proof that something worth losing existed, even if the existence is gone now too.*

*There is one thing left. One vial. I saved it from myself, from the scattering—I don't fully know how, only that the god let me, or maybe the god was tired of being the only one who got to choose what survives. It's sewn into the lining of your coat, left inside pocket, under the tear. When you're ready—and only when you're ready—you can open it. You can put back one memory. Choose carefully. Whatever you choose becomes the seed the rest of you grows from.*

*Choose like it matters. It does.*

*—Yara*

She sat on the chapel steps for a long time after the last page, the ledger open on her knees, the wind turning the earlier pages for her, backward through her own lost weeks, the handwriting growing younger and stranger with every leaf until it was a stranger's hand entirely, and then it was the first page again, and the wind stopped, and the morning went on being grey and patient around her.

*To me,* she thought. *I wrote to me. I'm the me it was written to. So I suppose I'm the addressee of my entire life.*

It should have been funny. She found she couldn't laugh. She found, in fact, that she couldn't do much of anything except hold the book against her chest and feel the hollow in it answer the hollow in herself, two absences recognizing each other across the leather.

* * *

She searched her coat because the letter told her to, and because obedience to her own instructions was the closest thing she had to instinct.

The coat was heavy wool, salt-stiffened, torn along the left seam as if it had recently been through something violent. In the lining, exactly where the letter promised, her fingers found a small hard shape wrapped in oilcloth and stitched in with crooked, urgent stitches. She unpicked them with a fingernail, slowly, aware that she was performing surgery on her own future.

Inside was a vial no longer than her thumb, stoppered with wax, and through the glass she could see—nothing. That was the strange thing. Not empty; *nothing*. Looking into it was like looking into a hole in the world. The grey morning light bent strangely around it and refused to go in. Her skin prickled. Somewhere deep in the hollow where her childhood used to be, something stirred and lay back down.

She did not open it.

She wanted to—God, she wanted to, with a hunger that frightened her, a hunger that seemed to come from somewhere older than the hollow, as if the wanting itself were a leftover, a fossil of the girl who had once tasted a forbidden drop of her own past and spent the rest of her life starving. But the letter had said *when you're ready*, and she was not ready, and she suspected that readiness was not the sort of thing you could rush, the way you couldn't rush a tide or a wound or a name learning to live in a stranger's mouth.

Instead she wrapped the vial back in its oilcloth, and instead of sewing it into the coat again, she tucked it into the ledger's spine, into the gutter between the pages, where the binding would hold it snug against all those words in her own hand. Book and vial together. Instructions and instrument. A self in kit form, waiting to be assembled.

Then she stood up.

Her legs held. That surprised her; she'd expected to be weaker. But the body, apparently, remembered what the mind could not—how to stand, how to walk down wet stone, how to keep balance when a gull screamed overhead and the wind shoved at her shoulders like a rude acquaintance. Muscle-memory without the memory. She was a collection of skills attached to a name attached to nothing, walking downhill toward a fishing village she could see now, smoke rising from its chimneys, boats knocking gently against a quay.

People would be there. People with faces she didn't know and histories she hadn't stolen and names she'd have to learn like a child learns them, one at a time, from scratch. The thought should have terrified her. It did terrify her. But underneath the terror was another thing, thinner and harder to name, something that felt almost like—

Possibility? No. More austere than that. *Blankness.* The particular clean terror of a blank page, which is also the only place a story can begin.

At the bottom of the steps she paused and looked back once at the drowned chapel. Water lapped at its threshold. Something vast and quiet had passed through here in the night—she could feel it the way a beach feels the withdrawal of an extraordinary wave, the sand still trembling, the air still rearranged. Whatever had slept inside her was asleep no longer, and awake no longer either. It simply *wasn't*, anywhere, in anyone's keeping, and the world was minutely, immeasurably different for it, and no one would ever know, including her.

"Goodbye," she said, to the chapel, to the god, to the girl who had walked up these steps carrying a war in her ribs. The word felt borrowed. Everything felt borrowed now. Perhaps that was all words had ever been.

Then Yara Galloway—who knew her name and nothing else—turned her back on the sea and walked toward the smoke and the voices, with a ledger addressed *to me* under her arm and a hole in the world stitched into its spine, and she did not look back again.

Behind her, on the top step, half-hidden by weed, something glinted: a second vial, unstoppered, empty, rolling gently in the wash of the tide. She never saw it. But the sea, which forgets nothing and keeps nothing, curled around it once, thoughtfully, and carried it down into the dark—to be found, someday, by someone else.
