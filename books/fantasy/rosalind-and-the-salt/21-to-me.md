## 21. To Me

The sea was the first thing she knew.

Not its name—she had no name for it yet—but its presence: a vast gray breathing at the edge of her awareness, patient as a sleeping animal. Salt in the air. Salt on her lips. Salt crusted white along the seams of her sleeves, as if she had been dipped in it and hung out to dry.

She opened her eyes to a sky the color of unpolished pewter, and lay still for a long while, because moving felt like a decision she did not yet know how to make.

*Yara,* said something inside her. Not a voice. More like a stone dropped into a well—a single word falling a long way before it landed.

That was hers. That much she could hold onto. She turned the name over in her mouth like a coin found in the street: *Yara.* It fit her hand. Whether it fit anything else, she could not say.

She sat up slowly on cold stone steps. Behind her rose the drowned chapel, half-swallowed by the cliffside, its bell tower leaning like an old man listening for footsteps that would never come again. The tide had withdrawn overnight, leaving the rocks slick and black and beaded with weed. Gulls argued somewhere above. Her body ached in places she couldn't account for—her palms, her ribs, the deep muscles of her back—as though she had carried something very heavy a very long way and then set it down.

Or let it go.

The thought arrived without context, trailing a strange grief behind it, grief with no face attached. She pressed her hand flat against her sternum, expecting—she didn't know what. An echo. A warmth. Something.

Nothing answered. There was only her heartbeat, steady and ordinary, and the sea keeping its own time beside it.

Then she saw the book.

It sat on the step beside her where she must have placed it herself, though she had no memory of placing anything anywhere ever. A ledger, bound in gray leather gone soft with salt-damp, its corners worn round. Across the cover, in ink that had dried in a hurry but not so hurried as to be illegible, someone had written two words:

*To me.*

Her handwriting. She knew this the way she knew her own name—not by evidence but by recognition, the way you know your reflection even in a warped mirror. The slight forward slant of it. The way the *m* trailed off like a wave losing interest in the shore.

She picked up the book. It weighed almost nothing, which seemed wrong. Books like this should weigh everything.

* * *

She read it three times before the words stopped sliding out from under her.

The first page was addressed to her, plainly, without flourish:

*If you are reading this, then you woke up. Good. That means it worked, which means I am sorry, because working was always going to cost exactly this.*

*I don't remember writing this yet. By the time you read it, neither of us will. So listen carefully, because we only get one conversation, and it's this one.*

*You will wake knowing three things: your name, the sound of the sea, and how to steal a memory. Don't ask me how I know the third one. You'll feel it in your fingers, like a language you were raised speaking. It's yours whether you want it or not. What you do with it is the only thing that was ever really ours to choose.*

*Here is what you need to understand about what's missing: it isn't lost. It's spent. There is a difference. Lost things can be found. Spent things bought something. Try to remember that when the emptiness gets loud—and it will get loud, especially at night, especially near water.*

*In the inside pocket of this book there is one vial. One. I could have saved more. I chose this one. When you're ready—and only when you're ready, don't rush it, rushing is how the guild got us in the first place—you'll know what to do with it.*

*One last thing. Somewhere out there is a small ledger of memories given away free. If you find it, add to it. If you become it, better.*

*—Me*

She sat with the letter for a long time after finishing it, the gulls wheeling overhead, the tide creeping back up the black rocks with its slow, unhurried certainty.

*Spent, not lost.* She turned the phrase over. It didn't comfort her, exactly. But it gave the hollow in her chest a shape, and a shaped thing is easier to carry than a formless one.

Inside the pocket, as promised: a single vial, no longer than her thumb, stoppered with wax the color of dried blood. She held it up against the gray sky. Inside, a thread of silver light coiled and uncoiled, restless as a fish in shallow water.

A memory. Someone's whole memory, or a piece of one, sealed in glass.

She caught herself salivating. Not hunger exactly—or not only hunger. Her fingers knew this object. They knew the wax, the weight, the way the light moved. Somewhere below thought, below identity, there was a craft in her hands, and the craft recognized its materials the way a singer recognizes a note.

But whose memory? And why this one, of all the ones she might have saved?

The letter said *when you're ready.* She decided she wasn't. She tucked the vial back into the pocket, and the pocket closed around it with a soft leathery sigh, and she told herself the ache she felt was only hunger for breakfast.

It was not only hunger for breakfast.

* * *

She spent the morning learning herself the way you'd learn a rented room—opening drawers, testing floorboards, discovering which things stuck and which things gave.

Her body knew things. That was the strangest part. She knew how to move quietly over wet rock without deciding to; her feet simply made no sound. She knew that if she cupped her palm behind her ear and hummed low, she could feel the vibrations of other heartbeats through stone—an old thief's trick, apparently, though she had no word for why she knew it. She knew the guildhall existed, knew it the way you know a scar: a fact about her past with no pictures attached. She knew there had been a man named Corvane who had been kind to her, and she knew, with equal certainty and no evidence, that his kindness had been a leash. Both facts sat in her side by side, orphaned, unexplained, true.

She knew Dain's name and flinched. She knew Sefra's name and ached. She could not have said why for either of them, and the not-knowing was its own kind of wound—the ghost of feelings whose causes had been spent.

*Spent, not lost,* she reminded herself, and walked on.

Near midday she found a fishing village huddled in the next cove south, all tarred rope and drying nets and smoke that smelled of kelp. People looked at her and looked away. She understood, dimly, that there was something about her that made looking difficult—some quality of blankness that others' eyes slid off of, like rain off oiled canvas. She had done that, apparently. To everyone. Everywhere.

At a cookfire by the seawall, an old woman was selling mussels in broth, and Yara discovered she had coins in her coat—three of them, worn smooth, of no mint she recognized. She traded one for a bowl and ate sitting on the wall with the wind pulling at her hair, and the broth was hot and briny and good, and she was surprised by how surprised she was to enjoy it. Pleasure, she was learning, was possible even in a person made mostly of empty. It just arrived without history. Every good thing tasted like the first time, because every good thing *was* the first time.

An odd freedom in that, she thought. And an odd poverty. Probably both at once was simply what being alive cost.

The old woman watched her eat with the frank curiosity of the very old. "You're not from here," she said. Not a question.

"No," Yara agreed.

"You've got a look like my sister had, after the fever took her years. Like a house swept clean." The woman ladled herself a bowl and sat down companionably. "Swept clean isn't the same as empty, mind. Floors are for standing on."

Yara looked at her. "Did someone pay you to talk to me?"

The old woman laughed until she coughed. "Girl, nobody's paid anyone to talk in this village since the tax boats stopped coming. I'm talking because you look like you could use a conversation, and because I'm old and the mussels won't sell themselves." She slurped her broth. "So. Talk."

And Yara found, to her astonishment, that she wanted to. Not to confess—she had nothing to confess *with*, no story to tell. But to practice. To try sentences out loud and see which ones rang true against whatever remained inside her.

"I think I did something," she said finally. "Something big. And I paid for it with—" she touched her temple, her chest, gesturing vaguely at the whole architecture of herself, "—with most of me. And I think it was worth it. But I'd like to know what it was."

The old woman considered this the way she might consider the weather. "Sounds like childbirth," she said. "You forget the pain on purpose. Body's mercy." She pointed her spoon at Yara's coat. "What's in the pocket, then?"

Yara's hand closed over the vial before she'd decided to move it. "A memory," she said. "Mine, I think. Or mine to give."

"Mm." The old woman nodded sagely. "My mother kept a jar of honey thirty years without opening it. Saved it for an occasion grand enough. Died with the jar full." She shrugged. "Occasions don't announce themselves, girl. Mostly they're Tuesdays."

* * *

She opened the vial at sunset, on the chapel steps, because it seemed right to do it where she'd woken, and because the old woman was correct about Tuesdays.

The wax broke away like old bread. The smell rose up at once—salt and smoke and something floral underneath, crushed stems, a garden near the sea. Her hands trembled. She noticed them trembling and let them.

*When you're ready.* Well. Ready was a luxury for people with more self to spare.

She tipped one drop onto her tongue.

The world folded.

Not darkness—she had expected darkness, expected the black slide of unconsciousness—but folding, like paper creased along lines she hadn't known were drawn there. And then:

*A chapel. This chapel, younger, its stones uncracked, candlelight swimming on walls that were still dry. Incense. The smell of the garden after rain. And a voice—*

*A voice, singing.*

It was not a song she knew. It was not a song anyone knew anymore, perhaps—it had the cadence of something older than the languages she almost remembered, rising and falling like the tide itself, like breathing, like a lullaby sung to something enormous and dying. And beneath the melody, a woman's voice, cracked and warm and absolutely certain, singing words that meant—

She didn't know what they meant. That was the miracle of it. She didn't need to know. The meaning lived in the melody the way salt lives in seawater: inseparable, invisible, everywhere. The song was about remembering. Or it was about letting go. Or it was about how those were the same thing, performed slowly, over a lifetime, the way the sea performs the shore.

The blind priestess. The knowledge surfaced gently, without pain: *a blind priestess sang this, in this chapel, and I stole it from her once, and later I came back and chose it, of everything, to keep.* There had been a raid. There had been a choice made in fire and running. There had been an old woman left smiling at a stranger. None of it had pictures. All of it had weight. The memory itself was only the song—but wrapped around the song, thin as onion skin, were the reasons she had chosen it, and the reasons were enough to make her throat close.

Because it wasn't hers. That was the point. Of everything she might have reclaimed—her childhood, her city, her mother's face, the hour that would have made her *someone*—she had chosen a memory that belonged to another person entirely. Not inheritance. Not birthright. A gift, taken and then given back to herself, chosen freely from among all the selves she might have rebuilt.

Identity built from choice, not blood. From Tuesday, not coronation.

The vision faded the way tide withdraws—not ending, just receding, leaving the sand of her glistening and changed. She opened her eyes on the gray evening, on the ruined chapel, on the sea saying its one long syllable against the rocks.

And without deciding to, without any thought at all, she began to hum.

The melody came out of her whole, unbroken, note-perfect, as if her body had been waiting all day—all life—for permission. It was a strange thing to hear in her own voice, that ancient tide-cadence, and stranger still that it fit, that it sounded less like a song she was performing than one she was remembering with her throat. The gulls went quiet. Or maybe they didn't; maybe she simply stopped hearing anything but the notes, rising and falling, rising and falling, a lullaby for a dead god, a cradle song for the sea, a hymn with no congregation except one blank-eyed girl on broken steps who did not know why she was smiling and did not need to.

She hummed it through twice. Three times. On the fourth pass she changed it—just slightly, a turn of phrase, a grace note added where the old melody had left space—and the song accepted the change the way the sea accepts a river: without complaint, without distinction. Hers now. Also no one's. Both true.

When she finally stopped, the sky had gone fully dark and the first stars stood out hard and clear over the water. She felt—she searched for the word and settled on it with some surprise—*lighter.* Not full. Not healed. But lighter, the way a boat is lighter for having put down cargo that was never really cargo, only ballast someone else loaded.

She took out the ledger. Beneath her own letter, on a fresh page, she wrote the day's date—she'd asked the mussel-seller; it was a Tuesday—and beneath the date:

*Slept on chapel steps. Woke. Ate mussels. Opened the vial.*

*It was the song.*

*I kept the right thing.*

She looked at the words a long moment, then added, in smaller letters:

*Note to next me, if there is a next me: the floors are for standing on.*

* * *

She slept that night in the chapel's lee, wrapped in her coat, the empty vial tucked in the ledger's spine like a bookmark. She dreamed of nothing—which was, she was beginning to suspect, not emptiness but rest, the dreamless sleep of a person whose debts were paid.

She woke before dawn to the sound of footsteps on the rocks below.

Not one set. Several. Careful feet, moving quiet the way she herself moved quiet, and that recognition woke her fully, hand already at the ledger. She eased to the edge of the chapel's shadow and looked down.

Three figures picking their way along the tideline in the last of the night's dark. Two of them moved like soldiers. The third moved like a scholar, pausing to touch the rocks, to lift a piece of driftwood, to hold it up to the graying east as though reading something written there.

And on the wind, faint but unmistakable, drifting up from the leader of the three—a snatch of humming.

The song. Her song. Their version of it, older, altered, passed hand to hand down a century like a coal kept alive in ash.

They were coming toward the chapel. Toward her. And the one who hummed lifted her head as if she'd heard Yara's heartbeat through the stone, and smiled into the dark, and called out in a voice like a bell across water:

"We know you're there. We brought you a gift—and a question."

Yara's hand found the vial in the ledger's spine. Empty now. But her pockets, she realized, were not.

She stepped out of the shadow to meet them.
