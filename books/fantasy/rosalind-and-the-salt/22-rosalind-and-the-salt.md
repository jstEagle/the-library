## 22. Rosalind and the Salt

The coast road smelled of brine and old rain, and Rosalind walked it the way she imagined water would walk if water had somewhere to be: without hurry, without hesitation, taking the shape of every hollow in the land.

She had been on the road for three months now, though *three months* was a thing she knew only because her ledger said so. The ledger told her other things too. That her name was Rosalind. That she had once known a blind priestess whose hands smelled of candlewax and drowned stone. That there was a song she should hum when the nights got long, and that she would not remember learning it, and that this was all right.

She hummed it now, low, under the wind. It had no words she could find, only a rising line like something being lifted out of deep water, and then a falling line like something being given back.

* * *

The village was called Ternholt, though the sign at its edge had been repainted so many times the letters sat on top of each other like sediment. Rosalind came into it at dusk, when the lamps were being lit and the smell of fish stew made the whole street feel like one open kitchen. She wore a thief's coat—she had kept the coat; the ledger said she'd earned it honestly, which was a joke she suspected she'd once found funny—and carried nothing but the ledger, a satchel of small glass vials, and a stick of chalk.

She did not steal memories anymore. She gave them away.

It had taken her some weeks to understand this about herself, reading her own handwriting by firelight: *To the widow in Marrow Bay, one hour of her husband's voice, taken from his mother's keeping after he died at sea. Free.* *To the boy who could not sleep for fear of the tide, one memory of stillness, borrowed from a monk who had too many. Free.* Every entry ended the same way. *Free. Free. Free.*

She had been, the ledger implied, someone who took. Whoever she had been before the blankness, she had been paid in coin to empty people's heads, and she had done it well enough to have enemies, or people who might as well have been. But the woman writing these entries had decided what to do with the skill instead, the way you decide what to do with a knife—same object, different story.

In Ternholt there was an old man who sat outside the tavern every evening watching the road as if it owed him money. His name, the barkeep said, was Aldous, and he had lost forty years to a fever, or so the fever was blamed; the truth was uglier and involved a debt collector from the interior who had come through twenty years back with a guild warrant and a leather case full of vials. Aldous remembered his childhood and his wedding day and then nothing until the fever, a great white gap where his working life should have been, his children's first steps gone, his wife's face during the hard winters gone.

Rosalind sat down beside him on the tavern step.

"You're the memory-thief," he said. Not unkindly. News traveled up the coast road faster than carts did.

"Memory-giver," she said. "Thief is the old trade."

"And what's in it for you?"

She considered the question honestly, because it deserved honesty. "I don't know," she said. "I wrote myself a note about it once. Let me check."

She opened the ledger to the page near the front, the one headed *To me* in her own hand, in ink gone brown. Most of the instructions were practical—which roads flooded, which towns fed travelers, how to know a fair price for a horse (you didn't; you let the horse decide). But near the bottom, in smaller letters, as if written late at night:

*You will want to know why you're doing this. You won't find the reason because you gave it away with everything else. That's the point. A gift with no reason attached stays a gift. Don't go looking for yours. Just keep giving them away.*

Aldous read it over her shoulder, slowly, the way old men read everything. Then he laughed—a dry sound, like wind through a door left open—and said, "Whoever you were, girl, she was smarter than most priests."

"She was smarter than me," Rosalind agreed. "But I get to keep the coat."

* * *

The giving itself was simple, if you had the hands for it. Rosalind had the hands for it; they seemed to remember even when she didn't. She held Aldous's weathered palm between both of hers, thumbed the wax seal off a vial—one hour of a daughter learning to walk, purchased years ago from a grieving father in a town whose name she checked against the ledger to make sure the grief had been honestly come by—and let the memory run down through her fingers like warm water.

She felt it pass through her, the way she felt all of them pass through her: a flicker of grass under small bare feet, a woman's laugh pitched high with pride, the staggering triumph of upright motion. For half a heartbeat it was hers. Then it was his, settling into the white gap like rain into cracked earth, and Aldous's face changed the way faces always changed—not younger exactly, but *lit*, as if a lamp had been carried into a room that had stood dark so long everyone had forgotten it was a room.

He wept without noise, which she had learned was how coastal men wept, salt going back to salt.

"There's more where that came from," she said. "Same time next month, if I'm passing."

"Why?" he asked again, because old men ask twice.

"Because somebody once gave me my name," Rosalind said, "and I intend to spend my whole life paying it forward without ever finding out who they were."

This was not in the ledger. It arrived in her mouth fully formed, the way the song did, and she suspected—I. M. Iversen suspects, and perhaps you do too—that it was the truest sentence she owned.

* * *

She stayed three days in Ternholt, because there was need. There was a girl of ten whose mother had sold two of the child's birthdays to pay a fishing debt—sold them legally, to a traveling broker with guild papers, and the girl walked around those holes in herself like a person walking around furniture in a dark house. Rosalind bought the birthdays back from the broker at four times their worth, out of the small purse she kept for exactly this, and returned them to the girl in the tavern's back room while the mother watched with her hands pressed over her mouth.

"It won't hold forever," Rosalind warned her afterward. "Memories put back grow in crooked. She'll remember the birthday, but she'll also remember, someday, that it was missing. That's not cruelty. That's just how mending works."

"Will she hate me?"

Rosalind thought of a guildhall full of vials, thousands of them, each one a room in someone's head standing dark. She thought of a man named Corvane, whom she did not remember but whose name appeared in her ledger once, in a list titled *Forgive him anyway,* with no explanation, and she had decided—reading it by firelight—that whoever he was, she had earned the right to that instruction and would follow it.

"Yes," she said. "For a while. And then she'll hate the world a little less than she would have, and that's the best any of us manage."

* * *

On the third night she climbed the bluff above Ternholt, where the chapel ruins stood—the drowned chapel was further down the coast, the ledger said, sunk below the tide line where the priestess had kept her candles burning in jars, but every stretch of coast had its own ruin, its own small monument to the century of forgetting. This one had been a watchtower once, then a shrine, then nothing. Its stones were furred with lichen the color of old silver.

Rosalind sat with her back against the wall and opened the ledger to write the day's entries.

*To Aldous of Ternholt, one hour of his daughter's first steps. Free.*
*To Maren, aged ten, her tenth and eleventh birthdays, bought back and returned. Free.*
*To the broker, nothing. He asked. I told him no. Some entries are just for the pleasure of writing them.*

And then she stopped, because there was another entry on the page. Below the last line she had written. In ink that was not yet dry.

Her hand did not shake. She noted that with a strange professional calm, the way you note the weather. Her hand did not shake, and the handwriting was her own—or nearly her own, a hand like hers seen in a mirror, the letters leaning the other way—and it read:

*Entry for tonight, added by no one:*

*A hundred years ago, a prophecy was written in a vault beneath a guildhall, and it named a date, and the date came, and a girl stood in a drowned chapel with a dead god's memory rising out of her like the tide, and two armies' heirs reached for it, and she refused them. She refused the crown and the sword and the throne of remembrance. She opened herself and let it go—all of it, the god's memory and her own, into the sea of collective forgetting, unclaimed and unmourned, so that no one would ever inherit it again.*

*The prophecy writers got one thing wrong. They thought the Salt would wake and choose. It never chooses. It only waits to be refused, and it has been waiting a very long time, and it is grateful, insofar as salt can be grateful, and gratitude is a poor word for what it left behind in the world: a loosening. Memories move more freely now. Griefs surface and are witnessed. Old debts of remembering are being paid all along this coast by a thief with a ledger, and no one can say anymore what is owed to whom, because the great ledger burned, and good riddance.*

*Here is the new prophecy, then, since someone must write one and the vaults are all empty:*

*A girl will come down the coast road carrying nothing. She will have given away her past and kept only a song. Kings and heirs and guildmasters will not know her name, because there is no name to know—it was spent buying the world its freedom. She will sit on a cold stone in the wind and read this entry in her own backwards hand and wonder, briefly, whether she wrote it herself in some hour she doesn't remember.*

*She didn't. Or she did. It doesn't matter. That is the whole of the miracle: it doesn't matter. Power unclaimed stays power no longer. It becomes weather. It becomes the light on the water at evening. It becomes a song a woman hums on a road for no reason, that other people catch and carry inland, humming, not knowing why, until the whole country is quietly full of it.*

*Remember her? No. Better: forget her, and be free.*

Rosalind read it twice. The wind moved the pages. Far below, the lamps of Ternholt burned in their little constellation, and beyond them the sea worked at the darkness, patient as a debt collector, endless as anything.

She looked for wet footprints on the tower floor. There were none. She looked for a second set of handwriting in the ledger, flipping back through months of entries. All hers, all leaning one way—except this one, leaning the other, drying even as she watched.

"Well," she said aloud, to the wind, to the sea, to whoever kept the books now. "That's either a ghost or a very good joke, and I've never met a ghost who could spell."

She laughed. It surprised her, the laugh—big and unguarded, bouncing off the ruined stones—and when it faded she found her eyes were wet, though she could not have said what she was grieving, and did not try. Grief without its object, the ledger had called it once, in an entry about a woman on the cliffs at Marrow Bay. *Let it be. It means the heart remembers what the head cannot. That's not a wound. That's a compass.*

She dipped her pen. Below the strange entry, in her own ordinary hand, she wrote:

*Received. Understood. Still giving them away free.*

Then she closed the book, and stood, and buttoned her thief's coat against the wind, and began the long walk down toward the lights of Ternholt, humming the song with the rising line and the falling line—the lifting-out line, the giving-back line.

Behind her, on the cold stone, the ink finished drying. And if you had been there—if anyone had been there—you would have seen the last of it fade, not erased but *absorbed*, sinking into the lichen and the salt-stained rock the way all unwritten prophecies sink into the world: becoming weather, becoming light on water, becoming, in time, a story told badly in taverns by people who only half believed it, which is the safest place a story can live.

The girl on the road did not look back.

She had written herself instructions about that, too. Somewhere. Probably.

She'd check tomorrow.
