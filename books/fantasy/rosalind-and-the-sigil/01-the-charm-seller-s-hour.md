## 1. The Charm-Seller's Hour

The night market of Greyhollow woke the way a cat wakes: all at once, and with opinions.

By the time the last honest shopkeeper had shuttered his windows and gone home to his supper, the lanterns along Ropewalk Lane were already lit — not by any licensed lamplighter, but by charm-sellers like Xanthe Thackeray, who paid a copper to the watch-turnkey named Brannick and another copper to the boy who pretended not to see Brannick take it, and who then hung their little glass lamps from their stalls and let the whole lane glow like a string of drowned stars.

Xanthe's stall was the third from the corner, wedged between a pie-wife whose pies were legally food and a fortune-teller whose fortunes were legally entertainment. Xanthe's charms occupied the space between: legally nothing at all. A knotted red thread for safe travel. A dried lavender sachet "for restful sleep" (the label said *restful*, which was important; the word *magic* would have been worth Xanthe's neck). Little clay beads baked with salt and rosemary that were guaranteed — in careful, lawyerly language — to "encourage a household toward harmony."

They worked, of course. That was the trouble. But they worked the way good soup works, the way a kind word works: gently, deniably, with no spark of anything the witch-finders could point to and say *there*. Xanthe had spent eleven years learning the difference between a gift and a crime, and the difference, as far as Xanthe could tell, was mostly paperwork and luck.

The luck Xanthe bought fresh every night.

"You're late," said a voice at ankle height.

A lean grey cat with one torn ear sat on the upturned crate beside the stall, washing a paw with the air of someone who had been waiting and intended to make it everyone's problem.

"I'm not late," Xanthe said, low, arranging sachets. "You're early."

"The baker on Cordwainer Street leaves his back window open," the cat said. "I've been sitting here since dusk watching moths. Do you know what it's like, watching moths you're not allowed to eat? It's a cruelty."

"It builds character."

"It builds *resentment*." The cat flicked its torn ear. "Two coppers and I'll tell you something worth hearing."

Xanthe slid two coppers off the stall edge without looking, the way they'd done a hundred nights running. The coin vanished. Somewhere in Greyhollow there was a cat with a small hoard of coppers buried under a specific step, and Xanthe tried not to think about what that meant for the local economy.

"Well?" Xanthe asked.

"There's a man been asking after charm-sellers," the cat said. "Down by the fish gate. Big coat, soft boots. Soft boots are worse than loud ones. Loud boots want you to hear them coming. Soft boots want you not to." The cat paused for effect, because cats always paused for effect. "He showed a badge. Black and silver."

Xanthe's hands went still over the lavender.

Witch-finders didn't come to the night market. That was the unwritten treaty of Greyhollow: the market existed in the cracks of the law, tolerated because half the city council bought love charms and the other half bought hangover cures, and nobody wanted to look too closely at where those came from. Witch-finders came when the treaty broke. Witch-finders came when somebody talked.

"Did he buy anything?" Xanthe asked.

"He bought a pie," said the cat. "He did not enjoy the pie. He watched the lane for an hour and left. But he'll be back. Men with soft boots always come back." The cat stood, stretched until its spine formed a perfect arch, and added, almost as an afterthought: "Also, the rats say the drains under Ropewalk have been busy tonight. More traffic than usual. They're upset about it. Rats are very territorial about their drains."

"Busy how?"

"How should I know? I don't go in drains. I'm not a philosopher." And the cat flowed off the crate and into the crowd, tail high, leaving Xanthe alone with two coppers' worth of unease and a lane full of customers.

* * *

The first hour of the Charm-Seller's Hour belonged to the regulars.

Old Marta came for her harmony bead — her third this season, because her son and his wife kept having the same argument about money, and the beads helped, though not enough to outlast the argument. A dockhand with rope-burned palms bought a traveler's knot and asked, as he always did, whether it worked on ships or just roads, and Xanthe told him, as always, that knots couldn't read maps but they knew water when they saw it, which he found deeply satisfying and meant absolutely nothing, except that it made him walk taller, which arguably was the charm working after all.

And then there was Pip.

Pip was nine, gap-toothed, and the daughter of the pie-wife, and she had appointed herself Xanthe's unofficial apprentice on the grounds that she'd once seen Xanthe talk to a pigeon and had decided this was the most interesting thing that had ever happened in Greyhollow.

"You did it again," Pip whispered, appearing at the stall's side with the stealth of a child who has never once been stealthy. "Last week. The pigeon. You went *coo-coo-coo* and then it went somewhere and came back."

"I was whistling."

"You were *talking*."

"Pip," Xanthe said gravely, "if I could talk to pigeons, do you honestly think I'd be selling lavender?"

"Yes," said Pip, with the terrible clarity of children. "Because you'd still need money, and also you like us." She planted her elbows on the stall. "What do pigeons even talk about?"

"Bread, mostly. Grudges. Which statues have the best views." Xanthe handed her a broken harmony bead, the ones kept in a jar for exactly this purpose. "Here. For your mum. Tell her it's the strongest one I have."

"It's cracked."

"Cracked things work harder. They've got something to prove."

Pip accepted this with the solemnity of a scholar receiving a rare text, and ran off, and Xanthe watched her go and felt the familiar ache underneath the evening — the ache that had a shape now, worn smooth by years, like a stone carried so long it fits the palm. Because Pip was right. Xanthe liked them. All of them, the whole ragged glowing lane of them. And liking people while lying to them, every night, in small necessary ways, was a loneliness with very good company.

The rats arrived at the second bell.

They came one at a time, casual as customers, along the shadowed lip of the drain grate behind the stall, and each one delivered its report in a scratchy whisper that no human ear could have caught under the market noise. This was the other economy Xanthe ran: gossip for crumbs, news for cheese rinds. The rats heard everything in Greyhollow, because everything in Greyhollow eventually passed through a wall or a cellar or a gutter, and the rats were paid to remember.

"Cart traffic at the fish gate after midnight," reported a rat with a chewed ear. "Unmarked cart. Two men unloaded boxes into the customs house. The boxes *breathed*."

"Breathed," Xanthe repeated.

"In a box way," the rat clarified. "Slow. Like sleeping things. We don't like it."

"Noted. Anything else?"

"A dog went missing off Tanner's Row. Not ran away. *Taken.* And—" the rat hesitated, whiskers twitching, and lowered its voice further, which for a rat already whispering was nearly subsonic— "the old ones in the deep tunnels say the stones are humming again. Under the city. The old singing-stones. They haven't hummed since before my grandmother's grandmother."

Xanthe kept their face pleasant and their hands folding sachets, and inside, quietly, a cold thread pulled tight.

Stones that hummed. Boxes that breathed. A witch-finder in soft boots. Any one of these could be nothing. Greyhollow produced strange portents the way the harbor produced fog — constantly, and usually meaning only weather. But three at once felt less like weather and more like a change in the wind itself.

"Thank you," Xanthe said, and set down a rind of good cheese, and the rat took it with dignity and vanished.

That was when she appeared.

* * *

Later — much later, in sewers and mountain passes and the covenant hall itself — Xanthe would try to remember the exact moment Rosalind Vane stepped into the lantern light, and would fail, because memory refused to behave. What stayed was not a moment but a collection: a green cloak beaded with river mist. Dark hair escaping its braid in a way that suggested the braid had lost an argument. Eyes the color of strong tea, doing a slow, skeptical inventory of the stall.

She was not a night-market person. Night-market people moved like water, slipping between stalls, never quite lit. This woman moved like someone who had never once in her life needed to be unlit, and was only now discovering that other people did.

Xanthe clocked the soft boots immediately.

Everything else about her said *scholar*, or maybe *grieving relative sorting through an estate* — there was a quality to her, a held-breath quality, of someone carrying something heavy and fragile. But the boots were wrong. Soft boots, big coat, asking after charm-sellers. The cat's warning sat up in Xanthe's chest and paid attention.

"Welcome to Thackeray's Charms," Xanthe said, warm and easy, the market voice. "Protection, prosperity, peace of mind. No refunds, no curses, no questions."

"No questions," the woman repeated. Her mouth did something complicated. "That's convenient. I have several."

"They're all the same question, usually. *Does it work?*"

"Do they?"

"That depends," Xanthe said, "entirely on what you need to believe."

The woman looked at Xanthe for a long moment — long enough that Xanthe became aware of their own heartbeat, which was unprofessional — and then said, "My grandmother died in the spring. I've been going through her things. She was..." A pause, recalibrating. "She was a particular woman. Particular women collect particular objects, and some of those objects have started behaving strangely, and I've been told — by a woman who sells pies, of all people — that if anyone in Greyhollow can tell me whether I'm losing my mind, it's you."

Behind her, the fortune-teller was pretending very hard to read cards and listening with her entire spine.

"What sort of strangely?" Xanthe asked carefully.

The woman reached into her cloak and set something on the stall between them.

It was a pendant — old silver, tarnished almost black, shaped like a knot that had no beginning and no end. And Xanthe's gift, the secret, executable, eleven-years-hidden gift, did something it had never done in front of a customer before.

It *sang*.

Not aloud. Nothing so obvious. But every beast within thirty feet turned its head toward the stall at once — the cats on the rooftops, the rats at the drain grate, a moth, absurdly, orbiting the pendant like a tiny devoted moon — and Xanthe heard, faint and clear as a bell rung underwater, a single word in the old tongue that no living creature in Greyhollow should have known:

*Bearer.*

Xanthe's hands, thank every merciful power, stayed steady on the stall. Eleven years of practice. Inside, they were screaming.

"It hums," the woman said. "At night. Sometimes it's warm. My grandmother wore it every day of her life and never once took it off, and she left me a letter that says — well. It says a great deal. Most of it sounds insane. One line keeps... staying." She recited it like something memorized against her will: "*When the hour turns, give it to the one who hears what is silent.*"

Silence, from the stall next door, of extremely aggressive card-shuffling.

"And the pie-wife said you were the one who hears what's silent," the woman finished. "So. Am I losing my mind?"

Every instinct Xanthe owned — every survival instinct honed across eleven years of careful, deniable, lavender-scented life — said the same thing: *deny it. Laugh. Sell her a traveler's knot and send her home.*

Instead, Xanthe heard themselves say: "What's your name?"

"Rosalind. Rosalind Vane."

Somewhere on a rooftop, a cat made a sound that was almost a laugh. *Vane,* the night seemed to say. *Of course.*

"Rosalind Vane," Xanthe said slowly, "I'm going to sell you a luck-charm."

"I don't need—"

"Everyone needs a luck-charm. That's the entire business model." Xanthe took down a small knotted cord — the best one, the one they'd made that morning with unusual care, for reasons they hadn't examined at the time and were examining now with mounting alarm — and pressed it into her palm, closing her fingers around it. Their hands touched. The pendant hummed. Every rat in the drain grate fell reverently silent.

"Luck-charms work like this," Xanthe said, quieter now, just for her. "They don't change what happens. They change what you're able to carry. And you, Rosalind Vane, look like a woman carrying something much too heavy, so." A breath. "Come back tomorrow night. After the market closes. There are questions I can answer and questions I can't, and I'd like to find out which yours are — somewhere without quite so many ears."

Rosalind looked down at the cord in her hand. Then up. And the held-breath quality about her shifted — not gone, but shared now, offered, like someone setting down half a weight to shake hands.

"Tomorrow," she agreed. Then, with the ghost of a smile that undid something in Xanthe's chest that had been locked a very long time: "You know, the pie-wife said you'd either help me or rob me."

"She undersells me. I could do both."

Rosalind laughed — actually laughed, brief and startled out of her — and the sound went through the night market like the first crack through ice, and Xanthe stood in the lantern light of the only life they had, holding the knowledge that tomorrow everything might end, and finding, to their considerable surprise, that they didn't care as much as they should have.

She lingered at the edge of the crowd a moment longer, green cloak and dark braid and impossible pendant, and then she was gone into the lanes.

From the drain grate, a rat's whisper, urgent and small: "*Speaker. Speaker, listen — the soft-boot man is back. He's asking the pie-wife your name.*"

Xanthe closed the stall with steady hands.

Inside, the cold thread pulled tight, and somewhere beneath the city, faint as a heartbeat, the old stones began — very softly — to sing.
