## 2. A Date Written in Blood

The candle between them had burned down to a stub, and Rosalind Vane had rehearsed this confession eleven times in her head and abandoned it eleven times.

It wasn't that Xanthe was unkind. Quite the opposite — they had spent the entire evening being so effortlessly kind that Rosalind kept losing her nerve. They'd charmed the innkeeper's surly dog into fetching their bread basket twice. They'd complimented her terrible boots with such sincerity she'd almost believed them fashionable. And now they were leaning across the table at the Drowned Lantern, turning her wine glass slowly by its stem, watching her with those quick grey eyes that seemed to notice everything and judge nothing.

"You've got your thinking face on," Xanthe said. "I've seen it before. It's the one where you look like you're about to argue with me about shipping tariffs."

"I don't argue about shipping tariffs."

"You argued about shipping tariffs for forty minutes last week. I timed it."

"That was *trade policy*, which is different, and you were wrong." Rosalind took a breath. The candle flame wobbled. Somewhere behind her, the tavern hummed with its ordinary evening noise — dice clattering, a fiddle tuning, someone laughing too loudly at a joke that probably wasn't funny. All of it suddenly felt fragile, like scenery painted on paper.

"Xanthe," she said. "I need to tell you something strange."

Xanthe's eyebrows went up, but their voice stayed light. "Strange like 'I once ate an entire wheel of cheese in one sitting' or strange like something else?"

"Something else."

"Ah." They set down the glass. "Go on, then. I'm braced."

Rosalind reached into her satchel — the old leather one with the brass buckles, the one she'd carried since she was nineteen — and drew out a book wrapped in oilcloth. She unwrapped it with hands that were not quite steady. It was a heavy thing, bound in cracked green leather gone soft as cloth with age, its spine stitched with thread that had once been gold. No title. No author. Just a small stamped emblem on the cover: a circle bisected by a line, worn nearly smooth by a century of thumbs.

"My grandmother died in the spring," she said. "You knew that."

"I did. I'm sorry. You said she raised you."

"She did. After my parents went to sea and didn't come back." Rosalind turned the book over in her hands, not opening it yet. Stalling. "When we cleared out her house in Vellamare, there was a chest under her bed. Locked. The key was around her neck when they — when we found her. Inside the chest were letters, some money, and this book. And a note in her handwriting that said: *For Rosalind, when she is ready. Not before.*"

Xanthe had gone very still. Not frightened-still. Listening-still. Rosalind had learned the difference over the past month, and it was one of the hundred small reasons she'd fallen for them.

"What kind of book?" Xanthe asked quietly.

"A prophecy book." She laughed, but it came out thin. "That sounds mad. I know it sounds mad. But it's — it's real, whatever real means for a thing like this. It's written in three hands, three different people across maybe sixty years, and it reads like — like entries. Observations. Some of it's gibberish, or poetry, or both. Some of it's terrifyingly specific. There are passages about the free cities. About a war nobody remembers having. About a Lord-Regent who—" She stopped herself. Careful. Even here, even with Xanthe, certain sentences were dangerous to finish aloud.

"Go on," Xanthe said. Their voice had lost all its playfulness.

"And there's a page near the end." Rosalind opened the book at last, to a ribbon-marked place two-thirds through, and turned it toward the candlelight. "There's a passage about a Sigil-Bearer. Someone who carries a mark, an inheritance, that can reforge something called the covenant. I read it the first night I found the book and thought nothing of it. Old words about old things. Then last week I read it again, properly, and I noticed the date."

She slid the book across the table.

Xanthe read. Rosalind watched them read — watched the candlelight move across their face, watched their lips form the words silently, watched the exact moment their expression changed from polite curiosity to something else entirely.

*The Bearer shall walk unnamed through the market of the hollow city,* the passage ran, in faded brown ink, *and there shall be sold to her a charm of no power at all, and she shall buy it anyway, and the hour of the covenant's waking shall begin.*

Below it, in the same hand, smaller, almost an afterthought:

*Rosalind Vane. The ninth night of the Ember Moon.*

Tonight was the ninth night of the Ember Moon.

"The charm-seller," Rosalind said softly. "In Greyhollow. The night market. That was you."

Xanthe looked up from the page. For a long moment they said nothing at all, and Rosalind felt the silence stretch and pull like taffy.

Then Xanthe snorted.

"Oh, that's — no. Rosalind. *No.*" They pushed the book gently back across the table, but their fingers lingered on the cover a heartbeat longer than dismissal required. "This is a lovely old book and your grandmother clearly adored you, and somebody a century ago wrote down a name because names repeat. Do you know how many Rosalind Vanes there have been? Statistically?"

"One, actually. I checked the Vellamare parish records. My grandmother's family has kept meticulous accounts for four generations, and there has never been another Rosalind Vane."

"Statistically improbable things happen constantly. I once met a man who'd been struck by lightning twice."

"Were either of those strikes prophesied a century in advance?"

"Well, he didn't mention it if so." Xanthe leaned back, arms crossed, but Rosalind noticed their foot was bouncing under the table — a fast, restless rhythm. "Love, listen to yourself. A century-old book predicted you'd buy a luck-charm from a stranger at a night market. What does that even accomplish? What's the prophecy *for*? 'Woman buys trinket.' Riveting. Where's my epic verse?"

"It's not just the trinket." Rosalind's voice dropped. Around them the tavern roared on, oblivious, and she was grateful for it. "Read further. The next passage. The one after the market."

Xanthe hesitated. Then they turned the page.

Rosalind recited it from memory, because she'd read it so many times the words had worn grooves in her mind: *On the ninth night of the Ember Moon the Bearer shall speak the truth of the book to one she trusts above all others, and the trust shall be tested before the candles burn out.*

Her eyes flicked to the candle stub between them. Perhaps half an inch left. Maybe twenty minutes.

"I'm speaking the truth of the book to you," she said. "Right now. Tonight. The ninth of the Ember Moon."

Xanthe stared at the page. Then at the candle. Then at her.

"That could be anyone's candle," they said. "That could be any—"

"Xanthe."

"—any night, honestly, candles burn out all the time, it's practically their whole—"

"*Xanthe.*"

They stopped. The fiddle in the corner had started up a reel; dice rattled; the innkeeper shouted cheerfully at someone about the price of eels. Ordinary life, going on around a table where two people sat frozen over a dead woman's book.

"I don't like it," Xanthe said at last, and all the humor had drained out of their voice, leaving something raw underneath. "I don't like that a book knows your name. I really don't like that a book seems to know what you're doing right now, while you're doing it. That's not how books work, Rosalind. Books are supposed to sit there and be ink."

"I know."

"People write prophecies *after* things happen and pretend they wrote them before. It's the oldest trick there is. Half the temples in the free cities run on it."

"I know that too. I've thought of every explanation. I've been thinking of nothing else for nine days." Rosalind wrapped her hands around her own cup, needing something warm to hold. "But here's what I keep coming back to. My grandmother locked this away and put my name on it. She *knew*. Whatever this book is, she believed it, and she believed it was mine, and she made sure it would find me only when I was ready. And then there's the rest of it, Xanthe. The parts I haven't shown you yet. The parts about the covenant. About magic that used to be lawful. About why it isn't anymore."

Something flickered across Xanthe's face — fast, gone before she could name it. Like a fish turning under dark water.

"Why do you care about that part?" they asked. Too casually.

"Because if any of it is true, then everything I was taught about the world is wrong. Because my grandmother was a quiet, sensible archivist who never told me a single strange thing in eighteen years of raising me, and she spent her whole life sitting on *this*, and I want to know why." Rosalind reached across the table and covered Xanthe's hand. Their skin was cool. Their pulse, when she found it at the wrist, was beating much too fast. "And because you're the person I trust above all others, apparently, according to a book neither of us believes, and I wanted you to hear it from me before I start hunting down scholars who might take it seriously. Which I intend to do. Tomorrow."

"Scholars," Xanthe repeated faintly.

"In Vellamare. The archive there has records going back—"

"Rosalind." Xanthe turned their hand over and caught hers, grip sudden and tight. "Listen to me. Don't show this book to scholars. Don't show it to anyone. Not yet."

"Why not?"

"Because—" They stopped. Rosalind watched them wrestle with something, jaw working, eyes fixed on the middle distance. When they spoke again, each word came out carefully, like stones placed one at a time. "Because books like this get people hurt. Not metaphorically. If even a piece of what you're describing is real — a Sigil-Bearer, a covenant, a Lord-Regent who did something terrible a century ago — then there are people whose entire power depends on that history staying buried. Witch-finders don't exist to catch witches, love. They exist to make sure nobody goes looking."

"That's very cynical."

"That's very *Greyhollow*." Xanthe smiled, but it didn't reach their eyes. "Look. Give me a few days. Let me ask around — quietly, carefully — about whether anyone else has heard of a book like yours. There are people who know about old things. Odd things. If your grandmother's book is genuine, someone will have heard whispers of it. And if it's a forgery, better we learn that before you embarrass yourself in front of an archivist."

Rosalind studied them. The candlelight caught the fine bones of their face, the ink stain permanently shadowing the side of their right hand — a charm-maker's stain, though they'd told her once, laughing, that it was from a bad pen. They were lying about something. She was almost certain of it. She'd known them a month and she was already learning the shape of their silences, the places where the easy charm went thin and something guarded showed through.

But she also knew this: when she'd bought that worthless little luck-charm — a bent nail wrapped in red thread, sold to her by a stranger with quick grey eyes and a smile like a secret — she had felt, for the first time in years, *seen*. And every instinct she owned said that whatever Xanthe Thackeray was hiding, it wasn't malice.

"All right," she said. "A few days. Quietly."

"Quietly," Xanthe agreed, and some of the tension went out of their shoulders. "Thank you. Now — can we please talk about literally anything else? Tell me again about the shipping tariff argument. I want to relive my victory."

"You didn't win, you simply refused to concede—"

Under the table, something touched Rosalind's ankle.

She flinched. "Xanthe. There's a—"

"I know," Xanthe said.

And Rosalind realized, with a lurch, that Xanthe had gone rigid — not listening-still this time but something else, head tilted, eyes unfocused, staring at nothing. Their hand still held hers, and it was trembling now.

"Xanthe, what's wrong?"

"Hush." The word came out flat and urgent. "Don't look around. Keep talking to me about anything. Tariffs. Weather. Anything."

"What is—"

"*Hush.*"

Rosalind talked. She talked about the price of eels, about the innkeeper's terrible singing, about whether her boots were fashionable after all, while Xanthe sat motionless with their gaze fixed on some middle point beyond her shoulder, and their lips moved — barely, silently, shaping words that weren't for her.

Then a sound cut through the tavern noise, thin and high and wrong: a shriek, from somewhere near the kitchen door. Not a cat. Not a child. Something smaller, wilder, terrified.

Every rat in the Drowned Lantern chose that moment to leave. Rosalind saw them — a brown river of them pouring along the baseboards, up the walls, out through gaps in the shutters, dozens of them, silent except for that one awful scream still ringing in her ears. The fiddle player stopped mid-reel. The dice game faltered. A woman stood up and swore.

"They're coming," Xanthe whispered. Their eyes had snapped back into focus and were fixed on Rosalind with an intensity that frightened her more than the rats had. "Rosalind, listen to me very carefully. Witch-finders. Four of them, maybe five, walking up Lantern Row right now, and they're asking after you."

The bottom dropped out of the world.

"*Me?*" Her voice cracked. "That's — they can't be — I haven't done anything. I sell cloth. I pay my taxes. I've never so much as jaywalked—"

"I know. I know you haven't." Xanthe was already moving — sweeping the prophecy book off the table and into their coat, dropping coins beside the candle, catching her wrist. "Which means it's not about what you've done. It's about what you *are*. Or what they think you are. Or what someone told them."

"But how do you know they're—"

"Later. I'll explain later, I promise, everything, I swear it on anything you like. Right now we go out the back." Xanthe pulled her to her feet. The candle stub between them guttered, drowned in its own wax, and the table went dark. "The trust gets tested before the candles burn out. Your book didn't lie about that part."

Through the front windows of the Drowned Lantern, torchlight bloomed on Lantern Row — orange and swaying, too many torches for an ordinary patrol. Voices outside, hard and official. The innkeeper's cheerful bellow had gone quiet.

"Back door," Xanthe breathed. "Now. Run."

* * *

The alley behind the Drowned Lantern smelled of fish heads and rain. Xanthe moved through it like they'd been born there — quick, low, certain — hauling Rosalind past stacked crates and a sleeping drunk who never stirred. At the alley's mouth they stopped, pressed her against the wall, and listened.

Rosalind's heart was slamming so hard she could hear it. "Xanthe. How did you know? Before the rats. You knew."

"Not now."

"Yes now. A book tells me I'll trust you above all others, and ten minutes later you're reading witch-finders' movements out of thin air, and you want me to wait until *later*?"

Xanthe turned. In the narrow dark of the alley, lit only by the distant torch-glow bleeding around the corner, their face was stripped of every ounce of charm — just a frightened, fierce, honest person making a decision. Rosalind watched them make it.

"If we live through tonight," they said, "I will tell you everything. Every single thing. And you're going to wish I hadn't."

From the front of the tavern came a crash — the door going in — and a voice like a drawn blade calling out, clear across the night:

"Rosalind Vane! By edict of the Lord-Regent, in the matter of prohibited inheritances — come out, or we come in!"

Prohibited inheritances. Her grandmother's chest. Her grandmother's *book*.

Xanthe's hand found hers in the dark and gripped it hard.

"Run," they said.
