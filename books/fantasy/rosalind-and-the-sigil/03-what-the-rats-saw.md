## 3. What the Rats Saw

The rat's scream was still ringing in Xanthe's ears as they bolted through Rosalind's back door and into the alley behind the Vane townhouse.

*Witch-finders. Coming now. Six of them, iron-shod, dogs with cut noses.* The words had arrived in that cold, precise way rats delivered bad news — no embellishment, no comfort, just the facts as a small creature understood them, which was usually better than how humans did.

"Xanthe!" Rosalind caught up at the mouth of the alley, breathless, one hand still clutching her grandmother's prophecy book against her chest like a shield. "What is it? What did you hear?"

*Don't say it,* Xanthe thought wildly. *Don't tell her anything. Tell her you heard a noise. Tell her you're drunk. Tell her—*

But the dogs were already baying somewhere beyond the garden wall, and the lie died in their throat.

"This way." They grabbed her wrist. Her pulse jumped under their fingers — quick, frightened, alive. "There's a grate at the end of the lane. Don't ask questions until we're under the street."

"The *sewer*?" Rosalind's voice climbed half an octave. "You want us to go into the—"

"I want us to not be burned," Xanthe said, and hauled.

* * *

The grate came up easier than it should have — Xanthe made a mental note to thank the rats later; someone had been working the rust loose for weeks, which meant someone had been expecting trouble — and then they were down, boots splashing into ankle-deep water that smelled of everything Greyhollow preferred not to think about. Above them, muffled through stone, they could hear the witch-finders' dogs arriving at the Vane house, frustrated, circling.

Rosalind stood in the dark with her skirts hitched up in both fists, breathing hard, staring at Xanthe with an expression Xanthe had seen before. On faces in crowds. On the witch-finders' posters. It was the look people got when they realized something impossible was standing next to them.

"You knew," she said quietly. "You knew they were coming before they got there."

"Rats talk," Xanthe said. It sounded absurd even as they said it. They pressed on anyway, because absurd was better than dead. "I mean — I talk to rats. And cats. And most things, really. Birds are rude. Fish don't listen. But rats—" They gestured vaguely at the darkness, where, if you knew how to look, dozens of small eyes were glinting. "Rats are excellent correspondents."

Silence. Water dripped somewhere far off. A rat the size of a loaf of bread emerged from a side channel, sat up on its haunches, and regarded Rosalind with what Xanthe could only describe as professional interest.

"You're a Speaker," Rosalind said.

The word landed like a dropped knife. Xanthe's whole body went cold. "You know that word?"

"My grandmother's book uses it." Rosalind's voice was steady, but her knuckles had gone white around the book. "Page after page. *The Speaker shall come from among the small voices. The Speaker shall hear what walls conceal.* I thought it was poetry. I thought—" She laughed, a strange, cracked sound. "I thought a lot of things tonight that turned out to be wrong."

"They'll kill me," Xanthe said. Flatly, because there was no gentler version. "If they catch me. That's not dramatics, that's the law. Beast-speech is corruption of the natural order. First offense, the tongue. Second offense—" They didn't finish. Everyone in Greyhollow knew how second offenses ended.

Rosalind looked at them for a long moment. The sewer dark pressed in. Somewhere above, the dogs had gone quiet, which was somehow worse than the baying.

And then she said: "Say something to it."

"What?"

"The rat. Say something. I've read about Speakers my whole life in books that called them monsters, and now one is standing in sewage holding my hand, and I need—" Her voice wobbled. "I need to know what it actually looks like. What *you* actually look like, doing it. Please."

No one had ever asked that. In twenty-six years of hiding, no one had ever asked Xanthe Thackeray to *show* them. People asked them to hide, to deny, to be less. Never once: *show me.*

Xanthe crouched. The big rat waited, whiskers twitching.

"Hullo, Bramble," Xanthe said — because of course it was Bramble, chief of the Undermarket colony, who owed Xanthe three favors and never let anyone forget it. To Rosalind it would have sounded like nonsense syllables, clicks and chirrs shaped almost-but-not-quite like human words. "You pulled the grate loose. Thank you."

*Borrowed a man's tools,* Bramble said. *He won't miss them. He sleeps like the dead and steals cheese from his own wife, so we consider it justice.*

"He says he borrowed the tools from a man who deserved it," Xanthe translated, and looked up at Rosalind, braced for horror.

What they saw instead stopped their heart.

Rosalind had gone down onto her knees in the filthy water without a thought for her dress, her face lit with something Xanthe had only ever seen on children watching fireworks. Wonder. Pure, undiluted, unguarded wonder.

"It's like listening to someone sing in a language you don't know," she breathed, "but understanding it anyway. Is that — do I have some of it? Is that why the book chose my name?"

"I don't know," Xanthe said honestly. "Nobody knows what Speakers are. We just... are."

Bramble sniffed, deeply unimpressed by the sentimentality. *The tall one smells of old paper and lavender. Also she is about to cry. Tell her the water is full of things that will make her cry worse if she kneels in it.*

"He says the water is unsanitary," Xanthe said.

That broke whatever spell held them both — Rosalind laughed, wet and startled, and scrambled up, and just like that the terror receded enough to breathe around. Not gone. Never gone. But manageable, the way fear became once you'd shown someone your worst secret and they hadn't run.

"We should keep moving," Xanthe said. "Bramble, is there a way out that doesn't come up inside a witch-finder's boot?"

*The Old Tanner's Run,* Bramble said at once. *Dry most of the year. Comes up behind the tannery, which stinks so badly even the finders' dogs refuse it.* He paused, and his whiskers drooped slightly. *Speaker. There is more. You should hear it walking.*

"That's ominous," Xanthe told Rosalind. "When a rat says 'you should hear it walking,' it means exactly what you think it means."

They walked. Bramble led them through branching channels with the confidence of a landlord giving a tour, chattering the whole way — gossip, mostly, because rats gossiped the way rivers ran, constantly and without apparent effort. Xanthe translated selectively. Rosalind learned that the baker on Cordwainer Street beat his apprentices (she went very still at that), that the harbormaster was smuggling brandy in coffins labeled as plague victims ("efficient," she said faintly), and that half the cats in Greyhollow considered themselves employed by the fishmongers' guild at rates no reasonable person would accept.

It was, Xanthe realized somewhere in the third mile, the strangest and best conversation of their life. Here was a woman who had learned, in a single night, that prophecies were real and witch-finders were at her door, and instead of shattering she was asking follow-up questions. About rat union disputes.

"Why are you smiling?" Rosalind asked.

"Because you're taking this remarkably well."

"Oh, I'm falling apart completely," she said cheerfully. "I'm doing it while walking, though. My grandmother always said a Vane falls apart with dignity or not at all."

* * *

The Old Tanner's Run was dry as promised and smelled like a crime against nature, exactly as promised. Bramble stopped where the tunnel widened, and his manner changed — the gossip fell away, and something older and graver settled over his small sharp face.

*Now,* he said. *The thing you should hear walking.*

The rats came out of the dark in numbers. Dozens, then scores, lining the tunnel walls in silent ranks, and Xanthe felt the hair rise on their arms, because rats did not gather like this. Rats squabbled and schemed and scattered. They did not assemble like a jury.

*Three nights past,* Bramble said, *a man came down into the deep tunnels. Not a finder. Worse. He carried iron that hummed, and he went to the sealed place beneath the Undermarket — the door that has been shut since our grandmothers' grandmothers. And he opened it, and he took pages. Old pages, soft as moth-wing, kept there since before the city had its name.*

Xanthe's mouth went dry. "Pages."

*We guarded them,* Bramble said simply. *As our line has always guarded them. It was the first bargain, Speaker. Before the breaking. The small voices keep what the tall folk lose.* His eyes — black, bright, terribly intelligent — fixed on Xanthe. *The man left two pages behind. Torn, in his hurry. We brought them to you.*

A rat smaller than Bramble scurried forward bearing, clutched delicately in its teeth, two scraps of heavy old paper.

Xanthe took them with hands that shook. Even by rat-light — the phosphorescent fungus the colonies cultivated — the script was legible, faded brown ink on paper gone amber with age. And the handwriting was familiar, because Xanthe had spent the evening pretending not to study the book Rosalind carried.

It was the same hand. The same prophecy. The missing pages.

Rosalind crowded close, reading aloud in a whisper. "*...and the Bearer shall walk in blood and fire, and the covenant broken shall call for mending...*" She stopped. Started again, lower. "*And the Bearer shall not mend it alone, for the covenant has two hands. There shall be a Speaker, who hears the small voices, and the Speaker's ear is the keystone lost from the arch—*'"

She looked up at Xanthe.

"*—and the Speaker's name is written in the keeping of the beasts, who have not forgotten it, though men have.*"

The tunnel was very quiet. A hundred rats watched, and none of them moved.

"They knew," Xanthe said. Their voice came out strange, thin. "A hundred years ago, whoever wrote this knew there'd be a — me. Someone who talks to beasts. They wrote it down and gave it to the rats to keep, because they knew that people would burn the book but the rats—" They laughed, and it came out closer to a sob. "The rats *kept it*. For a hundred years. Waiting for someone who could ask."

*We keep all bargains,* Bramble said, with dignity. *Even the ones men forget they made.*

"Xanthe." Rosalind's hand found theirs in the dark. Her palm was warm; theirs was ice. "This is why the finders came tonight. Not just the book — Ashfall's people have been digging under the city. They're looking for the same pages. They got most of them, but we have the part that matters. We have *you*."

"And that makes me the most dangerous criminal in Greyhollow," Xanthe said. "Do you understand what that means? It's not running from the law anymore. It's the Lord-Regent himself. He doesn't send six witch-finders after smugglers. He sends armies after keystones."

"Then we'd better not be here when the army arrives." Rosalind said it lightly, but her grip on Xanthe's hand was fierce. "Look at me. An hour ago I was a woman who found her name in a book and thought she was going mad. Now I'm a prophesied Sigil-Bearer standing in a sewer with a Speaker and a hundred rats, and do you know what I feel?"

"Terrified?"

"Furious," Rosalind said. "Furious, and awake, and *seen* — properly seen, maybe for the first time in my life. And I think you know exactly how that feels, because I watched it happen to you twenty minutes ago when you spoke to Bramble and I didn't flinch." She lifted their joined hands. "You showed me the worst thing about you and I got down on my knees in sewage to see it better. That's not nothing, Xanthe Thackeray. That's the opposite of nothing."

Above them, faint but unmistakable, came the tolling of the alarm bells. One, then two, then all of them — the signal that spread across Greyhollow when the witch-finders declared a hunt open. Somewhere up there, posters were being pasted to walls. Somewhere up there, neighbors who bought charms from a quiet stall in the night market were learning what a Speaker was, and being told to report one.

*The tall one speaks truth,* Bramble said. *The hunt is called. You cannot stay in the tunnels; the finders know the old maps now — the man who took the pages took copies of the maps too. You must leave Greyhollow entirely, Speaker. Tonight.*

"How?" Xanthe asked. "The gates will be sealed by dawn."

*Not the gates,* Bramble said. *The river. And not by rats — this is beyond even our reach. Go to the Undermarket, to the charm-sellers who owe you. Ask for Marlowe. Tell him the small voices sent you and the debt is called.* His whiskers twitched, and for the first time all night, the old rat looked — there was no other word for it — afraid. *And Speaker. When you go, take the pages. Take the book. Keep them together. Because there is a last line on the torn edge of the second page, half-burned, and my reader-rats have argued over it for three nights.*

Xanthe turned the scrap over. There, along the ragged tear, ghost-faint letters survived:

*...for the covenant demands...*

Demands *what?* Blood, the old stories always said. Blood, or obedience, or silence. The sentence ended in torn paper and a century of darkness.

"Bramble," Xanthe said slowly, "what does the covenant demand?"

*That,* said the chief of the Undermarket rats, *is the question that will get us all killed, one way or another. Come. Marlowe keeps a boat, and the river does not check papers — yet.*

And as they followed him deeper into the dark, Xanthe felt the terror in their chest shift shape — not gone, never gone, but changed, the way lead changes in fire. They had spent their whole life hiding. Now they were known — by a woman who knelt in filth to see them clearly, and by a hundred small voices who had kept their name safe for a hundred years.

It was the most frightening thing that had ever happened to them.

It was also, Xanthe thought, gripping Rosalind's hand and the torn pages tight, the first night of their life they hadn't spent alone.

Behind them, far above, the bells went on ringing — and somewhere in the citadel on the hill, a man with cold eyes was reading stolen pages by candlelight, and learning, at last, that the keystone lived.
