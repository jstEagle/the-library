## 4. The Usurper's Edict

The candles in Lord-Regent Corvus Ashfall's study were the finest tallow in the Nine Cities, and he despised them.

They guttered. They wept wax down their brass holders like nervous servants. They cast shadows that moved when they had no business moving, and Corvus Ashfall had built a kingdom — no, he corrected himself, a *regency*, and one day, if the gods were generous and the people forgetful, an empire — on the principle that nothing should move unless he commanded it.

He sat at his desk in the Citadel of Greyhollow with three reports before him, arranged in a fan as precise as a hand of cards. He did not read them immediately. That was the mistake lesser men made: snatching at information like beggars at bread. Information was a vintage. It wanted decanting. It wanted to be held up to the light and examined for sediment.

Outside the window, Greyhollow glittered below the citadel rock, ten thousand hearth-fires and lanterns and the sputtering glow of the night market, where his subjects traded in charms against headache and heartbreak and hens that wouldn't lay. Harmless nonsense, mostly. He permitted it. A people who believed luck could be bought in paper packets were a people who would never ask why their luck had gone so bad in the first place.

He picked up the first report. Routine. Grain shipments from Vellamare, delayed by weather. He set it aside.

The second concerned a smuggler named Odo Finch who had been skimming tariffs for six years with a cleverness Corvus almost admired. Almost. He made a small mark beside the name. Odo Finch would be arrested in spring, after the winter storms made the coastal roads impassable and he could not run. There was no urgency. Clever men who thought themselves safe grew careless; careless men grew slow; slow men were simply arrests waiting to happen. Corvus had learned long ago that patience was the sharpest instrument in any ruler's kit. Knives got all the songs written about them, but patience won the wars.

Then there was the third report.

It was thin. Two pages, written in the cramped hand of an informant who signed himself only as *Moth*. Moth was a rat-catcher by trade, which Corvus appreciated — there was a certain poetry in it — and Moth claimed that in the night market of Greyhollow, in a charm-stall run by a person of no particular reputation, something had happened that should not have been possible.

A cat had spoken. And someone had answered.

Corvus read the passage three times. The informant's account was maddeningly vague — secondhand, overheard, filtered through the ale-sodden memory of a fishwife — but the shape of it was unmistakable. A stall-keeper, murmuring to a street cat as though holding conversation. The cat, attentive. Listening. And then, according to the fishwife, the cat had *answered back*, and the stall-keeper had laughed as though the two of them shared a joke.

*Beast-speech.* The old gift. The forbidden gift. The gift that had not been confirmed in the living world for a hundred years — not since the last Speakers had been burned or broken or driven into the wild places, and the covenant between the realms of man and beast had been shattered like a dropped plate, its pieces scattered, its very existence rewritten out of the histories until children laughed at the notion that animals had ever talked at all.

Corvus leaned back in his chair and steepled his fingers, and for a long moment he allowed himself the luxury of feeling something very much like joy.

People assumed — his captains, his clerks, the trembling mayors of the free cities — that Corvus Ashfall feared magic. They had it precisely backward. Fear was for men who did not understand what they faced. Corvus understood perfectly. Magic was not a threat to his rule.

Magic was a *competitor*.

A farmer who could ask the crows where the wolves slept needed no Lord-Regent to protect him. A city that could bargain with the river did not need his granaries. A child born speaking to beasts would grow up knowing, in her bones, that authority was not handed down from citadels — it was *negotiated*, freely, between equals, and could be renegotiated whenever the terms went rotten. That was the poison at the heart of the old covenant. Not the talking animals. The equality.

So a century ago, his grandfather's grandfather's generation had done what wise rulers always do with inconvenient truths: they had burned them, buried them, and built a fortress on top. The Edict of Quiet. The witch-finders. The histories, carefully amended. It had worked beautifully.

And now, apparently, one of the old truths had crawled back out of its grave and set up a charm-stall in his own night market.

"Insolent," Corvus said aloud, to the empty room, and found he was smiling.

There came the knock he had been expecting — two raps, precise, unhurried. His visitors never waited to be called twice. That was why he kept them.

"Enter."

Captain Serah Dune of the Witch-Finders came in the way she did everything: without flourish. She was a lean woman of perhaps forty, grey at the temples, with a burn scar along her left jaw that she had never once explained to anyone and never once been asked about twice. She wore no insignia beyond a plain iron pin, because witch-finders, Corvus had decreed, should look like anyone. It made the population politer. One never knew who might be wearing the pin.

Behind her came Undersecretary Pell, a round, damp man whose genius lay entirely in logistics and whose courage lay entirely in other people. Between them, they were the two halves of Corvus's machinery: the will and the wheels.

"Lord-Regent." Serah stopped three paces from the desk. Pell hovered near the door, as though the reports might be contagious.

"You've read this?" Corvus slid the third report across the desk.

"I read it this afternoon, my lord." Her voice was flat as slate. "I also read Moth's previous four reports. He has reported a Speaking incident twice before. Once it was a drunkard imitating a rooster. Once it was an actual rooster."

"And your assessment?"

Serah Dune was silent for a moment. Corvus watched her the way he watched everything — closely, patiently, cataloguing. This was the thing about Serah that made her valuable and, in the deep cellar of his mind where he kept his honest thoughts, slightly unsettling: she did not perform loyalty. She simply *was* loyal, the way stone was hard, without needing applause for it. Men who performed loyalty could be bought. Men who simply possessed it could only be spent.

"The rooster," she said at last, "was genuine theater. But this report is different. The fishwife is known to me. She exaggerates prices, not events. And the stall-keeper—" she consulted nothing, having committed it to memory, "—sells charms at the corner of Tallow Row. No guild registration. No family name on record. Arrived in Greyhollow some years ago from nowhere in particular. Pays rent promptly. Has no enemies, no debts, and no history."

"No history," Corvus repeated softly. "In a city of forty thousand souls. How does a person acquire no history, Captain?"

"Carefully, my lord."

"Precisely." He rose and walked to the window, hands clasped behind his back, looking down at the sprawl of lights. Below, faintly, he could hear the night market — a distant murmur of commerce and laughter, the sound of a city that believed itself safe. "Do you know what I admire most about animals, Captain Dune?"

"My lord?"

"Their honesty. A wolf does not pretend to be your friend before it eats you. A crow does not flatter. When beasts bargained with men, a century ago, every agreement was true — sealed in scent and sound, unbreakable, because neither side could lie to the other." He turned. "Men lie constantly. I say this with affection; I am myself an accomplished liar. But beasts cannot be corrupted, cannot be bribed, cannot be made partners in a lie. Which means a Speaker is the one creature in my dominion I cannot control through ordinary means. Do you see the problem?"

Pell, by the door, made a small noise like a swallowed hiccup. Serah said, "I see it, my lord."

"One Speaker," Corvus continued, pacing now, slow and deliberate, boots quiet on the rug, "is a curiosity. Two is a rumor. Three is a movement. And movements, Captain, are how regencies end and revolutions begin. So we are going to make certain there is never a second Speaker. We are going to find this charm-seller, and we are going to do it quietly, thoroughly, and above all *publicly* — because the lesson matters more than the criminal. Do you understand the distinction?"

"A public example."

"A public *education*," Corvus corrected, with the mild tone of a tutor. "The people must see that the old gifts lead only to the pyre. Not out of cruelty — cruelty is wasteful, it breeds martyrs — but out of certainty. Fear fades. Certainty endures. When a mother in Vellamare feels the old blood stir in her child, I want her to smother the thought herself, out of simple good sense, before it ever becomes a word. That is the true Edict of Quiet, Captain. It isn't enforced. It's *inherited*."

He returned to the desk and drew a sheet of heavy cream paper toward him — the good paper, the kind reserved for instruments of state — and began to write in his small, immaculate hand. Pell crept forward to witness, as protocol required.

*"By order of the Lord-Regent of Greyhollow, Protector of the Nine Cities: let the free cities be sealed. Let the roads be watched, the harbors questioned, the gates counted twice. Let every stranger be asked whence they came and whither they go. Let the witch-finders act with full authority of fire and rope, answerable to none but the throne."*

He paused, quill hovering, and considered the last line. Then he wrote it:

*"Let no Speaker live."*

He signed it, blotted it, and held it out. Pell took it with both hands, the way one carries a sleeping snake.

"There is one more matter." Corvus sat again, and now his voice lost its lecture-hall warmth and became something quieter, which those who knew him understood to be far more dangerous. "This charm-seller. I want them taken alive."

Serah's eyebrows rose a fraction — for her, an expression of open astonishment. "Alive, my lord? The edict—"

"The edict is for the *population*, Captain. The edict teaches. But this one is a specimen. A living Speaker is a library burning down room by room, and I intend to read every book in it before the last page goes. How the gift manifests. Whether it can be detected. Whether it can be—" he chose the word with care, "—*transferred*. Imagine it, Captain. An army that speaks to its horses. Crows as couriers no assassin can intercept. Wolves that guard my borders and ask no wages." His eyes had gone bright, the way they did when he looked at maps. "The covenant broke because men wanted mastery instead of partnership. I am merely proposing a more efficient arrangement."

For just a moment — half a heartbeat, gone so quickly that a less patient observer would have missed it — something moved behind Serah Dune's eyes. Not disagreement, exactly. Something more like a cart shifting on a road, a load settling differently.

"Taken alive," she repeated. "Understood, my lord."

"Good." Corvus smiled, warm as banked coals. "You have your license, Captain. Fire and rope, as written. Spend it wisely — and spend it *visibly*. Let the free cities feel the walls closing. Panic makes people talk, and talking people make mistakes, and mistakes make footprints." He waved a hand, already turning back to his reports, to Odo Finch and his grain shipments and his beautiful, orderly kingdom. "Begin tonight. The market will still be open. Charm-sellers keep late hours."

Serah bowed — a short, economical bow — and turned for the door. Pell scuttled after her like a deferential shadow.

At the threshold, Corvus spoke once more, without looking up.

"Oh — Captain. One thing more."

She paused.

"If the specimen resists capture — if it comes to a choice between a dead Speaker and none at all — take the dead one. A martyr is nearly as useful as a library, and considerably easier to store." He turned a page. "Good hunting."

The door closed behind them with a soft, final click.

* * *

Serah Dune walked the long corridor of the citadel in silence, Pell trotting at her elbow, the sealed edict tucked inside her coat like a coal she was carrying to a fire.

"Well," said Pell, wiping his damp forehead with a handkerchief that had seen better decades. "Sealing the cities. That'll cause a stir at the harbor. Half the trade charters—"

"The charters will survive," Serah said. "Charters always do. It's people who don't."

Pell blinked at her, uncertain whether this was wit or warning, and settled — as he usually did — on silence.

They descended the outer stair into the cold. Below the citadel rock, the night market glowed against the dark like a spill of embers, and Serah could just make out the bunting of the stalls strung between lampposts, the drift of smoke from chestnut carts, the small bright chaos of a city enjoying its ignorance.

Twenty years she had worn the iron pin. Twenty years of pyres and interrogations and files on frightened people, and in all that time she had told herself the same story every officer of the Edict told herself: *the law is the law, and worse things walk without it.* It was a good story. It had carried her a long way, like a sturdy pair of boots.

But tonight, walking down toward the market lights with an order in her pocket that read *let no Speaker live*, she found herself thinking about the fishwife. About the cat. About a charm-seller with no history, laughing at a joke no human could hear.

About what, exactly, everyone was so afraid the animals might say.

She pushed the thought down where she kept all the others — deep, orderly, unexamined — and unhooked the lantern from her belt.

"Market first," she said to Pell. "Quietly. We watch the Tallow Row stall until we know everything: the face, the hours, the customers, the suppliers. Nobody moves tonight. Tomorrow we seal the cities, and the day after—" she looked up at the citadel looming black against the stars, "—we pay our new friend a visit."

And far below, in the crowd and candlelight of the night market, a woman with a luck-charm in her pocket was lingering at a stall on Tallow Row, laughing at something the charm-seller had said — while overhead, unnoticed by either of them, a rat froze on a rooftop ridge, whiskers trembling, staring down at the two figures climbing the stair from the citadel with their lantern lit.

The rat turned and ran.
