## 5. Outlaw Road

### Xanthe

The smuggler's wagon smelled of pickled herring and lies, and Xanthe loved it immediately.

"You'll want to ride in the false bottom," said Marta Quill, kicking aside a tarp to reveal a hollow beneath the fish barrels. "Witch-finders don't search what they're afraid of smelling."

"I'm not afraid of a little herring," Rosalind said, though her face suggested otherwise.

"It's not the herring they fear, love. It's the eels." Marta grinned with three teeth too many and dropped the tarp back over them both.

Xanthe had known Marta for six years — since the night she'd caught Xanthe arguing with her mule about the proper distribution of carrots and had, instead of screaming for the watch, sat down on a crate and asked, "And what's the mule saying, then?" That was the thing about smugglers: they were criminals already, so one more crime hardly made the ledger tremble. Smuggling contraband silk was a trade. Smuggling a person was a favor. Smuggling a Speaker out of Greyhollow while witch-finders burned charm-stalls at every gate — that was an adventure, and Marta had never once in her life turned down an adventure.

The wagon lurched into motion. Through a knothole in the planking above, Xanthe watched the walls of Greyhollow slide past, torchlight crawling along the stone like something living and hungry. At the Northgate, a line of travelers waited while witch-finders in ash-grey cloaks moved from cart to cart. One of them carried a long brass horn, curved like a swan's neck, and pressed it to his ear against the sides of barrels, listening.

"They're using listeners," Rosalind whispered. Her breath stirred the hair at Xanthe's temple. In the dark of the false bottom they were pressed shoulder to hip to knee, closer than they'd ever been, and Xanthe found it difficult to think about anything except the warmth of her.

"Listeners hear enchantment," Xanthe murmured back. "Spells have a sound, if you've got the ear for it. My charms are harmless — dried lavender and wishful thinking — but a listener can't tell the difference between a luck-charm and a curse. To them it all rings the same."

"And you? Do spells ring true to you?"

"Everything does." Xanthe hesitated. It still felt dangerous, saying it aloud, even here, even to her. Especially to her. "Birds mostly talk about food and territory. Dogs are philosophers. Rats are gossips with excellent memories and no loyalty whatsoever. The city has been talking to me my whole life, Rosalind. I just never let anyone hear me answer."

Rosalind was quiet a moment. Then, softly: "What is the city saying tonight?"

Xanthe closed their eyes and listened past the wheels, past the herring, past the frightened hearts at the gate. "Fear," they said. "The cats have moved their kittens into the storm drains. The pigeons won't roost near the citadel. And the rats say—" A pause. "The rats say the witch-finders have your description. Dark hair, green cloak, carries an old book. They're calling you the Sigil-Bearer now. Not a suspect. A *title*."

"Wonderful." Rosalind laughed, but it came out thin. "I went twenty-six years as nobody at all, and now I'm a title."

"You were never nobody." The words escaped before Xanthe could weigh them, and in the dark there was no way to pretend they hadn't been said. Rosalind's hand found theirs in the blackness — deliberate, unhurried, fingers lacing through fingers — and neither of them said anything else for a long while, because some things are better said by hands.

At the gate, the wagon stopped. Boots crunched around it. The brass horn touched the barrel above their heads, and Xanthe heard it too — a faint hum, the residual shimmer of the warding sigil Marta had paid a nervous hedge-witch to paint inside the false bottom. Legal enough to be sold openly before the edict; damning enough now to hang them all.

A rat was sheltering under the wagon's axle. Xanthe reached out along the old channels of speech, careful as threading a needle. *Little cousin. The man with the horn. What is he hearing?*

*He hears the sigil humming,* the rat said. *He is deciding whether it is worth opening the barrels.*

*What will he decide?*

*That depends on whether his captain is watching.* A pause, whiskers twitching somewhere in the dark. *She is watching.*

*Then we need him to decide it isn't worth the trouble.*

*Rats do not work for free,* said the rat, with the smugness of its entire species.

*I know. Name it.*

*The cheese in the third barrel. All of it. And you owe me a story someday, Speaker. A true one.*

"Deal," Xanthe breathed — and felt Rosalind startle slightly at hearing them speak to empty air, then settle, then lean closer, listening to a conversation she couldn't follow the way one listens to music in a language one doesn't speak.

Above them, the rat did its work: a scrabble of claws, a shriek, the sound of small chaos in the fish barrels. The listener yanked the horn away with an oath. "Rats," he muttered. "Whole city's gone to the rats."

"Always was," said Marta cheerfully, and the wagon rolled through the Northgate into the dark beyond the walls.

Only when Greyhollow was a smudge of orange behind them did Marta pull the tarp back and look down at them both, her grin gone quiet. "Right," she said. "Now somebody tells me why the Lord-Regent's own witch-finders want the two of you badly enough to seal a city. And I'd best believe the story, mind, because I've staked my wagon, my neck, and my reputation on it, and I'm fond of all three."

### Rosalind

They told the story twice on the road — once for Marta, once for her brother Fen, who drove the second wagon and interrupted constantly — and by the third telling Rosalind had learned something strange about truth: it grew easier to carry each time you set it down and picked it up again.

"So the book named her," Fen said, jerking his thumb at Rosalind without taking his eyes off the road. "By name. Before she was born."

"Before her grandmother died, anyway," Xanthe said. They rode beside the wagon now, having refused the false bottom on principle once the gates were behind them. "And it named me too. We found the missing pages sewn into the binding. There's a whole passage about a Speaker — someone who talks with beasts, who's meant to stand with the Bearer when the covenant—"

"When the covenant what?" Marta called from the rear wagon.

"That's the trouble." Rosalind had the book open on her knees, wrapped in oilcloth against the drizzle. "The pages tear off mid-sentence. *When the covenant is reforged at the three stones, the Bearer shall—*" She traced the broken line with one finger. It always stopped her heart a little, touching ink that had waited a hundred years for her. "*Shall* what? Die? Reign? Have tea? The book doesn't say."

"The book absolutely says something," Fen said darkly. "Books that hide things are worse than books that lie. At least liars you can catch."

Marta pulled her wagon alongside. "You keep saying *covenant*. My grandmother used to swear by the Covenant the way folk today swear by the Regent — 'Covenant keep you,' 'Covenant witness' — and nobody ever told me what it was. Some old religion?"

"Not religion." Xanthe had gone very still, in the way Rosalind was beginning to recognize: the stillness of someone listening to several conversations at once. "History. Real history, the kind they burned. Rosalind — read the marginalia again. The notes in the margins. Whose handwriting is it?"

Rosalind turned back forty pages, to where the cramped annotations began. "My grandmother's. Or — no. Wait." She tilted the page toward the grey light. "It's older than hers. She copied these. Look — same words, different hand. She was transcribing someone else's notes. Someone who'd studied the book before her."

"Read them aloud," Marta said. "All of them. We've got miles yet and nothing to do but listen."

So Rosalind read. The road unspooled beneath the wheels, wet hills folding into wetter valleys, while her voice carried the margin-notes forward a century:

"*The Covenant was no metaphor. It was law, written and witnessed. Before Ashfall's grandfather, magic was not crime but contract. Every Speaker registered at the covenant halls. Every beast-kindred sent voices to the councils. The tablet bound both realms: men would keep the wild places, beasts would lend their strength, and neither would rule alone.*"

"*Ashfall's grandfather broke it deliberately. He stole the original tablet and hid it, then wrote new histories declaring the beasts mad and the Speakers deceivers. Fear is easier to legislate than trust.*"

"*Three relics held the covenant's power: the sigil-stone, the quill of truth, and the tablet itself. Scattered to the free cities when the purges came. As long as they remain apart, the covenant cannot be remade.*"

"*If you are reading this, child, then the book chose you, which means the choosing has begun again. Find the others. Trust the Speaker. And whatever the last page seems to demand — question it. Prophecies are written by people, and people make errors, and some errors are made on purpose.*"

Silence held the little caravan for the length of a mile. Rain ticked on the oilcloth. Somewhere upslope, a crow made a remark that Xanthe, after a moment, translated with a crooked smile: "She says we're going the wrong way around the hill, and also that Fen owes her bread."

"Fen owes everyone bread," Marta said. "It's his defining feature."

But Rosalind barely heard the joke. She was looking at the last line she'd read, the one about errors made on purpose, and feeling the shape of something cold move beneath her ribs. *Question it.* Her grandmother — or her grandmother's teacher — had known how the prophecy ended. Had known, and had hidden a warning inside a warning, the way you'd hide a key under a doormat for someone you'd never meet.

"Your family kept this book a hundred years," Marta said quietly. "Kept it, and passed it down, and never told you a word until after she was gone. That's not cruelty, girl. That's hope with its head down. You don't shout hope when the wrong people are listening."

"My grandmother never shouted anything in her life," Rosalind said, and heard her voice crack on the middle of it, and was surprised by the grief arriving sideways, here on a smuggler's road in the rain. "She pressed flowers. She alphabetized her spices. And the whole time she was — she was part of *this*. She knew what I'd be."

"She didn't know," Xanthe said gently. "She *hoped*. There's a difference, and it matters. If she'd known, she'd have taught you to fight. Instead she left you a book and a warning, which means she wanted you to choose. Same as the prophecy wants us to choose, maybe. Maybe that's the whole point of the torn page."

Rosalind looked up at them — this stranger of three days' acquaintance who spoke with rats and gave away luck for pennies and had burned their own stall rather than let the witch-finders take so much as a dried sprig of lavender as evidence. Three days. It felt like years. It felt like the first honest years of her life.

"I keep waiting to wake up," she admitted. "Back in my flat, with the rent due and the book just a book. This morning I woke up under a smuggler's tarp smelling of herring, and my first thought was *good, we're ahead of schedule*, and I thought — who *am I* now?"

"Someone ahead of schedule," Xanthe said, and grinned, and Rosalind laughed despite everything, despite the sealed city shrinking behind them and the sealed future ahead.

Fen cleared his throat with tremendous delicacy. "Not to interrupt the — whatever this is — but if there's three relics in three free cities, and the Regent's sealing the roads one by one, we've got a arithmetic problem. Vellamare's four days south. After that, Highmoor's pass closes with the first snow. And word is Ashfall's offering a pardon to any city that hands over Speakers." He glanced at Xanthe, apologetic. "No offense meant."

"Some taken," Xanthe said lightly. "But accurate."

"Then we need friends in Vellamare," Marta said. "And lucky for you two, I know every smuggler, archivist, and disgraced customs officer in that city. But understand what you're asking, both of you. This stops being an escape the moment we point south. Escapes end. This—" she gestured at the book, the rain, the road, the whole improbable enterprise— "this is a *quest*. Quests have a mortality rate."

"We know," Rosalind said.

"Do you?" Marta's eyes were sharp and kind at once. "Because I've carried runaways before, and runaways run *from*. You two are running *to*. Different animal entirely. Needs different feeding."

Rosalind looked at Xanthe, and Xanthe looked back, and something passed between them that needed no beast-speech at all — an agreement, quiet and complete, like two hands lacing together in the dark.

"To, then," Rosalind said. "Vellamare. The sigil-stone. Whatever my grandmother started, we finish."

Marta nodded slowly, snapped the reins, and said, "Well. I did always want to be in a legend. Terrible pay, I hear, but the retirement plan is being remembered forever."

### Xanthe

They camped that night in a hollow below an abandoned shepherd's cot, and Xanthe walked the perimeter as the light died, trading news with the local wildlife.

The crows reported no soldiers on the southern roads. The foxes reported sheep, and opinions about sheep. A hedgehog reported nothing useful but demanded to be admired, and was. It should have been a peaceful accounting. It wasn't, because underneath the ordinary chatter of the fields ran a thread Xanthe had never heard before: questions.

*A Speaker,* said the old crow, hopping along the fence rail beside them. *A true Speaker, walking abroad again. Is it true, then? What the old ones say? That the silence will end?*

*I don't know,* Xanthe admitted.

*The wolves remember the covenant,* the crow said. *The wolves remember everything; it's why they're unbearable. They say when the Speaker comes south, the debts come due.* It fixed Xanthe with one bright eye. *Be careful what you promise on the road, Speaker. Beasts keep accounts longer than men do.*

Back at the fire, Rosalind sat wrapped in a blanket with the book open on her knees, though she wasn't reading it. She was watching the flames, and the firelight did something generous to her face, softening the fear into something more like resolve.

"The crow talked to you," she said. Not a question. She was getting good at telling when Xanthe had been conversing — a certain tilt of the head, a half-smile aimed at no one.

"She thinks I'm going to get myself into debt with the wolves."

"Will you?"

"Almost certainly. Everyone does, eventually. Wolves are the aristocracy of the beast world — terribly proud, wonderful singers, ruinous interest rates." Xanthe sat down beside her, close enough that their shoulders touched, and let the silence sit comfortably for a moment before saying what needed saying. "Rosalind. Tomorrow we cross into the open country, and after that there's no pretending. Every mile south, the danger gets sharper and my secret gets heavier. So I need to ask you something, and I need you to actually think about it, because you've only known me three days and you've seen exactly one trick."

"Talking to animals isn't a *trick*—"

"It's the least of it. Wait." Xanthe took a breath. "Ask me to stop. Now, tonight, while the wagons can still turn around. Marta would do it — she'd grumble, but she'd do it. You could go east, across the sea, change your name, burn the book. Live. I'd take you to the coast myself and I wouldn't blame you, not for one single day of my life. Because what's coming isn't a chase, Rosalind. It's a war, and prophecies are terrible maps for wars, and I—" The words snagged. "I would rather lose the covenant than lose you, and that's a selfish thing for the keystone to say, so I'm saying it now, in the open, where you can weigh it."

Rosalind was quiet for a long time. The fire popped. Somewhere in the dark, the hedgehog snuffled indignantly about something.

"You'd really let me go," she said finally. "Even now."

"I'd hate it. I'd write dreadful poetry about it. But yes."

"And that," Rosalind said, turning to face them fully, "is exactly why I'm staying." She held up a hand before Xanthe could speak. "Think about it the way you think about beasts. Everything wild trusts what it can read truly. You just showed me your whole hand — the fear, the selfishness, all of it — because you thought it might send me away. Anyone else would have hidden it. Ashfall hides everything; that's his entire kingdom, a fortress of hiding. And you're sitting in the mud offering me the door out because you'd rather be honest than safe." She shook her head slowly. "I spent my whole life around people who performed themselves. My grandmother was the only one who didn't, and I didn't understand her until this week. So no, Xanthe Thackeray. I'm not going to the coast. I'm going to Vellamare, to find out what my grandmother hid in my inheritance, and you're coming with me, because I've decided—" a breath, and her voice steadied— "that trusting you is the first decision I've ever made entirely on my own, and I intend to defend it."

The fire cracked. Xanthe found, to their considerable embarrassment, that their eyes had gone hot, and covered it by poking the coals with a stick until the stick caught fire, which solved nothing.

"That's the most romantic declaration anyone's ever made at me," they managed, "and it was mostly about honesty."

"Honesty *is* romantic. Ask any dog."

Xanthe laughed — really laughed, the first time since the night market, since the world had caught fire around them — and Rosalind leaned her head against their shoulder, and above the little camp the stars came out in their cold multitudes, indifferent and beautiful.

From the darkness at the edge of the firelight, the old crow watched them with one gleaming eye, then lifted off silently into the night — southward, toward the hills where the wolves were already stirring, already remembering, already keeping count of what the Speaker would owe.
