## 6. The City That Remembers

Vellamare smelled of salt and old paper, which Rosalind decided within an hour of arriving was the finest smell a city could have. Greyhollow had smelled of tallow and fear. This place smelled like her grandmother's study — if her grandmother's study had been built on stilts over a turquoise harbor and staffed by archivists who argued in three languages about punctuation.

"Stop smiling," Xanthe muttered beside her, hood drawn up against the morning sun. "You look like a tourist."

"We *are* tourists."

"We are fugitives with excellent posture."

Rosalind laughed despite herself, which was becoming a habit she neither trusted nor wanted to break. Ten days out of Greyhollow, ten days of smugglers' holds and roadside ditches and one memorable night spent sharing a hayloft with an indignant goat who, according to Xanthe, had called them both "soft-footed freeloaders" and meant it as a compliment. Somewhere along that road, terror had worn smooth edges into something almost bearable.

The Archives of Vellamare occupied a whole quarter of the city: white stone terraces stacked up the hillside like books shelved by a drunkard, connected by bridges and stairways and at least two water slides that the archivists insisted were "hydraulic delivery systems." Every free city kept its records here, they'd been told, going back past the Usurpation, past the Edicts, past the burning years. If anything remained of the covenant between human and beast realms — if anything explained why a century-old prophecy book had inked Rosalind's own name into its pages before she was born — it would be here.

And it would be under lock, behind three doors, guarded by a man named Prelate Ondine who had already refused them twice.

"He said come back Thursday," Rosalind reported, returning from her third attempt at the reading-room door. "He said it very politely."

"He's said 'come back Thursday' for six days. There is no Thursday in Vellamare; I asked a gull."

"You asked a—"

"There were gulls," Xanthe said defensively. "They gossip worse than rats. Anyway. We're doing this my way tonight."

* * *

His way, it turned out, involved the rats of the archive cellars, who were, in Xanthe's words, "underpaid, unappreciated, and furious about the new cat policy." By moonrise they stood inside the restricted stacks, lantern hooded, surrounded by shelves that rose into darkness like cliff faces. The rats had left a trail of gnawed candle-wax arrows on the floor, which Rosalind found both charming and mildly horrifying.

"This is theft," she whispered. "This is burglary. In a library."

"It's borrowing with ambition." Xanthe crouched, listening to something Rosalind couldn't hear — or could she? Sometimes, lately, when she watched Xanthe speak to the small voices of the world, she caught an echo of it herself, like a song heard through a wall. She didn't know what to do with that yet. She put it in the same mental drawer as *my name is written in a book of prophecies* and *I am apparently the Sigil-Bearer*, a drawer growing heavy enough to bend its hinges.

Xanthe straightened. "Second terrace. Covenant histories. The rat elder says the archivists sealed a section after the Edicts — everything about the old order went into a locked gallery and stayed there for a hundred years. Nobody's been in but dust and moths."

"And us."

"And us."

The sealed gallery door yielded to a key the rat elder produced from — Rosalind chose not to ask where — and swung open with a groan of disuse. Air rolled out, dry and papery and faintly sweet, like a room holding its breath for a century.

Inside, the lantern light found shelves of ledgers, scroll-cases, framed maps, and — Rosalind's heart stuttered — portraits. Dozens of them, stacked face-to-wall by someone in a hurry. She lifted the nearest frame and turned it around and forgot how to breathe.

A woman looked back at her. Grey-haired, sharp-eyed, with laugh lines carved deep around a mouth that knew exactly how much trouble it could get away with. She wore a pendant at her throat — a silver setting cradling a dark stone that seemed to hold the lamplight rather than reflect it.

"Grandmother," Rosalind said. Her voice came out strange. "Xanthe. This is my grandmother."

"That's not possible. Your grandmother lived in Greyhollow, you said. A seamstress. You told me she taught you embroidery and cheated outrageously at cards."

"She did cheat at cards. Outrageously. She also—" Rosalind turned the frame over. An archival label, yellowed and precise: **Maren Vane, Keeper of the Covenant, Vellamare Chapter. Year of the Breaking.** Below it, smaller: *Confiscated materials. Do not display.*

"Keeper of the Covenant," Rosalind read aloud, because saying it made it no more real but at least made it hers.

Xanthe took the frame gently from her hands. "Rosalind. Look at the rest of them."

She did. Portrait after portrait, stacked and labeled and hidden: men and women of every free city, some in scholar's robes, some in fishermen's oilskins, one in full armor with a wolf sitting at attention beside her like a fellow officer. Every label bore the same title. *Keeper of the Covenant.* Every label bore the same final stamp: *Confiscated.*

"They weren't erased," Rosalind whispered. "They were *filed*."

"Archivists," said a voice from the doorway, dry as autumn leaves. "We don't destroy. We shelve."

Prelate Ondine stood in the entrance, a small round man in grey robes, holding his own lantern and looking entirely unsurprised. Behind him, Rosalind noticed with a sinking feeling, sat an enormous orange cat, washing one paw with an air of professional betrayal.

"I wondered how long it would take the rats to earn their cheese," Ondine said. He stepped inside, closed the door softly, and looked at Maren Vane's portrait for a long moment. "You have her chin. And her habit of breaking into places."

"You knew her?" Rosalind's voice cracked down the middle.

"I was her apprentice. I was nineteen when Ashfall's witch-finders came for the chapter. She hid the registry, scattered the relics, and told me to forget her name until the world was ready to remember it." He set his lantern down. "I have been waiting forty years, child. Sit down. Both of you. It is a long story and I refuse to tell it standing."

* * *

They sat among the confiscated Keepers while Ondine poured tea from a flask he'd clearly brought expecting exactly this, and the story unfolded the way old stories do — not all at once, but in pieces that clicked together like a lock turning.

There had been a covenant, older than any kingdom now standing. Human and beast realms, bound by oath and by three relics held in trust across the free cities: a sigil-stone, a quill of truth, and the original tablet itself, on which the terms were graven. The covenant made magic lawful — not wild magic, but the *spoken* kind, the speech between realms, the gift Rosalind now watched flicker in Xanthe's every gesture. For centuries, Speakers had walked openly. Beasts had sat in councils. The world had been larger than it was now.

Then Corvus Ashfall had broken it. Not with armies at first — with paperwork. Forged edicts. A manufactured atrocity. A campaign of whispers until Speakers became witches and witches became fuel. The tablet vanished into the usurper's vaults, the relics scattered, the Keepers hunted, and a hundred years of silence settled over the world like ash.

"Maren saw it coming before anyone," Ondide said. "She was the one who insisted the first relic be hidden somewhere Ashfall would never think to look. Somewhere he'd already searched and found nothing." His eyes, bright and terribly kind, moved from the portrait to Rosalind's face, then lower — to her throat.

Rosalind's hand rose without her permission.

She had worn the pendant since she was seven years old. Grandmother Maren had fastened it on herself at the funeral-that-wasn't-a-funeral — "she'd have hated the word funeral," Rosalind remembered thinking even through her grief — pressing it into her palm and closing her fingers over it. *From me to you,* she'd said. *It's heavier than it looks. So am I.* Everyone had assumed it was sentiment. Rosalind had assumed it was sentiment. A silver locket, tarnished, too heavy for its size, that had never once opened no matter how she pried at it.

"It never opened," she said aloud. "I tried when I was nine. I broke a fingernail. I thought it was just... a keepsake."

"The best hiding places are things in plain sight that people stop seeing," Ondine said. "May I?"

With hands that trembled only a little, Rosalind unclasped the chain and laid the pendant in the prelate's palm. It looked so small there. So ordinary. A hundred years of hiding, wearing the face of a trinket.

Ondine turned it over. On the back, nearly worn smooth, was a mark Rosalind had always taken for a maker's stamp — a small circle crossed by three lines. Now, in the lantern light, she saw it for what it was. The same sigil that glowed faintly along the spine of the prophecy book. The same sigil that had appeared, unbidden, on the backs of Xanthe's hands since the night they fled Greyhollow.

"Sigil-stone," Ondine murmured. "Heart of the covenant. The seal by which oaths between realms were spoken into law. Ashfall burned half of Vellamare looking for this. Maren let him burn it. She stood in the square and watched, and she told me afterward that grief is just love with nowhere to go yet." He held it out to Rosalind. "She gave it somewhere to go. She gave it to you. She must have known — the prophecy names the Bearer's line. She simply refused to let the knowing be a burden. She let it be a gift instead. Wrapped in a birthday ribbon, as it were."

Rosalind took the pendant back and it *knew* her.

That was the only word for it. Warmth bloomed under her fingers, and the tarnish slid away like fog at sunrise, and the silver brightened until the sigil-mark on the back stood out crisp as the day it was cut. And then — gently, the way a hand opens — the pendant opened. The front split along a seam she'd hammered at uselessly at age nine, and inside, nested in velvet gone soft with age, lay a stone the color of a starless sky, drinking the lantern light and giving back something else. Not light. *Recognition.* The sense of being seen by something vast and patient that had waited a very long time.

"Oh," Rosalind said, very quietly. "Hello."

Across the gallery, Xanthe made a sound like someone had stepped on their heart. When Rosalind looked up, they were staring at the stone with their hands pressed flat against their own chest, and the sigil-marks on the backs of their hands were glowing through their gloves.

"It's warm," Xanthe whispered. "Rosalind, I can feel it. Like hearing a voice after years of shouting into wind. It's — it's *listening*—"

And Rosalind understood, in one clean stroke, why her grandmother had chosen her. Not the prophecy. Not fate. Maren Vane, who cheated at cards and hated funerals and carried a relic through a purge sewn into her bodice, had looked at her granddaughter — stubborn, curious, prone to breaking into places — and seen a keeper. Had decided that the safest vault in the world was not a cellar or a mountain but a person who would wear it every day without ever knowing, because knowing would have painted a target on a child's back.

Grief, which had lived in Rosalind's chest for fifteen years like a stone of its own, shifted. Loosened. Turned over and showed her its other face, and the other face was this: her grandmother had loved her with the patience of a hundred-year plan. Every card game cheated, every embroidery lesson endured, every ordinary Sunday dinner — all of it had been a wall around a secret, built brick by brick out of ordinary love.

"You're crying," Xanthe said helplessly. "Should I — is this a good crying? I can't tell. Rats are easier."

"It's good crying." Rosalind laughed wetly. "It's the worst good crying of my life."

Ondine tactfully examined his tea.

When she could speak again, Rosalind closed the pendant — it sealed itself at her touch, obedient now, home — and clasped the chain back around her neck. It settled against her collarbone, warm as a sleeping bird. "Two relics left," she said. "The quill. The tablet. And the tablet's in Ashfall's citadel, isn't it. Of course it is. He'd keep the one thing that proves him a liar under his own roof."

"You've been reading ahead," Ondine said approvingly.

"I've been having nightmares ahead. Same thing."

Xanthe moved closer, close enough that their shoulders touched, and looked at the stone through the gap in the pendant's seam. Up close, Rosalind could see the glow reflected in their eyes, and the wonder there, and under the wonder something rawer — hope, maybe, of the dangerous kind. "My whole life I hid what I am," they said softly. "And you — your whole life you carried the proof and never knew. We're quite a pair of locked boxes, aren't we."

"Between us we make one open book," Rosalind said, and then went red, because it sounded far more tender than she'd intended, and Xanthe smiled the smile that had gotten them chased out of three respectable establishments, and Ondine loudly announced that the tea was getting cold.

But the moment broke on something else. Because the cat — the enormous orange betrayer in the doorway — sat up abruptly, ears swiveling toward the window, and hissed at nothing at all. And outside, across the harbor, every gull on every rooftop lifted off at once, a white clatter of wings against the stars.

Xanthe went still in that particular way Rosalind had learned to dread. Head tilted. Listening.

"What is it?" Rosalind asked. "What do they say?"

Xanthe's face, when they turned back, had gone pale beneath the tan. "Ships in the night road. Black sails, no lights, riding quiet." They swallowed. "Witch-finders, Rosalind. Serah Dune's ships. They've found Vellamare."

In her palm, warm as a heartbeat, the sigil-stone pulsed once — twice — like something waking up. Or like something warning.

Ondine set down his cup with a click. "Well," he said. "I suppose Thursday has arrived early."
