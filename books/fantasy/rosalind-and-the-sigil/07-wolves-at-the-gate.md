## 7. Wolves at the Gate

The trouble with Vellamare, Xanthe decided on their third morning there, was that it was beautiful. Beautiful cities made people lazy. Nobody in Greyhollow would ever have hung laundry across a canal at dusk and then simply *gone to bed*, trusting the world to leave it be. In Greyhollow, the laundry would have been stolen by midnight, along with the line, the poles, and quite possibly the canal.

But Vellamare glittered. Its canals caught the morning light and threw it back in pieces of gold and rose, and its people sang from their balconies, and its gulls were the most insufferable gossips Xanthe had ever had the misfortune to listen to.

"—and the tall one sleeps with a knife under her pillow," a gull was saying from the roof opposite, "but the small one talks in her sleep, oh, you should hear it, *sigil this* and *covenant that*, and last night she said—"

"That's enough out of you." Xanne leaned out the window of the safehouse attic and flicked a crust of bread at the bird, who caught it midair with insulting grace.

"You never let me finish stories," the gull complained. "That's why I like the fishwives better."

"Fishwives don't have witch-finders hunting them."

"Neither do I, and yet here we are, both hiding in an attic." The gull ruffled its feathers, deeply pleased with itself.

Xanthe couldn't argue with that. They'd been in Vellamare four days since fleeing Greyhollow with smugglers' help — four days of lying low in the attic above a chandler's shop while Rosalind pored over her grandmother's papers downstairs and Xanthe tried very hard not to think about the fact that they were in love with a person whose name was written in a century-old book of doom.

They were mostly failing at the not-thinking part.

Below, through the floorboards, came the scratch of Rosalind's pen and her low voice reading aloud to herself, working through the archives' copies of covenant records. She'd been at it since dawn. She was always at it. Xanthe found it unbearable and wonderful in equal measure: the way she bent over a page like it might tell her secrets if she loved it hard enough.

A rat emerged from a knothole near the window — one of the harbor rats who'd adopted Xanthe within hours of arrival, because word traveled fast among the small voices when a Speaker came to town. This one was called Brindle, and she had the manner of a customs official.

"Movement at the east gate," Brindle said without preamble. "Grey cloaks. Silver chains. Dogs with them — poor devils, muzzled and miserable. Six wagons. They're asking after strangers."

Xanthe went cold all over. "How many?"

"More than six wagons' worth. They're spilling into the streets like spilled ink." Brindle's whiskers trembled. "And little speaker? They're not asking politely."

Downstairs, the scratching stopped.

Xanthe took the ladder two rungs at a time. Rosalind stood in the chandler's back room with a letter in her hand, and her face had gone the color of the morning fog off the harbor.

"The archivist sent warning," she said. "Serah Dune. She's here. In Vellamare. With full authority to search house by house." Her thumb pressed white against the paper. "Xanthe, she has a description of us. Both of us."

"Then we're leaving."

"We just got here. The sigil-stone—" Rosalind touched the heirloom at her throat, the stone her grandmother had hidden inside a locket for fifty years, the first relic of three. "We finally know what it is. We finally know what we're doing. And now—"

"And now we do it somewhere else. That's how staying alive works. It's a traveling sort of life." Xanthe tried for lightness and heard it come out thin as spun sugar. "Pack the book. Pack the papers. Leave the rest."

Rosalind looked around the little room — at the teacups they'd bought at market, the second blanket she'd insisted on because Xanthe ran cold, the whole small architecture of a life they'd started building in four days without either of them admitting it. Something in her face cracked and reassembled itself.

"All right," she said. "All right. The canals?"

"The canals are watched; the gulls say so. The land gates are watched. Which leaves—" Xanthe closed their eyes and reached outward, the way they'd learned to do since the sewers under Greyhollow, since the night Rosalind first saw them speak to rats and hadn't flinched. The city answered. Pigeons on the archive roof. Cats on the walls, dozens of them, Vellamare being a port city and therefore rich in cats. And beyond the walls, in the pine hills north of the city—

Wolves.

Vellamare's wolves were famous. Not hunted, not feared — *kept*, in the strange old way of free cities, the way things had been kept before the covenant broke. Shepherds left them the sick lambs and the wolves left the flocks alone, and once a year there was a festival where children left honey cakes on the boundary stones. Xanthe had thought it charming. Xanthe had not thought it might be useful.

Now they thought about it very hard indeed.

"There are wolves in the northern hills," they said. "Old packs. If anyone knows paths out of this city that don't go through gates..."

Rosalind stared at them. Then, slowly, she smiled — that smile that always made Xanthe feel like a struck bell. "You're going to ask the wolves for directions."

"I'm going to ask the wolves for an escort, actually. Directions are free. Escorts cost."

"What do escorts cost?"

"With wolves?" Xanthe pulled on their coat. "Usually more than you want to pay."

* * *

The boundary stones stood in a ring of old pines where the city's last orchards gave up and the wild began. Someone had left honey cakes that morning — two of them, wrapped in cloth, already raided by ants — and the smell of pine resin and old ritual hung in the air.

Xanthe sat down on the largest stone, which was either very brave or very foolish, and waited.

It didn't take long. Wolves, unlike cats, did not believe in making you wait; cats made you wait on principle, but wolves considered punctuality a form of respect. They came between the trees the way weather comes — gradually, then all at once — until the shadows at the edge of the clearing had eyes, and then shapes, and then there were nine of them standing in the green half-light, gray and silver and one enormous black male with frost on his muzzle.

The black one came forward alone. Up close he was bigger than any dog Xanthe had ever seen, with scars mapping his shoulders like an old sea chart, and his eyes were amber and unhurried and terribly intelligent.

"So," he said. His voice was like gravel turning over in a riverbed. "The rumors are true. A Speaker, in the city, after all these years."

"My name is Xanthe Thackeray."

"I know what your name is. The sparrows have been insufferable about it." He sat, folding his paws with terrible dignity. "I am Vael, and I lead the Northhollow pack, which is to say I am responsible for thirty-one mouths and one very stupid yearling who ate a bee yesterday and has been dramatic about it ever since. You want something, Speaker. Small voices don't climb hills to sit on cold stones for their health."

"Witch-finders have entered the city. By tomorrow they'll have searched every street. My companion and I need to leave north, quietly, tonight, by paths no human watches."

"And you want our paths."

"I want your paths, and if you'll give them, a guide."

Vael was quiet for a long moment. Around the clearing, the other wolves watched with that particular wolfish stillness that made Xanthe's skin prickle. Somewhere behind them, the yearling — a gangly young female with one ear that refused to stand up straight — whispered loudly, "*Is that really a Speaker, is that really—*" and was shushed by three different wolves at once.

"You know what we are, don't you," Vael said at last. "Not dogs gone wild. Not beasts in the shape of wolves. You know what we were, before."

Xanthe's mouth went dry. "Before the covenant broke."

"Before the covenant was *broken*," Vael corrected, and there was something old and bitter under the gravel of his voice. "There is a difference, little Speaker, and the difference is that breaking implies accident. What was done to us was done on purpose, with signatures." He rose and paced, and the pack parted for him. "My grandmother's grandmother remembered the old terms. The beasts kept the wild roads open. The humans kept the law kind. Speakers walked between, and neither realm forgot the other existed. Then the tablet vanished, the edicts came down, and suddenly we were vermin in the fields and the Speakers were kindling. For a hundred years we have remembered what your kind chose to forget."

"Not my kind," Xanthe said quietly. "I'm a criminal in every city in the free coast. My gift is worth my neck."

"Yes." Vael turned, and his amber eyes caught the light. "So it is. And yet here you sit, asking favors of beasts, and offering nothing."

The clearing went very still.

"I can offer gratitude," Xanthe said. "I can offer — when the covenant is reforged, when the edicts fall, whatever part my voice plays in that—"

"Gratitude." Vael tasted the word and spat it out. "Humans offer gratitude the way rivers offer water — endlessly, and only downhill. No, Speaker. If the Northhollow pack opens its roads for you, if we put our bodies between you and men with silver chains, then you will owe the beasts a debt. A real one. The kind that binds."

"A favor," Xanthe said slowly. "Unnamed."

"Unnamed and uncapped. To be called when we choose, for what we choose, and honored without bargaining." Vael's ears flattened slightly. "You should know what you're agreeing to. The last Speaker who owed my line a debt was asked to carry a message into a war camp. She did it. It nearly killed her. She never regretted it, but she never pretended it was free, either. Gifts that look free are the most expensive things in the world, little Speaker. Ask any wolf. We've watched humans for ten thousand years."

Behind him, Xanthe heard footsteps — soft boots on pine needles, and then Rosalind was there at the edge of the clearing, breathless from climbing, the locket glinting at her throat. She must have followed. Of course she'd followed. She looked at nine wolves in the shadowed pines and instead of running, she said, "Oh. *Oh*, they're real. The festival — the honey cakes — I thought it was just tradition—"

"It is tradition," Vael said, regarding her with interest. "Traditions are contracts nobody remembers signing. Yours is the Bearer, then."

"You know that too?" Rosalind's hand went to the sigil-stone.

"The sparrows," said three wolves at once, apologetically.

Rosalind laughed — a startled, bright sound that seemed to surprise even her — and Xanthe felt something in their chest twist. Here was the thing they could not put down: the world was burning toward them, witch-finders were crawling through the city below like ink through water, and Rosalind was laughing with wolves in a pine grove, and Xanthe would burn a great deal to keep that laugh alive.

Which was, they realized with a sinking feeling, exactly the lever Vael was pulling.

"You love her," Vael observed. It wasn't a question, and he said it in front of everyone, because wolves had no concept of tact and considered this a virtue.

"Vael—"

"It is relevant to the price. You will pay anything to keep her breathing. I am not wrong." He let the silence confirm it, merciless. "Then understand me, Speaker. I do not ask this debt because I am cruel. I ask it because the day is coming — soon, I think, the birds all say so — when the beasts will need a voice that humans will hear. And when that day comes, I need to know that the Speaker who walks between us does so bound by more than sentiment. Sentiment runs out. Debts hold."

Xanthe looked at Rosalind. Rosalind looked back, and shook her head slowly — *don't, don't do this for me* — but her hand had found the locket and her eyes were wet, and they both knew exactly what Xanthe was going to do, and had known since the moment the wolves stepped out of the trees.

"If I agree," Xanthe said, "it's my debt. Not hers. Whatever you call in, it comes to me."

"The debt is yours. It was always going to be yours." Vael stepped close, close enough that Xanthe could count the gray hairs frost-pale at his jaw, and pressed his broad forehead briefly, gravely, against theirs. The touch smelled of pine and rain and something older underneath, like the inside of caves. "Spoken and witnessed. The Speaker owes the beasts a favor unnamed. Rise owing, little Speaker."

"Rise owing," Xanthe repeated, and felt the words settle into their bones like a splinter — small, deep, and certain to ache someday.

Vael turned and loosed a sound that wasn't quite a bark and wasn't quite a howl, and the pack moved. Two wolves peeled away southward — decoys, Xanthe understood, big enough and bold enough to draw patrols. The rest flowed north between the trees, and the yearling fell in beside Rosalind and immediately began explaining, at length, about the bee.

"She's fine now," the yearling assured her. "Mostly. There was swelling. I was extremely brave. Are you really the one in the prophecy? Because Pip says the prophecy says you die, but Pip also says the moon is a bird's egg, so—"

"*Nessa*," Vael growled.

"—anyway," said Nessa, with the smoothness of the young changing subjects, "your left bootlace is untied."

* * *

They went north through the dark in single file, wolves ghosting ahead and behind, along paths that were barely paths — deer trails, drainage cuts, a stretch along an old aqueduct where the stones had been laid by hands a thousand years dead. Below and behind them, Vellamare lit up with torches, district by district, like a body lighting up with fever. Once, distantly, they heard a whistle-pattern Xanthe recognized from Greyhollow: the witch-finders' sweep-call, three rising notes and a fall.

"They're at the chandler's street," Brindle reported breathlessly, appearing on the aqueduct wall beside them. "Your room. They're in your room. They took the teacups."

"The teacups," Rosalind said faintly.

"For evidence. One of them drank from one first, which the cats found hilarious."

Xanthe should have felt fear. Some part of them registered that they ought to — witch-finders were in their room, their face was on a warrant, the machinery of Lord-Regent Ashfall's persecution was grinding through a beautiful city because of them. But mostly what they felt was Vael's words sitting in their chest like a stone swallowed whole. *Gifts that look free are the most expensive things in the world.*

They had given up their secret to save Rosalind in the sewers, and it had felt like freedom. This — this felt like a chain being forged, link by link, and the worst part was that they'd held the hammer themselves.

"Hey." Rosalind fell into step beside them, her shoulder warm against theirs. Nessa had trotted ahead to interrogate a hedgehog. "Whatever you promised them — whatever it costs later — thank you. I know you did it for me, and I know you think I don't know, and I do."

"I did it for the teacups, actually."

"Xanthe."

"They were good teacups."

Rosalind stopped walking. Ahead, the wolves paused too, patient, unbothered by human drama. She took Xanthe's face in both hands, there on the old aqueduct with the burning city glowing behind her, and said, very seriously: "When this is over — when the covenant is reforged and Ashfall is dust and nobody has to hide anything — I'm buying you a whole set of teacups. The good ones. With the painted flowers."

"That's the most romantic thing anyone's ever said to me."

"I mean it. Twelve cups. Fourteen."

"Fourteen," Xanthe agreed, and kissed her, brief and fierce, tasting salt and pine smoke, while the wolves politely studied the stars.

They walked on. The hills rose darker and kinder around them, and Vellamare's fever-glow sank behind the ridgeline, and somewhere in the middle of the night Xanthe realized the knot in their chest had loosened — not undone, never undone, the debt was in them now the way the gift was in them, permanent and price-tagged — but loosened enough to breathe around.

Because here was the arithmetic they kept arriving at, no matter how they ran the sums: everything that mattered had cost them. The sewers had cost them their secret. The wolves had cost them their future freedom of choice. Loving Rosalind would cost them, someday, something they couldn't yet name. And they would pay all of it again, twice, without hesitating.

That, Xanthe thought grimly, was probably what it meant to be the keystone of a covenant. Everything rested on you, and everything took its toll.

They crested a ridge at dawn and saw the road below — the north road, winding toward the next free city through country still green and unburned. And on the road, a quarter mile down, moving fast:

Horses. Grey cloaks. Silver chains catching the early light.

Not following them. Ahead of them.

Vael materialized out of the bracken at Xanthe's elbow, hackles lifted, his voice dropping to that riverbed rumble.

"Well, little Speaker," he said. "It seems someone told them where we'd be going. And I would very much like to know who."
