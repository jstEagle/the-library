## 8. Serah Dune's Doubt

The rain came down on Vellamare like a judgment, and Serah Dune stood in it because she did not trust the awnings.

That was the thing about her, the thing her witch-finders whispered about when they thought she couldn't hear — that Captain Dune trusted nothing, not the weather, not the witnesses, not even the ground under her boots. It wasn't quite true. She trusted arithmetic. Two fugitives had entered Vellamare through the Salt Gate three nights ago, disguised as pilgrims, according to a gate-guard who'd sold the information for less than it cost to buy his silence afterward. Two fugitives, one of them the Sigil-Bearer named in a book of prophecies that Serah had personally read twice and found, on both readings, to be badly punctuated.

Prophecies were like that. All thunder and no grammar.

"Captain." Her lieutenant, Brammel, hunched under an oilcloth he was holding wrong so that the water ran directly down his collar. "The harbor master says a boat went out two nights past. Small craft. No manifest."

"No manifest," Serah repeated. "In Vellamare. Where every fisherman's cousin is a smuggler and every smuggler keeps three ledgers."

"Yes, Captain."

"So we have no evidence at all, and you've brought me out into the rain to look at a dock."

Brammel had the grace to look sheepish, which was more than she could say for most of her people. She liked him for it, in the way she permitted herself to like things: quietly, provisionally, with an exit prepared.

"They went east," she said, before he could ask. "The wolves covered their trail going north, which means north is a lie. Wolves are honest creatures but terrible liars — they always overcorrect. Note it."

He noted it. He always noted it. Somewhere behind them, Vellamare's bells rang the third hour, and somewhere ahead of them, two fugitives were making for Highmoor, where the free cities would convene in nine days to decide whether to hand over a Speaker and a Sigil-Bearer or find their spines.

Serah intended to arrive first. Not to capture them — though she would, if the opportunity presented itself cleanly, because that was her commission and she honored her commissions the way other people honored gods, which is to say imperfectly but sincerely. She intended to arrive first because Lord-Regent Ashfall's orders had been specific in a way that made her teeth ache.

*Bring me the Bearer alive,* the edict read. *The Speaker may be taken however is convenient.*

*However is convenient.* She had burned people who were convenient. She had signed the warrants herself, in her own careful hand, because someone had to do the paperwork properly and it might as well be the person who least enjoyed it. That was her philosophy of service: if a thing must be done, let it be done by someone who will at least count the bodies accurately.

She counted everything accurately. It was why the discovery, when it came, could not be dismissed.

* * *

It came, as discoveries often do, in an archive, in the dark, while looking for something else entirely.

The Vellamare records-house was older than the city's walls, built by people who believed that paper outlasted stone and had been proven right so many times they'd grown insufferable about it. The archivist, a small round woman named Pell who smelled of lamp oil and dried figs, admitted Serah's warrant with the enthusiasm of someone greeting a tax collector.

"Witch-finder business," Pell said, leading her down stairs that spiraled below the waterline. "We don't get much of that. We got a man from Greyhollow once, years back, wanted everything from before the Edict destroyed. I told him destruction requires a court order and a fireproof room, and he didn't have either."

"What happened to him?"

"He drowned," said Pell cheerfully, "in a boating accident. Very sad. Anyway, here's the covenant section. Mind the damp."

Serah had come for shipping manifests. The fugitives had needed passage; passages left paper trails; paper trails were the only kind of magic Serah had ever fully believed in. But the manifests were filed adjacent to the historical records, and the historical records were organized by a librarian who had died forty years ago with a filing system known only to himself and possibly God, and so Serah Dune, waiting for Pell to fetch a lantern with a better flame, pulled a folder at random from a shelf marked *Edict Era — Correspondence.*

The folder should have contained routine administrative letters. The ratification of the Magic Proscription across the free cities. The standard exchange of seals.

Instead it contained drafts.

Three of them, in different hands, all corrections of the same document — the founding account of the Usurpation, the official history that every schoolchild in the nine cities learned: how the old King Aldric had gone mad with beast-magic, how the beasts had turned on their Speakers, how the covenant had collapsed into blood and fire until brave Lord Corvus Ashfall had outlawed magic entirely to save what remained of humanity.

Serah knew this history the way she knew her own name. She had recited it at her induction. She had taught it to recruits.

The drafts told a different story. Or rather — the same story, assembled differently, the way a confession can be assembled from innocent answers.

The earliest draft, in a clerk's careful script, described King Aldric as *a just ruler whose covenant with the beast realms held for two hundred years.* The second draft, annotated in a harder hand, struck *just* and inserted *weak.* Struck *held* and inserted *was exploited.* In the margin, in that same hard hand, a note: *The people must understand the covenant itself was the disease. Rewrite the collapse as inevitable.*

Serah knew that handwriting.

She had spent eleven years reading Lord-Regent Ashfall's correspondence. She had forged — no. She corrected that thought sharply, standing alone in a dripping archive. She had *authenticated* his seal a hundred times. She knew the way he crossed his t's like knife-cuts, the way his r's leaned forward as if hurrying toward violence.

The third draft was in that hand entirely. And at the bottom, where the finished version proclaimed that *no Speaker survived the Purge, praise the Vigilance,* the draft bore a line later struck out with such force the pen had torn the page:

*—though certain Speakers are recorded as having fled beyond the borders, and the covenant tablet itself remains unaccounted for—*

Unaccounted for. The tablet. The original covenant, the thing the whole history claimed had been destroyed in Aldric's madness, existed. Had existed, at the time of the writing. And Corvus Ashfall had cut the sentence out of history with a blade of ink.

"Captain?" Pell had returned with the lantern. "Found your manifests?"

Serah looked down at the folder in her hands. Rainwater dripped off her coat onto a floor that had held the truth for thirty years, patient as stone, waiting for someone to come looking for shipping manifests and leave with something heavier.

"Working on it," she said.

* * *

She did not sleep that night. She sat in the witch-finders' rented quarters above a chandlery, with the drafts spread on the table and a candle burning down between them, and she did the thing she did best, which was arithmetic.

Fact: the official history of the Usurpation existed in three versions, and the final version was authored by the man who benefited from it.

Fact: the final version declared the covenant tablet destroyed. A private draft, in Ashfall's own hand, acknowledged it survived — and then vanished from the record.

Fact: the Edict of Proscription, the law she had served for eleven years, rested entirely on that history. Remove the history and the Edict was not justice. It was theft. It was a man burning down a bridge and then charging tolls for the ferry.

There was a fourth fact, and it sat in her chest like swallowed ice: she had executed four people last year on testimony derived from that history. A charm-seller in Dunmark. An old woman whose chickens had spoken to her — or so a neighbor swore. Two children caught teaching star-language to crows. She had signed those warrants believing the law beneath her pen was load-bearing. That it held up something true.

Load-bearing. She almost laughed. It held up *him.*

Brammel knocked at some hour past midnight. "Captain? You've a light burning. Thought I saw — are you well?"

"Reading," she said, and swept the drafts into her satchel before he could see them, a motion so smooth and so fast that it frightened her more than the documents had. She was hiding evidence now. From her own lieutenant. From her own side.

"Reports from the road," Brammel offered. "Our scouts lost the fugitives' trail near the eastern marshes. Also — odd thing — a farmer swears his cattle all faced the same direction at dawn and lowed in unison. Toward Highmoor."

"Toward Highmoor," Serah repeated. The beasts were talking to each other. The beasts were organizing. Some part of her, the part that had always suspected the world kept better books than the Regency did, found this deeply satisfying.

"Shall I double the watch?"

"No. Get some sleep. We ride at first light."

When he'd gone, she sat back down and took out the drafts again, because she could not stop looking at them, the way you cannot stop touching a bruise.

Here was the trap she was in, laid out plain: she could burn these papers. She could report the archive visit as routine. She could ride to Highmoor, take the Bearer, deliver the Speaker's body as a convenience, and go on being Captain Dune, who trusted arithmetic and counted bodies accurately and never once asked whose ledger the counting served.

Or she could do what she was doing now, which was nothing, which was worse than treason because it was treason at the speed of a candle burning down.

Her father had been a farrier. He used to say a horse that throws its rider isn't wicked — it's telling you something about the saddle. Eleven years she'd worn the Regency's saddle, and tonight, alone in a chandlery's guest room with the rain coming down like a judgment, she finally admitted the obvious: the saddle was built for someone else. Built *by* someone else. Built by a man who had rewritten the world to fit it and called the rewriting salvation.

And yet.

And yet she did not burn the papers, and she did not send them anywhere either. Because doubt, properly handled, was like a wound probe — you didn't yank it out at the first touch. You followed it gently down to the bottom of the thing and learned the shape of what it had found. If Ashfall had forged the history, there would be more than drafts. There would be the tablet itself, hidden somewhere, the physical anchor of the lie. Documents could be disputed. A stolen covenant carved in stone could not.

Find the tablet, and the truth stopped being her private sickness and became a weapon.

She banked the candle. Outside, the rain eased, and in the wet dark of the chandlery yard, the horses shifted and stamped, and — she would have sworn to this later, though she told no one — a crow on the gutter ridge turned its head and regarded her for a long moment with an attention that felt less like a bird and more like a clerk checking a name against a list.

"You've got the wrong woman," she told it.

The crow blinked, unconvinced, and flew east toward Highmoor.

* * *

They rode at first light, and Serah rode at the rear, which was unusual, and said little, which was usual, and carried in her satchel against her spine three sheets of paper that weighed approximately nothing and approximately everything.

At the eastern crossroads, where the marsh road climbed toward the moor country, they found the sign she'd been half-expecting and wholly dreading: a waystone, old and moss-eaten, its carvings worn nearly smooth. Nearly. Brammel brushed away the moss with his sleeve and frowned.

"Beast-script," he said. "Old covenant markings. Harmless. Decorative, really."

"Read it anyway."

He squinted. "*Travelers under bond pass freely. The road remembers its friends.*" He laughed. "Superstition. The road doesn't remember anything."

"No," Serah agreed quietly, nudging her horse onward. "I expect it remembers everything. That's rather the problem."

Because she had finally understood, somewhere in the fourth hour of a sleepless night, what actually frightened her. Not the forgery — forgeries were common; power was mostly forgery with good tailoring. What frightened her was the pattern. The fugitives fled east. The wolves lied north. The cattle faced Highmoor. The crow watched her and flew toward the convening cities. Everything alive, everything small and furred and feathered, was moving in the same direction, like water finding a level, like a ledger balancing after thirty years of cooked accounts.

Something was coming to collect. And Serah Dune, who had spent her whole career on the collecting side of the ledger, understood with cold clarity that when the audit came, there would be no neutral column. You were either written in the debt or written against it.

Behind them, Vellamare's bells faded into the rain. Ahead, the moor country rose gray and enormous, and somewhere on it, in nine days, the free cities would vote, and somewhere nearer than that, two fugitives were walking toward a fate that a dead archivist's misfiled folder suggested had been scripted long ago by a man with knife-cut t's.

Serah touched her satchel strap once, the way a gambler touches a hidden card.

"Hold formation," she called to her riders. "We make Highmoor before the delegates do."

And privately, silently, in the place where her loyalty lived — that small, austere room she had kept swept and orderly for eleven years — she set the three drafts down on the table of her heart and locked the door behind them, and told herself she was keeping them safe.

Not hiding them. Keeping them safe.

The distinction mattered. She was almost sure it mattered.

Almost.
