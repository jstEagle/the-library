## 9. The Drowned Reliquary

### Xanthe

The temple had been drowning for three hundred years, and it showed no interest in company.

Xanthe stood at the edge of the flooded causeway with Rosalind beside them and the sea breathing against the broken arches below. The sunken reliquary of Saint Vess sat half-swallowed by the bay, its bell tower leaning like a drunk uncle at a wedding, its doors long since rotted to lace. Above the waterline, gulls argued over the roofline. Below it, Xanthe could feel something older than gulls.

"You're doing the face," Rosalind said.

"What face?"

"The one where you're listening to something I can't hear and deciding whether to tell me."

Xanthe glanced sideways at her. She was dressed for diving in borrowed fisher-leathers, her dark hair braided tight, the sigil-stone — her grandmother's heirloom, their first relic — hanging at her throat where it had begun, in recent weeks, to hum faintly whenever they drew near the other relics. Right now it hummed like a kettle deciding whether to commit.

"There are eels down there," Xanthe said. "Big ones. Old ones."

"Old eels. Terrifying."

"Rosalind." Xanthe kept their voice level, which took effort. "When I say old, I don't mean *elderly*. I mean these eels remember when this temple had a name. They remember when people still came here to pray. One of them claims to have personally witnessed a coronation."

"An eel witnessed a coronation."

"He's very proud of it. He's also very clear that we are not welcome, because the last humans who came here came with nets and hammers, and he has a long memory and a longer body."

Rosalind looked out at the water. The morning light lay on it like hammered tin. "How long a body?"

"Long enough that I'd rather have him as a friend."

She laughed — she'd been laughing more easily lately, and every time she did, Xanthe felt something in their chest unclench a little further, like a fist slowly remembering how to be a hand. That was the trouble with Rosalind Vane. She made hiding things feel less like survival and more like cowardice.

"So," she said. "Do we ask nicely, or do we sneak?"

"We ask nicely. Sneaking past a leviathan eel is how you become a story he tells other eels."

* * *

The water took them cold and green. Xanthe went first, the way they always did now, because the sea spoke a dialect of beast-speech all its own — slower, wetter, full of currents the way land-speech was full of wind — and someone had to translate.

Down and down. The temple's nave opened beneath them like the ribcage of something enormous and patient. Light fell through cracks in the vaulting in pale columns, and fish moved through those columns the way dust moves through sunbeams in an empty church. It would have been beautiful if Xanthe hadn't been counting the shadows along the walls, the ones too long and too smooth to be stone.

*They come,* said a voice, and the water itself seemed to say it, a pressure more than a sound. *The small warm ones. With the stone.*

*I come asking,* Xanthe answered, shaping the words carefully, the way you'd approach a strange dog with your palm open. *Not taking.*

*The last ones who came also said asking.* The great eel uncoiled from the shadow of the altar — and Xanthe had been right to warn her; he was longer than the nave itself, silver-grey and scarred, his eyes pale as drowned moons. *They took the bells. They took the gold. They left their hammers when the sea rose, and the sea keeps them still. Would you like to see the hammers?*

*No thank you,* Xanthe said, with complete sincerity. *I've seen enough hammers.*

Behind them, Rosalind's bubbles streamed upward in startled bursts. She couldn't hear the conversation — the deep speech didn't carry to human ears — but she could clearly see what was happening, which was that a serpent the size of a ship's mast had risen out of the dark to consider them, and Xanthe was floating there talking to empty water with the calm expression of someone discussing bread prices.

*You speak,* the eel said, circling them slowly. His body displaced the light. *Land-thing, and yet you speak. It has been long. Long enough that I had decided the Speakers were a story we told our young to make them behave.*

*I'm the only one I know of,* Xanthe admitted. *And until recently I mostly talked to rats.*

*Rats.* Something rippled through him that Xanthe chose to interpret as laughter. *Yes. You have the smell of rat-bargains on you. Very well, Speaker. What do you ask?*

*The quill,* Xanthe said. *The feathered quill of Saint Vess. It writes only truth. We need it — the covenant between your kind and mine was broken, and we mean to mend it. All of it. Every stolen voice.*

The eel went still. In the water, stillness from something that size was louder than movement.

*Every voice,* he repeated.

*Every voice.*

He turned then, unhurried, and swam toward the drowned altar, and Xanthe grabbed Rosalind's wrist and followed.

* * *

### Rosalind

Later — much later, wrapped in blankets on the smuggler's deck with salt drying white on her skin — Rosalind would try to explain to herself why she had done what she did next, and she would fail, because there was no explaining love's arithmetic. There was only the moment itself.

The altar chamber was a hollow of blue gloom beneath the temple's heart. And there, resting in a niche carved with worn wings, lay the quill: a feather long as her forearm, white shot through with veins of gold, untouched by rot or weed while everything around it wore a century of green.

The sigil-stone at her throat sang.

It wasn't a sound exactly. It was a resonance, a struck-bell feeling in her sternum, and it grew as she reached for the feather, and the great eel watched from the dark with his moon-pale eyes, and everything was fine, everything was going *fine*, until the temple shifted.

She felt it before she understood it — a shudder through the stone, a groan of old masonry settling the way old masonry does when something disturbs the balance it has held for centuries. A crack opened somewhere above. Dust bloomed in the water like smoke. And the column of the nave, the beautiful cracked-vaulted column of light they'd swum down through, began to come apart.

Stone fell. Water heaved. The current that rushed in to fill the collapsing space caught Rosalind like a hand catching a leaf, and suddenly she was not swimming but being *thrown*, tumbling end over end into the dark under-gallery where the light didn't reach, and the quill — she'd had the quill, she was almost sure she'd had the quill, her fingers had closed on it — was gone from her grip or maybe never in it, and the world was black water and roaring and the stone singing in her chest like a scream.

Her air ran out faster than panic could.

That was the thing nobody told you about drowning: it started quietly. One breath held, two, and then the body began making its arguments, and the arguments got louder, and the dark got softer, and some treacherous part of the mind suggested that resting might be nice, just for a moment, just until—

Something enormous touched her.

Not seized her. *Touched* her — a vast blunt snout nudging her side, gentle as a horse investigating an apple, and then the whole impossible length of the leviathan eel curved beneath her like a living raft, and the water began to move, and she was rising.

She was rising fast.

Light came back in increments — grey, then green-gold, then the shattered bright columns of the ruined nave, then the surface, exploding, and air, glorious filthy beautiful air, tearing into her lungs while she coughed up half the bay and clung to the slick silver flank of a creature out of legend, who held her at the surface with the patience of a dockhand holding a rope.

*Xanthe,* she wheezed, when she could. "*Where—*"

"Here." Hands — human hands, smaller, shaking — found her collar and hauled her onto the eel's back properly, and then Xanthe was there, treading water beside her, saying something rapid and liquid to the great beast, who rumbled a reply she felt in her ribs rather than heard.

"You *idiot*," Xanthe said, and their voice cracked clean down the middle. "You absolute — you saw the ceiling go and you went for the *quill*—"

"It was *right there*—"

"You nearly died for a *feather*!"

"It's a relic!" Rosalind's teeth were chattering; she couldn't seem to stop either the shaking or the shouting. "It's the second relic, it's the whole — we came all this way, and my grandmother hid it, and I thought—" Her voice failed her. Under all the fear, under the cold, there was something else, something that had surfaced with her, and it wanted out. "I thought if I lost it, after everything, after Vellamare, after the wolves, after you *taught me to talk to rats* — I thought I'd be letting everyone down. Everyone who kept it safe for a hundred years. Everyone who died keeping it hidden."

"And yourself?" Xanthe's hands were still gripping her arms, hard enough to leave marks, and their eyes were wet, though that meant nothing in the sea. "Were you on that list?"

Rosalind opened her mouth. Closed it.

"That's what I thought," Xanthe said softly.

The eel rumbled again, a long rolling note, and Xanthe flushed — actually flushed, in the middle of the bay, soaked to the bone.

"What did he say?" Rosalind asked.

"...He asked if all land-folk court each other by yelling accusations while sitting on his back, or if we're special."

"We're *sitting on his back?*"

"We're sitting on his back."

* * *

### Xanthe

Here is what Xanthe did not say, in the moment, and what the water said for them anyway.

Because that was the secret of the deep speech, the thing no one warned you about: underwater, words carried farther than you meant them to. Intent rode the syllables like stowaways. When you spoke to a leviathan eel in the open sea, you could not lie, and you could not hide, and everything you felt came through the current alongside everything you said.

So when Xanthe had shouted down into the dark — *find her, please, please find her, I will owe you anything, I cannot do any of this without her* — the eel had heard not just the request but the shape underneath it. The naked, terrified, unmistakable shape.

*You love this one,* the eel had observed, mid-rescue, with the tactless serenity of the very old.

*Now is really not the time—*

*You love her the way the tide loves the shore. Relentlessly. Against all sense.*

*She's dying, please—*

*Hold your breath, little Speaker. I am faster than your fear.*

And now they were on his back in the sunlight, and Rosalind was alive and furious and shivering, and the quill — the actual quill, retrieved by the eel himself from the rubble with a delicacy that would have astonished anyone who'd ever called him a monster — was tucked safely in Xanthe's sash, humming in harmony with the stone at Rosalind's throat.

*There,* the eel said. *Your truth-feather. Guard it better than the last fools did. And guard each other, since you are so obviously determined to die for one another anyway. It saves time if you succeed at living instead.*

"Thank you," Xanthe said aloud, because some things deserved air.

The eel lowered his head, and to Rosalind's visible astonishment, nudged her once more with that same apple-sniffing gentleness. Then he sank, and the sea closed over him like a book, and the two of them were alone in the swell, treading water, waiting for the smugglers' boat.

Rosalind spat out seawater and squinted at Xanthe. "What else did he say? Down there. You went red. Eels don't make people go red."

"Eels absolutely make people go red."

"*Xanthe.*"

"They may have commented," Xanthe said, studying the horizon with tremendous focus, "on certain observations available to anyone paying attention to the current."

"The current."

"Underwater words carry. Everything carries. Feelings especially. It's very rude, honestly, the ocean has no concept of privacy—"

"Xanthe." Rosalind had gone very still, treading water, and the sun caught the sigil-stone at her throat and threw a small trembling coin of light against Xanthe's cheek, as if the stone itself were pointing. "What did you say about me? Down there. In the dark. When you thought I was dying."

The boat was coming. Xanthe could see it, a smudge of sail beyond the breakwater, five minutes away, maybe less. Five minutes was nothing. Five minutes was a lifetime.

"I said," Xanthe said, and their voice came out steadier than they had any right to expect, "that I couldn't do any of this without you. Which was true. But it wasn't the whole truth, and the eel knew it, because the water doesn't let you keep the whole truth to yourself."

"What's the whole truth?"

"That I'm not afraid of witch-finders anymore," Xanthe said. "I'm afraid of a world where I knew you and then didn't. That's the whole truth. It carried whether I wanted it to or not."

The silence between them filled with the sound of the sea, which was not silence at all, but close enough.

Then Rosalind laughed — breathless, salt-lipped, half-drowned and entirely herself — and reached across the water and took Xanthe's hand, and neither of them said anything else, because the boat was arriving, and because some admissions, once spoken underwater where words carry, do not need saying twice.

* * *

They told the smugglers the temple had simply collapsed, which was even true. Old Tamsin, the captain, looked at the pair of them — wrung out, glowing, sitting shoulder to shoulder in the bow like a pair of cats sharing a sunbeam — and wisely asked no questions, which was her single greatest quality as a person.

It was evening when they finally examined the quill properly, spread on a chart table in the lamplit cabin, the sigil-stone and the feather side by side, humming together like two notes of the same chord.

"Try it," Rosalind said.

Xanthe dipped the golden tip in ink and wrote a test line on scrap paper: *The sky is green.*

The ink dried, faded, and rearranged itself into: *The sky is not green, and you know it.*

Rosalind snorted. "A pen that talks back."

"A pen that *can't lie*." Xanthe set it down carefully, and the humor drained out of the cabin as the implication settled over both of them. A quill that wrote only truth. A prophecy written — according to every archive in Vellamare — by covenant-keepers using just such a quill.

Which meant the prophecy could be trusted.

Which meant the final line, whatever it said, was true.

Rosalind was quiet for a long moment, her thumb moving over the sigil-stone in a slow, unconscious circle. "We should read it," she said at last. "The whole prophecy. End to end. No more skipping ahead one page at a time like frightened children checking the ending of a ghost story."

"Yes," Xanthe agreed.

Neither of them moved toward the satchel where the pages waited.

Outside, the night came down soft over the water, and somewhere far off, in the direction of the capital, a storm was gathering that had nothing to do with weather — though they wouldn't learn of it until the delegates convened at Highmoor, and by then Lord-Regent Ashfall's witch-finders would already be on the road, and Serah Dune would be reading reports that would change everything she believed.

But that was tomorrow's tide. Tonight there was a lamp, a deck, a quill that could not lie, and two people leaning together over a table, pretending — kindly, gently, for one more night — that they were ready to read the ending.
