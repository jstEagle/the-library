## 10. Prophecy's Fine Print

The inn at Saltmere Row had a name that changed depending on who you asked — the Gull's Rest to sailors, the Drowned Rat to smugglers, and "that place with the good stew" to Xanthe, who had eaten four bowls of it and was now eyeing a fifth with the unembarrassed appetite of someone who had recently nearly drowned.

"You're going to make yourself sick," Rosalind said.

"I was going to make myself *dead*," Xanthe said, tearing bread. "Stew is a step up. I intend to enjoy every step of the ladder."

Rosalind laughed, and the sound surprised her — easy, unguarded, as if her lungs had forgotten for a whole hour that they were fugitives. The fire in the hearth crackled. Rain worked at the windows. On the table between them lay the prophecy book, wrapped in oilcloth like something wounded, and beside it, on a square of velvet Xanthe had produced from somewhere, the second relic: the feathered quill that wrote only truth.

It didn't look like much. A quill never did. Its barbs were grey-white, shot through with threads of silver that caught the firelight and moved when you weren't looking directly at them, the way water moves in a glass you've set down. Rosalind kept catching herself watching it instead of Xanthe, which felt rude, but Xanthe was watching it too, so perhaps rudeness was mutual and therefore forgiven.

"It hasn't moved since we got here," Rosalind said.

"It's resting." Xanthe pointed with a spoon. "You would rest too if you'd spent three days underwater writing truth at eels."

"That was *you* writing truth at eels."

"I was writing truth at one eel, and she was very old and very chatty, and frankly I deserved a nap afterward more than the quill did." Xanthe's humor sat lightly on them as always, but Rosalind had learned — in sewers, on outlaw roads, in the terrible bright moment beneath the sunken temple when Xanthe's voice had pulled her back from the dark — to hear what lived underneath it. Underneath it now was the same thing that lived underneath her own silence: the memory of lips moving against her ear in the green water, words that carried because water carries everything, *I think I love you*, spoken where neither of them could pretend afterward that the other hadn't heard.

They had not spoken of it since. They had spoken of everything else — routes, rations, wolves, debts, the taste of leviathan-eel territory (briny, according to Xanthe, "like arguing with a very large grandmother") — everything except the sentence still hanging in the drowned dark behind Rosalind's eyes.

She reached for the book instead. Cowardice, dressed as scholarship. It fit her hands well enough.

"Shall we?" she said.

Xanthe set down the spoon. Whatever joke had been queued behind their teeth, they swallowed it. "Shall we," they agreed, and dragged their stool around the table until their shoulders touched hers, which was its own kind of answer, or its own kind of postponement. Rosalind couldn't tell the difference anymore, and wasn't sure she wanted to.

* * *

The book opened to the page it always opened to now, as if the spine had learned where they needed to be. Her grandmother's handwriting marched down the margins in violet ink — annotations, corrections, small furious arguments with the original scribe. *No.* *Wrong.* *He never understood the third clause.* Beside one line her grandmother had written simply, *Covetous man. Ask me about him someday,* and Rosalind's chest did the thing it always did, the folding-in, the grief that had become over these weeks less a wound than a inheritance: heavy, hers, load-bearing.

Rosalind dipped the quill in the little pot of ink Xanthe had bought from a peddler who swore it was squid-based and honest.

"Now?" Xanthe asked. "Tonight? We could sleep first. Sleep is also a step on the ladder."

"If we wait, I'll find reasons to wait forever." Rosalind smoothed the page flat. "The quill wrote truth at the bottom of the sea. It can manage a table."

She set the nib to the margin beneath the passage they'd read a dozen times — the broken covenant, the Sigil-Bearer, the Speaker, the relics, the road — and wrote, in her careful archivist's hand:

*What does the covenant require?*

For a long moment nothing happened. The fire popped. Somewhere downstairs, the innkeeper dropped something ceramic and swore about it with real artistry.

Then the quill shivered, like a bird shaking rain from its wings, and moved.

Not in her hand — she had learned that quickly; the quill tolerated being held the way a cat tolerates being held, technically permitting it while making clear whose idea nothing was. It walked itself across the margin in a hand that was not her grandmother's and not the original scribe's and not anyone's at all, an older hand, angular, patient, each letter placed like a stone in a wall:

*The covenant is sealed by the Bearer's blood.*

Rosalind read it. Read it again. The words were simple. That was the obscenity of them — how simple. Four words plus two. She had catalogued ten thousand lines of archival text in her life and knew all the ways language could hide meaning: cipher, allegory, dead idiom. This wasn't hiding anything. This was a door standing open on an empty room, and the room was cold.

"Sealed by blood," Xanthe said slowly. "Well. Blood-sealing is old magic. Every oath in the old law books has some blood in it. A prick of the finger, a drop on the—"

"*Bearer's* blood," Rosalind said. "Not *bearers'*."

The distinction hung there. Xanthe's spoon-hand had gone still.

The quill wasn't finished. It turned at the end of the line and came back, annotating its own annotation, the way her grandmother used to annotate her own letters, unable to let any statement stand without qualification:

*One blood suffices. One bearer gives. The other remains to hold the seal.*

Below that, smaller, almost apologetic, in the manner of legal texts everywhere:

*See final leaf.*

"There's no final leaf," Rosalind said. "I've been through this book since I was nine years old. There's no—" But even as she said it her fingers were moving, lifting the back board of the binding, pressing along the spine's hollow, because grandmothers who hid sigil-stones inside heirlooms hid other things in other places, and grief, she was learning, came with instructions if you knew how to read it.

The spine cracked softly. A leaf slid out — thinner than the others, vellum so fine it was nearly skin, covered edge to edge in her grandmother's violet hand.

Rosalind did not pick it up. She found she had to order her hands to do it, and they obeyed the way soldiers obey, stiffly, without enthusiasm.

* * *

Her grandmother's voice lived in the handwriting so completely that reading it was like hearing her again across the kitchen table, flour on her wrists, that particular gentleness she saved for hard truths.

*Rosalind, if you are reading this, then the quill has told you, or will. Forgive me for hiding it. I hid it the way I hid the stone — not from you, but for you, until you were strong enough to choose what to do with it. Here is what the covens agreed upon and what Corvus Ashfall knows and you must know too:*

*The covenant demands a death. Not cruelty — the old magic is not cruel, only literal. A bond that great must be paid for in what cannot be taken back, or it can be taken back too easily. One Bearer gives their blood willingly; the seal holds for a lifetime and beyond. This was always the shape of it. Every keeper before me knew. Every keeper before me hoped the prophecy would pass over their family like weather.*

*It did not pass over mine.*

*I have read the lines a hundred times and I cannot find a mercy in them. Perhaps there is none. Perhaps the mercy is elsewhere — in the choosing, in the willingness. Ashfall believes the prophecy can be broken by forcing the death on his terms, unwilling, defiled. He is wrong, but he is dangerous precisely because he is wrong in a way that kills.*

*If you love someone, my darling — and the book says you will, it has said so since before you were born, which I always thought was the book's most presumptuous habit — then you will understand why I am telling you this last:*

*Prophecies describe. They do not command. Remember that. Write it on your heart. What is described can be otherwise. My mother believed the words were walls. I believe they are weather. Weather changes. Walls do not.*

*Weather changes.*

The leaf ended there. Rosalind realized she had stopped breathing somewhere around *if you love someone*, and started again somewhere around *weather changes*, and that her eyes were wet, and that Xanthe was watching her with an expression she couldn't bear to look at directly, so she looked at the fire instead.

"Well," she said, and her voice came out steady, which felt like a betrayal of everything, and also like the only way through. "Fine print."

"Fine print," Xanthe echoed.

Neither of them said the next thing. Both of them were thinking it. Rosalind could feel it across the twelve inches of table between them like heat off a stove: *one of us*. The arithmetic was brutal in its simplicity. Two bearers, one blood. And underneath the arithmetic, quieter and worse, the question each of them was already asking alone: *which of us gets to be the one who stays?*

Because that was the shape of it, wasn't it. She had known Xanthe for a matter of weeks. She had known them for a lifetime, the way you know weather. And already — shamefully, wonderfully, terminally — she could not complete the equation with Xanthe's name in the subtracted column. The sum came out wrong. The sum came out as ash.

Which meant Xanthe was doing the same mathematics right now, across the table, with her name in the column, and arriving at the same impossible total.

"We should show this to the delegates at Highmoor," Rosalind said, because the alternative was saying anything true.

"The conclave." Xanthe nodded, grateful, grasping the rope she'd thrown. "Yes. If the free cities are going to decide whether to hand Speakers to Ashfall, they should know what he actually wants. He doesn't want to suppress the prophecy. He wants to *complete* it — his way. Unwilling blood. A murder dressed as destiny." Xanthe's jaw tightened. "That's not prevention. That's just killing her faster."

"Him," Rosalind said. "Or her. The book says Bearer. It doesn't say which."

"No," Xanthe said. "It doesn't say which."

And there it was again, the stove-heat, the shared unsaid thing, and Rosalind watched Xanthe fold the leaf — no, watched herself stop Xanthe's hand and take the leaf, because it was hers, it was her grandmother's, it was hers — and fold it into the book, into the hollow of the spine where it had waited twenty years for exactly this evening, and press the boards closed.

* * *

Later, when the fire had burned down to its red patience and the rain had settled into its overnight rhythm, Rosalind lay in the narrow bed above the common room and listened to Xanthe breathing on the pallet by the window. They had argued about the arrangement — Xanthe insisting she take the bed, Rosalind insisting they flip a coin, the coin landing on its edge twice in a row, which Xanthe declared "the rats' sense of humor" and refused to discuss further — and finally split the difference out of exhaustion.

She should sleep. Tomorrow they rode for Highmoor, two days' hard travel, into a room full of delegates deciding whether people like Xanthe were citizens or evidence. She should sleep.

Instead she lay in the dark and did the mathematics again, because she couldn't help it, because her grandmother had taught her that the worst texts deserve the closest reading.

*One bearer gives. The other remains to hold the seal.*

*Remains.* Such a gentle word for it. Remains. As if the one who stayed simply stayed, went on buying luck-charms and arguing with eels and eating fourth bowls of stew, carrying the seal under their ribs like a warm stone. As if remaining were a gift and not a sentence.

She thought about offering. She had already, in the privacy of the dark, composed the speech seventeen times. *It should be me. You're the keystone — the book says the Speaker is the lost piece, the covenant can't hold without your gift alive in the world. I'm just the flag. Flags are replaceable.* It was even logical. It was even probably true. She rehearsed it until the words wore smooth as river stones.

And then she imagined Xanthe's face hearing it, and the smooth stones cut her mouth.

Because here was the fine print under all the fine print, the clause her grandmother hadn't written but surely knew: you could not offer yourself to someone without asking them to live without you, and asking that was its own kind of taking. Love, it turned out, was not selfless. Love was two selfish hearts each trying to be the one who was spared the unbearable arithmetic, and calling the trying devotion.

Across the room, Xanthe stirred. "You're awake," they said. Not a question. Beast-speakers, she supposed, developed ears for breathing.

"A little."

"Me too." A pause. The floorboard by the window creaked as Xanthe shifted. "Rosalind?"

"Mm?"

"The quill. When it wrote the line. Did you notice—" Xanthe stopped. Started again. "The ink. It was red for half a second. Before it dried black. Did you see that?"

She had seen it. She had been hoping she'd imagined it.

"I saw it," she said.

"Right." Another creak. Silence stretched, taut as a mooring line. Rosalind stared at the ceiling and understood, with the awful clarity of the sleepless, that Xanthe was lying awake composing the same seventeen speeches she was, wearing the same smooth stones, and that if either of them spoke the offer aloud tonight, the other would spend the ride to Highmoor finding ways to make it unnecessary, and they would arrive at the conclave having quietly sabotaged each other out of love, which was the stupidest possible use of it.

"Goodnight, Xanthe," she said gently.

"Goodnight, Rosalind." A beat. "The stew really was good."

"It really was."

She listened until their breathing lengthened into sleep, and then she got up — quiet, quiet, the smuggler's walk Vellamare had taught her — and took the book to the window where the moon came through. She unwrapped the quill. It stirred against her fingers, drowsy, truthful.

*Describe the seal's terms precisely,* she wrote. *All exceptions. All alternatives. Leave nothing out.*

The quill considered. Then it began to move, and the first word it wrote was *None*, and Rosalind closed her eyes and made herself keep reading, letter by letter, because her grandmother had taught her that too: the worst texts deserve the closest reading, and the closest reading deserves a steady hand.

Downstairs, a horse arrived in the rain — hard-ridden, foam-flecked, carrying a rider with a sealed letter bearing the crest of Highmoor — and the innkeeper's boots thumped up the stairs toward their door, but Rosalind stood at the window with the quill scratching in the moonlight, reading the terms of her own ending, and did not turn around.
