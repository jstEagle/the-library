## 11. The Free Cities Convene

### I. Rosalind

Highmoor had been built by people who expected to be judged.

That was the first thing Rosalind understood about the free city perched on its granite shoulder above the cloud line. Every hall was tall and windowed, every chamber arranged so that any citizen could climb the public galleries and watch their delegates deliberate. The architects of Highmoor had believed in the healing power of being seen. It was a belief Ashfall's Greyhollow had never shared; Greyhollow built its cruelty in cellars.

Now the great Conclave Hall stood filled to its rafters with people who had come to decide whether Rosalind Vane was a person or a problem.

She stood at the edge of the delegates' floor with Xanthe beside her, wearing her grandmother's ring — the sigil-stone, though only three people in this room knew it — and watched the banners of nine free cities hang from the vaulted ceiling like patient birds. Vellamare's silver fish. Marrowdale's crossed hammers. Sableford's black sun. And at the far end, deliberately hung lowest, the grey banner of Greyhollow itself, which no delegate from that city would carry today because Greyhollow had no free delegates anymore. Only Ashfall's tax collectors and his fear.

"You're doing the thing," Xanthe murmured.

"What thing?"

"The thing where you stare at danger until it stares back." They tugged gently at her sleeve. "It works on cats. Delegates are harder."

"I'm not staring at danger. I'm staring at Delegate Ossen of Marrowdale, who has spent the last hour telling anyone who'll listen that surrendering Speakers is 'the prudent course.'"

"Ah. Yes. He has the face of a man who has never once been pricked by his own conscience. Like a well-fed turnip."

Rosalind laughed despite herself — a small, guilty sound that several nearby delegates turned to frown at. Let them frown. She had learned something crossing half the world with Xanthe Thackeray: laughter in dangerous rooms was not frivolity. It was a way of refusing to be made small.

The herald's staff struck marble three times. The Conclave was called to order.

### II. Xanthe

Xanthe had spent their whole life learning how to be invisible in crowded places. Shoulders angled down, eyes modest, voice pitched to the register of shop-talk and apology. Twenty-six years of practice, all of it useless now, because the Master of Rolls read out their name before the second bell had finished echoing.

"—and lastly, the matter of the fugitives: Rosalind Vane of Greyhollow, named in the Vane Prophecy as Sigil-Bearer, and one Xanthe Thackeray, identified by witch-finder report as a living Speaker of beasts."

The word *Speaker* went through the hall like a dropped glass. Not loud — halls like this didn't do loud — but everywhere at once, a thousand small intakes of breath, a thousand heads turning with the smooth synchronized horror of a flock deciding to flee.

Xanthe's hands wanted to hide inside their sleeves. Old habit. In the night market they'd spent years perfecting the art of looking like nothing: the charm-seller whose wares were only string and glass, whose conversations with alley cats were muttering, whose stillness around rats was merely a merchant's patience.

*They're going to look at you,* said a voice near the floor, and Xanthe glanced down to find a Highmoor house sparrow cocking its head from the base of the Master of Rolls' podium. The sparrows here were fat and entitled, as befitted a city where grain spilled freely. *Everyone is going to look at you. I would find somewhere else to be.*

"So would I," Xanthe breathed.

"Did you say something?" asked the Roll-Master's clerk, leaning down.

"No," said Xanthe, and then, because the moment had arrived whether they liked it or not, stepped forward into the open floor. "Yes. I did. I said — so would I."

Confusion rippled. Somewhere in the galleries a child giggled.

Delegate Ossen of Marrowdale rose, vast and upholstered, like a wardrobe given civic office. "Then let us dispense with ceremony," he boomed. "Lord-Regent Ashfall offers terms. Hand over the Speaker and the so-called Sigil-Bearer, disavow the prophecy, and Greyhollow's armies pass our borders without incident. Nine cities. One decision. I say we are merchants, not martyrs. I say we sell them the peace they're asking for."

"Peace," repeated a dry voice from the Vellamare delegation. Archivist Pellinor, ancient and sharp as a paper cut, got to her feet with the aid of two canes. "You use the word the way a butcher uses a curtain, Ossen. What Ashfall sells is not peace. It is an interval. Ask the beasts what happened the last time a strongman decided the covenant was inconvenient."

"There *is* no covenant," Ossen snapped. "There is a fairy story and a book of riddles, and now two fugitives asking nine cities to go to war over—"

"Over me," Xanthe said.

Their own voice surprised them. It came out steady. Underneath, their heart was performing something frantic and percussive, but the voice — the voice belonged to someone else entirely, someone who had apparently been waiting years for permission to speak.

Every eye in the hall found them. The sparrow on the podium gave an encouraging chirp that only Xanthe heard, and which translated roughly to *don't embarrass our species.*

"My name is Xanthe Thackeray," they said. "I was born in Greyhollow's Lantern Ward. My mother sold eels. When I was six years old, I told her one of her eels was sick, and she laughed, and then the eel died, and she stopped laughing, because I had known. I have known things animals were saying since before I knew words were for anything else." A breath. "I have spent my whole life hiding it. I sold charms that were only string and glass, and while I sold them, I bargained with rats for news and cats for warnings, and I told myself that keeping my head down was survival. That it was wisdom. That silence was safe."

They looked up — really looked, letting themselves be seen, which was the hardest thing they had ever done, harder than sewers or wolf-pacts or leviathan eels.

"It isn't safe. Silence is just a slower way of disappearing. Ashfall doesn't want you to hand me over because I'm dangerous. He wants you to hand me over because as long as one of us exists unhidden, everyone in this hall has to ask themselves what *they* might be. What their children might be. Fear is his whole government, and I am a walking audit of it."

Murmurs. Ossen's face had gone the color of boiled ham.

"The lady speaks of war," Xanthe went on, nodding toward Pellinor. "I don't want war. I've seen what witch-finders do to a market street; I fled through the drains beneath one. But I will tell you what the wolves of Vellamare told me, and what the crows tell me every morning, and what the rats of Greyhollow have known for a year: the beasts remember the covenant. They remember when the borders between your realm and theirs were law instead of luck. And they are done pretending otherwise. You may give me to Ashfall. He will burn me, and it will buy you a season. Then he will come for the next Speaker, and the next, and then for anyone who ever fed a stray cat too kindly. There is no version of this where you stay out of it. There is only the version where you choose which side of it to stand on."

Silence — but a different quality of silence than before. Not horror. Reckoning.

Rosalind's hand found theirs below the lip of the delegates' rail, and squeezed once.

*Well said,* said the sparrow. *Though you talked long enough that I ate three crumbs off the podium.*

### III. Serah Dune

Serah Dune stood in the public gallery of the Conclave Hall wearing a wool traveling cloak over her witch-finder's uniform, hood up, and reflected that she had committed at least four crimes since dawn and felt wonderful about none of them.

Not because of guilt. Guilt would have been simpler. No — it was the *uncertainty* that gnawed at her, the forged edicts folded against her ribs like a splinter working toward the heart. She had come to Highmoor on Ashfall's orders: observe the conclave, identify sympathizers, prepare the seizure lists. She had expected to find fanatics and fools.

Instead she had found a Speaker.

She watched the Thackeray person now — thin, sharp-faced, standing in the open floor with the particular rigid courage of someone terrified and proceeding anyway. Serah had burned Speakers. Eleven of them, over six years of service. She had told herself each time that the gift was corruption, that the creatures they spoke with were demons wearing fur, that the pyre was mercy of a kind.

But she had watched this one talk, and there had been no demon in it. There had been a frightened person describing a lifetime of flinching, and nine cities full of ordinary people listening, and something in Serah's chest had turned over like a body in sleep.

*The histories were forged,* she thought, for the hundredth time since Vellamare. *Ashfall wrote the crime himself.*

And yet here she stood, on his errand, taking notes.

Below, the vote was being called. Delegates rose city by city. Sableford: resist. Vellamare: resist, Pellinor's canes striking the floor like gavel blows. Two river towns: abstain, sweating. Marrowdale—

Ossen stood, opened his mouth, and delivered a speech about prudence that lasted four minutes and concluded, with visible reluctance, that Marrowdale would also vote to resist — but only, he emphasized, only if the Speaker could produce evidence of this covenant beyond a story and a stolen book.

Evidence. Serah almost smiled. She had evidence. It was folded against her ribs, and it convicted the man who'd sent her.

She slipped out of the gallery before the final tally, because a witch-finder captain lingering through a pro-magic vote was a detail she could not afford. In the corridor outside, she passed a delegate hurrying the other way — a small man in Marrowdale colors, moving fast, face slicked with sweat, a sealed letter clutched against his chest like a hot coal.

He didn't meet her eyes. People carrying letters to enemy camps never did.

Serah Dune walked on, and told herself she hadn't seen anything at all.

### IV. Rosalind

The vote carried. Seven cities to resist, two abstaining, none for surrender — Ossen's conversion bought, Rosalind suspected, less by Xanthe's speech than by the sight of Vellamare's trade fleet mobilizing in the harbor below, which had a way of clarifying a merchant's principles.

For one golden hour, it seemed possible. Delegates came to them in the antechamber — gruff, curious, some even kind. A Sableford smith pressed a knife into Xanthe's hand, good steel, "for whatever's coming." A young clerk from the river towns asked Xanthe, blushing, whether it was true you could apologize to a horse. ("You can," Xanthe said gravely. "Whether the horse accepts is another matter.")

Rosalind stood back and watched her love become, in real time, a public person, and felt pride and terror in equal measure, because she had read the prophecy's fine print. She knew what the quill had written. She knew the price inked at the bottom of the page, and she carried it alone, and she smiled anyway, because that was the deal she'd made with herself: whatever came, these hours would be real.

"We should move tonight," Xanthe murmured, finding her side during a lull. "Crowds make me itch. Also, there's a pigeon upstairs who says a man left the hall an hour ago riding hard south, and pigeons gossip worse than rats."

"A pigeon told you that?"

"Pigeons see everything from above. It's given them ideas."

Rosalind laughed — and then the doors of the antechamber banged open, and the laugh died in her throat.

Witch-finders. Six of them, in the heart of Highmoor, ash-grey cloaks and iron sigils, and behind them, pale and shaking, Delegate Ossen — pointing, his finger trembling, his face a portrait of a man who had sold something precious and knew it.

"There," Ossen said. "There. As agreed. Now Marrowdale's neutrality is assured — that was the arrangement—"

"*Arrangement?*" roared the Sableford smith, but the witch-finders were already moving, and the captain at their head — a woman with a scarred jaw and eyes like winter water — swept the room once, cataloguing exits, bodies, weights.

Her gaze snagged on Rosalind. Held. Something flickered there — something that was not triumph, something almost like hesitation — and then it was gone, buried under procedure.

"In the name of Lord-Regent Corvus Ashfall," the captain announced, "these persons are remanded for trial in Greyhollow. Resist, and this city joins the list of burned."

The antechamber erupted. Delegates shouting, guards drawing, the smith's new knife already bare in Xanthe's hand —

And through the high windows, from every rooftop and gutter and eave of Highmoor, ten thousand birds began to scream.
