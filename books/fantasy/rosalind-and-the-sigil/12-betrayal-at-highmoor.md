## 12. Betrayal at Highmoor

The conclave hall at Highmoor smelled of beeswax, old stone, and the particular nervousness of forty delegates who had all, at some point in the last hour, pretended not to look at me.

I'd been introduced — formally, by name, by Delegate Maren of Vellamare, who had a voice like a landslide and used it accordingly — as "the Speaker of Greyhollow." Not *a* speaker. *The* Speaker. As if there were a whole lineage of us and I was merely the current officeholder, like a mayor or a bell-ringer. I wanted to correct her. I was a charm-seller who talked to rats. My credentials were three hundred conversations with pigeons, most of which had been about bread.

But nobody laughed, so I kept my mouth shut and my hands folded on the table like a person who belonged there.

Rosalind sat beside me, close enough that our shoulders touched whenever either of us breathed too ambitiously. She wore her grandmother's heirloom at her throat — the sigil-stone, though only four people in this room knew that's what it was — and she had braided her hair back in the severe style she reserved for occasions when she intended to be taken seriously. She looked, frankly, magnificent. I kept almost saying so and kept almost getting us both killed for it.

"Delegate Thackeray," said the chairwoman, an ancient woman from Highmoor itself whose eyebrows had achieved a kind of independent authority, "you have the floor."

*Delegate.* They'd made me a delegate. Somewhere between Vellamare and here, without asking me, the free cities had decided I was a person of standing. It was like being promoted to admiral of a rowboat.

I stood. Forty faces turned toward me. Somewhere in the rafters, a crow I'd befriended on the road up — his name was Barnaby, he had opinions about cheese — shifted his weight, and I heard him mutter, *Don't slouch, don't slouch,* which was, I reflected, exactly what my mother would have said if my mother had been a bird.

"Delegates," I began, and my voice came out steadier than I deserved. "You know why we're here. Lord-Regent Ashfall has sealed the passes. His witch-finders are burning villages two days' ride from this hall. He has one demand: hand over the Speaker and the Sigil-Bearer, and the free cities will be left alone."

A murmur went around the room like wind through wheat.

"I am the Speaker," I said. "She is the Bearer. And I'm asking you not to do it."

"You ask a great deal," said a delegate from the eastern cities — Corvane, silver-haired, silk-voiced, with the well-fed calm of a man who had never once been hungry on purpose. "You ask forty cities to risk fire for two strangers."

"I ask you to risk fire for yourselves," I said. "Because that's what's actually on offer. Ashfall doesn't want two fugitives. He wants precedent. Give him one Speaker and every hidden gift in every city becomes a debt owed to him. Give him one Bearer and every prophecy becomes his property." I spread my hands. "I'm not the price. I'm the down payment."

That landed. I felt it land — the shift in the room, the way the murmuring changed pitch. Rosalind's knee pressed against mine under the table. *Good,* she mouthed.

And then Corvane smiled.

It was a small smile. A reasonable smile. The smile of a man about to say something sensible in a room full of frightened people, which is the most dangerous thing a man can smile.

"Passionate," he said. "And perhaps even sincere. But tell me, Delegate Thackeray — how do we know you are what you claim? Words are cheap. Even *beast* words." He let the word hang. "We have your testimony and a stolen book. Forgive me, but I'd sooner trust a merchant's ledger."

The chairwoman frowned. "Corvane, the archives of Vellamare—"

"Are archives," Corvane said smoothly. "Paper. What I propose is proof. Let the Speaker demonstrate the gift. Here. Now. Before this assembly."

The room went very quiet.

I understood the trap a half-second before I understood I was already in it. If I refused, I was a fraud, and fear would do the rest. If I complied, I performed magic before forty witnesses in a hall where half the delegations still had laws against it — laws they'd suspended to hear me, not to watch me use the thing they feared.

"That's not—" Rosalind started.

"It's a fair request," Corvane said mildly.

"It's theater," Rosalind said, rising, and I loved her so much in that moment that it actually interfered with my thinking. "You want a spectacle so you can call it sorcery the moment it suits you."

"And you want trust without evidence," Corvane replied. "In my experience, madam, those who refuse small proofs are usually concealing large ones."

Barnaby muttered something from the rafters that I was glad no one else could translate. It involved Corvane's ancestry and a dung beetle.

I raised a hand. "All right."

Rosalind turned to stare at me. "*Xanthe.*"

"There's a rat under the third bench on the east side," I said. "She's been listening to this entire circus. Her name is Pip, she has a torn ear, and she thinks the delegate from Saltmarsh smells like herring, which he does." I looked at Corvane. "Ask her anything. Ask her what color your boots are. You've been sitting; nobody sees your boots."

Corvane's eyes flicked down, involuntary, then back up. "This proves nothing. A confederate could—"

"A rat," I said, "who is currently complaining that the floor is cold and that someone dropped a candied plum under bench nine, which she is eating, and which she describes — her word, not mine — as *life-changing.*" I paused. "Also she says you checked your boots just now because you're wearing one black and one brown, and you hoped no one noticed."

Forty heads craned toward Corvane's feet. There was a beat of absolute silence, and then the entire eastern delegation leaned over to look, and Corvane's face did something complicated and expensive.

Laughter broke out — nervous, but real. And in that laughter, for one shining moment, I thought: *we've won them.*

That was the moment the doors blew open.

* * *

Later — much later, in the dark, going over it the way you pick at a wound — I would count the seconds. The doors had blown open maybe ten heartbeats after the laughter started. Witch-finders don't storm buildings on rumor. They storm buildings on certainty, and certainty takes time to arrange: positions, signals, men stationed in the yard since before dawn.

Which meant Corvane hadn't sold us out during the session.

He'd sold us out before it ever began.

They came through every door at once. I remember it in pieces, the way you remember any disaster: the grey cloaks, the iron sigils, Serah Dune's voice cutting flat and cold across the panic — *"The Speaker and the Bearer. No one else needs to die today."* The chairwoman shouting procedure at armed men as if procedure had ever stopped a drawn blade. A brazier going over, spilling coals across the flagstones. Delegates scattering like startled pigeons — and me, absurdly, thinking of Barnaby, who burst from the rafters in a clatter of wings screaming *OUT OUT OUT* into the ears of everyone present, which was the single most useful contribution any delegate made all day.

Rosalind grabbed my wrist. We went left, toward the servants' corridor behind the dais, because she'd walked the hall that morning while I'd been rehearsing speeches, because she always knew the exits, because she was the one who thought of everything.

"*There,*" she said, and shoved me through the doorway first.

That should have told me. She never went first. She went second, always, positioned to see the whole board. But my brain was full of smoke and shouting, and I went where she pushed me.

The corridor was narrow and lit by one guttering lamp. Behind us, the hall dissolved into chaos — I heard Dune's voice again, closer, and the crackle of the spreading coal-fire, and someone screaming a name. Ahead, a door, a yard, a wall, the pines, freedom. Twenty yards. Fifteen.

Then boots at the far end. Two witch-finders, coming up the corridor from the yard side. They'd covered the back exit. Of course they had. Dune didn't leave doors open; that was the whole horror of her, she read the same maps you did and got there first.

Rosalind's hand closed on the sigil-stone at her throat.

"No," I said. "No, no, *no* — Rosalind, whatever you're about to do—"

What she was about to do was step past me.

I saw it happen the way you see weather roll in. She planted herself in the middle of the corridor, lifted the stone, and light came off it — not the warm gold I'd seen in Vellamare, but a hard white flare, a struck match the size of a cathedral — and the two witch-finders reeled back, hands to their eyes, and the lamp blew out, and in the strobing afterglow she turned and looked at me.

Her face was calm. That was the worst part. She'd already done the arithmetic. I could see it finished behind her eyes.

"One of us runs fast," she said. "One of us runs *first.*"

"Don't," I said. It came out very small. "Rosalind, don't you dare—"

"The wolves owe you. The rats know these cellars better than their own mothers. You're the keystone, love — the prophecy doesn't work without you." She was backing away from me now, toward the flaring light and the blinded men, walking backward the way you do when you can't bear to turn around. "It works fine without me. It was always going to need me less than it needs you. I've known since the quill wrote it out."

"You *knew?*" The word tore out of me louder than I meant it. "You knew and you — we said — we agreed, no more arithmetic alone—"

"I lied," she said simply. "I'm good at it. You should be nicer to me about it later."

Boots behind us now too — Dune's people had found the corridor. Rosalind glanced back, judged distances with that terrible quick mind, and then she did the cruelest kindest thing anyone has ever done to me.

She kissed me. Fast, fierce, tasting of smoke, and then she put both hands on my chest and *shoved*, and the sigil-stone flared again, and the world went white, and I was falling through a door into the yard with her voice ringing in my skull like a struck bell:

"*Run, Xanthe.*"

* * *

I ran.

I want to be honest about that, because everything afterward depends on it. I ran. My body made the choice before my heart could file an objection, and I went over the yard wall with rats streaming ahead of me through a gap in the stones — *this way, this way, quick quick* — and down through the pine dark with torchlight blooming behind me like diseased flowers, and every stride I took was a vote cast in an election I hadn't consented to, the terrible arithmetic of survival doing its cold sums: *one captured or two, one broken or none, run run run.*

Pip rode in my hood the whole way, whispering directions. At some point I realized I was crying, and that Pip was patting my neck with one small paw, and that neither of those facts slowed me down.

"She planned it," Pip said. "From the moment the doors went. Maybe before. Rats see things, soft-foot. She looked at the corridor and then at you and her heartbeat went steady. Steady means decided."

"She *lied* to me."

"Yes," Pip said, with the gentle ruthlessness of her kind. "Lying is how small things survive big ones. You'd know."

I hated her a little. I loved her a little. Mostly I ran.

* * *

The smugglers' camp was three miles down-mountain, in a hollow where the pines grew thick enough to strangle the moonlight. Odile saw me come out of the treeline and read the whole story off my face in the time it took me to cross the clearing.

"No," she said.

"They have her."

"*No.*" She caught me as my legs quit — I hadn't known they'd quit until they did — and lowered me onto a crate with the brisk tenderness of a woman who had patched up worse than grief. Around the fire, the others went quiet. Tam stopped mid-joke. Little Fen, the boy who'd stowed away at Vellamare and whom we'd all pretended not to notice, looked at me with enormous eyes.

"We go back tonight," I said. My voice sounded like someone else's, scraped hollow. "Tonight, now, before they move her—"

"With what?" Odile crouched in front of me, gripping my shoulders, forcing my eyes to hers. "With six smugglers and a boy against Dune's whole company? Xanthe. *Look at me.* You walk back into Highmoor tonight, you die in the yard next to her, and then there's no one. No Speaker. No covenant. Nothing but Ashfall winning forever." She shook me once, gently. "Grief makes terrible generals."

"Then what do I *do?*"

And that was when the forest answered.

It started as a sound at the edge of hearing — a low, layered hum, dozens of throats, hundreds. Then the pines began to deliver them: wolves sliding out of the dark like grey thoughts, eyes catching the firelight. Crows dropping through the branches to line the limbs overhead, Barnaby among them, feathers roughed and furious. Foxes at the clearing's rim. And everywhere underfoot, pouring out of the leaf-litter in a moving carpet, rats — thousands of them, the mountain's whole quiet nation, come down to find me.

The wolf who reached the fire first was grey as winter and old as sin, and I knew her. Vaela. Queen of the Highmoor packs. She had given me the unnamed favor at Vellamare, and I had spent months hoping she'd forget it.

She sat down in front of me with the composure of a monarch attending a coronation, and she spoke so the whole camp could hear.

"The debt comes due, Speaker."

"Not tonight," I whispered. "Please. They took her *tonight.*"

"Which is why tonight," Vaela said. "You think we came down the mountain for the scenery?" Her lips peeled back from her teeth, and it wasn't quite a smile, but it was adjacent to one. "Ashfall has built his citadel on stolen ground and filled its cells with our silenced kin. The girl they took carries the stone that can break the silencing. And you—" she leaned close, breath hot, eyes ancient and absolutely certain, "—you are going to lead us through his gates and take it all back."

Behind her, the crows shifted on their branches. The rats went still, a thousand small bodies waiting. The whole dark forest held its breath around me, and I understood that the choice I'd been dreading since Vellamare had just been made for me — or rather, that it had never really been a choice at all, only a door I'd been pretending wasn't there.

Rosalind had shoved me through one door already tonight.

I stood up. My legs held. Somewhere above, Barnaby croaked, softly, *don't slouch.*

"How many wolves can you raise by tomorrow's dark?" I asked Vaela.

Her grin widened. "Speaker," she said, "how many *stars* can you count?"
