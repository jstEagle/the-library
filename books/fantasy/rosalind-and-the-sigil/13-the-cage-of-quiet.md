## 13. The Cage of Quiet

The cell had one window, and it was a lie.

It was painted on the wall — a clever trompe l'oeil of a moonlit sky, done in grays and a single optimistic blue — and Rosalind had spent her first hour in captivity staring at it, deciding that whoever had painted it deserved to be arrested for something. False hope, perhaps. Fraudulent astronomy.

"Three days," she said aloud, to no one. "I have been imprisoned for three days beneath a painted sky."

Her voice echoed off stone. The cell was small, dry, and cold in the particular way that meant the cold was intentional. A cot. A bucket behind a screen that offered privacy as a courtesy rather than a right. A candle stub that was replaced each morning by a guard who never met her eyes, which she had decided to interpret as professional embarrassment rather than cruelty.

They had taken everything else. Her boots — replaced with soft slippers, an insult she was still cataloguing. The heirloom at her throat, the sigil-stone she'd only just learned she'd been wearing all her life. Her hairpins, her belt, the little knife sewn into her sleeve that Xanthe had sworn she'd never notice and she had noticed immediately because Xanthe was a terrible smuggler of small knives.

And the book. They had taken the prophecy book, and that was the wound that wouldn't close. Her grandmother's handwriting. A hundred years of careful, secret ink. Somewhere above her, in this fortress of gray stone and grayer men, Lord-Regent Corvus Ashfall was probably reading it with his boots on a table.

*Let him,* she thought, and then, because spite required maintenance: *Let him read it twice.*

She sat on the cot and began, quietly, to recite.

---

The interrogations came twice a day, morning and evening, conducted by a man named Inquisitor Hallow who had the face of a disappointed schoolmaster and the patience of one as well. He was not cruel, exactly. He was worse: he was reasonable.

"Rosalind Vane." He always began with her name, as if checking it against a list. "You understand that cooperation is in your interest."

"I understand you keep saying that," Rosalind said. "I'm beginning to suspect it's a script."

He laid out paper. He laid out ink. He did this every time, with the ceremony of a man laying a table, and every time he asked her to write down the prophecy — all of it, word for word, from memory.

"You've read the text," he said. "You are, we believe, the only living person who has read it entire. Its contents are now matters of state security."

"Its contents are poetry," Rosalind said. "Bad poetry, in places. My grandmother underlined the worst lines. There's a simile about doves that I think she found personally offensive."

"Inquisitor," said the guard by the door, warily, as though she might be dangerous.

"She's not going to do anything," Hallow said wearily. "She's going to make jokes until we all die of old age. It's her whole strategy. I respect it, actually."

This was the trouble with Hallow. He kept *respecting* things, and it made him very hard to hate.

But here was what he didn't know, and what Rosalind held beneath her tongue like a coin: she *was* giving him the prophecy. Every day. Word for word. Just — not accurately.

She had discovered it on the second day, almost by accident. Asked to recite the opening lines, she had given them truly, and watched Hallow write them down, and then — because her mouth had been faster than her caution — she had continued past where the true text ended and simply *invented*. A line about rivers running backward. A verse concerning a black tower that would fall when its shadow was named. She had braced for correction.

None came. Hallow wrote it all down. He had never read the book; he only knew fragments, and none of them well. And so Rosalind Vane, captive, unarmed, slippered, had begun feeding the Lord-Regent's intelligence apparatus a prophecy of her own composition, one carefully crafted stanza at a time.

It was, she reflected, the most useful thing her grandmother's tutoring had ever produced. Ten years of memorizing genealogies and dead languages, and the skill that saved her life was the ability to sound authoritative while lying.

Today, Hallow arrived with a new question.

"The final line," he said. "The sealing of the covenant. You've reached it in your recitations. We need it precisely."

Rosalind kept her face smooth. *By the Bearer's blood.* She had read those words a dozen times since the drowned reliquary, and each reading had landed like a stone in her chest. One of them was meant to die. She hadn't told Xanthe. Xanthe hadn't told her whatever it was Xanthe wasn't telling her — she'd seen it in their face on the road, the same careful blankness, the same coin held under the tongue. They were both lying to protect each other, and it was the loneliest thing she had ever known.

"It's fragmentary," she said. "Water damage. The reliquary flooded centuries ago."

"You told us you could reconstruct it."

"I can *attempt* it." She paused, let him lean in. "The old covenant-tongue is ambiguous. 'Blood' might also be rendered 'life' or 'essence.' Some scholars read it as 'the Bearer's devotion.'"

"That is a much more convenient translation," Hallow said.

"Yes," Rosalind agreed pleasantly. "Isn't it?"

He looked at her for a long moment, and she saw the schoolmaster in him wrestle briefly with the suspicion that he was being played, and lose, because the alternative was admitting he'd been played for four days and his reports were already on the Regent's desk.

"Devotion," he repeated, writing it down.

"And a footnote," Rosalind added, "about doves."

---

* * *

That night — or what she assumed was night, the painted window being no help whatsoever — she heard the breathing.

It came through the wall. Not the wall with the false window; the far wall, where the stones were older and someone had once, long ago, chiseled a vent slot no wider than two fingers. At first she thought it was another prisoner. Then she realized the breathing was wrong — too slow, too deep, with a faint whistle at the top of each inhale, like wind over a bottle's mouth.

Rosalind crossed the cell and put her eye to the slot.

Beyond it lay a corridor she couldn't see, but she could hear — and smell, she realized, a smell of hay and animal warmth and something underneath it, sourer, like fear left too long in a closed room. The breathing came again, closer, and then a shape shifted against the faint light of a distant lamp, and she saw it.

A wolf. Gray-muzzled, enormous, lying pressed against the far side of the wall with its head turned toward the vent slot. Looking back at her.

"Oh," Rosalind said softly.

The wolf's ears moved. It made a sound — and the sound stopped her heart. It opened its jaws and tried to speak, or tried to howl, or tried anything at all, and what came out was nothing. A breath shaped like a voice, a silence wearing the costume of sound. The wolf strained, ribs heaving, mouth working, and produced only that hollow whistling exhale.

"No," Rosalind whispered. "No, no — what did they do to you?"

The wolf pressed closer to the slot. Its eye, amber and exhausted, held hers. And Rosalind understood, with the sick certainty of a scholar recognizing a citation, exactly what she was looking at.

The silencing sigil. Xanthe had spoken of it — rumors among beasts, warnings passed down the rat-lines of Greyhollow. A working laid over the citadel, over perhaps the whole borderland, that stole the voices of creatures. That was why the wolves hadn't come. That was why the crows circled Ashfall's towers without ever crying out. The usurper hadn't merely outlawed magic in the human realm. He had *muted* the other one.

And if you could mute a realm, Rosalind thought, her hands going cold against the stone — if you could build a machine for stealing voices — then the breaking of the covenant hadn't been an accident of history. It hadn't faded. It had been *done*, deliberately, by men who wanted the beasts voiceless before they took their lands.

The proof was breathing against her wall.

"I can't fix this tonight," she told the wolf, because honesty seemed owed. "I want you to know that I would if I could. But I can't. Tonight I can only talk to you, and I don't even know if you can understand me."

The wolf's tail moved. Once. Deliberately.

Rosalind laughed — a wet, unsteady sound — and slid down the wall to sit on the floor with her back against the stones, her mouth near the slot.

"All right," she said. "Then let me introduce myself properly. Rosalind Vane. Formerly of Greyhollow, currently of this charming cell, formerly owner of a necklace that apparently contains the fate of two realms, which my grandmother failed to mention at any point during my childhood, which I intend to raise with her at the earliest opportunity, wherever she is."

The tail again. Slower. Almost amused.

"You're laughing at me. Good. Laughing is allowed. Laughing is, in fact, encouraged, because the alternative in this place is thinking too much about the painted window, and I refuse."

She talked for a long time. About Greyhollow's night market, and the charm-seller she'd met there who bargained with cats and pretended not to. About the dinner where everything changed. About the sewers, the road, Vellamare, the water rising in the drowned temple and Xanthe's voice carrying through it — *hold on, hold on, I have you* — words she had decided were the truest thing anyone had ever said to her, delivered underwater, where words carry.

The wolf listened. When she paused too long, it nudged the wall, a soft thump: *go on.*

Somewhere in the middle of it, she heard other sounds join from further down the corridor. A rustle. A clicking, like claws on stone. Something that breathed in short, quick pulses — many somethings. They had gathered. Word traveled, even without voices. Especially, maybe, without voices.

"There are more of you," Rosalind said. "Aren't there. He's got a whole corridor of you."

Thump.

"They took your voices because your voices were the last thing standing between him and everything he wanted." She said it plainly, because they deserved plainness, not comfort. "And I think — I *think* — there may be a way to give them back. I'm wearing — I was wearing — there's a stone. My grandmother hid it. If it does what the prophecies say it does, then it isn't just a relic. It's a key. And keys," she added, "are famously bad at staying locked up."

The wolf made its empty sound again — but differently this time. Softer. Shaped, Rosalind could almost believe, like a question.

"Not yet," she answered. "But soon. And when it happens, I need you to do something for me. All of you." She pressed her palm flat against the cold stone, as near as she could get to touching them. "When your voices come back — however it happens, whenever it happens — I need you to use them. Loudly. Everywhere at once. Let every soul in this fortress hear what he stole. Fear is loud, my grandmother used to say. Make him listen to it."

Silence, of the ordinary kind. Then, from far down the corridor, a strange cascading sound — claws drumming on stone, dozens of them, a rhythm like rain beginning.

Rosalind smiled at the painted window across the cell.

"There," she said. "That's the spirit."

---

* * *

Inquisitor Hallow came at dawn with two guards and a different expression — the schoolmaster replaced, briefly, by something more careful.

"You'll be moved today," he said. "Higher floors. The Regent wishes to question you himself."

Rosalind's stomach dropped, and she set her face against showing it. "How flattering."

"He doesn't flatter." Hallow hesitated. Then, quietly, in the tone of a man setting down a weight he'd carried too far: "Your recitations. The verses about the black tower falling when its shadow is named. The river running backward. I checked them against the fragments in our archive."

Rosalind said nothing.

"They're not in any version we hold," Hallow said. "Which means either your book is unique beyond all reason—" He stopped. Looked at her. And she saw, with a strange lurch, that he wasn't angry. He was *relieved*, in some buried corner of himself, to have caught her — because catching her meant he didn't have to keep believing the rest of it. "Four days, Madam Vane. Four days of reports."

"Five," Rosalind said. "Don't undersell me."

One of the guards made a noise that was suspiciously close to a laugh and disguised it as a cough.

Hallow rubbed his eyes. "Why? You must have known I'd verify eventually. What did four days of nonsense buy you?"

Time, Rosalind thought. Time for the free cities to convene. Time for Xanthe to run, to bargain, to call in debts she knew nothing about. Time for a wolf with no voice to learn the rhythm of her footsteps on the other side of a wall.

"Practice," she said. "I'm a novelist. Everything's practice."

They bound her hands — gently, Hallow insisting on the gentle bindings, another thing she declined to thank him for — and led her out. As she passed the vent slot in the corridor beyond, she slowed, half a step, no more.

From inside the dark, soft as a heartbeat against stone: thump.

Rosalind Vane lifted her chin, fixed her eyes on the stairs ahead, and walked toward the Lord-Regent's tower carrying three things they hadn't searched out of her: a false prophecy, a true promise, and the memory of a voice saying *hold on* underwater, where words carry.

Above her, at the top of the gray stair, a door waited — and behind it, she would learn within the hour, Corvus Ashfall had been reading her grandmother's book.

All of it.

Including the last line.
