## 15. Ashfall's Bargain

Corvus Ashfall had always believed that a man could be judged by the state of his desk.

His own was a marvel of order: ledgers squared to the desk's edge, ink pots arranged by darkness, the reports of the realm stacked in descending weight of consequence. On the topmost pile sat a single sheet, and on that sheet, in Serah Dune's precise hand, were three words that had cost him a night of sleep.

*The Speaker lives.*

He read it again, though he had memorized it hours ago. The Speaker lives. Not *the Speaker is rumored*. Not *a beast-charmer may be at large among the smugglers' roads*. Dune did not deal in rumor. She dealt in fact, and she delivered them like knives — flat, sharp, and point-first.

Corvus set the page down and looked out the window of his study at the citadel of Greyhollow rising around him like a black ribcage. Below, the city sprawled in its winter grime, chimney smoke standing up straight in the still air. Somewhere down there, in the tangle of the night market or the sewers beneath it, a person existed who could talk to rats.

It was almost funny. He permitted himself a thin smile. A century ago, his grandfather's grandfather had burned Speakers by the cartload, and now the last of them had apparently survived by hiding in plain sight, selling luck-charms to gullible fishwives. History was not a grand tapestry. It was a farce performed by people too frightened to laugh.

"Lord-Regent." The door opened without a knock; only one person in the citadel dared. Serah Dune stood in the frame, travel-worn, her grey cloak still dusted with the mountain passes. "You sent for me."

"I sent for you four days ago."

"The roads were watched. By your own witch-finders, which I found instructive." She crossed the room and stood before the desk without being invited. Dune had never learned to grovel, which Corvus privately considered her most valuable quality and her most dangerous one. "You have my report."

"I have your report," he agreed. "What I do not have is my prisoner."

"The Sigil-Bearer is in the deep cells. Unharmed, as instructed." A pause, thin as a razor. "The Speaker escaped with the wolves' help. I lost them at the borderlands."

"You lost them."

"I was outnumbered by rodents, Lord-Regent. I would have preferred to lose them to something with more dignity, but the choice was not mine."

He studied her. That was the trouble with Serah Dune: she never flinched, so it was impossible to tell whether there was anything behind her eyes worth finding. "You believe they'll come for her. The Bearer."

"They came back for each other in Vellamare. They came back for each other under the drowned temple. Yes. They will come for her." Her gaze flicked, briefly, to the window, to the mountains beyond. "And when they do, they will bring every tooth and claw in the free cities behind them. Your edicts sealed the cities, Lord-Regent. Sealed things ferment."

"A colorful metaphor from a woman who burns people for metaphors."

"I burn people for magic. Metaphor is safe. For now." She set something on the desk: a slim volume, its leather cracked with age. "Recovered from the conclave at Highmoor. You should read the third chapter before you decide what to do with the girl."

"I know what's in the third chapter."

"Do you?" For the first time, something moved behind Dune's eyes — not doubt exactly, but the shadow where doubt would live if it ever took up residence. "Then you know it says the covenant is sealed by blood. One bearer's blood. One death. And you know what happens to a prophecy when its condition is met on the wrong terms."

"It ends," Corvus said quietly. "That is rather the point."

Dune held his gaze a moment longer than protocol allowed. Then she inclined her head — a degree, no more — and left him alone with his orderly desk and his disorderly thoughts.

* * *

The deep cells of Greyhollow citadel had been carved by masons who understood that despair is mostly architecture. The corridors narrowed as they descended. The torches grew sparse. By the time Corvus reached the lowest tier, the silence had a texture to it, thick and woolen, the silence of a place built to swallow sound.

Except that it didn't, quite. As he walked the row, he heard it — faint, persistent, coming from behind the reinforced doors. Scratching. Small claws on stone. And beneath it, barely audible, a rustling whisper, like wind through dry grass.

Rosalind Vane was talking to the mice.

Corvus stopped outside her cell and listened with professional interest. The girl's voice was low and rhythmic, and though he caught no words, he recognized the cadence. It was the same cadence the old texts attributed to Speakers — the listening voice, the voice turned outward. She wasn't a Speaker herself, then. Or she was something adjacent. Interesting. Everything about this girl was interesting, which was precisely the problem.

He signaled the guard. The bolts drew back with a sound like breaking bones.

The cell was clean — he'd ordered it kept clean; broken prisoners made poor bargains — and Rosalind Vane sat on the cot with her spine straight and her hands folded, as though he were arriving for tea. Her hair had been beautiful once, before six days underground. Her eyes were another matter entirely. Six days had not touched them at all.

"Lord-Regent," she said. "I wondered how long you'd hide behind your witch-finders."

"I don't hide behind anyone. It's one of my few virtues." He gestured, and the guard set a small table between them, laid with bread, cheese, a flask of wine, two cups. He poured for her himself. Hospitality, properly deployed, was more disarming than any chain. "You'll forgive the venue. My dining hall has better light and worse privacy."

"I'm not hungry."

"Eat anyway. Hunger makes people agreeable in the short term and stupid in the long one, and I need you intelligent." He sat, unhurried, and broke his own bread. "Do you play chess, Miss Vane?"

"No."

"A pity. It's the only game I respect. But let's play it anyway, verbally. I'll describe the board." He set down the bread. "Piece one: you are the Sigil-Bearer, named in a book a century old, wearing a relic your grandmother hid inside a family heirloom, which you have carried since childhood without knowing what it was. Piece two: your companion is the last living Speaker, currently at large, currently raising an army of — forgive me — rats. Piece three: the free cities are convening, my borders are leaking like a sieve, and my captain of witch-finders has begun asking questions about the historical record that I would prefer remained unasked." He smiled. "Your move."

"My move," Rosalind said, "is to tell you that Xanthe Thackeray wouldn't trade a rat's life for mine, let alone denounce them for a cell door. So whatever bargain you've dressed up as chess, you can save us both the performance."

"Ah." He nodded slowly, genuinely pleased. "There it is. Do you know how rare that is? Certainty. Most people, when I mention a bargain, begin calculating immediately — what they can afford to lose, what they can pretend not to know. You went straight to refusal. It's very noble." He leaned forward. "It's also based on an incomplete reading of the board. So let me show you the piece you haven't seen."

He took the prophecy book from his coat — Dune's recovered copy, its pages soft with handling — and turned to the final chapter. He did not give it to her. He simply held it where she could see the line, the one the quill of truth had annotated in ink that shimmered faintly even now, even here, in the dead heart of his citadel.

*Sealed by the Bearer's blood.*

"I know," he said gently, "that you know."

Something happened in her face. It was very small and very fast — a flicker, a contraction, gone in half a heartbeat — but Corvus Ashfall had spent thirty years reading faces in courtrooms and council chambers, and he saw it the way a fisherman sees the twitch of the line. She knew. She had known since Highmoor, or since the drowned temple, and she had told no one. Not even him. Especially not him.

"There it is again," he said softly. "That look. You wear your secrets in your mouth, Miss Vane. You press your lips shut over them. It's charming. It's also fatal."

"If you think you can turn me against Xanthe with a line of prophecy—"

"I think nothing of the sort. Turning you against Thackeray would be a waste of the only competent thing either of you has done." He rose and walked to the cell's narrow window slit, hands clasped behind his back, and when he spoke again his voice had changed — lower, slower, the voice he used for verdicts. "Let me tell you what I actually want. Not your denunciation. Not your recantation. I want the prophecy dead, and I want it dead cleanly, because a living prophecy is a country with two thrones, and I did not spend twenty years climbing over corpses to share mine with a book."

"And killing me accomplishes that?"

"Killing you *complicates* it. If you die at my hand, the prophecy is martyred, not ended. Every outlaw, every Speaker, every sentimental farmwife with a hidden gift becomes a pilgrim to your grave. No." He turned back to face her. "The prophecy must be *fulfilled*, Miss Vane. Properly. On record. Witnessed. The Bearer dies sealing the covenant — publicly, ceremonially, undeniably — and when the seal produces nothing, when no covenant rises and no golden age dawns and the beasts stay beasts and the world stays exactly as broken as it was, then the whole edifice collapses. The book becomes a curiosity. The Speakers become charlatans. The free cities stop dying for a fairy tale." He spread his hands. "I am not offering to murder you. I am offering to *end a war before it starts*. There is a difference, and the difference is everything."

Rosalind had gone very still. "You've thought about this."

"I have thought about little else for a week." He sat again, and now he poured the wine, and pushed the cup across the table until it rested against her folded hands. "Here is the bargain. You renounce the prophecy — freely, before witnesses, in the great hall, with scribes present. You declare the book a grandmother's grief-fueled fantasy. You name Thackeray a fraud who used parlour tricks and trained animals to deceive you. In exchange: your freedom, full pardon, passage to any city you name, and Thackeray's life — banished, hunted never, left to sell their charms in some backwater where no one will ever ask questions again."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then the witch-finders find Thackeray. Not Dune — I'll give the task to Captain Vess, who has a reputation you may have heard of. And when they're brought back here, I won't need a bargain at all. I'll have a Speaker and a Bearer in my cells, and I'll fulfill the prophecy myself, on my terms, with neither of you choosing anything." He tilted his head. "You see the shape of it? Either way, the Bearer's blood seals the covenant. The only question is whether it buys your love a life, or buys you both a knife."

The silence stretched. Down the corridor, faintly, the mice scratched at their walls, and Corvus fancied — absurdly — that they were listening.

"You truly believe this," Rosalind said at last, and there was something new in her voice. Not fear. Wonder, almost. "You believe you're saving people."

"I *know* I'm saving people." And here Corvus felt the truth of it rise in him like heat, the certainty that had carried him through twenty years of purges and politics: "I was nine years old when the last Speaker's war burned the eastern provinces. I watched a man call lightning out of a clear sky and level a granary with three hundred souls inside, and he wept while he did it, Miss Vane. He wept, and the lightning came anyway. Magic does not make people good. It makes them *certain*, and certainty is the only weapon that has ever killed more than hatred. Your grandmother's generation wrote a pretty book about covenants and harmony between realms. I have read the histories underneath the histories. Harmony is a pause between wars. I intend to make it permanent." He stood, buttoning his coat. "You think me a tyrant. I can live with that. Tyrants sleep well. It's the idealists who lie awake."

"The eastern provinces," Rosalind said quietly. "My grandmother was born there."

For one strange moment, the Lord-Regent of Greyhollow and the prisoner in the deep cell regarded each other across a small table with bread going stale between them, and Corvus had the fleeting, unwelcome sense of looking into a mirror polished by someone else's grief.

"Three days," he said. "Think it over. Eat the cheese; it's wasted otherwise." At the door he paused, his hand on the bolt. "One more thing, since you value honesty. Thackeray doesn't know about the blood-line yet, does he? You've carried it alone." He shook his head slowly. "That's the cruelest part of prophecies, Miss Vane. Not the dying. The *carrying*. I'd spare you that, at least. Whatever else you decide."

The bolts slammed home behind him.

* * *

In his study, Corvus poured himself a measure of the good brandy — the bottle he saved for nights when the machinery required oiling — and stood at the window watching the moon come up over the mountains.

It would work. It would work because it was true, or true enough. The girl loved the Speaker; therefore she would trade her silence for their safety; therefore she would stand in his great hall and renounce the book; therefore the prophecy would die of dignity rather than defiance, and the realm would pass into the long gray peace of a story no one believed anymore. And if she refused — well. He was not a cruel man. He was a thorough one. The distinction mattered to him deeply, and he suspected it would matter to history.

He raised the glass to the mountains, to the dark passes where a Speaker was even now gathering an army of small voices, and drank.

Below, in the deep cells, the scratching had stopped.

The mice, all together, all at once, had gone silent — as if something had passed through the stone and told them, in the old tongue, to listen.

Far to the north, past the borderlands, past the sealed passes, the first wolves had begun to move.
