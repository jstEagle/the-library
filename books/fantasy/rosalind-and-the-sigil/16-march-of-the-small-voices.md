## 16. March of the Small Voices

The rats came first.

They always did — Xanthe had learned that much in thirty years of listening to small things. Before any army marched, before any banner was sewn or any oath was sworn, the rats knew. They had known in Greyhollow the night the witch-finders came, and they had known in Vellamare, and they had known at Highmoor while a delegate named Master Ellery Voss smiled across a banquet table and sold them all for a purse of Ashfall's coin.

Now, on the first cold morning of autumn, they came to the borderlands in their thousands, pouring out of hedgerows and culverts and the foundations of ruined barns, and they arranged themselves on the frost-browned hillside in something that was unmistakably, deliberately, a formation.

"They've been practicing," Xanthe said faintly.

Beside them, Bramblewhisker — an elderly rat with one ear chewed to lace and the bearing of a retired general — sat up on his haunches and twitched his whiskers in what could only be called smugness.

*Three weeks of drill,* he said. *The young ones needed discipline. Left to themselves they'd have charged the citadel in a mob and gotten eaten by the first cat with ambitions.*

"You drilled them."

*Someone had to.* He groomed a paw with tremendous dignity. *You said you'd lead us. One does not present an undisciplined host to one's Speaker.*

Xanthe looked out over the hillside. Rats in their tens of hundreds, ranked in ragged squares. Beyond them, wolves sat in patient rows along the ridgeline like a jury that had already decided the verdict. Crows wheeled overhead in slow, deliberate spirals, and every so often one would drop out of the sky to land on a wolf's head, which the wolf endured with the weary tolerance of a soldier whose friend will not stop doing the bit.

And scattered among them all — perhaps forty souls, walking on two legs — the outcast Speakers. The ones who had spent their lives hiding gifts in cellars and haylofts and the backs of charm-stalls. A woman who could call moths. Twin boys who spoke to horses. An old man whose gift was so modest it had never even been illegal until now: he could make bread rise perfectly, every time, and had been run out of three towns for it anyway, because fear does not check whether the magic is useful before it lights the pyre.

"Gods help us," Xanthe murmured. "It's an army."

*It is,* Bramblewhisker agreed, *a work in progress.*

---

They had gathered at the Wolf Queen's summons, though she would have bristled at the word *summons*. Maelis preferred *invitation*, delivered personally, usually by showing up at your campfire at midnight and staring at you until you understood.

She stood at the head of the ridge now, gray as winter and half again as tall as any horse Xanthe had ever seen, her scarred muzzle lifted to test the wind. When Xanthe climbed up to her, she did not turn her head.

*Your two-legs are slow,* she said.

"They're not soldiers. They're bakers and hedge-witches and a man who talks to bees."

*The bees are already here.* She flicked an ear toward a low golden hum drifting over the hillside. *They volunteered. Loudly.*

Xanthe laughed despite everything — despite Rosalind in a cage in Ashfall's citadel, despite the debt still unpaid, despite the fact that they were marching an army of vermin and outlaws against the most fortified stronghold in the known world. Laughing felt like treason against despair, which made it the only honest thing left.

"Maelis." The humor drained out of them as quickly as it had come. "You know what I'm asking. This isn't the favor. The favor comes later, at the walls. This march — this is me asking beasts to die for a covenant most of them never benefited from. It was broken before any of us were born."

Maelis turned then, and her eyes were the color of old ice, and very old indeed.

*Broken,* she said. *Yes. My grandmother's grandmother remembered the last of it. She told her daughter, who told her daughter, who told me: once, the two-legs listened. Once, when the herds moved, men rode ahead to warn the villages, and when the wolves took a sick deer instead of a farmer's cow, men left the forest edge alone in thanks. Then the tablet went silent, and the silencing came, and one by one the voices went out of the world.* Her lip curled from her teeth. *Ashfall thinks he stole power when he took the tablet. He stole our voices. There is no debt in what we do now, little Speaker. This is not payment. This is collection.*

Somewhere down the slope, one of the young wolves howled — a short, eager bark of sound — and forty more answered, and the sound rolled down into the valley where the human Speakers stood, and Xanthe saw the old bread-riser fall to his knees in the frost, weeping, because he had never in his life heard wild things sing and meant to be heard.

*We march at dusk,* Maelis said. *The crows say the citadel's eyes are on the roads. So we will not use the roads.*

"Then where—"

*The mountains.* And there was something in her voice then, something almost sly, which Xanthe had not thought a wolf capable of. *There is a pass. The two-legs think it is closed.*

---

Dusk came down purple and cold, and the army of small voices moved.

It did not move like armies in ballads. There were no drums. There was, in fact, a strict rule against drums, established after the first night of drilling when a enthusiastic badger contingent had attempted percussion and woken every hawk in three valleys. Instead the host flowed through the borderlands like water finding its level — wolves loping in wide flanking lines, rats traveling in the ditches and drain-tunnels in rivers of dark fur, the human Speakers riding cart-horses and plow-horses lent willingly (and, Xanthe suspected, after some negotiation conducted entirely without their knowledge), and above it all the crows, wheeling and dipping, carrying word back and forth faster than any rider.

Xanthe rode near the front on a broad-backed mare named Duchess, who had opinions about pace and expressed them by simply ignoring all instructions and maintaining her own preferred amble. Beside them trotted Bramblewhisker, riding in state in Xanthe's hood, and behind them came the twins who spoke to horses, giggling, because apparently Duchess had been complaining about Xanthe's rein-hand the entire way and the boys found it hilarious.

"She says my rein-hand is fine," Xanthe said.

*She says you steer like a sack of turnips,* said the taller twin, Pip, delighted. *Also she wants to know if there will be apples at the citadel.*

"Duchess, we are marching to war."

Duchess flicked an ear and did not break stride, in the manner of one who has heard promises before.

The humor helped. Xanthe clung to it the way a climber clings to rock — because underneath it was the other thing, the thing that woke them before dawn every morning with Rosalind's face behind their eyes: her hands pressed flat against the visiting-room glass before the guards pulled her away, her mouth shaping words no glass should have had to carry. *Don't you dare come alone. Don't you dare be stupid and noble. Bring everyone.*

So Xanthe had brought everyone.

On the third night, camped in the black pines below the mountains, the crows brought news.

Her name was Sorrowlock — she had chosen it herself, she said, because if you were going to be a bird of ill omen you might as well commit — and she landed on Xanthe's knee with the particular stiff-legged formality that meant she had something official to report and wished it noted.

*Witch-finder column,* she reported. *Two hundred picked riders. Moving south along the King's Road under the witch-finder captain herself. Serah Dune. They will reach the mountain garrisons in four days. If the garrisons hold the passes, we are meat.*

The camp went quiet around them. Wolves raised their heads. The moth-caller, Wren, stopped mid-sentence with her hands still glowing faintly silver where she'd been teaching the twins a cantrip.

"There's no way through if the passes close," Xanthe said slowly. "Maelis said there was a pass the two-legs think is closed."

*Closed,* Sorrowlock agreed, *by order. Sealed with stone and iron eleven years ago, when Ashfall took the throne. The Grey Stair, it's called. It goes straight over the shoulder of the mountain and comes down behind the citadel's own gardens, if the maps in my grandmother's head are right, and my grandmother was never wrong about anything except the number of eggs she'd laid.* She ruffled her feathers. *But closed is closed. Stone and iron, Speaker. Unless someone opens it.*

"Who would open it?"

Sorrowlock looked at Xanthe with one bright eye, and then, with great deliberation, turned her head to look at the whole assembled camp with the other, and said nothing at all, in the manner of crows, which is somehow louder than speech.

That was when the second crow arrived — a ragged young male named Crumb, breathless, tumbling out of the northern sky to land in the firelight with his feathers askew.

*The witch-finder,* he gasped. *Dune. She's broken off from her column. She's taken six riders, no more, and gone up into the mountains — north fork, the old pilgrim trail. And Speaker — she's got the garrison muster-books with her. The original ones. The ones from before the Edict.*

Xanthe felt the whole camp lean in, wolves and rats and humans alike, drawn by the same thread.

"And?" they asked. "What of it?"

Crumb caught his breath, and when he spoke, he dropped his voice as though the mountains themselves might be listening.

*She's been reading them. For days, ever since Vellamare. My cousin's been shadowing her since the river — Dune doesn't sleep, she just sits with those books by the fire, and she reads, and sometimes she laughs, this awful little laugh, like something breaking. And tonight, tonight she reached the Grey Stair in her column's orders, and the order says the pass was sealed by decree of the old king, may-he-rest, twenty years before Ashfall was born — but the seal-mark on the paper is Ashfall's own sigil, the raven-and-crown, and it's dated wrong, the ink's wrong, anyone who's handled a hundred documents can see it's wrong, and she saw it.* His voice shook. *She read it three times. Then she burned her copy of the orders. Then she told her six riders to wait at the trailhead, and she went up the mountain alone.*

The fire cracked. Somewhere in the dark, Maelis's eyes reflected the flames like twin coals.

"She knows," Xanthe said. The words came out strange, half wonder, half dread. "She knows the histories are forged. She's known since Vellamare. And now she's—" They stopped, started again. "She's going up to the Grey Stair. Alone. To open it."

*Or to die on it,* said Bramblewhisker pragmatically, from the hood. *Witch-finders are not famous for mercy, and traitors less so. If her own riders catch her—*

"They won't." Xanthe was surprised by their own certainty. "She sent them away. You don't send witnesses away unless you mean to do something you can't take back." They stood, legs stiff, heart hammering. "Crows. I need eyes on that pass. Every bird you can spare. If Dune opens the Grey Stair, we go through it tonight, ahead of her column, ahead of everything. And if she fails—"

*If she fails,* Maelis finished, rising to her feet with a sound like a landslide learning to walk, *then we go through the garrisons the old way. Over their bones.* She lowered her great head to Xanthe's level, and her voice gentled by perhaps one degree, which from Maelis was practically a lullaby. *Sleep, little Speaker. Tomorrow we climb.*

---

They climbed.

All the next day the army of small voices went up into the mountains by goat-tracks and game trails, the wolves ranging wide to find the paths, the rats scouting every scree slope for loose stone, the horses picking their way with a patience that suggested Duchess had extracted contractual guarantees regarding apples. The air thinned and sharpened. Snow dusted the high shoulders of the peaks. And always overhead the crows wheeled, watching the Grey Stair, waiting.

Near sunset, Sorrowlock came down out of the dark like a thrown knife.

*It's open,* she cried, banking low over the column, and the word ran down the line like fire through dry grass — *it's open, it's open, she opened it* — and Xanthe stood in their stirrups to look, and there it was.

High on the mountain's shoulder, a seam of darkness split the snowlit rock: the Grey Stair, unsealed. And standing small and solitary at its mouth, a lantern in her hand, a figure in a witch-finder's coat — Serah Dune, who had hunted them across half the free cities, who had burned the forged orders, who had climbed alone to undo with her own hands the lie her whole life had been built on.

She could not have seen the army in the failing light. But she must have heard it — the wolves beginning to howl, low and long and rolling, the sound Xanthe now understood was not menace at all but the oldest song there was, the one that meant *we are coming, and we remember.*

Serah Dune stood aside from the pass. Deliberately. The way one stands aside from a door.

*Well,* said Bramblewhisker softly, from the hood. *Justice outside the law. Who would have thought it would wear a witch-finder's coat.*

Xanthe spurred Duchess forward — amble abandoned, apples promised, all bargains honored — and the army of small voices poured toward the open pass: wolves and rats and crows and bakers and bee-speakers and children who had hidden their gifts their whole lives, streaming up the mountain in the last light, toward the citadel where Rosalind waited, toward the hall where the stolen tablet slept, toward whatever the dawn would bring.

Behind them, far to the south, torches kindled along the King's Road as Dune's own column discovered her empty camp.

Ahead, above the pass, the citadel's towers stood black against the stars.

And somewhere inside those towers, Xanthe thought, gripping the reins, steering like a sack of turnips and not caring — somewhere in there, Rosalind was alive, and angry, and waiting, and the silencing sigil that held every stolen voice in the city was about to meet the one thing its maker had never planned for:

An army that had nothing left to lose, coming quietly, on small feet, in the dark.

*Hold on,* Xanthe whispered, to a woman behind walls, across miles of frozen stone. *We're bringing everyone.*
