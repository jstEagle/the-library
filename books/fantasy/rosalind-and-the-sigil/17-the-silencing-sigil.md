## 17. The Silencing Sigil

The silence was the worst part.

Rosalind had expected the citadel of Lord-Regent Corvus Ashfall to be cruel in obvious ways — iron, torchlight, the smell of blood and wet stone. It had all of those. But beneath them, woven through everything like a second set of walls, was the quiet.

Not peace. Not stillness. *Silence*, deliberate and total, pressing against her ears like deep water. She'd been in the Cage of Quiet for six days now, and she had learned that the silencing sigil did not merely stop sound. It stopped *voice*. She could hum, clap, drag her chains across the floor, and the sounds came out thin and muffled, as though the air itself were embarrassed to carry them. But when she tried to speak — truly speak, words with weight behind them — nothing came. Her mouth moved. The room swallowed it whole.

She had tested it the first night by reciting the prophecy aloud, the false version she'd invented for her interrogators, full of nonsense lines about rivers of milk and a Bearer who would arrive riding a goose. Even alone, even for no one, she couldn't hear herself say it. The words died somewhere between her tongue and the dark.

*They don't just imprison you here,* she thought, lying on the narrow cot with her hands laced behind her head, staring at a ceiling she had memorized crack by crack. *They take your voice away first. Everything else is just housekeeping.*

Somewhere below her — three floors down, if the rats' gossip she'd overheard through the wall was accurate, and rats were rarely inaccurate about architecture — there were cells full of beasts. Wolves, crows, an old marsh-bear someone had dragged out of the borderlands. Creatures whose voices had been stolen by the same sigil, kept not for ransom or information but simply because their voices were dangerous. Because a beast that could speak could testify. And Ashfall's entire reign rested on a history that could not survive testimony.

Rosalind pressed her hand flat against her sternum, where the heirloom hung on its chain beneath her shirt.

Her grandmother's pendant. A dull grey stone in a setting of tarnished silver, the kind of thing a pawnbroker would wave away without an offer. She had worn it every day since she was twelve and had spent years assuming it was worthless, because Grandmother Vane had been many things — sharp, secretive, terrifying at cards — but sentimental about junk was not one of them.

It was the first relic. The sigil-stone. Hidden in plain sight on the neck of a girl who hadn't known what prophecy was until it arrived at her dinner table wearing her own name.

*I'm sorry I called you ugly for fifteen years,* she told it silently. *You've grown on me.*

The stone did not answer. Stones rarely did. But it sat warm against her skin, warmer than it should have been, and it had been growing warmer all week — since the night Xanthe escaped the conclave at Highmoor with witch-finders howling at their heels, since the moment Rosalind had stepped backward into the ambush so that Xanthe could step forward into the dark and live.

Six days. She tried not to count them and counted them anyway, the way you can't help counting the spaces between lightning and thunder.

---

The interrogator came at midday, which Rosalind had decided was a character flaw rather than a scheduling choice. His name was Wexler, he smelled of ink and vinegar, and he had the particular patience of a man who believed cruelty was simply thoroughness applied over time.

"The Regent is a reasonable man," Wexler said, arranging his papers on the little table they'd brought in for the purpose. "He offers you freedom. All you must do is stand in the square and renounce the prophecy. Renounce the Speaker. Say the covenant is a fable."

"Mm," said Rosalind.

"You cannot even speak here. You would be given back your voice for the occasion. A public voice, before crowds."

"Mm-hm."

Wexler looked up from his papers. Six days of this had worn grooves in him; she could see them. "You understand that your silence changes nothing? That the Speaker will be taken regardless? That the beasts will remain voiceless whether you cooperate or rot?"

"Oh, I understand perfectly," Rosalind said — or meant to say. What came out was a breath shaped like nothing. The sigil ate it. She smiled instead, wide and pleasant, the smile her grandmother had taught her for use against cardsharps and tax collectors.

Wexler gathered his papers with slightly more force than necessary and left.

Rosalind lay back down. Under her shirt, the sigil-stone was almost hot now. And — she frowned, pressing two fingers to it — it was *ticking*. Not ticking, exactly. Pulsing. A slow rhythm, like something breathing under a blanket, or a heart deciding whether to bother waking up.

She sat up.

She had examined the stone a hundred times in her life. As a child she'd scratched at it, dropped it, once buried it in the garden for a fortnight to see if Grandmother would notice (Grandmother had noticed within the hour and said only, "Dig it up, dear, it doesn't like the cold"). She knew its every flaw: the hairline crack across its face, the way the silver setting caught light wrong.

But she had never seen it glow.

It wasn't glowing dramatically. It was glowing the way embers glow when you think the fire's dead — a faint seam of gold along the hairline crack, brightening and dimming in time with that slow pulse. And with each pulse, Rosalind felt something she had no better word for than *pulling*. As though the stone were leaning toward something. Or something were leaning toward the stone.

Toward the ward. The great silencing sigil that wrapped the citadel like a hand around a throat.

*Oh,* Rosalind thought. *Oh, you clever old woman.*

Grandmother Vane had been a covenant-keeper. She had hidden the first relic where no witch-finder would ever think to look: on the neck of her own granddaughter, disguised as family rubbish. And a relic of the covenant — the pact between human and beast realms, the thing that made magic lawful, the thing Ashfall had broken and buried — a relic like that would hate a silencing ward the way fire hates a locked door.

It wasn't waiting for permission. It was waiting for proximity. For her to get close enough to the heart of the thing.

Rosalind got up and began, very quietly, to plan.

---

Getting close to the heart of the silencing ward turned out to require less heroism and more paperwork, which felt fitting. Every institution, even tyranny, ran on forms.

She learned the rhythms of her jailers over the next day. Meals came twice; the turnkey, a broad woman named Hesk with kind eyes and terrible posture, lingered longest at the evening delivery because the corridor above played music she liked to hum along to (badly, silently, thanks to the ward). The interrogations happened midday. And once a week — tomorrow, as it happened — the prisoners' chamber pots went down to the cistern level, carried by two guards who hated the job and took the stairs past the lowest cells, where the beasts were kept.

Rosalind didn't need to go to the cistern. She needed to go *past* it. Close enough.

So she did the one thing a prisoner has in abundance: she made herself inconvenient. She folded her blanket into shapes. She arranged her hairpins — confiscated, then returned, then confiscated again, then forgotten in a drawer — into patterns on the windowsill that Wexler insisted were coded messages and were, in fact, mostly decorative. On the morning of the pot-run, she wedged her cot against the door hinge so that it jammed, and when Hesk came to fix it, Rosalind pointed urgently down the corridor, mimed falling, clutched her ankle, and produced an expression of such profound theatrical suffering that Hesk sighed, unlocked the door, and half-carried her toward the infirmary stair — which, conveniently, passed the cistern landing.

The sigil-stone burned against her chest like a coal wrapped in linen.

At the cistern landing, Rosalind heard them.

Not with her ears. With something older. Through the stone, through the ward, through six days of stolen quiet, she felt them: wolves, three of them, packed into a cell built for one, their voices crushed down to a pressure behind their ribs. A crow, alone, who had been silent so long it had forgotten its own song. Something huge and patient in the deepest cell, breathing slow — the marsh-bear, maybe, or something grander. They weren't asleep. They were never fully asleep. They were lying in the dark being *unheard*, and the weight of it was enormous, a cathedral of unsaid things.

Rosalind stopped walking. Hesk said something. Rosalind didn't hear it.

Because the stone cracked.

Not broke — *cracked open*, the hairline seam flaring gold, and warmth poured through her, down her arm, into her palm, which was somehow pressed flat against the wall without her having decided anything. And the wall — the stone of the citadel itself, soaked for years in stolen voices — drank the warmth and shivered.

"Miss?" Hesk's hand on her shoulder. "Miss, you've gone white—"

Rosalind pulled the chain over her head.

She held the sigil-stone up in both hands, and it blazed, and she opened her mouth and found — impossibly, wonderfully — that her voice was *there*. Not eaten. Not swallowed. Rising up through her like water finding a crack in a dam.

"**I HEAR YOU,**" Rosalind said, to the wolves, to the crow, to the vast patient thing below, to every creature this citadel had muted. "**YOU ARE NOT SILENT. YOU NEVER WERE.**"

The ward answered.

It came down on her like a wave — the full weight of the silencing sigil, Ashfall's masterwork, decades of stolen voices compressed into a single crushing note of *no*. Rosalind's knees buckled. Hesk shouted something and grabbed for her. The gold light guttered.

And then the stone did what relics do, what covenants do, what Grandmothers build them to do.

It pushed back.

The seam of light split the stone clean in half — and every voice the ward had ever swallowed came out.

---

Later, people in the city below would describe the sound differently. Some said it was a bell. Some said it was a wolf-pack singing. One fishwife swore it was her late husband's voice saying her name, and she wept into her nets and refused to amend the account.

It was all of those. It was the crow remembering its song mid-flight of sound, a rough black ribbon of noise unspooling up through six floors of stone. It was three wolves howling in a cell too small for one, their voices arriving not through their throats but through the walls themselves, through the iron, through the marrow of the building. It was the marsh-bear — and it *was* a bear, an enormous, ancient, gravel-throated bear — uttering one syllable that was less a word than a verdict.

And underneath all of it, threaded through it, holding it together like a spine: Rosalind's own voice, laughing and crying at once, shouting into the golden ruin of the ward:

"**LOUDER!**"

The towers shook. Dust sifted from ceilings in the barracks. In the scriptorium, a clerk knocked an inkwell into a year of records and did not care. The silencing sigil didn't shatter the way glass shatters — it tore, like cloth, like a lie finally meeting a fact, ripping outward from the cistern landing in seams of failing light, and through every tear poured sound. Rats in the walls, chattering triumphantly. Dogs in the kennels answering the wolves. The cistern water itself seeming to ring like a struck bowl.

Hesk had fallen against the opposite wall, hands over her ears, eyes enormous. She wasn't afraid, Rosin realized. She was listening. Listening the way starving people eat.

"It's true, then," Hesk said hoarsely, over the roar. "All of it. The old stories. Magic was *lawful* once." She looked at Rosalind — at the broken stone, the blazing seams in the air — and made a decision so fast it seemed she'd been carrying it for years, waiting for a place to put it down. She unhooked the ring of keys from her belt and threw it to Rosalind.

"Wolf cell's third on the left," she said. "I'll mind the stairs."

---

Rosalind ran.

The keys sang in her fist. The corridor lights flickered with each pulse of the dying ward, and everywhere she ran, voices rose to meet her — beasts calling out from their cells, testing their returned voices like people testing ice, disbelieving, then believing, then *roaring*. She threw open the wolf cell and three gaunt, furious, magnificent wolves flowed past her legs, and the largest paused just long enough to press its forehead hard against her knee — a debt acknowledged, a promise filed.

"*The Speaker lives,*" the wolf said, and its voice in her head was like wind off snowfields. "*We heard the rats. We are coming.*"

"Tell everyone," Rosalind gasped. "Tell the birds, tell the bears, tell the—"

The tower shuddered. Somewhere far above, alarm bells — real ones, human ones, panicked ones — began to clang, and boots thundered in stairwells, and a horn blew three short notes that Rosalind had read about in smugglers' gossip and never hoped to hear: *prisoner loose, seal the levels.*

She also heard, threading under the chaos, from beyond the citadel walls, from the hills and the borderlands dark — a sound like the world clearing its throat.

Howls. Hundreds. Crows like a torn sky. The small, vast army of everything Ashfall had ever silenced, coming to collect.

Rosalind Vane stood in a corridor full of freed voices, clutching half a stone and a stolen keyring, and laughed out loud — her voice, her own, loud enough to shake dust from the rafters.

Then she turned toward the upper levels, toward the covenant hall, toward wherever Xanthe was at this moment fighting their way through, and started to run.

*Hold on, love,* she thought, taking the stairs three at a time. *I'm bringing the noise.*
