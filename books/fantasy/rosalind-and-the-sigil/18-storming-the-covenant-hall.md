## 18. Storming the Covenant Hall

### Xanthe

The citadel of Lord-Regent Corvus Ashfall had been built to be hated. Every tower was a raised chin, every wall a sneer, every window a slit through which the powerful could look down on everyone they had decided to fear. Xanthe had spent their whole life in cities like Greyhollow, where power kept itself small and sly and hid behind charm-stalls and locked doors. This place did not hide. It stood on its black crag above the borderlands with its teeth out.

Xanthe came up the causeway at the head of an army that had no banners and no drums and no business whatsoever being an army.

Rats poured along the drainage channels by the thousand, a grey river with a purpose. Crows wheeled overhead in ragged spirals, screaming down corrections to the wolves below — *left, left, the gate's weak on the left* — while wolves flowed through the outer gardens like smoke given teeth. Behind them all came the outcast Speakers, men and women who had spent decades flinching, now walking upright with animals at their shoulders and terror and joy fighting openly on their faces.

"Gate's open," said Marrow, the wolf queen's second, trotting at Xanthe's knee. Her voice was a growl wrapped around words. "The tall one with the sad eyes opened it."

Serah Dune. Xanthe hadn't seen her do it, but the crows had, and the crows told everyone everything. The witch-finder captain had walked to the mountain pass, looked at the forged edicts tucked inside her coat for the hundredth time, and then simply stepped aside and let the march come through. *Sad eyes*, the crow had said. Xanthe wondered what it cost a person to open a gate knowing there was no un-opening it.

"Don't get sentimental," Marrow advised, reading Xanthe's face the way wolves read everything. "Sentimental Speakers trip."

"I'll bear it in mind."

The first real resistance came at the inner courtyard, where Ashfall's guards had formed ranks behind pikes, and above them, on the walls, witch-finders were hauling something up onto the parapets — brass cages, and in the cages, stones carved with the silencing sigil.

Xanthe felt it before it activated: a pressure in the ears, a wrongness, like a door slamming on a room full of singing. Then the ward dropped over the courtyard and every voice in it died.

Not the human voices. Those still worked; Xanthe could hear officers shouting orders, boots on stone. But the beast-voices went silent as cut strings. Marrow staggered beside them, mouth opening on a howl that made no sound at all, and around the courtyard dozens of animals froze mid-stride, suddenly alone in their own heads for the first time since they'd joined the march.

The stolen quiet. The same quiet Rosalind had described from the cells below. Ashfall had built his whole citadel on it.

Xanthe's hands shook. Not with fear — with fury, which was rarer and more useful. They pressed both palms flat against the flagstones and reached down through the stone, past the foundations, into the earth beneath the crag, where the rats lived in their tens of thousands and where, thank every listening god, the silencing ward did not reach.

*Rats,* Xanthe said, into the deep dark under the world. *The cages on the west wall. Chew the ropes.*

It took eleven seconds. Xanthe counted. Eleven seconds of watching witch-finders aim their brass cages outward, eleven seconds of the courtyard held in that horrible cotton silence — and then the counterweight ropes frayed through all at once, and forty brass cages crashed down off the parapet and burst on the flagstones, and the sigil-stones cracked, and the silence tore open like a seam.

Marrow's howl came back mid-note, furious and enormous. The crows dove. The wolf line hit the pike wall at the exact moment the guards flinched from the falling cages, and after that the courtyard stopped being a battle and started being a rout.

"The hall," Xanthe gasped to Marrow. "Where's the covenant hall?"

"Under the keep. Deep." Marrow's muzzle was red; her eyes were bright. "The old badger who scouts for us says there are stairs behind the armory. He says—" she paused, and her ears went back, "—he says someone is already running for the stairs. A woman. Dark hair. She fights like she's angry at the stairs personally."

Xanthe's heart performed a maneuver that would have embarrassed them if anyone had been watching.

"That's mine," they said. "That one's mine."

### Rosalind

Rosalind Vane had escaped from a cell using a necklace, a stolen hairpin, and a chorus of freed beasts screaming the towers half-down, and she was not going to pretend afterward that any part of it had been dignified.

The heirloom sigil-stone — her grandmother's locket, the thing she'd worn since she was nine years old without once wondering why it hummed when she held it too long — had burned white when she pressed it against the cell ward. The silencing sigil across the citadel had shattered like ice on a pond, and every voice Ashfall had stolen came roaring back at once, and Rosalind had run through the chaos of the unlocking with her heart in her throat and one name in her head, repeated like a prayer with better grammar.

*Xanthe. Please be alive. Please be alive and insufferable about it.*

She'd found a knife somewhere between the cells and the armory — taken off a guard who would wake with a headache and a story — and now she took the stairs behind the armory three at a time, down into the bones of the keep, following a pull she couldn't explain except that the locket was warm against her sternum and getting warmer, like a compass needle finally swinging home.

The prophecy book's missing pages had named her. *The Bearer shall come bearing the mark.* They had also, she remembered with a cold slide in her stomach, named the price. *And the covenant shall be sealed by the Bearer's blood.* She hadn't told Xanthe. There had never been a right moment, and then there had never been time, and now there was only this: stairs, darkness, warmth at her chest, and the growing certainty that whatever waited at the bottom wanted exactly what the prophecy wanted.

Well. It could want. Wanting wasn't getting.

The stairs ended at a door of black oak banded in iron, and the locket flared so hot she had to hold it away from her skin. Carved into the lintel above — so worn she nearly missed it — ran a line of the old script, the same script from her grandmother's book. She couldn't read it, but she didn't need to. She'd seen this exact carving before, on the first page, above the first line of the first prophecy ever written down:

*Here the two realms spoke as one.*

Rosalind put her shoulder to the door. It opened — unlocked, unguarded, almost courteously — and she stepped into the covenant hall.

It was smaller than she expected. That was the first shock. After a century of stories, she had imagined a cathedral; instead it was a round chamber the size of a modest dining room, lit by a shaft of moonlight from a broken ceiling far above, and utterly plain except for what stood at its center.

The tablet. The original covenant, the thing Ashfall had stolen to make himself a regent. It stood upright on a plinth of living granite, taller than a person, and it was covered edge to edge in the old script, and it was *broken* — a jagged crack running through it like a lightning strike frozen in stone. And set into the crack, glowing faintly, was the shape of a missing piece. A gap in the middle of the tablet, roughly the size and shape of a locket.

"Oh," Rosalind said aloud, to nobody. "Oh, you have got to be joking."

Beside the plinth, laid on a block of stone as deliberately as an offering, was a knife. Old, bronze-hilted, ceremonial, and unmistakably meant for a hand and a palm and a covenant sealed in blood.

Rosalind looked at the knife. The knife looked, in the way of knives, extremely available.

"No," she said firmly, to the room, to the prophecy, to her grandmother's ghost wherever it was lurking. "I've read this chapter. I know how it goes. One bearer bleeds, the other grieves, everyone writes sad songs about it for a hundred years. I decline."

She picked up the knife anyway.

This requires some explanation, and the explanation is this: if the covenant demanded blood, then blood it would get, but it would get it on *her* terms, quickly, before Xanthe could arrive and do anything noble. Xanthe was the keystone — the Speaker, the lost half of the covenant, the one whose gift the whole broken world had been waiting on. Rosalind was just the girl with the necklace. Of the two of them, the arithmetic was simple and terrible, and Rosalind had done the arithmetic every night since the quill had shown her the final line, and every night she had arrived at the same answer and hidden it in the same silence.

If someone had to bleed out on a stone floor tonight, it was going to be the one whose death the prophecy could survive.

She turned the blade toward her own left palm.

The door behind her banged open.

### Xanthe

Xanthe took the stairs four at a time, which was reckless, and arrived in the doorway of the covenant hall in time to see Rosalind Vane standing in a shaft of moonlight with a ceremonial knife turned toward her own hand.

Everything Xanthe had planned to say — and they had planned quite a lot, mostly jokes with grief hiding underneath them, because that was how Xanthe handled things — went out of their head at once.

"*No.*"

Rosalind spun, and her face did three things in one second: relief so naked it hurt to look at, joy, and then — fastest of all — guilt, snapping over the other two like a mask dropping. She shoved the knife behind her back with the smoothness of a woman who had never once been convincing at anything.

"Xanthe! You're alive. Wonderful. Great. How were the stairs?"

"There's a knife behind your back."

"There is absolutely not a knife behind my back."

"I speak to rats, Rosalind. Rats tell me things. Do you know what else tells me things? My eyes." Xanthe crossed the chamber in six strides and caught her wrist, and Rosalind, to her credit, fought for exactly one heartbeat before her shoulders dropped and the knife came out from behind her, hilt-first, surrendered like a confession.

They stood there holding the knife between them, neither letting go, breathing hard, moonlight and locket-glow turning them both silver.

"You were going to cut your own palm," Xanthe said. Not a question.

"It doesn't need both of us." Rosalind's chin came up, defiant, wet-eyed. "It needs a Bearer. I'm the Bearer. You're the Speaker — the keystone, the actual point of the entire prophecy. If I die sealing it, the covenant lives and you live and the beasts get their voices back and the world gets fixed, and the only cost is—"

"You."

"The only cost is me," Rosalind agreed, as though this were a reasonable line item in a budget. "Which I've already grieved about, privately, at length, very efficiently, and I'm fine now, so if you'd just let go of my wrist—"

"No."

"Xanthe—"

"*No.*" It came out louder than Xanthe intended, cracking in the middle, and the sound of it bounced around the little round room and came back sounding like every night since the sewers, every mile since Greyhollow, every single moment Xanthe had spent falling in love with this infuriating brave stupid woman while quietly doing the same arithmetic in the opposite direction. Because here was the thing Rosalind didn't know: Xanthe had read the final line too. Had read it in the drowned reliquary by quill-light while Rosalind slept, and had spent every mile since carrying it the way you carry a stone in your boot.

Because the prophecy said *Bearer*. And the quill, which wrote only truth, had written something else in the margin, small and slanted, that Xanthe had memorized and told no one: *the blood of the one who binds must be freely given — and the binding runs through the Speaker.*

Through the Speaker. Through Xanthe. The covenant's keystone. The math, done honestly, only came out one way.

So they had each been carrying the same secret in opposite pockets for weeks, two people in love each planning to die alone rather than mention it, and now they stood in the room where it mattered, and the secret was done being patient.

"You read the last line," Rosalind whispered. She knew. Of course she knew; she was looking at Xanthe's face and seeing her own dread reflected there. "When? When did you—"

"The reliquary. While you slept." Xanthe's laugh came out wrecked. "You recited prophecies falsely at your interrogators and bought me time to escape, and I repaid you by secretly deciding to die. We're a disaster. We're the worst-planned rescue in the history of two realms."

"The margin note," Rosalind said slowly. "The quill wrote something, didn't it. Something you didn't tell me." Her grip tightened on the knife. "Something that says it's supposed to be *you*."

"And yours says it's supposed to be *you*." Xanthe nodded at the locket, glowing at her chest like a small determined sun. "Your grandmother hid the first relic on your neck and the prophecy calls you Bearer. Mine says the binding runs through the Speaker. We've each got a document supporting our case. It's like a tribunal in here."

"Then we settle it the civilized way," Rosalind said, and moved.

Later, historians would describe what happened next in the covenant hall with great solemnity. What actually happened was that two exhausted, terrified, desperately in love people wrestled over a bronze knife in a moonlit basement, and it was not majestic. Rosalind was stronger than she looked and fought dirty; Xanthe was faster and fought dirtier. The knife clattered to the floor. Both dove for it. Their heads knocked together with a crack that would have felled a mule, and they ended up sprawled side by side on the cold stone, seeing stars, laughing in the unhinged breathless way of people who have been afraid too long.

"We're going to kill ourselves before the prophecy gets the chance," Rosalind wheezed.

"Would serve it right."

But the laughter drained away, because the locket was rising off Rosalind's chest — actually rising, tugging at its chain toward the broken tablet — and the crack in the ancient stone had begun to glow, and the shaft of moonlight from the broken ceiling had narrowed, sharpened, focused down onto the plinth like a finger pointing. And in the glow, letters of the old script were surfacing along the crack, letter by letter, the final line assembling itself in light:

*THE COVENANT SHALL BE SEALED BY THE BEARER'S BLOOD.*

And beneath it, new, wet, as though being written even now by an invisible quill:

*ONE BEARER. ONE DEATH. CHOOSE.*

The knife, lying on the floor between them, trembled, and rose, and hung in the air at the height of a hand, hilt offered, patient as winter.

Neither of them moved.

"Xanthe," Rosalind said quietly. Her hand found theirs in the dark and gripped it, hard, grounding, real. "Whatever you're thinking — whatever noble ridiculous thing you're thinking — don't."

"I could say the same."

"I know. That's the problem." She laughed, shaky. "We've been trying to save each other from ourselves for weeks. It hasn't worked once."

The knife waited. The tablet glowed. Somewhere far above, the battle for the citadel roared and cracked, and the freed voices of a hundred stolen creatures sang through the stone.

And Xanthe looked at the hanging blade, and at the woman they loved, and at the prophecy that had arranged this whole century like a trap with only one door — and thought, very clearly, the thought that would change everything:

*A trap only has one door if you agree to use it.*

"Rosalind," they said. "What if we don't choose?"

Her eyes came up. In the locket-light they were fierce and wet and entirely awake.

"What do you mean?"

"The quill," Xanthe said, already reaching for the satchel at their hip, where a feathered relic that wrote only truth had ridden since the drowned temple. "The quill rewrote the annotator's notes once. It's a covenant instrument — it's *made* to write the terms. And the terms say one bearer's blood." Their heart was hammering, but the idea had hold of them now, huge and impossible and shining. "It says one. It doesn't say *which*. And it has never once met two bearers who refused to pick."

Above them, the final line pulsed, waiting. The knife turned slowly in the air, patient, certain.

Rosalind stared at Xanthe — and then, slowly, terribly, wonderfully, she began to smile.

"Get the quill," she said.
