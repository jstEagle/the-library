## 19. Rewriting the Line

### Xanthe

The covenant hall had not been opened in a hundred years, and it smelled like it.

Dust lay over everything in a grey velvet coat — over the broken benches where delegates of two realms had once sat side by side, over the shattered mosaic of a wolf and a woman shaking hands, over the great stone tablet that rose at the hall's heart like a tombstone for the world that used to be. Xanthe's boots left prints in a century of silence.

Behind them, the citadel roared. Freed beasts poured through corridors that had held their voices prisoner; witch-finders scattered before a tide of rats and crows and wolves with old grievances and excellent memories. Somewhere above, Serah Dune was doing whatever Serah Dune did, which lately seemed to involve betraying her own side in the most efficient possible manner.

None of it mattered as much as the tablet.

"Rosalind." Xanthe's voice came out strange in all that dust. "It's here."

She came to stand beside them, ash-streaked, one sleeve torn to ribbons, the sigil-stone at her throat glowing faintly like a coal refusing to die. She looked, Xanthe thought, like every painting of saints he'd ever mocked, and twice as unlikely.

The tablet was taller than either of them, black stone shot through with veins of silver that might once have been writing. Most of it had gone dark. But near the bottom, one line still burned faintly, patient as an ember:

*The covenant is sealed by the Bearer's blood.*

Rosalind read it aloud, and her voice didn't shake, which Xanthe knew cost her something. "I've been reading this line for weeks. In the book. On the quill's annotations. It's very insistent."

"It's wrong," Xanthe said.

"It's a prophecy. It's never wrong. That's rather the whole problem with prophecies."

"No." Xanthe turned to face her. His hands were steady, which surprised him. He'd expected terror. What he felt instead was something quieter and far more stubborn. "Prophecies are written by people, Rosalind. People who could only imagine one way for this to end. One bearer bleeding so the other can walk away whole. It's arithmetic. It's cowardice dressed up as destiny."

"And what's your alternative?" Her eyes were bright and wet and furious. "Because I've done the sums too, Xanthe. I've stood in front of that book every night since Highmoor trying to find another answer, and there isn't one. The sigil wants blood. One of us pays so the other lives. So I've decided—"

"I know what you've decided. You've been insufferably noble about it for three days. You keep volunteering for things and then smiling at me like I won't notice."

"You were going to do the same thing!"

"Yes," Xanthe admitted. "But I'm better at hiding it. I've had practice. Hiding things is my entire tragic backstory."

And despite everything — the battle overhead, the dust, the dark line burning on the stone — Rosalind laughed. A real laugh, cracked down the middle, but real. "We are the two worst prophesied heroes in the history of prophecy. We spent a month each secretly planning to die for the other and neither of us noticed."

"I noticed," said a dry voice from the doorway.

Ashfall stood framed in the shattered doors, and behind him, witch-finders with crossbows, and behind them, impossibly, silence — because even the freed beasts had stopped, drawn to the hall like iron to a lodestone. The Lord-Regent looked less like a man and more like a man-shaped absence, all black coat and colder eyes.

"You are both so predictable it borders on the devotional," Ashfall said. He walked forward unhurried, boots printing the dust. "One of you dies tonight. That was always the shape of it. I merely intended to choose which, and when, and to make certain the story ended in a way no one could use against me." He gestured at the tablet. "Do you know why I stole this? Not for power. Power is simple. I took it because a living covenant means shared authority — human and beast, bound willingly. And *willingly*, children, is the word that has ruined every ruler since the beginning of time. Willingness cannot be commanded. So I broke the covenant instead. I silenced the beasts. I outlawed the gift. I wrote myself a quiet kingdom."

"You forged the histories," Rosalind said.

"I *edited* them. History is only prophecy that already happened. And now—" his gaze settled on the knife lying on the altar before the tablet, an old thing of bone and silver, "—the prophecy will be fulfilled on my terms. One death. Sealed. Finished. No covenant, no shared power, no more inconvenient willingness. Ever."

Xanthe looked at the knife. Then at Rosalind. Then at the tablet's burning line.

And understood.

Not a plan, exactly. Something older than plans — the same thing he felt when a rat told him where the bread was, when a wolf named her price, when Rosalind first lingered at his charm-stall buying luck she probably didn't need. Every bargain he'd ever struck had worked the same way. Not payment. *Exchange.* Two parties, both giving, both gaining. Nobody bled alone.

"Rosalind," he said softly. "Give me your hand."

### Rosalind

She gave him her hand. Of course she did. She'd been giving him her hand since a night market in Greyhollow, since a luck-charm that had actually worked, since a sewer lit by rat-eyes and the moment a boy she'd known for two days spoke to the darkness and the darkness answered kindly.

She thought he meant to hold it while he took the knife himself. She braced to fight him for it.

Instead he drew his own belt-knife — small, honest, the one he used to carve charms — and pressed his palm to hers, fingers laced, skin to skin.

"Xanthe, what—"

"The prophecy says the covenant is sealed by the Bearer's blood." His eyes were calm and absolutely alight. "It doesn't say *whose*. It doesn't say how much. It doesn't say we can't share. Prophecies are contracts, Rosalind, and I have spent my whole life reading the fine print of contracts. This one has a loophole wide enough to drive a caravan through."

"That's not a loophole, that's madness—"

"It's a bargain," he said. "The only kind I've ever made. Both parties give. Both parties gain. Nobody bleeds alone."

Above them, Ashfall's voice cracked like a whip: "Stop them."

Crossbows leveled. And the hall answered.

Crows poured through the high broken windows like spilled ink. Rats flooded from the walls in silver rivers. Wolves filled the doorways shoulder to shoulder, and among them — Rosalind saw her and nearly wept — the wolf queen of Vellamare, scarred and golden-eyed, holding the line without being asked. The beasts did not attack. They simply *surrounded*, a living wall between the crossbows and the altar, every eye fixed on the two figures with joined hands.

"They're waiting," Rosalind breathed. "They know. They've always known what the covenant was supposed to be."

"Then let's stop keeping them waiting." Xanthe lifted the bone-handled knife from the altar and set its edge against their joined palms. "Together. On three. Or whenever you're ready. I'm flexible. I've been told I'm very accommodating for a heretic."

"If we die—"

"We won't. And if we do, we'll be extremely annoying about it in the afterlife. Now."

He cut. She cut. One stroke, both palms, and their blood ran mingled down their laced fingers and dripped onto the black stone of the tablet —

— and the tablet *woke*.

Silver veins blazed from dark to brilliant, racing up the stone like dawn crossing a valley. The dead letters rekindled. Words that had been silent for a century began to move, rearranging themselves, and Rosalind felt the quill of truth grow hot inside her coat, straining toward the tablet like a hound toward home.

She pulled it free. It leapt from her hand.

The quill struck the tablet's surface and began to write by itself, scratching out the old line in showers of silver sparks:

*The covenant is sealed by the Bearer's blood—*

—and then, letter by letter, rewriting:

*—of BOTH bearers, given TOGETHER, bound WILLINGLY. Two hands. One bond. Neither sacrificed. Both chosen.*

The words sank into the stone and the stone drank them, and light went up through the floor, through the foundations, through the roots of the mountain itself. Rosalind heard it leave the hall — a sound like a bell the size of the sky, rolling outward across the borderlands, across the free cities, across every cage and chain and silencing sigil the usurper had ever built.

Every ward built on the old broken covenant failed at once.

She felt it happen in her own chest: the heirloom sigil-stone flaring white-hot and then settling into a warm, steady glow, no longer a relic but a *keystone*, seated at last in an arch that finally stood complete.

Ashfall screamed.

Not with rage — though there was rage in it, oceans of it — but with something worse. Recognition. The sound of a man watching the foundation of his life dissolve. "No. NO. It requires a death! It has ALWAYS required a death! That is the price of order—"

"No," Xanthe said, and his voice carried to every corner of the hall, to every ear, human and otherwise. "That was the price of *your* order. Ours costs less and pays better. Ask them."

He gestured at the beasts.

And they came.

Not the wolves, not first. The smallest things came first. Rats in their hundreds poured past Ashfall's frozen witch-finders and swarmed up the Lord-Regent's boots and coat — not biting, not tearing, just *climbing*, clinging, a thousand small bodies with a thousand small voices, and every one of them speaking at once. Crows dropped from the rafters to his shoulders. Mice from the walls. Moths, beetles, sparrows that had lived their whole lives in the citadel eaves, voiceless and unseen, all of them rising around him like a storm made of witnesses.

And they *spoke.*

*You silenced us,* said the rats. *You wrote us out of the law,* said the crows. *You made us vermin, made us game, made us nothing,* said the sparrows and the mice and the moths, a chorus of small furious voices building and building until the hall rang with it, until Ashfall staggered under the weight of a century of everything he had refused to hear.

"Get OFF me—" He tore at his coat. Witch-finders raised their crossbows and found wolves at their throats and lowered them again, slowly, the way men lower things when they have stopped believing in their own cause. Serah Dune appeared in the doorway with irons in her hand and an expression Rosalind would remember for the rest of her life: not triumphant. Just tired, and finished.

"Lord-Regent Corvus Ashfall," Serah said, "by the authority of the restored covenant and the assembled free cities, you are under arrest. Also, sir — the rats would like a word. Several thousand words, actually. They've been saving up."

The small voices closed over him like water.

### Xanthe

Later — much later, after the irons, after the witch-finders had been marched out under wolf escort, after the sun came up through the broken windows and turned all the dust to drifting gold — Xanthe sat on the steps of the altar with Rosalind's head on his shoulder, both of their palms bandaged with strips of his shirt, because apparently heroes were expected to improvise.

Their hands ached in perfect unison. He found this inexplicably funny and couldn't stop grinning about it.

"Stop laughing," Rosalind murmured, not opening her eyes.

"Our wounds match. We have matching wounds. That's either romantic or deeply unsanitary."

"It's both. Go to sleep, beast-speaker."

Around them, the hall was filling — not with soldiers but with delegates, with wolves curled on broken benches, with rats riding shoulders, with Serah Dune standing awkwardly by the door holding the forged edicts like a bouquet she wasn't sure anyone had asked for. The mosaic of the wolf and the woman shaking hands caught the morning light, and beneath the dust, Xanthe could see now that the artist had painted a third figure between them: small, ordinary, listening.

A speaker. There all along, in the oldest picture of the covenant, waiting a hundred years for someone to notice.

Rosalind stirred. "The book," she said sleepily. "The prophecy book. It's still in my pack. The last page is blank."

Xanthe looked at her. She was smiling with her eyes closed.

"Is it, now," he said.

"Mm. Blank pages are just futures nobody's written yet." She burrowed closer. "Someone should get started."

Outside, the free cities were waking to a world with new law in it, and somewhere in the mountains the wolves were singing, and on the altar steps two people with matching bandaged hands sat in the gold dust and did not let go.

Xanthe leaned his head against hers, and for the first time in his life, hid nothing at all.
