## 20. After the Fire

The citadel's great hall still smelled of smoke and wet ash when Serah Dune walked in with twelve soldiers at her back and a list of names folded in her breast pocket.

She had written the list herself, by candlelight, in the three days after the fall. It was not a long list, as lists of the guilty went — Ashfall had kept his witch-finders lean, the way he kept everything, rationing even cruelty — but every name on it had earned its place through years of diligent, obedient horror.

"Captain Vess," she said, and watched the man flinch. He had been trying to look inconspicuous near the far wall, which for a man of Vess's build was like trying to hide a wardrobe behind a curtain. "Step forward."

He did, because obedience died hard. That, Serah thought, was rather the whole problem.

"You signed the order for the Hollowfield burnings," she said. "Fourteen households. You countersigned the transport manifests for the silencing sigil's installation in the beast-cells. You personally authorized the use of quiet-irons on children." She let that sit. The hall was very quiet; even the soldiers who hadn't known were now looking at Vess the way one looks at a meal that has turned out to contain something unspeakable. "Do you dispute any of it?"

Vess's mouth worked. "I was following edict," he said finally. "Lawful orders. Under the Regency—"

"The Regency is ended," Serah said. "The edicts were forged. And you knew."

That last part landed harder than she'd expected. She saw it move through him like a current through water — not surprise, exactly. Relief, maybe, or its ugly cousin: the hope that if everyone was guilty, no one would be punished properly.

"I have documents," Serah continued. "Originals from the Greyhollow archive, authenticated by the Vellamare college of heralds and by the quill itself — yes, *that* quill, the one that writes only truth, which I understand has been very busy this week. Your signature appears on procurement orders predating the 'discovery' of Speaker crimes by eleven years. You helped build the fire before there was anything to burn." She unfolded her list. "You are under arrest. All of you are. Tribunal trials begin in Highmoor in one month. You will be treated lawfully, fed adequately, and permitted to feel sorry for yourselves in private."

One of the younger witch-finders — a boy named Pell, barely old enough to shave, who had joined the order because it came with boots and a sense of importance — began to cry. Serah found she could not summon contempt for him. Only tiredness, deep as a well.

*We recruited them young,* she thought. *I recruited them young. I told them they were keeping people safe.*

She read out the rest of the names. Nobody ran. Running required believing there was somewhere to run to, and the news from the borderlands made that a difficult proposition: wolves escorting supply carts, crows delivering messages for the free cities' post, rats — reportedly — negotiating grain contracts with granary masters and driving a famously hard bargain. The world had turned over in a fortnight, and the old machinery of fear lay on its side in the road like a cart with a broken axle, wheels still spinning, going nowhere.

* * *

The tribunal convened in Highmoor's moot-hall, which had been built for arguments about fishing rights and tariffs and was therefore, Serah reflected, the perfect venue for the trial of a century. There was something steadying about arguing epochal matters beneath a ceiling painted with trout.

She presented the forged edicts on the third day. She had rehearsed the presentation eleven times, and still her hands shook when she laid the documents on the table — not from fear, but from the strange weight of holding a lie so large that an entire legal order had been built upon it, like a cathedral raised on a foundation of painted cloth.

"Delegates," she said. "I served Lord-Regent Corvus Ashfall for nine years. I believed in the work. I want that stated plainly, because everything I say next will be more useful to you if you understand that I am not a convert telling you what you wish to hear. I was a true believer, and I was wrong, and here is how I know."

She walked them through it. The founding edict of the magic laws, dated forty-one years past, bearing the seal of the old king — except the seal's wax matched a batch manufactured six years later; the Vellamare chandlers' guild kept meticulous records, because chandlers always do. The "historical record" of the Speakers' War, quoted in every schoolbook, traceable to a single manuscript in Ashfall's private collection, in Ashfall's own hand, disguised by an archaic script he had learned — the ink analysis suggested — specifically for the purpose. The quill of truth had annotated a copy of the forgery in front of witnesses, writing across the page in letters of burning gold: *This is false. He wrote it himself. He was proud of it.*

A delegate from Saltmere — a narrow woman with ink-stained fingers and the expression of someone who had audited things — leaned forward. "Captain Dune. You discovered these forgeries while pursuing fugitives. Why did you not report your discovery to your superiors?"

"Because my superior wrote the forgeries," Serah said, and a ripple went through the hall. "And because I did not yet know what else to do with the truth. I hid it. I sat with it for weeks like a stone in my boot. I am not here to claim virtue. I am here to hand you the evidence and let you decide what to do with a woman who enforced unjust laws faithfully, doubted them privately, and acted only when acting became convenient." She paused. "Though I will note, in my own defense, that 'convenient' arrived in the form of an army of small voices at the gates, which is not a convenience I would recommend waiting for twice."

There was laughter — startled, slightly guilty laughter, the kind that escapes a room that has been holding its breath. Serah let it pass. Humor, she had learned, was a door: people would walk through it into rooms their fear had kept locked.

The questioning went on for hours. She answered everything. Yes, she had burned warrants. No, none had resulted in executions — she had slow-walked those files for years, burying them in procedural mud, though two of her prisoners had died in cells regardless, and she carried those names too, added to the list in her own private ledger where she kept the debts she could never repay, only acknowledge. Yes, she had hunted Xanthe Thackeray and Rosalind Vane across three cities with every resource at her command. No, she did not regret being bad at it.

"They outran me," she said, "because they were right and I was wrong, and it turns out that being right is worth approximately four horses and a head start."

* * *

On the fifth day, the tribunal acquitted her.

She had expected it — the delegates had signaled their direction days ago, in the way they stopped asking hostile questions and started asking helpful ones — and still the verdict landed strangely, like a coat handed to her that she wasn't sure fit. Justice, it turned out, felt less like triumph than like being measured for something and finding yourself between sizes.

She gave her closing statement anyway, because she had promised herself she would say it aloud in a room with power in it, whatever the room decided.

"Do not rebuild the witch-finders," she said. "I know the temptation. Something burned down, and the instinct is to rebuild the same house with new timber. But the house was the problem. An order whose purpose is to hunt people for what they *are* will always find its forgeries, its Ashfalls, its comfortable lies — because an order built on fear will believe whatever justifies the fear. If you must have wardens, let them guard against cruelty itself, whoever commits it, human or beast or both. Let them be audited by chandlers' guilds and schoolteachers and, frankly, by rats, who appear to be excellent accountants. Let the law be something that can be embarrassed." She looked around the trout-painted hall. "I am resigning my commission. Not because I think you'll fail. Because I don't yet trust myself to serve without flinching at my own reflection, and a warden who flinches helps no one. Ask me again in a year. I may be worth hiring. I intend to be."

She set her captain's insignia on the table beside the forged edicts, and the sound it made was very small for the size of what it ended.

* * *

Afterward, in the corridor outside the moot-hall, she was ambushed by a delegation she had not anticipated: a rat, a crow, and a wolf, arranged in a row on the flagstones with the patient air of a committee.

Serah had spent nine years learning to read faces, and the last month learning that animals had them too. The wolf's face said *we have been discussing you.* The crow's said *I have already formed an opinion.* The rat's said nothing at all, which was somehow the most unnerving of the three.

"You're the Speaker liaison," Serah said. "Thackeray's colleague. The one with the ledger."

The rat produced, from some inner pocket of reality Serah chose not to examine, a small scroll, and unrolled it with a paw.

"*Item one,*" the crow read aloud, in a voice like a hinge that wanted oiling. "*The Dune woman buried execution files for six years. Credit: substantial. Item two: the Dune woman arrested our kind for speaking. Debt: also substantial. Item three: the Dune woman opened the mountain pass. Credit: enormous. Item four: she resigned instead of taking power, which the wolves consider either wisdom or a trick, pending observation.*"

"We're observing," the wolf said gravely.

"I see," said Serah. "How long does observation typically take?"

"However long it takes," said the wolf, which was the sort of answer that explained why wolf bargains were feared across three kingdoms.

The rat nudged the scroll toward her. At the bottom, beneath the items, was a line in careful claw-marks. The crow translated: "*The beasts request that Captain Dune — former Captain Dune — accept a position as human advocate to the beast-councils. Not a warden. An advocate. Someone who argues our cases in your courts, and yours in ours, and who can be fired by either side when she is wrong.*"

Serah looked at the scroll for a long moment. Somewhere beyond the corridor windows, Highmoor was loud with rebuilding — hammers, argument, someone teaching a flock of starlings to sing a protest song, badly.

"You want to hire me," she said, "to be embarrassable."

"Accountable," said the crow.

"That is what I said," said the wolf.

Serah thought about the stone in her boot, the weeks she had sat with the truth and done nothing, and the list of names in her pocket, and the two names in the other list, the ones no tribunal could reach. She thought about a year from now, and whether she'd be worth hiring then, and decided that the only way to find out was to be hired now and grow into the coat.

"On one condition," she said. "When I'm wrong — and I will be, regularly, possibly before lunch — I want it minuted. In writing. In your ledgers and mine. A justice that can't be embarrassed becomes a forge again. I've seen the forge from inside."

The rat made a note. The crow looked faintly delighted. The wolf, after a pause, extended one enormous paw, and Serah shook it, and her hand disappeared entirely for several seconds, which she understood to be binding.

* * *

That evening she wrote two letters.

The first went to Xanthe Thackeray and Rosalind Vane, care of Greyhollow. It was short. *The trials proceed. Vess talks; most of them talk; guilt is proving less loyal than they hoped. The beasts have hired me, which I suspect was Thackeray's idea, and which I have agreed to, which I suspect was the point. Tell the rats the grain contract arbitration goes to Saltmere next month and I will be watching the granary masters closely. — S.D.*

The second letter she did not send. She kept it in the drawer with her old insignia and the private ledger of names. It was addressed to the two prisoners who had died in her cells, and it said only: *It was not enough, what I did, and it was late, and it was yours more than mine. I am giving the rest of my working life to making the word 'warden' mean something that would not have needed forgiving.*

Then she closed the drawer, trimmed her lamp, and turned to the stack of case-files the beast-councils had delivered — the first docket in history, as far as anyone knew, to include a complaint filed by a hive of bees against a honey magnate, with supporting testimony from the bees themselves, who had apparently been *extremely* thorough.

Outside, the night was full of small voices, all of them talking at once, none of them quiet, and Serah Dune — unemployed, re-employed, and cautiously hopeful — rolled up her sleeves and got to work.
