## 21. The Open Door

The morning the law died in Greyhollow, it did not die with trumpets or proclamations nailed to the guildhall doors. It died the way most old cruelties die — quietly, on a Tuesday, while everyone was busy with other things.

Xanthe learned of it from a pigeon.

*They took down the edict board,* the pigeon reported, strutting along the windowsill of the little shop on Candlemaker's Row that had been a charm-stall and was now, apparently, a school. *A man with a ladder and a very bad hat pried the whole thing off. Someone threw a turnip at him. He deserved it.*

"Did he?" Xanthe said, feeding the pigeon a crust.

*Everyone deserves a turnip sometimes,* the pigeon said philosophically, and flew off to spread the news to less well-informed birds.

Xanthe stood at the window for a long moment after that, crust forgotten in hand. The edict board had hung at the corner of Thimble Lane for as long as anyone could remember — the list of prohibited practices, the schedule of fines, the notice that Speakers would be reported to the witch-finders' office by order of Lord-Regent Ashfall. Children had grown up beneath it the way children grow up beneath weather, never questioning that some skies rain and some skies forbid.

And now a man with a bad hat had pried it off, and someone had thrown a turnip, and that was that.

Xanthe's hands were shaking slightly. They noticed this with detached interest, the way one notices a neighbor's roof losing shingles in a storm — *ah, so that's where I kept all my fear. Apparently it lived in my hands.*

The bell over the shop door rang.

It still startled them, the bell. In the old days the door had stayed shut and latched during teaching hours, and the children had come in ones and twos through the alley, hooded, looking over their shoulders, the way one enters anywhere one is not supposed to be. Now they came in the front. They came loudly. Some of them brought snacks.

"Mistress Thackeray!" announced Pip Wrenfield, aged nine, who could speak to moths and was mortified about it. "Mistress Thackeray, the board's *gone.* My da saw it. He says we're allowed now. He says it in a loud voice in the *street*, which I think means he's either very happy or has gone mad."

"Both are possible," Xanthe said. "In your father's case, likely."

Behind Pip came the others, trickling in with the particular damp, eager energy of children released into a morning that had turned out interesting: Marisol, who heard what earthworms thought about drainage; the twins Odo and Bett, whose gift was shared between them like a coat neither could wear alone — she spoke to crows, he understood them, and neither worked without the other; and last, always last, small Ferren, who crept in as though the repeal might be revoked mid-threshold, and who could talk to anything with feathers and had once, memorably, negotiated a truce between a kestrel and the entire pigeon population of Tanner's Court.

Ferren stopped just inside the door, eyes going to the window, to the empty corner of sky where the edict board used to hang.

"It's really gone," Ferren said, in the voice of someone who has been told a thing many times and only now believes it.

"It's really gone," Xanthe said. "Turnip and all."

* * *

The lesson that morning was listening.

This was, in Xanthe's opinion, the whole of beast-speech boiled down: not words, not grammar, but listening. Anyone could learn to hear a crow say *bread, bread, mine, bread*. The art was hearing what the crow meant underneath — the difference between *mine* as greed and *mine* as fear, between *bread* as hunger and *bread* as an offering, the first word of a friendship.

"Close your eyes," Xanthe told them. "What do you hear?"

"Bett breathing," said Odo.

"I am *not*—"

"You breathe like a bellows, it's a wonder the birds don't flee the city."

"*Children,*" Xanthe said, with the mildness that somehow worked better than sharpness ever had. "Beyond the room."

Silence. Then, slowly, the answers came.

"A dog, three streets off. She's arguing with a cat about a wall."

"The river. It sounds... full? Like it's pleased about something."

"Sparrows," Ferren whispered. "There's a sparrow right outside and it's saying — it's saying *warm, warm, good stone, warm*." Ferren opened their eyes, shining. "It likes the chimney bricks. It's telling its flock about the chimney bricks."

"And what does that tell *you*?" Xanthe asked.

Ferren considered. "That... everything's talking? All the time? About everything?"

"Everything's talking all the time about everything," Xanthe agreed. "Most people just never learned to listen. Which means most people have spent their whole lives standing in the middle of a conversation and calling it silence." They let that sit. "That's what your gift is. Not a curse. Not a crime. An ear, in a world that's been shouting this whole time and wondering why nobody answered."

Marisol raised her hand. She was the cautious one, the one who still checked the street before entering, who flinched at boots on cobblestones. "Mistress Thackeray. Is it — is it true you were scared? When you were our age?"

The room went quiet in the way rooms do when children ask the real question.

"Yes," Xanthe said simply. "Terrified. Every day. I sold charms at the night market — harmless ones, luck-charms, mostly superstition and string — because it let me be near magic without *being* magic, if you follow me. And every time a rat told me gossip or a cat complained about the weather, I looked over my shoulder. For years. I got very good at looking over my shoulder. I could've won prizes."

"What changed?" Odo asked.

Xanthe thought of Rosalind Vane buying a luck-charm and lingering. Thought of a rat screaming a warning across a dinner table. Thought of sewers and wolves and drowned temples and a covenant hall where two people had refused, together, to do the arithmetic the world demanded of them.

"Someone saw me," Xanthe said. "Really saw. And discovered that being seen didn't kill me. Turns out I'd been wrong about that my entire life." They smiled. "You'll want to write that down. It's the only lesson today worth ink."

* * *

After lessons, the giving away.

This was the part Xanthe loved most now, though it had taken some getting used to. The stall on Candlemaker's Row still stood — same scarred counter, same crooked awning — but the sign had changed. ROSALIND AND THE SIGIL, it read, in Rosalind's handwriting, because Rosalind had painted it herself one afternoon and declared the matter closed before Xanthe could object to the presumption of putting their story on a shopfront. Beneath it, smaller: CHARMS, FREE. YES, FREE. NO, WE DON'T WANT YOUR COIN. GIVE IT TO THE BAKER.

The old customers were baffled. That was half the fun.

Old Mistress Corbie, who had bought a luck-charm every month for six years against her husband's gambling habit, arrived with coin already counted out and stood gaping at the new sign like a woman who'd found her church converted to a bakery.

"Free," she said suspiciously.

"Free."

"What's wrong with them?"

"Nothing's wrong with them. We're simply no longer in the business of charging frightened people for hope."

"Hm." Mistress Corbie eyed the display — the little woven knots for safe travel, the river-stones painted with sigils of calm, the bird-feather tokens that were, these days, occasionally *actually blessed by actual birds who had opinions about the recipient*. She selected a knot, then paused. "And the luck? Does it work any better free?"

"Better," Xanthe said solemnly. "Charged charms carry the resentment of the buyer. Free ones carry the goodwill of the maker. Everyone knows this. It's in all the best grimoires."

"Liar," said Mistress Corbie fondly, tucking the knot into her reticule, and left a pastry on the counter anyway, because Greyhollow folk had decided among themselves that if the charm-seller wouldn't take coin, the charm-seller would be fed, and there was nothing Xanthe could do about it except grow rounder and more beloved.

But it wasn't the old customers who mattered. It was the new ones. The ones who came hesitantly, glancing at the empty corner where the edict board had been, as if checking whether the absence might be temporary. The woman who whispered that her son talked to the horses and the horses talked back and she hadn't known whether to be proud or afraid. The man who hadn't spoken above a murmur in twenty years because his gift was thunder-reading and he'd learned young that weather-knowledge in a Speaker's mouth sounded too much like prophecy. The girl, maybe twelve, who stood at the counter turning her cap in her hands and finally said, "I can hear what the dead leaves say. Is that — is that a real gift, or am I just strange?"

"Both," Xanthe said. "The best gifts usually are."

To each of them Xanthe gave a charm, and directions to the school, and the sentence that had become a kind of creed around Candlemaker's Row: *Come listen. Everything's talking.*

* * *

Evening found Xanthe on the shop's back step with a cup of tea and an audience of rats.

The rats of Greyhollow had, since the fall of Ashfall, developed a certain civic pride. They considered themselves veterans. They mentioned the sewers often. Pip, who had heard the stories secondhand, treated them like returning soldiers, and the rats had grown insufferable accordingly.

*The grain tax is repealed too,* reported the eldest rat, a scarred veteran named Sergeant Crumb, settling onto the step with the air of one delivering a state address. *And the new magistrate says the drains will be cleaned monthly. MONTHLY. Under Ashfall we went years.*

"Progress," Xanthe said.

*We bled for those drains,* Sergeant Crumb said darkly. *Well. Not bled. But we were extremely inconvenienced.*

From the rooftops, a crow called down — one of Bett's flock, carrying the evening's news roundup: two free cities had formally recognized the new covenant, a third was debating it, and Serah Dune had testified before the tribunal for eleven hours and reportedly reduced a senior magistrate to tears. *She cried, the magistrate,* the crow added with relish. *Big wet sobs. Into his beard.*

"Good," Xanthe said, meaning all of it.

The tea was cooling. The light was going gold and long across the rooftops, the way it did in autumn when the city smelled of woodsmoke and baking bread and, faintly, of the river telling itself its endless stories. Somewhere below, Marisol was explaining drainage philosophy to a fascinated circle of earthworm enthusiasts. Somewhere above, sparrows argued about chimney bricks. The door of the shop stood open behind Xanthe — actually open, unlocked, unbarred, because doors in Greyhollow did that now.

For twenty years Xanthe had kept every door shut and every window latched and every shoulder angled toward the sound of boots. It occurred to them now, sitting on the step with tea and rats and the whole chattering city, that they had never once, in all that time, sat with their back to the street.

They tried it. It felt like taking off a coat they'd forgotten they were wearing.

*You're smiling,* observed Sergeant Crumb. *It's unsettling.*

"I'm healing," Xanthe said.

*Is that what it looks like?*

"Shut up, Sergeant."

*Make me. Oh — wait.* The rat's ears pricked. *One more item for the evening report. There's a stranger asking after you at the corner of Thimble Lane. Well-dressed. Red hair. Carrying a book.*

Xanthe's heart performed a small, familiar somersault, twenty years of fear and one year of joy colliding and joy winning by a mile. "Describe the book."

*Old. Leather. There's a sigil stamped on the cover, and — hold on, she's showing it to the baker now, everyone's very excited — pages. Blank pages. Lots of blank pages at the end.*

Xanthe set down the tea.

Rosalind, coming up Candlemaker's Row in the gold light with the prophecy book under her arm and a look on her face that meant she'd had an idea — that specific, luminous, slightly dangerous look that had started all of this, one year ago tonight, over a luck-charm and a lingering — and Xanthe rose, brushing crumbs from their knees, and went to meet her in the open doorway, unhurried, unhidden, back to no one.

"Well?" Xanthe called. "What does it say?"

Rosalind grinned and held up the quill. "That's rather the question, isn't it? I think it wants us to find out."
