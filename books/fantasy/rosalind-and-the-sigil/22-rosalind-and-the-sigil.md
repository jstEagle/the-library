## 22. Rosalind and the Sigil

The prophecy book lay open on the kitchen table of the little house above the charm-stall, and Rosalind Vane had a pen in her hand and no idea what to write.

She had been sitting there for the better part of an hour. The afternoon light came through the window in long amber bars, catching the dust motes and the steam rising off her untouched tea, and the book sat before her like a patient old animal — which, in a way, it was. Its leather cover had softened over the centuries until it felt less like binding and more like hide. Its pages smelled of cedar and smoke and, faintly, of her grandmother's hands.

A century ago, some covenant-keeper had written Rosalind's name in this book. Had written *tonight's date*, inked in blood-red beside a line about a Sigil-Bearer walking into Greyhollow's night market to buy a luck-charm from a stranger with kind eyes and something hidden behind them. She had spent a year of her life being written about — hunted because of it, nearly drowned because of it, nearly *killed* because of it, because of a sentence set down by someone who never met her and never asked her leave.

And now she held the pen.

"You're scowling at it," Xanthe said from the doorway. "It can feel that, you know. Books are sensitive."

"Books are not sensitive."

"This one absolutely is. It sulked for three days after we left it in Vellamare. I heard it."

"You did not hear it. You heard a mouse in the wall and decided the mouse was delivering literary criticism."

"The mouse was very well-informed," Xanthe said, coming into the kitchen. They were still dressed for the market — sleeves rolled, apron off but the chalk dust of prices-not-yet-written on their knuckles — and they leaned over her shoulder to look at the blank page, close enough that she could smell the rosemary oil they used on their hair and feel the warmth of them along her arm. A year now, and that warmth still landed in her chest like a struck bell. "What's the trouble? It's only a page."

"It's only a *page*," Rosalind repeated. "Xanthe. Every word anyone has ever written in this book has come true. Mostly terribly. There is a page in here that describes my grandmother's funeral in detail she could not possibly have known in advance, down to the fact that the priest would sneeze during the eulogy. He did. Twice." She turned the pen over in her fingers. "If I write in it, I'm not writing. I'm *decreeing*. What if I write something wrong? What if I write something right in the worst possible way?"

Xanthe was quiet for a moment. Then they pulled out the chair across from her, sat down backward on it with their arms folded over the backrest, and looked at her with the particular patience they reserved for frightened animals and Rosalind on the subject of destiny.

"May I tell you what I think?" they said. "You don't have to agree. You're allowed to disagree. That's rather the point of you."

"That's the most romantic thing you've ever said to me."

"I know. Listen." They tapped the open book gently, between two columns of faded script. "This book told everyone who read it that one of us had to die. It said so plainly, in its own ink, in its own hand, and every clever person who studied it agreed, because books are supposed to be true and prophecies especially so. And we cut our palms together over a tablet older than any of these pages, and the quill rewrote the ending, and the world *let us*." Their voice went soft at the edges. "The book isn't a cage, Roz. It never was. It's a conversation. Someone wrote their part; we wrote ours. The only rule I've learned this year is that the future is unwritten until somebody chooses it — and even then, somebody else can choose again."

Rosalind looked at them for a long moment. "When did you become wise?"

"I've always been wise. I was hiding it behind the rat gossip."

"You were hiding it behind *extortionate pricing on love charms*."

"They were fairly priced," Xanthe said with dignity, "and half of them worked, which is a better rate than most priests manage."

Rosalind laughed — the real laugh, the one that had taken months to come back after the citadel, after the cage of quiet, after all of it — and dipped the pen.

* * *

She wrote slowly.

Not because the words were hard to find, but because she wanted to be honest with each one, the way the quill of truth had taught her — that relic still resting in its case beneath the floorboards, still warm to the touch if you pressed your palm flat against the wood, as though it dozed there, dreaming in true sentences. She didn't need the quill tonight. She needed only to mean it.

*On this night, one year past,* she wrote, *the night market of Greyhollow smelled of fried onions and wet wool, and a woman named Rosalind Vane bought a luck-charm she did not need from a stranger she did not yet know.*

She paused. Across the table, Xanthe had gone very still, watching her write, the way they watched wild things come to a hand held out — hopeful and braced for flight at once.

*I did not need the charm,* she continued. *I needed the stranger.*

The ink caught the lamplight. Somewhere in the walls, faintly, a mouse moved — and Rosalind would have sworn, then and forever after, that it paused to listen.

*Here is what the old pages got wrong, or perhaps only incomplete: they thought a story could end. They thought a covenant was a thing you sealed once, in blood, and then it simply held, like a door nailed shut. But doors want opening. Covenants want tending. Love wants choosing, daily, freely, or it is none of those things.*

*So let this page say what no page before it dared: the story is not finished. It is continued. Tomorrow is not decreed. Tomorrow is invited.*

She hesitated over the last line, then wrote it small, almost shyly, at the bottom of the page:

*Rosalind Vane, who chose. And Xanthe Thackeray, who was chosen, and chose back.*

She set down the pen. Nothing thundered. No sigil flared, no wolf howled at the window, no quill flew up out of its case to annotate her grammar. The book simply lay there, accepting the new page the way the earth accepts rain — without argument, as though it had been waiting a hundred years for someone to pick up the pen and stop being afraid of it.

"Well," Xanthe said thickly. Rosalind looked up to find them blinking hard at the ceiling. "I'm not crying. There's chalk dust."

"There is no chalk dust within six feet of you."

"There's *ambient* chalk dust. It travels."

Rosalind reached across the table and took their hand — the left one, the one with the thin white scar across the palm where the knife had kissed it, twin to her own. She pressed her scarred palm to theirs, an old habit now, a greeting, a vow renewed in miniature.

"Happy anniversary," she said.

"Happy anniversary," Xanthe said. Then their stomach growled, loudly enough that the mouse in the wall went silent in alarm, and the solemnity of the moment collapsed entirely. "Also, happy anniversary dinner, because I have been smelling Pip Wrennage's onion cart since sundown and I am holding on by a thread."

* * *

The night market of Greyhollow had changed in a year, and hadn't changed at all, which was the best thing about it.

The same crooked lanes strung with lanterns. The same smell of onions and cardamom and river-mud and frying dough. The same fortune-teller on the corner who had once, Rosalind now knew, been quietly reporting the comings and goings of Speakers to the witch-finders out of fear, and who now paid her debt to the neighborhood by teaching letters to anyone who'd sit still long enough — beast-children and human children alike, crammed together on the same bench, arguing over the alphabet.

But there were new things too. A stall where a badger sold honey and took payment in songs. A knot of children chasing a fox through the crowd while the fox's mother watched with tolerant amusement from atop a barrel. The old edict-posts, where Ashfall's seal had once hung, now papered over with playbills and lost-cat notices and, on one of them, a child's charcoal drawing of a wolf shaking hands with a man, both of them grinning in a way that suggested the artist had never actually seen either species smile.

Nobody looked twice at Xanthe anymore when they stopped to murmur with the market cats. Nobody crossed the street when Rosalind laughed too loud at whatever the cats had said. Once, that would have been a death sentence, spoken aloud in public. Now it was Tuesday.

"Pip!" Xanthe called ahead. "Two of the usual, and don't skimp the onions, I'll know."

Pip Wrennage, a barrel-shaped man with forearms like hams and a heart like a cathedral, looked up from his cart and beamed. "The famous couple! Two of the usual, extra onions, and none of your coin neither — you paid me back a year of luck-charms, Thackeray, and I've decided the debt runs lifelong."

"We gave away *three* charms."

"Three charms that made my wife laugh for the first time after her sister died. Lifelong debt." He thrust two paper cones of fried onions at them with the finality of a man closing negotiations. "Argue with me and I'll add a fourth."

"Don't argue with him," Rosalind advised. "He's done this to the baker, the fishmonger, and a crow. The crow brings him buttons now. It's very sweet and slightly alarming."

They walked on through the market, unhurried, eating onions out of paper cones like the pair of disreputable romantics they were. A tabby cat fell into step beside Xanthe and delivered what appeared to be a lengthy report; Xanthe nodded gravely, scratched its ears, and translated for Rosalind in a low voice: "The miller's boy has been leaving fish scraps for the gulls again, which is sweet, but the gulls have formed opinions about him and are planning a demonstration."

"Gulls can't demonstrate."

"These ones have a banner. Don't ask me how. I've learned not to ask."

Rosalind was still laughing when they reached the bridge over the Greywater, and they stopped there, as they always did, leaning on the old stone rail to watch the lantern-light shiver on the black water. Below them, the city breathed. Above them, the first stars were coming out, and somewhere in the dark beyond the rooftops, a wolf sang — not a warning, not a summons, just a song, the way wolves sang now, because they could, because someone had finally listened.

"A year ago tonight," Rosalind said, "you sold me a luck-charm."

"I sold you an excellent luck-charm."

"You told me it would bring me 'a change of fortune.'"

"And it did! Look at you. Fortune: changed." Xanthe grinned, and then the grin gentled into something truer. "For the record — I knew the moment you handed me your coin. Not the prophecy part. I didn't learn that till later. I just knew you were going to matter. You had the look of someone carrying something heavy and pretending it was nothing. I've spent my whole life around creatures pretending things were nothing. Rats are terrible at it. So are you, for the record."

"I'm excellent at it."

"You cried at a squirrel last week."

"He was *brave*, Xanthe. He faced down that hawk knowing he couldn't win."

"Yes," Xanthe said softly. "That's rather what I meant."

Rosalind leaned her head against their shoulder. The river murmured below. The market hummed behind them. In her pocket, the heirloom sigil-stone — her grandmother's legacy, the first relic, the keystone of everything — rested warm against her hip, quiet now, its great work done, content to be jewelry again.

"Do you ever think about what comes next?" she asked. "Really next. Not tomorrow-next. Year-next. Ten-year-next."

"All the time." Xanthe's hand found hers, scarred palm to scarred palm. "I think about a bigger schoolroom. I think about traveling to the other free cities, teaching anyone who wants it. I think about whether gull demonstrations will escalate. I think about growing old with someone who cries at brave squirrels, and I think — honestly, Roz, more than anything — I think I'd like to find out what happens next instead of reading about it first." They squeezed her hand. "Is that all right? A future nobody prophesied?"

Rosalind lifted her head and looked at them — at this person the century-old book had sent her to, or whom she had stumbled into on her own, depending on which page you believed — and felt the answer rise in her the way the tide rises, inevitable and glad.

"It's the only kind I want," she said.

They kissed on the bridge, unhurried, unhidden, while the market cats tactfully looked elsewhere and the gulls, far off in the dark, rehearsed.

And in the little house above the charm-stall, on the kitchen table, the prophecy book lay open to its newest page, the ink long dried, waiting — patiently, the way all good stories wait — for whoever would pick up the pen next.
