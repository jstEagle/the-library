## 1. The Ledger of Small Impossibilities

The candle had burned down to a stub the color of old teeth before Kestrel Crane found the error in the accounts.

He sat in the back room of the Guildhall of Impossible Things, where the ceiling sagged like a tired shoulder and the shelves leaned together as if sharing secrets. Three ledgers lay open before him: one for coin, one for favors owed and repaid, and one—the third, bound in grey calfskin with no title on its spine—for everything else. The guild dealt in three currencies, Odile Voss liked to say. Gold was the least interesting of them.

Kestrel dipped his pen and corrected the entry. *One memory, medium: river-water and lamplight, sold to Mrs. Ansel for her dying husband. Paid: two crowns and a jar of plum preserves.* The preserves were not in the ledger. He added them under *miscellaneous*, because Odile would notice if the pantry numbers didn't reconcile, and Odile noticed everything except, he hoped, him.

That was the arrangement. That was the whole architecture of his life, balanced as carefully as these books: Kestrel Crane, bone-carver, second-rate appraiser, a man of no particular gifts who happened to sit in the back room and keep the guild's papers. Nobody looked twice at a clerk. Clerks were furniture that could write.

In his left hand, half-hidden beneath the ledger's edge, he held a disc of whalebone the size of a saucer.

"Again," said the girl. "From *again*."

Her voice came from everywhere and nowhere, the way voices always did when they lived in bone—not in his ears but behind them, in the soft wet architecture of the skull. She had been dead for six days. Her name was Merrin Hale, she was eleven years old, and she had drowned in the millrace below Tallow Bridge while trying to retrieve a hat the wind had stolen. The hat was red. This mattered to her enormously. It was the first thing she'd told him, and the last thing she would ever tell anyone, and so it would be the first thing carved into the bone.

Kestrel set down his pen and picked up his graver.

"I'm listening," he said, though he had been listening all evening, and though the words cost him nothing to say and everything to hear.

*It was red,* Merrin said. *Not just red—red like the wax on Father's letters, red like when you press your thumb too hard on your own arm. Mother made it from dyed wool she traded three eggs for, and I wore it even when it wasn't cold, even indoors, even at supper, and she pretended to be cross about it but she wasn't, I could tell she wasn't.*

The graver moved. Kestrel did not think about what he was carving; thinking ruined it. His hands knew the work the way a river knows its bed. Fine lines fanned out across the whalebone's face—a hat, small and tilted, caught mid-flight above brown water—and around it, in spirals tight as fern-fronds, the texture of wool, the weight of wind, the particular quality of a mother's feigned annoyance. Anyone who looked at the finished piece would see a pretty trinket. Only someone with the gift—or someone standing very close to the moment of Kestrel's death, which amounted to the same thing—would *hear* it.

That was the trade. That was the impossible thing the guild sold, quietly, without signage or advertisement: memories cut into bone, legible only to the dead and the nearly-dead. Grief-stricken families bought them for the dying, so that a grandmother could carry her wedding day into whatever came next. The condemned bought them in place of last rites. Once, years ago, a man had bought one for himself, healthy as an ox, because he said he wanted to know in advance what it felt like to be remembered properly. Kestrel still didn't know if that was wisdom or madness, and the guild's ledgers, characteristically, did not distinguish.

"Slower," Merrin said. "You're going past the part with the dog."

"There's a dog?"

*There's a DOG. Biscuit. He chased the hat first, that's why I went after it, because Biscuit got it in his mouth and dropped it in the water and looked SO proud of himself—*

Kestrel laughed despite himself, a short startled sound in the empty room, and reworked the spiral to admit a small scruffy shape, ears back, triumphant over catastrophe. The dead were supposed to be solemn. In Kestrel's experience they were nothing of the kind. They were chatty, petty, funny, obsessed with hats and dogs and debts and the exact wording of arguments they'd lost twenty years earlier. Death, as far as he could tell, edited nothing. It only removed the ability to leave.

That was why he did this. He'd stopped pretending otherwise long ago, even in the privacy of his own skull, where honesty was unavoidable. Coin kept the roof up; the guild gave the work a shape and a price and a rulebook. But the truth was simpler and stranger: every voice trapped in ivory and bone spoke to him whether he wanted it or not, a constant low murmur underneath the world, and carving was the only way he knew to make the murmuring mean something. To give the dead somewhere to go that wasn't just *inside him*, accumulating, until he drowned in other people's last words the way Merrin had drowned in cold brown water.

A bone-carver who couldn't carve was just a man haunted. Kestrel had been both. He preferred this.

*Tell me when we get to the end,* Merrin said, quieter now. *I want to know how it sounds.*

"It will sound like you," Kestrel said. "I promise."

Outside, Greyharbor was settling into its evening noises: cart wheels in the wet cobbles, gulls arguing over the fish-market leavings, the foghorn on the breakwater moaning its one long note like a bored god. Somewhere below, in the guildhall proper, Odile Voss was holding court over the evening's petitioners—a farmer who wanted rain remembered, a widow who wanted nothing remembered at all, a merchant with something wrapped in oilcloth that he claimed was "just a curio" and that Kestrel would be asked to appraise later, because everything brought to the Guild of Impossible Things eventually crossed the back-room desk, and everything that crossed the desk eventually touched bone.

Kestrel worked until the candle guttered, then lit another from its corpse and worked more. The hat took shape. The dog took shape. The millrace unspooled in white lines, and the bridge arched over it, and beneath the bridge—here his hand slowed—he began the figure of a girl, arms up, hair fanning, already becoming part of the water that would become part of the bone that would become, if he did his work well, the closest thing to a resurrection the world allowed.

*That's me,* Merrin said, when he reached it. Not frightened. Curious, almost. Children were often like that at the end; they hadn't yet learned that death was supposed to be unspeakable, so they spoke of it.

"That's you."

*I look smaller than I thought I'd look.*

"You look exactly the right size."

She was quiet awhile. The graver whispered. Downstairs, the last petitioner left, and Odile's boots climbed the stairs with their familiar uneven rhythm—left foot firm, right foot favoring the old wound she never discussed—and paused outside his door without knocking, which was Odile's way of knocking.

"You're still at it," she said through the wood. "The Hale girl."

"She has a lot to say."

"They always do." A pause. "The merchant's package is on my desk. He wants it read by morning and he's paying in silver, which means it's either worthless or dangerous."

"Which do you hope?"

"Neither. I hope it's boring. Boring keeps us alive." Her boots shifted. He could picture her exactly: lean and grey as a winter heron, sixty-odd years of secrets folded into a coat too big for her, eyes that catalogued exits the way other people catalogued faces. "Finish the girl. Then sleep. And Kestrel—" the pause stretched, and something in it made him set down the graver, "—lock the third ledger in the strongbox tonight. All of it. Names and all."

"The ledger's coded—"

"Codes are for honest men's enemies. Lock it anyway." Her boots moved on, unhurried, and Kestrel sat looking at the door for longer than the instruction warranted.

Odile Voss had founded the guild's charter herself, thirty years ago, back when trading in the impossible was merely eccentric rather than criminal. Three rules, carved into the lintel over the guildhall door where every member walked beneath them daily:

*First: the dead speak freely or not at all.*
*Second: no memory carved shall serve a living master's ambition.*
*Third: no name given in trust shall pass these walls.*

The third rule was the reason the guild survived. Kings and magistrates and, lately, certain very patient men in grey coats had all come asking for lists—who could hear the dead, who could read dreams, who could pull a confession out of a murder victim's knucklebone—and the guild had always answered with the same polite shrug. We keep accounts, gentlemen. We don't keep names.

Mostly true. Entirely necessary. And lately, Kestrel had noticed, increasingly tested. The petitions had changed in flavor over the past year. More soldiers' widows. More families of the imprisoned, wanting last words smuggled out through bone because paper could be read by the wrong eyes. And once, three months ago, a woman who paid triple rate to have her son's voice carved and then asked, casually, whether the guild also bought bones that already had voices in them. Old ones. Royal ones.

Odile had declined the sale so smoothly the woman probably never realized she'd been refused. But Kestrel had heard the bones in the vault shift that night, restless as roosting birds, and he had not slept well since.

He finished Merrin's hat. He finished her dog, her bridge, her water, her small determined figure rising through all of it. Last of all, in the bone's inner ring, where only the dying would think to listen, he carved the end itself: the cold, the green light tilting overhead, the strange floating calm, and then—because she insisted, because it was hers to insist—the very last words, dictated twice so he got them exact:

*Mother, I tried. Tell Biscuit he's a good boy anyway.*

Kestrel set down the graver and pressed his thumb flat against the finished disc, and for one moment the room filled with red wool and river-cold and a dog's ridiculous pride, and then it faded, sealed, done. A life, filed. He entered it in the third ledger: *One memory, complete. Medium: whalebone. Commissioned by J. Hale, Tallow Bridge. Fee waived.*

Fee waived was against guild policy. He wrote it anyway. Some entries he simply refused to balance.

He locked the ledgers in the strongbox as instructed—all three, including the grey one with its cipher of names—and blew out the candle, and stood a moment in the dark listening to the house settle. From the shelf by the window, the guild's stock of raw material watched him with the patience of the mineral kingdom: walrus tusk, elk antler, a coil of elephant ivory older than the city, and one small box, locked, that he did not open and did not need to, because even closed it murmured to him like a sleeping child talking in its sleep. Every carver had such a box. Every carver pretended theirs was silent.

Kestrel Crane was twenty-six years old, and if you had asked him that night whether he was happy, he would have said the question was badly formed. Happiness was for people whose lives weren't built on concealment. What he had was better than happiness: he had work that mattered, a roof that held, a mentor who trusted him exactly as far as trust could safely extend, and a gift worn so lightly no one had ever once suspected it. He was furniture that could write. He was invisible, and the invisibility fit him like the red hat had fit Merrin—worn even indoors, even at supper, loved precisely because it never came off.

He climbed the narrow stair to his room, undressed in the dark, and was nearly asleep when the bells started.

Not the hour bells. Not the alarm bells either, which rang fast and jagged. These rang slow, spaced, deliberate—the pattern used only for royal proclamation, and only ever at night when the crown wanted attention before the criers could reach the squares. Kestrel lay still and counted. Twelve strokes. A pause. Twelve again.

Downstairs, faintly, Odile's door opened.

From the strongbox in the back room—from inside the locked grey ledger, from inside the cipher, from inside the hundred names it held—Kestrel would have sworn he heard something stir. Not a voice. Not yet. Just the sound a page makes when a draft moves through a closed room, turning toward what comes next.

He got up and dressed, and did not bother with the candle.
