## 2. A Proclamation in Ash

The bell began at dawn.

Kestrel heard it first through the whalebone on his bench—a thin vibration running up through the trestle and into his palms before the sound itself reached his ears, the way he felt most things first: through the dead things that had once lived in water or earth or air. He set down his graver and listened with both ears, the living one and the other, the one that never stopped listening whether he wanted it to or not.

Not the guild bell. The guild bell was bronze and sweet, tuned by a founder who had believed impossible things deserved beautiful instruments. This was iron, cracked-voiced, tolling from the direction of the market square in a rhythm Kestrel had never heard in nine years beneath Odile Voss's roof.

Then came the criers.

Their voices carried over the rooftops of Saltmere Row in ragged relay, hoarse men on hired horses reading from parchment they clearly did not understand, and the words arrived at the guildhall window like birds flung against glass:

*"By order of His Majesty the King, let it be known throughout the realm: all persons possessed of unlawful arts—speakers to the dead, readers of flesh and bone, dream-walkers, ash-callers, and every other practitioner of the forbidden gifts—shall present themselves for registration within thirty days, upon pain of—"*

A pause. The crier's voice wavered, steadied.

*"—upon pain of fire."*

Kestrel stood very still in the workroom. Dust motes turned in the gray light. On the bench before him, the half-finished carving of the drowned girl looked up with eyes no bigger than peppercorns, her small mouth open around a word she had been dictating to him for three days and had not yet finished.

*They're coming,* she whispered from the bone. *I can feel them coming. Everyone always comes.*

"Hush," Kestrel said, out of habit, though the dead girl was right. He could feel it too—not in the bone but in the floorboards, in the way the whole city seemed to have stopped breathing. Doors closing. Shutters drawing. Somewhere a dog barking itself hoarse and being kicked silent.

He went downstairs.

* * *

The guildhall of the Guild of Impossible Things occupied three narrow houses knocked together behind a chandler's shop, its entrance marked by nothing but a brass knocker shaped like a question mark worn smooth by two centuries of frightened hands. Inside, the rules were painted on the stairwell wall in letters old Marta Crane—no relation, though she'd claimed him anyway—had mixed herself from lampblack and egg:

**HEAR ALL. REPEAT NOTHING.**
**CARVE THE DEAD. PROTECT THE LIVING.**
**BE NO ONE.**

Kestrel had read those rules every morning for nine years, and every morning *be no one* had sat easiest in him. He was good at being no one. He had a face people forgot, a name common as dirt, a walk that made him furniture. He kept the ledgers under three false names and carved commissions under four more. When clients came to have their dead mother's last words pressed into a locket-bone, they saw a clerk, a scrivener, a man who smelled faintly of whale oil and apologized too much.

Now he took the stairs two at a time and found the great room already filling.

They came in wet from the fog off the harbor: Fen the ash-speaker, whose hands shook so badly he'd taken to wrapping them in wool; the twins Ilse and Maren, dream-readers who finished each other's sentences and each other's nightmares; old Brother Cassian, defrocked and deaf in one ear, who could taste lies on parchment; a dozen others, faces Kestrel knew from ledger entries rather than conversation, because the guild's first rule was that you did not ask. You paid your tithe, you left your name in the book if you dared, and you trusted the guildmaster to keep the walls standing.

Odile Voss stood at the head of the long table with the proclamation unrolled before her, and Kestrel watched her read it twice.

She was not a large woman. She had never needed to be. Sixty years of trading in impossible things had given her a stillness that made rooms rearrange themselves around her, and her eyes—the color of old pewter—did the work of an army. But Kestrel, who spent his life reading what was written in things rather than on them, saw her thumb press white against the edge of the parchment. Saw the pulse jump in her throat like a bird trapped indoors.

"Thirty days," she said. Her voice filled the room without rising. "Registration means the collar. Those of you who've come up from the south know what the collars do." Silence answered her; everyone knew. "Refusal means the pyre. And hiding—" she let the word hang, "—hiding means they hunt you, and hunted gifted are burned faster than registered ones. That is the arithmetic we are given."

"And what do we give back?" Fen asked. His wool-wrapped hands were fists now. "Do we run?"

"You do nothing," Odile said. "You go home. You open your shops. You carve your charms and read your dreams exactly as quietly as you always have, and you let me do what I am paid to do, which is to make this guild impossible to find." Something moved behind her face then, quick as a fish under ice, and Kestrel would spend the rest of the day trying to decide whether it had been fear or calculation. "Go home. All of you. Kestrel—stay."

They filed out past him without meeting his eyes. Fear did that; it made everyone no one.

When the last of them had gone, Odile rolled up the proclamation with hands that did not shake at all anymore, and looked at him across forty feet of dark wood.

"Bring the ledger," she said.

* * *

The Ledger of Small Impossibilities lived in a box under the floor of Kestrel's workroom, wrapped in oilcloth, bound in calfskin that had once belonged to a calf and nothing else—he'd checked, the first week, because you checked everything when your gift meant everything spoke. Nine years of names. Not just names: gifts, habits, haunts, debts, fears. Who could hear the dead and who could only smell them. Who drank. Who had children. Who would break under questioning and who would not.

It was, Kestrel had always understood, the most dangerous object in the city. He had also understood, with the simple faith of a man who has found one safe harbor, that Odile Voss would die before letting anyone else touch it.

She built the fire herself, in the workroom hearth, while he held the ledger and did not understand.

"Page by page," she said. "We burn the middle years. Everything since the southern registrations started. Give it here."

"Odile." He had never called her anything else, though guild law said *guildmaster*, though everyone else said *guildmaster*. She had told him, the day she took him in off the mudflats with a stolen whalebone under his arm and voices pouring out of him like blood, that a boy who heard the dead should learn to speak plainly to the living. "These are people. These are—Maren's nightmares are in here. Fen's sister's grave is in here. If the Inquisition takes the hall—"

"They will not take the hall," Odile said, "because there will be nothing in it to take." She held out her hand. "The front years stay. The founding entries, the old trade routes, the dead languages—we need those to be a guild at all. But the recent names, the living names, the names that would hang thirty people in thirty days—" Her pewter eyes held his. "Those burn."

And Kestrel, who had balanced these accounts for nine years, who knew every entry the way a priest knows his psalms, opened the ledger to the middle pages and felt the full weight of what she was asking.

Because his own name was there.

Not *Kestrel Crane*—he had never been fool enough to write that. But entry forty-one, year six: **the bone-hearer, male, young, keeps accounts, lodges above the workroom, gift total (dead speech through worked ivory and bone), risk extreme, exposure catastrophic.** He had written it himself, at her instruction, in the cipher she'd taught him, because she'd said the guild must know its own assets even when those assets could not be known. He had thought it meant protection. A shepherd counting sheep so the wolves couldn't.

He had never once thought it meant inventory.

"Odile," he said again, and his voice came out wrong, smaller. "My page."

Her hand did not move. The fire popped. Down in the harbor a ship's bell rang the hour, indifferent, and somewhere far off another iron bell had begun its cracked tolling in a second town, and a third.

"Your page burns with the rest," she said.

"It's ciphered. No one but us could—"

"No one but us *yet*." For the first time something cracked in her voice, hairline-thin. "Kestrel. Listen to me as a ledger-keeper listens. The Inquisition does not knock politely. They take the hall, they take the books, they take the bones—and they take whoever knows how to read them. Do you understand? It isn't the page that endangers you. It's your *head*. Every name in this book lives in your skull, and skulls, my dear boy, can be opened." She finally crossed the room and put her hand over his on the ledger, and her fingers were cold as the whalebone he carved. "So we burn the map. And you become no one so completely that even I could not find you. That is the only mercy left in the arithmetic."

He wanted to believe her. God, he wanted it the way the drowned girl wanted to finish her sentence. He looked down at the pages—Fen's shaking hands, the twins' shared dreams, Brother Cassian's parchment-tongue—and he thought: *she is saving them.* And underneath, quieter, in the voice he usually reserved for the dead: *she is saving the guild, and you are the part of the guild that is cheapest to lose.*

Because that was the thing about being no one. It meant there was no one to bargain for.

He tore out the pages himself. It seemed important, afterward, that he had done it with his own hands—that he had fed the middle years into the hearth one leaf at a time while Odile stood watch at the window, and watched forty-one rise up in flame, the cipher curling black, *risk extreme, exposure catastrophic* glowing orange for one heartbeat like a brand before it went to ash.

The heat breathed against his face. Behind him, on the bench, the drowned girl's unfinished carving caught the firelight, and he heard her whisper—not from the bone, he realized, but from memory, from all the hundreds of bones he'd ever held:

*Everyone always comes.*

"Yes," he murmured to her, to all of them. "But some of us get sold first."

"What?" Odile said, without turning.

"Nothing, guildmaster."

* * *

He did not sleep. Near midnight he sat at his bench with a candle stub and tried to carve, because carving was the only prayer he knew, and the whalebone lay cold under his hands and gave him nothing. The dead girl's voice was gone from it—or he was too rattled to hear, which amounted to the same thing. Silence where there had always been company. He was beginning to learn the shape of the world the crown was building: a silence enforced by fire.

Toward the small hours, boots on the stairs. Odile, in her coat, travel-dusted already, carrying a sealed letter and a face he had never seen her wear: the face of a woman doing sums she hated.

"You're up," she said, unsurprised.

"I'm always up. The dead keep poor hours." He turned the whalebone over. "Where does the letter go?"

"South. To friends." She set it on the bench between them, and for a moment neither of them touched it. "Kestrel. What I did tonight—"

"Was arithmetic," he said. "You taught me the guild runs on arithmetic."

Something flickered across her face—wounded, maybe, or maybe only tired. "In thirty days the criers stop and the riders start. Until then, you carve nothing signed, you see no clients, you are a scrivener who smells of whale oil and apologizes too much. Can you do that?"

"I've done it for nine years."

"That's not what I asked."

He looked at her then, really looked, the way he looked at bone: for what was written underneath. And underneath the pewter eyes and the stillness and the cold competent hands, Odile Voss was afraid—not of the Inquisition, he realized. Or not only. There was something else in her, some older debt, some door in her past she'd been keeping locked so long she'd wallpapered over it. He had catalogued enough guilty consciences to know one at close range.

"Guildmaster," he said carefully, "is there something you haven't told me?"

For three full seconds Odile Voss, who had faced down magistrates and smugglers and once, famously, a dragon-cult with poor aim, did not answer. Then she picked up her letter, and smiled the smile she used on clients who asked too many questions, and said:

"Sleep, Kestrel. Whatever comes, the guild stands. That's all any of us need to know."

She went down the stairs and out into the dark, and the question-mark knocker clicked softly behind her.

Kestrel sat alone with his candle and his silent bone, and in the hearth the ashes of forty-one names settled and shifted, and one of them—only one, a curl of calfskin-edged paper that had lodged against the grate and escaped the flame—breathed loose in a draft and drifted to the floor at his feet.

He picked it up. A corner of his own cipher, no bigger than a thumbnail: **...exposure catas...**

He held it a long time. Then he fed it to the candle, watched it burn to nothing, and began, very quietly, to plan how a no one might survive what was coming.

Far to the east, past the harbor bar, the Spire stood against the stars with its crown lit gold, and Kestrel—who could not yet know why—felt the silence in his bones lean toward it like a tide.

Three days later, a package would arrive at the chandler's shop, wrapped in oilcloth, containing a fragment of royal seal-bone and a note bearing no signature: *Appraise this. Tell no one.*

But that was three days away, and the fire was warm, and the night was long, and Kestrel Crane sat up with the ashes of his own name and listened—for the first time in his life—to nothing at all.
