## 3. What the Dead King Says

The parcel arrived on a Tuesday, which Kestrel would later think of as the last ordinary day of his life.

It came the way all appraisal work came to the Guild of Impossible Things: wrapped in oilcloth, tied with plain twine, no return address, and left with the blind lamplighter on Coriander Street who asked no questions because he could not see them anyway. The guild's rules were simple and absolute on this point. No names on parcels. No questions about provenance until the fee was paid. And never—*never*—let a client see your face change while you worked.

Kestrel carried it up three flights to his workroom at the top of the guildhall, where the morning light came through leaded glass in gray diamonds and everything smelled of bone dust and beeswax and the faint mineral tang of the polishing compounds he mixed himself. He set the parcel on the scarred oak bench beside the whalebone he'd been carving for three days—the drowned girl's memory, nearly finished now, her small ghost gone quiet in the ivory since dawn as if she too were waiting to see what the post had brought.

He weighed it first. That was habit, and habit was survival. A pound and a half, dense for its size, with the particular heavy warmth that bone always carried even after the flesh had been gone from it for centuries. He turned it over. Something inside shifted with a soft click, like a knuckle cracking.

"New client?" asked the drowned girl's voice from the whalebone, thin as breath on winter glass.

"Maybe." He kept his voice low. Talking to carvings was not against guild rules, but it was the sort of thing that got you watched if the wrong person heard. "Go back to sleep, Mira."

"I wasn't sleeping. I was listening." A pause, the way she paused when she was deciding whether to say something. "That one's loud."

He frowned. Bone spoke to him in degrees—a fingerbone might murmur, a skull might hold a conversation—but the voices were always muffled, always at one remove, as if heard through a wall or deep water. This parcel sat silent on his bench, and yet Mira, carved from a completely different animal in a completely different ocean, had heard something through the oilcloth.

He untied the twine.

Inside was a second wrapping, this one silk, dyed the deep imperial violet that by law belonged only to the crown—and that every fence, smuggler, and black-market dealer in Vhalen had learned to dye cheaply and sell anyway. Beneath the silk lay straw packing, and beneath the straw, cradled like an egg, a fragment of bone the length of his forearm.

He knew what it was before he touched it. Every carver did. Royal seal-bone—the phalange of a monarch's sword hand, severed at coronation according to the old rite, cured in consecrated ash, and pressed into wax for every edict, pardon, and declaration of war for the length of a reign. There were perhaps forty authenticated fragments in the kingdom. Most lived in the Reliquary under guard. One had been stolen eleven years ago and never recovered. The rest were legend.

This one was stained brown at the broken end. Old blood, soaked deep, the kind that never washed out because it had been there when the bone still lived.

"Provenance," he murmured, and reached for his ledger.

The rule was that he recorded before he listened. Weight, dimensions, coloration, fracture pattern, any marks of tooling. It kept him honest; it kept him sane. A carver who listened first and catalogued second would find himself drowning in other people's deaths before lunch. Odile Voss had drilled it into him fifteen years ago, when he'd come to her a half-starved boy who couldn't walk past a butcher's shop without weeping. *The ledger is your levee, Kestrel. Build it high.*

So he built it high. He measured. He sketched the fracture in graphite. He noted the imperial assay mark near the joint—a crowned tower, struck twice, meaning the bone had been verified at two separate accessions. He noted the blood. His pen moved steadily, and his hands did not shake, and if his heart had already begun to hammer he saw no reason to write that down.

Then he picked it up.

The dead did not speak all at once. That was the first thing they taught you, or rather the first thing you learned, because no one survived the learning otherwise. A voice arrived the way tide arrives—first a seep, then a pull, then the whole gray weight of it. Kestrel braced himself the way he always did, feet flat on the floorboards, spine straight, breath let out slow, and opened the door in his mind that most people never knew they had.

The tide came in.

It came in like a landslide.

The scream hit him so hard he staggered backward into the shelving, and jars of polish rattled, and somewhere below a shelf of finished work clinked like frightened teeth. He caught himself on the bench. The bone was in his hand. He could not remember picking it up.

*I AM NOT DEAD.*

Not words, exactly. Not sound. What he heard was older than sound, pressure made meaning, a voice the size of a cathedral compressed into a fragment the size of his arm, and it was screaming, had been screaming, would go on screaming—

*I am not dead I am not dead I am NOT DEAD—*

"Stop," Kestrel whispered. "Stop, stop—"

Fifteen years of practice said: *shallow the channel.* You could not shut the door once it opened, but you could narrow it, let the voice through in sips instead of floods. He tried. He gripped the bench edge and breathed and narrowed, narrowed, and the scream compressed itself obediently into something almost bearable, a man's voice now instead of a collapsing building, though a man screaming with everything he had was its own kind of terrible.

"—listen to me, whoever holds me, LISTEN—" The voice broke apart and reassembled, frantic, ragged. "You can hear me. I can feel you hearing me. Fifteen years. Fifteen years of nothing and now someone *hears*—"

"Fifteen years," Kestrel repeated stupidly. His mouth was dry. "Who—who are you?"

A silence. Even the scream stopped, and the silence was worse, a held breath the size of a kingdom.

"You don't know," the voice said at last, and there was something underneath the desperation now, something cold and careful, the voice of a man remembering how to be a king. "You don't know whose hand you're holding."

Kestrel looked down at the bone. At the crowned tower struck twice. At the blood.

Every schoolchild in Vhalen knew the story. King Aldous the Third, the Beloved, dead these eleven years of a wasting fever, mourned by a kingdom that still hung black crepe on the anniversary. His son too young to wear the crown alone, so the regent ruled in his name—Regent Lord Cassian, patient, gray-eyed, unaging somehow, though that was only a joke told in taverns. Eleven years of regency. Eleven years of crepe.

And here, in his workshop above a guildhall that officially did not exist, Kestrel Crane held a piece of the dead king's own hand, and the dead king was telling him—

"I'm not dead," the voice said again, quieter now, and the quiet was more horrifying than the scream. Quiet meant he had screamed himself out, over and over, for fifteen years, to an empty room. "I am in the crown. Do you understand? He wears me. Every renewal rite, he presses my seal-bone into the wax and I *feel* it, I feel my own name signing away my kingdom, and the whole court bows to the thing wearing my face—"

"Stop." Kestrel set the bone down on the bench with exaggerated care, the way you'd set down a live coal. His hands were shaking now; the ledger could record that later. "I'm setting you down. I need to think."

"NO—" The voice flared, desperate again. "No, please, they'll move me, they always move me, and then it's another decade of dark—you're the first voice in fifteen years, do you know what that's—please. Please. My daughter was six years old. Is she—is she alive? Does she—"

Kestrel closed his eyes. This was the trap of his gift, the thing no one warned you about because no one who learned it ever came back to warn anyone. The dead did not want conversation. They wanted rescue, revenge, remembrance—they wanted *things*, and they had nothing but time and your ear to get them. Every carver who went mad went mad the same way: not from hearing voices, but from believing them.

But the dead lied the way rivers ran downhill—easily, constantly, toward their own grief. And this voice was not lying. Kestrel had spent fifteen years learning the texture of truth in bone, the grain of it, the weight. Lies felt slick. Grief felt wet. Fear felt like this: jagged, repetitive, circling the same wound.

*I am not dead I am not dead I am not dead.*

"Mira," he said hoarsely. "Mira, are you awake?"

"Yes." Small, immediate, frightened. She had heard everything. Of course she had. "Kestrel, what is that?"

"What do you hear? From it. Just—tell me what you hear."

A long pause. When the drowned girl spoke again, her voice had gone strange, flattened, the way it did when she was listening hard. "Chains," she said. "Not iron chains. Word-chains. Like the prayer-locks the priests put on us, but bigger. Older. And underneath the chains—" Her voice wavered. "Underneath there's singing. Someone singing very far away, trying to keep the chains from closing all the way. But they're closing anyway. Slowly. They've been closing for a long time."

The room seemed to tilt. Kestrel sat down hard on his stool.

Word-chains. Binding-work. He knew the theory the way every gifted person knew it—as cautionary tale, as nightmare, as the reason the guild's third rule existed (*never carve what you cannot uncarve*). To bind a living soul into an object was the oldest forbidden art, the crime the Inquisition had supposedly been founded to prevent, the sin so grave that even mentioning it in a guildhall was worth a flogging. It required royal blood. It required consent given falsely or taken by force. And it required three bones of the victim, gathered at three separate moments of vulnerability, because a soul anchored at a single point could tear free, but a soul anchored thrice—

Thrice.

His eyes went to the fragment on the bench. Seal-bone. The sword-hand phalange, cut at coronation. *One.*

Somewhere in the kingdom were two more pieces of Aldous the Third, and between them they held a living man prisoner inside the crown of Vhalen, and the man who wore that crown signed edicts with the prisoner's own severed hand.

"No," Kestrel said aloud, to the room, to himself. "No. This is not my problem. This is so far beyond—"

"You're going to help me," the king's voice said. Not pleading now. Certain. "You have to. You're the only one who can hear me."

"I'm going to appraise you, log you, and give you back to whoever sent you, and forget every word of this." He pulled the ledger toward him. His handwriting, when it came, looked like someone else's: *Fragment, royal seal-bone, provenance suspect. Assay mark genuine. Voice present: male, adult, claims—*

His pen stopped.

Claims what? If he wrote it down—if he wrote *claims to be the king, claims binding, claims the regent is false*—then the words existed outside his head, in ink, in a guild ledger, in a city where criers had spent two weeks shouting about sedition and the burning of traitors' books. Odile had burned half the accounts ledger a month ago rather than let a single gifted name fall into the wrong hands. What would she burn over *this*?

*Kestrel,* said the voice, gentler now, terribly gentle. *What is your name?*

"Crane," he said. Then, cursing himself: "Just Crane."

"Crane." The king tasted it. "You're frightened. Good. Frightened means you understand what I'm telling you. Listen to me. I don't know how much time you have—I don't know how much *I* have. The chains close a little more each year. Some days now I can't find the edges of myself. Some days I think in his thoughts and I can't tell they aren't mine." A pause, and when the voice resumed it had dropped to a whisper, urgent, intimate, the whisper of a man leaning close. "There is a woman in the Spire. Gray dress, silver chain of office. She carries messages to the vault where they keep the other pieces. She hums when she thinks no one hears—she doesn't know I hear, through the crown, through the walls, I hear *everything* in that place—she hums a lullaby my wife used to sing. Find her. Or don't. But if you log me and hand me back and walk away, Crane, then in ten years there will be no one left in this bone to talk to, and the thing wearing my face will sign another decade of edicts with my hand, and you will have helped him do it. You will have *appraised* him. Weighed him. Found him genuine."

Kestrel stared at the page. The unfinished sentence sat there like an open wound: *Voice present: male, adult, claims—*

Below him, through the floorboards, he heard the guildhall waking. Pip's uneven footsteps crossing the counting room. The smell of frying onions rising up the stairwell. Ordinary sounds. An ordinary morning, an hour ago.

"Kestrel," Mira whispered from the whalebone. "Your face. You've gone white as me."

He looked at the bone. The bone looked back, in the way that bone did—not with eyes, but with attention, with the awful patience of the dead, who had nothing but time and had already waited fifteen years.

"If I help you," he said slowly, "and anyone finds out—the Inquisition burns the gifted now. It's law. It's proclaimed. Helping you isn't charity, it's—" He laughed, and it came out wrong. "It's treason with extra steps."

"Yes," the king agreed simply. "It is."

"And if I refuse?"

Silence. Long enough that he thought the connection had frayed. Then, quietly, with the terrible dignity of a man who had rehearsed this answer to an empty room for a decade and a half:

"Then you refuse. And I will still be here. Screaming. And you will still be able to hear me. That is the whole of it, Crane. That is what my father's fathers bought with all their crowns." A breath that was not a breath. "I would not wish my hearing on anyone. But since you have it—since you are the one man in Vhalen who cannot pretend he didn't hear—"

The stairwell creaked. Footsteps, light and quick, taking the stairs two at a time, and then a knock at his door, three sharp raps, Pip's knock, and Pip's voice, bright with the particular excitement that in Kestrel's experience always preceded disaster:

"Kest! Kest, you'll want to come down. There's a man at the gate. Fine coat, gray gloves, says he's here about a parcel he sent up this morning." A pause. "Kest? He's asking if the appraisal's done. He says—he says it's *urgent*. Says it belongs to the Regent Lord personally."

On the bench, silent now, sealed, patient, the fragment of a living man's hand lay in its pool of morning light.

Kestrel looked at the unfinished line in his ledger. *Claims—*

He dipped his pen.
