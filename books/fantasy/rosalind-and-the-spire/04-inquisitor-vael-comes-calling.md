## 4. Inquisitor Vael Comes Calling

The knock came at dawn, and it was wrong.

Kestrel knew the sound of every hand that ever touched the guildhall door: the postman's double rap, Odile's cane-tap in threes, the soft knuckle of clients who came ashamed of what they carried. This was none of those. This was iron on oak, measured and patient, the knock of someone who had never once been made to wait.

He was at his workbench with the drowned girl's whalebone half-finished, her last words curling into the ivory grain in letters no larger than a grain of rice. *Tell my mother the water was warm.* The ghost had gone quiet an hour ago, satisfied, sinking back into whatever sleep waited between carvings, and for that hour Kestrel had been only a man making small marks in bone. He treasured such hours. He counted them like coins.

The knock came again.

Below him, he heard the guildhall wake—shutters, footsteps, Odile's voice calling something too low to catch. Then many boots. Not the shuffling dozen of a morning market crowd. Boots in step.

Kestrel set down his burin with hands that did not shake, because he had trained them not to, and blew the bone dust from the girl's name. He slid the whalebone into the false bottom of his ledger drawer, beneath three years of balanced accounts, and went down the stairs slowly, one hand on the rail, composing his face into the mild blankness of a clerk woken early.

Through the leaded window beside the door he saw them: gray cloaks, gray helms, and at their center a figure in black so deep it drank the dawn. Tall. Still. The gray-cloaks arranged themselves around the guildhall's front steps the way water arranges itself around a stone.

*Inquisition.* The word arrived in his mind already cold.

Odile stood in the entry hall, and she had put on her good coat—the wine-dark one with the silver clasp she saved for tax assessors and funeral clients. Her face was composed. Her hands were not; they were folded too tightly at her waist, knuckles pale as tallow.

"Kestrel," she said, without turning. "Stay by the stairs."

"I gathered that."

The door opened before anyone knocked again. It simply swung inward, and the man in black stepped through as though the guildhall had been holding the door for him all along.

He was younger than Kestrel expected. That was the first obscenity of him. Inquisitors in the ballads were ancient, leather-faced, eyes like nailheads. This one might have been five-and-thirty, with a scholar's long face and a mouth built for patience rather than cruelty. His hair was iron-gray at the temples, prematurely, as if the color had been frightened into him by something he'd read. He carried no weapon that Kestrel could see. He carried a book instead—a slim volume bound in gray leather—and he held it the way a priest holds scripture, or a butcher holds a knife.

"Inquisitor Vael," Odile said. Her voice was steady. Kestrel marveled at it. "You honor a humble house of record-keeping."

"I know what this house is, Guildmaster Voss." Vael's voice was quiet, almost gentle, and that was worse than shouting. It was the voice of a man who had never needed to raise it, because everyone leaned in to hear. "I know precisely what it is. That is why I am here." He opened the gray book. "Shall we begin?"

* * *

They began in the great room, where the guild's ledgers lived in their cedar cabinets, and where eleven of the guild's members had assembled in various states of terrified composure. Vael walked the length of the room once, unhurried, trailing one gloved finger along the cabinet edges as if taking their measure for furniture. Then he stopped before the hearth and turned.

"The king's edict is seven days old," he said. "I will not insult you by reciting it. You have heard the criers. Register your gift, surrender it, or burn. Those are the mercies offered." He let the silence sit. Somewhere upstairs a floorboard creaked, and eleven people flinched in unison, a small terrible chorus. "I am not here for the criers' version of events. I am here because I have a list."

He raised the gray book.

"Master Pellam, chandler. Gift: he can tell when a flame has been lit by a dying hand." A page turned. "Widow Anselm. She reads the intentions of bread dough, which sounds absurd until you learn she predicted a granary collapse from the behavior of a sourdough starter." Another page. "Tobias Finch, apprentice. He dreams other people's dreams and cannot stop. He is fourteen years old."

Each name landed like a stone dropped down a well. Kestrel stood among the guild members near the back, and he watched each face as its name was spoken—the flinch, the closing, the quick arithmetic of *how does he know*. Master Pellam's gift was known to perhaps four people alive. Kestrel was one of them. He had carved a memory for Pellam's dead brother two winters past.

The list was not intelligence. The list was confession. Someone had given the Inquisition the guild's private accounts, name by name, gift by gift.

Kestrel looked at Odile. Odile looked at the floor.

Vael continued down the pages, and Kestrel stopped listening to the names and started listening for the absence of one. *Kestrel Crane.* Four syllables that would end his life or remake it. The Inquisitor turned another page. Turned another. The room grew warmer; Kestrel realized he was sweating.

Then Vael closed the book.

"That is the list I was given," he said. "It is incomplete." His gaze moved across the assembled faces, unhurried, and Kestrel felt it pass over him the way a searchlight passes over a hiding man—not stopping, but not blind either. "There is a name missing. A significant one. And I find, Guildmaster Voss, that I am in a generous mood this morning."

"Generous," Odile repeated.

"I will take the incomplete list at face value. These ten—" he indicated the trembling assembly "—will be escorted to the capital for assessment. Assessment is not condemnation. Some of them may even return." A pause, delicate as a scalpel. "In exchange for the missing name."

The silence that followed had weight and texture. Kestrel heard his own heartbeat in it.

"You misunderstand us," Odile said. "We are clerks. We keep accounts for—"

"You keep accounts for the dead," Vael said, still gently. "And for the gifted who wish to speak to them. Do not mistake me for a fool, madam; fools do not rise to my office. I have read your guild's founding charter. I know what 'impossible things' means in your ledgers." He tucked the gray book into his cloak. "But here is what you must understand about me, Guildmaster Voss. I did not come for your clerks. Your chandler and your widow and your dreaming boy are nothing. Kindling. What I came for is a bone-hearer strong enough to matter, and I am told—I am reliably told—that such a person works in this house under a false name, and that the guildmaster knows exactly who he is."

*He.*

Kestrel felt the word go through the room like cold water through cloth. Several heads turned—not toward him, thank every silent saint, but toward the idea of him, the absent man.

"And if I refuse?" Odile said.

"Then I take all eleven of you, and I burn this building with its ledgers inside it, and I keep looking. I always find them, madam. It is the only thing I have ever been good at." Vael smiled then, briefly, and it transformed his face into something almost kind, which was the most frightening transformation yet. "Consider instead what I am offering. One name. Ten lives returned. A guildhall left standing. I call that generous. I suspect history will call it worse, but history is not here, and I am."

Odile was quiet for a long moment. When she spoke, her voice had changed—flattened, emptied, the voice of a woman stepping off a ledge.

"There is no bone-hearer here."

"Madam."

"There is no bone-hearer here," she said again, "because there is no bone-hearer anywhere. But there is a carver. A man who keeps our books and cuts memorial inscriptions, and who has, over the years, developed certain... methods. If such a man existed—if he agreed to serve the crown's inquiry voluntarily, as a consultant, under protection—would that satisfy the edict? Would his service purchase the guild's compliance? All of it?"

Kestrel understood, in one sick lurching instant, what she was doing. She was selling him. Not his name—she would not say his name, could not bring herself to, or perhaps was preserving deniability for both of them—but his function, his gift described in the thinnest possible euphemism, laid on the table like a purse of coin.

She caught his eye across the room. And what Kestrel saw in her face was not triumph, not cold calculation, but something far more unbearable: apology. Genuine, anguished apology, from a woman who had already decided.

*How long,* he thought, *have you known what I am? How long have you been deciding?*

Vael turned. Slowly. Deliberately. And this time the searchlight stopped.

His eyes were pale gray, nearly colorless, and they rested on Kestrel with the particular attention of a man examining a specimen he has traveled a long way to collect.

"There you are," the Inquisitor said softly.

* * *

They did not bind his hands. They bound his hearing.

The mute-band was a circlet of dull metal, jointed like a serpent's spine, and the gray-cloak who fitted it to Kestrel's throat did so with the tenderness of a nurse and the efficiency of a hangman. It sat below his jaw, cool against the pulse point, and when the clasp closed Kestrel heard—nothing.

Not silence. Silence was a thing he knew, the ordinary deafness of empty rooms. This was different. This was a wall erected between him and the world's hidden layer, a deadening, as if someone had wrapped his skull in wet wool. The ghosts that murmured perpetually at the edge of his perception—the old ones soaked into the guildhall's foundations, the whale-bone girl sleeping in his desk upstairs, the thousand small voices he had spent thirty years learning to live among—all of it cut off at once, cleanly, like a door slammed on a choir.

He staggered. A gray-cloak caught his elbow, and he shook the hand off and stood on his own, because he would be damned before he leaned on any of them.

"It dampens the resonance," Vael said, watching from across the hall with his hands folded. "Bone sings, Master Crane—or should I say, bone *spoke*, and you listened. The band mutes the song. You will feel it as a kind of... hollowness. Most wearers report headaches. Some report worse." He tilted his head. "You may remove it when I no longer need you unable to overhear the evidence."

"The evidence," Kestrel said. His own voice sounded strange to him, flattened, alone. Thirty years of company, gone in one clasp. "You mean the bones you've confiscated. You want me to interrogate them."

"I want you to *listen* to them. Interrogation implies the bones have something to hide." Vael came closer, and up close Kestrel could see the fine lines at his eyes, the ink stain on the side of his right index finger—a scholar's stain, earned late nights over documents. "What I want, Master Crane, is the truth about a piece of royal seal-bone that passed through this guild six weeks ago. Appraised, I'm told, by a carver whose name does not appear in any ledger. A carver who heard something in it that made him burn his notes."

The room tilted. Six weeks. The fragment that had arrived wrapped in oilcloth, anonymous, paid triple rate. The screaming. *I am not dead. I am not dead. I am bound, do you hear me, I am bound—*

He had burned the notes. He had told himself the memory would fade. It had not faded; it had grown roots.

"I don't know what you're talking about," Kestrel said.

"No," Vael agreed mildly. "You wouldn't. Not yet." He gestured, and the gray-cloaks moved—Odile first, then Pellam, then the others, marshaled toward the door with that terrible gentleness. Odile paused at Kestrel's side. For one moment her hand hovered near his arm, not touching, close enough that he could feel the warmth of it.

"The ledgers," she said quietly. "Third cabinet. Everything balances. I made sure of it."

*Everything balances.* As if that were the thing being taken from him. As if accounts were what mattered. He looked at her—at the woman who had taught him cipher and craft, who had fed him when he arrived at fifteen with nothing but a stolen burin and a head full of voices—and he found that rage and grief had fused in him into something with no name, a dense black ore too heavy to lift.

"Yes," he said. "Everything balances, Guildmaster."

Something flickered behind her eyes—wounded, maybe, or ashamed—and then the gray-cloaks walked her out into the gray morning, and she was gone.

* * *

The guildhall emptied. The Inquisitor's men packed the cedar cabinets onto a cart, wrapped the guild's seals in canvas, took even the brass fittings from the doors as evidence of—something. Kestrel sat on the stairs of his own home, collared, guarded, and watched thirty years of careful anonymity get loaded onto a wagon like scrap.

Vael found him there near midday. The Inquisitor dismissed the guards with a flick of two fingers and sat down on the stair below Kestrel, uninvited, unhurried, arranging his black cloak around him like a man settling in for a long journey.

"You hate her," Vael observed.

"No."

"She sold you. I watched her do it. There was no coercion I could detect—she offered before I asked."

"She bought ten lives with mine." Kestrel kept his eyes on the empty street. "That's not hatred. That's arithmetic. She's a guildmaster. It's what we do."

"Mm." Vael was quiet a moment. "My mother sold me to the Church of the Quiet Flame when I was nine, because the alternative was watching me be stoned by our neighbors. They'd discovered I could taste lies. Do you know what that's like, Master Crane? Every kindness with a flavor of rot underneath. Every vow sour on the tongue." He said it without self-pity, the way a man reports weather. "She made the same arithmetic your guildmaster made. I have spent my life deciding whether it was betrayal or love. I still haven't answered the question."

"Why are you telling me this?"

"Because you are going to hear things, in the capital, that will make you want to lie to me. And I want you to understand that lying to me is a waste of your time. I taste lies. It is a small, ugly gift, and the edict says I should have registered it years ago, and yet here I am, wearing black instead of ash." He rose, brushing off his cloak. "The cart leaves within the hour. There is a cell with your name on it—not a punishment, Master Crane. A courtesy. Guests of the inquiry sleep better than prisoners."

He paused at the door, silhouetted against the gray light.

"One more thing. The seal-bone. Whatever you heard in it—whatever screamed at you—you will tell me eventually. Everyone tells me everything eventually. The only variable is how much of you is left intact when you do." He settled the gray book into the crook of his arm. "I would prefer a great deal of you intact. I have read your appraisals. Even unsigned, even burned, the fragments I recovered were extraordinary. You have the finest ear I have ever encountered, Master Crane. It would be a tragedy to waste it."

The door closed behind him.

Kestrel sat alone in the stripped guildhall, in the hollow ringing silence of the mute-band, and pressed his palm flat against the banister his own hands had polished for thirty years. No voices rose to meet him. No dead murmured in the wood. For the first time since childhood, Kestrel Crane was entirely, absolutely alone inside his own skull, and he understood at last why the living feared it so much.

Upstairs, in the false bottom of a drawer the Inquisition had somehow failed to open, the drowned girl slept on in her whalebone, her last words waiting: *tell my mother the water was warm.*

He would not be the one to deliver them now. Perhaps no one would.

Within the hour, the cart rolled east toward the capital, carrying ten bound guild members, one collared bone-carver, and an Inquisitor who rode alongside with the gray book open on his knee, reading names.

Kestrel did not look back at the guildhall. He looked forward, at the road, at the smudge of the Spire rising above the horizon like a needle threaded with cloud—and against his throat, faint and rhythmic as a second heartbeat, the mute-band hummed with everything it was swallowing.

Somewhere beneath that humming, muffled almost to nothing, he thought he could feel the seal-bone screaming.

Or perhaps it was only the road.

He would know soon enough.
