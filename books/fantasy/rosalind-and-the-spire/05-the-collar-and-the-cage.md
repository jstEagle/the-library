## 5. The Collar and the Cage

The collar was iron and bone, and it fit like a hand closing over a mouth.

Kestrel had felt it go on in the guildhall—cold, then warm, then *present*, the way a toothache becomes part of the architecture of the skull—and in the first hour of the march he had wept without meaning to, tears freezing on his jaw in the morning wind. Not from fear. From silence.

He had not been silent since he was six years old, when he'd pressed his ear to his grandmother's carved chess knight and heard her say, *mind the bishop, love, it lies.* The dead had murmured at the edges of every day of his life since: whalebone sighing with drowned sailors' hymns, ox-bone rattling with old arguments, ivory humming lullabies no living throat remembered. Even silence, true silence, had always been full of them. Now there was nothing. The mute-band sat against his throat like a stone dropped down a well that never hit bottom.

*You get used to it,* he told himself, and did not believe it.

They marched him east for nine days. Four Inquisitors in grey lacquered coats, faces like coins rubbed smooth, and Inquisitor Vael on a black horse that never once shied or sweated. Vael spoke to him perhaps six times in nine days, and each time it was a question shaped like courtesy.

*Do you eat meat, Master Crane?*

*How long have you kept the guild's accounts?*

*When you carve, do you choose what the dead say, or do they?*

That last one he answered honestly, because lying to Vael felt like lying to a mirror. "They choose. I only listen."

Vael had smiled then—a small, precise smile, like a key turning—and said nothing for two more days.

On the ninth day the capital rose out of the plain, and Kestrel forgot, briefly, to be afraid. He had seen it in engravings: the Spire like a needle of white stone stitching cloud to earth, the city spilling around its base like ink from a bottle. Engravings did not convey the sound of it—the bells, the gulls, the hundred thousand small human noises—or the way the Spire seemed not to stand among the buildings but to *watch* them, the way a heron watches water.

He could not hear the bones in the baggage cart behind him. But he found himself turning toward it anyway, again and again, like a man reaching for a hand that wasn't there.

* * *

They did not take him to a dungeon. They took him to an office.

It was on the fourth floor of the Inquisition's bastion, a square grey tower crouched at the Spire's western foot like a dog at its master's heel. The office had a desk, a lamp, a chair for Vael, and a stool for Kestrel. On the desk lay a ledger bound in green leather, and beside the ledger, arranged on velvet with the tenderness of a jeweler, forty-three pieces of bone.

"Confiscated," Vael said, settling into the chair. "Over eleven years. Every piece seized from a registered gift-bearer, a smuggler, or a fool. We know what most of them are, in the way that matters—which are dangerous, which are merely sentimental. What we do not know is what they *say.*"

Kestrel looked at the collar's reflection ghosted in the lamp glass. "You've cut my throat and now you want me to sing."

"I want you to work." Vael folded his hands. "You are a bone-carver who keeps ledgers. Very well. Keep this one. Provenance, material, condition—and whatever you can infer about the voice inside, if any. You will find," and here the smile again, thinner, "that the collar does not stop your gift entirely. It stops you from *hearing freely.* Under close attention, at short range, fragments come through. Enough to catalogue. Not enough to matter."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then you will be very bored in a cell for the rest of your life, and the guild will be very short one more name." Vael slid the ledger across the desk. "You already understand how names are spent, Master Crane. Spend yours well."

Kestrel picked up the pen because his hands needed something to do that was not shaking.

The first piece was a knucklebone, sheep, polished dark by handling. He held it as he'd held ten thousand pieces of bone, thumb finding the grain, and listened—not with his ears, which were muffled under the band, but with the older listening underneath, the one that lived somewhere behind his sternum.

A fragment came through, thin as light under a door: *—and she never came back, she never—*

Sheep knucklebone. A woman's voice. Waiting for someone who never returned. He wrote it down in his careful bookkeeper's hand, and hated himself a little for how good it felt to write anything at all.

* * *

The days took on a shape, and the shape was a cage with comfortable bars.

Morning: brought up under escort, collared, seated. Catalogue until his eyes burned. Midday: bread, cheese, water, no conversation except Vael's occasional questions, which Kestrel answered with the flat precision of a man reading inventory. Evening: escorted back down four flights to a cell that was clean, warm, and absolutely silent, and which he had begun to think of as the inside of his own skull made solid.

The bones were the worst and the best of it. Worst, because each fragment of voice arrived broken—half a sentence, a sob with no face attached, a name repeated until it stopped meaning anything—and his carver's hands itched to give them shape, wholeness, rest, and could not. Best, because they were voices, any voices, and a drowning man does not complain that the rope is frayed.

By the second week he had catalogued thirty-one pieces. A rib that whispered a child's counting rhyme. A tooth that only ever said *forgive me* in a language he didn't know but understood anyway. A fingerbone, human, that said nothing at all, and frightened him more than all the others, because he could feel something in it—something coiled and patient, listening back.

He told Vael about that one. Vael took it away personally, and did not return it to the tray.

"You're useful," Vael observed one evening, watching Kestrel wash the dust of the day's bones from his hands. "I suspected you would be. Odile Voss described you as meticulous."

The name landed like a slap. Kestrel kept his eyes on the basin. "She would know."

"She has known many things, for many years." Vael said it lightly, the way a man drops a coin down a well to hear how deep it goes. Kestrel refused to give him an echo. But he carried the sentence back to his cell, turned it over in the dark, and did not sleep.

* * *

It was on the seventeenth day that the thoughts started arriving.

At first he thought the collar had cracked—that the silence had worn through somewhere, because the voices in the vault had begun, faintly, to change. They were still fragments, still thin. But they were beginning to arrange themselves around him, the way birds settle on a new fence post. And threaded among them, unmistakably, was a voice that was not dead.

*You're the one who flinches,* it said, dry as paper, when he set down the rib with the counting rhyme. *Every time. Right here.* And though there was no finger to point, he felt exactly where she meant: the small involuntary twitch in his shoulder when a fragment cut off mid-word. *You flinch like it hurts you.*

He looked up. The vault office had a barred window; below it, the bastion's exercise yard, where prisoners were walked in circles under guard. A woman stood at the bars' edge looking up—not at the window, he realized. At him, somehow, through it. Small, sharp-faced, hair the color of wet sand chopped off at the jaw, wearing the drab smock of a holding prisoner with the posture of someone wearing a costume she intended to return.

*I'm not dead,* the voice said, amused. *Before you ask. I get that a lot.*

His pen had stopped moving. One of the escorts shifted by the door. Kestrel bent back over the ledger and wrote, in the margin, in letters so small they were almost not letters: *who are you.*

*Sera Quill. Hedge-witch, hedge-thief, currently between engagements.* A pause, and then, with something that was almost gentleness: *You can stop writing. I hear the thinking, not the writing. The collar doesn't touch me. Nothing touches me, that's sort of my whole problem.*

*That's impossible,* he thought, deliberately, staring at the page.

*Mm. Guild of Impossible Things, isn't it? Heard the guards talk. You keep the books.* He felt her attention shift, a physical sensation, like a draft from an open door. *Funny place to end up, a guild of impossible things. Full of people who never believed anything was.*

*What do you want?*

There was a silence then, long enough that he risked a glance at the window. She was still there, walking her slow circle, hands loose at her sides, face tilted up.

*Company,* she said at last, simply. *Seventeen days in a stone room with nothing but my own head, and then suddenly there's a second head next door, loud as a market bell. I'm not going anywhere, bone-man. Neither are you. We might as well talk.*

* * *

They talked, in the way of two people separated by bars and walls and the whole machinery of an Inquisition: in snatches, in the margins of other things. He catalogued bones and she sat in his skull like a cat in a sunbeam, commenting.

*That one's lying,* she said of a scapula that claimed to be a saint's. *Saints don't gossip. That thing's been gossiping for two hundred years.*

*How would you know?*

*Because I grew up three doors from a reliquary thief, and everything holy I ever met talked too much.* And then, another day: *You count when you're frightened. Did you know that? Ledger numbers. Little sums running under everything else. It's very tidy. It's very sad.*

*Stay out of that.*

*Can't. You leave the door open. Grief doesn't lock.*

He hated her for a week for that. He kept talking to her anyway.

She told him, in pieces, how she'd been taken—caught on the Spire road with something wrapped in oilcloth against her spine, a relic she would not name, pursued by hounds that did not breathe. She told him the holding cells were beneath the bastion, that there were eleven other gifted down there waiting for the assizes, that two of them were children. She told him the assizes were a formality; the last three sessions had ended in ash carts.

And she asked him questions he did not want to answer, in the soft relentless way of water working at mortar.

*What did Vael mean, your guildmaster sold you?*

*She saved the others.*

*She saved herself and used you as the coin. Those aren't the same thing, however neatly you enter them in the books.*

*You don't know her.*

*No. But I know the arithmetic. I've done it myself.* A pause. *That's why I got caught, actually. I stopped doing it. Stopped leaving people out of the sum. It's a bad habit, caring. Expensive. I recommend against it.* And he felt, under the dryness, something raw and recent, and did not press, because she had not pressed him.

On the twenty-third day, cataloguing a vertebra that hummed with a ferryman's work-song, he caught himself explaining to her—without being asked—what the binding felt like from the listener's side. How a bound voice wasn't silenced but *tightened*, wound round and round until it could only repeat itself. How the worst ones weren't the screaming bones but the contented ones, the ones that had forgotten they'd ever said anything else.

She was quiet for a long time after that.

*The thing I was carrying,* Sera said finally, *when they took me. It was a fingerbone. Old queen's, if the stories hold. And it wasn't repeating. It was the only bone I ever held that was still saying something new.*

*What was it saying?*

*Help me,* she said. *Over and over. But new every time. Like it meant it fresh each time it said it.*

Kestrel's pen hovered over the ledger. Through the barred window the Spire stood white against the afternoon, needle-straight, indifferent, and for no reason he could name he thought of the royal seal-bone that had come to the guildhall a lifetime ago—three weeks, it had been three weeks—and the king's voice screaming inside it: *not dead, only bound.*

*Where is it now?* he asked. *Your queen's fingerbone.*

Another pause, longer, and when her voice came back it had changed—all the dryness gone out of it, replaced by something quick and bright and dangerous, a struck flint.

*Vaults, bone-man. Deep ones. Under this very tower.* He felt her attention sharpen to a point, felt her almost smiling. *Tell me. How are you at keeping ledgers? Because I've been counting the guard rotations for twenty-three days, and I think it's time somebody audited this place.*

Below the window, the yard bell rang for evening lock-in. Sera Quill lifted her face to the bars one last time, and the thought she sent him was so clear it might have been spoken aloud:

*Tomorrow. Bring your pen. We're going to need it to forge a signature.*
