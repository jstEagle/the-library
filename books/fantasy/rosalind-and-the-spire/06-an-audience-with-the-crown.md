## 6. An Audience with the Crown

They washed him before they let him see the king.

Two servants in gray livery scrubbed the road dust from his arms and hair with water so hot it felt like penance. They took his coat—the good one, whalebone buttons that Odile had carved herself—and gave him a plain robe of undyed wool that smelled of cedar and something underneath, something medicinal. Incense, he realized. They were burning incense in the halls to cover a smell only he would have noticed anyway.

The collar sat against his throat like a second spine. It was iron and silver, fitted so precisely it might have been made for him, and beneath its weight the world was silent in a way Kestrel Crane had never known silence. Fifteen years of whispers—of drowned girls dictating last words and grandmothers complaining about their grandchildren from inside lockets—gone. Just gone, as if a door had been bricked over while he stood on the wrong side of it.

He kept reaching for the voices the way a tongue returns to a missing tooth. Nothing. The mute-band hummed faintly when he tried, a sound he felt more than heard, like a wasp trapped behind his sternum.

*You'll be presented at the ninth bell,* Vael had said that morning, not looking up from his papers. *Wash. Dress. Say nothing unless the king addresses you. If he addresses you, answer plainly.* A pause, thin as a knife's edge. *And Kestrel—if you feel anything in there, anything at all, you will keep your face still. Do you understand me? The regent reads faces the way you read bone.*

Kestrel had understood. He understood now, being marched down a corridor whose walls were hung with the portraits of dead kings, each one painted with eyes that followed, each one silent—though he could not have heard them if they'd screamed.

That was the worst of it. He didn't know anymore what was silent because it was empty, or silent because his gift was.

* * *

The throne room of Vellamere had been built by men who wanted visitors to feel small, and they had succeeded across four centuries without revision. The ceiling vanished into painted clouds. Columns rose like petrified trees. Sunlight fell through windows of stained glass depicting the kingdom's founding—the first king kneeling, the Spire rising, angels or saints or whatever passed for them pressing a crown onto a worthy head.

And at the far end, on a dais of black marble, beneath a canopy worked in gold thread so dense it seemed to drip: the king.

Theron IV. Fifteen years on the throne. Fifteen years since the plague winter took his queen and nearly took him too, since he'd risen from his sickbed changed—or so the story went—harder, quieter, wiser. The Wise King, the priests called him now. The Restorer.

Kestrel walked the length of that long floor and listened as hard as he had ever listened in his life.

Nothing.

Not nothing the way stone is silent. Stone has a hush to it, a density; even unworked bone has the faint pressure of whatever lived in it once, the way a room holds the shape of a voice after the speaker leaves. But the man on the throne gave back less than stone. Kestrel's senses, straining against the collar until his temples ached, found a smooth blankness where a man should have been—a wall where a window should have opened.

He knew every kind of silence the dead could make. This wasn't one of them.

This was the silence of a chair.

*Your Majesty.* Vael knelt two steps below the dais, and Kestrel, nudged hard between the shoulder blades by his escort, went down beside him. The marble was cold through the wool. *The bone-hearer of the Guild of Impossible Things, delivered under seal as commanded.*

"Delivered." The voice came from above, mild and unhurried, the voice of a man discussing the weather with an old friend. "Like a parcel."

"As you wished, Majesty."

"I did wish it." A rustle of silk. "Rise, both of you. I dislike speaking to the tops of heads. It makes conversation feel like an audit."

They rose. Kestrel lifted his eyes carefully, obediently, to the face of his king—and made himself look at nothing, at the middle distance, at the column just past the royal left shoulder. He catalogued what he saw anyway, because cataloguing was how he stayed alive, and he had been doing it since the criers came with their ash-edged proclamation.

King Theron IV was fifty-one years old. The man on the dais looked fifty-one. His hair was silvered at the temples in exactly the fashionable pattern, his beard trimmed to exactly the approved length, his hands folded on the armrests in exactly the posture of every portrait Kestrel had ever seen of him.

Exactly. That was the word that kept arriving, cold and insistent. Every detail correct. Every detail *still*. In the whole slow length of Kestrel's approach, the king had blinked twice.

Men blink. Even dying men, even bored men, even men holding themselves rigid for ceremony—they blink, shift, breathe visibly in the chest. This man breathed the way furniture breathes, which is to say not at all, which is to say the eye fills in the motion because it expects it.

"You are Kestrel Crane." Not a question. "Carver. Appraiser. Keeper of ledgers." The mouth moved around the words with perfect articulation and no warmth whatsoever. "The Inquisitor tells me you can hear the dead in their bones."

"I can hear what remains in them, Majesty," Kestrel said. His voice came out steadier than he felt. "Echoes. Memories. Sometimes more than memories. It depends on how the person died, and what they loved."

"What they loved." The king considered this. Or appeared to. "Then you are a kind of priest."

"No, Majesty. Priests intercede. I only listen."

"And what do the dead say, listener? When you put your ear to them. What do they say?"

Kestrel chose his words the way a man chooses footing on ice. *They say let us go. They say we are tired. They say someone remembers us wrong.* Any of those truths would land somewhere dangerous. "Mostly," he said, "they say goodbye. To things they never got to say it to."

Something happened then that he would turn over in his mind for nights afterward. For a fraction of a heartbeat—a gap no wider than a blink—the king's expression failed. The mildness dropped away like a mask slipping sideways, and underneath it Kestrel saw—not anger, not madness, but *attention*, vast and cold and fixed on him with the hunger of a drowning thing watching a rope—

Then it was gone, smoothed over, and the king smiled the exact smile from the coinage.

"You are useful," Theron said. "The Inquisitor believes so, and the Inquisitor is rarely wrong about instruments. You will remain in the capital. You will appraise for the vaults. And you will report to Inquisitor Vael everything you hear." The smile widened by a precise degree. "Everything. The dead can be so indiscreet."

"Yes, Majesty."

"Good. Then we understand one another." The king turned his head—an inch, no more—toward Vael. "See that he's settled. And Inquisitor—" that vast attention flickered back to Kestrel for one final instant, "—the collar stays on. Indiscretion runs in his guild, I'm told. Like blue eyes."

It was dismissal. They bowed, turned, began the long walk back down the hall—and Kestrel made it eleven steps before the crown screamed.

* * *

There was no warning. One moment the world was its new, terrible quiet; the next, sound poured through the mute-band like water through a cracked dam, and Kestrel staggered, caught himself, kept walking because stopping meant questions and questions meant death.

It came from above and behind—from the canopy, from the dais, from the head of the man they'd left seated on his own hollow throne.

It was not one voice. That was the first thing he understood, and understanding it nearly buckled his knees. It was a chorus, dozens strong, hundreds, layered and grinding against itself like millstones—voices pressed together until they had fused into a single sustained note of anguish. Men and women, old and young, some shrieking and some whispering and one, nearest the surface, closest to the edge of the sound, repeating over and over in a cadence Kestrel recognized from a hundred carvings:

*I am here. I am here. I am here. I am HERE—*

A king's cadence. A king's diction. The voice of a man who had spent a lifetime being listened to, crushed flat into the topmost layer of that screaming mass, fighting his way up through the others the way a drowning man fights through wreckage.

*Fifteen years,* the chorus howled, or one voice within it howled while the rest moaned beneath. *Fifteen years in my own crown, in my own house, wearing my own name—*

The collar flared white-hot against his throat. The mute-band clamping down, punishing the leak, and Kestrel tasted blood and realized he'd bitten his tongue. He walked. He put one foot in front of the other down that endless marble floor, past the painted founders, past the columns, his face held in the stillness Vael had demanded, and the scream poured through him and out of him and no one else in the hall so much as turned their head.

The guards hadn't moved. The courtiers along the walls hadn't stirred. The king sat serene beneath the golden canopy while his own stolen voice tore itself apart inches above his skull.

Because no one else could hear it. Because the binding was built to sing only to ears like his.

*He knows,* Kestrel thought, and the thought arrived whole and freezing, the way the worst thoughts do. *Vael knows. He brought me here to hear it. This was the test. This was always the test.*

At the far doors, the escort pulled him aside into an antechamber, and Vael followed a moment later, closing the door on the throne room's gilded silence.

They stood alone among banners of extinct houses. Vael studied him the way he studied confiscated relics—with patience, with appetite, with the particular care of a man handling something that might yet prove valuable or might have to be destroyed.

"Well?" Vael said quietly.

"The king spoke to me," Kestrel said. "I answered. He's given me to your vaults."

"That isn't what I asked."

"Then ask it plainly, Inquisitor, because my hearing is in a box of iron and silver, and I can't hear anything but what people say."

Something moved behind Vael's gray eyes—not quite amusement, not quite respect. He stepped closer. Close enough that Kestrel could smell ink and cold iron and, beneath it, the same medicinal incense they'd burned in his washing chamber. The Inquisitor wore it on his skin. He bathed in the smell of concealment.

"When you stood before the throne," Vael said, each word laid down like a card, "did you hear anything?"

Kestrel held his face still. He had spent his life keeping his face still while the dead shouted through him—grieving mothers' sons, murdered merchants, drowned girls—and never had the discipline mattered more. He thought of Odile burning half the ledger. He thought of her saying, *you're the least expensive thing I have left to trade.* He thought of the chorus, and the king's voice climbing through the wreckage of it, insisting *I am here* to a hall full of people who could not hear him.

"I heard a king," Kestrel said. "Being boring."

For three heartbeats Vael said nothing. The silence stretched, and stretched, and Kestrel understood that his entire remaining lifespan was balanced inside it, a coin on its edge.

Then Vael smiled—a real smile, small and tired at the corners, almost human. "Yes," he said. "Kings usually are." He turned for the door, then paused, hand on the iron ring. "You'll begin in the vaults tomorrow. There is a great deal to catalogue, Master Crane, and I warn you—it whispers."

The door closed behind him.

Kestrel stood alone in the antechamber of the hollow king, his throat burned raw beneath the collar, blood drying on his lip, and let himself shake—just once, head to heel, like a dog coming out of deep water.

The throne was empty. Had been empty for fifteen years. The man was a glove, and the hand inside it was the crown, and the crown was full—full of the bound and the stolen and the screaming, full of a true king crying *I am here* into a silence built to swallow him.

And Vael had watched him the whole time. Had asked the question twice, in two ways, weighing every twitch. Which meant either Kestrel had passed the test—

Or Vael already knew the answer, and this whole audience had been staged for another set of eyes entirely.

Somewhere below his feet, down flights of stairs and locked doors, in the vaults where confiscated bones waited in the dark, something began to whisper through the collar's iron—faint, muffled, unmistakable.

A woman's voice. Young. Sharp as a struck flint.

*Hey. Hey, bone-man. Yes, you—I can see you standing there. Can you hear me?*

Kestrel went very still.

*Good,* said the voice, with the unmistakable relish of someone who had been waiting a very long time. *Then listen carefully, because I've been stuck down here with these chattering relics for six weeks, and I have a proposition.*
