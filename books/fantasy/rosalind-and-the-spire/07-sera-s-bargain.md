## 7. Sera's Bargain

Sera Quill had learned to count her nights in the Inquisition's cells by the sound of boots.

Not the guards' boots—those were predictable, a slow shuffle of men who hated their work and did it anyway. She counted the other kind. The quick ones. The ones that came with keys and questions and the particular silence of someone about to hurt you. Three nights in this cell, and she had heard that silence twice.

Tonight it came again, but wrong. The boots were quick, yes—but they stopped at the wrong door. Not hers. The cell two down, where they'd dragged the bone-carver three days ago, collared and gray-faced, like a man being buried while still breathing.

Sera pressed her palm flat against the cold stone and listened with the part of herself the collar couldn't touch.

The mute-band around her own throat was iron and salt, worked with sigils she could almost read, and it had done its job well enough for the first day. Her hedge-craft was small magic—knots and whispers, charms against fever, the reading of a stranger's mood in the set of their shoulders. But the collar had missed one thing, or the smiths who made it had never imagined it: she could still hear thoughts. Not words, exactly. Weather. Pressure changes in the air of another mind, the way a sailor reads a storm before the clouds come.

And the bone-carver's mind was loud. Loud and strange, full of voices that weren't his—faint, layered, like a choir heard through a cathedral wall. Even muffled by his collar, even drowned in whatever drugs they fed him, he leaked. Grief. Fear. And underneath it all, a stubborn, meticulous attention, like a man balancing accounts in the dark.

She'd been listening to him for three days. He didn't know it yet.

The boots moved on. A door clanged. Sera let out a breath and went back to working the loose stone at the base of her bench, the one she'd been worrying with a splinter of her belt buckle since the first night.

She'd been caught on purpose. That was the part no one would believe, if anyone ever asked. Six weeks of casing the Inquisition's citadel in Vhalen's Cross—the guard rotations, the cart schedules, the one stretch of vault corridor where the torches burned green because the mortar was soaked in old ward-salt. Six weeks, and then she'd walked into the reliquary wing dressed as a laundress with a forged chit and a prayer, and gotten within arm's reach of the case before the wards sang.

Almost. *Almost* arm's reach. She could still see it through the memory's glass: a fingerbone, no longer than her smallest joint, mounted on black velvet under glass, labeled in the Inquisition's neat hand. *Relic 1-A. Provenance disputed.*

Disputed. That was the Inquisition's word for *we know exactly what this is and we will die before admitting it.*

The old queen's fingerbone. First bone of the binding. Sera had grown up on the story the way other children grew up on lullabies—her grandmother's grandmother had been a midwife in the queen's household, back when the kingdom still had a true king and a queen who kept her own counsel. When the queen died—or when they said she died—her hands were not buried whole. One finger went into the ground with her. The rest went into the making of something terrible, something that bound a living voice into dead ivory so it could be worn like jewelry.

Sera hadn't believed any of it until she'd held her grandmother's knuckle-charm and heard, for the first time, the thin silver thread of a voice inside it. After that, belief was just arithmetic. If one voice could live in one bone, how many bones did it take to cage a king?

Three. Everyone who knew anything agreed on three. And the Inquisition had the first one sitting in a velvet box like a saint's tooth.

She got the loose stone free on the fourth night and found behind it not freedom but a rat's nest and a dead spider, and sat back on her heels feeling foolish. Escape from a cell was nothing. Escape from the citadel was the hard part, and the hard part required either a miracle or an accomplice.

Which was why, when the guards came for the bone-carver the next morning, she stood up at her bars and said, "You're wasting your time with him."

The guard—a heavy man with a beard going gray in patches—looked at her the way men looked at things that talked. "Back off, witch."

"He can't hear anything through that collar, can he? That's the point of it." She let her voice carry down the corridor, toward the cell where Kestrel Crane was being marched past in irons. "But I can hear him fine. Ask your Inquisitor if she wants to know what's rattling around in a bone-hearer's head. Or don't, and explain to her later why you didn't."

The guard hesitated. Hesitation was a door, and Sera had spent her whole life learning which doors opened on hesitation and which opened on clubs. This one, grudgingly, opened. They took her up instead of leaving her, and that was how she came to be standing in an interrogation room across a table from Kestrel Crane, both of them collared, both of them watched, having a conversation that was really two conversations—one aloud, for the listeners, and one silent, in the pressure-language only she could read.

*They're listening,* she pushed at him, careful, gentle, the way you'd touch a bird with a broken wing. *So we say boring things. Nod along.*

He looked at her with eyes the color of wet slate, and she felt his attention settle on her like a hand—startled, wary, probing. He couldn't hear her thoughts; the collar saw to that. But he could see *that* she was doing something, and after a moment he gave the smallest nod.

"Tell them what you told me," the guard prompted.

"I told him," Sera said loudly, "that the guild sent me. That we're colleagues. That the Guild of Impossible Things has interests in common with—" she gestured vaguely upward, at the citadel, at the crown, at everything, "—the crown's justice. Obviously."

It was nonsense and everyone knew it was nonsense, but nonsense bought time, and time bought them a shared hour in a holding room while some clerk decided whether their statements matched. An hour was enough.

*My name is Sera Quill,* she pushed, once the door shut and the observation slit went dark. *I'm the one who tried to steal the queen's fingerbone. Yes, that one. No, I didn't get it. Yes, I know where it is. And yes—I think you already know what it's for.*

Kestrel Crane sat very still. Up close, without the gray film of the cell between them, she could see what the collar was doing to him: the way his fingers kept moving against his knee, tracing shapes, carving air. A man whose hands needed work and whose gift had been gagged. She knew that hunger. It was the same one that had driven her into the vault.

*You're wondering if I'm bait,* she went on. *Fair. Check my mind. Oh—you can't. Right.* She almost smiled. *Then check my face. I've been trained to lie since I was six years old, and I'm not lying now. That should count for something.*

"You're a thief," he said aloud, and his voice was rougher than she expected, scraped raw by disuse. "You broke into the most guarded building in the capital for a piece of bone the size of a thumbnail."

"For *the* piece of bone." She leaned forward. "Don't tell me you haven't figured it out. You heard the seal-fragment. You heard the king. Everyone says you did—that's why Vael keeps you breathing instead of burning you with the rest of us."

Something flickered across his face—pain, quickly filed away. *Careful,* she thought, watching it. *That's a wound, not a secret.* Aloud he said, "I appraised a fragment. I heard... something. Voices carry in bone. Sometimes they're old grief. Sometimes they're madness worn into the material." He said it like a man reciting a defense he'd prepared for a tribunal. Like a man who wanted very much to believe his own testimony.

"And sometimes," Sera said quietly, "they're kings."

The silence stretched. Through the pressure of his mind she felt him turn something over, huge and heavy, the way a man turns a stone to see what lives underneath. Then, barely audible: "If I agree to listen—if I *pretend* to listen—they'll move us somewhere worse. Somewhere with fewer doors."

"Somewhere with more doors," she corrected. "Prisons are built to keep people in. Vaults are built to keep things in. I've been in both, and I promise you, the second kind has better locks and worse guards, because nobody expects the inventory to walk out." She let that sit. "I have a way out. I need your help to use it. You need what I know. That's the whole bargain. Everything else is decoration."

"What do you need from a deaf bone-carver?"

"Your ears," she said, "when we get them back. And your hands. And the fact that you're the only person in this building the Inquisitor trusts enough to walk unshackled—which, incidentally, makes you the only person in this building who can get close enough to the vault to open a window for me."

* * *

They talked for the rest of the hour in the doubled language, aloud and beneath, and by the end of it Sera had given him the shape of the thing and watched him do what careful men do with dangerous shapes: measure them, weigh them, look for the flaw.

Here was the shape. The reliquary vault lay three levels down, behind the warded corridor with the green-burning torches. Its locks answered to a key-ring carried by the Under-Custodian, a soft man named Pell who took his supper at the same table every night and drank exactly enough to be slow. Sera had six weeks of his habits memorized. What she didn't have was a reason to be in the vault corridor at suppertime—and that was where Kestrel came in, because Vael had begun using him as an appraiser, dragging him from cell to strongroom to identify confiscated relics. All Sera needed was for Vael to send him down to the deep vault once. Once was enough. He would faint, or stumble, or drop something; she would be waiting in the laundry chute that fed the undercroft; and between his distraction and her lockwork, Relic 1-A would change custody.

"After that?" he'd asked.

"After that we're fugitives with a dead woman's finger and a story no one wants to hear. We find whoever else knows the old binding. We find the other bones. And we decide whether the screaming above the throne is worth starting a war over." She'd shrugged. "I never plan past the second door. Planning past the second door is how you die in the third room."

He'd looked at her then—not with warmth, not yet, but with the particular respect of one survivor recognizing another's craft. *You've done this before,* he'd pushed at her, though he couldn't hear her answer. *Been alone in a place designed to kill you.*

Her whole life, she hadn't said. Since the fever took my mother and the parish took our house and I learned that kindness is a currency and I was born bankrupt.

* * *

On the sixth night, the collar spoke.

Sera woke to a hum in her teeth, a vibration in the iron at her throat that had never been there before, and lay still in the dark, cataloguing. The hum rose and fell. Words, almost. Not quite words. Someone testing the bands remotely, or the bands themselves waking to some schedule she didn't know.

And beneath the hum, faint as breath on a winter window, she felt something new in the pressure of the dark. Not Kestrel—he was two floors up, she'd learned the shape of his sleeping mind by now, orderly even in dreams, ledgers all the way down. This was older. Heavier. A pressure like deep water, like something vast turning over in its sleep beneath the citadel's foundations.

It noticed her noticing. The pressure gathered, focused, and for one vertiginous moment Sera felt herself seen by something that had been watching this building for years, patient as rot.

*Little listener,* it seemed to say, though no words crossed the distance—only the certainty, cold and amused. *You should have stayed a thief.*

Then it withdrew, and the hum in her collar died, and Sera lay in the dark with her heart slamming and understood, with total clarity, two things.

First: the regent-thing knew she was here. Had perhaps always known. Her capture, her cell, her careful six weeks—it had watched all of it the way a cat watches a mouse run a maze, curious whether the maze had an exit.

Second: that meant the game was bigger than theft. Bigger than escape. Whatever she and Kestrel did next, they would be doing it inside the attention of the thing that wore the crown—and it had just told her, in its cold amused way, that it was willing to let them try.

*Why?* she thought into the dark, and got no answer, and didn't sleep again.

In the morning, the guards came early, and the order had changed. Not the interrogation room. Not the cells. Chains, and a wagon, and Inquisitor Vael herself on the loading step with a ledger under her arm, saying, "The regent requires an audience. Both of you. Wash your faces; you'll want to look presentable for the crown."

Beside Sera, Kestrel Crane went white to the lips. She felt his mind go narrow and sharp, a blade laid flat—and beneath the fear, unmistakable even through the iron, a single thought shaped so clearly she could almost hear it:

*The crown screams. Now you'll hear it too.*

Sera lifted her chin, met Vael's pale eyes, and smiled the smile of a woman with nothing left to lose and one card still hidden in her sleeve.

"Well," she said. "Lead on."
