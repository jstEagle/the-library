## 8. The Vault Beneath

The Inquisition kept its secrets the way a miser keeps coins: counted, catalogued, and buried where no one could spend them.

Sera had learned this from a laundry-woman who had learned it from a guard who had learned it from his own loose tongue after three cups of plum wine. The deep vault lay beneath the Bastion of the Quiet Hand, four levels down, past the interrogation cells and the records hall and the long gallery where confiscated relics were displayed like trophies in glass cases. But the true hoard—the bones too dangerous to show, too valuable to destroy—was sunk into the bedrock itself, behind a door of black iron that no single key opened.

"No single key," Sera murmured, crouched beside Kestrel in the shadow of a drainage arch as the last bell of compline faded across the Bastion courtyard. "Three keys. Three officers. Captain of the watch, Keeper of Relics, and—" she tapped her own chest, "—the Inquisitor himself."

"Then we're robbing Vael personally." Kestrel's voice came out flat through the mute-band at his throat. The collar sat against his skin like a slug of cold iron, and for eleven days it had been exactly what its name promised: silence. Not deafness—he still heard carts and bells and Sera's voice—but the dead were gone from him, muffled behind a wall he could feel but not breach. He had not realized until it was taken how much of his life had been spent in two rooms at once, the living world and the whispering one. Now he lived in only one, and it was lonelier than any cell.

"Not robbing," Sera said. "Reclaiming. There's a difference. It matters to me." She pulled her hood lower. In the dark she was all edges and patience, a hedge-witch built out of angles and stubbornness, and Kestrel had learned in eleven days of shared captivity and three days of shared flight that her patience was the most dangerous thing about her. "The fingerbone was mine first. I bled for it before they ever put you in chains."

"You told me. Twice."

"Because you keep looking at me like I'm going to apologize." She grinned, quick and wolfish. "I don't apologize. I itemize."

* * *

They went in through the water.

It was Sera's plan, because it was always Sera's plan when the plan involved something filthy, freezing, or fundamentally undignified. The Bastion's drains carried away the wash-water of two hundred soldiers and the blood-water of the cells below, and no one guarded a pipe that smelled like this one did. They waded it by candle-stub light, Kestrel with his trousers rolled to the knee and his boots slung around his neck, breathing through his mouth, counting arches the way the laundry-woman had counted them for Sera: seven arches, then the junction, then left where the water ran colder.

The cold water was a lie, or the laundry-woman's memory was. It ran warmer. Kestrel noticed it at the fourth arch—a faint heat against his shins, like standing downstream of a forge—and stopped so abruptly that Sera walked into his back.

"What?"

"Do you feel that?"

She put her hand in the current. Her face changed. "Warm," she said slowly. "Underground water doesn't run warm unless something under the ground is burning."

"Or listening." He said it without meaning to, and the words felt strange in his mouth, unaccompanied. For eleven days every observation had been a coin spent into silence; now he heard only his own voice, small and singular, and hated how much it sounded like a man talking to himself.

They pressed on. At the junction they climbed from the channel up an iron ladder slick with condensation, and emerged through a grate into a corridor that smelled of lamp oil and old stone and, beneath both, something else—something Kestrel knew the way a man knows the smell of his childhood house.

Bone. Old bone, stored bone, bone in quantity.

His whole body turned toward it like a compass needle. He caught himself with his hand flat against the wall, heart hammering, and Sera watched him with an expression he couldn't read in the dark.

"It's close," he said. "I can't hear it. But I can feel it. Like—" he searched. "Like standing outside a door with singing on the other side."

"That's either very beautiful or very grim."

"With the Inquisition? Both."

* * *

The Keeper of Relics was a fat, fastidious man named Corvel who took his supper at the same hour every night and slept, according to the laundry-woman, like a sack of turnips. His office stood between the records hall and the stair that descended to the vault door, and his keys hung on a hook beside his bed because he believed, sincerely, that thieves never robbed a room that smelled of onions.

He was wrong about the thieves. He was right about the onions; the smell reached them halfway down the corridor, and Sera gagged theatrically while Kestrel picked the simple lock on the office door.

Inside, the office was immaculate. Ledgers in ranked rows. A brass instrument for measuring skull circumference. A map of the capital with pins marking every registered gift, every burned name, every quiet disappearance—and Kestrel looked at those pins for one long breath, at the little clusters of them in the poor quarters, the riverside, the tanners' row, and made himself look away. Later. Grief later. Work now.

The keys were where the laundry-woman promised. Sera lifted the ring, weighed it, and frowned.

"Three keys," she said. "But which three?"

Kestrel took the ring from her. He couldn't hear the keys themselves—they were iron, not bone—but he remembered the vault door from the day they'd marched him past it in chains, remembered the way the guards had handled their copies with a particular reverence, kissing each key to the lock in sequence. The largest key would be the captain's. The smallest, worn smooth, the Keeper's. And the third—

The third was warm.

Not iron-warm. Warm the way the water had been warm, warm with something underneath, and when he closed his fist around it he felt the faintest tremor, like a pulse felt through a wrist.

"Sera."

"I see your face. What is it?"

"This key has been near the relics a long time." He turned it over. Stamped into the bow was the sigil of the Quiet Hand, and beneath it, so small he almost missed it, a maker's mark: a spire with a crack running down its length. "Someone made this who knew what the vault held. Someone who wanted whoever carried it to be able to find the right bone."

"An inside man?"

"Or a woman." He thought of Odile Voss, of her steady hands and her ledgers and the way she had said *you'll understand someday, Kestrel, why I do what must be done*, and pushed the thought down hard. "Doesn't matter. Come on."

* * *

The stair down was cut straight into bedrock, and the deeper they went, the more Kestrel felt it: pressure building behind the mute-band, a tide pressing against a dam. By the twentieth step his teeth ached. By the fortieth, the collar had begun to hum—not audibly, nothing Sera could hear, but he felt it in the bones of his jaw, a thin whine like a wet finger circling the rim of a glass.

"Your face is doing something alarming," Sera whispered.

"The collar knows where it is. Whatever's down there—it fights the band. Or the band fights it. Either way, we're close."

The black iron door filled the bottom of the stair like the entrance to a tomb, which, Kestrel thought, was precisely what it was. Three locks, set in a triangle. He kissed the large key to the top lock and turned it; the mechanism groaned like a waking animal. The second key, the worn one, went easier. Then the third, the warm one, and the moment its teeth seated in the lock, everything happened at once.

The humming became a scream.

Not sound—pressure. The mute-band clenched against his throat hard enough to bruise, fighting whatever rose up from below, and Kestrel dropped to one knee with both hands clawing at the iron. Through the gap between collar and skin he felt it pouring in anyway, a flood finding cracks in a wall: whispers, hundreds of them, thousands, a congregation of voices all speaking at once and all saying the same thing.

*Here. Here. Here.*

"They know we're coming," he gasped.

"Can they tell anyone?" Sera hauled him upright. "Turn the key!"

He turned it. The third lock clicked home, and the door swung inward on hinges that had been oiled recently and well—someone maintained this place, someone came down here often—and the sound that rolled out over them was not sound at all but a wind made of voices, and the mute-band cracked.

Not broke. Cracked—a single sharp line splitting the iron from clasp to clasp, hairline-fine, and through that line the dead came pouring back into Kestrel Crane's head like light through a split shutter.

He went down again. This time Sera let him.

It was too much. It was everything. Eleven days of silence and then all of it at once: the vault beyond the door was vast, a cavern hollowed from the living rock and shelved floor to ceiling with bones—skulls in rows like pottery, femurs bundled like firewood, ribs racked like laths, fingerbones strung on wire like beads—and every single one of them was awake, aware, screaming or singing or praying or simply saying *my name is* and then a name, and another name, and another, a litany of the confiscated dead that went on and on and on.

"*Kestrel.*" Sera's voice, distant, urgent. "*Breathe. You're bleeding.*"

He touched his nose. Blood. Of course. He wiped it and got up, swaying, and stepped through the door into the dark of the vault, and the voices changed.

They stopped screaming.

In its place: attention. Thousands of dead turning toward him the way sunflowers turn toward sun, and the pressure of that regard was worse than the noise, because he understood what it meant. They had been in the dark a long time. Some of these bones were old—centuries old, older than the guild, older than the binding—and not one of them had ever been carved, released, given rest. The Inquisition didn't destroy dangerous relics. It collected them. A prison with no warden's mercy, no ledger, no end.

"Oh," Kestrel said aloud, and his voice shook. "Oh, you poor things."

*Carver,* said the nearest skull, a woman's, yellowed with age. *Carver, carver. You've come at last.*

"I haven't come for you." The guilt arrived then, early and total, exactly as he'd known it would. "I—I'm sorry. I can't free you. Not tonight. Not yet."

*Then why come?*

"For one bone. One." He moved down the central aisle, Sera at his shoulder with the candle-stub, and everywhere he looked the shelves stretched away into darkness, and everywhere the voices rose softly around him like the surface of a lake disturbed by rain. *Carver. Carver. Remember me. My name was Aldous. My name was Betha. I was a dream-reader. I could make rain. I was nine years old, I was nine years old, I was—*

"Stop," he whispered. "Please."

And—this was the terrible part—they stopped. All of them. Ten thousand dead holding their breath for him, because he was the first person in years who could hear them, and they would not risk driving him away.

Sera touched his arm. "Which shelf?"

"The queen's fingerbone. If it's here, it'll be—" He closed his eyes and listened past the chorus, listening the way Odile had taught him: not for the loudest voice but for the oldest resonance, the deep note under the shallow ones. Royal bone rang differently. Seal-bone, coronation-bone, bone that had been sworn upon—it carried harmonics like a struck bell. "There. Left side. Deep shelf. Follow me."

* * *

The reliquary case stood apart from the common shelves, in a niche of its own, behind glass and iron filigree. Inside, on a cushion of rotted velvet, lay a single fingerbone no longer than Kestrel's thumb, browned with age, wrapped at the base with a thread of gold wire gone green.

Even before he touched the glass, the collar screamed.

The crack in the mute-band widened with an audible snap, iron flaking away from his throat in small burning scales, and sensation flooded in—not just hearing now but *feeling*, the bone's presence rolling over him like heat from an open oven. This was the thing the band had been built to smother. This was why the vault sat four levels underground behind three locks: not to keep thieves out, but to keep *this* muffled, this one specific voice, which even cased in glass and iron and stone still rang like a cathedral bell struck once a century.

Through the widening crack in the collar, Kestrel heard it speak.

It did not say its name. It said, in a woman's voice, dry and imperious and terribly tired:

*"At last. Someone with ears. Listen to me, boy, because I will only have strength to say this once: the crown remembers what it was. The crown remembers the hands that made it. Ask the guildmaster what she traded, and ask her what she took in payment—and do not trust the answer she gives you first."*

Kestrel froze with his hand on the case.

"Odile," he said. "Why would it say Odile?"

"Who's Odile?" Sera was already working the case's lock with two bent pins, quick and practiced. "Your guildmaster? The one who sold you?"

"She didn't sell me. She traded me. There's a difference." Even as he said it, he heard how it sounded. How it had always sounded. "She saved the others. Most of them."

"Mm." Sera's pins clicked. "My mother used to save most of our things from the debt collectors. We ate very well on what she saved." The lock gave. She lifted the glass away with the reverence she showed to nothing else in the world, and nodded at him. "Take it. Your hands, not mine. I stole it once and it burned me for a week. It wants a carver's touch."

He reached in. The moment his fingers closed around the fingerbone, the gold wire warmed against his skin, and the voice poured up his arm—not words now, just presence, vast and patient and *royal*, a queen's anger banked down to embers over decades, waiting.

And the collar shattered.

It went all at once, iron bursting from his throat in a spray of shards that rang against the stone floor, and the sound of it in the silent vault was enormous, and ten thousand dead voices cried out at once—not in alarm but in *recognition*, the sound of a bell rung in a churchyard, and Kestrel staggered under the flood of his own returning gift, whole and unmuted for the first time in eleven days, hearing everything, hearing *everyone*.

*The carver is unbound,* the vault whispered. *The carver is unbound.*

"We need to go," Sera said. "Now. That collar had a ward on it—I felt it die. Somewhere upstairs, someone just felt it too."

* * *

They ran.

Up the stair, through the onion-smelling office, down the drain-channel with the stolen relic wrapped in Kestrel's shirt against his chest, warm as a sleeping bird. Behind them, faintly, an alarm bell began to swing, and boots thundered on stone, and torchlight bloomed orange in the junction behind them.

But the vault had given them one gift more. As they fled, the voices followed—not physically, nothing so crude, but Kestrel felt them at his back like a crowd pressing toward a door, and wherever the pursuit passed close to the old bones stored along the drainage galleries, the dead *pushed*. A femur rolled under a guard's boot. A skull shifted on its shelf and toppled with a crack that brought two soldiers running the wrong way. Small treacheries. Tiny delays. The confiscated dead, spending their only currency on the first man who had ever heard them and called them *poor things*.

*Go, carver,* they whispered. *Go. And remember us. Remember our names.*

"I will," Kestrel gasped, splashing through the cold shallows. "I swear it on my craft. Every name. I'll carve every name."

He meant it. That was the worst of it. He meant it with his whole heart, and he knew, even as he swore, that he might die before he could keep it—that Sera and he were two people against a Bastion, that the guards behind them numbered in dozens, that somewhere above, Inquisitor Vael was waking to the news of a broken collar and would bring to bear on them an intelligence that did not sleep.

They came up out of the drains in the tannery district, stinking and soaked and alive, and Sera laughed—a wild, disbelieving bark of laughter—as she hauled him over the wall into the alley dark.

"The fingerbone," she said. "We actually have it. One of three."

"One of three." Kestrel pressed his hand flat against his chest, against the bundle, and the queen's voice pulsed there, faint and furious and patient. And beneath it, quieter, the words she had given him turning over and over like a stone in a stream:

*Ask the guildmaster what she traded. Do not trust the answer she gives you first.*

"Sera," he said. "When we get out of the city—we need to find Odile."

Sera went still against the wall, listening to the alarm bells fade behind them. "I thought she sold you to Vael."

"She did." He started walking, into the dark, toward the undercity gates. "And I'm starting to think that was the least of what she's sold."
