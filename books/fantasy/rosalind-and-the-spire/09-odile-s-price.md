## 9. Odile's Price

The undercity of Vhal Maren had been a city once, before the river changed its mind and swallowed the lower quarters whole. Now it was a drowned ribcage of streets: archways that had been doorways, staircases descending into black water, bridges built by people who had never imagined the water would rise to meet them. Sera Quill moved through it like she'd been born there, which, she admitted, she nearly had.

"Keep your hand on the wall," she said, three steps ahead with a shuttered lantern swinging at her hip. "Left fork is a dead end. Right fork is also a dead end, but the dead end has a ladder."

"And after the ladder?"

"After the ladder we stop being interesting to anyone."

Kestrel's boots found slick stone. Above them—somewhere far above, muffled through forty feet of brick and earth—the bells of the Inquisition were still ringing the hunt. They had been ringing since dawn, when the vault alarm finally died. His collar was cracked but not broken; he could feel the dead the way you feel weather through a window. Pressure without sound. It was maddening. It was mercy.

They had the fingerbone of the old queen wrapped in oilcloth against his chest, tied there like a second heart. Every few steps he thought he felt it pulse.

* * *

They surfaced in a cistern chamber where someone had once tried to make a home: rotted pallets, a child's shoe gone to lace, candle stubs fused to the floor in little wax graveyards. Sera lit a proper candle and they ate hard bread and older cheese, sitting on a dry ledge above water so still it held their reflections perfectly, two smudged ghosts looking up at them from below.

Kestrel caught himself doing arithmetic he hated. *How long before Vael thinks to check the guildhall? How long before Odile—*

"Stop," Sera said. She said it around a mouthful of bread, casual as commenting on the weather. "You're thinking about her."

"I'm thinking about everyone she didn't warn."

"You're thinking about whether she sold you." Sera brushed crumbs from her knees. "I've been thinking about it too. Want to hear my professional opinion?"

"That's what I keep you for."

"She didn't sell you today. Today she had no choice—Vael came with soldiers and a list, and one name on a list buys a hall full of lives. That's arithmetic any guildmaster would do." She looked at him steadily. "But Vael knew about the fingerbone, Kestrel. He knew it was coming to market. He knew the seller. That's not today's arithmetic. That's years of it."

Kestrel turned the queen's bone over in his mind. The fragment of royal seal-bone that had started everything had come to the guild for appraisal, wrapped in anonymity, paid for in coin nobody asked questions about. He had assumed a scavenger. A tomb-robber. Anyone.

He had never once assumed Odile.

"Say it plainly," he said.

Sera blew out a slow breath. "I think your guildmaster has been feeding the Inquisition for longer than either of us has been in trouble. And I think we should find out before we run anywhere she might know about."

* * *

Finding out meant going back toward the light, which meant going back toward the hunt, which meant Kestrel spent the next hour following a hedge-witch down corridors that smelled of river rot while every instinct screamed at him to go deeper instead. They emerged through a coal merchant's cellar into the Warrens, the crooked district where the guildhall itself stood, and the first thing Kestrel saw was smoke.

Not the guildhall. Not yet. But close—a chandler's shop on Guildmaster's Row, gutted, Inquisition troopers picking through the ashes with rakes like men harvesting a field.

"They're burning the street," Sera breathed. "House by house."

"Not the street." Kestrel's stomach dropped through the wet dark beneath him. "The ledger. They're looking for names. They're burning everything Odile ever—" He stopped.

Everything Odile ever kept.

* * *

They watched from a rooftop across the Row, flat on their bellies behind a chimney stack furred with moss. Below, an Inquisitor in grey walked the line of troopers with a list in his hand, and each time he read a name aloud, a house was marked with white chalk and left standing—for later. Each time he shook his head, the house burned.

"He's not hunting the gifted on this street," Kestrel whispered. "Look. He skips the dyer's—half the dyer's family are hedge-touched, everyone knows it. He skips the midwife. He's only burning places Odile *kept things*."

"Or places she told him to burn."

The thought arrived between them like a knife laid on a table.

They waited through the long grey afternoon until the troopers withdrew, until the Inquisitor folded his list away with the crisp satisfaction of a man completing a task, until the smoke thinned and the street filled with the dazed, bucket-carrying grief of neighbors. Then they went down, and went in—not through the guildhall's front doors, which stood open and empty and wrong, but through the bone-carver's entrance below, the one only guild members knew, the one Kestrel had used for eleven years.

The workshop was untouched. Of course it was. It was exactly the kind of place the list would have spared.

Odile Voss was sitting at the master's desk in the back room, very still, in the posture of a woman who had been sitting there a long time. Her silver hair was unbound, which Kestrel had never seen. On the desk before her lay the guild's remaining ledgers, stacked neat as coffins, and beside them a single sheet of paper covered in her small, precise handwriting.

She didn't startle when they came in. She had heard them; she always heard everyone; that was half of what a guildmaster was.

"Kestrel," she said. Her voice was steady. Her hands were not. "And the Quill girl. Sit down. Both of you. There are things you should hear from me rather than from ash."

* * *

Sera did not sit. She put her back to the door and her hand somewhere near the knife at her belt, and Kestrel understood that whatever happened next, Sera had already decided whose side of the desk she was standing on. He stayed standing too. It felt important, suddenly, not to be seated across from Odile like a client, like a subordinate, like a student. Not anymore.

"The paper," Kestrel said.

"In a moment." Odile folded her hands. For eleven years those hands had taught him to read grain, to listen through ivory, to balance impossible accounts against impossible debts. He had loved those hands the way you love a wall that has held up your roof through every storm. "Ask me first. You've earned the asking."

"Did you send the seal-bone to the guild?"

"Yes."

"Were you the anonymous buyer? The one who moved the queen's fingerbone toward market?"

"Yes."

"Have you been feeding Vael information?" His voice came out flat and strange, a stranger's voice. "Names. Movements. The binding."

Odile closed her eyes. When she opened them again they were dry, and that was somehow worse than tears. "For nineteen years," she said. "Since before you carved your first sparrow. Since before the king died—or didn't. Yes, Kestrel. Everything you're about to ask. All of it. Me."

The room tilted. He gripped the edge of a workbench and the tools rattled, and distantly he registered that his hands were shaking, and that the cracked collar was letting something bleed through the silence—the bench itself, old oak, old iron, whispering its dumb metal dreams—and even that felt like betrayal, because the last thing he wanted was to hear anything right now except her answer.

"Why," Sera said, from the door. Not a question. A demand wearing a question's clothes.

"Because I was there." Odile rose, slowly, joints cracking like green wood, and crossed to the tall cabinet where the guild kept its true treasures. She unlocked it with a key from around her neck—the master key, Kestrel realized, the one that opened everything—and took out a box of black wood that Kestrel had dusted a hundred times and never once seen opened. "Because nineteen years ago I was the crown's bone-carver. Before the guild. Before any of this. I bound voices for the court because the court paid, and because I told myself the voices were criminals, traitors, monsters, and that bone was kinder than rope." She set the box on the desk. "And then the regent asked me to bind a king."

The words fell into the room and did not echo. Even the building seemed to hold its breath.

"There was a plot," Odile said. "There is always a plot; you know this; you read the old ledgers. The king was to die, quietly, and a regency would follow, and the regent—who was not then what it is now, or perhaps it always was, I have stopped being able to tell—wanted insurance. A king's voice, bound into the crown itself. Proof of death that could never be buried or exhumed. A binding so deep the man would be *alive*, Kestrel. Alive and screaming, forever, where only the right listener could ever hear him." Her jaw tightened. "Three bones, the ritual required. A fingerbone of the queen who loved him. The sternum of the saint who crowned him. And the royal seal-bone, broken from his own signet ring, pressed to his lips at the last. I made two of the three. I carved the queen's fingerbone into its vessel myself, in this city, in a workshop smaller than this one. And then I ran. I took the third bone—the seal-bone—and I ran, because even then, even drunk on court gold, some part of me knew what we were making was not a prison. It was a throne."

Kestrel's mouth was dry. "You hid it in the guild."

"I founded the guild to hide it." She laughed, short and terrible. "A hundred gifted souls, a hall of secrets, a ledger of impossible things—all of it, all these years, a shell around one bone. Every rule I ever taught you, every lesson about concealment and quiet and never being interesting—that was never philosophy, boy. That was camouflage. I built a herd of rare animals around myself so the hunters would never look too closely at the one thing I carried."

"And the seal-bone fragment," Kestrel said. "The one that came for appraisal. The one that let me *hear him*. That was you."

"I needed to know if anyone alive could hear the king through a vessel. If the binding was weakening. If there was—" For the first time her voice frayed. "If there was anyone who could undo what I helped make. And there was. There was my quiet, careful, brilliant carver, who heard a dead man scream and came to me shaking, and I sent him straight into the regent's reach, because the alternative was carrying it alone until I died." She looked at him, and her eyes were the eyes of a woman standing at the bottom of a well she had dug herself. "Vael has known about the binding for years. I told him enough to keep the guild alive—names, small names, carvers who could flee, listeners who could hide. Never the bones themselves. But when the fingerbone surfaced, when your gift touched it, the arithmetic changed. He gave me a choice: the guild, or the secret. And I chose—" She spread her hands. "You see what I chose. I am choosing it still. I will choose it until they burn me."

"You sold the original binding's secret," Sera said quietly, "a long time ago. Didn't you. Not the bones. The *method*. How a voice is bound into a crown. What it takes. What it costs."

Odile did not deny it.

"That's how the regent renews itself," Kestrel whispered. The horror assembled itself in him piece by piece, cold and complete. "That's why the rite exists. You taught the thing on the throne how to stay worn."

"I taught it how to survive," Odile said. "And it has survived very well. And now it knows the last two vessels are loose in my city, and it knows who trained the boy carrying them, and by tomorrow morning it will know everything else, because I am going to tell it." She lifted the sheet of paper from the desk—the small precise handwriting, the list. Kestrel saw his own name on it. He saw Sera's. He saw the drowned monastery written in a margin, and Saint Aldric, and the word *sternum* underlined twice. "This goes to Vael tonight. Unless."

"Unless what?" Sera's knife was fully in her hand now.

"Unless you kill me first," Odile said simply. "Which would be just, and would change nothing, because Vael already knows I'm the source and already suspects I've turned. Or unless you take the master key, and the ledgers, and the map I'm going to give you, and you finish what I started nineteen years ago in the opposite direction. Unbind him. All of them. Every voice I ever helped shut into bone." She pushed the key across the desk. It slid the way all keys slide, ordinary and final. "I don't ask forgiveness. I was a guildmaster of impossible things; I know the price lists better than anyone. I ask for the work. Give the work to someone whose hands shake less than mine."

Nobody moved. Somewhere above them, faintly, the bells began again—not the hunt this time, but the hour, six heavy strokes rolling down through the bones of the building, and Kestrel thought: *even the bells are skeletons, even the bells are dead men's voices, hung in bronze and made to speak.*

His hand closed over the key. It was warm from her neck. He hated how much that mattered to him, and he took it anyway.

"The monastery," he said hoarsely. "The monks. You wrote it down for Vael."

"I wrote it down for *you*," Odile said. "It's the same page. It has always been the same page. That is the whole of my confession, Kestrel Crane, and it is shorter than it deserves to be." She sat back down at her desk, straightened the ledgers that no longer had names in them, and became, deliberately, a woman waiting to be arrested. "Go. Out the carver's door, down, and keep going. There's a blind man in Tanner's Reach who reads bone the way I read faces—he'll want payment in stories, and he'll tell you things about the sternum that will keep you awake. Tell him Odile sent you. Tell him she's finally paying her debts."

Kestrel got as far as the doorway. Then he turned, because eleven years deserved at least that.

"Was any of it real?" he asked. "The guild. Teaching me. Any of it?"

Odile Voss smiled, and for one moment she was entirely the woman who had taught him to listen, and the grief in Kestrel's chest tore cleanly down the middle.

"All of it was real," she said. "That's what makes it unforgivable. Go, boy. Be impossible."

They went down through the carver's door with the key against Kestrel's heart beside the queen's fingerbone, two relics of two different betrayals, beating together. Behind them the guildhall stood silent, already belonging to the past. Ahead, the undercity waited, black water rising in the dark, and somewhere in Tanner's Reach a blind man was keeping a map of every bone in the kingdom—including, if Odile's margins told the truth, the one guarded by monks who had been praying for nineteen years without permission to stop.
