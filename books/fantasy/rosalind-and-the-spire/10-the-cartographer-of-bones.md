## 10. The Cartographer of Bones

The mapmaker lived above a chandler's shop in the Tallow Quarter, where the air was thick enough to chew and every window had been painted black since the Proclamation in Ash. Sera climbed the stairs behind Kestrel, one hand on the wall, the other closed around the queen's fingerbone in her pocket. It had not stopped whispering since the vault. She'd begun to think of it as a companion she couldn't shake—a small, cold voice murmuring about gardens and grief and a name that might have been hers, once, if names could be inherited like debts.

Kestrel knocked the pattern Odile's key had shown them: three slow, two fast, one slow.

"Odile Voss is dead," said a voice from within. It was dry as old paper dragged over stone. "If you've come to rob me, do it quietly. If you've come to weep, do it elsewhere."

"We've come for a map," Sera said.

A long pause. Then: "Everyone comes for a map. Come in, then, before the tallow-sellers see your faces. They gossip worse than candles burn."

* * *

The room inside was nothing like Sera expected.

She had imagined scrolls, inkpots, the cluttered chaos of a scholar's den. Instead the walls were lined with bones—hundreds of them, thousands, hung on cords of sinew from ceiling to floor so that the whole chamber breathed when they entered, swaying and clicking in a slow tide. Femurs and ribs, jawbones and knucklebones, bird skulls no bigger than thumbnails and the great curved rib of something Sera didn't want to name. Each bone had been scored with fine lines, dense as scripture, and each one turned—slowly, gently—as if reading itself.

In the center sat an old man at a table worn smooth as riverstone. His eyes were white as boiled eggs, filmed over entirely, and he did not turn his face toward them when they came in. He turned his hands instead, palms up, listening with his skin.

"You brought bone," he said. "Old bone. Royal." His nostrils flared. "And you—" the palms swung toward Sera "—you're carrying it against your heart like a stolen child. Sit down before you drop it."

"How did you know—" Sera began.

"I know everything bone knows," the mapmaker said. "I am Maren Corvus. I was the Guild's cartographer for forty years, back when the Guild still trusted its own. Sit. Both of you. The tall one breathes like a man who hasn't slept in a week, and the little one breathes like a woman who's been running since the day she was born. Neither of you will fall over standing."

Sera sat. Kestrel remained on his feet a moment longer, and she watched him take in the hanging bones the way a drowning man takes in water—with hunger and horror in equal measure. Without the collar, she knew, he would be hearing all of them at once. Every scored line a voice. Every voice a life. Even muffled by the cracked band still locked around his throat, he went pale as whey.

"They don't scream," Maren said mildly, to Kestrel. "Not in here. I score them to sleep first. A kindness I learned from your guildmaster, before she learned to sell people instead of save them." He folded his hands. "Now. You want the second bone. Don't bother looking surprised; the fingerbone has been singing since you crossed my threshold, and it sings of its siblings. Bones miss their family. Everything does."

"The sternum of Saint Aldric," Kestrel said hoarsely. "Do you know where it is?"

Maren Corvus smiled, and it was not a comforting sight. "Child, I know where *everything* is. That is what a cartographer is—not a maker of maps, but a keeper of places. Places remember. Bone remembers better." He rose, joints cracking like green wood, and walked into the hanging forest of relics without touching a single cord. His fingers found what they sought by some sense Sera couldn't fathom: a long bone, dense and yellowed, carved edge to edge with lines finer than hair.

He carried it back and laid it across the table between them.

"This is the radius of a pilgrim who walked the Salt Road in the reign of Aldric himself. He drowned with the monastery. They all drowned with the monastery." He tapped the bone with one nail, and the sound it made was wrong—not the knock of dead matter but a low, resonant tone, like a bell underwater. "Touch it, hearer. Tell me what you feel."

Kestrel hesitated. Then he laid two fingers on the radius, and Sera watched his whole body change—the way a man changes when a door he thought bricked-over swings open onto a cathedral. His lips parted. His eyes went somewhere far away and very deep.

"It's wet," he whispered. "It's—it's still wet. After two hundred years, this bone is still *wet*."

"Good," said Maren. "Now listen deeper."

Kestrel listened. Sera heard it too, faintly, through the hedge-witch's channel she'd opened between them on the road—a thin thread of shared perception that let her borrow his senses when she dared. What came through made her stomach lurch: water, endless and dark, pressing down; and beneath the water, voices. Hundreds of them. Thousands. Not screaming. Not wailing. *Chanting.* A psalm in Old Liturgical, rising from the drowned dark in perfect unison, patient as sediment, endless as tide.

She pulled back, gasping. Kestrel didn't. He stayed under for a long time, and when he surfaced his face was streaked with tears he hadn't noticed falling.

"What is it?" Sera asked. "What did you hear?"

"Aldric's Monastery," Kestrel said. "It's real. It sank—they say the sea took it, but the sea didn't take it. The binding took it. Someone pulled the whole monastery *down*, whole and entire, monks and stones and chapel bells, and pinned it under the water, and the monks are still—" his voice cracked— "they're still praying. They never stopped. Two hundred years and they never stopped, because whoever bound them caught them mid-prayer and the prayer can't finish. It can't ever finish. That's the lock. The psalm is the lock."

Maren nodded slowly, like a man confirming a diagnosis he'd already written. "The Drowned Choir," he said. "Yes. That is what we called it, in the years when there were still people alive who remembered such things. The regent-thing's makers were thorough. When they bound the king, they hid the pieces of their working in places no living soul could reach—and they salted each hiding place with guards who cannot die, cannot rest, and cannot stop." He spread his scarred hands over the pilgrim's radius. "You have felt the first guard. Now you will meet the second."

* * *

Maren drew the map while they watched, and Sera would remember the making of it for the rest of her life.

He did not use ink. He took a fresh sheet of vellum, weighted its corners, and then—working entirely by touch—he began to lay bones upon it. Small ones first: fish vertebrae scattered in an arc that became, unmistakably, the coastline of the Sundered Reach. A curl of shell for the bay. A sliver of driftwood for the river mouth. And then, moving his hands with the certainty of a man setting a table he'd set ten thousand times, he placed a single vertebra at the center of the bay and pressed it down until it stuck, and around it he arranged six smaller bones in a ring, and the shape they made together was a building. A cloister. A church seen from above, drowned beneath the ghost of water the bones themselves remembered.

"There," Maren said softly. "There it is. As true as the day it sank. The sea has shifted the sands, but the bones haven't moved, because the bones *can't* move. Nothing down there moves except the prayer."

Sera leaned close. The vertebra at the center was scored with lines, and now that she looked—really looked—she saw that the lines weren't random. They were words. The same psalm, over and over, spiraling inward toward the bone's heart, and at the very center, in letters smaller than a grain of rice, a single phrase repeated:

*Domine, exaudi orationem meam.*

Lord, hear my prayer.

"And the sternum?" Kestrel asked. His voice was rough. "Where in the church?"

"Where it always is," Maren said. "Under the altar. The heart of the saint beneath the heart of the church, beneath the heart of the sea. The binding put it there deliberately—the sternum is the keystone of the working. Remove it and the choir falls silent, the waters lose their grip, and the second bone is yours." He paused. "I will tell you also what I would tell any client, which is that no client has ever come back to ask why I warned them. Because none of them survived to ask."

"Tell us anyway," Sera said.

Maren turned his ruined eyes toward her, and though they saw nothing, she felt seen through to the marrow.

"The monks are not ghosts the way you understand ghosts," he said. "Ghosts linger. Ghosts grieve. These do neither. They were bound *in the act of prayer*—caught between one word and the next, suspended forever in the most sacred moment of their lives. Do you understand what that means, hedge-witch? It means they are not suffering. It means they are *happy*. They are held forever in the arms of the thing they loved best, and they will defend that moment with everything they have, because to them it is not a prison. It is paradise." He let that settle. "You cannot frighten them. You cannot bargain with fear, because they have none. And when you reach for their saint's heart, they will not rage at you. They will *sing at you*. And their song will pull the water into your lungs as gently as a mother pulling a blanket over a sleeping child, and you will drown in perfect peace, loving every moment of it, and that, hearers, is the cruelest weapon I have ever mapped."

The silence after his voice stopped was total. Even the hanging bones seemed to have gone still, as if the whole room were holding its breath.

Sera found she had gone cold all over. She had faced Inquisitors. She had faced the vault's wards and the undercity's knives and her own certain death more times than she could count, and she had always been able to find the fear's handle, the way you grip a blade. But this—drowning happy, dying grateful, killed by kindness—

"That's monstrous," she said quietly.

"Yes," Maren agreed. "That is rather the point. Whoever built the crown understood that the strongest cage is the one the prisoner loves. Remember that, when you stand before the third bone. Remember that the king may not wish to leave it."

Kestrel's head came up sharply. "You know about the king."

"I know about everything bone knows," Maren said, "and the seal-bone fragment that passed through this city last month sang for three nights before it reached your guildmaster's table. A king bound alive. Yes. I know." He gathered the bones off the vellum one by one, returning each to its cord, and left the map lying bare between them—coastline and cloister rendered in absence, in the shapes things had left behind. "Take it. Take the pilgrim's radius too; it will open doors below that no key can reach. The drowned recognize their own."

"Why help us?" Sera asked. It was the question she should have asked an hour ago, and she hated herself for the delay. "You knew Odile. Odile sold the Guild out years ago. Everyone who helps us dies or betrays us. So why?"

Maren's hands stilled among the hanging bones.

"Because forty years ago, Odile Voss and I stood where you are standing," he said. "Young, gifted, furious, certain we could unmake the crown. And she looked at the scale of it—all of this, the choir, the guards, the centuries of careful, loving cruelty—and she decided it was too large to fight and too useful to destroy. She chose survival. I chose memory." He touched the nearest femur, almost tenderly. "Someone had to keep the map. Someone had to wait for the ones who wouldn't choose survival." The ruined eyes found Kestrel. "You broke her collar in the vault, hearer. The collar she wore for thirty years without knowing it was a collar. Whatever else she was, whatever she sold—she kept you hidden until you were ready. Whether that was mercy or strategy, even her bones won't tell me now. But I think—I *think*—it was both."

Kestrel said nothing. His jaw worked once, twice. Sera reached under the table and squeezed his wrist, and he covered her hand with his own and held on like a man holding a rope over a long drop.

* * *

They left before dawn, taking the map wrapped in oilcloth and the pilgrim's radius in Kestrel's pack, and Maren Corvus stood in his doorway of clicking bones and gave them his final gift.

"The tide gate at Grayhaven opens at slack water, three nights hence," he said. "There is a salvage-diver named Brannock who owes the Guild a debt older than either of you. He'll take you down as far as divers go. Past that—" the white eyes tilted upward, toward nothing, toward the weight of the sea waiting beyond the walls "—past that, you go alone, and you go down into a church where every soul in the pews will love you to death."

He turned back inside. The bones began to sway again behind him, breathing their slow tide, and the door closed.

They walked through the Tallow Quarter in silence, past shuttered windows and painted glass, and it was Sera who finally spoke, because someone had to.

"He's right," she said. "About the scale of it. Kestrel, I felt what you felt through that radius. I heard the choir. Two hundred years of unbroken prayer, thousands of voices, and that's just the *second* bone. There's a third, and it's on the king's head, guarded by whatever the regent-thing is, and we're—what? A bone-hearer with a cracked collar and a hedge-witch with a stolen relic and a map drawn in fishbones?" She laughed, and it came out wrong, high and thin. "We're going to drown in paradise and the kingdom will never even know we tried."

Kestrel stopped walking. In the gray pre-dawn light, with the cracked mute-band dull at his throat, he looked less like a man than like a knife someone had tried to break and failed.

"When I touched that radius," he said slowly, "the monks' song wasn't just a lock. I heard the words underneath the words. *Domine, exaudi orationem meam.* Lord, hear my prayer." He met her eyes. "Sera—for two hundred years, nobody has. That's the whole of it. They pray and pray and no one hears, because everyone who could hear them died or ran away. Except us." A ghost of the fierce, quiet thing she'd first seen in him in the vault kindled behind his exhaustion. "We're the only two people in the world who can actually *hear* them. Maybe that's not a weapon pointed at us. Maybe that's the one thing they've been waiting for."

Sera stared at him. Then, despite everything—the terror, the cold, the sea waiting three nights away—she started to laugh, really laugh, for the first time since the vault.

"You want to walk into a death-trap full of blissful drowned monks," she said, "and *answer their prayers*."

"I want to free them," Kestrel said. "And then I want the sternum. In that order. It matters which order."

They walked on toward Grayhaven as the sky went from ash to iron, and behind them, high above the chandler's shop, in a room full of sleeping voices, the cartographer of bones stood alone among his hanging dead and listened to the two of them fade down the street—one heartbeat quick with fear, one steady as a psalm—and permitted himself, for the first time in forty years, to hope.

Three nights, Sera thought. Three nights until the tide gate opened, and the sea, and the choir, and the church that had been waiting, patient as sediment, for someone to finally, finally hear it.

She only prayed they'd be ready for what answered.
