## 11. Drowned Prayers

The monastery had been drowning for two hundred years, and it had not yet finished.

Kestrel felt it before he saw it—a pressure in his chest like a held breath, a hum beneath the water that was not water-sound at all. The collar's crack had given him back perhaps a quarter of his hearing, and even that quarter strained against what waited below: forty voices, maybe fifty, singing in a key no living throat could hold.

"Saint Aldric's Rest," Sera said, standing at the lip of the sinkhole with her arms crossed against the morning cold. Below them, the flooded nave spread out like a drowned cathedral's ghost—arches rising from green-black water, their tops furred with weed, their windows still holding fragments of colored glass that turned the sunlight into scattered jewels on the surface. "The mapmaker wasn't lying about the depth."

"He rarely does." Kestrel knelt and tested the water with two fingers. Cold enough to stop a heart in an hour. He looked at Sera. "You don't have to come."

"You keep saying that." She was already untying her boots. "You keep being wrong."

He had learned, in the weeks since the vault, that arguing with Sera Quill was a form of prayer she did not answer. So he said nothing, and began to strip down to his shirt, and tried not to think about what waited below.

Not the water. The water he understood. It was the singing he feared—the chorus of monks who had been bound mid-prayer, mid-*verse*, their last breaths caught in stone and relic and turned into a wheel that turned and turned and never finished the psalm. The cartographer had warned him: *They will not let you take him. They have been guarding him so long they have forgotten they were ever anything else.*

Kestrel had spent his life listening to the dead. He had never yet had to ask them for something they did not want to give.

* * *

They went down on a rope, into water that bit like teeth.

The light died fast. Ten feet down, the jewel-scatter of sun gave way to a brown murk; twenty feet, and Kestrel was swimming by feel and by the faint glow of the witch-lamp Sera had sealed in wax against her palm—a hedge-craft trick, a firefly's worth of light that would burn underwater because it burned on something other than fire.

The nave opened around them like the inside of a bell.

And there—there it was. The singing.

It came through the water not as sound but as *pressure*, each note a hand pressing against his eardrums, his ribs, the backs of his eyes. Even mangled by the cracked collar, even filtered through fathoms of flood, the chorus was vast. Forty men, the mapmaker had said, bound in the hour the monastery fell, when the regent-thing's agents had come for Saint Aldric's bones and found the monks kneeling in the dark, refusing to give them up.

So the agents had done what the regent-thing always did with things it could not take.

It had made them part of the taking.

Kestrel hung in the green dark, weightless, and let the song pour through him. And slowly, painfully, the way a man learns a language from a fever dream, he began to hear the words beneath the music.

*—and we shall not be moved, and we shall not be moved, and we shall not—*

Over and over. Two hundred years of over and over. Not a prayer anymore. A wall.

*Sera.* He reached for her through the link they'd discovered in the vault, thought-shape to thought-shape, clumsy as a child's drawing. *Can you hear them?*

*A feeling,* she sent back. Her thought-voice was sharp-edged, practical, a knife wrapped in cloth. *Like standing too close to a bell. What are they saying?*

*That they won't move.*

*Then we move them.*

But it was not so simple, and he knew it, and when his feet found the flooded floor of the nave—stone slick with silt, tilted toward the collapsed apse—he knew something else too. Something that stopped him where he stood.

The monks were not gone.

They were *here*. All around him in the murk, shapes that were not quite shapes, kneeling rows of them stretching back into the dark, robed figures of pale absence, and every one of them was singing with its mouth open, and every one of them was looking at him.

* * *

He had carved ten thousand voices out of bone and ivory. He had heard drowned girls and murdered kings and children who died asking for their mothers. He had thought himself past flinching.

He flinched.

Because this was different. These dead were not whispers trapped in material. They were *choir*, they were *congregation*, they were a single organism of grief with forty heads and one throat, and when Kestrel Crane stood dripping in their midst with murder in his errand, the song changed.

The words shifted.

*—who comes, who comes, who comes into the house of the Lord—*

*I do,* Kestrel answered, and did not know at first whether he had spoken aloud or only thought it loudly enough. In this place, it hardly mattered. *My name is Kestrel Crane. I am a bone-carver. I hear the dead.*

The silence that followed was worse than the singing.

Forty faces turned toward him in the dark, and the water itself seemed to lean in, and Sera's hand found his arm in the blackness—she couldn't see them, he realized, couldn't see any of it—and her grip said: *what is happening, what is happening, tell me what is happening.*

*They're listening,* he sent. *God help me, they're waiting.*

Then the oldest of the shapes rose. It was taller than the others, or seemed so, and its robes were rotted to ribbons, and where its hands should have been there were only suggestions of hands, cupped together in the attitude of a man holding something precious—or begging.

*You are the one who frees,* it said. Not aloud. Through the water, through the stone, through the collar's broken lattice, straight into the marrow of him. Its voice was all forty voices braided into one, and underneath the braid Kestrel could hear the separate strands: fear, devotion, exhaustion beyond the reach of language. *We know your kind. The carvers came once, long ago. They took our brother Anselm's voice for a music box.*

Kestrel's stomach turned. He knew that story. He had *told* that story, in the guildhall, as a cautionary tale about the old days before Odile's rules. He had never imagined meeting the proof.

"I'm not here to take," he said aloud, and the words came out as bubbles, and somehow the monks heard them anyway. "I'm here to give."

*Give.* The word moved through the chorus like wind through wheat. *No one gives. The ones with crowns take. The ones with knives take. The ones with kind voices take most of all.*

"I know." His chest ached. The water was freezing and he had been under too long and none of that mattered beside the ache. "I've been taken from myself. Recently. By the same crown that did this to you." He paused. "I'm going to ask you for something. And I want you to understand, before I ask, that I will not lie to you. Lying to the dead is the one sin my guild punishes with expulsion. We keep ledgers. Every voice we free goes in the ledger. Every debt we owe gets written down where we can't pretend we never owed it."

The eldest monk drifted closer. Close enough now that Kestrel could see the hole in its chest—not a wound, exactly. A *missing*. A shape of absence where something had been taken, or given away, or hidden.

*Ask,* it said.

"The regent-thing wants Saint Aldric's bones. Has wanted them for two hundred years. Your brothers died refusing." He swallowed. "I'm asking you to let me succeed where they failed. Because Aldric's sternum is the second bone of the binding—the binding that holds a true king prisoner inside a crown while a thing wears his face. Free me to take it, and I swear to you on my ledger, on my gift, on whatever is left of my soul when this is done: I will come back. I will unbind every one of you. You will finish the psalm. You will rest."

Silence again. But it was a different silence now—the silence of deliberation, of forty minds that had not been asked a question in two centuries suddenly being asked the largest one of their existence.

And then, from somewhere deep in the chorus, a single voice broke from the braid. Young. Frightened. *Brother Anselm's* voice, Kestrel realized, the stolen-and-returned echo of a music box's ghost.

*How do we know? How do we know you'll come back? Everyone says they'll come back.*

Kestrel closed his eyes.

"They don't," he said. "You're right. They don't. People say they'll come back and then the road is long and the world is loud and the dead are easy to forget, because forgetting is how the living survive." He opened his eyes. "I can't prove it to you. I can only offer you the only collateral I have."

*Which is?*

"My hearing. My gift. If I break my oath, may I go deaf to every voice in every bone until the day I die. May I carve ivory for the rest of my life and hear nothing but my own hands. That is not a curse to you. I know that. But to me—" His voice cracked, and he let it. "To me it would be losing the only country I've ever belonged to."

The young monk's shape wavered. And the eldest raised its suggestion-of-hands, and the whole chorus breathed—one vast, watery inhalation, two hundred years compressed into a single drawn breath—

And Sera screamed.

* * *

She screamed in his mind first, and then, a heartbeat later, with her actual throat, and the sound came through the water warped and terrible: *KESTREL. THE DOOR.*

He spun. At the far end of the nave, where the collapse had opened the monastery to the sinkhole above, the rope they'd anchored was moving. Not swaying. *Moving*—hauling upward, fast, by hands they could not see.

Vael's divers. They had followed. Of course they had followed; Vael always followed; Vael was a man who treated other people's escapes as personal correspondence requiring a reply.

The plan had been simple, the way plans are simple right up until they aren't: dive, negotiate, take the sternum, ascend. Sera had insisted on anchoring herself at the breach—not out of distrust, she'd said, but because someone had to hold the door, and hedge-witches held things better than bone-carvers did. She'd wedged herself into the gap between two fallen arches, braced against the current of their own bubbles, ready to collapse the loose masonry behind them if pursuit came.

Pursuit had come. And now she was collapsing the door with herself half inside it.

*Kestrel—* Her thought-voice tore like fabric. *I can't hold it and swim. The stones are coming down, I triggered the fall, I can't—*

The eldest monk was still reaching toward him, the bargain hanging unfinished in the drowned air, and Kestrel stood at the exact center of the choice the mapmaker had promised him, the one he'd pretended wouldn't really happen:

The dead, or the living. The oath, or the woman. Finish the negotiation that two hundred years of patience deserved—or abandon it, possibly forever, because Sera Quill was about to be buried under a hundred tons of consecrated masonry and no amount of freedom for forty ghosts would weigh against that.

*Go,* the eldest monk said, and its voice was terribly gentle. *We have waited two hundred years. We can wait while you save your friend. That is what monks are for.*

"No." He said it aloud, bubbles streaming. "No, you misunderstand. I'm not choosing between you. I'm *refusing* to choose."

He did the impossible thing, the thing Odile would have called reckless and Sera would have called typical and the guild's ledgers had no column for. He reached out with both hands—to the chorus and to Sera at once—and he *pulled*.

To Sera he sent everything: the layout of the falling arches as the monks' ancient knowledge painted it in his mind, the load-bearing pillar, the gap, the gap, *the gap on your left, roll left, NOW—*

And to the monks he gave the only thing he had left to give. He opened his gift all the way, wider than he'd ever dared, past the cracked collar's limits, past safety, and he let them *in*—let forty bound souls pour through his hearing and out through his voice, and he sang for them.

Badly. Off-key, breathless, underwater, in a dead liturgical tongue he barely knew. But he sang their own psalm back to them—the verses after the verse they were stuck on, the *ending*, the doxology they had been denied for two centuries—and the effect was lightning in deep water.

The chorus *shuddered*. Forty shapes convulsed as if struck, mouths opening wider, the endless wheel of their binding catching—for one glorious, terrible moment—on the next line. *—and we shall not be moved, and we shall not—* and then, ragged, astonished, *we shall rise—*

The stones above Sera stopped falling.

Not gently. They stopped the way a fist stops mid-swing—held, trembling, straining against their own weight. Forty ghosts, half-unbound, pouring two hundred years of discipline into a single act of refusal: *we shall not fall either.*

*MOVE,* Kestrel screamed at Sera, and she moved—rolled left through the gap he'd shown her as a slab the size of a coffin slammed down where she'd been, missing her by the width of a psalm. The impact shook the whole nave. Silt exploded outward in a blinding cloud.

And through the cloud, gasping, bleeding from the ear where a chip of stone had clipped her, Sera Quill swam to him and grabbed his face in both hands and shouted soundlessly: *you absolute lunatic, you absolute—*

*Later,* he sent. *Bargain first.*

* * *

The eldest monk hovered before him, and the hole in its chest had changed. Where the absence had been, there was now a faint light, guttering like a candle in a draft—the light of a soul that had remembered, for one moment, what finishing felt like.

*You sang us the end of the psalm,* it said. *Two hundred years we begged heaven for the ending, and it came from a thief in a wet shirt.*

"Not a thief. A ledger-keeper." Kestrel's lungs were burning. There was no more time and they both knew it. "The sternum. Please."

The monks parted. Down the kneeling rows, through the blinding silt, at the heart of the apse beneath the shattered altar, something glowed—the same faint candlelight as the elder's wound, but stronger, steadier. A reliquary of lead and glass, fused to the stone by two centuries of flood, and inside it, white as moonrise even through the murk:

A sternum. Human. Enormous in the way relics are enormous—not in size but in *presence*, in the way it made the water around it seem thin, made the dark seem temporary.

*Take it,* the elder said. *And keeper-of-ledgers—*

"Yes?"

*When you come back—and you will come back, because we have decided to believe you, which is the bravest thing a dead man can do—* the light in its chest flickered, almost like laughter, almost like weeping— *sing us the rest. All of it. Every verse. We have forgotten how the middle goes.*

Kestrel's eyes stung, and the water hid it, and he was grateful.

"On my ledger," he said. "Every verse."

He pried the reliquary free. The stone fought him, then surrendered all at once, the way old things do, and the moment his fingers closed around the leaden case he felt it—the second bone, humming against the fingerbone of the old queen in the pouch at his belt, two notes of a chord reaching for each other across two hundred years of silence.

Sera's hand locked around his wrist. *Up. Now. Before the divers cut through the east cloister.*

They kicked for the surface, for the narrow thread of grey light where the sinkhole opened to the sky, the reliquary dragging at him like a sleeping child. Behind them, in the dark, forty voices were singing—not the wheel, not the wall, but something new and halting and joyful, the middle verses of a psalm reconstructed from memory, wrong in places, magnificent in all of them.

* * *

They broke the surface gasping, hauled themselves out onto the sinkhole's rim, and lay on the wet grass heaving like landed fish while the morning sun did its best to convince them the world was kind.

Sera spoke first. "They held the stones. Did you—was that you, or—"

"That was them. Half-free and they still spent it on us." Kestrel sat up, dripping, and pulled the reliquary into his lap. His hands were shaking. "I made them a promise, Sera. I have to go back for them. After. Whatever happens with the crown—I go back."

"I know." She wrung out her hair, and he expected mockery and got none. "That's why I'm still here, Crane. You're the only person I've met who makes promises to people who can't collect." She nodded at the pouch at his belt. "Open it. I want to see what two bones look like together."

He drew out the queen's fingerbone and set it beside the reliquary, and the moment the two relics touched the grass between them, the hum became a chord—audible, physical, a vibration in his teeth and breastbone. And through the cracked collar, through his half-restored hearing, the chord resolved into something almost like speech. Almost like instruction.

The fingerbone whispered: *first the hand that bound.*

The sternum answered: *then the heart that wore.*

And Kestrel went very still, because he had spent weeks assuming the third bone of the binding was lost—buried, scattered, hidden in some vault or grave they'd never find. He had built the whole desperate architecture of their quest on that assumption.

But the bones were not whispering about a search.

They were whispering about a *location*. Somewhere high. Somewhere public. Somewhere worn, openly, ceremonially, renewed once a year before the whole court.

Somewhere already on the king's head.

"Sera," he said, and his voice came out strange. "The third bone isn't lost."

She looked at him. Water ran off her chin. "Then where is it?"

He raised his eyes, as if he could see the Spire from here, sixty leagues distant, its crown of binding glinting above the capital like a splinter of frozen scream.

"It's the crown," he said. "It was never hidden, Sera. It was *enthroned*."
