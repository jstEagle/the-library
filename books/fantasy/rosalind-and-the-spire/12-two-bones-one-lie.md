## 12. Two Bones, One Lie

The safehouse was a cooper's cellar beneath a shuttered chandlery in Saltrow, and it smelled of tallow, brine, and old fear. Sera had chosen it because the walls were thick with packed earth and the only entrance was a trapdoor hidden under a false floor of curing hams—because, she said, no Inquisitor alive would volunteer to search a room full of pig fat. Kestrel had not argued. He had stopped arguing about hiding places somewhere around the third time Vael's riders passed within a street of them.

Now he sat at a barrel-top table with two bones laid out before him on a square of black cloth, and he could not make himself touch either one.

The fingerbone of the old queen came first: slender, yellowed, its nail bed still bearing the ghost of a lacquered polish. It had spent years in the Inquisition's vault, catalogued as *relic, provenance disputed*, and it whispered even now, faintly, through the cracked collar at his throat—a woman's voice reciting something over and over, worn smooth by repetition, like a stone in a river.

The sternum of Saint Aldric lay beside it, broad as a dinner plate, browned with age and river-silt. The monks' chorus clung to it still, though their voices had gone quiet since the drowned monastery, since his promise. *We will be freed,* they murmured, patient as tide. *You swore it.*

Between them, on the cloth, Kestrel had drawn the binding's architecture from memory—the diagram the blind cartographer Maren had scratched into wax for him, reading the queen's bone like a page. Three anchors. Three bones of the original rite. One to hold the voice, one to hold the will, one to hold the shape.

*One to hold the shape.*

He had read that line a dozen times since the monastery. He had turned it over the way a man turns a coin he suspects is clipped. And every time, some small animal part of him had flinched away from the conclusion waiting underneath, the way a tongue finds a broken tooth and then, cowardly, avoids it.

Sera came down the ladder with a lamp and a loaf of black bread, and paused when she saw his face.

"You've been sitting like that for an hour," she said. "Like a man at a funeral who's just realized he doesn't know whose."

"I think I know whose," Kestrel said.

She set the bread down without taking her eyes off him. She had a way of going very still when she was frightened, this hedge-witch—he'd learned it in the vault, in the cartographer's attic, in the flooded dark of the monastery while she held a door against water that wanted them both. Stillness was her armor. She wore it now.

"Say it anyway," she said. "Out loud. Things sound smaller out loud."

He almost laughed. Almost. Instead he reached out and set his fingertips on the queen's fingerbone, letting the collar's crack widen just enough, letting the whisper through.

The dead queen spoke, as she always spoke, in the voice of a woman dictating her own funeral: *...and let the third anchor be set where all may see it, for a secret can be stolen but a throne cannot, and what is enthroned is beyond the reach of thieves...*

"Again," Sera said quietly. She had heard it before too. She knew why he kept returning to it.

He lifted his hand and pressed it flat against Saint Aldric's sternum instead. The monks stirred, a rustle of gray voices. *Hold the shape,* they chorused, in Latin gone soft with drowning. *The rite requires three: the voice, the will, the shape. Voice to speak the bound one's silence. Will to bend him to the binder's purpose. Shape—*

"Shape," Kestrel said. "Shape to keep him looking like himself." He looked up at Sera, and his mouth had gone dry. "A binding of fifteen years. A king who has been 'alive' all that time. Seen at windows. Seen at processions. Seen renewing his own face every year at the Rite." His hand trembled against the sternum. "What holds his shape, Sera? What keeps the regent-thing wearing him convincing enough to fool a whole court, year after year?"

The lamp flame leaned in a draft that wasn't there.

"The crown," Sera said.

Not a question. She had gotten there ahead of him—maybe days ahead, maybe since the cartographer's attic—and had been waiting, the way she waited for everything, still and armed, for him to catch up.

"The crown," Kestrel agreed, and saying it aloud made it real in the worst way, made it heavy in the air between them. "The third bone isn't lost. It isn't hidden in a vault or sunk in a monastery or sewn into a pilgrim's cloak. It's been sitting on the king's head in front of ten thousand witnesses for fifteen years. We've been hunting a relic that parades itself through the capital every feast day."

* * *

They did not speak for a long time after that. The cellar ticked and settled around them. Somewhere above, the chandlery's owner—who took their coin and asked nothing—moved through her shop, and the floorboards creaked her small domestic litany: kettle, broom, bolt, bed.

It was Kestrel who finally moved. He could not help it; his hands needed work the way other men's lungs needed air. He drew the black cloth closer and began arranging the two relics side by side, measuring, comparing, doing with bone what he had always done: reading it. The collar's crack pulsed at his throat, half-healing and half-wounding, and through it the whispers came clearer than they had any right to, as if the relics recognized something in him that was coming apart.

"The queen's fingerbone is the first anchor," he said, thinking aloud, carving the words into the air the way he would carve them into whalebone. "Voice. That's why the king screams through seal-bone fragments—his voice leaks along the binding's lines. Any piece of royal bone resonates with him because the voice-anchor is tuned to his blood." He touched the sternum. "Saint Aldric. Will. The monks were bound mid-prayer because prayer is will made habitual; Aldric's sternum held centuries of disciplined intention. It bends the bound one's will to the binder's. That's how the regent-thing makes him—" He stopped. Swallowed. "Makes him compliant. Makes him *agreeable*. Makes the court believe the king has simply grown quiet in his age."

"And the crown," Sera said, lowering herself onto the crate across from him, "is the shape. The face he wears. The mask that doesn't slip." Her eyes were bright and hard in the lamplight. "Which means the regent-thing didn't hide the binding, Kestrel. It *published* it. It put the last anchor on public display and dared anyone to understand what they were looking at."

"It enthroned the lie." Kestrel heard his own voice go flat and strange. "That's what Odile understood. That's what she sold Vael all those years ago—not the location of three hidden bones. The *method*. Bind a king, then crown the binding. Let the whole kingdom guard your prison for you, out of love and law and habit. Every oath of loyalty sworn to the crown is an oath sworn to keep the binding intact. Every ceremony, every coronation renewal—it's maintenance. It's the regent re-tightening the screws in front of everyone."

He thought of the audience chamber. Of the man on the throne, beautiful and vacant, smiling his painted smile. Of the crown above that head—the gold filigree, the sunburst rays, the way it had *screamed*, a sound like a cathedral falling, a sound he had told no one the full truth of because there had been no one to tell.

Fifteen years. Fifteen years of screaming, and the whole court had stood beneath it and heard nothing, because hearing the dead was a crime, and so the kingdom had arranged itself perfectly around its own deafness.

"We can't steal it," Sera said. Not cruelly. Just the way she said true things. "You understand that. A fingerbone comes out of a vault in a night. A sternum comes out of a drowned church with a promise and a fight. But the crown sits on the king's head inside the Spire, behind the Inquisition, behind Vael, behind whatever the regent-thing actually is, during a court that would burn us both for existing. You can't pickpocket a crown off a king."

"No," Kestrel said. "You can't."

"You're agreeing with me too fast. When you agree with me too fast it means you've already thought of something terrible."

He looked at the bones. At the diagram. At the little wax map Maren had given them, with the Spire inked at its center like a spider in a web—and he realized, with a lurch, that Maren had drawn it that way on purpose. The blind mapmaker had known. Had probably known for years. Had sent them into a flooded monastery and pointed them at a vault, patiently, the way you send a child gathering kindling, because the child needs to learn the shape of the fire before you tell them what it's for.

"There's exactly one moment," Kestrel said slowly, "when the crown leaves the king's head."

Sera went still again. Stillness as armor. But he saw the fear come into her eyes and stay there.

"The Rite of Renewal," she said. "Once a year. The regent takes the crown off to renew the disguise—to pour new shape into it, or whatever abomination it does—and for the length of the rite, the third anchor is *unguarded*. On an altar. In the open." She laughed, one short bitter note with no humor in it anywhere. "In the middle of the Spire. Surrounded by the entire court, the entire Inquisition, and the thing that made the binding in the first place. That's not a window, Kestrel. That's a door painted on a wall."

"It's the only door there is."

"Then we don't go through it. We find another way. Unmake the binding differently—break the first two anchors and starve the third. Cut the voice free and the will free and let the shape collapse on its own."

"And the king?" Kestrel asked softly. "If the shape-anchor holds when the others break, the regent-thing keeps its mask. Keeps the throne. Keeps wearing him. We'd have freed his voice and his will and left them floating homeless in the dark while the thing that stole them smiles on for another fifty years." He shook his head. "No. The binding turns inward—that's how these rites work, I've heard enough bound things to know. Break the anchors in the wrong order and the binding doesn't fail, it *feeds*. The crown has to be broken last, or broken first, or broken *at the same moment*—and every version of that ends with our hands on it, in the Spire, during the Rite."

The lamp guttered. Neither of them moved to trim the wick.

* * *

Later—much later, when the bread had gone stale between them untouched and the candles had burned down to thumbs—Kestrel took out the thing he had been avoiding all evening: a scrap of whalebone the size of his palm, blank, sanded smooth. His knife. His oldest habit. When the world became unbearable, he carved, and the carving made the unbearable thing small enough to hold.

He carved the Spire.

He had never seen it up close except once, from a prison wagon, hooded—but he had heard it. That was worse. Buildings remembered their own construction, if you listened: every keystone hummed with the mason's count, every foundation stone with the weight it carried. The Spire had sung to him through the wagon's boards, a vast cold chord of granite and ambition, and threaded through it, thin as wire, the king's scream.

His knife moved. The tower rose in miniature: base, shaft, crown-chamber, spire-tip. He carved the throne room. He carved the altar where the Rite was performed—he had to guess at it, and the guessing made his stomach turn, because guessing meant imagining, and imagining meant seeing Sera or himself standing on it.

"What are you doing?" Sera asked. She had been watching him for some time. Hedge-witches, he was learning, watched everything.

"Making it small," he said. "That's the trade. That's always been the trade. The guild balances impossible things by making them ledger-sized. A grief becomes a line item. A haunting becomes an entry. You write it down and it stops being infinite." He turned the carving in the light. "So. The impossible thing, ledger-sized: we need to enter the Spire during the Rite of Renewal, reach the crown while it's off the king's head, and shatter it—which we cannot do with force, because anything that important is warded against force. It has to be unbound. By a bone-carver. Who hears the dead." He looked up. "By me. Standing in the heart of the thing that hunts people like me, on the one night of the year the regent-thing is busiest, weakest, and most alert, all at once."

"That's not ledger-sized," Sera said. "That's ledger-*suicidal*."

"Yes."

"And you're going to do it anyway."

He didn't answer immediately. Instead he laid his palm flat on the table, beside the two relics, and let himself listen—really listen—for the first time since the monastery. Through the collar's crack the whispers rose: the queen reciting her funeral instructions, patient, practical, a woman who had planned her own posthumous war. The monks chanting their gray patience. And beneath them both, fainter, older, threaded through everything like a vein of ore through stone—

*...not dead... not dead... I am NOT DEAD...*

The king. Leaking through the binding's lines even here, even now, fifteen years deep in his prison, still fighting. Still screaming into a kingdom that had been taught not to hear.

"He held on for fifteen years," Kestrel said. "Alone. In the dark. Wearing his own body like a stranger's coat while something else smiled with his mouth. If he can hold on that long, I can walk into the Spire." He closed his fist around the whalebone Spire until the edges bit his palm. "But I'm not walking in alone, and I'm not walking in with two people and a knife. This stopped being a theft the moment we learned what the third bone is. It's a war now. And wars need soldiers."

Sera tilted her head. "You have an army in mind?"

"I have a *guild* in mind." He said it and felt the word land strangely in his chest—grief and hope tangled together, because the guild had sold him, and the guild had saved him, and the guild was still out there in its smugglers' cellars and ash-marked safehouses, hunted and scattered and *alive*. "Every gifted soul the Inquisition has ever listed. Every bone-hearer, dream-reader, ash-speaker, ghost-caller who's run instead of registering. They're not scattered, Sera. They're *waiting*. Waiting for someone to tell them the truth about what's on the throne. Waiting for someone to give them a job worth burning for."

"The truth about the throne gets people killed."

"The lie about the throne is killing them already." He pushed back from the table. The two relics gleamed on the black cloth—the voice and the will, two-thirds of a king's freedom, useless without the third. "We need the underground. All of it. Smugglers, safehouses, the ash-marks, the whole hidden country inside the country. And there's exactly one person who knows how to reach all of it, who has the trust and the keys and the guilt to spend."

Understanding dawned on Sera's face, followed immediately by distaste. "Odile."

"Odile."

"She sold you to Vael."

"She sold *everything* to Vael, years ago, and she's the only person alive who knows every door in the gifted underground—including which ones Vael already knows about, which is worth more than the ones he doesn't." Kestrel gathered the relics, wrapped them, and tied the cloth with hands that were steadier than he felt. "I'm not asking her for forgiveness. I'm asking her for her network. And then I'm going to stand in front of the woman who taught me everything I know about hiding, and ask her to help me do the least hideable thing anyone has ever attempted."

He climbed toward the trapdoor, then paused, one hand on the ladder, and looked back at the little carved Spire lying on the table beside the lamp—small, detailed, absurd, a tower you could close your fist around.

Outside, far off across the rooftops of Saltrow, a bell began to ring. Not the hour bell. The *court* bell—the one that rang only for proclamations from the Spire.

Sera appeared at his shoulder, and they listened together as a crier's distant voice rose and fell, rising again, carrying words down the dark streets word by broken word:

*"...by order of His Majesty... the Rite of Renewal shall be held... seven days hence... all loyal subjects..."*

Seven days.

Kestrel closed his hand around the whalebone tower until it bit blood, and thought: *the crown comes off in seven days, and I am not ready, and no one will ever be ready, and the king has been screaming for fifteen years.*

"We need Odile," he said. "Tonight."
