## 16. The Rite of Renewal

They dressed us in white.

That was Sera's idea—*if you can't hide from them, look like something they've already blessed.* The ritual servants of the Renewal wore undyed wool, hoods cut close to the skull, faces powdered with chalk so that in candlelight every one of them looked like a ghost already. Forty-two of us stood in the undercroft beneath the tannery on Corvess Street while Maren Ash-Speaker moved down the line with a pot of lime and ash, painting our faces white, our hands white, even the rims of our ears.

"You'll be deaf to each other," she said, working the paste across Kestrel's cheekbones. "No signals. No whispers. Once you're inside, you're alone."

"I'm used to it," Kestrel said.

She looked at him then, really looked, her eyes pale as skimmed milk. "You're not. That's what frightens me. You've spent your whole life alone and now you've got people, and people are how plans die."

He didn't answer, because she was right. He could feel them all around him—the bone-hearers with their sleeves full of relics, the dream-readers chewing valerian root to keep their gift quiet, old Tam Voss who could make a lock weep open with a touch. Forty-two impossible things dressed as servants of the crown that hunted them. Every one of them had a reason to run. None of them would.

Sera came through the line checking collars—or rather, checking the absence of collars. Kestrel's mute-band had cracked in the vault beneath the Inquisition, and what remained of it he wore anyway, a broken ring of black iron at his throat like a scar made visible. She touched it once, lightly, the way you'd touch a wound to see if it still hurt.

"Can you hear them?" she murmured. "The bones we're carrying?"

He could. Everyone in the room was carrying something dead: knucklebones sewn into hems, a saint's tooth in a locket, splinters of coffin-lid tucked into boot heels. To anyone else the undercroft held forty-two nervous rebels. To Kestrel it hummed like a hive—a low, layered murmur of the small dead, confused and hopeful, whispering *why are we moving, why are we moving, why are we moving.*

"Like a choir tuning up," he said.

"Let's hope they sing on key." She pulled his hood forward. "You know where you're going?"

"The heart of the Spire. Up the pilgrim stair, through the Hall of Seals, past the reliquary—" He recited it the way Odile had taught him to recite ledgers, column by column, no feeling in it. "And when the rite reaches its ninth hour, the regent goes alone into the crown chamber. That's when I go in."

"And if the ninth hour comes and you're not there?"

"Then the king stays bound another year," Kestrel said, "and we do this again next year with fewer of us."

Sera's jaw tightened. She hated arithmetic like that. She'd told him once that hedge-witches learned early never to count what they couldn't afford to lose, because counting was a kind of promise, and promises were how the world got its hooks in you. But she'd counted anyway. They all had. Forty-two names written in the new ledger, in Kestrel's own hand, each one a debt he intended to collect back alive.

Maren finished the last face. "Blessings are cheap," she announced to the room, "so take one free. Go quietly. Come back loudly."

Someone laughed. It sounded almost like courage.

* * *

The Spire rose out of the capital like a needle through cloth, white stone streaked grey by fifteen years of the regent's rain, and around its base the city had gathered for the Rite the way a body gathers around a wound.

Kestrel saw it all from the back of the servant-column as they crossed the Processional Square: banners of the doubled crown, the sun-wheel effigies the children carried, the great bronze bells silent in their yokes because the rite demanded silence until the ninth hour. Tens of thousands of faces turned up toward the Spire, hungry for the moment when the king—when the thing wearing the king—would appear on the high balcony and renew itself before their eyes. Kestrel knew what they'd actually see. A shimmer. A settling. Flesh remembering what shape it was supposed to hold. He'd heard the cost of that shimmer once, through a fragment of seal-bone, and he had not slept properly since.

The servant-columns entered through the Gate of Alms, and there the first test came: Inquisition wardens with long silver wands that sang a thin note when they passed over anything bound or gifted. Kestrel watched the wand pass over the woman ahead of him—Dulcet, a dream-reader, seventeen, chalk-white and shaking—and watched the note stay flat, muffled by the lead-foil lining Sera had sewn into every hem. Then his turn. The wand hovered at his throat, right over the cracked collar, and for one endless second it trembled—

"Move along," the warden said, bored. "Servants to the east cloister. Pilgrims to the square. No stopping."

Kestrel moved along. His heart kept going without him.

Inside, the Spire was colder than stone should be. Not draft-cold—*held* cold, the cold of a cellar where something is kept. And everywhere, everywhere, the bones. He had known intellectually that the Inquisition stored its confiscated relics here, but knowing was nothing against the reality of walking corridors lined with them: saints' fingers in niches, martyr's ribs behind glass, whole skeletons of the gifted hung in iron cages like chandeliers, and every single one of them turning toward him as he passed, every voice rising at once—

*you hear us, you hear us, carver, carver, you came back for us—*

He clenched his teeth and did what Odile had taught him: gave the voices a ledger-entry. *Noted. Filed. Pending.* It was cruelty of a kind, but it was also mercy; hope deferred hurt less than hope abandoned. *I will come back,* he told them, silently, in the language only the dead could hear. *When the crown breaks, you break with it.*

The chorus settled—not quiet, never quiet, but organized, like a crowd taking seats.

The plan was simple, which meant it was doomed. Simple plans survived contact with the enemy about as well as glass survives contact with floors. The forty-two would disperse through the servant warrens: twelve to the bell-yards to jam the alarm mechanisms, eight to the gatehouses to hold the doors open for retreat, six to the archives where Sera believed the records of the original binding were kept, and four—Kestrel, Sera, Tam Voss, and a gaunt ash-speaker named Brother Ilm—to climb the pilgrim stair and wait out the hours near the crown chamber.

The stair was nine hundred steps. Kestrel counted them the way he counted everything, and by step three hundred he had stopped hearing the other three behind him, which was wrong, because Sera's boots had a click in the left heel she'd never let him fix.

By step five hundred he was sure.

By step six hundred he heard the shouting start below.

It rolled up the stairwell like water finding a drain—shouts, then the flat silver note of the wands singing in chorus, then a sound he knew from the raid on the guildhall: the wet crack of a binding-net firing. Somewhere below them, the plan had touched the floor.

"They have lists," Tam Voss breathed, catching up to him on the landing, his chalked face slick with sweat beneath the powder. "Boy, they have *lists*. Names. Our names. How—"

Kestrel knew how. He had known since the vault, since the escape went too easily, since Vael had let them walk out of the undercity with two bones and a map. The regent-thing did not need to hunt them. It only needed to let them gather, the way you let flies gather on honey before you bring the paper down.

But saying it aloud would kill whatever was left of the morning, and the ninth hour was coming whether they despaired or not. "Keep climbing," he said. "Doors open at the ninth hour or not at all."

They climbed. Below, the net-cracks continued, patient as threshing.

* * *

The Hall of Seals opened onto the crown chamber's anteroom, and there the four of them hid themselves among the ritual apparatus—great waxen effigies of the doubled crown, censers the size of coffins, racks of vestments stiff with gold thread. Through the anteroom's lattice screen Kestrel could see the crown chamber itself: a round room at the very apex of the Spire, its walls set with ten thousand bone fragments mortared in spirals, and at its center the empty dais where the regent would stand at the ninth hour to receive the renewal.

The bones in the walls were screaming. Quietly. The way people scream in their sleep.

Kestrel pressed his forehead to the lattice and listened, because listening was the only prayer he knew. Ten thousand fragments, harvested from fifteen years of hunts, worked into the architecture of the lie. Each one a voice. Each one a name that had once been in a ledger somewhere, maybe even in his. *Noted,* he told them. *Filed. Pending.* And beneath the filing, for the first time, he let himself say the true entry: *Soon. Hold on. Soon.*

Hours passed. The bells of the city rang the sixth hour, the seventh, though inside the Spire the silence-rule held and the sound came through stone as a shiver rather than a ring. Sera sat beside him with her knees drawn up, and at some point her hand found his and stayed there, not romantic, just anchored, the way you tie off a boat.

At the eighth hour, everything happened at once.

The anteroom door banged open and wardens poured in—not two or four but a dozen, wands drawn, nets coiled—and behind them walked Inquisitor Vael in ceremonial grey, unhurried, as though arriving at a dinner party.

"Four of you," Vael said, scanning the vestment racks. "I expected more. Your guildmaster always oversold you."

Tam Voss moved first, bless him and damn him—he stepped out with both hands raised, palms open, and the nearest locks in the room began to weep open, hinges sagging, net-coils unspooling themselves, the wands' silver notes bending flat. "Run," Tam said pleasantly, to all of them, to no one. "I've got the doors."

What happened next took four seconds and Kestrel would spend the rest of his life replaying them. A warden fired a net anyway, through the sagging coils, and it took Tam in the chest mid-gesture. The binding-net sang its wet crack, and Tam Voss's voice—that easy, lock-charming voice—stopped. Not died. *Stopped*, the way a clock stops, and Kestrel felt it happen through his gift: a presence snuffed into a small hard knot of silence, folded up, pocketed. Taken to be bound later. Taken to be mortar.

Brother Ilm dragged Sera toward the far archway. Kestrel hesitated one heartbeat over Tam's body—one heartbeat, the most expensive purchase of his life—and then Vael's eyes found his through the vestment rack, and Vael smiled, and did not order the wardens to fire.

"Go," Vael said softly. "You're expected."

Kestrel ran. Behind him he heard Sera fighting, heard Ilm shout, heard the nets sing twice more—and then he was through the archway and alone on the final stair, nine hundred steps climbed and the last twenty left, and the bells of the city were ringing the ninth hour.

The doors of the crown chamber stood open.

Of course they did. He was expected.

Kestrel Crane, bone-carver, keeper of ledgers, walked into the heart of the Spire with two stolen bones against his chest and no allies left above ground that he knew of, and the doors closed behind him without being touched, and on the empty dais, wearing the king's body like a coat put on carefully that morning, the regent-thing turned to greet him.

"You brought them," it said, in the dead king's ruined voice. "Both pieces. After everything, you brought them straight to me."

The spiral walls leaned in. Ten thousand voices held their breath.

Above the thing's head, on its cushion of iron and velvet, the crown began—very faintly, in a register only Kestrel could hear—to scream.
