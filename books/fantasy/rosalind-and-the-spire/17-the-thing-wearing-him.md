## 17. The Thing Wearing Him

The doors closed behind him with a sound like a book shutting.

Kestrel stood alone in the throne room of the Spire, and the silence was the worst part. Not true silence—his collar had cracked in the vault weeks ago, and fragments of hearing leaked through it still—but a curated silence, the hush of a room where every whisper had been eaten. The dead did not speak here. The dead *could not*. Whatever sat upon that throne had made certain of it.

The rite was hours away. The underground's plan was simple in the way plans are simple before they meet reality: Sera would hold the servants' door with half their people, the ash-speakers would blind the Spire's watchers, and Kestrel would walk up the long stair to the crown itself while the kingdom knelt in ceremony below. He had expected guards. He had expected Vael.

He had not expected an empty throne room, one lamp lit, and the king waiting for him like a host expecting a dinner guest.

"Bone-carver." The voice was warm. That was the first horror of it—the warmth. Fifteen years of screaming inside a crown, and the mouth beneath it produced a voice like a hearth in winter, like his mother reading aloud, like every kind thing anyone had ever said to him at the exact moment he'd needed to hear it. "You came early. I respect that. The punctual ones are always the dangerous ones."

Kestrel kept his hands loose at his sides. In his coat, wrapped against his ribs, the fingerbone of the old queen and the sternum of Saint Aldric pressed together like two halves of a sentence waiting for its ending. Two bones. One lie. And the third bone gleamed above the regent's brow, gold-cased and singing so faintly that only someone like Kestrel could catch it—a note held fifteen years without breath.

"I'm told you wanted an audience," Kestrel said.

"I'm told *you* wanted one. You've been asking after me since the monastery. Since the vault, if Odile's reports are honest—and they were, right up until they weren't." The king rose from the throne, and Kestrel's stomach turned, because the movement was wrong in no way he could name. Graceful. Human. A man of sixty-odd years descending three steps with the ease of a much younger body. The face was the king's face from the coins: heavy jaw, grey-shot beard, eyes the color of river water. It fit him the way a glove fits a hand. Perfectly. Which meant it wasn't a face at all. It was a *fitting*.

"You're not him," Kestrel said. "I heard the real one. In the seal-bone. He screams."

"Yes." The regent-thing said it simply, without shame, and somehow that was worse than any denial. "He does. He has been screaming for fifteen years, and I have been listening to it for fifteen years, and I will tell you something no one else in this kingdom knows, bone-carver: it never becomes easier. Not once. Not ever." He gestured toward the lamp-lit table beside the throne, where wine waited in a decanter and two cups stood filled. "Sit. Please. I have been looking forward to this conversation more than you can imagine."

"Why?"

"Because you are the first person in fifteen years who might actually *understand*."

* * *

Kestrel did not sit. But he did not leave either, and the regent-thing seemed satisfied with that, settling onto the edge of the dais rather than the throne itself—an odd courtesy, as though even it would not sit where the screaming was loudest.

"Do you know what I was?" it asked.

"A thief."

"A clerk." The word landed strangely in the vast room. "A records-keeper in the chancery of King Aldous the Second, may his memory be what it is. I inventoried grain shipments. I reconciled tax ledgers. I had a wife who embroidered badly and a daughter who was afraid of geese." The river-water eyes fixed on Kestrel, and there was something in them that Kestrel recognized with a lurch of vertigo—the look of a man balancing accounts. "I was nobody. I was *nothing*. And then the old queen died, and the court sorcerers needed a vessel for the binding, and they chose me because my ledger-hand was neat and because no one would miss me."

"The binding," Kestrel said carefully. "The original binding. The three bones."

"You've found two. I know. I let you find them." The regent smiled, and the smile fit the face as perfectly as everything else. "Oh, don't look betrayed. Vael let you escape the second time; I let you find the monastery; the drowned monks gave up Saint Aldric's sternum because I loosened their chorus a year ago. You are clever, bone-carver, but you have been walking down a corridor I built, and every door opened exactly when you pushed it."

Kestrel's hand drifted toward his coat. The regent raised both palms, placating.

"I didn't stop you. Ask yourself why. Ask yourself why a creature that fears nothing would permit the two pieces of its own prison to walk out of the deepest vault in the kingdom and into the hands of a man who hears the dead." He poured wine into one cup, then the other, uninvited. "Because I want you to understand. That's all. Before tonight, before whatever you intend—I want one person in this world to understand what happened to me. Is that so monstrous a request?"

"It is when you're wearing a dead man's face."

"This isn't his face." For the first time, something moved under the surface of the voice—not anger, but grief, worn smooth as sea glass. "His face is in the crown. This—" the regent touched its own cheek, and the skin dimpled like wax, briefly, before smoothing, "—this is what I could build from what I remember of myself. I have been Aldous the Third for fifteen years. Do you know how much of a man survives that? How much of *me*? I remember geese. I remember her embroidery, red thread on grey cloth, crooked little birds. I do not remember her name." The cup trembled, very slightly, in its hand. "I invented a name for her last winter just so I could say goodnight to her memory. I called her Maren. She was not Maren."

Kestrel stood very still. He thought of the drowned girl whose last words he had carved into whalebone, all those months ago, in a guildhall that felt like another life. He thought of the monks bound mid-prayer, and the bargain he had struck with them, and the guilt that still slept under his ribs like a stone. He thought of Odile dying with the master key in her fist, asking forgiveness he had given but could not grant.

He knew what it was to be consumed by a thing you served. He had simply never seen it wear a crown.

"What do you want from me?" he asked.

"Honesty. You'll find I deal in nothing else—it's the one luxury of being a monster." The regent set down the untouched wine. "I am going to show you something. It will feel like a dream. It is not a dream, and afterward you may hate me, and I will accept that. But you will understand."

It extended one hand, palm up. And though Kestrel's gift lived in bone and ivory, though the collar's crack had given him back only whispers, he felt the pull of it anyway—a tide going out of the world, drawing him forward.

"Don't," he said, and stepped forward anyway.

* * *

The vision took him like deep water takes a dropped coin.

He was in the chancery, forty years gone or perhaps yesterday. Candle-smoke and ink. A thin man with ink-stained fingers—himself, the regent, whole and mortal and forgettable—copying figures by lamplight while somewhere above, in the private chapel, the court sorcerers prepared the binding of a dying queen's relics. Kestrel watched through the clerk's eyes. He felt the clerk's heart: the small ambitions, the fear of the head steward, the ache in his back from fourteen hours at the desk. A life measured in ledgers. A life that added up to almost nothing, and knew it.

Then the summons came. Down the white stair, into the undercroft, where the old queen's fingerbone lay on black velvet and the sorcerers stood in a ring, and the Grand Sorcerer—Vael's master, Vael's teacher, Vael's face thirty years younger—said the words that Kestrel now understood had been rehearsed for a generation:

*A binding must be willing, or it rots.*

They told the clerk it was an honor. They told him the realm required it. They told him his family would be provided for, ennobled, remembered. And the clerk—who had spent his whole life adding other people's columns and coming up short—looked at the fingerbone of a queen and felt, for the first time, *significant*.

"They lied about all of it," the regent's voice said through the vision, quiet as falling ash. "There was no honor. There was no provision. My wife died in a debtor's house four years later. My daughter—" the vision flickered, a girl running from geese, laughing, then gone, "—I don't know. I have never known. The binding took my hands first. Then my voice. Then everything except the part of me that keeps the counts."

The vision changed. The undercroft became the crown, and the crown became a country seen from above: Kestrel saw through the regent's stolen eyes across fifteen years of reign. Harvests balanced. Wars averted by letters written in a dead king's hand. Plagues quarantined within a week. Rebellions strangled in the cradle by men arrested before they had decided to rebel. A kingdom that ran, for the first time in living memory, like a well-kept ledger—every column reconciled, every debt collected, every anomaly corrected.

And threaded through all of it, everywhere, the binding: voices pulled from bones and bells and birthing-chants, folded into the crown, filed away. The gifted hunted not out of cruelty but out of *bookkeeping*. Every free voice was an error in the account. Every error corrected.

"I was a clerk," the regent said, and now the vision was only darkness and the crown's slow scream beneath it, patient as erosion. "And I have kept the perfect ledger. No famine in fifteen years. No civil war. No child in the capital has starved since I wore this face. I have done more good than any king in the history of this throne, and I have done it by consuming every voice that might have disagreed with me." A pause. "Tell me which of those sentences is the lie, bone-carver. Take your time. I have asked myself the same question every night for fifteen years, and I still cannot answer it."

* * *

Kestrel came back to himself on his knees on the cold flagstones, gasping, the two hidden bones hot against his ribs as though they had been scalded.

The regent crouched before him—not looming, crouching, the posture of a man comforting a sick friend—and its borrowed face was full of something that looked unbearably like kindness.

"Now you understand," it said softly. "Not what I am. What I *was*. The crown did not make me cruel. It made me *thorough*. There is a difference, and the difference is the whole history of this kingdom." It rose, straightening the king's coat with a small habitual tug that must have belonged to some dead tailor's memory. "So here is my offer, offered honestly, as promised. Tonight, during the rite, you will bring me the two bones. Not surrender—*partnership*. You have heard what I hear. You know the binding can be gentled; you gentled the monks yourself, they sang for you, didn't they? Help me rebuild it properly. Willingly. We will bind no one who does not choose it, and those who choose it will keep their names, their memories, their *Marens*. A chorus instead of a prison. Order without rot. You could carve the new binding yourself—you are the finest bone-hearer alive, and I say that with a connoisseur's knowledge."

"And the king?" Kestrel's voice came out raw. "The one screaming in your crown. What happens to him in your gentle new chorus?"

The regent was silent for a long moment. Outside the high windows, the sky was turning the bruised purple of late afternoon. Somewhere below, the city was lighting lamps for the rite.

"He goes," the regent said at last. "That is the price of everything I have built, and I pay it nightly, and I will pay it tonight. One voice, bone-carver. One voice, weighed against a kingdom that does not starve. You of all people know that every ledger balances somewhere." It extended its hand again, palm up, patient. "Or you can take your two bones up the mountain tonight and try to shatter the third, and the release will tear the binding open all at once—every voice, every year, everything I have swallowed—and this city will drown in fifteen years of the dead arriving all at once. Perhaps you can carry that. Perhaps your guild can. But I wanted you to choose knowing. That is the only mercy I have left, and I spend it on you because you are the first person in fifteen years who looked at me and heard *both* of us."

Kestrel got to his feet. His legs shook. He looked at the offered hand—at the fingers that had once held a pen over grain ledgers, that had signed death warrants in a dead man's hand, that remembered geese.

"I balance accounts too," Kestrel said. "For a guild of impossible things. And the first rule of our ledger is that some things don't balance. Some debts you just refuse to collect." He stepped back from the hand. "The king screamed for fifteen years. That's not a line item. That's the whole book."

Something moved behind the river-water eyes—regret, or hunger, or the ghost of a clerk who had once been refused something and learned to take it anyway.

"I hoped," the regent said quietly, "you would say that. Vael wagered you would. We had a bet." It turned toward the windows, toward the darkening Spire, and when it spoke again the warmth was gone from its voice, and what remained was older and colder and infinitely tired. "The rite begins at moonrise. Go and do what you came to do, bone-carver. I have taken the liberty of clearing your path—no guards, no Vael, no interference. A fair test. You against the binding, with nothing in between." It glanced back at him, and for one terrible instant the borrowed face slipped, and Kestrel saw beneath it: not a monster, not a king, but a thin grey man made entirely of arithmetic, holding a pen over a column that would never come out even.

"But do hurry," the regent-thing said. "Your witch is already at the servants' door. And my captain of the Inquisition has been waiting for her there since dawn."

* * *

Kestrel ran.

He took the stairs three at a time, past tapestries of kings who had never existed, past windows filling with the first stars, the two bones hammering against his ribs like a second heart. The corridors were empty, exactly as promised—cleared, curated, a corridor built for him to run down—and that was its own kind of trap, and he ran anyway, because Sera was at the door, Sera who could hear his thoughts even through iron, Sera who had once told him in the dark of a cell that she'd survived this long by never being where they expected.

*Hold the door,* he thought at her, as hard as he had ever thought anything. *Sera. Hold the door.*

Far below, muffled by stone, he heard—or imagined he heard, or heard with the broken fragment of gift that was all the collar had left him—a sound like a bell cracking, and a voice cut off mid-word.

Then the Spire went dark, every lamp at once, and the rite began.
