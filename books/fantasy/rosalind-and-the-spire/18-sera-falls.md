## 18. Sera Falls

The trap was not a trap. That was the worst of it, later—when Sera had time to turn the hour over and over in her mind, looking for the crack where the light had gotten in. There was no crack. There had never been a crack. Vael had simply waited until the moment when she believed herself safest, and the moment she believed herself safest was the moment Kestrel's hand closed around hers in the smugglers' tunnel beneath the Tanner's Row, and she let herself think, for one whole unguarded breath: *we might actually do this.*

She felt the thought leave her like heat leaving an open door.

Hedge-magic had always worked that way for her—leaky, porous, a sieve instead of a wall. She could hear other people's thoughts through stone and silence and even through bone, but her own mind was a house with every window open, and she had spent thirty years learning to shut them, and in that one breath of hope she had left them all flung wide.

The Inquisition did not come through the tunnel. They came *up* through it, through a hatch she had walked over twice without seeing, and the first thing Sera knew of the ambush was the smell of cold iron and lamp-oil and then hands—six pairs of them, gloved, practiced—taking her weight at the shoulders and knees so smoothly she didn't even stumble.

She heard Kestrel shout her name from somewhere ahead, muffled as if through water, because they had already dropped a hood over her head, and the last thing she got through the wool was his voice cracking on the second syllable, and the sound of him being held back, and the sound of him being held back *hard*.

Then iron at her throat. Not a mute-band—the crude collar they'd cracked in the vault—but something finer, something that hummed against her skin like a struck tuning fork, and her gift went out like a candle pinched between wet fingers.

The silence afterward was the loudest thing she had ever heard.

* * *

They kept her hooded for two days. She knew it was two days because they fed her twice, and Inquisition rations came morning and evening, gray bread and grayer soup, and the man who brought them—she named him Turnkey in her head, because he smelled of keys and had a habit of clearing his throat before speaking, as though rehearsing—never answered a single question.

She asked anyway. Thirty years of surviving alone had taught her that questions were free, and answers were rare enough to be worth the asking.

"Where is he?"

Throat-clear. Door closing.

"Is he alive?"

Throat-clear. Door closing.

"Did he give you the bones?"

On the second evening, after this last question, the Turnkey paused. She heard the tray wobble slightly in his grip. And in that wobble she read everything she needed to read, because Sera Quill had spent her life reading people who would not be read, and a man's hands told truths his mouth had been ordered to keep.

Kestrel still had the bones. Which meant Kestrel was still free. Which meant—

Which meant she was the lever, and someone intended to pull her.

She lay back on the cot and stared through the hood at nothing and made herself breathe slowly, the way she'd taught herself during the year she'd lived in a root cellar hiding from a hedge-lord's enforcers, the way she'd taught herself during the winter the fever took her mother's voice before it took her mother. Panic was a fire and fear was its fuel and she had only so much fuel. She banked it. She let it smolder.

And in the smoldering, in the dark behind the hood, she let herself feel the thing she had been refusing to feel since the hatch opened under her feet:

He will trade for me. Of course he will trade for me. He traded himself once already, walked into Vael's custody with his chin up and his ledgers balanced, and he will walk into whatever room they put me in and lay the two bones of the binding on the table like a merchant settling a debt, and he will do it without hesitating, and I will hate him for it, and I will love him for it, and both of those things will be true at once, and neither of them will save us.

Because she knew what the bones were worth. She had bled in a flooded monastery for one of them. She had watched Kestrel kneel in the dark among drowned monks and promise ghosts their freedom, and she had carried Saint Aldric's sternum against her own chest for nine days while it whispered prayers in a dead saint's voice, warm as a sleeping bird.

Two bones. Half the binding. The difference between a kingdom ruled by a thing wearing a dead man's face and a kingdom ruled by—well. By whatever came after. Nobody had promised that what came after would be better. Only that it would be *true*, and Sera had built her whole crooked life on the conviction that truth was worth more than comfort, and now the truth was going to cost exactly what it had always been going to cost, and the price was her.

She laughed into the hood, a small ugly sound, and the Turnkey outside the door cleared his throat nervously.

* * *

On the third day they took the hood off in a white room.

That was the first surprise. She had expected the deep vault, or a cell, or the interrogation chambers the underground whispered about—rooms with drains in the floor. Instead: a round chamber high in the Spire itself, walls of pale stone, a single window looking down across the capital's rooftops to the river, bright with morning. A table. Two chairs. A pitcher of water, actual water, and a cup of glazed clay.

Inquisitor Vael stood at the window with his hands clasped behind his back, watching the city as if it were a garden he had planted.

"You look terrible," Sera said. Her voice came out rusty. "Have you been sleeping?"

"No." He turned. He looked, if anything, worse than terrible; there was a hollowness around his eyes that hadn't been there in the guildhall, a man burning his candle at both ends and the middle too. "Sit down, Mistress Quill."

"I'd rather stand."

"Sit down," Vael said, "or I will have you seated, and the men who seat you are less gentle than I am."

She sat. She drank the water, because pride was a luxury and dehydration was not. The collar hummed at her throat. Through the window, far below, she could see the market square, tiny figures moving like insects, a cart of oranges spilled and bright as a wound. Somewhere down there were people who knew her name. Somewhere down there the underground was waiting for word from her and Kestrel, and the word was three days late.

"Do you know," Vael said, "what happens at the Rite of Renewal?"

"I know what everyone knows. The king renews his vow. The crown is blessed. Everyone weeps on cue."

"The crown is renewed," Vael said quietly. "Do you understand the distinction? The regent does not merely wear it. It must be *maintained*. Fifteen years ago it was bound with three bones and a chorus of voices, and voices fray, Mistress Quill. Voices wear thin, the way rope wears thin, the way anything wears when it is asked to hold more than it was made to hold. Every year at the Rite, the binding is fed."

"Fed," Sera repeated.

"Reinforced." He crossed to the table and sat opposite her, and up close his eyes were the color of old ice, and she saw that his hands, folded neatly on the table's edge, were trembling very slightly. That frightened her more than anything else in the room. Vael did not tremble. "New voices are woven into the crown. Willing ones, historically. There has never been a shortage of people willing to give their voices to the crown, in exchange for position, for pardon, for the belief that they are serving order." His mouth did something that was almost a smile and entirely joyless. "Lately the supply has thinned. The regent requires... stronger thread."

"And you're shopping."

"I am offering," Vael said, "a choice."

There it was. She had known it was coming since the hood came off, had known it since the hatch, maybe since the tunnel, maybe since the day she'd been caught stealing a queen's fingerbone out of a reliquary with nothing but a hairpin and sheer stupid nerve. Choices, in Vael's world, were the shape the cage took when it wanted you to build the bars yourself.

"Say it," she said.

"Surrender is not required. Cooperation is not required. All that is required is your voice." He said it the way a physician says *the procedure*, flat and clinical, and somehow that was worse than cruelty. "At the Rite, you will stand in the Spire's heart. You will be given the choice to speak freely into the binding—a single sentence, your own words, freely chosen—and the crown will take it, and take you with it, and you will spend whatever remains of your existence as part of the thing that keeps this kingdom from tearing itself apart. You will not suffer. I am assured the transition is—" he searched for the word, and failed to find a honest one, and settled for "—quiet."

"And if I refuse?"

Vael looked at her for a long moment. Outside the window, a bell began to ring somewhere, noon or alarm, impossible to tell.

"If you refuse," he said, "we proceed to the alternative, which is that we find another way to make you useful, and I promise you, Mistress Quill, having seen the alternative performed, I would choose the crown."

"You've seen it performed."

"I have performed it." No flinch. No apology. Just the flat fact of it laid on the table between them like a knife. "I told Crane once that order is worth any price. I have paid prices you cannot imagine, and I have never once regretted the purchase. This kingdom stands. Children go to school. Grain comes up the river. The borders hold. None of that survives what you and he intend. When the crown breaks, everything bound inside it breaks loose—fifteen years of screaming, all at once, into the heads of every gifted soul in the capital, and half of them will go mad, and the mad will burn things, and the burning will not stop at the Spire. You think you are freeing a king. You are opening a floodgate."

"Maybe," Sera said. "Or maybe you're the floodgate, and the water's been rising behind you for fifteen years, and every year you patch the cracks with somebody's voice."

"That is precisely what I do," Vael said. "Yes."

For a moment neither of them spoke. The bell below stopped ringing. A gull cried somewhere beyond the glass, absurd and ordinary.

"He'll come for you," Vael said. "You know that. It is the only variable I could not plan around, because it is not rational. He should run. With two bones and the underground behind him, he could vanish into the marsh countries and live out his life carving whalebone for fishermen. Instead he will come here, to the Spire, into my custody, because you are in it. Men do not do that for strategy. They do it for—" he paused, and she watched him decide whether to say the word, and say it anyway— "love. It is the one irrationality I have never been able to eliminate, and believe me, I have tried."

Something cold slid down Sera's spine. "You're using me as bait."

"I'm using you as bait," Vael agreed, "and I am telling you so, because I find that informed bait cooperates better than surprised bait. He will send word within a day. He will propose terms. The terms will be the two bones in exchange for your release, and I will accept those terms, and I will keep them—I want you to understand that, because it matters to what happens next. I keep my bargains to the letter. But understand the letter, Mistress Quill. He will give me the bones, and I will release you, and you will walk out of the Spire alive and free and whole, and the Rite will proceed with a binding reinforced by the queen's fingerbone and the saint's sternum, and the regent will endure another year, and another, and the water behind the dam will rise another fathom. And you will spend the rest of your life knowing that you were the price of it."

"And if I refuse to walk?"

"Then I will have you carried."

* * *

They brought Kestrel's message that night, as promised. Not a letter—Vael burned letters unread, or claimed to—but a strip of whalebone, three inches long, carved in Kestrel's fine close script, delivered to her cell by the Turnkey with his rehearsed throat-clearing.

She read it by the light of the slot-window, turning it over and over, reading it until she could have recited it in her sleep.

*Sera. They have offered the exchange. I am going to take it. Before you argue: I know what it costs. I have known since the monastery that the third bone is the crown, and I have been trying to find a way to reach it that does not pass through you, and there isn't one, and I will not let you be the thread that binds it. Take your freedom. Live loudly. I will find another way, even if it takes years. Even if it takes the rest of my life. — K.*

Below the signature, almost too small to see, a tiny carved mark: a bird, wings folded, perched. The mark he put on the memory-bones he carved for the dead. The mark that meant *I will remember you.*

Sera sat down on the cot's edge and held the little bone in her fist until the edges bit her palm, and she did not cry, because crying was fuel, and she was saving her fuel.

*Live loudly,* he said. *Find another way.* As if there were years. As if the regent-thing would sit patiently on its stolen throne while Kestrel spent a decade hunting a way into the Spire's heart. As if Vael would leave either of them alive that long once the exchange was done. She had read the letter's underside the way she read everything: *he means every word, and every word is wrong, and he knows it's wrong, and he's saying it anyway because it's the only kindness left that he can offer me.*

He was trading the kingdom for her. He knew it. He was doing it anyway.

And the fury that rose in her then was not the hot kind. It was the cold kind, the kind that crystallizes, the kind that keeps. Everything gained—the fingerbone bought with a broken collar, the sternum bought with drowned lungs and a monk's chorus promised their freedom, the underground rallied, the plan built bone by bone through the whole long autumn—all of it, all of it, about to be set on a table in a white room and pushed across to the man who had spent fifteen years feeding people to a crown. For her. Out of love. And she would be freed, and she would live loudly, and every loud living day would taste of this moment, this cell, this little bone with its folded-winged bird.

"No," she said aloud, to the empty cell, to the humming collar, to the gull wheeling past the window. "No, you don't. You stubborn, ledger-balancing fool. You don't get to buy me with the world."

* * *

The exchange happened at dawn, in the white room, and it happened exactly as Vael had said it would, because Vael was a man who kept his bargains to the letter, and the letter was a knife.

They brought her in first. Kestrel stood across the room between two guards, and he looked awful—hollow-cheeked, unwashed, a fresh bruise shadowing one eye—and when he saw her his whole face broke open with relief before he mastered it, and that mastery, that effort of mastering it, hurt her worse than the collar ever had.

The bones lay on the table between them. Both of them. The queen's fingerbone in its wrapping of black silk, and Saint Aldric's sternum bare, pale as moonstone, and even across the room Sera could feel them the way you feel a hearth from a doorway—except the collar ate the feeling before it reached her, and all she got was the sight of them, inert and lovely, the two most dangerous objects in the kingdom sitting on a table like items in a shop.

"Both bones," Vael said. "Verified by my appraisers. In exchange: Mistress Quill walks out of the Spire through the river gate, unharmed, uncollared, with escort to the bridge and no pursuit for one day. These are the terms Crane proposed and these are the terms I accept."

"Kestrel." Her voice came out steadier than she felt. "Don't."

He looked at her. Just looked, across the white room, across the table with the bones on it, and his eyes were wet and furious and certain, and she understood in that look that she had known him for one season and would know him for the rest of her life, and that he was going to do this thing no matter what she said, and that she loved him for it and hated him for it and would carry both forever.

"There's no other way," he said quietly. "I looked. Believe me, I looked."

"There's always another way."

"Not this time." He picked up the bones. Held them a moment, both together, cupped in his two hands like water he was afraid of spilling. Then he set them in Vael's palms, and Vael closed his fingers over them, and Sera heard—through the dead air, through the humming collar, through nothing, just with her plain human ears—the faintest sound, like a sigh, like a note held fifteen years finally allowed to bend.

The guards took her arms. She didn't fight them. Fighting was fuel, and she was saving her fuel, and besides—she had read the letter's underside, and now she read the room's.

They walked her toward the door. Behind her, Vael was giving quiet orders; ahead of her, the corridor ran gray and long toward the river gate and the bridge and one day of freedom, and she counted the guards—one, two, three, four, five, six, seven—for a woman who was supposed to be released, and she noted that none of them would meet her eyes, and she noted that the escort's route bent, very slightly, away from the river gate and toward the Spire's interior stairs, and she understood.

To the letter. To the letter, and no further.

"Kestrel!" She twisted in the guards' grip, found his eyes one last time across the white room. He was already turning, already being taken the other way, deeper into the Spire, toward the heart of it, and she shouted the only thing left worth shouting, the thing she would have carved on her own memory-bone if anyone ever made one of her: "It wasn't wasted! Do you hear me? Whatever happens—it wasn't wasted!"

And then the door closed between them, and the white room was gone, and Sera Quill went down into the dark of the Spire with six guards and a lie, toward the place where the crown was kept, toward the binding that frayed and wore and hungered, and she smiled—not because she was beaten, but because Vael had made one mistake, the only mistake a careful man can make.

He had told her the truth about the Rite. New voices were woven in willingly. Freely chosen. A single sentence, her own words.

And Sera Quill had spent thirty years hearing what people truly meant, and she knew exactly which sentence she would choose, and it was not the one Vael expected, and when the crown reached for her voice it would reach for a hook dressed as a surrender—and she would be waiting for it, smiling, furious, and free.
