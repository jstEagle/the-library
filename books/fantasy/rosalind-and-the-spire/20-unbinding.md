## 20. Unbinding

The Spire's heart was a room that should not have existed.

Kestrel felt it before he saw it—a pressure behind his eyes, a fullness in his ears like standing at the bottom of a well. The collar was long gone, cracked to fragments in the vault beneath the Inquisition, and without it the voices came at him in a flood: every bound thing in the Spire screaming at once, a cathedral of suffering with him standing in its nave.

"Kestrel." Sera's hand found his arm in the dark. Her fingers were shaking. "The monks are holding. As long as they can."

Through the stone beneath his feet he heard them—the drowned chorus of Saint Aldric's monastery, freed three days past and still singing, their prayers turned outward now instead of inward. They had followed him. They were holding the doors of the Spire against Vael's soldiers with nothing but the weight of their own deaths.

*We bought you minutes,* they sang. *Spend them well.*

Ahead, the corridor opened into a chamber of white bone and black glass. The Spire's heart. And there, suspended above an altar of fused vertebrae, hung the crown.

It was smaller than he remembered from the throne room. Smaller than it had any right to be, for all it contained. A circlet of yellowed ivory, worked into shapes that hurt to look at—faces, hundreds of them, pressed into the material like flowers pressed between pages. Some of the faces were screaming. Some had stopped screaming so long ago that only the shape of the scream remained.

And inside it, faint as a candle seen through a wall, a voice.

*Is someone there?*

Kestrel's breath left him. Fifteen years he had listened to the dead. He knew every register of their speech—the whispers, the wails, the flat recitations of those who had forgotten they had ever been alive. But this voice was none of those. This voice was *waiting*. It had been waiting so long that hope had worn down to something thin and bright as wire.

"I'm here," Kestrel said aloud, and his voice cracked on it. "Your Majesty. I'm here."

*You can hear me.* Wonder, then terror, then a rush of words like a dam breaking. *You can hear me—you have to go, boy, you have to run, it knows when I speak, it will feel—*

The crown screamed.

Not the king. The other thing inside it. The sound that came out of the ivory then was not a sound at all but a tearing, a pressure, a cold that dropped the temperature of the chamber ten degrees in a heartbeat. The faces in the crown writhed. The black glass walls of the room fogged white with frost.

And out of the frost, the regent-thing assembled itself.

Kestrel had seen it across throne rooms and processional squares, always at distance, always wrapped in ceremony. Up close it was worse than any rumor. It wore the king's body the way a glove wears a hand—correctly, precisely, and yet somehow *inside out*. The face was the king's face, handsome and gray-bearded, but the eyes moved wrong, tracking things that were not in the room. Where its shadow should have fallen, there was instead a second shadow, thinner, older, shaped like something that had once crawled rather than walked.

"You," it said. Its voice was the king's voice played backward through grief. "The little ledger-keeper. The bone-listener. Do you know how many of your kind I have unmade?"

"I keep accounts," Kestrel said. His hands were steady. He was surprised by that. "I'd like to see yours."

Something moved behind the thing's eyes—amusement, or hunger; with it, the two had long since merged. "You have two of the three. You gave up the third to save your witch." It smiled with the king's mouth, and the smile did not reach anything living. "Did you think I would simply let you walk them into my heart?"

Behind Kestrel, boots rang on stone. He did not turn. He knew that tread.

"Inquisitor," the regent-thing said warmly. "You arrive at last."

Vael walked past him into the chamber, and Kestrel finally looked at him—at the man who had collared him, dragged him, hunted him across half a kingdom, and who had stood in a cell two nights ago offering him everything short of mercy. Vael's armor was scorched. There was blood on his cheek that might have been his own. In his hands he carried a case of black iron, and Kestrel knew what was inside it before the lid opened: the fingerbone of the old queen, and the sternum of Saint Aldric. Everything gained. Everything lost in a single choice made out of love.

He did not regret it. That was the terrible truth he carried. If they put Sera in front of him again, he would make the same trade again, and again, until there was nothing left to trade.

"The bones, Inquisitor," the regent-thing said.

"Yes." Vael set the case on the altar. His eyes found Kestrel's, and held them, and there was something in them Kestrel had never seen before—not triumph, not hatred. *Grief*, worn like a uniform two sizes too small. "But first, a word with the carver."

"Sera—" Kestrel started.

"Is alive. Unbound. In a cell she could escape from if she truly wished to—and she does not wish to, because she is waiting to learn whether you live." Vael's jaw tightened. "That is the last kindness I will do either of you. Understand me, Crane. I read your file. All of it. Every ghost you carved free, every ledger entry, every name you hid from my lists for six years." He drew a breath. "You are the best of the gifted I have ever hunted. And that is why you must understand what I am about to say."

"Then say it."

"The binding was an act of mercy." Vael's voice was quiet, and that made it worse. "Fifteen years ago the kingdom was dying. Plague in the lowlands, famine in the hills, three claimants raising armies. The old king dead, this one crowned over a pyre of lawsuits and knives. And the regent—" he nodded at the thing wearing the king, without flinching, without looking away, "—the regent ended it. One will. One order. No succession crisis, no civil war. Fifteen years of peace, purchased with one man's voice."

"Purchased with *every* voice," Kestrel said. "Every gifted soul burned or collared since. Every monk drowned mid-prayer. You call that peace?"

"I call it *order*." For the first time, Vael's composure fractured, just at the edge. "You have never watched a kingdom tear itself apart, Crane. I have. I was a child in Corvane when the interregnum came. I ate bark. I watched my mother sell my sister for a sack of meal. Order is worth any price. Any price. And I will pay it myself if I must."

He turned to the altar. He lifted the queen's fingerbone in both hands, the way a priest lifts a relic, the way a man lifts a thing he has hated all his life and cannot put down.

"And now," Vael said, "I complete the binding."

*No,* screamed the king's voice, thin and far. *No, please—*

*Kestrel,* said another voice, closer, warm as a hearth in winter. *Now. While they are busy. NOW.*

The ghosts rose.

They came up through the floor of the Spire's heart like water finding its level—every ghost Kestrel had ever carved, every memory he had ever freed from whalebone and walrus tusk and the cheap grave-goods of paupers' coffins. The drowned girl with her last words finished at last. The soldier who had dictated letters home for eleven years. The midwife, the thief, the child who had died asking for its mother and gotten instead a stranger with a carving knife and patient hands. They poured into the chamber as light, as cold, as a sound below hearing, and where they passed, the frost on the black glass bloomed into patterns like ferns, like feathers, like wings.

The regent-thing shrieked and spun. "*What is this—*"

"You kept accounts too," Kestrel said. "So did I."

He had planned none of this. That was the truth he would carry afterward, in the years when he tried to remember how it had happened: there had been no plan, only a door opening. When the monks of Saint Aldric had been freed, they had not scattered into whatever waits beyond death. They had *remembered*. They had told the others. And the others had told everyone Kestrel had ever released, and somewhere in the deep dark below the world, the freed dead had begun to gather, the way birds gather before a storm, the way a crowd gathers outside a prison on the morning of an execution—because they knew. Because they had all been bound once, and they knew what binding was, and they had come to end it.

"*Impossible,*" the regent-thing gasped, and for the first time its voice was its own—no king's timbre, no borrowed majesty, just a raw dry rasp like wind through a ribcage. "The bindings hold. Bindings *always* hold—"

"Not," said the drowned girl, speaking through Kestrel's own mouth because he let her, because after eleven years of dictation she had earned it, "when someone keeps the ledger."

Vael moved. Of course he did—he was Vael, and the order he served was cracking around him, and he would die holding the pieces together because there was nothing else in him to hold. He drove the queen's fingerbone toward the altar, toward the socket where the third bone would complete the circuit, and Kestrel threw himself across the stone between them.

The bone touched Kestrel's chest instead.

Light went through him.

Not pain—or not only pain. It was every voice he had ever heard arriving at once, no longer filtered through gift or collar or careful craft, raw and total, the entire choir of the dead given leave to sing. He heard the king crying out fifteen years of his son's name. He heard the monks finishing a prayer begun four hundred years ago. He heard the regent-thing—not the monster, the *person* it had been, some desperate court sorcerer centuries back who had reached for the crown to save a dying dynasty and been swallowed by it, screaming inside its own stolen shape, *I didn't mean this, I didn't mean any of this*—and Kestrel, even now, even here, felt pity lance through him like a splinter of ice.

And then he heard the crown begin to break.

It did not shatter the way glass shatters. It split the way ice splits on a spring river—one crack, then a hundred, then a sound like the whole sky clearing its throat. The faces in the ivory came loose one by one, each releasing a voice as it fell: queens and fools and soldiers and children, fifteen years of the harvested dead pouring out of their prison in a column of silver light that punched upward through the Spire's heart, through the Spire itself, through roof and cloud and night.

The regent-thing unraveled.

There was no better word. It came apart the way smoke comes apart in wind, the king's shape sloughing off it in strips, and beneath the shape was the thing it had been, and beneath the thing was almost nothing at all—a hunger, a habit, a very old fear of a very old darkness. It screamed as it went. It screamed in its own voice at last, and the scream was small, and human, and terrible, and then it was gone, and the throne room above them, Kestrel somehow knew, held nothing but an empty chair and falling dust.

Vael was on his knees beside the altar.

There was a wound in him that Kestrel's eye refused, at first, to understand—a shard of the splitting crown, thrown like a spear, driven through the Inquisitor's chest. He had stepped into its path. Kestrel understood that with a horrible clarity. At the last instant, when the bones flew and the light erupted, Vael had moved—not away, but *between*, putting his body where the regent-thing had stood, because the regent-thing was the order, and the order was dying, and he was going to die with it or not at all.

"Crane." Blood at his lips. His voice was already distant, already halfway to wherever he had spent his life sending others. "Was I—"

Kestrel knelt. He took the man's hand. He had spent his life listening to the dead, and he knew what the dying needed most was not absolution but *witness*.

"You believed," Kestrel said. "All the way through. You believed."

Vael smiled. It transformed his face, made him briefly, appallingly young. "Then write it down," he whispered. "In your ledger. Write that I—"

He did not finish. Kestrel waited, listening, the way he always waited, giving the voice time to find its last words.

There were no last words. There was only silence—and then, faintly, rising from the cooling body, a sound Kestrel had never heard before: a voice leaving a body *freely*, unbound, uncarved, going wherever the freed dead go, needing nothing from him at all.

Kestrel bowed his head over the Inquisitor of the Crown, and wept.

* * *

The light was still rising when Sera found him.

She came through the shattered doors with ash in her hair and the monks' song behind her, and she took in the scene—the broken crown in pieces on the altar, Vael's body, Kestrel kneeling in the center of it all with silver light pouring through him like a man standing under a waterfall—and she ran to him.

"Kestrel. Kestrel, look at me. Are you hurt? Kestrel—"

"It doesn't hurt," he said.

And then he realized he hadn't heard her.

Not her footsteps. Not her breathing. Not the monks, though their song filled the Spire from foundation to spire. Not the dead girl, not the soldier, not the king—who was standing, Kestrel saw dimly through the fading light, at the edge of the chamber, a gray-bearded man in funeral clothes, weeping, mouthing words of thanks that Kestrel could no longer read.

The gift was burning away. He could feel it going—not torn, not taken, but *spent*, poured out through him in the great release, every drop of it gone into the unbinding the way a candle gives itself entirely to the flame. The silence that rushed in behind it was the largest thing he had ever felt. It was not empty. It was enormous. It was the silence of a room after music, holding the shape of everything that had sounded there.

Sera was saying his name. He could see her lips move. He could see the tears on her face, and the wonder, and the fear.

He reached up and touched his own ears, and began, helplessly, to laugh.

* * *

By dawn, the Spire belonged to no one and everyone. The people of the capital came out into the streets to see the light still rising from its peak like a second sunrise, and the gifted came out of their cellars and attics and hiding places, drawn by the sudden absence of the hunt, by the silence of the Inquisition's bells, by a feeling in the air like a held breath finally let go.

Kestrel sat on the Spire's steps with Sera's shoulder warm against his, deaf to the dead for the first time since he was six years old, watching the freed king walk barefoot down the great stair into a kingdom that did not yet know what to do with him.

"He's looking at you," Sera said, close to his ear, loud enough to reach him. "The king. He keeps looking at you."

Kestrel shook his head slowly. There would be honors. There would be a seat at court, a title, perhaps a tower of his own with a forge and a workshop and servants. He already knew, with the calm certainty of a man balancing a final column, that he would refuse all of it.

Because down in the city, moving through the waking streets, he could see them coming—not voices now, never voices again, but *people*: bone-hearers and dream-readers and ash-speakers, the hunted and the hidden, walking openly toward the guildhall across the river, where a new ledger lay open on a clean desk, waiting for names.

Sera squeezed his hand. "What will you tell them?" she asked. "When they ask what happened?"

Kestrel looked at the broken crown, at the light, at the silent, enormous morning.

He opened his mouth to answer—and found, for the first time in his life, that the words would have to come from him alone.
