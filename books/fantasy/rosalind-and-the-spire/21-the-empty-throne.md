## 21. The Empty Throne

Sera Quill had spent her whole life learning how to leave places.

Hedge-craft taught you that early: never sleep twice in the same barn, never owe the same person twice, never let a village learn your face well enough to miss it when the mob came with torches and righteous certainty. She had left forty-one towns, three cities, one burning abbey, and a man in Marrow's Ford who still owed her six silver pieces and an apology. Leaving was the one craft she'd mastered before her gift ever woke.

So it was a strange and bitter thing to stand in the Spire's great hall, three days after the world broke open, and realize she did not want to go.

The throne room had not been cleaned yet. No one had dared. The regent-thing had unraveled at its center like a rope of rotted silk, and what remained of it lay where it fell—a drift of grey ash and something finer, almost like hair, that the wind through the shattered windows kept trying to carry away and the stone kept refusing to give up. Servants had swept everywhere else. Here they walked wide, eyes down, as though the floor itself were grieving.

And above it all, on the dais, sat the empty throne.

Not empty of a king—the king stood beside it, talking quietly with a cluster of exhausted lords who kept glancing at him as if he might dissolve too. Empty of *weight*. The crown was gone. Kestrel had split it, and the split had gone all the way down through fifteen years of stolen reigns, and now Aldric the Second—Aldric the Returned, the bells were already calling him—stood in plain wool with silver threading his beard and a stoop to his shoulders that no portrait had ever shown.

He looked, Sera thought, like a man who had been screaming for a decade and a half and had only just been allowed to stop.

She leaned against a pillar near the doors, arms crossed, watching. Her body still ached from the binding Vael had tried to force on her—the collar's cold was gone but its memory lived in her throat, a phantom band she kept touching the way a tongue worries a missing tooth. Three days, and she still woke at night reaching for her own voice, terrified it wouldn't answer.

It always answered. That was the miracle she couldn't stop testing.

"—and the Inquisition's records will be opened," the king was saying. "Every name taken, every bone confiscated, returned or released as their keepers' kin determine. I will not rebuild this kingdom on the same foundation the thing used."

"Your Majesty," said a lord whose rings probably cost more than Sera's entire criminal career, "the gifted cannot simply be—*restored*. There must be oversight. Registration, at minimum. Some structure of—"

"There will be structure." The king's voice was quiet, and every lord in earshot went still, because they had all heard stories by now about what quiet meant from a man who had spent fifteen years listening to himself be buried alive. "Structure built *with* them, my lord, not upon them. If that displeases anyone, the door is behind you."

No one moved toward the door.

Sera smiled despite herself. Fifteen years bound in a crown and the man came out of it knowing exactly which levers to pull. Of course he did. He'd had nothing to do for a decade and a half except listen—to the court, through the crown's stolen ears; to the regent-thing's thoughts, muffled but constant; to the whole rotten machinery of a kingdom run by a parasite wearing his face. He knew everything. He was the most dangerous kind of ruler: one with no illusions left to flatter.

Her gaze found Kestrel across the hall.

He stood apart, as he always did, near the broken windows where the morning light came in grey and clean over the city. He was holding one of the guild ledgers—he seemed to hold one constantly now, like a talisman—and he wasn't reading it. He was just looking out at the rooftops, at the smoke rising from ten thousand chimneys, at a kingdom waking up to find itself changed overnight.

He hadn't heard anything since the unbinding. She'd checked, gently, twice. Snapped her fingers beside his ear while he slept. Spoken into the silence behind him. Nothing. The gift that had filled his whole life with the dead had burned away in the same fire that freed them, and he carried his deafness now the way she carried her scarred throat: carefully, publicly, like a wound that had purchased something worth the price.

He looked smaller without it. And somehow more solid.

"Sera Quill."

She turned. A page stood at her elbow—young, nervous, clearly instructed to be polite to the hedge-witch in the patched coat because the king had personally said so. "His Majesty requests your presence. Both yours and Master Crane's."

"Both of us, is it." She pushed off the pillar. "Lead on, then. Before the lords work up the courage to object to me."

* * *

The king received them in a small chamber off the hall—not the throne room, she noticed, not yet, maybe never again—and dismissed his attendants before either of them had finished bowing. Which told her everything about what kind of audience this would be.

"You're the one who held the door," he said to her first.

She blinked. "Majesty?"

"In the drowned monastery. Kestrel told me. You held a flooded doorway against the pressure of the sea for—" he glanced at Kestrel, "—how long?"

"Long enough," Kestrel said.

"Four minutes," Sera said. "Give me my due. Four minutes and some change, and I coughed seawater for two days, and nobody thanked me properly."

"I'm thanking you now." The king's voice was dry, but there was warmth under it, banked like coals. "You held a door so that a saint's sternum could be carried out of the dark, and that bone helped crack the crown that held me prisoner for fifteen years. So. Thanking you now, and formally, and in front of witnesses when I do it properly at the renewal—I mean, at the coronation." Something flickered across his face at the slip, old grief or old habit, and passed. "There is a great deal to rebuild, and I find I want builders who have already proven they can break things correctly."

Kestrel shifted. Sera watched him do it—the small tell, the weight moving from foot to foot, the ledger clutched a little tighter. She'd known him barely a season and she could read him like one of his own bones. He already knew what was coming. He'd probably known since the moment the crown split.

"Kestrel Crane," the king said. "Bone-carver. Ledger-keeper of the Guild of Impossible Things. The man who heard me screaming inside my own crown when no one else in this kingdom could." He stepped closer, and up close the change in him was starker—the mortal sag of him, the human tiredness, the eyes that had seen the inside of his own imprisonment. "I am offering you a seat at this court. Not a decoration. A seat. Warden of Voices, if you want a title—we'll invent a better one. Authority over the disposition of every bound relic in the realm. A place in the council that decides, going forward, what happens to people like you. People like her." A nod at Sera. "People like the ones I failed."

The words hung in the room, enormous, gilded.

Sera held her breath. Part of her wanted him to say yes—gods, part of her wanted it for purely selfish reasons, because a friend with a seat at the high table was a friend who could pull every hunted soul in the kingdom out of the fire with a signature. But another part of her, the part that had survived forty-one departures, already knew.

Kestrel was quiet for a long moment. Then he set the ledger down on the king's own table, carefully, spine aligned with the edge, the way he did everything.

"Majesty. When I was a boy, my mother sold me to a bone-broker for two crowns and a promise." His voice was even, unhurried. "The promise was that I'd be fed and taught and safe. What I learned, in the years after, is that promises made by people who own you are just prices written in prettier ink. Every offer I've ever been given came with a collar attached somewhere. The guildmaster collared me to save her guild. The Inquisitor collared me to hunt my own kind. The regent-thing offered me visions of power, honestly given, and it nearly had me—it was *kind*, Majesty, that's the horror of it, it was kinder than most men." He met the king's eyes. "You're offering me a chair at a table where thrones get decided. I know you mean it kindly. I believe you mean it kindly. But a seat at court is just a crown you sit in instead of wear, and I have spent this whole year learning what crowns do to the heads beneath them."

The silence stretched. One of the lords' voices drifted faintly from the great hall, arguing about registration.

"I watched you listen," Kestrel went on, softer now. "Fifteen years, bound, hearing everything, powerless to act. That's what power is, from underneath. From above it's the same thing with better furniture. I don't want the furniture. I want my bench, and my knives, and my ledger, and the road." He paused, and something in his face gentled. "What I will do, freely, for as long as you want it: I'll consult. When you find a bound bone and don't know whether to free it or bury it, send word to the guildhall, and someone will come who can hear—or, failing that, someone who remembers how. But I won't live here. I won't sit there. I'm sorry."

The king looked at him for a long time. Then, unexpectedly, he laughed—a short, rusty sound, like a hinge unused for years.

"Fifteen years," he said, "I dreamed about the day I'd be free enough to choose my own council. And the first man I choose tells me no." He shook his head slowly. "Good. *Good.* Do you know how many yes-men I've endured? An entire kingdom of them, nodding at the thing that wore my face. Take your bench, Master Crane. Keep your road. But hear me—" and his voice dropped into something that was not quite a command and not quite a plea, "—when I send word, someone comes. Swear it."

"I swear it," Kestrel said. "On the guild's ledger. We trade in impossible things, Majesty. A king who keeps his word qualifies."

* * *

They walked out together through the long gallery, past windows still boarded from the unbinding, past servants who stared and whispered and didn't quite dare approach. Sera waited until the gallery emptied, until it was just the two of them and the grey light, and then she stopped walking.

"So," she said. "Exile."

"It's not exile if you choose it."

"That's literally the definition, Kestrel. I've checked. Professionally."

He huffed something that was almost a laugh. They stood in the cold light, and she realized with a small, sharp pain that this might be the last time they stood anywhere together, just the two of them, with nothing trying to kill them.

"You could stay," she said. It came out rougher than she intended. "You heard him. Warden of Voices. You could fix it from the inside—all of it, the registries, the vaults, every bone in every cellar from here to the border. You'd be good at it. You'd be *miserable*, but you'd be good at it."

"And then in twenty years I'd be the lord everyone nods at," he said, "and some kid with a gift would look at me and see the collar instead of the hand. No." He picked up the ledger again, turned it over in his hands. "The guild needs rebuilding. Odile burned half our names and sold the other half. Someone has to write the new book. Someone has to open the doors." He glanced at her sideways. "Come with me."

And there it was. The question she'd been circling for three days, the one she'd been avoiding by counting ceiling beams and cataloging her aches and memorizing the Spire's corridors as if knowing them could make staying reasonable.

Because she wanted to. That was the terrible, disorienting truth of it. She wanted to walk back to that crooked guildhall with its burnt ledger and its smell of whalebone and tallow, wanted a bench of her own and a door that locked from the inside and a friend across the room who didn't flinch at what she was. She'd spent her whole life being the thing villages burned, and Kestrel had looked at her hedge-craft, her thieving, her four-minutes-against-the-sea stubbornness, and called it *useful*. Called *her* useful. Nobody had ever—

But.

"The king asked me to stay," she said quietly. "Did he tell you? Last night. He wants hedge-witches in the new order. Wants people who learned magic in ditches and kitchens, not towers. Says the towers are how we got the regent." She laughed, hollow. "Me. Advising a king. My mother would have died laughing, if she'd bothered to stick around long enough to meet a king."

"Sera—"

"He's right, though. That's the worst part. Somebody has to stay and make sure it holds. Somebody who knows how fast these things rot." She looked out the boarded window, at the sliver of city visible between planks—smoke, bells, a kingdom deciding what to become. "If I leave now, I'll always wonder. And wondering's worse than drowning. I did both once; I know."

Kestrel was silent. Then he nodded, slow, and she saw him take it the way he took everything—carefully, completely, carving it into himself somewhere permanent.

"The guildhall will have a room," he said. "Always. With a window facing east, if you want it. And a lock on the outside of the door, so you can leave whenever you need to and know it'll still be there."

"That's the most romantic thing anyone's ever said to me," Sera said, and her voice cracked on the last word, and she covered it badly with a cough, and he pretended not to notice, which was the kindest thing he'd ever done for her.

They stood together a moment longer. Then he held out his hand—not a courtier's handshake, but forearm to forearm, smuggler's grip, the grip of people who'd crawled out of a vault and a flood and a burning rite side by side. She took it.

"For the ledger," he said. "What name do I write you under? Sera Quill, hedge-witch, advisor to the crown?"

She considered. Forty-one towns. Three cities. One burning abbey. Six silver pieces in Marrow's Ford.

"Write *friend*," she said. "Under occupations. First entry in the new book."

He squeezed her arm once, hard, and let go, and walked away down the gallery with his ledger under his arm and the grey light on his shoulders, getting smaller, getting gone, and she stood there in the palace of a restored king and felt the strangest grief of her life: mourning a departure that hadn't happened yet, for a life she was choosing not to live.

Then she straightened her patched coat, wiped her face with her sleeve, and went to find the king's steward, because there were registries to tear up and vaults to open and a kingdom to keep honest, and gods help anyone who got in her way.

Behind her, far below in the heart of the Spire, workers had begun clearing the ash where the regent-thing had fallen. And among the fine grey drift, one of them found something that did not burn: a single sliver of bone, thin as a splinter, cold as deep winter—which, when the worker's bare finger touched it, whispered a single word in a voice like the closing of a door.

*Again.*

The worker heard nothing. But the bone remembered. And somewhere in the dark between the walls of the world, something old took note that a crown, once split, leaves a shape behind—and began, patiently, to look for a head to fill it.
