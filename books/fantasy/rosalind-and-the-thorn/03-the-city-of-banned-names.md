## 3. The City of Banned Names

Cael had spent four years learning the city's underside, and still the way down made his palms sweat.

He led Orsen through the tannery district after midnight, past vats that reeked of lye and rot, to a drainage grate no wider than a coffin. She looked at it, then at him, with the flat expression of someone deciding whether to be insulted.

"You're joking."

"The Wardens don't joke either. That's the point." He pried the grate up with an iron hook he kept chained inside his coat. "In first. I'll follow."

She went. That was the first thing he noted about her—she didn't argue long, and she didn't hesitate where it counted. The second thing was the way she kept one hand pressed flat against her satchel the whole time, as if the map inside might bolt like a spooked animal.

It might. He'd seen what it did in the forest. He'd been tracking Dossen Hale for three weeks when the man's trail simply ended in a stand of birches that grew at angles trees shouldn't grow, and then the air had torn open and spat out a soot-streaked woman holding a folded square of paper that breathed.

The tunnels under Thornhollow were older than the city above them. Cael knew this because the Undercroft's archivists had proven it—dug through strata of brick and clay until they found walls laid by hands that predated the Sundering War itself. The founders liked to say Thornhollow rose from nothing, pure and magicless, a monument to what humanity could build without the old corruption. The truth was less flattering. The city had been built on top of something, and everyone in power had agreed never to look down.

He watched Orsen navigate the dark. She moved like a person who'd grown up in cellars, counting her steps, reading the air. When they reached the third junction she stopped without being told and said, "There's water ahead. Moving."

"Undercroft cistern. You've been down here before?"

"No." A pause. "My mother drew maps of places she'd never been. Maybe some of it sticks."

He let that sit. He'd read the purge records; he knew which family names had burned brightest. He wasn't ready to hand her that knowledge yet, not in a tunnel with no exit but forward.

* * *

The Undercroft announced itself by lamplight—dozens of lamps, wasteful and defiant, hung along a vaulted corridor that had once been, by the masonry, a temple or a granary or both. People moved through the glow: a woman grinding pigment, two men arguing over a ledger, a girl no older than ten copying text onto scraps of bleached linen. Everyone glanced at Orsen. Nobody stared, which in the Undercroft was a form of courtesy.

"You have children down here," Orsen said quietly.

"We have everyone down here eventually." Cael steered her toward the archive room, where Maren held court most nights. "The Wardens' trials take parents. The workhouses take the rest. We take what's left."

"And you call yourselves archivists."

"Some of us. Some call themselves soldiers. Maren calls us all witnesses." He stopped at a door banded in green copper. "One thing before we go in. They'll ask what the dying man told you. Tell them everything."

Orsen's face did something small and controlled. "Everything," she repeated.

"He gave you a map that redraws itself. That makes you the most important person in this city, whether you want to be or not. They deserve the whole truth."

"I agree," she said, and he believed her, and that was the last honest moment between them for a long while.

* * *

Maren was fifty, broad as a door, with grey shot through her black hair and hands stained permanently blue from decades of ink work. She'd been the Warden archive's chief cataloguer before she'd read too much and asked too many questions. Now she ran the Undercroft the way she'd run her old department: ruthlessly organized, allergic to sentiment.

She looked at Orsen for a long moment, then at the satchel.

"Open it."

Orsen opened it. The map lay on the table between them, and even folded it seemed restless, its edges shifting like something asleep and dreaming of running.

"Dossen Hale carried this out of the prison-gate country," Maren said. "We tracked him for two years. Do you know what he was?"

"A stranger who bled on my stall."

"He was my colleague. Before your time. Before mine, almost." Maren unfolded the map with hands that were steady and careful, the way one handles a wound. "Look at the western margin. See the notation?"

Cael leaned in. In faded brown ink, half eroded: *the thirteenth turn approaches. the gate thins.*

"He wrote that eleven days ago," Maren said. "Then he walked into Thornhollow, found the one stall in the night market selling charms that weren't quite charms, and died pressing this into a stranger's hands." Her eyes came up. "Because he recognized something. Didn't he."

It wasn't a question. Cael saw Orsen decide—not visibly, nothing so crude—but he'd spent years reading people in interrogation transcripts, and he knew the micro-architecture of a lie being built. He said nothing. He wanted to hear what she'd build.

"He said 'the thirteenth turn,'" Orsen said. "That's all. He was dying. It didn't make sense."

Every word true. Every word arranged into a wall. Cael felt something turn over in his chest—not anger, not yet. Recognition. He knew what it was to hold a piece of truth back because the whole truth would cost more than you could pay.

Maren studied her the way she studied forged documents. Then she nodded slowly, and Cael understood she hadn't believed a word—and had decided to proceed anyway. That was Maren. Truth could be excavated later. Momentum mattered now.

"Then let me tell you what doesn't make sense," Maren said, and pulled a ledger from beneath the table, ancient, its cover scarred with burn marks. "The official history says the Sundering War ended when the founders destroyed the Heartroot. Burned it out of the world. Purged the magicians, razed their enclaves, and built Thornhollow on the ashes. Clean. Final."

"That's the story every schoolchild learns," Orsen said.

"It's the story written by the victors, and it is a lie in its central fact." Maren opened the ledger. The pages were covered in a script Cael had spent four years learning to read—the pre-Sundering cartographic shorthand, the language of people who mapped living things. "You cannot destroy the source of magic. Magic isn't a fire. It's a root system. Cut it down and it grows back through whatever soil remains. So the founders didn't destroy it." She turned the page. A drawing filled it: a gate, ringed in thirteen moons, inked in lines that seemed to move if you didn't look directly. "They buried it. Caged it behind a seal keyed to the lunar cycle. And then they built their charter on the lie, and their Wardens on the charter, and for two hundred years the peace of Thornhollow has rested on a prison nobody admits exists."

Cael watched Orsen's hands. They'd gone very still on the table's edge, and there was a tremor in them that had nothing to do with fear.

"The map leads to the gate," Maren continued. "Hale died getting it out because the Wardens harvest from the Heartroot—a trickle, enough to fuel their instruments, their authority, their immortality of influence. If the seal fails on the thirteenth turn, they lose everything. So they'll be moving. And so will we." She closed the ledger. "You carried this map out of a forest that doesn't exist. It chose you. Whatever you think you know, whatever you're keeping close—" her voice gentled by a degree, "—you're one of us now. There's a bunk, a ration card, and a place at the archive table. All we ask is honesty."

Belonging, offered freely, on false pretenses. Cael saw Orsen feel it—he saw the hunger flicker across her face before she killed it. Seven years selling harmless charms in a city that outlawed her. Of course she wanted in. Of course she lied to get there.

"Thank you," Orsen said. "I'll help however I can."

Maren dismissed them both with a nod toward the dormitory corridor, and Cael walked Orsen out through the lamplight, past the girl copying texts, past the woman grinding pigment, into a side alcove with two pallets and a curtain for a door.

"You lied to her," he said quietly.

Orsen set the satchel down. "So did you, when you told her the trail went cold instead of telling her about the forest."

"That's different."

"It's exactly the same." She sat on the pallet, exhausted suddenly, all at once, like a marionette with cut strings. "I'll tell them. When I understand it myself. Right now all I have is a dead man's whisper and a map that won't stop moving, and if I say that out loud, your leader will start making plans around guesses. I've seen what happens when powerful people plan around guesses." Something old and cold moved behind her eyes. "People burn."

He should have pushed. Four years in the Undercroft had taught him that unchallenged lies calcified into policy. But looking at her—soot-streaked, hollow-eyed, gripping that satchel like a drowning woman grips driftwood—he heard himself say, "Get some sleep. Maren holds council at dawn."

He turned to go, and her voice caught him at the curtain.

"Cael. The stranger. Hale." She was staring at her own hands. "When he pressed the map into my hands, he said something else. Something I haven't told anyone." A breath. "He called me by name. My full name. A name I haven't used since I was nine years old."

The lamp guttered. Somewhere in the deep tunnels, water moved through stone, patient as a countdown.

"And the worst part," Orsen said, "is that I think he knew my mother."
