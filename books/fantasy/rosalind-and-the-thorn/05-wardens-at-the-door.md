## 5. Wardens at the Door

Maren had always known the Undercroft would fall. She had planned for it the way a sailor plans for drowning—practically, without sentiment, with the lifeboats counted twice. What she had never planned for was the sound of it: the first Warden boot on the stair, iron-shod and unhurried, because the Wardens never hurried when they already knew they'd won.

She heard it from the map room, where she'd been awake since the second bell, checking the ledger of names. Forty-one souls slept below Thornhollow's foundations, in the old cisterns and archive vaults the founders had sealed and forgotten. Forty-one people who trusted her to know when to run.

The boots told her the running was over.

"Up," she said quietly, and then louder, moving through the corridors, hammering on doors with the flat of her hand. "Wardens. Everyone up. Now."

The Undercroft woke the way it had drilled: no screaming, no stampede. Lamps guttered out one by one as hands smothered them. Children were lifted half-asleep onto shoulders. The archivists wrapped their most precious documents in oilcloth and slung them across their backs like soldiers shouldering packs. Maren watched it happen with cold pride and colder arithmetic—the east tunnel was blocked for repairs, the river gate needed four minutes to open, and the Wardens would be through the main stair in two.

The numbers did not work. They never had.

* * *

Cael reached her at the junction by the dry well, Orsen close behind him, the cartographer's face pale but steady. Maren had seen that look before, on people the night before their trials. It wasn't fear. It was someone deciding what they were willing to become.

"The river gate," Cael said. "I can get it open if you buy me time."

"You have none." Maren kept her voice level. Above them, through the ceiling of earth and brick, the boots had stopped. That was worse than the marching. Stopping meant they were positioning. Stopping meant they knew every exit but one, and were savoring the last.

"They're waiting for something," Orsen said.

Maren turned on her. "They're waiting because we're not going anywhere. Because someone sold us." She let the words land. "Three days ago a runner came back early from the tannery district. Tam Voss. He's been asking questions about our routes since midsummer, and tonight he's missing, and the Wardens are at our door. You do the sums."

Orsen's jaw tightened. "You think I brought them here."

"I think you arrived three weeks ago carrying the single most dangerous object in this city, and I think the man who gave it to you died within sight of a Warden patrol post. So yes. I think the door they're opening was unlocked by your arrival, whether you meant it or not."

"Maren." Cael stepped between them. "Not now."

"Now is exactly when. If we survive this, she needs to understand what her secrets cost." Maren looked past him at Orsen. "Whatever that map is, whatever it showed you in the dark while my people slept—tonight it stops being yours alone. Either you trust all of us with it, or you've been using us as a wall to hide behind."

Above, the ceiling boomed. Once. Twice. A ram, or a shaped charge of that dead grey powder the Wardens favored. Dust sifted down through the lamplight like grey snow.

And then the world tore open.

It didn't come through the stair. It came through the wall—a seam of white light splitting the brickwork, widening, and through it stepped High Warden Vess herself, or so Maren would learn later; at the time she only saw the silver-grey coat, the bare head, the utter calm of a woman walking into a room she owned. Behind her came a dozen Wardens in file, blades drawn, and the light sealed shut behind them like water closing over a stone.

"Sealed passages," Maren breathed. "They have passage-magic." The word tasted like ash. Magic, in the hands of the men who burned people for having it. Seven years of raids, of trials, of names read aloud in the square—and the whole time, the Wardens' own hands had never been clean.

Vess's voice carried without effort, pitched for everyone. "By charter of the Founders' Council, you are detained under the Edict of Sundering. Come forward, kneel, and be named. Resist, and be named posthumously."

There was nowhere to go. Maren ran the geometry again and again and it refused to change: forty-one people, one corridor, twelve blades. She caught Cael's eye and saw him reach for the same arithmetic and fail the same way. Some of the younger rebels were already moving forward, hands rising, the old animal instinct to submit to uniformed power.

Then Orsen Dunmore unrolled a map in the middle of a Warden raid.

Maren saw it only in fragments afterward, the way you see lightning. The paper glowing faintly, lines moving on it like things swimming. Orsen's hand pressed flat against the page. Her lips moving around words Maren couldn't hear—or maybe there were no words, maybe the map simply answered to touch the way a lock answers a key.

The air beside the dry well split open.

Not white light. Not the Wardens' clean surgical tear. This was ink—a vertical spill of darkness shot through with threads of gold, like night poured through a crack in the world, and beyond it Maren glimpsed trees. Real trees, moving in real wind, under a sky with too many stars.

"Through!" Orsen shouted. "Now—all of you—through!"

For one heartbeat nobody moved. Forty-one people who had spent their lives being told magic was death stood looking at a door made of it.

Then a child screamed—not in terror, in eagerness—and broke the spell. The Undercroft moved. Archivists shoved families toward the shimmering dark; Cael stood at its edge hauling people through bodily; Maren found herself doing the same, gripping arms, pushing backs, counting heads against her ledger out of pure habit. Nine. Fourteen. Twenty-two. The Wardens recovered from their shock and started forward, Vess's voice cracking out commands, and a young rebel named Pell—sixteen, quick, brave in the way that gets boys killed—turned back to grab his mother's bundle of papers.

A Warden's hand closed on his arm.

Maren lunged, too far away, already knowing it. She saw Pell twist, saw the blade rise, saw the boy who would talk—who would tell them everything, the tunnels, the names, the routes north—staring at her across ten feet of stone he couldn't cross.

And Orsen crossed it for him. The ink-door stretched. It moved across the chamber like a living thing, like a mouth, and swallowed Pell and his captor both, and snapped shut, and the last thing Maren saw before the darkness took her own outstretched hand was the High Warden's face—calm no longer, hungry now, fixed on Orsen Dunmore with an expression Maren recognized from ledgers and wanted posters:

*Found you.*

* * *

They came out into wet grass under a sky salted with stars, scattered across a hillside like seeds flung from a fist. Behind them the ink-door hung in the air for three heartbeats longer, trembling—and through it, before it closed, Maren heard the Wardens shouting, and saw torchlight, and understood with a lurch that the door had opened onto somewhere the Wardens could follow. Somewhere with tracks. Somewhere findable.

Then it folded itself away, and there was only wind, and thirty-eight people breathing hard in the dark.

Thirty-eight. She counted twice. Three hadn't made it out—one dead in the raid before the door opened, she'd seen the body—and Pell was gone, taken, dragged through to the other side alone. Sixteen years old, terrified, and in possession of every secret the Undercroft had. He would talk. Everyone talked, eventually; Maren had built her life on the assumption that everyone talked, which was why she'd built in layers and cutouts and lies. But Pell knew things the layers couldn't protect.

"He'll give them the northern routes," she said. Her voice came out flat. "By tomorrow noon they'll have every safehouse between here and the border."

Cael was kneeling beside Orsen. The cartographer was on her knees in the grass, and even in the starlight Maren could see how grey her face was, how her hands shook around the rolled-up map as though it weighed as much as a person. As though it were drinking her.

"You used it in front of them," Maren said. "In front of *all of them.*"

"I saved their lives."

"You saved their lives and showed thirty-seven witnesses a working passage-spell, and handed the Wardens a captured boy who can describe it down to the color of the light." Maren crouched to bring her face level with Orsen's. She kept her voice low, because the survivors were listening, because hope was a resource and she wouldn't deplete it publicly—but she didn't soften it either. "Do you know what happens now? Every Warden in the province just learned that the Edict is a lie they tell other people. Do you know what a cornered hypocrite does? She burns everything. Vess won't run trials after this. She'll run massacres and call them quarantines."

"Then we move faster than she does." Cael rose, stepping into the space between them again, and Maren felt the old ache of it—he'd been her best archivist, her steadiest hand, and since the day the stranger died he'd been Orsen's shadow first. "Pell doesn't know about the Heartroot. He knows tunnels and names. We can burn those ourselves before the Wardens extract them—we have until dawn, maybe. And the map got thirty-eight people out of a sealed room. Maren. *Thirty-eight.*"

"At what cost?" Maren pointed at Orsen's shaking hands. "Look at her. That thing isn't a tool, it's a debt collector. And you—" she rounded on Cael— "you've stopped seeing it. You look at her and see your sister, and I don't blame you, but I can't afford to. I have thirty-seven people on this hillside and no roof, no stores, no network, because our location is ash. Everything we built. Everything we *were.*"

"Maybe that's the point," Cael said quietly.

"Don't."

"The rebellion existed to fight a lie. Tonight we all watched the Wardens use magic through a wall. The lie is dying anyway, Maren. She's just—" he gestured at the hillside, the scattered survivors, the dark horizon where the thirteenth moon would soon be rising— "she's just faster."

Maren looked at Orsen Dunmore for a long moment. The woman hadn't defended herself. She knelt in the wet grass holding her map like a wound, and when she finally spoke, her voice was hoarse and certain.

"Pell held on to me when the door took him. He felt it pull. He'll tell them it can be followed." She lifted her eyes. "So we stop running from the question and answer it. The map goes where it's going—the Heartroot, the prison, whatever's at the end of that road—and we get there before Vess understands what she has. That's the only version of this where any of us live."

It was reckless. It was the single most reckless proposal Maren had heard in eleven years of underground war, and the worst part, the part that sat in her chest like a swallowed stone, was that it was also the only plan left. The Undercroft was dead. The slow patient work of decades was ash in a single night. All that remained was speed, and a map that drew its own roads, and a cartographer who paid for every mile in pieces of herself.

"Fine," Maren said at last. "We move at first light. And Dunmore—" she held the woman's gaze— "from tonight on, nothing about that map is private. Not from me. Not from anyone walking with us. The next secret you keep is the one that kills us all."

Orsen nodded. But Maren saw it—the flicker, quick as a fish turning, the look of a woman who was already keeping one.

Behind them, low on the eastern horizon, the sky had begun to pale. And above the paling, faint as breath on glass, hung a sliver of moon that had no business existing—a thirteenth moon, waning fast, counting down.
