## 6. The Road That Isn't There

**Orsen**

The map made its first road at dawn, and it did it the way a spider makes a web—patiently, out of nothing, right in front of her.

They had walked all night through forest that had no name on any chart Orsen had ever drawn. The trees grew wrong here, too close together, their bark scarred with old burn marks from the Sundering War that no one was supposed to know had happened. Then the light came up gray through the canopy, and Orsen unfolded the map to check their position, and found a road.

Not marked on the map. *Made* by it—a thin line of ink running westward off the page's edge, and when she looked up from the paper, there it was in the world: a track of pressed earth winding between the trees, soft-edged, new, as if the ground itself had only just decided to be walkable.

"It's building as we go," Cael said beside her. He'd been awake for hours; he slept like a cat, in short suspicious intervals. "I watched it happen around midnight. The line appeared, and then the road followed it. Like the ink is laying down the world ahead of us."

"Roads don't work like that."

"No," Cael agreed. "They don't."

Orsen crouched and put her palm flat against the earth. It was warm. Not sun-warm—the sun hadn't cleared the ridge—but warm the way a sleeping animal is warm, the way the map had been warm against her ribs since the night the stranger died. Since the night *her aunt* died, though she still couldn't make herself say the word even inside her own skull.

The map wanted them moving. She could feel it now, a low pull behind her sternum, gentle as a hand at the small of her back. West. Always west, toward the mountains where the thirteenth moon would turn.

"Then we walk," she said, and folded the map away, and walked.

* * *

**Cael**

He had been tracking Sera Vane for eleven months before she died, and in all that time he had never once seen her face. He knew her by her habits: the way she bought candles in odd numbers, the way she always sat facing doors, the ink stains on her left ring finger that meant she drew with her whole arm, not her wrist. A cartographer's stains. He'd thought, more than once, that if the rebellion ever needed a forger of impossible maps, he'd find her again.

Then she'd bled out at a charm-seller's stall in Thornhollow, and the impossible maps had found him instead.

Cael watched Orsen Dunmore walk the road the map was making and tried to reconcile the two women in his head. Sera had moved like someone carrying something breakable. Orsen moved like someone who'd already dropped it and lived with the sound of the breaking ever since. Same stains on the fingers, though. Same way of holding a pen.

"You're staring," she said, without turning around.

"I'm navigating."

"You're staring at my hands."

He was. "Your aunt drew the same way."

That landed. He saw it land—in the slight hitch of her stride, quickly corrected. They'd been three days on the road and he'd learned her tells: the hitch meant grief, the silence after it meant she was deciding whether to share or hoard. She hoarded more than she shared. He respected it. He was a professional hoarder himself.

"My mother drew that way too," Orsen said finally. "Executed for it. So I learned to draw small. Charms. Trinkets. Maps that stayed inside their borders." She glanced back at him, and the morning light caught the exhaustion under her eyes. "This thing doesn't stay inside anything."

"No," Cael said. "It doesn't."

They crested a rise and the road bent south, obedient to some logic Orsen couldn't see and he could only guess at. Below them lay a valley that should have been farmland—he remembered it from the old surveys, pre-Sundering, grain country—and instead it was a sea of white flowers he had no name for, blooming out of season, out of place, out of every record the Undercroft's archives possessed.

"The land remembers what we burned," Cael said quietly. "That's what Maren thinks. The Sundering didn't kill magic. It drove it underground, into roots and roads and things like this." He gestured at the flowers. "And now the map is walking us along the seams."

"And when we reach the seam's end?"

"Then we find out what they were so afraid of they built an entire city of banned names over it."

* * *

**Orsen**

On the fifth night, the map took something from her.

She didn't notice at first. That was the horror of it—that you could lose a thing and not feel the shape of the absence until you reached for it. They'd made camp in the lee of a stone marker, one of the old boundary stones from before the war, its inscriptions worn to ghosts. Cael was asleep. The moon was wrong overhead—too large, too pale, thirteen days early—and Orsen sat up watching it and reached, idly, for the memory of her mother's voice.

It wasn't there.

Not faded. Not blurred. *Gone*, the way a room is gone after a fire: not empty but absent, a hole with edges. She could remember that her mother had sung to her—she had the fact of it, filed and labeled—but the voice itself, the tune, the words, all of it had been eaten clean. She searched herself the way she'd search a house for a lost key, turning over every memory, and found the space where the song had lived, smooth-walled and vacant, like a cell swept for a prisoner who'd never existed.

Her hands were shaking when she unrolled the map.

In the firelight the ink looked wet. Fresh. And there—at the western edge, where the road they'd walked that day now stood rendered in fine confident lines—she saw it. Tiny lettering, in her aunt's hand, in handwriting she'd never seen before and recognized instantly because it was nearly her own:

*Paid in memory. One song, one voice.*

The map was eating her.

Not her blood this time, not her body—it had taken enough of that already, binding her, pulling her through its own ink. It was feeding on the interior country, the one no cartographer had ever charted. Every mile of road it laid down, every flower-valley it conjured, every impossible bridge over impossible water—all of it paid for out of her.

She thought about the stranger dying at her stall. *The thirteenth turn.* She thought about how calm Sera Vane must have been, pressing this thing into a stranger's hands. Had she known? Had she spent herself down to the last memory, paying for roads she'd never walk?

Orsen looked at Cael, asleep with his back against the boundary stone, his face slack and young in the firelight. Three days ago he'd told her about his sister—how she'd been taken in the trials at fourteen, how the Wardens had returned her name to the family in a sealed envelope with a receipt number, how he'd joined the archives the same year because someone had to remember what the city wanted forgotten. He'd told her with his voice perfectly level and his hands shaking, and she had thought: *here is a person who knows exactly what it costs to lose something.*

If she told him about the map's price, he'd try to stop her. He'd take the map, or burn it, or turn them back toward Thornhollow and safety and the slow suffocation of a peace built on lies. And the thirteenth moon would turn without anyone at the gate, and everything her mother had died for, everything Sera Vane had bled out for, would be for nothing.

So Orsen folded the map closed, and lay down with her back to the fire, and began, silently, deliberately, to memorize herself. Her mother's face. The smell of the shop on Ropewalk Lane. The exact weight of Cael's hand when he'd pulled her out of the Warden raid, gripping her wrist hard enough to bruise. She catalogued them like landmarks, fixed them like stars, because a cartographer knows better than anyone: what isn't written down can be redrawn, but what is forgotten is gone forever.

Above her, the thirteenth moon slid another degree west.

Twelve days, the map whispered, in ink she felt rather than read. *Twelve days, and I will need more.*

* * *

**Cael**

On the eighth day, the road led them through a village that wasn't there.

Cael knew it wasn't there because he'd archived its destruction personally—Hallowmere, razed in the second purge, population four hundred and eleven, all names recorded, all records then seized and burned by the Wardens. He had spent two years reconstructing the list from tax fragments and parish scraps. He could recite forty of the names by heart.

All four hundred and eleven of them were standing in the street.

Not ghosts—or not only ghosts. Figures of ink, half-formed, going about the business of a market day that had ended thirty years ago. A woman argued cheerfully with a fishmonger whose stall held nothing. Children chased a dog that was more suggestion than animal. And everywhere, threaded through the scene like veins through marble, lines of the same black ink that flowed across Orsen's map, rising out of the ground, feeding the whole impossible village.

"They're not real," Orsen said. Her voice was strange. Flat. He'd noticed her getting flatter over the past days—shorter answers, longer silences, a certain careful quality to her speech, like someone crossing ice. "They're drawn. The map is drawing them. From somewhere."

"From where?"

"I don't know." But she wouldn't look at him when she said it, and Cael had spent eleven months reading a dead woman's habits, and Orsen Dunmore was developing the same ones. Sitting facing doors. Hoarding silences.

He let it go. There were things he hoarded too.

That night, camped at the village's edge among ink-figures who murmured in voices like wind through reeds, he sat beside her at the fire and said, "My sister's name was Lira."

Orsen turned. He hadn't meant to say it. He had never said it aloud to anyone—not the name, not once in nine years. In the Undercroft she was file 4471, trial record incomplete. Names were dangerous; names were how the Wardens found the families of the condemned.

"She liked maps," he went on, before he could stop himself. "Drew them on everything. On her shoes. On the table. Drew a map of our house from above, from the air, like a bird sees it, and she'd never been higher than the roof. I kept it. After. Hid it inside a book of tax law where no one would ever look." His hands were shaking. He let them. "When they took her, the Wardens asked me questions for six hours. At the end, the questioner leaned across the table and said, *your sister's drawings were very good. Where did she learn it?* As if it mattered. As if the crime were the drawing and not the girl."

Orsen's hand came over his. Cool fingers, ink-stained, steady. She didn't say *I'm sorry*. She said, "Tell me what she looked like."

No one had ever asked him that. Everyone else had asked what she'd done, whether she'd resisted, whether the family had known. Orsen asked what she looked like, and Cael found that his throat closed and opened and he could answer: the gap in her teeth, the way she squinted at distances, the ink smudge she always had on her cheekbone by midday like a beauty mark.

"She'd have liked you," he said. "She'd have liked your maps."

Something crossed Orsen's face then—something quick and pained, there and gone, like a fish under ice. He almost asked. He was learning her tells, and that one was new, and it frightened him more than any of the others.

Instead she squeezed his hand and said, "Get some sleep, Cael. Long road tomorrow."

There was. He could feel it—the map's pull stronger now, urgent, the ink practically humming through the ground beneath them. Twelve days to the gate, if the moon kept its schedule. Eleven, maybe, given the pace the map was setting.

He slept. And in the morning, when he woke, he found Orsen sitting apart with the map open on her knees, writing in its margins in a small fierce hand, fast, like a woman copying out a book before the library burns.

He didn't ask what she was writing.

Some part of him, the part that had spent nine years archiving other people's losses, already knew.
