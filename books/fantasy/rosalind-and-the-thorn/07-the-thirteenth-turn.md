## 7. The Thirteenth Turn

The road ended where the map said it would, which was the first wrong thing.

Roads that lead somewhere end at doors, at walls, at rivers. This one simply stopped—a seam of pale stone running through the forest floor like a scar on skin, and beyond it a clearing where nothing grew. Not dead ground. Refusing ground. The grass gave up three paces out and then just declined to exist, as if the world had drawn a line and decided the argument was over.

Orsen stood at the edge of it with the map open in her hands and felt it go still against her palms. In seven days of travel it had never been still. It breathed when she slept. It shifted when she dreamed. Now it lay flat and quiet as a thing pretending to be paper.

"It's waiting," she said.

Cael came up beside her, breath ragged from the last mile of road that had unspooled beneath their feet like thread from a spool. He looked worse than she did—hollow-eyed, his archivist's coat torn at the shoulder where a branch had taken a piece of him—but his eyes went straight past her to the clearing, and she watched him do what he always did: read.

"Stone under the grass," he murmured. "Old work. Pre-Sundering." He crouched, touched the seam, pulled his fingers back fast. "It's warm."

The thirteenth moon hung low in the east, fat and white and wrong—too large, too near, its face marked with a ring no almanac had ever recorded. It had been growing for days. Tonight it sat directly above the clearing like an eye deciding whether to blink.

"The gate's here," Orsen said. "Under us. I can feel it through the map." She could feel more than that. For a week the map had been drinking her—one memory at a time, small sips she'd only lately learned to notice. The name of her first teacher. The taste of the honey cakes sold outside the Warden barracks. Her mother's voice saying *north* with a particular lilt she could no longer reproduce. Each loss left a smooth place, a polished absence, and now, standing over whatever slept beneath this refusing ground, the sipping had become a pull.

She didn't tell Cael. There were things you carried alone or they became heavier.

They crossed together.

Halfway across the bare circle, the ground answered them. Light rose through the stone in slow veins, silver-white, tracing patterns too regular to be natural and too organic to be built—concentric rings, a spiral, letters in a script that predated Thornhollow's founding by a thousand years. And at the center, where the light converged, stood a door.

Not a door in a wall. A door standing alone, upright in the middle of the clearing, tall as two men, black as wet ink, its surface moving faintly—the way the map moved, the way water moves when something beneath it is alive.

"That's not a prison," Cael whispered. "That's a mouth."

"No," Orsen said, though her voice shook. "Prisons have locks. That has a hinge."

And then the Wardens came out of the treeline.

There was no ambush in it, no shout of discovery. They simply emerged, forty strong in their grey lacquered armor, torches hooded, and arranged themselves around the clearing with the terrible ease of people who had done this many times before. At their center walked a woman in white-trimmed grey, unhurried, her hands folded at her back, her face lined and calm as a judge's.

High Warden Vess.

Orsen had seen her twice in seven years—once at a distance during the annual Oath parade, once across a courtroom when a charm-seller two stalls down had been sentenced to the quieting. Up close she was smaller than her reputation and colder than her legend, and her eyes found Orsen immediately, the way a compass finds north.

"Cartographer Dunmore." Her voice carried without effort. "You've cost me three inspectors, two informants, and one very expensive informant inside the Undercroft. I'd say you've been busy, but we both know you haven't. The map has been busy. You've merely been carried."

"You knew about this place." Cael stepped forward, and two Wardens' hands went to their blades. "You knew where the Heartroot was. Seven years of purges, seven years of burning books—and you knew."

Vess didn't look at him. "Archivist Cael. Yes, I know who you are; your sister's file crossed my desk." Something flickered across her face—not regret, exactly. Bookkeeping. "And yes. We've known since the founding. Who do you think built the harvest machinery?"

The words landed in the silence like stones into deep water.

"Harvest," Orsen said.

Vess finally turned fully toward her, and up close her calm resolved into something worse than cruelty: patience. The patience of someone who had waited thirty years and could wait thirty more.

"Did you truly believe the Sundering destroyed magic? That the founders won a war against power itself?" She gestured at the standing door, at the moon above it. "Magic cannot be destroyed. It can only be moved. So we moved it—in here. Behind a gate that opens once every cycle of thirteen moons, and only to blood that carries the key. The founders' blood. Your blood, cartographer. Every thirteen turns, the Wardens come here, and we draw off what the land needs to stay green, and we keep the rest. The surplus pays for order. For peace. For every year Thornhollow has not burned itself down."

"You've been farming it," Cael said hoarsely. "You've been *farming* it while you burned people for carrying charms."

"We burned people for chaos," Vess said, without heat. "I have watched cities run on free magic. I've read the histories you people hide in your Undercroft. Cities of glass and screaming. The ban isn't a lie, archivist. It's a dam. And dams require engineers." Her eyes came back to Orsen. "Which brings us to terms."

"I don't make deals with jailers."

"You will want this one." Vess drew a folded document from her coat and held it out—not to take, merely to be seen. Even at ten paces, Orsen recognized the seal. The High Court of Records. "Elian Almeida. Cartographer. Executed in the third purge, posthumously listed among the traitors of the Sundering conspiracy. Her name has been struck from every registry in the city for twenty-two years." Vess let that sit. "This document restores it. Full pardon. Full honors. Her maps returned to the public archive under her own name, taught to students, remembered. Everything you have wanted since you were old enough to understand what they took from you."

Orsen's breath went out of her.

She hadn't known she wanted it said aloud until this stranger said it. Her mother's name. Not cleared quietly, not smuggled back into some rebel archive where it would be one more martyr in one more cause—but restored, official, permanent. She thought of the smooth places inside herself where memories used to be, and how the one thing she still owned whole was her mother's execution, and how she had carried it like a coal for twenty years because it was all she had left to carry.

"And in exchange," Vess said gently, "you give me the map, walk away, and never speak of this clearing. You live free. Unwatched. Rich, if you like. And your mother dies again—properly this time, honored instead of erased."

The moon climbed.

Orsen felt it happen in her chest before she saw it overhead—the thirteenth turn arriving like a tide change, pressure dropping, the standing door beginning to hum a note below hearing. The veins of light in the stone brightened. The map in her hands grew warm for the first time since the night market, warm as a living hand, and she understood suddenly why it had drunk her memories all this way: it wasn't feeding itself.

It was teaching her. Showing her, memory by memory, what a life weighed. What a bargain cost.

"Orsen." Cael's voice, low, urgent. "Don't listen to her. Whatever's behind that door, it doesn't belong to the Wardens, and it doesn't belong to the rebels either, and it sure as hell doesn't belong to a bargain made under a lying moon."

"Everything belongs to whoever holds the lock," Vess said. "That is the entire history of the world, archivist. I'm offering her a place on the right side of it. Her mother's name, her freedom, her future." She extended the document all the way now, arm steady. "Take it. The gate opens in minutes, and after it opens, there are no more offers. Only consequences."

Orsen looked at the paper.

She thought of her mother's hands, ink-stained, drawing coastlines by candlelight. She thought of the stranger dying against her stall, whispering *the thirteenth turn*, pressing a living map into her grip—her aunt, though she hadn't known it yet, though the map had shown her only fragments. Family. All of them cartographers. All of them dead or hunted for the same gift.

She thought of the smooth places inside her, and how many more there would be if she walked away, and how the map had already begun to feel less like a burden and more like a limb.

The moon reached its apex. Directly overhead. Perfectly centered above the standing door.

Light poured down the door's black surface like water down glass, and the humming note resolved into something almost like speech, almost like a name being pronounced very slowly by a very patient mouth. The Wardens raised their harvesting instruments—rods of grey metal that Orsen now understood had been pointed at this clearing every thirteen moons for two hundred years, drinking from a prison and calling it peace.

"Well, cartographer?" Vess asked. "Your mother's name. Your life. One word."

Orsen closed her hand around the map.

It pulsed against her palm—once, twice—like a heart counting down. And in the space between pulses she felt it press something into her, not a memory this time but an instruction, clear and cold and final, and the shape of it made her blood run thin:

*The gate opens from the inside.*

She lifted her head. Met Vess's patient eyes. Met Cael's frightened ones.

"You're right," Orsen said. "One word."

She took a step toward the door.

Behind her, Cael shouted her name—and the map tore itself from her hands, unfolding in the air between them, pages spreading wide as wings, and the last thing she saw before the ink took her was Cael reaching for her and finding, instead, empty air where a woman had been.

* * *

Inside, there was no falling. There was only the sensation of being *read*.
