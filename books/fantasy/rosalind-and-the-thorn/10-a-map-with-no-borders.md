## 10. A Map With No Borders

The first thing to come back was the smell of rain.

Not the memory of it—Orsen had no memories left of that night at the Heartroot, or of most of the year before it—but the smell itself, arriving on a grey morning through the open shutters of a shop that did not yet have a name. She stood in the doorway with a broom she'd bought secondhand and let the water wash the dust off the cobbles outside, and she thought: *this is what it feels like when something ends without breaking.*

Thornhollow had changed the way a river changes a stone. Slowly, then all at once.

The Wardens' charter had not been revoked; it had simply stopped meaning anything. Without the Heartroot's stolen power feeding their sigils, their badges were only tin, their oaths only words. High Warden Vess had resigned by proclamation—a single sheet nailed to the Wardenhouse door, unsigned—and the Wardens themselves had drifted home like men waking from a long fever, blinking at the city they'd been policing and finding they no longer understood why. Some stayed on as constables. A few fled north. One, Orsen heard, had opened a bakery in the Lantern Quarter and made bread so good people forgave him almost everything.

Almost.

The rebels fared worse, in a way, because they had built their whole selves around a wound. Maren came to the shop once, in the third week, standing in the doorway with her arms crossed like a woman guarding an empty room.

"You could have given it to us," Maren said. "You know that."

"I know you would have fought over it until there was nothing left to fight for."

"That's not your choice to make."

"No," Orsen agreed. "It was mine." She set a cup of tea on the counter between them, an offering and a dismissal both. "There's work now, if your people want it. The old roads are surfacing all over the valley—roads nobody's walked in two hundred years. Someone has to survey them before someone builds on them wrong."

Maren stared at her for a long moment. Then she took the tea, drank half of it standing up, and said, "We'll want fair rates."

"Fair rates," Orsen said, and that was the treaty that ended the Sundering War's last war: one cup of tea and a promise about cartography fees.

* * *

Magic came back the way spring comes—not announced, just noticed one morning, everywhere at once.

It was gentler than anyone had feared. That was Orsen's doing, though she couldn't remember doing it; Cael told her later, in the weeks when he still knew her, that the ground itself had seemed to drink the Heartroot's power, spreading it thin through root and rain and soil until it wasn't a treasure anymore but a condition, like weather. Lamps lit a little easier. Bread rose better. Children in the Lowmarket discovered they could make puddles ripple in patterns if they hummed, and their mothers shushed them out of habit before realizing there was no one left to shush them *for*.

No one burned for it. That was the part Orsen measured every day against the ache in her chest where something used to be. No one burned.

She had opened the shop in the same stall-row where she'd once sold charms—the harmless kind, the kind that were mostly hope and string. But this place had walls and a window and a sign she painted herself, because she couldn't trust her own hand to draw anything true anymore:

**ALMEIDA & CO. — HONEST MAPS**

The *& Co.* was a lie of optimism. She worked alone.

Honest maps were harder than they sounded. Every map she'd ever drawn before had drawn *itself*, ink crawling into shape under her fingers while she watched like a stranger at her own table. Now her hands were just hands. She surveyed on foot, measured with chain and compass, argued with distances, got them wrong, went back. It was slow. It was humiliating. It was the only work she could imagine doing.

And sometimes, late at night, she caught herself reaching for a memory and finding a smooth blank place instead—her mother's face, gone; the sound of the stranger's dying whisper, gone; whatever had passed between her and Cael on the road that wasn't there, gone, gone, gone. The map had eaten well. She was what remained after the meal.

She kept a ledger of the losses anyway. Not to mourn them—to *invoice* them. Somewhere in her, stubborn as a splinter, lived the belief that a debt recorded might someday be paid.

* * *

He came in on a Tuesday, in the hour when the light goes gold and shopkeepers start thinking about closing.

The bell over the door rang, and Orsen looked up from a chart of the eastern fens, and a man stood in the doorway holding his hat like he'd forgotten why he took it off.

She knew him. That was the strange cruelty of it—she knew his face from the raid on the Undercroft, from the road, from fragments other people had described to her. Tall, dark-haired, a scar through one eyebrow, eyes that looked at things slightly too hard, as if checking them for hidden doors. Cael. The archivist. The rebel who had walked beside her toward the end of the world.

But knowing a face isn't knowing a person, and everything else was blank paper.

"Can I help you?" she asked.

He didn't answer right away. He was looking at her the way she'd seen travelers look at coastlines they'd dreamed about—recognition arriving ahead of any reason for it.

"I don't—" He stopped. Started again. "I've been walking past this shop for six days. I live three streets over. I don't walk past shops." He laughed, short and embarrassed, and put his hat on the counter as if setting down something heavy. "I'm Cael. I was—I think I was someone to you. I don't know how I know that. I woke up in the Undercroft in autumn and everyone treated me gently, like I'd been ill, and there was a gap in me shaped like a person, and I've spent the whole winter trying to find who fits in it."

Orsen's hand had gone, without her permission, to the ledger beneath the counter. To the page she'd titled *LOSSES* in her own careful print.

"There was a man named Cael," she said. Her voice came out steady, which surprised her. "He was an archivist. He tracked a dying stranger through Thornhollow because he thought the truth mattered more than the law. He lost his sister to the Wardens' trials, and he never stopped being angry about it, and he was right to be angry." She turned the ledger around and slid it across the counter. "I can't tell you more than that. I paid for what happened with the parts of myself that remembered. He paid with the parts that remembered *me*. We're even, I suppose. Nobody wins a trade like that."

Cael looked down at the ledger. His jaw worked. When he spoke again, his voice was rough. "What do I do with it? The feeling? There's this—it's like grief, but it's pointed at you, and I don't even know what I lost."

"You don't do anything with it," Orsen said. "That's the honest answer. You carry it. I carry mine. And maybe—" She hesitated, hating how much hung on the word. "Maybe we start where strangers start. I need someone who knows the eastern fens. The old roads are surfacing out there, and my last assistant drowned a measuring chain and quit. The pay is bad. The company is a woman missing large portions of herself. But the work matters."

Cael looked at her for a long time. Outside, the rain had started again, soft on the cobbles, and somewhere down the street a child was humming to a puddle, and the lamplighter's flame caught on the first try, the way flames did now, everywhere, all the time, quietly, like the world breathing.

"The feeling," Cael said slowly, "isn't going anywhere. I can tell. It's got roots."

"So do I," Orsen said. "Apparently that runs in the family."

He almost smiled. Then he held out his hand across the counter, formal as a treaty, and she took it, and his grip was warm and unfamiliar and somehow exactly right, the way a road is right when you've never walked it but your feet already trust it.

"Almeida & Co.," he read off the sign, glancing up. "Who's the Co.?"

"Ask me again tomorrow," Orsen said. "Ask me every day, if you want. Sooner or later one of us will remember enough to answer honestly."

* * *

That night she locked up late and stood a moment in the empty street, looking east, where beyond the rooftops the fens waited with their resurfacing roads—old paths rising out of the earth like veins remembering blood, more of them every week, threading toward places no living map had ever charted.

In her satchel, wrapped in oilcloth, lay the one artifact she'd kept: a scrap of the old map, no bigger than her palm, dead ink on dead vellum. It hadn't moved since the Heartroot. She checked it every night anyway, the way you check a grave.

Tonight, for the first time, one line of it had shifted—a hair-thin road, new-drawn, running east out of Thornhollow toward the fens. Toward the blank space at the map's edge where the world ran out.

Toward something that wanted finding.

Orsen stood in the lamplight a long moment, feeling the loss in her chest and the pull in her feet, and understood that the world was not done drawing itself—and that some part of her, the part that remained, had already agreed to follow.

She went inside, took down a fresh sheet of vellum, and began to measure.
