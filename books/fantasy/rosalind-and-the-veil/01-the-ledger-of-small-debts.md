## 1. The Ledger of Small Debts

The candle had burned down to its last thumb's width when Juniper Ravensworth finally set down her pen and flexed her aching fingers.

Around her, the counting room of the Guild of Impossible Things settled into its nighttime silence — that particular hush of old buildings, all timber and stone and secrets, creaking as they cooled. The ledger lay open before her, its pages soft as cloth from decades of handling, and by the guttering light she checked the evening's figures one last time. Three relics appraised. One oath renewed at standard interest. A vial of borrowed courage returned by the widow Hessel, six months late, with an apology and a small tin of honey cakes Juniper would not eat.

She wrote it all down. That was the work, or half of it: to write things down so they could not be argued with later. Ink was honest in a way people were not. Ink stayed where you put it.

*Debts outstanding: eleven. Debts forgiven: two.* She paused over that second line, pen hovering. Forgiveness was expensive. Her grandmother had taught her that before teaching her anything else — *a debt forgiven is a debt you now own twice, child, once in coin and once in memory, and memory is the dearer currency.* Grandmother Ravensworth had been dead nine years, and Juniper still heard her voice most clearly when the accounts were open, as though the ledgers themselves were haunted by arithmetic.

She ruled the line closed with a single stroke and turned to the other half of her work.

It waited for her on a square of black velvet beside the inkwell: a rib bone, human, cleaned and pale as sea-washed driftwood. It had come to her three days ago in the hands of a girl — no, a woman, though she'd looked like a girl, hollow-eyed and rain-soaked, who had climbed the guild steps at dusk and asked, in the flat voice of someone who has rehearsed a thing too many times, whether it was true that the Guild of Impossible Things could make the dead speak.

Juniper had told her the truth, which was more complicated than yes.

What Juniper did was not speaking, exactly. The dead did not talk; they *remembered*, and memory has no voice of its own. What they had instead was weight. Grief has mass. Love has mass. And bone — bone, which is the body's own ledger, the one account every living thing keeps from first breath to last — bone holds that weight the way wax holds a seal. Carve a name into bone with the right hand, in the right stillness, and the name begins to hum. Hold it to your ear and you hear not words but the shape of them: the dead woman's laugh folded into her daughter's name, the particular way she had said *come inside, supper's getting cold,* forty thousand times across forty years until the saying of it wore a groove in the world deep enough to be found again.

That was the gift. That was the curse. Depending on the hour, Juniper could not always tell the difference.

She picked up the rib and turned it in her fingers, feeling for the grain. Every bone had one, a faint spiral like the growth rings of a tree, and the carving had to follow it or the memory would snag and stutter. This one had belonged to a woman named Odile Ferrant, dead eleven days of a fever that had taken her quickly, which was a mercy, and taken her mid-sentence, which was not. Her daughter had told Juniper that part on the guild steps, wringing her hat in her hands. *She was telling me where she'd hidden my father's letters. She got as far as the word "under" and then she was gone. I've looked everywhere. Under everything.*

So the commission was this: carve Odile Ferrant's name into her own rib, and hold the bone to the daughter's ear, and let the dead woman finish her sentence if the groove was deep enough. Simple work. The kind Juniper could do in her sleep, and sometimes nearly did.

She took up her knife.

It was a small thing, the blade no longer than her forefinger, its handle worn to the shape of her grip by four generations of Ravensworth hands before hers. Grandmother had put it in her palm at age seven — *you'll have the talent or you won't, and either way you'll have the discipline* — and Juniper had felt the bone under her first clumsy cuts answer her like a cat answering a familiar footstep. She had never asked where the talent came from. Families had gifts the way houses had drafts: you learned to live with them without tracing them to their source. Asking questions about inherited things, Grandmother said, was how you found out what else came with them.

The knife bit. Thin curls of bone fell away white as snowfall onto the velvet, and Juniper worked the letters slowly, following the spiral grain, each stroke a small act of attention. *O. D. I. L. E.* Then the surname, smaller, tucked beneath like a shadow: *Ferrant.* The candle flame leaned in the draft from the window and righted itself. Somewhere above her, in the rafters of the guildhall, something shifted its weight — a pigeon, probably. Probably.

And then, as the last letter closed, the humming began.

It was very quiet at first, the way distant water is quiet. Juniper set down the knife and lifted the rib to her ear with both hands, the way one lifts a shell, and listened.

Not words yet. Words came later, if they came at all, and only for the person the memory was meant for. What Juniper heard was the raw material underneath: warmth, mostly. A kitchen. Rain on a roof that leaked in the northeast corner and never got fixed because fixing it would have meant admitting it leaked. A lullaby sung badly, off-key with great confidence. And threaded through it all, insistent as a burr in wool, a single unfinished thought circling and circling like a moth against glass:

*under — under — under the —*

*Soon,* Juniper thought at it, the way she might soothe any restless thing. *Soon. Your daughter comes tomorrow. Finish your sentence then.*

The humming gentled. That was the strange courtesy of the dead, the thing no one believed until they heard it: they were patient. They had nothing left but patience.

She wrapped the rib in linen, blew out the candle, and sat a moment in the dark, listening to the building breathe.

* * *

Morning came grey and smelling of river. Juniper carried the wrapped bone up through the guildhall, past the reliquary shelves where impossible things slept in cotton and glass — a key that unlocked yesterday, currently out on loan; a mirror that showed the viewer at seven years old, cracked since before anyone could remember; forty-one jars of preserved last words, their contents logged and cross-referenced in the master catalogue. Past the partners' wing, where Master Corvane's door stood shut, as it always stood shut, though Juniper had the persistent sense of being audited whenever she walked by it. Out the front doors, down the thirteen steps worn concave by centuries of grieving feet, to where the daughter was already waiting.

She had been waiting a long time. Juniper could tell by the way she stood — planted, braced, like someone who had arrived hours early rather than risk arriving late. Her eyes were swollen and dry, past weeping into that arid country beyond it.

"You're the carver," the woman said. Not a question.

"I'm Juniper Ravensworth." She held up the linen bundle. "I've finished."

The woman's breath went out of her all at once, as if she'd been holding it for eleven days.

They sat together on the steps, because this part could not be done indoors; Grandmother had insisted carved bones spoke best under open sky, where the dead had room. Juniper unwrapped the rib and pressed it gently to the woman's ear, cupping her hands around it to hold the sound close.

"Hear anything?" Juniper asked, though she knew the answer already. The humming had changed overnight. It had found its listener.

The woman's face did something complicated — crumpling and steadying at war with each other — and then her eyes went wide and wet and fixed on some middle distance where her mother evidently stood.

"*Under the third stair,*" she whispered, repeating it as she heard it. "*The loose one. In the box with the Christmas candles. Oh — oh, Mama, I looked there, I looked—*" A broken laugh. "She's laughing at me. She says I looked *beside* it, not *under* it, and she's laughing—" The woman pressed the bone harder to her ear as though she could climb inside it. "Is that really her? Is that really how she sounds?"

"It's how she remembers sounding," Juniper said. "Which is better."

The woman cried then, properly, the storm after the drought, and Juniper sat beside her and did not touch her and did not look away, because looking away was its own kind of lie. This was the part of the work no ledger could hold: the moment the debt between the living and the dead was paid down by a few coins' worth of hearing. It happened on the guild steps perhaps twice a month, and every time, Juniper watched grief turn briefly into gratitude, and gratitude reach out its warm hands toward her—

—and every time, she stepped back from the reaching.

"That's the full commission," she said, when the woman's breathing had evened. "The bone stays with you. There's a fee according to the schedule." She named it. The woman paid it from a purse that had clearly been counted and recounted, and pressed Juniper's hand between both of hers, and said, "Thank you, thank you, you gave me my mother back," and Juniper withdrew her hand as gently as withdrawing from anything, and said the thing she always said:

"I didn't give her back. I just made sure she wasn't talking to air."

But the woman wasn't listening anymore. She was climbing the steps backward, clutching the rib to her chest, mouthing *under the third stair* like a prayer, and at the top she turned and called down, "You must have loved her, whoever taught you this. You must have loved somebody terribly, to do this work."

Juniper stood on the thirteenth step and considered the question honestly, because the woman deserved honesty even if she hadn't waited for the answer.

"No," she said, to the empty street. "I'm just good at it."

* * *

The rest of the day was accounts.

This was the arrangement Juniper had with the Guild of Impossible Things, struck when she was nineteen and Grandmother newly dead: she kept the ledgers, all of them, in exchange for a workroom, a stipend, and the guild's considerable protection. The partners liked having her precisely because she wanted nothing from anyone. Master Corvane had said as much, once, in his dry ledger-keeper's voice, approving of her the way one approves of a well-made lock. *"Miss Ravensworth is an asset. She balances. You can build on a woman who balances."* He had meant it as praise. Juniper had gone home and lain awake wondering why it had felt like being described as a wall.

She spent the afternoon in the counting room entering the morning's fee, reconciling the honey cakes against the widow Hessel's late return (entered under *gratuities, edible, non-binding*), and beginning the quarterly audit of the oath-book, where the guild recorded promises made and promises kept. Oaths were the guild's oldest trade, older than the relics, older than the building. People swore things here — to remember, to forget, to return, to never speak of — and the guild held those oaths in trust, charging interest in services rendered.

Juniper was halfway down the *oaths sworn, pending* column when she noticed it: an entry in her grandmother's hand, ninety-one years old by the dating convention, that she had read a hundred times and never once seen.

*Received of an unnamed party: one country's silence, held in perpetuity. Consideration: see sealed annex. Do not audit. Do not ask.*

Her grandmother's handwriting, unmistakable — the sharp downstrokes, the R's like drawn knives. But nonsense. Grandmother had died at eighty-four; ninety-one years ago she'd have been a child. Unless the entry had been backdated. Unless it had been copied forward from an older book. Unless—

"Miss Ravensworth."

Juniper's pen stopped mid-stroke, leaving a small blot like a period at the end of a sentence she hadn't finished writing.

Master Corvane stood in the doorway of the counting room. She had not heard him come up the stairs, which was unlike him; he was a large man who moved like furniture being rearranged, deliberately and audibly. His face wore its usual expression of patient appraisal, the look of a man valuing goods.

"The partners require the quarterly figures by week's end," he said. "Also — there is a matter. A child was seen at the guild door last night, near midnight. Barefoot. Asking entry." His eyes rested on Juniper a moment too long. "The night porter turned her away, naturally. Guild policy after dark. But the porter says she spoke strangely. Some gibberish tongue." He smiled without warmth. "You didn't hear anything, I suppose?"

Juniper looked down at the ledger, at her grandmother's knife-sharp handwriting, at the blot of ink like a full stop.

"No," she said. "I didn't hear anything."

Corvane nodded, satisfied, and withdrew. Juniper sat alone with the accounts and the candle and the growing cold, and told herself the lie she had just spoken was simply good bookkeeping: nothing entered, nothing owed.

But her hands, which had never once lied to her, were trembling. And somewhere far off — or perhaps not far off at all, perhaps just outside, perhaps just below the window, perhaps standing patiently on the thirteenth step where the grieving always stood — something was humming in a language she had never been taught, and understood completely.
