## 2. A Knock at Odd Hours

The knock came at the hour Juniper trusted least — that thin grey seam between night and morning when the ledger of the living ran lowest and the dead grew bold with hunger.

She was at her desk in the counting room, third floor of the guildhall, working by the light of a single tallow candle because good candles were for clients and bad ones were for her. The Guild of Impossible Things kept strange hours by necessity; grief did not observe business days, and neither did the debts it left behind. Tonight she had been reconciling the month's accounts: two relics returned to their owners' families, one oath discharged, three years of borrowed time collected from a man who had spent them badly and wept when she came for them. Ordinary work. The kind that let her hands move and her mind stay quiet.

The knock was small. Three taps, spaced evenly, like a child counting.

Juniper set down her pen. The guildhall had a bell at the street door, a heavy bronze thing that rang through every floor and woke even Master Corvane, who slept like the dead themselves and resented the comparison. No one had rung it. Which meant whoever stood below had not used the door as doors were meant to be used, or had knocked on it without pulling the rope, or —

She went down the stairs with the candle held high, past the humming doors of the appraisal wing, past the long gallery where the guild's stranger inventory slept under dust sheets. The hall was cold in the way old stone buildings are cold, a cold that seemed to come from underneath, from the foundations, from whatever the guildhall had been built on top of before anyone thought to ask.

At the street door she paused, hand on the bolt, and listened.

Nothing. No breathing, no shifting of weight, none of the small sounds a person makes while waiting to be let in. But the candle flame leaned toward the door, the way flames lean toward an open window, and Juniper had learned forty years of other people's lessons about trusting flames.

She opened it.

The child stood on the threshold stone as though she had grown there, like something planted. She was perhaps seven years old, small for it, dressed in a nightgown that had once been white and was now the color of road dust and worse things. Her feet were bare. Not cold-bare, not lost-child-bare — bare the way a bird's feet are bare, as if shoes were a custom of some country she had never visited. Her hair was dark and cut short with what looked like a knife's carelessness, and her eyes were the pale grey of rain over slate, and they looked up at Juniper without a flicker of fear.

That was the first wrong thing. Children who arrived at the guild door at odd hours were always afraid. Fear was the fare they paid for admission. This one stood in the doorway of a building full of relics and oaths and borrowed time, in the smallest hours of the night, alone and shoeless, and regarded Juniper Voss with the mild patience of someone waiting for a shopkeeper to finish with another customer.

"You're late," said the child.

Not in any tongue Juniper knew. That was the second wrong thing. The words were clear, deliberate, shaped with a child's careful precision around sounds that did not belong to any language of the capital or the coast or the river provinces or anywhere else Juniper had ever traded. There were vowels in them that opened like doors into dark rooms. There was a consonant that sounded like wind moving through bone.

And yet.

Juniper stood very still, the candle trembling in her grip, and felt something turn over in her chest — a sensation she had no name for, because she had never needed one. It was the feeling of hearing a word you half-remembered from a dream. It was the feeling of standing in a house you had never entered and knowing where the stairs were.

"I don't understand you," she said, in the common tongue, slowly.

The child frowned, considered this, and tried again. Different words this time, but the same language — the same impossible vowels, the same bone-wind consonant. And beneath the strangeness of it, beneath the sheer wrongness of hearing syllables that should not exist, Juniper heard the shape of what the child was saying. Not the meaning. The *shape*. Grief has a grammar all its own, and Juniper had spent her life learning to read it in the voices of the dead: the falling cadence of loss, the repeated return to a wound, the way sorrow circles its own center like a moth.

The child was saying: *I looked. I looked and I looked and there is nothing where it was.*

"Come inside," Juniper heard herself say.

She would think about that later — how easily the words came, how little resistance her own caution offered. Juniper Ravensworth did not take people in. People were inventory, or clients, or complications, and she had arranged her life with the same precision she brought to her ledgers so that almost no one occupied more than one category at a time. The last person she had taken in was a stray cat, and the cat had turned out to be a familiar belonging to a hedge-witch with a litigious streak, and the settlement of that affair had cost her a full season's profits and most of her faith in strays.

But the child walked past her into the hall as if summoned, and Juniper closed the door behind her, and that was that.

* * *

The problem, of course, was the senior partners.

The Guild of Impossible Things was governed, in theory, by its charter and, in practice, by three people: Master Corvane, who owned the largest share and acted like it; Mistress Aldery, who handled contracts and had a voice like a filing cabinet closing; and old Master Fenwick, who had founded nothing and remembered everything. They kept apartments on the upper floors and opinions on everything, and their collective verdict on unaccompanied children arriving at midnight was not difficult to predict.

Juniper found Aldery first, because Aldery kept late hours over her contract books and could be relied upon to be awake and disapproving in equal measure. She descended on the counting room with her shawl clutched around her shoulders and her spectacles pushed up into her iron-grey hair, and she looked at the child — who had seated herself, uninvited, in Juniper's own chair, with her bare feet tucked up and her hands folded in her lap like a small magistrate — and said:

"No."

"She hasn't asked for anything yet."

"They never do. That's how it starts. First they sit in your chair, then they eat your bread, then they're a ward of the guild and Corvane is drafting an apprenticeship indenture that binds them until they're thirty." Aldery peered at the child over her spectacles. "Where are your parents, girl?"

The child answered at length, in the bone-wind language, with the grave courtesy of someone repeating information they have already given many times.

"What is that?" Aldery demanded. "Gibberish? Is she simple?"

"She's not simple," Juniper said quietly. "I don't know what it is."

"Well, I know what she is. She's trouble wearing a nightgown, and we are not a charity. We are barely a business." Aldery's voice dropped into the register she reserved for quoting charter clauses. "Article Nine. The guild shall admit no dependent persons whose maintenance cannot be secured against future earnings. Do you want to stand surety for her, Ravensworth? With what? Your bones?"

"With my share," Juniper said, and hated herself a little for how fast it came.

Aldery threw up her hands and went to fetch Corvane, which was the worst possible outcome, and Juniper knew it, and had said it anyway.

Corvane arrived in his dressing gown, which was better quality than most men's coats, and he did not look at the child at first. He looked at Juniper. He had a way of looking at people that made them feel appraised — weighed, measured, and entered into a ledger against their will — and he deployed it now with the leisurely thoroughness of a man who enjoyed his work.

"A child," he said.

"A child."

"Barefoot. At three in the morning. Speaking—" he tilted his head toward the girl, who gazed back at him with that unnerving placidity— "whatever that is. And our Miss Ravensworth has offered her own share of the guild as surety within four minutes of opening the door. You'll forgive me if I find the sequence of events instructive."

"The alternative was putting her back on the street."

"The street," said Corvane, "has been dealing with children for rather longer than we have, and has developed procedures." He crouched then, abruptly, bringing his face level with the child's, and Juniper saw him do the thing he did with inventory: the quick professional cataloguing. Teeth, eyes, hands, posture. Health, breeding, condition. "Can you speak anything useful?" he asked her. "Trade tongue? River cant? Anything a client might pay to hear?"

The child looked at him for a long moment. Then she spoke again — that same language, those same impossible vowels — but slower this time, and softer, and she reached out one small hand and touched his dressing-gown sleeve, gently, the way you touch something to comfort it.

Corvane straightened as if scalded.

"It's nonsense," he said. "It's worse than nonsense. Nonsense can be sold to collectors." He brushed his sleeve where she had touched it, a small fast motion, and Juniper filed that away — Corvane, flinching from a seven-year-old's fingers — for later examination. "Fenwick will side with me. Aldery already has. Take her to the poorhouse on Cordwainer Street in the morning, Ravensworth, and consider your surety offer withdrawn before it embarrasses us both."

He swept out. Aldery followed, pausing only to give Juniper a look that contained, buried deep beneath the disapproval, something that might have been pity.

The child watched the door close. Then she looked up at Juniper and said something in the bone-wind tongue, and smiled — a small, tired smile, the smile of someone much older wearing a face much younger — and patted the arm of the chair she was sitting in, invitingly.

Juniper did not sit in her own chair. She sat on the bench by the window instead, at a careful distance, and pulled her account book toward her, and pretended to work, and listened to the child breathe.

* * *

Sleep did not come for either of them, though for different reasons. The child eventually curled up in the chair itself, knees to chest, a knot of a girl with her chin on her knees, watching the candle with wide grey eyes. Juniper worked. Or made the motions of working. Her pen kept stopping.

Because here was the thing she had not told Aldery. Here was the thing she was still turning over like a coin of uncertain mint.

When the child spoke, Juniper did not hear gibberish. She heard *almost-words*. Words with the flesh torn off them, words like carvings someone had started and abandoned. When the girl had spoken to Corvane — that slow, soft speech, that gentle touch — Juniper had caught, floating up through the strangeness, fragments. Not meanings exactly. Echoes of meanings. Something like *gone*. Something like *all of it gone*. Something that recurred, three times, four times, that had the cadence of a name being called into a valley where no echo comes back.

And once — only once, when the child first spoke from the doorstep — Juniper had heard, beneath the girl's small voice, something else entirely.

She was a bone-carver. She heard the dead the way musicians hear pitch: constantly, involuntarily, as a condition of being alive. Usually the dead were quiet at the guildhall; the stones here were thick, and the traffic of relics and oaths kept them at a respectful distance, the way church bells keep pigeons off a roof. But when the child had said those first words on the threshold, Juniper had heard — faint, far down, like voices through a floor — a great many other voices saying the same words with her. Hundreds. Hundreds of voices, layered under a child's voice like sediment under a streambed, all speaking together, all speaking that same impossible language, and the weight of them had pressed against Juniper's hearing the way deep water presses against the ears.

She had not told Aldery that either. Some things you did not tell the senior partners until you understood them yourself, and Juniper understood nothing, except that a barefoot child had knocked on her door carrying a country's worth of ghosts under her tongue, and that Juniper — against charter, against sense, against every careful wall she had built around her own life — had already decided.

The candle guttered. Grey light began at the window, the false dawn that promises and withdraws. In the chair, the child's eyes finally drifted shut, though her hands stayed clasped, and even asleep her lips moved sometimes, shaping silent syllables, practicing, keeping the language warm the way you keep coals alive under ash.

Juniper put down her pen. She crossed the room — quietly, though she suspected the child was not truly asleep — and draped her own shawl over the small hunched form. The girl did not wake, but her hands loosened, just slightly.

Outside, the city was beginning its ordinary noises: carts, gulls, a drunkard singing three streets over. Inside, in the counting room of the Guild of Impossible Things, a child slept in a chair speaking wordlessly in a tongue that had no right to exist, and Juniper Voss stood over her with a candle burning low, doing the arithmetic she could not avoid.

Surety against future earnings. Her share of the guild, pledged on a nameless girl who spoke a dead language out of nowhere. Every instinct she possessed, every hard-won rule of her careful, walled, competent loneliness, said *return to sender*.

But the dead did not knock on doors. The dead waited to be carved, and came when called, and never, in twenty years of listening, had Juniper heard them speak all at once, in layers, in a chorus — unless they were carrying something too heavy for one voice to hold.

The child murmured in her sleep. One word, twice, tender as a lullaby.

And Juniper, who had never been taught it, who had no reason under heaven to know it, understood it anyway — the way you understand a note is true, the way you understand a debt is yours.

The word meant *home*.
