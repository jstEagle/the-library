## 3. What the Bones Say

The child slept in the guild's smallest guest room, which had not been a guest room in eleven years and still smelled faintly of the lavender Juniper had once put in the sheets for a client who never came back to collect her husband's shinbones. Juniper had checked on her three times before dawn — once out of caution, once out of curiosity, and the third time because she could not sleep herself and found that watching the girl breathe was easier than listening to her own thoughts.

Barefoot. Unafraid. Speaking a language that made the guildhall's doors hum.

Juniper sat at her workbench with the lamp turned low and the day's accounts closed, and she did what she always did when the world presented her with an account she could not balance: she reached for bone.

Her tools were laid out in their proper order — the fine burins of goose-bone and boar tusk, the polishing leathers, the little saw no bigger than a thumb. In the shallow dish beside them lay the pieces she had set aside for morning work: a knuckle from a man who had wanted his wife told he forgave her; a sliver of rib from a woman who had died owing nine crowns to a fishmonger and had asked, with her last coin, that someone simply *say so* to her daughter, as if the debt were the only thing keeping her tethered.

Juniper picked up the rib sliver and turned it in the lamplight. It was good material. Old, but not too old. Bone held memory the way wax held a seal — pressed into it, faithful to it, legible long after the hand that made it was dust. That was the whole of her gift, though clients liked to dress it up in mystery. She carved a name into bone, and the name carried. When the right person held the carving close — against the breastbone, usually, or tucked beneath the pillow — they heard what the dead had left unsaid. Not voices, exactly. Something older than voice. A pressure of meaning, like water against a dam.

She had been doing it since she was seven years old, when she had scratched her grandfather's name into a chicken bone on a dare from her own grief and heard him sigh her childhood nickname from somewhere behind the world. Her grandmother had gone very quiet about it, and then very practical, and by the following winter there had been tools, and lessons, and rules. *Never carve a living name. Never carve your own. Always charge something, even a farthing, or the gift curdles.* Juniper had followed the rules for thirty years without ever asking where they came from, the way one follows the load-bearing walls of a house without wondering who built it.

Tonight, with a mute child asleep down the hall speaking Vellish in her dreams — Juniper had heard it through the door, a soft running mutter like water over stones — tonight the rules felt less like walls and more like a fence around something that had begun pacing.

*Test her,* said the thought that had been waiting since midnight. *You know you're going to. Do it properly.*

* * *

The child woke at first light without any of the confusion waking usually brings. One moment her eyes were closed; the next they were open, dark and level, taking in the ceiling beams, the window, Juniper standing in the doorway with a tray of bread and honeyed milk, as if she had merely paused between one room of her life and the next.

"Good morning," Juniper said. "I'm going to call you nothing yet, because I don't know your name and I refuse to guess."

The girl looked at the bread. She ate with the efficiency of someone who had known hunger recently enough to respect it but not so recently that it had made her greedy. When she finished, she folded her hands in her lap and waited, which was more courtesy than most adults showed Juniper in a week.

"I'm going to show you some things," Juniper said. "Carved bone. If you understand what they are, don't tell me in words. Just touch them. Can you do that?"

The girl nodded once, gravely, and Juniper had the odd sensation of being the one tested.

She brought out her display case — the one she used for clients, glass-topped, lined in green felt. Inside, arranged like a jeweler's wares, lay the guild's samples: a finger-bone carved with a wedding vow; a vertebra inscribed with directions to a buried strongbox; a small round button of sheep bone bearing nothing but the word *forgiven*, which Juniper kept because it was the first piece she had ever sold and because she liked the weight of it in her palm on bad days.

She opened the case and set three pieces on the table between them.

The girl leaned over them without touching. Her lips moved — that same liquid language, barely voiced, a whisper of syllables — and her fingers hovered above the wedding-vow finger-bone the way a person hovers over a candle flame, wanting warmth and fearing the cost.

Then she touched it.

Juniper braced herself the way she always braced when a client first handled a carving: for the flicker, the intake of breath, the tears or the laughter or the terrible stillness. What she had not braced for was what happened to *her*.

It began as a wrongness in her ears, a sensation like pressure changing underwater. Then the workshop sounds — the creak of the building, the cart-traffic outside, the kettle beginning its low complaint on the stove — thinned and stretched, and beneath them rose another sound entirely.

Not the child's voice. The child wasn't making a sound at all. But through the point where the girl's fingertip rested on the carved bone, something was *coming*, the way cold comes through a cracked window.

Hundreds of voices. Hundreds upon hundreds, layered like sediment, like the strata of a cut hillside, all of them speaking at once and none of them speaking louder than a breath. Juniper had spent thirty years hearing the dead one at a time — one name, one bone, one message, tidy as invoices. This was not that. This was a crowd. This was a *country*.

They were saying things she almost understood. Words with shapes she recognized the way you recognize a face across a crowded market — familiar, unplaceable. A woman counting children under her breath. An old man reciting what sounded like boundaries — *from the grey stone to the river bend, from the river bend to the three birches* — over and over, the way people recite prayers or property lines. Someone singing, very softly, a tune with a falling cadence that made Juniper's chest ache before she knew why. And underneath everything, steady as a drumbeat, a single phrase repeated by so many voices it stopped being language and became weather:

*Remember us. Remember us. Remember us.*

The girl snatched her hand back.

The crowd cut off like a door closing. The workshop sounds rushed back in — kettle, carts, creaking beam — and Juniper realized she was standing, both hands flat on the table, breathing hard, while the child watched her with an expression far too old for her face. Not frightened. *Confirming.*

"You heard that," Juniper said. It wasn't a question. Her voice came out scraped. "You heard—"

The girl shook her head slowly. Then she pointed at Juniper's chest. Then at her own ears. Then, with terrible clarity, she pointed at the bones on the table, and made a gesture Juniper needed a moment to parse: fingers spreading outward from the mouth, outward, outward — many mouths. Many voices.

*I didn't hear anything,* that gesture said. *You did.*

"That's impossible." Juniper sat down because her legs advised it. "That is not how this works. One bone, one voice. One debt, one payment. I've never—" She stopped. Pressed her knuckles to her mouth. Thirty years of clean, orderly commerce with the dead, and in the space of a heartbeat the whole ledger had been thrown open to a windstorm of pages.

The girl slid off her chair, came around the table, and stood at Juniper's elbow, looking up at her with an expression of such patient expectation that Juniper laughed — a short, unsteady sound with no humor in it.

"You want me to try again."

A nod.

"It hurt."

A shrug, small and eloquent: *so does everything worth knowing.*

Juniper looked at her hands. They were steady now, mostly. Carvers' hands, her grandmother had called them — hands that kept their counsel. And it occurred to her, in the way that true things arrive, sideways and unwelcome, that her grandmother had taught her these rules with the particular intensity of someone fencing off a field they knew contained a well. *Never carve a living name. Never carve your own.* Why? Whose rule was it originally? Where had a lamplighter's widow in a northern city learned the grammar of bone at all?

She picked up the second piece — the vertebra with the strongbox directions — and held it out.

"Touch it again," she said. "But lightly. Fingertip. And if the crowd comes back—" she didn't know how to finish, so she finished honestly, "—then we'll both know something neither of us wants to know."

The girl touched it.

This time Juniper was ready, or believed she was, and it made no difference. The pressure came again, but different — the dead here spoke in fragments, urgent, overlapping, and now that she was listening for it she could hear *texture* in the crowd, regions of it, clusters of voices that belonged together the way families belong together. Near the surface, closest to the bone and the girl's touch, the voices were recent, thin, frayed. Deeper down they thickened, gathered mass, became chorus, became — at the very bottom of the sound, so deep it was less heard than felt through the table, through the floor, through the soles of her feet —

— became a single voice, vast and patient, that did not speak words at all but *names*. Thousands of names, recited in order, unhurried, the way a ledger is written. And every name it spoke, the crowd answered, softly, *here, here, here*, the way congregations answer a litany.

Juniper pulled her hand off the table as if it had touched a stove.

The silence afterward rang. The girl stood with her hand still resting on the vertebra, watching Juniper with those dark, level eyes, and slowly, deliberately, she lifted her hand away and pointed at the space where the sound had been. Then she pointed at the ground. Then she drew, in the spilled flour near the bread board, a shape: a rough circle, and inside it lines radiating outward like streets, like roots, like cracks in ice.

*A place,* the drawing said. *All of them, from one place.*

"There's no place," Juniper whispered. "There's no country that — the names, the way they answered — I've read every atlas the guild keeps. There's no land whose dead are *organized* like that. The dead are scattered. The dead are lonely. That's the whole tragedy of them. That's why my work exists." She was talking too much and knew it and couldn't stop. "Whoever they are, they're not scattered. They're *kept*. All of them, together, in order, like — like entries in a ledger that someone is still maintaining."

The girl went very still.

Then she pointed at the flour circle, and pointed at herself, and said one word aloud — the first word she had offered since arriving, clear and deliberate, in that running-water language:

"*Vellam.*"

Home. Juniper didn't know how she knew that. She knew it the way she knew the weight of a farthing, absolutely and without derivation, and the knowing frightened her more than the crowd of the dead had, because it meant the language was already inside her, had been inside her all along, sleeping in whatever room of the blood a grandmother's secrets are kept in.

* * *

They worked through the morning.

Juniper would not have called it that at the time — she would have said she was conducting an assessment, gathering facts, protecting the guild's interests — but by noon she had to admit the truth: she was not testing the child. The child was testing *her*, patiently, piece by piece, holding each carved bone to her own ear first (hearing nothing, confirming nothing) and then passing it across the table like a cup passed at a well.

And every time, the crowd came.

By the fourth piece Juniper had learned to keep two fingers on the wood of the table rather than the bone itself, which dulled the flood to a murmur she could think through. By the sixth she had begun, shamefully, greedily, to *enjoy* it — and the enjoyment scared her worse than the dread had. Thirty years of solitary commerce, one voice at a time, each transaction closed and paid, and now here was the opposite of everything her discipline was built on: an open channel, a chorus, a communion she hadn't asked for and could not stop wanting.

Because the dead in the crowd weren't suffering. That was the thing she kept circling back to, the thing that unsettled her professional understanding down to its joists. The dead Juniper served were unfinished — snagged on debts and apologies and unforgiven wives. These dead were *waiting*. There was a difference, and the difference had a temperature. Waiting was colder and cleaner and infinitely more sad. They were not snagged. They were *filed*. Somewhere beyond the reach of every map, someone or something had gathered a whole nation's dead into perfect order, and they were waiting for — for what?

For a reader, said the crowd, in its thousand-layered whisper, whenever she came close to the question. For a hand. For a hand that writes.

At midday the girl ate more bread and honey and fell asleep sitting upright in the chair, mid-chew nearly, the way children fall asleep when they have finally decided a place is safe. Juniper eased the tray away and stood looking down at her.

Small. Thin. Barefoot even now, refusing the socks Juniper had offered twice. A child of perhaps eight, with a scar on one heel old enough to have faded, and hands that were oddly careful — hands that moved like they'd been taught to handle fragile things.

"You need a name," Juniper said quietly. "I can't keep calling you 'the child' in the ledger. The ledger hates ambiguity. Ambiguity is how empires fall and guilds get audited."

The girl slept on.

Juniper fetched the guild register — the great leather book in which every item, client, and transaction was entered in her own cramped hand — and stood over it with her pen uncapped, and found she could not write *unknown female child, approx. 8 years, speaks unknown tongue* without feeling that she was committing a small violence. Names mattered in her trade more than in most trades. A name was the handle by which the dead could be gripped. To leave a person unnamed in her books was to leave them loose in the world.

Think, she told herself. What do you actually know?

The girl had appeared at night, barefoot, from a direction Juniper couldn't determine, as if the street itself had produced her. She feared nothing in the guildhall except, briefly, the sealed cabinet where Juniper kept the failed carvings — the ones that had come out wrong, the names that wouldn't take. She ate like someone relearning trust. She listened to the dead the way other children listened to music. And when she had drawn that circle in the flour — the place, the home, *Vellam* — she had drawn it and then, without any apparent decision, had added a small mark beside it. A bird. A small brown bird with its beak open, singing.

Juniper looked at the sleeping child and the mark came to her fully formed, the way names sometimes did in her trade — arriving rather than being chosen.

"Wren," she said aloud.

The girl's eyes opened instantly, completely, as if the word had been a key turning. She looked at Juniper. She touched her own chest. "*Wren?*" she repeated — not in Juniper's language; in hers, shaping the foreign word carefully, tasting it — and then she smiled.

It was the first smile Juniper had seen on her. It rearranged her entire face, made her suddenly, devastatingly young, and Juniper felt something shift in her chest that she recognized with alarm as affection, arriving ahead of schedule, ignoring all standard procedures.

"Wren," Juniper confirmed, and wrote it in the register: *Wren, arrived by unknown road, speaks the tongue of no known country. Status: guest. Fee: deferred.*

She capped the pen. She was reaching to close the book when her eye caught the flyleaf — the inside front cover, where the guild's founding details were recorded in her grandmother's hand, the hand Juniper had seen ten thousand times and never once studied: *Guild of Impossible Things, established by Rosalind Ravensworth, who learned the craft from her mother, who brought it out of—*

Out of what?

The ink there was damaged. Water-stained, or scraped, or — Juniper tilted the page toward the window light — *deliberately effaced*, scratched away with a blade so thoroughly that only the ghost of the letters remained, a shadow of writing like a bruise under skin. Two words, maybe three, erased with care and force.

Juniper stared at it for a long time.

Behind her, Wren hummed — a small, tuneless, contented sound, the hum of a child in a safe room. And beneath the hum, so faint Juniper might have imagined it, might very much have preferred to have imagined it, the bones in her display case began, all together, very softly, to sing.
