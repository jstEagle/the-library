## 4. The Guild of Impossible Things

The house was full of doors.

Not the ordinary kind — not doors that opened onto kitchens or stairs or the small grey rooms where people kept their brooms and their sadnesses. These doors were *alive*. Wren could feel them the way you could feel a sleeping dog through a blanket: warm shapes under a surface, breathing slow.

She counted them on her first morning. She was very good at counting. Counting had been the last thing her mother taught her before the smoke came — *count your steps, little bird, count them and I will find you* — and so she counted everything now, because counting was like holding someone's hand.

Eleven doors in the long hall upstairs. Seven down. Three that were only half-doors, cut off at the top as if the house had grown tired of making them. And one at the back, past the shelves of jars and the smell of dust and old fat, that did not hum at all.

The other doors hummed.

It was a low sound, lower than bees, lower than the big bell in the city that rang when somebody important died. Wren felt it more than heard it, because hearing was only one way a body could listen and hers had learned others. The hum went up through her bare feet and settled in her back teeth and made the soft place behind her eyes itch. Each door hummed a different note. Some of them were almost songs. One, near the stairwell with the loose rail, hummed the exact note her mother used to make when she rocked the cradle with her foot while her hands were busy with dough.

Wren stood in front of that door for a long time.

She did not open it. Opening things was how you got taken. She knew this the way she knew the taste of ash: completely, without needing to be told again.

But she put her palm flat against the wood, just for a moment, and the humming changed. It went *around* her hand, like water going around a stone in a stream, and then — this was the strange part, the part she would keep even after everything else was taken from her — it went *into* her hand. Up her arm. Into the hollow place in her chest where a whole country used to be.

Something in there said hello.

She couldn't have said what it said. Words came out of her sometimes, words the tall bone-woman called Vellish, but they came out scrambled, like birds flushed from a hedge, and she could never catch the same one twice. Inside her head it was worse and better both. There were voices in there. Not her voice. Hundreds of them, layered like leaves on a forest floor, pressed down and down until they were almost soil. They spoke all day and all night and mostly she let them, the way you let rain fall. But when the doors hummed, the voices turned toward the sound. All of them at once. Like sheep turning toward a shepherd's whistle.

*Doors*, they said, in their hundred folded-over ways. *Remember the doors.*

* * *

The tall bone-woman's name was Juniper. Wren knew this because the woman said it to her three times, slowly, pointing at herself each time, the way you do with someone who might not keep language. *Ju-ni-per.* It sounded like a bird's name. Wren decided privately that this was correct and that the woman had once been a bird and had forgotten, the way Wren had forgotten most things, and that someday she would tell her so.

Juniper gave her bread with honey on it and did not ask questions, which Wren understood to be a great kindness, because questions were hooks and hooks were for pulling things out of people. Juniper also gave her socks. Grey ones, too big, that bunched at her toes. Wren wore them anyway. Bare feet found the doors too well; the socks muffled the world down to something a person could walk through without weeping.

Mostly.

Because of the door at the back.

* * *

She found it on the second morning, following the smell of tallow and old paper past the shelf of jars — jars with things floating in them that she did not look at directly, because looking directly at floating things invited them to look back — into a room the others did not seem to use much. A workroom. A table scarred white with knife-marks. Little curls of bone scattered on the floor like pale wood-shavings, and they *sang*, faintly, each curl holding its one small note, and Wren stepped carefully among them as if among sleeping babies.

And at the back of the workroom, set into the wall where no wall should have anything set into it, low, almost at the floor, small enough that only a child or a kneeling adult could pass through it:

A door that did not hum.

Wren crouched in front of it for a long time.

Every other door in the house was busy being alive. This one was doing something else. It was *aching*. That was the only word she had for it, and she was not sure it was a word she owned or a word that had drifted up out of the hundreds of voices inside her, but it fit the way a key fits a lock it was cut for. The door ached. It ached the way a missing tooth aches. It ached the way an empty chair aches at supper. It ached the way a name aches when everyone who ever said it is gone.

She touched it with one finger.

The ache moved into her finger and climbed, slow as honey, up to the hollow place in her chest, and the hundreds of voices went quiet — truly quiet, for the first time since the smoke — and then one voice rose up alone out of all of them, clear as a glass bell, and it said:

*This is the way we went.*

Then it was gone. All the voices came flooding back like water returning to a streambed, and Wren sat down hard on the floor among the singing curls of bone, and she did not cry, because crying made noise and noise made the grey men come. She had learned that too. She had learned it in a cellar, under a floor, with her mother's hand over her mouth, learning it.

But she looked at the small low door for a long time, and she put her hand on it again, gently, the way you touch a wound that belongs to someone you love.

*I'll come back,* she promised it. Promises were the only currency she had left. She spent them carefully.

* * *

That afternoon the man came.

He came up the stairs like weather coming over a hill — you saw him coming long before he arrived, and knowing did not help. He was broad and neat and his coat was the colour of good ink, and he smelled of paper and of something underneath the paper, something cold and mineral, like coins held too long in a fist. His face was arranged the way shop windows are arranged: everything placed, nothing accidental, nothing true.

He stopped in the hall where Wren sat on the third step, wearing her too-big socks, and he looked at her the way the jar-things looked, if jar-things could look: measuring.

"So," he said. "This is it."

*It.* Wren flinched. She knew that word. In every tongue she had ever been, in the tongue under her tongue, in the tongue of the smoke and the cellar and the running, *it* was the word that meant *not-a-person*. Her mother had taught her that too, though she hadn't meant to. On the last morning, the soldiers by the gate had pointed at the line of people and said *it* and *it* and *it*, and her mother had gripped her shoulder and said, in the old tongue, *you are a who, little bird, whatever they count you as* — and then the smoke had come, and the counting had won anyway.

"This is the child," Juniper said, and her voice was careful, flat, like a board laid over a hole. Wren understood boards laid over holes. She approved of them.

"The child." The man's voice was smooth as poured wax. "You've taken in an unregistered acquisition, Miss Ravensworth. Do you know what that makes her?"

"A child," Juniper said again.

"It makes her *inventory*." He smiled. His teeth were excellent. "Until appraised. You know the forms. Unclaimed goods, found on guild property, of unknown provenance — there's a whole schedule for it. Schedule Nine. I wrote half of Schedule Nine myself."

Wren did not understand the words. Most of them. But she understood the shape of what was happening, because she had seen its shape before, in another country, in another life, worn by other men with other excellent teeth. Men who measured. Men who smiled while they measured. Men whose smiles never reached their eyes because their eyes were busy elsewhere — busy counting.

She looked at Juniper instead. Juniper's hands were doing something Wren had not seen before: they were curled, tight, at her sides, like the fists of someone holding a rope from the inside.

"And what," Juniper said, in a voice that was quiet the way deep water is quiet, "do you propose she is?"

"Resonant material." The man said it easily, comfortably, the way you'd say *oak* or *wool*. "Unusual resonance, frankly. I've handled a great deal of material in my time, Miss Ravensworth — relics, oaths, deathbed confessions, the odd saint's knuckle — and I have never encountered anything that sings like this. Did you know she hums? Matches the doors. Spontaneously. Without instruction. That is not nothing. That is *value*."

He crouched down then, which surprised Wren; large men rarely came down to the level of children unless they wanted something. He crouched and looked at her eye to eye, and his eyes were brown and polished and utterly empty, like two buttons sewn on a coat with nobody inside it.

"Hello," he said pleasantly. "I'm Master Corvane. Can you say your name?"

Wren said nothing. Saying nothing was a skill. She had practiced it in cellars and ditches and the backs of carts, and she was, by now, very good.

"No?" Corvane straightened up, brushing his knees, unbothered. "Doesn't matter. Names can be assigned. Everything gets assigned, eventually. That's what ledgers are *for*." He paused, and something in his pause had a weight to it, like a stone dropped into still water. "You know, there are parties who would pay extraordinary sums for a live source. Extraordinary. Standing orders. Old ones. From before either of us was born. For exactly this — a speaker of a dead language, young enough to be shaped, unattached, undocumented." He spread his hands, generous, terrible. "I could have her appraised by week's end. Certified. Catalogued. And you, Miss Ravensworth, could stop pretending she's a foundling and start drawing your percentage."

"Get out of my workroom, Corvane."

"It's the guild's workroom."

"Get out of my workroom."

Corvane laughed — a real laugh, which was worse than a false one, because it meant he found the whole thing genuinely funny — and he went, unhurried, down the stairs, his boots marking the boards in a rhythm Wren's bones recognized and hated: the rhythm of a man who always comes back.

* * *

That night Wren lay in the narrow bed Juniper had made for her, in the room with the sloped ceiling, and she listened to the house.

The doors hummed their eleven notes and their seven notes and their three half-notes. The wind worried the chimney. Somewhere below, Juniper's lamp burned late; Wren could see the light under the door, a thin gold seam, and it steadied her the way a lit window steadies anyone walking home in the dark, even when home is a stranger's house in a stranger's city in a world that has eaten her own.

And beneath all of it, under the humming, under the wind, under the gold seam of light — the ache. The small low door at the back of the workroom, aching in the dark, keeping its ache like a candle keeps its flame.

The hundreds of voices stirred inside her. They were restless tonight. They turned and turned like birds in a cage before weather, and fragments rose to the surface of her mind and sank again, and some of them she caught:

*a door that does not sing is a door that remembers*

*we went through it, we all went through it, carry us back to it*

*the child carries, the child carries, put it in the child*

*she will know what to do, the bone-woman will know, her hands will know, her grandmother's hands knew—*

Wren sat up in the dark.

Her grandmother's hands *knew*?

She didn't understand. The voice had already sunk back into the layers, folded away somewhere below reach, and when she groped for it, it was gone, the way fish are gone the moment you look straight down into water. But the words stayed, hanging in her chest like a lantern in fog.

Her grandmother's hands knew.

Whose grandmother? Hers? Her grandmother was smoke now, like everything. Or—

Or the bone-woman's.

Wren lay back down. She pulled the blanket up to her chin. She thought about the tall woman with the bird's name, the woman who gave honey-bread and grey socks and did not ask hook-questions, the woman whose hands were always moving, always carving, always making small white things that sang.

Maybe, Wren thought, drifting toward sleep, maybe the voices knew something about Juniper that Juniper did not know about herself. Maybe that happened to people. Maybe everyone was a locked door and only the dead had the keys.

In the morning, she decided, she would take Juniper by the hand — her fingers were always ink-stained and cool, like river stones — and she would lead her down past the jars, past the table scarred white, down to the small low door at the back that did not hum.

And she would show her the ache.

She fell asleep planning it, and dreamed, for the first time since the smoke, of a garden instead of fire: a garden with a wall, and a gate in the wall, and someone standing at the gate with their hand raised, waiting.

Waiting to show her the way through.
