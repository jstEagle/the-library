## 5. Vellish

The rain had followed them all the way down Copperlane, thinning from a proper storm to a grey drizzle that beaded on Juniper's hood and made the whole city smell of wet slate and lamp oil. Wren rode on her hip, a small warm weight that had grown no lighter for all the strangeness of the past three days. The child did not squirm. She did not chatter. She watched everything with the particular stillness of a bird deciding whether the hand reaching toward it held seed or snare.

Juniper had left her at the guildhall only once since taking her in — an hour, no more, while she saw to the ledgers — and had returned to find Master Corvane standing over the girl with a measuring cord and an expression of professional appetite. He had not touched her. He had not needed to. The look alone had been enough to make Juniper's stomach drop through the floorboards of her composure, and she had invented an errand with such speed and fluency that she half-believed it herself, and carried Wren out into the streets before the man could finish his sentence about *resonant valuation*.

So now they were going to see Ambrose Quill, and Juniper was telling herself this was ordinary business. An appraisal of a different kind. A scholarly one. Nothing to do with guild writs or standing orders or the price of a child's throat.

Ambrose Quill kept his shop at the bottom of Inkwell Stair, in a building so old it leaned against its neighbor like a drunkard leaning on a friend. The sign above the door read QUILL & DAUGHTERS: RARE BOOKS, RESTORED, though everyone knew there were no daughters and never had been; Ambrose had carved the words himself forty years ago in a fit of optimism that had outlived every other part of him. Inside, the shop smelled of vellum and beeswax and the particular dustiness of paper that has been loved too long. Books stood in stacks that defied both gravity and cataloguing, rising from floor to ceiling in precarious towers, and somewhere among them — Juniper had never determined where — Ambrose himself lived, slept, ate, and conducted his slow war against forgetting.

He was seventy if he was a day, with ink-stained fingers and eyes like two chips of river glass, and when Juniper pushed open his door with her shoulder he looked up from a folio he was mending and said, without any greeting at all:

"You've brought me something written."

"I've brought you something spoken."

His eyebrows went up. In forty years of trade, Juniper suspected, no one had ever come to Ambrose Quill asking him to identify a language by ear. Languages lived on pages. Languages died on tongues. That was simply the order of things, as settled as the sequence of the liturgy.

"Spoken," he repeated, setting down his brush. "By whom?"

Juniper shifted Wren from her hip to the stool beside the counter. The girl sat the way she did everything — perfectly straight, hands folded, watching. "By her. She came to the guildhall four nights ago. Barefoot. Alone. Speaking this." Juniper took a breath. "And I need you to tell me what it is, and I need you to be honest with me about what you find, because I have a feeling honesty is going to matter more than usual."

Ambrose studied the child for a long moment over the rims of his spectacles. Then he leaned across the counter, bringing his face close to hers, and spoke — gently, slowly, in a language Juniper did not know but recognized instantly, because she had been hearing it for days now falling out of Wren's mouth in fragments: soft vowels, syllables that rolled like water over stones, a rhythm that seemed to lean backward even as it moved forward.

*Vellu senneth. Vellu mara osten.*

The child's whole body changed.

It was the smallest thing — a loosening in the shoulders, a softening around the eyes, the way a fist unclenches — but Juniper saw it the way she saw everything now, with the terrible attention of someone who had spent years learning to read what others could not. Wren reached out and touched Ambrose's hand, once, and answered him. A full sentence. Two sentences. Her voice, which had been a whisper in the guildhall, gathered something like strength, and the words came faster, tumbling, a child reporting urgently to the first adult who could hear her.

Ambrose listened. And then, very quietly, he began to weep.

Not dramatically. Not the way mourners weep at gravesides, with performance and handkerchiefs. He wept the way old men weep when something long-buried surfaces without warning — silently, helplessly, the tears simply arriving as though summoned by a word he hadn't heard spoken aloud in four decades. He took off his spectacles and pressed his knuckles against his eyes and said, in a voice that shook:

"It's Vellish."

Juniper felt the word land somewhere beneath her ribs. "Vellish," she repeated. "I don't know it."

"No," Ambrose said, and his voice was strange — hollowed out, careful, the voice of a man handling something fragile. "You wouldn't. Nobody knows it. That is rather the entire point."

He rose from his chair and moved through the stacks with the surefootedness of long habit, climbing a stepstool, reaching into a shadowed alcove behind a shelf of herbals, and drawing down a flat wooden box that had clearly not been opened in years. He set it on the counter between them and lifted the lid, and inside, wrapped in oilcloth, lay a book.

It was small — smaller than a hymnal, bound in boards covered with worn black leather — and it was the most beautiful object Juniper had ever seen in a shop full of beautiful objects. The pages were dense with script unlike anything she knew: flowing, upright, marked with diacritics that rose above the lines like birds startled off a wire. Beside the text ran columns of drawings — plants, stars, tools, faces — each one labeled in the same flowing hand.

"What is it?" Juniper asked.

"A herbal," Ambrose said. "A Vellish herbal. One of perhaps nine volumes of its kind left in the world, and I say 'perhaps' because I have spent thirty years trying to count them and the number keeps shrinking." He turned a page with fingers that trembled slightly. "This belonged to my wife."

Juniper looked up sharply. "Your—"

"She was born there," Ambrose said simply. "In Velland. Before the Reckoning. Before—" He stopped, and pressed his lips together, and started again. "You're a ledger-keeper, Mistress Ravensworth. You deal in records. So let me give you the record, because almost no one alive can, and fewer still are willing."

He closed the herbal and laid both hands flat upon it, as though it might otherwise fly away.

"Forty-one years ago there was a country called Velland. It sat east of the Marrowfen, north of the old imperial road, bounded by rivers on two sides and mountains on the third. It was not large. It was not rich, except in certain respects that did not interest empires until suddenly they did. It had a language, a liturgy, a calendar that began the year at the thaw rather than the frost, and a tradition of bone-carving that produced work so fine that collectors in this city paid fortunes for it and never asked where it came from." His eyes flicked, briefly, to Juniper's hands. "Bone-carving. Yes. You'll want to hear that part again later, I think.

"And then there was the Reckoning. I will not pretend to understand what happened — nobody does, not fully, and those who might explain it are either dead or sworn to silence. What I can tell you is what the record shows. One autumn, Velland existed. The following spring, it did not. No war was declared. No treaty signed. No borders redrawn, because there were no borders left to redraw. The maps were amended. Every map. By decree, in a single season, cartographers scraped the name from their plates and re-engraved the space as *unclaimed territory*, and within five years even *that* designation was gone, and the region appeared on maps as blank — as nothing at all, as an absence shaped exactly like a country.

"The people — the ones who got out, and there weren't many — were scattered. Resettled. Some here. Some south. And every one of them, every survivor, was required to swear an oath. Not to the crown. Not to any court. To *something else*. Something that doesn't appear in any legal register I've ever been able to find, and believe me, I have looked. The terms of the oath were simple and absolute: never speak the country's name, never teach the language, never write it, never pass it on. Break the oath and—" he paused, and his voice dropped to almost nothing, "—and be unmade. That is the phrase my wife used. *Unmade.* Not killed. Killed leaves a body, a grave, a name on a stone. Unmade is different. Unmade means the forgetting takes you back, all the way back, until there is nothing left to remember that you ever were."

The shop had gone very quiet. Rain ticked against the window. Somewhere in the stacks, a settling book sighed.

"And your wife," Juniper said carefully, "swore it?"

"She swore it. And she kept it for thirty-eight years. She taught our daughters lullabies in the trade-tongue and told them they were from the Marrowfen districts, and she never once slipped, not in sleep, not in sickness, not at the end." Ambrose's voice cracked slightly. "On her last night, fevered, she spoke Vellish for six hours straight. Names. Places. Her mother's recipes. The words for snow in three different months. And then she asked me to burn her pillow, because she'd dreamed into it, and dreams counted, she said. Dreams counted." He looked at Juniper. "I burned the pillow. I kept the herbal. I have never decided whether that was love or theft."

Wren, on her stool, had been listening with the fixed attention of someone who understands far more than she can say. Now she reached out and put her small hand flat on the herbal's cover, next to Ambrose's, and said something soft and long in Vellish — and Ambrose closed his eyes as though the sound itself were a wound and a mercy at once.

"She says," he translated loosely, "that the book smells right. Like a house. Like a house where someone is cooking."

Juniper's throat tightened. "Can you read it? The language?"

"A little. My wife taught me greetings, endearments, kitchen-words. Enough to recognize, not enough to follow." He opened the herbal again to its first page. "But here is what matters, Mistress Ravensworth, and why I asked you to be honest with me about what you find. Because I am going to ask you a question, and I want you to answer it slowly."

He pointed to a line of script near the top of the page. Beneath it, in faded brown ink, someone had added a translation in the trade-tongue — his wife's hand, he said, from years ago. The translation read: *Every leaf remembers the tree.*

"Do you understand that line?" Ambrose asked. "Before you look at the translation. Just the Vellish. Look at it and tell me what comes to you."

Juniper looked.

The script flowed across the page in its tall, bird-startled characters, and she expected nothing — expected the pleasant blankness of any foreign text, the way a locked door expects nothing of the person who cannot pick it. But that was not what happened. What happened was worse and stranger. The shapes were unfamiliar, yes, and yet underneath the unfamiliarity something stirred, some pattern-recognition that operated below language entirely, the way a melody heard once in childhood rises up complete from nowhere. She found her eye tracing the line and her mind supplying, unprompted, a sense — not words, not quite, but the *shape* of meaning, the way you can feel the shape of a key through cloth.

"Every leaf remembers the tree," she said slowly. "Or — something near it. Things return to what they grew from. That the small carries the whole." She stopped. Frowned. "Why do I—"

She looked up at Ambrose, and found him looking at her with an expression she had last seen on her own face, three nights ago, listening to a barefoot child speak grief in an unknown tongue.

"Say another," she said. Her voice had gone thin. "Show me another line. Any line."

He turned pages, chose at random, pointed. Juniper bent over the text, and the same thing happened — not understanding, exactly, but *half*-understanding, meaning arriving sideways, by feel, like recognizing a face in bad light. A passage about roots. A passage about harvest. A passage whose sense came to her as *what is given to the ground is not lost, only lent.*

"I was never taught this," she said, and heard how defensive her own voice sounded, how much it wanted the statement to be true and simple. "No one taught me this. I don't speak Vellish. I've never seen Vellish script before tonight."

"No," Ambrose agreed softly. "Almost certainly no one taught you. Which raises the question of how you're reading it."

"I'm not reading it. I'm—"

"Half-hearing it," Ambrose said. "Yes. That is precisely the term my wife used, once, near the end, when she was careless with the fever. Half-hearing. She said the children of the scattered families half-hear the language — that it sits in them like a splinter under the skin, too deep to remove and too shallow to forget, and that most of them go their whole lives without knowing it's there, and that the Veil—" he used the word deliberately, and Juniper noticed he flinched slightly as he said it, as though even naming the thing cost something, "—the Veil prefers it that way. Prefers the splinter dormant. Because a dormant splinter can be ignored. An awakened one starts working toward the surface."

Juniper stood very still in the dusty shop, holding a dead woman's herbal and a living child's future in the same pair of hands, and felt the ground do something it had never done before. It tilted. Not much. A degree, perhaps. But enough.

Because she knew what else she had inherited. She knew where her gift came from — the carving, the hearing, the bone-work that let her give a dead woman's goodbye to her daughter. She had always assumed it was a Ravensworth thing, a family knack, the way some families threw tall children or good teeth. Grandmother Sera had carved. Great-aunt Ilsa had carved. It went back and back, a thread of skill running through the women of her line like wire through a bead.

But skills did not arrive from nowhere. Trades had founders. And if the language of a vanished country sat dormant in her blood, half-heard, waiting —

"How," she said, and had to start again. "How does a language get into someone's blood without anyone teaching it?"

Ambrose looked at her for a long moment. Then he turned back to the first page of the herbal, to the line about leaves and trees, and tapped it once with an ink-stained finger.

"Every leaf remembers the tree," he said. "My wife wrote that translation herself, and when I asked her what it meant — really meant, not botanically — she told me it was the oldest saying in Velland. Older than the liturgy. Older than the calendar. It meant that nothing which grows from a thing can be wholly cut from it. Cut the branch, burn the orchard, scatter the seeds across the sea — the leaf still remembers. The sap still runs to the old pattern. You can take the tree out of the country, but you cannot take the country out of the tree." He met her eyes. "Or out of the wood. Or out of the bone."

The rain had stopped. Through the fogged window, the lamps of Copperlane were coming alight one by one, yellow coins in the gathering dark.

"There's one more thing," Ambrose said, and his voice changed — dropped lower, went careful, the voice of a man about to handle something sharp-edged. "Before you go. There's a reason I'm telling you all this instead of sending you away, and it isn't only sentiment. When you came in, I said you'd brought me something spoken. I should have said you'd brought me something impossible. A living speaker of Vellish, born after the Reckoning, walking freely through the streets of this city — that is not supposed to exist. The oath forbids it. The Veil enforces it. And yet she stands here, breathing, speaking, touching my wife's book as though she remembers the house it came from." He glanced down at Wren, who was watching them both with her grave, unblinking attention. "Which means one of two things. Either the Veil is weakening — and I have seen no evidence of that; if anything the erasures grow more thorough every year — or the Veil has *let* her through. Opened a crack and passed her out into the world on purpose. And if the second is true, then the question becomes: why? What does a thing that erases countries want with a child it bothered to spare?"

Juniper had no answer. She only knew that the question settled into her chest and stayed there, cold and specific, like a coin swallowed wrong.

"And there's a last thing," Ambrose continued, "which I offer with some reluctance, because I don't know what it means and I distrust men who claim to know what things mean. My wife, in her final lucid year, made me promise something. She said: *if a child ever speaks the tongue in your shop, you will write down the date, and you will keep the book safe, and you will wait.* I asked her, wait for what? And she said—" he paused, and his river-glass eyes caught the lamplight, "—she said, *wait for the carver. The carver will come with the child. The bones will know what to do.*"

The shop was silent except for the tick of the cooling stove.

Juniper looked down at her own hands — the scarred knuckles, the fine white nicks along the thumbs, the hands that had spent twenty years listening to the dead through bone — and understood, with a clarity that felt less like thought than like a door swinging open onto a cold room, that Ambrose's dead wife had known she was coming. Had known it forty years ago, or whenever she had made the promise, sitting in this shop with her herbal and her half-hearing children and her sworn silence. Had known that someday a carver would walk through that door with a Vellish-speaking child on her hip, and that the two of them together would mean something the world had been arranged to prevent.

"I don't understand," Juniper said. It was the truest sentence she had spoken all evening.

"No," Ambrose agreed. "Neither do I. But my wife did, I think, and she is beyond asking." He closed the herbal, wrapped it back in its oilcloth with the tenderness of a man dressing a wound, and slid it across the counter toward Juniper. "Take it."

"I can't—"

"You can. You must. It's not a gift and it's not a loan; it's a delivery. Forty years late, but deliveries run late in my trade too." He pushed it firmly into her hands. "There's a page torn out. Has been since I received it. I never learned what was on it, only that my wife tore it out herself, carefully, along the gutter, and gave it to someone, and would never say who. I used to think it was a kindness — sparing me whatever it contained." His mouth twisted. "Lately I think it may have been a warning."

Juniper took the herbal. It weighed almost nothing and far too much.

At the door, she paused. Wren had already climbed onto her hip, arms looped around her neck, cheek against her collarbone, and Juniper felt the child's lips move against her skin — murmuring, half-asleep, a stream of soft Vellish syllables that might have been a prayer or a counting-song or a list of names.

And Juniper, who did not speak Vellish, who had never been taught a word of it, who had just been told that no living person should know it —

— understood her.

Not the words. The *meaning*. The way you understand weeping in any language. The child was saying goodbye to something. Saying it over and over, the way children do, making a ritual of it, a little litany against the dark. *Goodnight to the house. Goodnight to the garden. Goodnight to the names.*

Juniper stood in Ambrose Quill's doorway with a dead woman's book under her arm and a living child's litany in her ear, and felt the past reach up through forty years of careful silence and close its hand around her ankle.

"Thank you," she said to Ambrose.

"Don't thank me," he said. "Wait. She told us both to wait. Go home, Mistress Ravensworth. Check your grandmother's things. If the carver's gift came down your line the way the language comes down mine, then somewhere in your family's keeping there is a page, or a bone, or a box, that has been waiting for this exact evening." He smiled, and it was the saddest smile she had ever seen on a living face. "And when you find it — and you will find it — remember that I told you: the number of Vellish books keeps shrinking. Someone is collecting them. Someone has been collecting them for forty years. And I have never once been able to learn who."

* * *

The walk home was long and the fog was thick, and Juniper held the herbal against her chest the entire way like a shield, like a letter she was afraid to open, like a debt she had not known she owed.

Wren slept against her shoulder, heavy now, trusting, her breath warm through the wool of Juniper's coat. And as they crossed the bridge over the canal — the water black and slow beneath them, carrying its cargo of reflected lamps — Juniper became aware of a sound. Faint. Rhythmic. Rising not from the child but from somewhere deeper, from the river itself, from the stones of the bridge, from the marrow of her own bones.

Hundreds of voices. Layered. Whispering. Speaking Vellish.

The dead, she thought. The dead of a place with no name, pressing close, trying to be heard through the only instrument they had left — a carver who half-hears, and a child who remembers, and a bridge over dark water, and a book with a page torn out.

She stopped in the middle of the bridge and stood very still, and the voices washed through her and past her, and one of them, clearer than the rest, repeated the same phrase over and over, patient as tide:

*Find the page. Find the page. Find what she sold.*

Juniper walked the rest of the way home in silence, and did not sleep, and in the morning, before the guild partners were awake, before Corvane's measuring cords and standing orders could reach her, she climbed the narrow stair to the room where her grandmother's belongings sat in boxes, sealed these six years, waiting.

She broke the seal on the first box with her thumbnail.

Inside, on top of a folded shawl, lay a bone stylus, worn smooth by decades of a hand she had loved — and beneath it, folded twice, a single page torn along the gutter from a ledger, written in Vellish script, in handwriting she knew better than her own.

Her grandmother's handwriting.

And recurring down the margin, in the trade-tongue, in letters that got smaller and tighter as they descended, as though written by someone afraid of being overheard, one word, over and over:

*price. price. price. price.*
