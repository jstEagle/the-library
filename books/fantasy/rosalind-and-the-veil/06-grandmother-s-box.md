## 6. Grandmother's Box

The stylus was lighter than it should have been.

Juniper held it up to the lamp and turned it slowly, the way she turned every bone that came across her bench, and found nothing wrong with it — no flaw, no grain running strange, no old fracture healed crooked. It was simply a tool, worn to the shape of use: the shaft polished to a soft yellow gleam where a thumb had pressed for years, the point ground fine and then finer, and near the butt end, so faint she had to tilt it against the light twice before she believed it, three small notches cut in a row.

She knew those notches. Every carver in her line made them. A tally of work finished, or of debts paid, or — if you were superstitious, and bone-carvers were nothing if not superstitious — of names set free. Her own current stylus bore eleven. She had never asked anyone what they meant because there had never been anyone to ask. The knowledge had come down to her the way everything else had come down: silently, through objects, through habits, through a dead woman's hands reaching out of a box to shape a living one.

Her grandmother had been dead four years, and Juniper had not opened this trunk until now.

That was not grief. She wanted to be honest with herself about that, at least; honesty was the first rule of any ledger, and she kept her inner accounts as carefully as the guild's. Grief would have been simpler. Grief would have explained why she had let the trunk sit in the corner of her room under a dust sheet, unopened, while she walked past it every morning and every night, while she slept ten feet from it, while she ate her supper within arm's reach of it and never once — not once in four years — lifted the sheet.

No. What had kept the sheet on was something colder and more specific, and she finally had a name for it now, sitting here at midnight with a Vellish page unfolded under her lamp.

Fear.

* * *

The child was asleep in the loft above the workshop. Juniper could hear her breathing if she listened — a small, even sound, like a bellows worked very gently — and she had caught herself listening all evening, the way a sailor listens for weather. Wren. She had named the girl Wren because she was small and brown and quick and had appeared out of nowhere in the way of small birds, materializing on the guild step as though she had always lived there and everyone else had only just noticed. The name had felt right in her mouth. It had also felt, in some way Juniper could not account for, familiar — as though she had chosen it from a list she'd been given long ago and had forgotten receiving.

And the language. That was the thing that would not leave her alone. She had spent two evenings now telling herself she did not understand it, that the sounds were merely sounds, that a woman who spent her life listening to the dead through carved bone developed an ear for patterns and nothing more. But tonight, holding the page, watching her grandmother's handwriting march down it in letters she could read without effort — *without effort*, that was the obscene part, she had never studied a word of it in her life and she could read it the way she read the trade-tongue — she ran out of ways to tell herself the lie.

She understood the page.

Not all of it. The grammar had gaps in it, places where meaning went soft and slid sideways, and some words she could only circle around, catching their edges. But the bones of it — the numbers, the dates, the recurring terms — those came to her clean, the way a tune comes back to you that you have not heard since childhood and cannot remember learning.

It was a ledger page. Of course it was a ledger page. Her grandmother had been a ledger-keeper too, before the tremor took her hands; Juniper had grown up believing the ledgers were the family trade entire, that the bone-carving was a private oddity, a quirk, a Ravensworth inheritance like the long fingers and the intolerance of cilantro. Now she saw the truth of it laid out in faded ink: the carving and the keeping were one trade, always had been, and her grandmother had practiced both in a language that officially did not exist.

Juniper smoothed the page flat and began, properly this time, to read.

* * *

The top third was ordinary bookkeeping, if anything done in a dead tongue could be called ordinary. Entries in a neat columnar hand: quantities received, quantities rendered, dates in a calendar she half-recognized and half-didn't — the month-names were wrong, or rather they were right in a way that made her chest ache, because she had dreamed those month-names, she realized, she had dreamed them more than once, waking with words like *Sowmonth* and *the Grey Fortnight* dissolving off her tongue. Received: forty-one units of prepared bone, source unspecified. Rendered: nine memorial inscriptions, fee waived. Rendered: one binding, terms private. Received: —

There the ink changed color. Not dramatically; someone had simply taken up a different pen, or the same pen after it had been dropped in something, and the entries after that point were written in a brown-black that looked dried-in rather than mixed. And the hand changed too. Not the letterforms — those stayed her grandmother's, unmistakably, the same forward-leaning *l* and the same cramped little numerals Juniper used herself without ever having decided to — but the pressure. The later entries were dug into the paper. Whoever wrote them was pressing hard enough to emboss the next page beneath.

*Received, in full settlement:* the entry began, and then a word Juniper did not know, a long word with a doubled consonant that her eye kept snagging on, and *— per contract verbal, witnessed by none, price as agreed.*

*Price.*

There it was again. Down the margin, smaller and tighter with each repetition, the word marched: *price, price, price.* Not written as a note to self about an amount owed. Written the way a person writes a word when they are testing whether it still means anything. When they are trying to make it mean less by writing it many times. Juniper knew that trick. She did it herself with difficult words — *dead*, *gone*, *never* — when she was balancing the accounts of the bereaved late at night and the words got heavy. You write the word until it goes numb. You write it until it is only ink.

Her grandmother had not succeeded. Even faded to rust-brown, even repeated until the margin could hold no more, the word still had weight. Juniper could feel it coming off the page like cold off a stone.

Below the final entry, in the trade-tongue this time, in handwriting gone suddenly loose and young-seeming, as if the writer had stopped caring who read it:

*Let them look. Let them audit me. There is no column for what I was given and no column for what I paid. Let them find this when I am past accounting.*

Juniper sat back. The lamp hissed. Above her, the child breathed.

* * *

She did the thing she always did when her mind refused to settle: she worked. She cleared a space on the bench, took up the rib-bone she had been shaping for the cooper's daughter — the young woman with the drowned father, who had saved for eight months to afford a single goodbye — and set the stylus her grandmother had worn smooth against its surface.

She told herself she was only testing the tool. Old styli had temperaments; some carried residue of their former work, harmonics sunk into the grain, and a careful carver learned a new instrument before trusting it with a client's grief. That was the professional explanation, and it was even true, as far as it went.

But her hands were shaking slightly, and she knew why. If the stylus carried her grandmother's residue — if the bone remembered the hand that had held it — then using it would be the closest thing to speaking with her grandmother that Juniper would ever have. And she was afraid of what her grandmother might say.

She made the first stroke anyway. Habit is stronger than fear; that was the whole foundation of her trade.

The bone took the line cleanly. Too cleanly. The stylus moved through the rib the way a hot knife moves through tallow, and the sound it made was not the dry whisper she knew but something lower, resonant, a hum she felt in her wrist-bones rather than heard — and along with the hum came the other thing, the thing that happened when she carved, except it had never happened like this, never this strong, never this much like standing in a crowd.

Voices. Not one voice. Layers of them, dozens, scores, all speaking at once and all speaking the same syllables, the way a congregation speaks a response — and the syllables were Vellish, and she understood them, and what they said was:

*Ask her what it cost.*

Juniper lifted the stylus. The voices cut off like a door closing. The workshop was silent except for the lamp and the child's breathing above, and the rib-bone lay on the bench with a single perfect stroke down its length, deeper than she had ever cut, deep enough that she would have to sand the whole face and start over.

She sat for a long time.

Then, being who she was — a keeper of accounts, a woman who believed that any horror could be survived if it were entered accurately in its proper column — she took a fresh sheet of paper and began to make notes. Facts first. She wrote: *Stylus, bone, provenance: E. Ravensworth (grandmother). Carries resonance far exceeding normal residue. Responds to Vellish. Source of voices: unknown — possibly residual, possibly* — and there her pen stopped, because the honest completion of the sentence was *possibly live,* and she was not ready to write that.

She wrote instead: *Page torn from ledger, Vellish script, author E. Ravensworth. Content: transactions dated approximately forty years prior. Recurring term: "price." Final note suggests deliberate concealment from guild audit. Implication: transaction concealed was significant, denominated in something other than coin.*

She looked at what she had written. It was accurate. It was useless. It was a map of a country with the middle torn out, and the middle was the only part that mattered: *what was sold, to whom, and what the price was.*

Forty years ago. She did the arithmetic without wanting to, the way you probe a bruise. Forty years ago her grandmother had been a young woman, younger than Juniper was now, already keeping ledgers, already carving. Forty years ago — and here the thought arrived fully formed, the way the worst thoughts do, wearing no warning — forty years ago was the Reckoning. Forty years ago was the year a country died, the year the maps were redrawn and the archives sealed and the survivors scattered and sworn, the year the antiquarian Halvard had spoken of with that particular hush scholars reserve for catastrophes they can profit from.

Velland.

Her grandmother had been writing Vellish in the year Velland fell. Had been keeping accounts in the tongue of a nation while it was being erased, in a hand that pressed harder and harder, in margins full of a word she was trying to write numb.

And Juniper could read it. Could read it *natively*, without study, without memory of learning — which meant the language hadn't come to her from books at all. It had come down the same channel the notches on the stylus had come down, the same channel as the gift itself. Inheritance. Blood-memory. Which meant her grandmother had spoken Vellish to her, or around her, in the cradle-years before memory forms — had sung it, perhaps, or cursed in it, or counted in it over a crib — and had then, at some point, stopped. Had sealed it away. Had left Juniper a fluency with no key, a house whose doors were locked from the outside.

Why would a woman do that? Why give a child a language and then take it back?

Unless she hadn't taken it back. Unless something else had. Unless the silence had been a term of the contract — *per contract verbal, witnessed by none* — unless the price had included the words themselves, and her grandmother had paid it, and the payment had been collected from everyone downstream, generation by generation, a tithe of forgetting that Juniper had mistaken all her life for simple privacy.

The thought was too large. She set it down carefully, the way you set down something that might go off.

* * *

Above her, the floorboard creaked.

Juniper went still. Then the creak came again, followed by the soft double-scuff of bare feet, and she knew the rhythm of it already — four nights, and she knew it. Wren climbed down from the loft the way she did everything, without hesitation and without noise beyond the minimum physics required, and appeared at the foot of the stairs wrapped in the blanket Juniper had given her, hair sleep-flattened on one side, eyes wide open and entirely awake in the way of children who have woken from something.

"You should be asleep," Juniper said, in the trade-tongue.

Wren looked at her. Looked at the bench. Looked, with an expression that contained no curiosity at all, only recognition — the flat, unsurprised gaze of someone seeing a thing exactly where they expected it to be — at the open trunk, at the folded shawl, at the page lying under the lamp.

Then she spoke. One word, quiet, in Vellish, pointing one small finger at the ledger page.

Juniper understood it instantly, effortlessly, horribly. It was the word from the margin. The word her grandmother had written until it went numb.

*Price.*

"No," Juniper said — and heard how her voice shook, and hated it. "No, that's — you can't know that word. You're a child. Where did you—" She stopped. Took a breath. Started over, slower, the way she spoke to the newly bereaved. "Do you know what that word means?"

Wren considered the question with the seriousness of a magistrate. Then she nodded, once, and touched her own chest with the flat of her hand, and said something longer — a sentence, several sentences, tumbling out with the sudden urgency of a child who has finally been asked the right question — and Juniper stood there in the lamplight understanding nearly all of it, understanding it in her blood where no dictionary had ever been, and the meaning of it settled over her like snowfall:

*They took the name. They took the name and they paid for it, and everyone who was paid is still paying, and I was sent to find out who kept the money.*

The lamp hissed. Somewhere in the walls of the old guildhall, a door hummed — one of the humming doors Wren saw and Juniper did not, the doors the girl had tried to tell her about with gestures and patience, doors that ached, she'd said, doors that wanted things. Juniper looked down at the ledger page, at her grandmother's cramped and desperate hand, at the word marching down the margin like a procession of mourners.

Somewhere in this city, she thought, there must be more pages. A ledger does not consist of one leaf. Somewhere there was a spine, a cover, a full accounting — and somewhere there was the other party to a contract signed *witnessed by none*, and contracts like that, in her experience, always had a collector.

She looked at Wren, who was looking back at her with the terrible patience of the sent.

"Tomorrow," Juniper said quietly, in Vellish, her first voluntary sentence in a language she had never learned, and the shape of it in her mouth felt like putting on a dead woman's coat. "Tomorrow we follow the money."

Wren nodded, satisfied, and held out one small hand — not to be helped upstairs, Juniper realized, but asking. Asking for the page.

Juniper did not give it to her. Some debts, she was learning, you carry yourself.

But she folded it along its old creases, tucked it into her shirt, over her heart, where the important papers went — and blew out the lamp.
