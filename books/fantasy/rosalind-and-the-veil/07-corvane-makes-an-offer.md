## 7. Corvane Makes an Offer

Master Aldous Corvane believed — and had built forty years of quiet prosperity on the belief — that every problem in the world was a ledger waiting to be balanced.

He rose before dawn as he always did, in his rooms above the counting-house on Candlemaker's Row, and performed the small liturgies of order: water heated to precisely the temperature that would not scald, tea steeped for exactly four minutes, the day's correspondence read twice, once for content and once for what the writer had tried not to say. His hands were long and clean and very still when they were at rest, which was most of the time. Men who moved their hands too much, he had observed over decades of negotiation, were men who could be read like broadsheets. He preferred to be read like a sealed letter.

The Guild of Impossible Things kept three ledgers, though only two were ever shown to apprentices. The red ledger recorded debts of coin. The black ledger recorded debts of obligation — favors owed, oaths witnessed, names held in trust. And the third ledger, the one bound in grey leather that lived in a drawer beneath his left hand at all hours, recorded inventory.

It was the grey ledger Corvane opened that morning, with the city still dark outside and the lamps turned low, because overnight there had come to him a report from the night-warden at the guildhall door, written in the warden's careful, slightly frightened hand:

*A child came at the third bell. Barefoot. Unafraid, which is worse than afraid. Speaking something old. Ravensworth has taken her in.*

Corvane read it four times. Then he set it down, laced his fingers together on the desk's edge, and allowed himself the rare luxury of thinking slowly.

A child. Barefoot, so no family of means. Unafraid, so either simple or extraordinary. Speaking *something old* — the warden would not have written *old* if the child had been mumbling nursery nonsense; the warden had heard enough gutter-tongues and trader-pidgins to know them, and this had frightened him into precision.

And Ravensworth had taken her in.

Corvane did not dislike Juniper Ravensworth. Dislike was an expense, and he avoided expenses the way other men avoided sin. But he had watched her for eleven years, since she first presented herself at the guild's examination with a carved rib-bone in her palm and a dead woman's voice caught inside it like a fly in amber, and he had catalogued her the way he catalogued everything: *asset, mispriced.* She had the finest ear for resonance the guild had seen in a generation, and she used it for sentiment. She carved goodbyes for grieving daughters and charged barely enough to cover the bone. She kept accounts of small debts nobody had asked her to keep. She was, in short, a woman sitting on a fortune and using it as a cushion.

But she was also, he reminded himself, a Ravensworth, and the Ravensworths had a history with the guild that predated his own tenure by a comfortable margin. One did not move against a name like that carelessly. One moved against it *procedurally*.

He finished his tea. He dressed in the grey suit that made petitioners think of gravestones without knowing why. And he went down through the sleeping counting-house to the archive below, where the guild kept its precedents, because Aldous Corvane had learned early that in any contest between sentiment and precedent, precedent won — provided you knew where to find it.

* * *

The archive occupied three floors beneath the counting-house, and it smelled of cold stone and older paper. Corvane carried a single lamp and did not need more; he had walked these aisles so many years that his feet knew the way the way a tongue knows its own teeth.

The Reckoning-era holdings were shelved in the deep stacks, in the section the senior partners called, with the delicate euphemism of merchants everywhere, the *Difficult Period*. Corvane ran his finger along the spines until he found what he wanted: *Guild Transactions, Years of the Reckoning and After, Vol. IV.*

He carried it to the reading table, set his lamp beside it, and opened it to the pages he had last consulted nine years ago, when a similar question — smaller, cruder — had crossed his desk.

There it was. Standing Order Eleven.

He read it aloud to himself, softly, because some sentences deserved the dignity of a voice:

*"In perpetuity, the Guild shall hold itself ready to purchase, appraise, and resell persons of resonant capacity, hereinafter 'last speakers,' being individuals possessed of living connection to extinguished tongues, extinguished lands, or extinguished lineages. Rate of purchase to be determined by scarcity. Scarcity to be determined by the Guild."*

The ink was brown with age. The seal at the bottom was the guild's own, pressed by hands eighty years dead, and it was as binding now as the day it was set — more binding, in fact, because standing orders grew stronger with time the way roots grow stronger under stone. Three separate charters had reaffirmed it. Two tribunals had upheld it. Once, memorably, a partner named Ellery Voss had tried to have it struck from the books on grounds of conscience, and the motion had failed fourteen to one, and Voss himself had been bought out of his partnership within the year at a price everyone agreed was fair.

Fair. Corvane turned the word over. It was remarkable how much cruelty a fair price could carry, if the price was paid promptly and the paperwork was clean.

He sat for a long while in the lamplight, and he did not feel like a villain. This is the thing his enemies — and he supposed, in the way of things, Juniper Ravensworth was becoming one — never understood about him. Villains required appetite. Corvane required only arithmetic.

Consider it plainly, he told himself, as he considered everything. A mute child — the report said mute, or near enough, speaking only in that old tongue — arrives at the door of a trade guild. What are the possible futures?

One: she stays with Ravensworth, unregistered, uninsured, unappraised. If she is what the warden's fright suggests she is, this is a catastrophic waste. Resonance of that quality, left unrecorded, is a candle burning in an empty room. Worse: unregistered assets attract thieves, and thieves do not negotiate. The child would be stolen within the month, sold across three borders, and the guild would have neither profit nor credit for having sheltered her. Sentiment, left unmanaged, always ended in someone else's ledger.

Two: she is registered, appraised, and purchased — by the guild, at a fair price, under Standing Order Eleven. She would be housed, fed, examined by professionals who understood what she was. Her resonance would be documented, catalogued, protected. Buyers for last speakers existed — they always had; there were collectors in the southern principalities who paid sums that made even Corvane pause — but the guild's standing practice was to sell *access*, not flesh. An hour of listening. A season of recording. The child would outlive her usefulness the way a quarry outlives its cutting, which is to say, transformed rather than destroyed.

Three: nothing is done, and the thing hunting her — because Corvane had been in this trade long enough to know that things which erase countries do not leave loose ends wandering barefoot through cities — finds her. And then the guild has lost the asset entirely, and Ravensworth has lost whatever she imagined she was saving, and the world is exactly as silent as it was before, minus one child.

Only one of these outcomes was defensible. Only one of them had paperwork.

He closed the volume with a soft thump that echoed down the stacks like a door shutting somewhere far away, and he felt the particular calm that always settled over him when the moral question resolved itself into a procedural one. It was not that Corvane lacked a heart. It was that he had trained his heart, over forty years, to beat in columns.

* * *

The guildhall stood on Weeping Lane, in the oldest quarter of the city, and it was — Corvane conceded this freely — a beautiful building, all honeyed stone and impossible angles, its doors humming faintly to those with ears tuned for it. He arrived at mid-morning, when the hall would be awake but not yet busy, and he was announced by a junior clerk whose hands trembled slightly on the visitor's book. Good. A little fear kept clerks honest.

Juniper Ravensworth received him in the small parlor off the main hall, the room where she conducted her sentimental little business, and he noted immediately — because he noted everything — the changes since his last visit. A blanket folded on the window bench that had not been there before. A wooden cup, child-sized, on the sideboard. A smell of porridge lingering under the beeswax.

She was standing when he entered, which was correct, and she did not offer her hand, which was also correct; they had never liked each other enough to pretend otherwise.

"Master Corvane."

"Mistress Ravensworth." He took the chair she indicated, arranged his coat, and placed his leather case on his knee. "I'll come directly to business, if I may. I know you prefer it, even when you pretend otherwise."

Something flickered at the corner of her mouth. "Business."

"A matter of inventory." He opened the case and drew out a single sheet, laid it on the table between them, and turned it so she could read it upright. "Standing Order Eleven. Reaffirmed in the charters of three successive administrations. I've brought the citation page as well, should you want to verify the seals."

She looked at the paper the way one looks at a snake discovered in a linen chest. "I know what Standing Order Eleven is."

"Then you'll understand why I've come." He let the silence sit for exactly three seconds — he had timed silences the way musicians timed rests — and then continued, gently, because gentleness was simply another instrument and he played all of them. "A child came to your door three nights ago. Barefoot. Speaking a language the night-warden describes as old. You've taken her in. Fed her. Given her a blanket." He nodded toward the window bench. "All of which does you credit, Mistress Ravensworth. I mean that sincerely. Compassion is a virtue, and rarer in this trade than it ought to be."

"But."

"But the guild cannot permit an unregistered person of apparent resonant capacity to remain in its halls. Not out of malice. Out of *duty*. If she is what she appears to be — and I confess the warden's description has me hopeful — then she is precisely the class of asset Standing Order Eleven exists to protect. There are parties," and here he lowered his voice, confiding, drawing her in whether she wished it or not, "who have maintained open orders for such persons for decades. Collectors of the extinct. Archivists of the erased. They pay magnificently, Juniper — may I call you Juniper? — and more importantly, they pay *reliably*. No haggling. No defaults. Grief, properly capitalized, is the steadiest market in the world."

"You're talking about buying a child."

"I'm talking about *appraising* a child," Corvane corrected, with the mild patience of a man correcting a junior clerk's arithmetic. "Purchase follows appraisal, and appraisal follows consent — yours, as her current custodian, and eventually hers, when she's old enough to give it meaningfully. The guild has standards. We are not slavers, whatever our critics say over their wine. We are custodians of scarce things, and we keep them better than anyone else could. Would you leave a Stradivarius in a barn? Of course not. Why would you leave a last speaker in a parlor?"

"She isn't a violin."

"No," he agreed pleasantly. "She's considerably rarer."

He watched Juniper's hands. They had gone still in her lap — the stillness of a woman gripping something invisible — and he catalogued it, filed it, moved on. Under the table, he knew, she would be doing her own accounting: the cost of refusing him, the cost of the scene, the cost of the enemies made. She was intelligent. Intelligence was the tragedy of people like her; it meant she could see the trap clearly, and would climb into it anyway, believing her sentiment would find a seam in the bars.

"Where is she now?" he asked.

"Sleeping."

"At mid-morning?"

"Children keep odd hours when they arrive at odd hours." Her chin lifted a fraction. "You've done your research, Master Corvane. Tell me — what do your sources say she speaks?"

"Vellish," Corvane said, and watched the word land.

He had expected flinching. He got something stranger: a stillness so complete it seemed the room itself had stopped breathing, a stillness with a hum underneath it, as though the doors in the hall beyond had leaned closer to listen. Interesting. Very interesting. The Ravensworths and Velland — he had wondered, going through the old volumes, why that family name appeared so often in the Difficult Period's margins, always in the columns marked *paid in full*.

"You know the word," he said.

"I know the word is forbidden."

"Forbidden is a word for children and priests. In commerce we say *scarce*." He leaned forward, and for the first time that morning he let something warm show through the grey — not kindness, nothing so expensive, but the glow of genuine professional excitement, which was the nearest thing to warmth he owned. "Do you understand what you are sitting on? A country erased root and branch, forty years gone. Every map burned, every ledger struck, every survivor sworn dumb. And into your lap walks a child who carries the whole of it in her mouth. Do you know what the southern collectors pay for a single authenticated recording of an extinct tongue? Now multiply that by a *speaker*. A living, growing, self-renewing speaker. She isn't inventory, Juniper. She's a mint."

"She's eight years old."

"Which means eighteen years of yield before maturity, and residual value after." He said it flatly, deliberately, because he wanted her to hear the machinery turning — wanted her to understand that this was not cruelty but *process*, gears that had been turning since before either of them was born, oiled by charters and seals and the accumulated respectability of a century. "You may hate the arithmetic. Many do. But the arithmetic does not require your love. Only your signature."

"And if I don't sign?"

Corvane sat back. He had prepared for this too; he prepared for everything, and the preparations were the truest affection he was capable of.

"Then the matter goes to the full partnership," he said. "As a compliance question. Unregistered resonance in guild halls is a charter violation, Juniper — not by you, you're blameless, you *took her in*, which the record will show was generous — but the hall itself falls under guild jurisdiction, and the partnership cannot ignore a standing order brought to its attention. I would be obliged to bring it. Colleagues expect it of me." He spread his long clean hands, palms up, a gesture of helpless transparency he had practiced until it looked like sincerity. "I would argue for leniency, naturally. A grace period. Time for the girl to be... transitioned gently. But fourteen votes against one conscience, Mistress Ravensworth. We both remember how that went for poor Ellery Voss."

The lamp flame bent in a draft from the corridor. Somewhere deeper in the hall, faintly, one of the humming doors changed its note — a low, aching tone, like a finger drawn around the rim of a glass. Corvane had heard that note before, in this building, and had never liked it. Doors, in his experience, should open or shut. Doors that *felt* things were bad for business.

"May I see her?" he asked. "Before you decide anything. Appraisal requires observation. Five minutes. I'll pay for the five minutes — standard consultation rate, credited against any future transaction."

"There will be no future transaction."

"Then the five minutes are free," he said, smiling, "and I am a generous man."

She did not answer. And in the silence, from the stairwell behind the parlor wall, came a sound that stopped them both: a child's voice, singing. Soft, wordless-seeming, in that old tongue the warden had feared — except that Corvane, who had spent forty years training his ear the way swordsmen train their wrists, heard what lay underneath the melody. Layers. Hundreds of layers, voices stacked in a throat too small to hold them, the way a cathedral holds a congregation.

The hair rose along his forearms, and Aldous Corvane — who had not been frightened by anything in thirty years, who had sat across tables from men who bought and sold grief by the pound — understood, with the clarity of a man reading a figure already entered in his own hand, that he was looking at the greatest asset of his career.

And that he was not the only one who had noticed. Because the song faltered, just once, and beneath the faltering he heard — or thought he heard, and in his trade *thought he heard* was the same as heard — a second sound underneath the child's voice. Something vast and patient, moving through the city outside like weather, like a hand smoothing a wrinkle from a map.

The Veil, the old volumes called it. And the old volumes, Corvane reflected, closing his case with a click, had been right about everything else.

"Five minutes," Juniper Ravensworth said at last, quietly, rising. "And Master Corvane — whatever you think you're buying, you should know that I have read Standing Order Eleven too. All the way to the bottom."

"Ah," said Corvane pleasantly, following her toward the stairs, toward the singing, toward the largest entry the grey ledger would ever hold. "Then you know it has no bottom. That's rather the point of standing orders."

Behind them, at the back of the hall, the aching door began — very softly, very patiently — to knock.
