## 10. Wren Remembers Fire

The room above the guildhall had two beds and one window, and the window did not open all the way. Wren had tried it on her first night, when the walls of the place were still strange to her, when every door in the corridor hummed like a struck bell that would not finish ringing. The window opened only a hand's width, enough for cold air but not for a child, and she had decided then that this was either an accident or a kindness, and she could not tell which.

She was used to deciding things without knowing which they were.

Now she lay in the narrow bed with its blanket that smelled of cedar and old wool, and she listened to Juniper Ravensworth breathing in the other bed. Juniper breathed the way she did everything — carefully, evenly, as though even sleep were a ledger to be balanced. In the day she was all sharp edges and ink-stained fingers, a woman who spoke to bones more easily than to people. But asleep, her breath was just breath, and it made a rhythm, and rhythms were the only thing in the world that had never once frightened Wren.

Rhythms were how you knew a thing was still alive.

She counted them. She always counted them. Four in, four out, the small creak of the bedframe, the tick of the cooling stove. Outside, the city muttered and clattered and went about its business of not knowing. Inside, the guildhall's doors hummed their low note, and beneath the hum — if she pressed her ear to the pillow and held very still — she could hear the far door at the back of the hall, the one that ached. It ached the way a tooth aches, the way an empty socket aches, and some nights she thought: *that is where home went.* Some nights she thought: *that is the crack I came through.*

Tonight she thought nothing at all, because sleep took her between one count and the next, the way the Veil had taken everything else — quickly, without negotiation, without a receipt.

* * *

It began with smoke.

Not real smoke. The stove was banked; the candle was out. But Wren dreamed smoke first, always smoke first, because smoke had been the last thing Velland said to her. It rolled into the dream from under the dream's own door, grey and patient, and it did not sting her eyes because it was not made of burning. It was made of forgetting. That was the trick of it, the thing no one who had not been there could understand: the Veil did not come with fire. Fire came after. Fire was what the city did to itself in its last hours, lanterns knocked over in the panic of being erased, hearths flaring as houses lost the memory of their own chimneys. But the Veil itself was smoke without heat, smoke that unmade rather than consumed, and Wren had stood in it, seven years old and barefoot on cobbles going soft as bread under her feet, and watched the street behind her become a street that had never been.

In the dream she was running. She was always running, and she was always carrying something, and in the waking world she could never remember what it was, but in the dream her arms were full and heavy and precious and she could not look down at them because looking meant stopping and stopping meant —

"Mama," she said, or tried to say, and the word came out in Vellish, the way it always did, *Mamusha*, and the smoke swallowed the word before it finished leaving her mouth. That was the worst part. Not that the Veil took streets and towers and the bridge with the ninety-nine lamps. It took words *in flight*. You could be saying a name, half-said, and the country would end before the name did, and somewhere — she was sure of this, sure in her marrow — there were names hanging in the air over Velland still, unfinished, waiting forty years for mouths to complete them.

The bells began. In the dream, the cathedral bells rang backwards — not tolling out but drawing in, each peal a sound being taken back, and with every backward ring another piece of the city went quiet inside her. The baker's name. The color of the river in autumn. Her grandmother's hands. She felt them leave like teeth pulled, little sockets of emptiness opening all through her memory, and she ran harder, arms full, toward —

Toward the Door. Toward the crack. Toward the narrow place where the Veil had not quite finished sewing itself shut, the gap behind the tannery where the world still smelled of the world. Someone had put her through it. Big hands, rough palms, a voice saying words she had carried out with her like embers in cupped fingers, words she had repeated ten thousand times since so they would not go the way of the rest:

*Remember the fire but not the burning. Remember the names but not the faces. Carry it out. Carry it out. Someone must carry it out.*

And then the gap closed behind her like a mouth, and she was in a ditch on the far side of nowhere, barefoot, alive, holding whatever it was she held, and the sky above her was a sky with no Velland in it, and it has been that sky ever since.

In the dream she turned to look at what was in her arms.

She had never once managed to look. Every night the same failure, the same waking at the edge of seeing, and tonight was no different — except that tonight the smoke followed her up out of the dream. Tonight it came with her.

* * *

Wren woke already screaming.

She did not know she was screaming until she heard it, thin and high and in Vellish, words tumbling out of her that she did not choose — *the lamps, the lamps, someone count the lamps* — and the room was full of smoke. Real smoke, or smoke enough. It poured off her skin like steam off a hot stone, grey ribbons curling from her shoulders, her hair, her open mouth, and the ceiling of the little room darkened as though a fire burned in the walls' memory, and the smell — juniper and cedar and ink — drowned under the smell of that night: wet ash, scorched flour from the bakery that had been mid-loaf when the end came, the iron tang of fear.

A lamp flared. Juniper was there.

Later, Wren would try to remember exactly what happened, and the remembering would be imperfect, dream-tangled, but certain things she kept forever. She kept the fact that Juniper did not shout. She kept the fact that Juniper, waking into a room filling with impossible smoke coming from a child she had known less than a fortnight, did not run for help, did not fetch Master Corvane, did not do any of the sensible things a sensible woman does when the impossible leaks into her bedroom. She crossed the floor in three steps and knelt by Wren's bed and gathered her up, blanket and all, the way you gather something that might shatter, and she said — in plain trade-tongue, calm as bookkeeping:

"I have you. Nothing here is burning. Count my breaths."

Counting. She had said counting, as if she knew, as if bone-carvers understood that a frightened child is a clock that has come loose from its case and needs only a rhythm to hold to. Wren pressed her face into the hollow of Juniper's shoulder and counted. Four in. Four out. The smoke thinned. It did not vanish — it settled, low along the floorboards, sullen, reluctant, the way fog settles in a valley at dawn — but it thinned, and the screaming in Wren's throat wound down to sobbing, and the sobbing wound down to shaking, and the shaking went on and on, longer than the smoke, longer than the nightmare, because the shaking was not really about the dream at all. The shaking was forty years old. It belonged to everyone who had ever stood in that smoke, and it had simply chosen her small body as the place to live.

Juniper held her through all of it. She did not say *there, there*, which is a nothing-phrase. She did not say *it was only a dream*, which is a lie told to children about the truest things they know. She sat on the edge of the bed with Wren against her chest and she rocked, slightly, and after a long time she said, quietly, almost to herself:

"The lamps. You said count the lamps. Ninety-nine, aren't they. On the bridge."

Wren went still.

She looked up. Juniper's face was pale in the guttering lamplight, drawn, and her eyes had the look Wren had seen once before, the day she had tested her with carved bone and gone white as the carving itself — the look of a woman hearing a language she has never been taught and understanding it anyway.

"You know the bridge," Wren whispered. In Vellish. Because some things could not be said in any other tongue; the trade-tongue was a coat too big for them, and they fell off the shoulders of the meaning.

"No," Juniper said slowly. "I don't. I've never — " She stopped. Started again. "Say it again. What you said in your sleep. Say it slowly."

"*Kto schotet lampy, tot pomnit most,*" Wren said. Who counts the lamps remembers the bridge. A skipping rhyme. Children sang it on the bridge itself, stamping their feet on each number, and the ninety-ninth stamp was always shouted, always, because the ninety-ninth lamp was the lucky one, the one that —

"Whoever counts the lamps," Juniper said, and her voice did something strange in the middle of the sentence, bent like a board under weight, "*remembers the bridge.*"

They looked at each other. The smoke had nearly gone now, only a haze near the ceiling, and the lamp flame stood straight again, and outside the window the indifferent city went on being loud and alive and ignorant.

"How do you know that," Wren said. Not a question. An accusation, almost, though she did not mean it as one. It was the voice of a child discovering that a locked box in her own chest had a second keyhole, and that someone else had been walking around all this time carrying the key without knowing it.

"I don't," Juniper said. And then, honestly, which Wren would later understand was the rarest thing about her: "Or I do. I don't know how. My grandmother used to hum something. I thought it was nonsense. A made-up tune." Her hand had drifted, without her noticing, to the string of beads at Wren's throat — the little carved bones Wren wore day and night, the only thing she had carried out of the smoke besides herself. "May I?"

Wren nodded.

* * *

The beads were her treasure and her burden both. There were eleven of them, carved from different bones — a knuckle, a sliver of rib, a bird's wishbone worn smooth, others she could not name — and each had been worked with marks too fine for a child's eye, spirals and strokes and tiny stacked lines that she had always understood, without being told, were writing. Names, maybe. Or promises. Or debts. They had been pressed into her hands at the last moment, along with the words, by whoever had thrown her through the crack, and she had spent four years in orphanages and workhouses and roads clutching them while matrons tried to take them away as choking-hazards, and she had bitten every single one of those matrons.

Juniper lifted the string with both hands, careful as lifting a sleeping bird, and turned the beads one by one in the lamplight.

She did not touch them to her skin. Wren saw that she wanted to — saw her thumb hover, hover, hover — and then Juniper closed her eyes and let her fingertips rest on the largest bead, the knuckle-bone, and Wren watched her face change the way a pond changes when a stone goes in.

"Oh," Juniper said softly. "Oh, there are *people* in here."

And then the room filled again — not with smoke this time, but with fragments, spilling off the beads and into the space between them, and whether Juniper spoke them aloud or Wren dreamed them shared she could never afterward untangle, and perhaps it did not matter. Fragments:

*A woman laughing in a doorway, flour on her hands, calling a name — the name won't come, the name is smoke, but the laugh comes through whole.*

*Ninety-eight lamps lit and the ninety-ninth dark, and an old man climbing the bridge ladder in the rain to relight it, muttering, "Not on my watch, not while I'm alive."*

*A table set for six, and five chairs filling, and the sixth empty, and everyone pretending not to look at it.*

*A hand pressing beads into small hands. Rough palm. The voice again, the voice from the dream, urgent and low:* carry it out, carry it out — *and above the voice, a tower, and above the tower, the sky beginning to go wrong, the stars sliding like beads on a broken string—*

Juniper gasped and let go. The beads swung. The lamp steadied. For a moment neither of them moved, and the silence between them was not empty; it was crowded, full of everything that had just passed through it, like a room after a procession.

"That was your city," Juniper said at last. Her voice was rough. "That was — those were real people. I heard them. I heard them the way I hear — " She stopped, and Wren watched her decide whether to finish, and admired her for finishing. "The way I hear the dead. When I carve. Only I wasn't carving. I was only touching."

"They're not dead," Wren said. "I don't think they're dead. I think they're — " She searched for the word and found it, the word from the rhyme, the word from the beads, the oldest word she owned. "*Unfinished.*"

Juniper looked at her for a long moment. Then she did something Wren had never seen her do. She laughed — one short, startled, unguarded note, half grief and half wonder — and pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes.

"Unfinished," she repeated. "Yes. That's exactly the word for it. That's the word for half the entries in my ledgers, if I'm honest. Debts unfinished. Goodbyes unfinished." She lowered her hands. Her eyes were bright and wet and fierce. "Wren. Listen to me. The smoke tonight — it came *out* of you. I've handled relics that wept, that bled, that sang. I've never seen memory pour off a living person like that. Whatever the Veil did to you, it didn't finish either. You're not just carrying the country." She touched the beads again, lightly, one fingertip, and let go. "You're leaking it. And I think — I think it's leaking into me."

Outside, far across the rooftops, a bell tolled the hour. Three. Wren counted the strokes automatically, the way she counted everything now, and on the third stroke a thought arrived that made her cold from the crown of her head down, colder than the smoke, colder than the ditch she had woken in four years ago:

The bell was tolling wrong. Backwards. Drawing sound in instead of sending it out.

Only once. Only a single stroke, gone so fast she might have imagined it — except that Juniper had gone rigid beside her, staring at the window, and when she turned back her face was the face from the fragments, the face of someone watching stars slide off their string.

"Wren," Juniper said quietly. "How long has it been since anyone else in this city heard Vellish spoken?"
