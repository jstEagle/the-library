## 11. The Sworn Silence

The capital's tenements leaned against one another like old men at a funeral, holding each other up out of habit rather than affection. Juniper climbed the stairs of the third such building on Saltcote Row with Wren's hand wrapped tight in hers and a name written on a scrap of paper she had paid too much for: *Hollis Fenner, lamp-oil merchant, once of somewhere he will not say.*

The antiquarian had given her two other names besides, and a warning shaped like an apology. "They won't thank you for finding them," he'd said, counting coins into her palm as though paying for a burial. "Men who survive an erasure learn to be very good at not existing. You'll be asking them to exist."

Wren stopped on the landing outside the fourth-floor door and tugged Juniper's sleeve. She pointed at the wood — scarred, paintless, a door that had been repainted so many times its grain was buried alive beneath the layers — and then pressed her palm flat against it.

"What is it?" Juniper asked.

Wren made the sound she made when words failed her: a small exhale through the nose, almost a laugh, almost grief. She tapped the door twice, then touched her own chest, then shook her head slowly. *Someone in there knows me. Or knew what I know.* It was impossible, of course. The child had come out of nowhere, out of a country that was nowhere, and this was a lamp-oil merchant in a city that had forgotten Velland before Wren was born. But impossibility was Juniper's trade. She had learned to stop flinching from it.

She knocked.

The footsteps inside were slow, deliberate, the footsteps of a man who wanted whoever waited on the other side to hear him coming. The door opened a hand's width. A chain caught it. One eye appeared in the gap, brown and wet and old, and looked at Juniper for a long moment before dropping to Wren.

Juniper watched the eye change.

It did not widen. That was the terrible thing. It did not widen with surprise; it contracted, the way a wound contracts, pulling in on itself, and the man behind it made a sound low in his throat like something tearing that he refused to let become a word.

"Master Fenner," Juniper said. "My name is Juniper Ravensworth. I keep ledgers. I carve bone. I need—"

"No." His voice was gravel poured over glass. "No, no. Take it away." He wasn't looking at her. He was looking at Wren the way a man looks at floodwater rising past his doorstep. "Take it away from my door."

"She's a child. She's lost everything. She speaks—"

"I know what she speaks!" The chain rattled as his hands shook. "Do you think I've forgotten? Do you think any of us forget? We are not permitted to forget, Mistress Ravensworth. We are only permitted to be silent about it, forever, until we die, and even then—" He stopped himself as though he'd said too much, which he had, and his face closed like a ledger snapping shut. "I sell lamp oil. I have sold lamp oil for forty years. There is no other country. There never was. Get away from my door."

"Please," Juniper said, and heard her own voice go quiet and level, the voice she used with grieving daughters when they brought her their mothers' ribs. "I'm not here to make you speak. I'm here because a child walked out of the dark speaking a dead tongue, and something is hunting her for it, and I don't know enough to protect her. You don't have to tell me anything. Just look at her. Look at her and tell me you see nothing."

The eye in the gap went to Wren again. Wren, who had been silent through all of it, lifted her free hand and — deliberately, gently, the way one touches a sleeping animal — signed something in the air. Her fingers moved in shapes too quick and fluid for Juniper to follow, but the man behind the door followed them perfectly. Juniper saw it land in him like a stone dropped down a well: the long fall, the long silence, and then, faintly, from very far below, the splash.

He began to cry. He did it silently, expertly, the crying of a man who had practiced for decades, tears running down into the creases of his face without a single sound escaping him.

"I can't," he whispered. "Girl. Little one. Whatever they sent you for, whatever you think I can do — I can't. If you have any mercy, and it looks like someone taught you mercy where you came from, you'll take her and go, and you'll stop asking, and you'll pray — pray to whatever's left to pray to — that nothing ever asks you the question you just asked me."

The door shut. The bolt went home. Then, after a moment, a second bolt, and a third, and Juniper stood in the dim corridor listening to a man barricade himself against a child.

Wren's hand had gone rigid in hers. When Juniper looked down, the girl was staring at the door with an expression far too old for her face — not hurt, exactly. Recognizing. As if she had seen this exact fear before, worn by other faces, in another place.

*They're afraid of something,* Juniper thought. Not of me. Not of her. Of *speaking.*

* * *

The second name took them across the river to a widow's room above a dyer's shop, where the air itself was stained violet and every surface smelled of mordant and urine. Old Sefa Ilves — the antiquarian had hesitated over whether Ilves was even her true name; *they changed them, most of them, or had them changed* — received them in a chair by the window with her hands folded like a woman posing for a portrait she intended to outlast.

She listened to Juniper's whole speech without interrupting. That alone marked her as different from Fenner. When it was done, she said, in a dry and reasonable voice:

"You want to know about Velland."

The word landed in the violet room like a struck bell. Juniper felt Wren flinch — not away from it, but *toward* it, the whole small body leaning like a plant toward light.

"I do," Juniper said carefully.

"Then I'll tell you what I tell everyone, which is nothing, and I'll tell you why, which is also nothing, because the why is part of the nothing." Sefa Ilves smiled without warmth. "You're a bone-worker. I can smell it on you. Char and marrow and the little oils you use. So you'll understand me when I say: some things are carved to be kept, and some things are carved to be *buried*, and the difference is who did the carving. I didn't carve my silence, mistress. It was carved into me. And I have learned, these forty years, not to pick at other people's carvings. Pick at yours."

"There is no mine," Juniper said. "I wasn't born there. I barely know the name."

"Mm." The old woman's eyes flicked to Wren, held there, and something passed over her face — recognition, refusal, recognition again, each drowning the last. "And yet you came to my door saying the word Velland like you were setting it down on a table between us, careful not to spill it. Where did you learn to carry a word like that?"

"My grandmother wrote it. Once. In a ledger I found after she died."

Something moved behind Sefa Ilves' eyes then, quick and involuntary, and Juniper — who had spent her life reading faces the way other people read weather — caught it before it could be smoothed away. Not guilt. Not fear, exactly. *Grief*, old and shelved and dusted weekly, kept like a room no one entered but no one dared sell.

"Your grandmother," the old woman repeated. "Ravensworth. Yes. I remember the name from — from trade. From ordinary trade." She unfolded her hands and refolded them. "Go home, bone-worker. Burn the page. Some debts are better left unbalanced. Some accounts should close with the people who opened them."

"You know what's in the page."

"I know nothing," Sefa Ilves said, with terrible gentleness. "That is my entire fortune, and I guard it more carefully than you guard your guild's silver. Nothing is a very expensive thing to own, child. I paid everything I had for it. Don't ask me to spend it on your curiosity."

"It isn't curiosity." Juniper heard her own voice harden and let it. "There's a Veil. It erased a country and now it's erasing the last person who remembers it, and she is seven years old, and she is sitting in your violet-smelling room while you explain to me how wisely you spent your life buying nothing. Forgive me, madam, but I keep ledgers, and I know a bad investment when I audit one."

For a long moment the old woman said nothing. The dye-works below breathed its chemical breath up through the floorboards. Then Sefa Ilves turned her head and looked at Wren directly, fully, for the first time — and Juniper saw the old woman's composure crack along a seam she had clearly maintained for four decades.

"You were born inside," she whispered. Not a question. "After. They said no one — they said the ones born after couldn't—" She stopped. Her folded hands had come apart without her noticing; they trembled in her lap like birds. "Oh, you poor *thing*. Oh, you poor, doomed—"

She caught herself. Physically caught herself, one hand flying up to cover her own mouth, and Juniper watched the woman wrestle her own voice back under lock. When she spoke again, around her fingers, the words came out flattened, sanded smooth of everything:

"There is an oath. All of us who came out took it — had it taken *for* us, some of us, in our mother's mouths before we could choose. It isn't a promise the way you understand promises. Promises are kept by the people who make them. This one is kept *on* them." Her eyes were wet and furious and afraid. "There is a thing — a condition, a presence, call it what you like — and it listens. It has listened for forty years. Speak the name of the country and it hears. Speak the names of the dead and it hears. Break the oath and it does not punish you, bone-worker. Punishment implies you continue afterward. It *unmakes*. Not kills — unmakes. Your body stays. Your name goes. Everyone who ever met you finds a hole where you were, and fills it with something else, and goes on. I have seen it done. Once. To a man who got drunk on the anniversary and sang."

Her voice had dropped to almost nothing. "He sang four lines. By the fifth line there was no one singing. By morning his wife had remarried — the wedding was already planned, you understand, the guests already notified, as though it had been arranged for years — and no one in that street could tell you who had lived in the rooms above the cobbler's shop. I went to look. I stood in the empty rooms and I could not remember either. I stood there *knowing* I had known him and reaching for his face and finding — smoothness. Like a page scraped clean for reuse." She laughed, one dry note with no humor anywhere in it. "So. Now you know the price of truth in this trade, ledger-keeper. Truth costs the speaker everything, including having been anyone. Are you still so eager to balance your books?"

Juniper's mouth was dry. Beside her, Wren had gone very still, and her small hand had found Juniper's coat hem and gripped it.

"Yes," Juniper said.

Sefa Ilves stared at her. "You haven't understood a word."

"I understood every word. And yes. Because whatever oath your generation swore, it was sworn to save something — a remnant, a seed, a *chance* — and that chance is sitting next to me getting thinner every week. An oath that requires a child to be devoured isn't an oath anymore. It's just the trap wearing the oath's clothes." She rose. "Thank you for spending some of your nothing on us, madam. I'll try not to waste it."

At the door, the old woman spoke once more, so quietly Juniper almost missed it.

"There's a third name on your paper. Maren Ostrova. Laundry-woman, by the tanneries." A pause, in which forty years of discipline fought visibly with something older and stronger. "Maren never cared much for being careful. If anyone breaks, it will be her. Tell her—" The hand came up to the mouth again. Behind it, muffled: "Tell her Sefa still keeps the festival bread recipe. Even now. Even useless. Tell her that."

* * *

The tanneries announced themselves a quarter mile before they arrived, a reek of lime and rendered fat that made Wren wrinkle her nose and press her face into Juniper's shoulder. The tenements here were lower, meaner, built right up against the stinking water, and the laundry-woman's rooms were the top floor of a building whose staircase had opinions about visitors — each step groaning a different note of protest, so that stealth was impossible and Juniper gave up trying.

Maren Ostrova opened her door before Juniper finished knocking.

She was smaller than Juniper had imagined, a round, sturdy woman in her seventies with forearms like dock rope and white hair cut brutally short, practical as a hedge. She looked at Juniper. She looked at Wren. And then she stepped back and swung the door wide and said, in a voice pitched for the whole stairwell to hear:

"Well. Come in before the whole street sees you standing there like a pair of lost geese."

Inside was small and fierce and clean: a stove, a bed, a table scrubbed white, and on every wall — hanging from nails, propped on shelves, bundled in baskets — herbs. Bundles and bundles of dried herbs, labeled in a careful hand, filling the room with a green smell that fought the tannery stink to a draw. Juniper recognized perhaps a third of them.

"You're a healer," she said.

"I'm a washerwoman who knows things washerwomen shouldn't, same as I've been for forty years." Maren shut the door, shot the bolt, and then — Juniper watched her do it with growing astonishment — dragged a chair across the floor and wedged it under the handle. "Sit. Both of you. Girl, sit by the stove, you're half blue. There's bread on the shelf and honey if you can find the jar, and don't mind the jars that rattle, those are just bones, I boil them for stock like anyone sensible."

Wren was already moving, drawn by some instinct Juniper was beginning to recognize — the child read rooms the way Juniper read ledgers, scanning for the true entries. She found the honey. She sat by the stove. And Maren Ostrova pulled a second chair up close to Juniper's, sat down heavily, took both of Juniper's hands in her rope-hard grip, and said:

"Now. Before you say one word. Let me look at you."

She looked. It was less like being examined than like being *read* — the old woman's gaze moving over Juniper's face, her hands, the bone-handled knife at her belt, with an attention that grew stranger and stranger, until at last Maren made a sound deep in her chest, half sigh and half sob, and released her hands.

"The hands," she said. "I knew it the moment I saw the hands. Carver's hands. My grandmother had those hands. The knuckles, the way the thumb sits. Forty years I've told myself I imagined it, that every bone-carver looks like that, and then you walk up my stairs with a *child* on your arm and I know — I know whose granddaughter you are." Her eyes filled and she wiped them impatiently with her wrist, furious at them. "Ravensworth. Of course. Of *course* it would be that house that came back."

"You know my family."

"I know what your family cost." Maren said it flatly, without cruelty, the way one states a price. "And I know you don't. And I know why you don't, and we'll get to that, because it's the same reason Fenner wouldn't open his door and Sefa — Sefa sent you, didn't she. Stubborn old boot. She sent you to me because I'm the weak one. Forty years and she's still managing me." Something like fondness moved through the anger. "Yes. There's an oath. Every soul that came out of Velland before the Veil closed took it, willingly or otherwise, and it binds in the blood, and it is enforced by the thing itself. You break it, you get unmade. Not killed. *Unmade.* Sefa told you that part, I expect, with her nice frightening story about the singer. It's all true. It's worse than true. Ask me how I know."

"How do you—"

"My husband." Maren's chin came up. "Tomas. Broke it in year nine. Not drunk, not careless — deliberate. Our daughter was dying, you understand, fever, and the healers had given up, and Tomas decided that if the Veil wanted our silence so badly, then our silence must be worth something, and he stood in the square and he *priced it*. Shouted the old names. Ours, the neighbors', the dead's. Said, come and collect, and give me back my girl." Her jaw worked. "The fever broke that night. Our daughter lived. She's a grandmother herself now, in the eastern provinces, and she has never once wondered about her father, because there is no hole where Tomas was — there's a *fullness*. A whole invented life, a brother who never existed who misses him, letters in a trunk in a hand no one wrote. The Veil doesn't leave gaps, bone-worker. Gaps invite questions. It writes fiction, and the fiction is always better documented than the truth ever was." She laughed, short and hard. "I'm the only person alive who mourns him, and half the time I can't remember his face either. I keep a carving of it. Under the floor. Would you like to know what I keep under the floor?"

Juniper's heart was hammering. "Yes."

Maren crossed to the corner, pried up a board, and lifted out a bundle wrapped in oilcloth. Inside lay a slab of river slate, and scratched into the slate with a nail — crude, loving, redone so many times the surface was worn to lace — was a man's face. And around the face, packed edge to edge in tiny characters Juniper could not read but *recognized*, the way one recognizes a melody heard in childhood: writing. Vellish. Pages and pages of it, a whole smuggled archive scratched into a single stone.

"Every night," Maren said quietly, "I write one line on this slate. One line of anything — a memory, a recipe, a name. And every morning I rub it out and write a new one, because keeping is breaking, but *almost*-keeping — holding it in the hand and letting it go before dawn — the Veil permits that. It permits the shape of the crime without the substance. So I have spent forty years committing one line of treason per night and washing it out per morning, and that, mistress ledger-keeper, is what survival looks like when you've sworn what we swore." She set the slate in Juniper's hands. It was warm from the wrapping, warm as a living thing. "Now. You've come to ask me to break it properly. To speak the names aloud, to tell you about the Door Out, to help you fight a thing that unmakes people. And here is what I want you to understand, before you ask, so you can't pretend later that you didn't know."

She sat back down. She took Juniper's hands again, and her grip was iron, and her eyes were bright and wet and absolutely clear.

"If I speak — truly speak, past the line, the way Tomas did — I don't die. Understand that first. Death would be a mercy and the Veil is not merciful. I will simply *stop having been*. You'll stand in this room and feel certain you came here alone. The slate will be blank, or worse, it will have some other history written on it. The child will not remember the woman who fed her honey. And I — whatever I am afterward — I will not suffer, because suffering requires a self, and I will have none. That is the cost of truth. That is the invoice. Every elder you visit tonight is looking at that invoice and choosing, hour by hour, day by day, to keep paying the other price instead — the price of silence, which is watching everything we loved get eaten one memory at a time and smiling at the neighbors over the fence." She exhaled. "Forty years I've chosen silence. And now the Veil has gotten greedy, and started hunting children, and sending its grey men out past the borders it used to respect, and I find, at seventy-three, that my arithmetic has changed."

Outside, far off across the rooftops, a church bell rang the hour, and Juniper became aware that the light through the one small window had gone the color of old honey. They had been in this room for hours. Wren was asleep on Maren's bed, curled around the last of the honey jar, breathing slow.

"Maren," Juniper said. "What do you need from me? What can I—"

"Nothing yet." The old woman squeezed her hands once, hard, and let go. "But listen, and listen well, because I'll only say this part once and then we'll both pretend I didn't. The Door Out stands where the name stood — your bones have been whispering that to you, don't look surprised, I can see it all over you, you hear them the way my grandmother heard them. And the Door opens soon. Once a generation, at the turning, and the turning is *this winter*. Which means the Veil knows it too, and it is hurrying, and that is why the grey men are abroad and why a child was pushed through a crack in the world wearing nothing but her own language." She glanced at the sleeping girl, and her whole craggy face went soft and ruined with tenderness. "They didn't send her out to save her, bone-worker. They sent her out because she's the key, and keys get carried to locks. The question — the only question — is who else is carrying her, and toward what."

She rose, banked the stove, and pulled a blanket over Wren with hands that had washed ten thousand linens and buried, secretly, one entire country.

"Sleep here tonight," Maren said. "Tomorrow I'll teach you what I can of the tongue, and the road, and the turning. And tomorrow night—" she paused at the window, looking out at the dark over the tanneries, and her reflection in the black glass looked suddenly very old and very calm, "—tomorrow night I'm going to start practicing. One line a night, rubbed out by morning. Except I think, this time, I may let a few of them stay."

Far away, back across the sleeping city, in a guildhall full of humming doors, a man with ink-stained fingers was drawing a fresh sheet of paper toward him and uncapping his pen — and Master Corvane had already decided, with perfect procedural calm, exactly what Wren was worth.
