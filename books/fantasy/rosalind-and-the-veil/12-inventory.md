## 12. Inventory

Master Aldous Corvane kept his ledgers the way other men kept their consciences: clean, current, and closed to inspection.

He rose before dawn as he always did, in the narrow grey hour when the guildhall's humming doors were at their quietest — not silent, never silent, but murmuring rather than singing — and he dressed without assistance, having long ago dismissed the idea that a man of his standing required a valet to witness his rituals. His coat was charcoal wool, unadorned but for the guild's clasp: a small silver hand holding a small silver key. He polished it himself each morning with a chamois cloth until it caught the lamplight like a fish turning in dark water.

The counting room occupied the third floor's eastern corner, a room designed by a man who had understood that light was a form of disclosure and had therefore provided very little of it. One window, tall and narrow, faced the brick wall of the chandler's warehouse next door. The lamps on Corvane's desk burned behind green glass shades, so that every document he read took on the color of deep water, of pond ice, of things seen through a veil.

He found the phrase apt. He had been thinking about veils lately.

The girl — Wren, they were calling her, though no one had yet entered the name into any book that mattered — had been in the guildhall nine days. Nine days of Ravensworth's soft-footed obstruction, of the junior partners developing sudden scruples, of old Mistress Halloway in the appraisal wing refusing on three separate occasions to certify the child for valuation. Nine days, and still no entry in the Register of Holdings.

Corvane did not intend to let there be a tenth.

He unlocked the bottom drawer of his desk with a key worn smooth as a river stone, and from it he lifted a ledger bound in black calfskin so old its spine had gone the color of dried blood. The Reckoning Ledger. Fewer than six people alive knew it existed; Corvane suspected only two of those six could still read the shorthand in its margins, and he was one.

He opened it to the marker and began, with the unhurried pleasure of a man doing work he was genuinely good at, to build his case.

* * *

There was a particular satisfaction, Corvane had found over thirty years in the trade, in precedent. Passion made men reckless; conviction made them loud; but precedent made them *right*, or at least made rightness unnecessary. A man armed with precedent did not need to persuade. He needed only to file.

And the guild's archives concerning the Reckoning era were, he thought fondly, among the finest collections of filed horror in the civilized world.

He turned to the entry dated in the autumn of the year the maps changed — the year Velland went from being a country to being a rumor to being nothing at all, all within the span of a single fiscal quarter. The handwriting belonged to Master Emeric Voss, then Senior Partner of Acquisitions, and it was beautiful: copperplate so fine it looked engraved, each figure ruled twice, each judgment delivered in the passive voice as though the judgments had simply occurred, like weather.

*Received into inventory,* the entry read, *forty-one persons of Vellish origin, being refugees of the late unpleasantness, appraised under the Resonant Goods Provision (Guild Charter, Article IX) as follows—*

Corvane ran his finger down the column. He knew it by heart, but he read it anyway, the way a musician reads a score he can play blind. Forty-one persons. Appraised individually. A grandmother valued high for her fluency; two children valued higher, because children's resonance was unspoiled, unmuddied by grief the grieving party could name. There was a note in the margin in Voss's smaller hand: *NB — the little ones fetch best whole. Do not let the brokers separate them from their effects; the effects carry half the value.*

Their effects. Their beads, their buttons, the little carved birds Vellish mothers hung over cradles. Corvane set his pen down for a moment and looked at the green-shaded lamp, and permitted himself, briefly, the memory of what those words meant. Then he picked the pen up again. Sentiment was a cost, and costs were to be entered honestly in the ledger and then paid, once, and never re-incurred.

What struck him — what had always struck him about this page, since the first time he'd read it as an apprentice with cold hands and a head full of romance about the antiquities trade — was how *kind* the language was. Received into inventory. Appraised. Provisions. The Reckoning-era clerks had not been monsters cackling over their abacuses. They had been careful men doing careful work, men who wrote *the little ones fetch best whole* in the same hand that wrote love letters home, and who slept soundly after, because the ledger balanced and the charter authorized and the world, whatever else it was, was orderly.

That was the lesson Corvane had taken from his apprenticeship, and he had never once had cause to regret it: the difference between atrocity and administration is only whether someone has filled out the forms.

He would fill out the forms.

* * *

By mid-morning the counting room smelled of ink and sealing wax, and the first document lay finished beneath the paperweight: a plain sheet of good rag stock, headed with the guild's arms, titled in the conventional formula.

*Notice of Appraisal and Claim of Holding.*

He had drafted it four times before dawn and settled, finally, on simplicity. The guild, it stated, had received into its custody a minor female child of unknown provenance, currently lodged at the hall under the protection of Junior Partner J. Ravensworth. The child exhibited linguistic capabilities consistent with classification under Article IX of the Guild Charter. Pursuant to said article, and to the precedents enumerated in the attached schedule, the child was hereby entered into the Register of Holdings as a resonant good, pending formal valuation.

Attached schedule: eleven citations, drawn entirely from the Reckoning era. Corvane had chosen them with a connoisseur's care. Not the ugliest entries — ugliness invited argument, and argument invited scrutiny, and scrutiny was the one commodity the guild had never learned to price — but the most *boring* ones. Entries where the language was dry, the signatures were multiple, and the outcome had never been contested. Precedent, properly deployed, was not a weapon. It was furniture. You did not attack a man with furniture; you simply arranged the room so that everywhere he turned to sit, you had already been.

He sanded the page, blotted it, and sat for a moment listening to the building.

The doors were humming again. They always hummed more when something was about to happen, though Corvane had never determined whether the doors knew things or merely echoed the people who walked through them. Ravensworth claimed — in her careful, sideways way, the way she claimed everything, as though afraid a straight sentence might cost her — that the doors remembered. That each threshold in the old hall had stood somewhere else once, had been a door out of some burning house or flooded valley or erased country, and carried the sound of what it had witnessed.

Corvane considered this superstition beneath contempt and privately, in the green-lit privacy of his own skull, found it plausible. He had walked through the back door of the hall — the one at the end of the corridor, the one the juniors avoided, the one that ached, though none of them ever said *ached* aloud — and he had felt it too. Like pressing a tongue to a cracked tooth. Like a debt noticed but not yet due.

He wondered, sometimes, which country that door had come out of. He wondered it more often lately, and he noted the increase in frequency the way he noted any irregularity in the accounts: with displeasure, and a resolution to correct it.

* * *

At noon he went down to see the girl.

This was not sentiment. He told himself it was not sentiment with the same firm clarity he applied to all his self-tellings, and the telling held, mostly, the way a well-kept ledger holds: every line justified, every sum carried forward correctly. An appraiser who had never seen the asset was a negligent appraiser. He was going down to inspect inventory. That was all. That was the whole of it.

She was in the courtyard behind the kitchens, sitting on the mounting block with her knees drawn up, watching the pigeons on the granary roof with an attention that seemed less like a child's idle watching and more like a creditor reviewing accounts. She was thin. Barefoot again — someone had given her shoes and someone, presumably the same contrary will, had abandoned them somewhere. Her hair was the brown of river water and she wore one of the kitchen maid's cast-off smocks, and around her wrist, where no sensible person would wear anything valuable, she had strung a set of small carved bone beads.

Corvane stopped in the shadow of the archway and looked at the beads, and felt the tooth-ache feeling start up behind his sternum.

He knew that carving. Not those specific beads — he had never seen those specific beads — but the *hand* of them. The style. Vellish bone-work had a signature that forty years of erasure had not managed to file away: the lines cut shallow and close, so that the pattern only resolved at a certain angle of light, as if the carver had understood that some things should be legible only to those already looking. The guild's vaults held crates of it. Resonant goods. He had personally signed the manifests.

The girl turned her head and looked directly at him, and Corvane, who had stared down brokers and bailiffs and once, memorably, a warden of the Veil itself across a negotiating table, found that he was the first to look away.

"Master Corvane," he said, because courtesy cost nothing and established the register. "You are Wren."

She didn't answer. Of course she didn't answer; she answered almost no one, except — the reports were unanimous on this, and unanimity among guild staff was rarer than honest weights — except Ravensworth, to whom the child spoke in that tongue that made the junior partners cross themselves and the senior partners reach for their valuation books.

"I'll be direct," Corvane said. Directness was wasted on children, generally, but this one watched him the way his own accountants watched him, waiting for the figure behind the flourish, and he respected that even as it unsettled him. "There are parties who have expressed interest in you. Serious parties. The kind whose interest does not expire. If you remain in this hall unregistered, you are a liability to everyone sheltering you, and liabilities get settled. If you are registered — properly, under my authority — you become protected property. Untouchable. Do you understand the difference?"

She understood. That was the devil of it. She tilted her head, and her eyes went narrow and knowing, and for a moment Corvane had the vertiginous sense of being appraised himself — weighed, marked, entered into some private column he could not read. Then she looked back at the pigeons, and the moment passed, and he told himself it had meant nothing.

He left a sweetmeat on the mounting block beside her, the way one leaves feed with livestock one intends to purchase, and hated himself for the gesture's accuracy, and did it anyway.

* * *

The filing took place at four o'clock, in the Scriptorium, before witnesses.

He had chosen his witnesses as carefully as his citations. Mistress Halloway was absent — summoned, by a note in Corvane's hand, to an appraisal in the Lower City that he had invented that morning; her scruples were legendary and her counter-signature was therefore unavailable to obstruct him. In her place he had secured Brother Quill, the guild's registrar, a man so old that the Reckoning was for him not history but memory, and so punctilious that he would rather die than witness a filing incorrectly.

"The instrument," Corvane said, laying the Notice on the great table, "concerns the child currently resident under Junior Partner Ravensworth's protection."

Quill adjusted his spectacles and read. Corvane watched him read, and watched the old man's face do what faces did with this material: nothing at first, then a small gathering at the brow, then the settling — the terrible, familiar settling — into acceptance. Quill had countersigned worse. Everyone in this room's lineage had countersigned worse. That was the architecture of the thing; that was why it stood.

"Article Nine," Quill said at last. His voice was dry as the sand he'd just sprinkled. "Resonant goods. We haven't invoked the provision since—" He stopped. Calculated. Corvane watched him calculate and admired the precision of it even now. "Since before my registration."

"Which is precisely why it remains valid," Corvane said. "A provision unused is not a provision repealed. The charter is explicit. And the precedents—" he tapped the schedule "—are unimpeachable. Eleven filings from the Reckoning era, all countersigned, all uncontested. The guild traded in last speakers then, Brother Quill. It is not a new trade we begin today. It is an old one we resume."

"And the child's consent?"

Corvane smiled, gently, the smile he used on clients who asked about provenance. "Property," he said, "is not consulted."

The word landed in the room and sat there. Quill looked at it. Corvane watched him look at it, and understood — with the calm, procedural clarity that was his gift and his curse — that the old man would sign. Not because he was wicked. Quill was not wicked; Quill watered a plant on his windowsill and sent coins to his sister in the coast towns and had once, decades ago, wept over a consignment of confiscated wedding rings. He would sign because the alternative was to make a scene, and scenes required convictions, and convictions required believing that the ledger could be wrong, and no man survived forty years in this guild while believing that.

The quill scratched. The wax dripped. The seal came down — the silver hand, the silver key — and it was done.

*Registered: one female minor, designation pending, classified resonant good, custody assigned to the Office of the Senior Partner of Acquisitions.*

Corvane collected the documents, thanked the witnesses with genuine warmth, and climbed the stairs to his counting room with the papers under his arm and the taste of sealing wax in his mouth.

It was only at the desk, entering the day's transactions in his personal book, that he allowed himself to notice the irregularity. The date. He had written it automatically, in his flawless hand, and only afterward did the number resolve into meaning: the anniversary. Forty years ago this very week, Master Emeric Voss had signed the first of the forty-one into inventory, in this same room, perhaps at this same desk, in this same green light.

Coincidence, Corvane wrote in the margin, and then, because he was an honest bookkeeper even with himself, he crossed it out and wrote instead: *noted.*

* * *

Ravensworth came up at dusk. He heard her on the stairs — she climbed quietly, but the third stair sang for everyone eventually — and he did not look up from his ledger when she entered, because making people wait was a form of interest, and interest compounded.

"You've filed her." Juniper Ravensworth's voice was level. He gave her credit for that. Most juniors would have shrilled.

"I've registered an asset," Corvane said, finishing his line of figures before raising his eyes. "Under charter provisions older than both of us. Under precedents your own predecessors helped establish, Miss Ravensworth — I cite Master Emeric Voss in my schedule. Family, I believe."

Something moved behind her face, quick and buried, like a fish under ice. "You dug up the Reckoning Ledger."

"I keep the Reckoning Ledger. Someone in this guild must. Erasure, Miss Ravensworth, is a service like any other — and services, once rendered, generate records. The Veil takes countries. It falls to men like me to take minutes." He closed the book. "You've grown attached. I understand. Attachment is natural, particularly in your... specialty. But consider the arithmetic. There are buyers for a last speaker whose offers do not lapse. There are wardens whose patience does not either. Registered, the child is mine, and everything I hold is defended by the full weight of guild writ. Unregistered, she is a loose thread, and loose threads get pulled — by people far less gentle than I am."

"You're calling yourself gentle."

"I'm calling myself *procedural*," Corvane said, "and in this world, Miss Ravensworth, procedure is the only mercy that scales."

She stood a moment longer in the green light, and he saw her decide — visibly, the way one watches a clerk decide whether to contest a figure — not to fight it here, not tonight, not on ground he had prepared. She was learning. He noted that too, in the margin of himself, under *liabilities, emerging.*

"Valuation's scheduled?" she asked.

"Thursday. Independent assessor. High standard." He paused, and permitted himself one indulgence, small and precise as a pinprick. "Do give the child my regards. And tell her — tell her the beads she wears are worth more than she knows. To certain markets."

She left. The door hummed shut behind her, low and long, like a note held by someone who had forgotten how to stop.

Corvane sat alone in the green light with his perfect books, and told himself the ache in his chest was indigestion, and entered it in no ledger at all.

Outside, far off across the rooftops, the evening bells rang the hour — and beneath them, faintly, from the direction of the old back door at the end of the corridor, came a sound like a great many voices trying very hard to remember a word.

Corvane locked the Reckoning Ledger in its drawer, and did not sleep well, and entered that, too, under *noted.*
