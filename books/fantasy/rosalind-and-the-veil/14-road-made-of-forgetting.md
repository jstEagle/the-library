## 14. Road Made of Forgetting

The first village was called Harrow's Bend, and it did not know itself.

That was how Juniper thought of it afterward, though at the time she only noticed small things: the way the innkeeper's wife paused over the word *north*, as if reaching for something on a high shelf; the way the milestone at the crossroads had been chiseled smooth, its letters scraped away so thoroughly that the stone seemed embarrassed by them. A milestone with no name was not a milestone. It was just a rock that had once been asked a question.

They had been walking for two days since leaving the city — two days of hedgerows and cart-ruts and Wren's small hand tucked into Juniper's larger one, gripping in a rhythm like a pulse. Juniper had planned the flight the way she planned everything: as an entry in a ledger. *One child, removed from inventory. One guild, defrauded of nothing it ever rightly owned. One debt, assumed willingly.* She had forged their exit in the night-book with her grandmother's steady hand copied out letter by letter, and the doors had hummed them through without complaint, and for half a day she had felt something dangerously close to joy.

Joy, she knew now, was a currency. It would be collected.

"Are you hungry?" she asked Wren, because it was easier than asking what she actually wanted to ask, which was *do you feel it too?*

Wren shook her head, then nodded, then made the small gesture she used for *a little* — palm flat, hovering, neither up nor down. The girl had not spoken aloud since they fled. Juniper had told herself this was caution, a sensible silence on open roads. But she had begun to fear it was something else: that speech itself was dangerous now, that every Vellish word Wren swallowed was a coin hidden from a thief who could hear counting.

The inn at Harrow's Bend was low-beamed and smelled of tallow and boiled mutton. The common room held four farmers, a drover, and a dog that regarded Wren with more interest than any of the humans did. Juniper counted out coppers for bread, cheese, and a bed in the loft, and asked the question she had promised herself she would ask carefully, casually, the way one asks about weather.

"We're bound north," she said. "Past the old river country. Is the road good through there?"

The innkeeper — a broad man with flour to his elbows — smiled the smile of a man answering a question he'd answered a thousand times.

"North of here?" He shrugged. "There's the river road as far as the ferry, and past that there's nothing much. Hills. Some ruined places the quarry folk use for shelter. Why would you be going?"

"Just to see," Juniper said. "Family land, they say."

Something moved behind the innkeeper's eyes. Not suspicion. Not quite. It was the look of a man who has been asked to remember a thing and finds, when he reaches for it, that his hand passes through empty air where the thing should be. His smile stayed fixed but went strange at the edges, like a painting hung slightly crooked.

"There's no family land up there," he said, gently, the way one corrects a child. "Never was. That country's all fell in and gone to scrub, and before that—" he frowned, genuinely groping now, "—before that I couldn't say. There wasn't anything before that, I expect."

"There was a country," Wren said.

Juniper's heart stopped. The girl's voice was thin and clear as a wire, and she spoke the trade-tongue with a foreign grain buried in it, vowels shaped by a mouth that had learned other shapes first.

The common room went quiet. The four farmers looked up. The dog stood.

And then — this was the part Juniper would turn over and over in the nights that followed — the room did not react the way rooms react to strangeness. No one stared at the child. No one asked who she was or where she'd come from or why a barefoot girl spoke words no one should know. Instead, the farmers looked at each other with mild, puzzled blankness, the look of men trying to recall whether they'd left a gate open, and one by one they returned to their ale. The moment slid off them like rain off oilcloth.

The innkeeper laughed, kindly. "A country! Children say such things." He set down the bread and cheese and ruffled Wren's hair with a hand that meant no harm and did enormous harm anyway. "There's no country north of the river, little one. There never was. Now eat your supper."

*There never was.* Four words, said with total sincerity, by a man standing forty miles from the border of a nation that had fielded armies within living memory. Juniper took the plate with steady hands because her hands had learned steadiness the way other people learn prayers — through long practice in the presence of grief — and led Wren to a corner table, and sat, and did not eat.

She understood, watching the farmers laugh at some joke, what she was looking at. This was not ignorance. Ignorance is a hole where knowledge should be, and holes have edges; you can feel the rim of them. This was different. This was knowledge *replaced* — a smooth surface laid over the wound, seamless, self-consistent, utterly without seams to pick at. The Veil did not make people forget and leave them wondering. It gave them a world in which there had never been anything to wonder about.

It was, Juniper thought with a professional's horror, the finest forgery she had ever seen. No ledger showed its workings. No entry betrayed the hand that made it.

Wren ate her bread in small, precise bites, watching the room with dark eyes. When she finished, she looked up at Juniper and touched her own throat, then pointed north, then made the flat hovering gesture again: *a little*. Juniper didn't understand until the girl took her finger and pressed it, gently, against Juniper's sternum, below the collarbone, where a word might sit if a word were a stone you'd swallowed.

*A little,* the gesture said. *You still have a little.*

"Yes," Juniper whispered. "I remember it exists. For now."

Wren's expression did not change, but her hand came across the table and closed over Juniper's fingers, and stayed there, and that was answer enough.

* * *

They slept in the loft, under a roof of warped shingles, and Juniper lay awake listening to the sounds a building makes when it thinks no one is listening — the tick of cooling timber, the scurry of mice in the thatch, the wind testing the door latch like a patient burglar. Beside her Wren breathed in the slow rhythm of true sleep, and Juniper took out Maren's map.

It was not a map in any cartographer's sense. It was a length of linen, embroidered in thread that had once been many colors and was now mostly the color of remembering — faded rose, faded river-blue, faded green. Maren had stitched it over three nights in her tenement room, hands shaking, lips moving constantly in a silent recitation, because — she had explained — the map existed only while she could hold the whole of it in her mind at once, and the Veil pulled at the edges of it like a tide. *I am giving you my country the only way left to give it,* the old woman had said. *In thread. In stitches. In the shape my hands remember even when my mouth is forbidden.*

On the linen, Velland spread north of the river in careful stitched outline: the double bend of the Velle, the forest called the Whispering Ord, the terraced hills, and at the center, worked in gold thread gone brown, the capital — which Maren had not named, because she could not, because the name caught in her throat like a fishbone and drew blood when she forced it. She had marked it instead with a small stitched star and said only: *the Door stands beneath the palace, and the palace stands where the name stood.*

Juniper traced the route with one finger. Three days' walk to the ferry. Then into country that was Velland and officially was not, where every village would be Harrow's Bend, every innkeeper would smile his seamless smile, and every mile north would take them further from a world that admitted they were real.

She looked at the sleeping child beside her — this scrap of a person she had known eleven days and would unmake guild law and burn her own inheritance to protect — and felt the isolation of the road settle over her like a second blanket. They were two people carrying a country that no longer believed in itself. Every person they met would be kind to them and wrong about everything. Every kindness would be built on the erasure they were walking to undo. There was no one to appeal to. There was no authority above the lie, because the lie had eaten the authorities first.

She folded the map away and did not sleep, and in the grey hour before dawn she heard — or dreamed she heard — a sound from the road below: footsteps, unhurried, passing the inn and continuing north. Not heavy enough for a man, not light enough for a deer. And underneath the footsteps, fainter than breath, something that was almost a voice saying almost a word, over and over, the way a child repeats a phrase to keep from forgetting it.

By full light there was nothing on the road but dew and their own footprints from yesterday, and the dew had already filled those in.

* * *

The second village was called Stony Ford, and it had a church, and the church had records, and this — Juniper told herself — would be the test. Parishes keep registers. Baptisms, marriages, burials, going back centuries, ink on vellum, the most stubborn form of memory outside bone. If the Veil could reach into a parish chest and rewrite fifty years of entries, then no ledger anywhere was safe, and everything Juniper was — everything the Guild of Impossible Things was — rested on sand.

The priest was a young man with ink-stained fingers and the eager manner of someone rarely visited. Yes, certainly, he could show them the registers; travelers so seldom asked. He brought out the great book with reverence and opened it across the table, and Juniper ran her finger back through the decades, back past her own birth-year, back toward the year of the Reckoning.

The entries were beautiful. Small, careful, human. *Baptized, Agnes, daughter of Tomas the miller. Married, Aldous Fletcher and Bridget Hale. Buried, Goodwife Ermengarde, aged 84, having outlived three kings.* Page after page of ordinary lives faithfully kept.

And then, forty years ago, the handwriting changed.

Not gradually. On one page the old curate's cramped brown script recorded a burial, a birth, a marriage — and on the very next page, in a different hand entirely, in ink so fresh it might have dried that morning, the register resumed as though nothing had intervened. Forty years of entries in a hand that belonged to no one, recording baptisms and weddings and funerals in flawless, anonymous script, each entry perfect and each entry somehow *empty*, like fruit made of wax. Juniper turned pages faster. Decades of it. The same hand. The same impossible freshness. As if the book had been writing itself all this time, diligently inventing the lives of a parish, and everyone in the parish had simply agreed not to notice.

"Curious," the priest said behind her, and his voice had that crooked-smile quality she had heard in the innkeeper's. "I've often wondered about this hand. It's not Father Anselm's. But of course it must be — who else would have written it?" He laughed softly, pleased with the circularity of it. "Books don't write themselves."

"No," Juniper said quietly. "They don't."

She looked up at him and made herself ask. "Father. Forty years ago. Was there trouble in this region? A war, a plague, a — a change of borders?"

He considered it honestly, she would grant him that. He reached for it the way the innkeeper had reached, with genuine effort, a man climbing toward a shelf. And Juniper watched his face as he climbed, watched him find the shelf bare, watched him come back down smiling and untroubled, and it was worse than if he had lied.

"Borders?" he said. "No. Nothing has changed here in living memory. We're a quiet parish. Always have been." He paused, and for one flickering instant — one — something moved behind his eyes, some drowned thing surfacing, and he said, in a voice not quite his own: *"We used to trade with them over the water. Before."*

Then it was gone. He blinked. "Before what?" he said, to himself, mildly. "Strange. It's gone whatever it was." And he smiled his seamless smile and offered them tea, and Juniper accepted because refusing would have required explaining, and there was no explaining this. There was no language yet for what was happening, except perhaps Vellish, and she could not speak it, and soon — she understood this with growing certainty — soon no one would be able to speak it at all.

Outside, Wren waited by the churchyard wall, reading the lichen-covered stones. Juniper joined her and followed the girl's gaze. The oldest stones were worn smooth. Of course they were; that was weather. But the wear was wrong — uniform, recent, thorough, the wear of a cloth wiped across a slate rather than the slow tooth of rain. Names had been on these stones. Something had licked them clean.

At the gate, Wren stopped and looked back at the church, and then at Juniper, and made a series of gestures, quick and urgent: her hands closing like a book; her fingers walking; her palms pressed together and then flung apart, opening, emptying.

Juniper translated slowly. "The book closes. The words walk away. The hands open, and everything spills out." She knelt so their eyes were level. "Is that what the Veil does? It doesn't break things. It opens them and lets everything run out?"

Wren nodded. Then she added one more gesture, and this one Juniper understood without translation, because the girl performed it slowly, deliberately, looking Juniper full in the face: she laid her own small hand flat against her chest, over her heart, and then lifted it away, and held the emptiness of her lifted palm between them.

*It takes what you carry,* the gesture said. *Not what you own. What you carry.*

* * *

They walked north through the afternoon, and the country began to lie to them in earnest.

It was subtle at first. A bridge over a stream that had no stream — dry arches spanning dry grass, built for a river that the maps agreed had never run there, though the cut of its valley was older than any map. An orchard of trees bearing no fruit anyone could name, in rows too regular for wildness, pruned by hands whose village had been subtracted from the world. A lane that forked, and both forks bore milestones, and both milestones were blank.

By evening the erasure was not subtle at all.

They crested a hill and saw, in the valley below, the ghost of a town. Not a ruin — ruins are honest; ruins admit what they were. This was a town mid-sentence, a town interrupted: roofs half-faded into hillside, a church tower that was solid at the base and transparent as smoke at the belfry, streets that appeared when the light struck them sideways and vanished when it shifted. Fences ran to nowhere. A well stood in a dooryard where no house had ever been, or where a house had been so thoroughly unwritten that even its absence had been tidied away.

And the names. Juniper felt them before she understood them — a pressure in the ears, a taste like copper coins, the sensation she knew from her carving when a dead woman's name wanted saying and no living mouth would say it. The valley was *full* of names. They pressed up out of the ground like spring water, hundreds of them, thousands, street-names and family-names and the names of dogs and boats and cheeses and hills, all of them straining toward speech and finding no one to speak them, pooling in the hollows of the land like snowmelt with nowhere to go.

Wren stood at the crest of the hill with the wind pulling her hair northward, and her lips were moving.

Juniper watched her, and the hair rose on her arms, because the girl was not mouthing one name. Her lips moved the way water moves over stones in a flood — continuously, in currents, branching and rejoining — and though no sound crossed the distance, Juniper could *feel* the shape of what she was saying, the way you can feel music through a floor. The girl was greeting the town. Name by name by name. Reading it back to itself before the last of it drained away.

"Wren," Juniper said softly. "Don't spend yourself. Not here. Not yet."

The girl's lips stilled. She looked at Juniper with an expression far too old for her face — the expression of someone doing arithmetic with the only coins she has — and then she pointed down into the valley, at the fading church tower, and held up her fingers.

Five. She folded one down.

Four. Another.

Juniper's stomach dropped. "Days? The town has four days?"

Wren shook her head slowly, and corrected her with terrible gentleness: she pointed not at the valley but at the whole horizon, the whole dimming north, the entire invisible country lying beyond it under its seamless shroud — and folded another finger down.

Three.

*Everything,* the gesture said. *Three.*

And then, because the child was who she was, she took Juniper's hand, and turned them both north, and took the first step down the hill.

They made camp that night in the lee of the well that stood in its houseless dooryard, and Juniper did not sleep. She sat with her back against the cold stone and her ledger open on her knees — the real ledger, the private one, the one she kept in her head because paper could be seized — and she ran the accounts the way her grandmother had taught her, item by item, because numbers were a floor to stand on when everything else tilted.

*Assets:* one child, priceless, irreplaceable, exhausted and asleep against her side. One map stitched in dying thread. One gift of hearing, hers by inheritance, still functioning. One grandmother's box of secrets, mostly decoded, all of it damning. One destination: the Door Out, beneath the palace, where the name stood.

*Liabilities:* the entire machinery of forgetting, working northward ahead of them and behind them, patient as water. Three days, maybe fewer, and shrinking. A guild that would send writs after them. Wardens she had not yet seen but could feel the way animals feel weather. And a truth she had carried since her grandmother's box came open: that her own fortune, her own gift, her own place in the world had been purchased with a country's name, and that the seller's blood ran in her veins.

Somewhere past midnight, the pressure of the names in the valley changed. It gathered. It concentrated — south of them, back along the road they had come, at the edge of hearing, at the edge of *believing* — and it resolved into something Juniper had heard described in guild files she had once filed away as superstition: a sound like many voices speaking one sentence slightly out of step, a sound that unmade what it repeated, a sound the old traders called *the Recitation.*

The wardens were on the road behind them. And they were not merely following.

They were erasing the road — village by village, mile by mile, the whole track of Juniper and Wren's flight being smoothly, seamlessly unwritten behind them — so that when anyone came looking, there would be no record that two travelers had ever passed this way at all.

Juniper closed the ledger. She woke Wren with a hand over the girl's mouth to swallow the cry, and pointed north, and they walked out of the dying town under the last stars, faster now, while behind them the night went quiet in a spreading circle, like ice forming across a pond — and somewhere in that silence, unhurried, patient, something grey began to walk.
