## 15. Maren's Map

The tenement on Candlewick Row had a smell Maren knew better than her own hands: wet plaster, tallow smoke, and the particular sourness of poverty that has stopped apologizing for itself. Forty years she had lived in rooms like this one, and in all that time she had never once said the word aloud. Not in her sleep. Not in fever. Not when the coughing took her last winter and she was certain, lying in the dark with her lungs full of gravel, that she was going to die among strangers who did not know what country she came from — that she would be buried under a stone bearing a name that fit her like a borrowed coat.

She had kept the oath. She wanted that recorded somewhere, even if nowhere kept records anymore of the things that mattered. She had kept it when the Veil's grey shapes passed in the street below her window and she pressed her face into the pillow so as not to look at them. She had kept it when other survivors broke — old Tomas, who lasted eleven years before he wept the name into his soup one evening and was simply not there by morning, his bowl still warm, his chair still rocking. She had kept it through every silence, every erasure, every small daily murder of memory that survival demanded.

And now there was a child standing in her doorway, barefoot on the rotten boards, looking up at her with eyes the color of the river at Velland's heart, and saying words Maren had not heard spoken by a living mouth in four decades.

*Good evening, grandmother. We are looking for the door.*

Maren's hand went to the doorframe. The wood was soft with rot and her fingers left dents in it, and she was grateful for the pain because it proved she had not died and dreamed this.

"Come in," she said, in Trade Tongue, because her mouth refused — *refused*, the way a locked gate refuses — to shape anything else while the light was on and the curtains open. "Quickly. Quickly now."

* * *

The girl was called Wren. The woman who had brought her was called Juniper Ravensworth, and that name went through Maren like a fishhook finding old scar tissue.

She knew the name. Of course she knew it. Every survivor of the Reckoning knew it, though none of them could say how they knew it, because the knowing itself had been scrubbed down to a smooth pebble of dread with no inscription left on it. Ravensworth. A merchant family, the ledgers said, risen from nothing forty years ago, prosperous beyond reason, lucky beyond accounting. Maren had seen their seal once, on a shipping manifest in the capital's customs house, and had felt her teeth ache and her eyes water and had not known why until this very moment, standing in her own narrow room with its one window and its saints'-day candle stubs, watching the granddaughter of the woman who had sold them all stand awkwardly among her chairs.

"You know me," Juniper said. It was not quite a question. She had a carver's hands, Maren noticed — strong knuckles, fine scars across the fingertips, the right thumbnail worn short from bracing a blade. Hands that worked bone. That was its own kind of terrible. That gift had come out of Velland like blood out of a wound, and here it was, generations later, living in the family that had bought its escape with the rest of theirs.

"I know your name," Maren said. She lowered herself into her chair by the window, joints complaining, and studied the child instead, because the child was easier. Wren had climbed onto the stool by the cold stove and sat with her knees drawn up, turning slowly, taking in the room: the patched quilt, the chipped blue bowl, the icon of Saint Ferren that Maren kept turned to the wall because its face reminded her of her sister. The child looked at everything the way returning sailors look at coastline.

"She speaks it," Maren said quietly. "The tongue."

"She doesn't speak much of anything else," Juniper said. "Not to me. I only understand pieces. I don't know why I understand pieces." Her voice was steady but her hands were not; they moved against each other, thumb worrying thumb, a carver's habit of worrying material. "I'm told you might. Understand more than pieces, I mean. About what's happening to her. To us."

"Who told you?"

"The dead," Juniper said, without flinching, and Maren felt something in her chest turn over — not fear exactly, or not only fear. Recognition. In Velland, before, there had been people like this. Listeners. Bone-speakers. They had sat in the churchyards on feast days and let the ancestors complain about the weather and the young people and the government of heaven, and everyone had found it comforting rather than dreadful, the way you find a familiar voice comforting even when it grumbles. Maren had been seven the last time she'd sat at a listener's knee, and she could still feel the wool of the old man's coat, and the smell of the bones he wore strung at his wrist, and the sound of her great-grandmother's voice coming out of the air beside her ear, thin as thread, saying *mind the hem of your dress, little bird, you've dragged it through the mud again.*

Forty years of silence, and this woman walks in carrying the whole drowned choir in her fingertips.

"There is an oath," Maren said. "You should know that first, before anything else. All of us who came out — the ones who were adults, who understood what was being asked — we swore it. Not to each other. To *it*. To the Veil. We swore we would not speak the country's name, nor teach the tongue, nor write it, nor sing it, nor tell the children where they came from. And the Veil holds us to it. You've seen what happens to those who break it."

"Tomas," Juniper said softly. "And others. I've been reading the gaps in the city's records. People who existed and then didn't. Rooms that were always empty. Widows who were never married."

Maren laughed, and it came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "You get used to grieving people no one else remembers. That's the first thing they don't tell you about surviving an erasure. The second thing is that you stop being sure you remember them either. Memory wants witnesses. Take the witnesses away and it starts eating itself." She looked at Wren. The child was watching her with that unblinking patience that Maren remembered from the goats of her grandmother's village — an animal patience, older than speech. "But her. She wasn't sworn. She was born inside. Whatever holds us doesn't hold her, or holds her differently. When she talks, the grey ones come sniffing, yes?"

"They've followed us since the border roads," Juniper said. "Faceless men. They walk at night. They—" She stopped, and Maren watched her decide how much truth to spend. "They unmake things. Spoken memories. There's a village back along our road where half the innkeeper's childhood is just... gone. Smoothed over. He laughs about it. He thinks he was a happy child."

"That's how it works," Maren said. "It doesn't take the whole cloth. It takes the threads, one at a time, until the pattern goes and you're holding a rag and calling it a blanket." She rose, slowly, and went to the chest at the foot of her bed. Her hands shook — age, mostly, though she told herself some of it was choice. What she was about to do was the largest thing she had done in forty years, larger than crossing the mountains with nothing, larger than burying her sister's body in foreign ground because no cemetery would take a grave with no country written on it.

The oath mattered. She had built her whole long survival on the oath, the way a spider builds on a single anchor-thread. But an oath, she thought, kneeling painfully before the chest, was a promise made to keep something safe. And the thing the oath kept safe was not her. It was not even the dead. It was the silence itself — and the silence had grown fat and hungry and had begun to eat children.

An oath that served the hunger instead of the people was just a leash with the collar on the wrong neck.

"Maren," Juniper said behind her. "What are you doing?"

"What I should have done thirty-nine years ago," Maren said. "Being useful."

* * *

The map was not paper. Paper burned, paper rotted, paper got confiscated in the Reckoning year when the soldiers went house to house with their brass instruments that hummed near any written word of home. The map was in her body. It had always been in her body; that was the mercy and the cruelty of the Veil's method — it could burn the charts and smash the survey stones and melt the boundary markers, but it could not easily reach the inside of a stubborn old woman's skull, not without taking the whole woman, and in the early years it had wanted witnesses alive. Witnesses to nothing. Living proof that there had never been anything to prove.

She had walked the whole country twice before the fall, as a young woman, collecting rents for her father and courting a boy in the river towns whose name she had paid away piece by piece over the decades just to keep the dangerous parts intact. She had walked it, and she had sewn, and so she had done the only sensible thing a frightened woman could do in the last autumn before the end: she had taken a length of good linen and begun to stitch.

It had taken her eleven nights, working by moonlight because candlelight showed in windows. Eleven nights, and then she had unpicked it all and re-sewn it inside the lining of a winter coat, wrong side out, every village reduced to a knot, every river to a running seam of blue-grey thread, the mountains a ridge of padded stitching, the capital — the capital a small circle of gold thread at the center, worked and reworked until the gold was nearly black with handling.

Then she had traded the coat to a refugee column heading south, because wearing it was death and losing it was only grief, and she had spent the next forty years remembering every stitch anyway, because that was the joke of it, the filthy joke: the Veil could take the object but the knowledge lived in her hands. Her fingers remembered the tension of the thread. Her shoulders remembered hunching over the work. Some nights she woke with her hands moving on the blanket, sewing seams that weren't there, laying out rivers on the quilt, and she would lie in the dark and feel the whole country pass beneath her fingertips like a landscape seen from a high pass.

Now she took down the quilt from her bed — the patched one, ugly, mended a hundred times — and spread it on the floor between them, and knelt over it, and began to talk.

"Here." Her finger pressed a worn patch, a square of different cloth let in after a burn. "This is the valley of the Aldwater. The river comes down from the passes here, runs east, and there were nine bridges on it, and the ninth was the ferry bridge, and the ferryman's name was Osk and he charged a toll nobody ever named aloud because naming it made you pay double." She glanced up at Juniper. "You've met the toll."

Juniper's face changed. "My mother's face," she said, barely above a whisper. "I paid with my mother's face."

Maren closed her eyes briefly. *There it is,* she thought. *The price, already being collected. It starts small. It always starts small.* "Then you know the country isn't only behind the river," she said. "It's in you now. It took payment. That means it considers you entered." She returned to the quilt. "Here, the mill towns. Here, the high pasture where the sheep were so fat the children used them for pillows. Here—" her finger found a patch shaped like a broken heart, mended with red thread, "—the shrine of the Unburied. You'll want that. Whatever you're carrying that lets you hear them, the dead gather thickest there, and they'll tell you the rest of what I can't."

"And the capital?" Juniper asked.

Maren's finger moved to the center of the quilt, to a place where the original fabric had worn through entirely and been backed with leather, dark and smooth as a scar. "Under it," she said. "Whatever you're looking for. The Door Out. The old stories all agree it stands beneath the palace hill, in the undercroft where the founders were first buried, before the palace was built on top of them. My grandmother swept that undercroft for sixty years and swore on her deathbed that the far wall was warm." She heard her own voice go strange. "I haven't thought of my grandmother in twenty years. Do you understand? I *chose* not to, because choosing not to was safer than having her taken. This is what the oath costs, Ravensworth. Not the big things. The grandmothers. The small warm things, surrendered one per year, like tribute."

Wren slid off her stool and came and knelt at the edge of the quilt. She put one small finger on the leather patch, dead center, and said something in Vellish — three words, soft, certain.

Maren's breath left her.

"What did she say?" Juniper asked.

"She said," Maren managed, "*the door remembers me.*" She looked at the child, really looked, and saw what she had been refusing to see since the doorway: not just a refugee, not just a last speaker. A key that had learned to walk. "Child. Who sent you out? Who put you through the crack?"

Wren considered her for a long moment. Then she spoke again, slowly, the way one speaks to the very old or the very foolish, and Maren translated in a voice gone thin: "Everyone pushed. But the door chose. The door chooses one each time it opens. Once a generation, it opens — a crack, a breath, no wider than a cat. Last time it opened, my mother was a girl, and it chose no one, and it closed, and everyone wept. This time it chose me." The child's chin lifted. "It will not open again in my lifetime after this. The generation turns. Soon — the stories say the door opens at the turning of the season when the veil is thinnest, and the season is close. Weeks. Maybe less. After that, whoever is inside stays inside forever, and whoever is outside forgets outside forever."

The room was very quiet. Somewhere below, a neighbor was frying onions, and the ordinary smell of it rose through the floorboards like an accusation — the world going on, dinner getting made, while underneath everything a country waited to be either born again or buried for good.

"Soon," Juniper repeated. Her carver's hands had gone still at last, flat against her thighs, the way hands go still before they pick up a blade.

"Soon," Maren agreed. "And the grey ones will be thickest between here and the river, because the Veil knows the season too. It's not stupid, whatever else it is. It's a shepherd. It intends to bring its flock all the way home to nothing." She began folding the quilt, then stopped herself, and instead rolled it loosely and tied it with the cord from her waist. "Take it. The patches are the map. I'll come with you as far as my legs allow and tell you what each stitch means, because thread alone won't save you — you need the names, and the names are the one thing I can still give freely, because—" her voice caught, steadied, "—because I have decided, at the end of a long life of cowardice, that the oath was a leash and I am done wearing it. If the Veil comes to unmake me for it, let it. I am seventy-one years old and I have been unmade slowly for forty years anyway. Better all at once, with the word in my mouth."

"You don't have to—" Juniper started.

"I do, though." Maren smiled, and it was an old smile, worn smooth, but real. "That's the secret nobody tells you about oaths, girl. Keeping one can be the bravest thing you ever do. Breaking it, at the right moment, for the right reason — that can be braver still." She picked up her stick, and her good cloak, and looked around the room that had held her captivity so long it had almost fooled her into calling it a life. On the sill, the turned-away saint watched the wall.

At the door, she paused. Below in the street, the lamplighter was making his rounds, and his flame bloomed orange against the dusk — and for a moment, in the space between two lamps, the dusk seemed to move. Seemed to stand. Grey as wet slate, patient as arithmetic, at the corner opposite, where it had no errand and no neighbor and no reason in the world to be waiting.

Maren's hand found Wren's shoulder, and the child's small bones felt like kindling under her palm.

"Back door," she murmured. "Now, and quietly. It seems the Veil has heard I've decided to be brave — and it has decided to be punctual."
