## 16. Hunters of the Veil

Wren counted her teeth with her tongue before she opened her eyes, because that was how she knew she was still herself. Twenty small ones, all in their places, none of them gone soft like chalk. The night before, in a dream she could not hold on to, something had been counting them too.

The morning was grey in a way mornings were not supposed to be. Not cloud-grey, not rain-grey, but a flat pressed colour, as if someone had laid a heavy stone over the sky and left it there. Juniper was already up, folding their blankets into the shape of nothing so no one would know anyone had slept by the milestone. She did everything in shapes of nothing now. Wren had learned to watch for it: the way Juniper brushed out their tracks with a branch, the way she never said Wren's name above a whisper, the way she looked at the road behind them more often than the road ahead.

"Up," Juniper said softly. "We walk while the light is bad."

Wren liked the light being bad. Bad light meant fewer eyes. She pulled on her boots — Juniper's boots, really, stuffed at the toes with wool — and ate the heel of bread Juniper handed her without tasting it. Tasting took attention, and attention belonged to listening.

Because they were being followed. Wren had known since two days ago, since the village where the innkeeper's wife had smiled and smiled and could not say what lay east of her own fields. Since the well with no name carved on its stone. Something walked behind them the way cold walks behind you through a door left open. It did not make footstep sounds. It made *absence* sounds — the space where a bird should have been singing, the hole in the wind where a leaf should have turned over.

She had not told Juniper yet. She was trying to find the words in this language, which kept sliding off things like water off a greased pan. In Vellish there was a word for what followed them. In this tongue there was only *nothing*, and *nothing* was too big and too small at once.

They walked. The road went down through country that kept forgetting itself. A fence ended in the middle of a field, going nowhere, having forgotten where it was going. An orchard stood full of trees that had once been pruned by careful hands and now grew wild, because no one remembered owning them. Wren touched one trunk as she passed and felt, faint and far away, the ghost of a ladder against bark, a basket, a woman humming. The tree remembered. Only the people had let go.

"Juniper," she said, when the silence between them had grown long enough to hold a secret. "There are men behind us."

Juniper stopped so completely that it was like watching a clock decide not to be a clock anymore. "How many?"

"I don't know. They don't come in numbers. They come in—" Wren searched. Her hands helped her; they always did. She held up her fingers and then closed them slowly, one by one, until her fist was empty. "Like that. Like counting backwards until there isn't anything."

Juniper's face did the thing it did instead of fear, which was to become very neat and very still, like a ledger closing. "Grey?" she asked. "Tall? Faces like—"

"Like smooth stone," Wren said. "Like an egg nobody wrote a face on." She had seen them only in the edges of dreams, but she knew them the way you know a scar. "The wardens. The ones who came after the fire. They unmake what is spoken. If they hear a memory spoken aloud, they can eat it out of the air, and then out of the person who spoke it."

Juniper looked at her for a long moment. "You've seen them eat a memory."

Wren nodded. She thought of the baker's wife, whose name had been Sefa, who had run into the street during the burning shouting her daughter's name over and over so the girl could find her in the smoke — and how one of the tall grey shapes had bent toward the sound, and how the name had stopped mid-word, and how the baker's wife had stood afterward with her mouth open, calling nothing, calling the shape of nothing, not knowing why her arms were lifted. Wren had been four. She had hidden under a cart. Some things you do not remember so much as you keep them, whole and cold, like a stone in the belly.

"They follow me," Wren said. "I'm the loudest thing left. I'm the last one born inside. Everything I am is made of the country they erased. I'm like—" she frowned, hunting the picture, "—like blood in water. Things that hunt come from far away for blood."

Juniper crouched down to her level, which she did sometimes, though it put her face close enough that Wren could see how tired she was underneath. "Then we get quieter," Juniper said. "No names spoken. No stories told aloud on the road. If you need to tell me something, you carve it, or you write it, or you show me. Can you do that?"

"Yes," Wren said. Then, because it mattered and because lying to Juniper felt like breaking a bone of her own: "But quiet doesn't stop them. Quiet only slows them. They don't follow my voice, Juniper. They follow my *being*. I'm a hole in their erasure. Holes pull."

Something moved behind Juniper's eyes — Wren saw it, the quick arithmetic of a woman who kept accounts even in her heart — and then Juniper nodded once, briskly, and stood, and shouldered the pack.

"Then we stop being a hole," she said. "We become a crack. Cracks are harder to fill."

Wren didn't know what that meant, but she liked the sound of it, the way she liked the sound of most of the strange strong things Juniper said.

* * *

They left the road at noon, cutting across a fallow field toward a line of hills, and it was there, among the stubble, that Wren found the first proof.

It was a milestone. Or it had been. It lay broken in the grass, and on its flat face someone had once cut words, and the words had been scraped away — not weathered, not worn, but *scraped*, deliberately, deeply, by something that wanted the stone blank. And around the scraping, in the grass, in a wide ring, everything was grey. Not dead. Grey. The grass stood upright and healthy and utterly without colour, like grass in a drawing of grass made by someone who had heard of green but never seen it.

Wren stood at the edge of the ring and felt it through her boot soles: a hollowness, a taken-ness. This was where they had stopped once. Where they had stood and listened and found nothing to listen to, and moved on.

"Juniper," she whispered, and pointed.

Juniper knelt at the ring's edge and did not touch it. Good, Wren thought. Don't touch. Touching teaches it your shape. That was something the dead had taught her, back in the sunken cathedral, back when they crowded around her like moths around the only lit window in a dark country. The dead knew the wardens. The dead feared them worse than they feared forgetting, because the wardens could take even the memory of the dead, and then there was truly nothing left of a person at all.

"It's recent," Juniper murmured. "Days. Maybe less." She looked up at the hills, then back along the way they'd come, and Wren watched her do the thing she did — turn fear into figures, terror into entries. "They're not chasing us. Chasing implies urgency. They're *herding*. Cutting angles. Making sure we stay on roads they choose." Her jaw tightened. "Someone is directing them. Wardens don't think for themselves. Someone with maps and money wants us funneled somewhere."

Wren thought of the guildhall, of Master Corvane's office with its drawers full of labels, of his voice like a scale weighing everything. She didn't say his name. Names spoken aloud were food now. But she saw Juniper thinking the same shape of thought, and they understood each other, which was a rare and precious thing, like finding a coin in old bread.

They went up into the hills, and the hills were kind to them, full of gorse and shadow and the small honest noises of things that lived without knowing about countries or veils. Wren began, quietly, to talk to the dead.

Not aloud. Never aloud anymore. But she carried three of Juniper's carved bones in a pouch at her neck — a knucklebone, a bird's wishbone, and one small rib polished smooth — and if she held them and thought hard, the dead could lean close, the way neighbours lean over a fence. The dead of Velland were everywhere out here, thin as breath on glass, and they were frightened too. *They are coming behind you*, the dead told her, in the wordless way the dead spoke, meaning poured straight into the marrow. *Three. No — the third is two folded together. They walk the road you abandoned. They have your smell.*

*What is our smell?* Wren asked.

*Memory*, said the dead. *You smell like remembering. To them that is the smell of meat.*

Wren walked faster after that.

* * *

Near dusk they came down into a hollow where a shepherd's hut leaned against the hillside like a tired animal, and Juniper decided — with the particular stubbornness Wren had begun to recognize as love wearing work-clothes — that they would sleep under a roof for one night, because Wren's cough was back and the child needed warmth or she'd be no use to anyone, least of all herself.

The hut was empty. The hearth was cold but the chimney drew, and there was dry peat stacked by the wall, and a straw pallet that smelled of sheep and years. Juniper got a fire going small and shielded, heated water, made Wren drink it with some bitter leaf steeped in it that tasted the way medicine ought to taste, which is terrible. Then she sat across the fire from Wren with a bone in her lap and her knife, carving, because carving was how Juniper prayed, though she'd probably have denied it if asked.

Wren watched the knife move and felt the dead gather, gently, around the little fire's warmth. They liked Juniper's carving. It gave them somewhere to sit, the way a bench gives a traveller somewhere to rest.

"Tell me about the fire," Juniper said quietly. "Only if you want. We won't speak of it again after tonight. But I think I need to know what we're walking toward, and you're the only map of it that exists."

Wren wrapped her hands around her cup. The heat hurt and helped.

"It was night," she said. "The Veil comes at night. It comes like fog but it falls like a lid." She stared into the flames and let the remembering rise, which was dangerous, which was exactly what the wardens fed on, but Juniper had asked, and trust was also a thing you spent. "First the bells stopped. Not silenced — *stopped*, in the middle of ringing, like a hand laid over a mouth. Then the streets started going wrong. You'd look down a lane you'd walked every day of your life and it would be... shorter. Ending sooner than it should. And the ends weren't walls. They were just *not*. Your eyes slid off them."

Juniper's knife had gone still.

"People shouted names," Wren went on, and her voice went small and even, the way it did when she carried something heavy so it wouldn't spill. "That was the worst part. Everyone shouting everyone's names, mothers and fathers, because someone had learned — the elders knew, they'd always known the rules — that a name spoken might hold a piece of you outside the Veil. So the whole city was one great shout of names, thousands of names, all at once, all the time, louder than the fire. And the wardens walked through it eating. You'd hear a name cut off, just — cut, like thread — and then a person would stand still, holding a hand toward someone they couldn't see anymore, asking who they were reaching for."

She drank. The bitter leaf had gone cold.

"My mother pushed me into a crack," Wren said. "There was a crack in the Veil — a seam, near the eastern gate, where the old boundary-stone had split years ago and nobody mended it because the stone was older than the city and everyone was a little afraid of it. She pushed me through and she said—" and here Wren stopped, because the words were Vellish, and the words were the last thing her mother ever gave her, and she had never said them aloud since the fire, and saying them here, in this hut, in this country, felt like opening a door she could not close.

Juniper waited. Juniper was good at waiting. It was one of her truest crafts.

"*Run until the world has a name again,*" Wren said, in Vellish, in her mother's cadence, in a six-year-old's broken grammar that had never gotten any better because there had been no one to correct it. "*And when it does, find the ledger-keepers. They will know what you are.*"

The fire popped. Somewhere far off, a fox screamed once and fell silent.

"And then the seam closed," Wren said, "and I was on the wrong side of everything, and I walked, and I walked, and people kept looking at me strangely when I spoke, and eventually I learned to be quiet, and then I found the guild door, and I knocked." She looked up at Juniper across the fire. "You opened it. Nobody else would. You opened it because you heard grief in the words instead of nonsense. I've decided that's the most important thing that ever happened to me. I've decided it even before whatever happens next."

Juniper's eyes were bright and wet in the firelight, and she did not wipe them, because Juniper believed feelings should be entered in the ledger honestly rather than hidden. "Your mother," she said. "Do you remember her name?"

"No," Wren said, and the word fell out of her like a dropped stone. "That's the trick of it. That's the cruelty of it. I remember her *hands*, her voice, the way she smelled of bread and lamp-oil, everything except the name. Because the name was the last thing she spoke, and the wardens were already close, and I think—" her breath hitched, "—I think they caught it as it crossed the seam. I think they ate my mother's name out of the air between us, and that's why I can't find it. It's not lost. It's *taken*. There's a difference. Lost things can be found."

The dead stirred around the fire, and Wren felt their attention sharpen to a point, and one of them — an old man, a teacher once, she thought, from the feel of him, ink-and-chalk — leaned close to her marrow and said: *Ask the bone-carver. Names live in bones longer than they live in air.*

Wren looked at Juniper's hands, scarred and sure, turning the half-carved bone in the firelight.

"Juniper," she said. "When we get to the Door Out — when we get to wherever we're going — could you try? Could you carve for my mother? Not to call her back. Just to write her down. Even if we never learn the name. Even if the bone stays blank. I want somebody to have tried."

Juniper reached across the fire and took Wren's hand, and her grip was warm and rough and certain. "I'll carve every hour of every day if that's what it takes," she said. "I'll wear my fingers to the wrist. That's a promise, and I don't make those lightly — ask anyone I owe money to."

Wren laughed, a real laugh, short and surprised out of her, and for a moment the hut was warm all the way through.

* * *

She woke because the fire had died and the silence had changed its weight.

That was the thing about the wardens: they did not make noise. They made the absence of noise, and if you had grown up inside the Veil, you had learned to hear absences the way sailors hear weather. The silence outside the hut was not sleeping-silence. It was held silence. Listening silence. Silence with intent.

Wren sat up without moving the straw. Through the gap under the door, where moonlight should have lain silver on the ground, there was a band of utter grey. Not dark. Grey. The colour of the scraped milestone. The colour of the ring in the grass.

Three shapes stood in the yard. Tall, still, patient as posts. Their faces were smooth as eggs, as unwritten stone, and where faces should have been there was only the faint suggestion of features, like a memory of a face fading — because that was what they wore, Wren realized, with a cold that started in her teeth and spread downward. They wore the *idea* of a face, worn nearly through. They had been people once. Perhaps. Long ago. People who had given everything to the Veil until the Veil wore them like gloves.

One of them raised its head. Slowly. Toward the hut. Toward her.

It could not come in. She didn't know how she knew, but she knew — thresholds meant something, even to them, maybe especially to them; a threshold was a kind of name, and names were the one thing they could not carry. But it stood there, and the other two stood there, and the grey of them pressed against the door like deep water against a hull, and Wren felt her own edges beginning to blur, her memories going soft at the borders, her mother's hands, her mother's voice, the smell of bread and lamp-oil — *hold on, hold on* — she grabbed the carved bones at her neck and squeezed until the edges bit her palm, and the pain anchored her, and the dead crowded in around her like a wall of thin cold arms, and the pressure eased.

In the pallet beside her, Juniper slept on, exhausted past waking, her knife still loose in her hand even in sleep, a habit Wren found both frightening and comforting.

Wren did not wake her. There was nothing Juniper could do against three wardens except die memorably, and Wren had already lost everyone she loved once. She would not spend this one.

So she did the only thing she could do, the thing she was made for, the thing that had carried her out of a burning country through a crack in the end of the world: she stayed awake, and she held her memories tight, and she watched the grey shapes through the gap under the door, hour after hour, while they stood in the yard like sentries at a gate that would never open.

And near dawn, when the grey began to thin, the tallest warden did something she had never seen one do before.

It bent down, slowly, joints moving like a puppet remembering strings, and it placed something on the doorstep. Then all three turned — there was no hurry in them, no anger, only the terrible patience of arithmetic — and walked away into the paling mist, and the birds came back to the yard one cautious note at a time, and the world resumed its colours like a man resuming a conversation.

Wren waited until the light was honest. Then she woke Juniper, and together they opened the door.

On the threshold lay a single sheet of thick grey paper, dry despite the dew. On it, in writing that looked less written than *pressed* — as if the letters had been stamped through from some other side of the world — were words in trade-tongue, formal as a contract:

*THE GUILD ACKNOWLEDGES RECEIPT OF ONE (1) RESONANT ASSET, LAST-BORN. DELIVERY TO BE MADE AT THE RIVER CROSSING BY THE FULL MOON. RESISTANCE WILL BE ENTERED AS A LOSS AND DEDUCTED ACCORDINGLY.*

Below it, in smaller characters, almost gentle:

*Master Corvane extends his compliments.*

Juniper read it twice. Her face went through several weathers. When she looked up at Wren, her voice, when it came, was very quiet and very steady, the way it got right before she did something impossible.

"The river crossing," she said. "That's the border. That's the edge of the old country." She crumpled the paper in her fist, then — because she was Juniper, because she was who she was — smoothed it out again and folded it away into her coat. Evidence. Every entry goes in the ledger, even the threats. *Especially* the threats.

"They're herding us to the river," Wren whispered. "Just like you said."

"Yes," Juniper said, and slung the pack onto her shoulder, and looked east toward the hills and the country beyond them that no map admitted to. "Then we'd best arrive before they expect us. Nothing annoys a ledger like an entry made early."

They set off down the hill at a pace just short of running, and behind them the yard kept its new silence, and ahead, somewhere past the hills, a river waited that marked the edge of everything — and Wren, trotting at Juniper's side, felt the dead stir along her bones like a wind rising, all of them whispering the same wordless word, over and over, the way a crowd whispers before a door opens:

*Home. Home. Home.*
