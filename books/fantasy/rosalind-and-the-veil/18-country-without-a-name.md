## 18. Country Without a Name

The river was behind them by morning, and Juniper did not look back at it.

She had paid too much to that water already — her mother's face, gone down its throat like a coin into a well — and she could not bear the thought of what else it might ask for if she gave it the courtesy of a glance. So she kept her eyes forward, on Wren's small back and the grey road unwinding ahead of them, and she told herself that grief deferred was not grief denied. She would enter it in the ledger later. She entered everything in the ledger eventually. It was how she kept from drowning.

But the road itself began to change before they had walked an hour, and after that there was no room left in her for anything but attention.

It started with the grass.

Juniper knew grass the way she knew bone — by species, by season, by the sound it made underfoot. The meadows of the capital were fescue and rye, tame things, planted and replanted for centuries. What grew around their boots now was none of that. It came up silver at the root and green at the tip, and when the wind moved through it, it moved *wrong* — all together, in one long breath, as if the whole field were a single animal turning over in its sleep. And under the wind-sound there was another sound, fainter, that Juniper might have called imagination if her life had not spent the last month dismantling that word: a low, layered murmur, like a congregation heard through a cathedral wall.

Wren stopped walking. Her bare feet — Juniper had bought her shoes three villages ago and the child abandoned them every morning within sight of open country — pressed into the silver-rooted grass, and the murmur rose to meet her like water rising around a stone.

"What is it saying?" Juniper asked, though she already half-knew, because the words were arriving in her own skull now, uninvited, in a language she had never been taught and somehow carried anyway, the way you carry your mother's lullabies without remembering learning them.

Wren looked up at her. In the flat white light of this sky — and the sky here was wrong too, paler than it should be, like cloth washed too many times — the girl's eyes seemed almost black.

"It's saying my name," Wren said. "All of it. The long part."

Juniper crouched beside her. "You have a long name?"

"Everyone does." Wren said it patiently, the way she said most things, as if reporting on weather. "Mine is—" and then she made a sound that was not a word so much as a small architecture of sound, vowels stacked like stones, and the field *answered*. The grass bowed outward from where they stood in a slow ring, silver tips trembling, and beneath the wind the murmur swelled into something that was unmistakably, terribly, *delight*.

Juniper's hands shook. She put them in her pockets like a woman hiding evidence.

They walked on. The road was no longer a road, exactly — it had been one once, she could see the old paving stones surfacing and sinking beneath the turf like whales rolling under sea-ice — but the land remembered where it went, and their feet followed the memory without being asked. Twice they passed standing stones carved with script that Juniper's eyes slid off of until Wren touched them, and then the letters stood up sharp and legible and *known*, and Juniper read them aloud before she could stop herself, and each time she did, something in the ground under her boots exhaled.

By midday they reached the first ruin, and that was when Juniper understood, fully and finally, what kind of country they had entered.

* * *

It had been a farmhouse. She could see the shape of it even in ruin — the low stone walls, the stub of a chimney, the foundation of a barn beyond — and it had been burned; the stones wore a black crust that forty years had not softened. Nothing grew inside the walls. The silver-grass stopped at the threshold as if at a line drawn in salt.

Wren walked straight in, the way children walk into places adults circle, and stood in the middle of the fallen roof-timbers, and said something in Vellish.

Juniper heard it as a sentence of perhaps six words, soft, almost conversational. And the house *stood up*.

Not rebuilt — she would swear to this later, in ledgers and in court if it came to that, she would swear the house was never *rebuilt* — but remembered. The walls filled themselves the way a dried sponge fills with water, timber rising out of timber, thatch knitting over the rafters in a rush of gold-brown, the chimney drawing itself tall against the pale sky. A door appeared in a doorway. Smoke-smell bloomed faintly from the hearth, woodsmoke and baking bread and something herbal underneath, and a window lit with a warm interior glow that had no fire behind it. For three heartbeats the farmstead stood complete and inhabited-feeling, curtains stirring, a bucket hanging by the well-chain —

— and then it let go. The thatch sighed back into absence, the walls drained away, and the burned ruin sat in its ring of dead ground exactly as before, except that now the black crust on the stones looked less like scorch-marks and more like a bandage over a wound that wanted to reopen.

Wren turned around. Her face was wet.

"That was Hesta's house," she said. "Hesta made bread with plums in it. I can't remember Hesta's face but I remember the plums." She wiped her nose with her sleeve. "I only said good afternoon. I didn't know it would listen."

Juniper knelt in the grass outside the threshold and pulled the girl against her shoulder and held on while the small body shook. Over Wren's head she looked at the resurrected-and-vanished house, and the ache that opened in her chest was one she had no honest name for, because it was homesickness, pure and physical, the dull sweet pain of missing a place —

— and she had never been here. She had never seen this country. It had been erased eleven years before Juniper was born. Whatever she was grieving, she was grieving secondhand, inherited, a debt of feeling passed down the blood like the bone-gift itself. Her grandmother had walked out of these fields carrying whatever Juniper was carrying now, and had apparently carried it in silence for sixty years, and Juniper thought: *what did that cost her? What does silence cost, per year, compounded?*

She was a ledger-keeper. She caught herself doing the arithmetic before she could stop.

* * *

They made camp that night in the lee of a hill that answered, Wren said, to a name meaning *the shepherd's tooth*, and Juniper built the fire while the girl wandered the near slope touching things — fence posts, a milestone, the trunk of an apple tree grown wild and crooked — and wherever she touched and spoke, the world brightened by one degree. The apple tree blossomed. Out of season, out of nowhere, white petals shaking loose into the dusk. The milestone's worn face sharpened until Juniper could read the distances carved there, and the distances were given in leagues to towns whose names rang in her chest like struck bells: *Serevan. Ostmere. The White Terraces.* Towns that existed on no map in any atlas she had ever balanced a ledger upon.

She wrote them down anyway. That was becoming her whole vocation, she realized, watching the firelight move on the page: she was writing down a country while it still had the strength to be written.

The dead came with the dark.

She had felt them since the river — a pressure at the edge of hearing, a crowd waiting politely behind a door — but here, past the border that was not a border, they did not wait. They gathered the way fog gathers in a hollow, thickening between the trees, pooling in the ruins along the slope, and where they were thickest the firelight bent toward them as if curious. Juniper could not see faces. She saw suggestion: the shape of a shawl, the set of shoulders, the outline of a man leaning on a staff he no longer needed. Hundreds of them. Perhaps thousands. All facing inward, all facing her and the child, all silent in a way that was louder than any shouting she had ever heard.

"They're not going to hurt us," Wren said. She had come back to the fire and sat close against Juniper's side, and she spoke around a mouthful of the last of their bread, entirely unbothered. "They're waiting for you to carve."

"I know." Juniper's voice came out rougher than she intended. "I can feel it. Like standing in a shop while everyone watches you count the till."

"You're not counting," Wren said, with the terrible precision of children. "You're deciding who goes first. They know that. They're being polite about it."

Juniper looked at the fog-shapes ringing the firelight and felt the truth of it settle over her like snowfall. They were patient. They had been patient for forty years. But patience is not the same as peace, and every one of those assembled silences was a name that had gone unspoken, unrecorded, unmourned in any register anywhere in the world — because the world had agreed, all at once, in the year of the Reckoning, that these people had never existed, and agreement is the strongest magic there is.

She took out her knife and the length of antler she had been saving, and the crowd of the dead leaned in as one, and the firelight bent further, and somewhere in the massed grey a woman began, very softly, to hum a tune that Juniper's grandmother used to hum while kneading dough.

Juniper did not cry. She was proud of that, later. She carved instead.

* * *

The first name that came through her hands was a farmer's — *Aldric Voss*, no relation, or perhaps relation, here who could say — and as she cut the letters the humming stopped and a presence moved close enough that the fire dimmed, and she felt the name leave the bone and arrive somewhere, received, witnessed, *kept*. The relief that rolled off the assembled dead was nearly physical, a warmth at her back like sun through glass.

The second name belonged to a girl of nine. The third to a man who had been a ferryman, and at the feel of him Juniper flinched, thinking of the river and its toll, and carved his name twice as carefully to make amends to some ferryman somewhere for what his trade had cost her.

She worked until her fingers ached and the moon crossed the shepherd's tooth and Wren fell asleep against her ribs. And in the smallest hours, with the child breathing slow and the dead standing their silent watch, Juniper set down the knife and spoke aloud, testing, into the listening dark:

"Serevan."

The word left her mouth and the night *took hold of it*. Every fog-shape turned. The ruined farmhouse on the far slope flickered — one heartbeat whole, roof and hearth and plum-bread smell — and the apple tree rained blossom, and the very ground under her seemed to draw a breath of recognition, of *homecoming*, as if the country itself had been lying in a dark room for forty years and someone had finally spoken its own name back to it.

And then, from everywhere and nowhere, the dead answered. Not in horror. Not in demand. In chorus — hundreds of voices layered like the voices under Wren's speech, saying the word after her, tenderly, correcting her accent by a hair, *giving it back to her more true than she'd found it*, and the sound was so full of love and loss braided together that Juniper pressed both hands over her mouth to keep from joining it, because she suspected that if she started she would never stop, that she would stand in this field naming towns until dawn and then until she died, calling home a home that had never held her.

*This,* she thought, *is what she sold.*

Her grandmother had stood in a room with men who owned the Veil, and they had offered her a price, and the price had been this — this ache, this chorus, this whole country's right to be spoken — and Rosalind Ravensworth had taken the money and the lives of her children and walked west, and had never once, in sixty years, taught her granddaughter so much as a single word of the language that lived in her bones anyway.

Because it couldn't be taught. Because it *was* the inheritance, and teaching it would be confessing it, and confession broke oaths enforced by the Veil itself.

Juniper looked down at Wren, asleep, trusting, born inside the erasure and sent out through a crack in it like a seed through floorboards. The last speaker. The last child of Serevan and Ostmere and the White Terraces, sleeping in the cold with her bare feet curled up, guarded by a stranger who was starting to understand that she would burn down the Guild of Impossible Things, the capital, and the Veil itself before she let anyone make an inventory entry of this girl.

The dead were still watching. Patiently. Politely.

But as Juniper banked the fire and settled Wren against her coat, she noticed that the crowd had changed its arrangement. All night they had faced inward, toward the fire, toward her. Now, in the deep hours, they had turned — every grey suggestion of a shoulder, every outlined head — to face east, deeper into the vanished country, toward the pale horizon where the erased capital must lie.

Toward whatever it was they wanted done before the Veil finished its work.

And at the very front of them, closest to the fire, closest to her, stood a figure Juniper could almost see clearly: a woman in a shawl, humming a dough-song, wearing the posture Juniper had watched across a kitchen table for the whole of her childhood.

Her grandmother's ghost had come home. And it was looking east, and waiting, and it did not turn around.
