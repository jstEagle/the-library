## 19. The Choir of the Unburied

The cathedral had sunk.

Not fallen — Juniper had seen fallen buildings on the road through the nameless country, and they wore their ruin honestly, walls slumped like tired men, roofs open to a sky they no longer trusted. This was different. The whole structure had settled into the earth the way a body settles into a grave: slowly, deliberately, with its hands folded. Only the crown of it remained above ground — a ring of broken pinnacles like fingers reaching up from a burial mound — and the rest descended in terraces into a hollow that the locals (the few who would look at them at all) called simply the Downtaking. No one dug there. No one grazed animals near it. The grass around its rim grew green and thick and utterly silent, as though sound itself had been harvested from the field and carried below.

Wren had felt it before Juniper saw it. The child had gone still on the road at midday, head tilted like a bird's, and said one word in Vellish — *down* — and then walked toward the hollow with the certainty of someone returning to a house she'd been told burned down.

Juniper followed because there was nowhere else to follow. That had become, somewhere over the last hundred miles, the shape of her life: Wren walking, Juniper following, the dead pressing close enough behind to feel like weather.

She heard them before she reached the first terrace.

It began as a pressure in her ears, the way a storm announces itself in the teeth. Then it resolved — not into words, not yet, but into attention. Hundreds of minds turning toward her all at once, the way a congregation turns toward the door when it opens late. She had carried the dead with her for years; she had carved their names into bone for coin and kindness alike, and she had always thought of them as guests, quiet ones, seated politely at the edges of rooms. This was not politeness. This was a hunger so old and so disciplined that it had learned to wait.

"Juniper." Wren stood on the second terrace, beside a door half-swallowed by earth. Her voice was very small against the vastness below. "They know you're here."

"I know," Juniper said. "I can hear them."

"No." The girl shook her head slowly, and her hand found Juniper's sleeve — the first time she had reached for comfort rather than given it. "I mean they *know you*. They've been waiting for a carver. They say—" she listened to something Juniper could not yet hear, or could not yet bear to hear— "they say the last one died before I was born."

The stair down was stone, worn to a shallow bowl by feet that had stopped existing forty years ago. Juniper counted the terraces as they descended: seven of them, each narrower than the last, each lined with the shapes of things buried upright. Pews. Pillars. A baptismal font cradling a small garden of pale mushrooms that glowed faintly, giving the dark a color like old milk. And everywhere, worked into every surface, carving — Vellish letters in spirals and columns, saints' names and shopkeepers' names and the names of ships, cut deep by hands that had believed stone would outlast forgetting.

The Veil had tried to erase the names. Juniper could see where it had worked: grooves smoothed shallow, edges softened, some inscriptions reduced to blank bands of stone like scars where text had once lived. But the cathedral had sunk beneath the Veil's reach, drowned in its own foundations, and what sank deep enough had kept its letters the way a drowned man keeps his last breath.

At the bottom lay the nave.

It was flooded — not with water but with something thicker and slower, a grey light that pooled ankle-deep and moved like syrup. Juniper's boots broke its surface and sent slow ripples outward, and with each ripple the dead came closer.

They did not look the way she had expected. In the bone-yards of the capital, in the guildhall's back rooms, the dead had always been presences, pressures, voices without faces — grief given grammar. Here they had shapes. Faint ones, grey-on-grey, standing among the pews in the posture of people who had never left: a woman with her sleeves rolled as if mid-wash; a boy holding something invisible, perhaps a hoop, perhaps a dog's lead; an old man bent over a counter that had rotted to nothing, still weighing goods only he could see. They were not frightening. That was the worst of it. They looked like a town caught mid-morning, mid-errand, mid-life — and then all of them, at once, turned to face her.

And the sound arrived.

It was not a shout. It was not even loud, at first. It was a choir — hundreds of voices speaking one sentence, layered over itself until the separate words blurred into a single sustained tone, like a bell struck once and never allowed to stop ringing:

*Ninevah sel vethra. Ninevah sel vethra.*

*Carver. Carver.* She knew that much now; three weeks of Wren's fragments had taught her more than any antiquarian's books. *Record us. Write our names.*

"They're asking," Wren whispered. Her eyes were enormous in the mushroom-light. "They've been asking since the Reckoning. There was a carver here, in the city — a guildwoman, she used to come down and write them into bone. But she died, and after that—" The girl pressed both hands over her ears, though the sound was not truly sound. "After that nobody wrote anybody."

Juniper stood in the flooded nave with her satchel heavy against her hip — the satchel where she kept her knives, her blanks, her little stock of prepared bone — and understood, with the slow cold certainty of a ledger balancing against her will, exactly why the dead had guided them here. Why the phrase at the bone-yard had led them across the river. Why every field and ruin in this country answered to old names.

They didn't want rescue. They wanted to be written down before the Veil finished eating them.

* * *

She set up her workbench on the altar steps, because it seemed right to work facing them, the way a priest faces a congregation. It took an hour to clear the stone, to lay out her tools in their familiar order — the fine graver, the broad chisel, the burnisher her grandmother's grandmother had supposedly held — and in that hour the dead drew nearer by inches, patient as tide.

"Tell me your rules," she said aloud, and her voice cracked strangely in the drowned air. "Every trade has rules. What do I carve? How many? Where do I begin?"

The answer rose out of the choir like cream rising in milk. Not one voice — never one voice — but a consensus, a settling, and Wren translated it in her halting way while Juniper's hands hovered over the first blank rib-bone.

Begin with the unburied. The ones whose graves the Veil opened and emptied. Their names are going first.

So she began with a woman named Sefa Ilves, who had been a beekeeper, who had died at seventy-one and been buried under a plum tree, and whose grave the Veil had unmade so thoroughly that even the tree was gone. Juniper carved the name into the bone and spoke it aloud as she finished — *Sefa Ilves* — and the grey woman with the rolled sleeves smiled, and thinned, and did not vanish but *settled*, the way flour settles in a jar, becoming part of something rather than leaving it. The choir sighed. The sound of that sigh went through Juniper's chest like cold water.

She carved the next one. And the next.

By the time the light above changed — though down in the nave there was no day, only the mushrooms' milk-glow and the grey flood — she had written thirty-one names. Her neck ached. Wren slept curled against the altar like a cat, exhausted from translating, and Juniper worked alone in the humming company of the dead, and found, to her shame and relief in equal measure, that she was happy.

That was the thing she had never told anyone about her gift: it was not a burden to sit with the dead. It was the opposite. The living wanted things from her — prices, favors, explanations, the performance of normalcy — but the dead wanted only one thing, and it was the one thing she was made to give. With them she was not strange, not guarded, not the woman who talked to bones. She was simply necessary. She had built her careful distance from other people on this secret foundation, and now, kneeling on a dead altar in a drowned church, she admitted it fully to herself: she had chosen the dead because the dead were easy to love.

The dead of Velland were about to teach her what love cost.

* * *

On the second night she stopped counting hours.

The work had a rhythm and the rhythm had her. Blank bone from the satchel, then bone gathered from the cathedral's own ossuary — a chamber off the north transept where the sinking earth had preserved shelves upon shelves of the parish's ancestors, and where the oldest dead waited with a courtesy that broke her heart, letting the newly dead go first. Graver to groove. Name spoken. Name settled. Next.

The names came in floods now. They had forty years of backlog. A ferryman named Oskar Pell. Twin sisters, Lira and Loma, born within an hour of each other and dead within a day of each other, eighty years apart. A cobbler, a schoolmistress, four brothers lost in the same winter. Whole families arrived together and asked, with terrible politeness, whether they might be written on the same piece, and Juniper learned to fit six names into the curve of a single scapula, letter stacked inside letter like birds roosting.

Her hands knew this work. That was the trouble. Her hands had been trained since childhood to ignore the body's complaints — the burnisher callus, the ache in the thumb joint, the way her eyes dried and stung in bad light. Her hands reported nothing. So it was Wren who noticed, waking on the third night, that the altar steps were spotted dark.

"You're bleeding."

"It's nothing. It happens."

"It's a lot of nothing." The child took Juniper's wrist and turned it to the mushroom-light, and Juniper saw what her own nerves had declined to mention: the pad of her right index finger split along the grain, blood welling with every stroke of the graver and running down into the letters, so that the names of the dead were being written in the red of the living. Half the bones in her finished pile wore rust-brown signatures.

"You have to stop," Wren said. "You have to sleep. You haven't eaten since—"

"Since we got here. I know." Juniper flexed her fingers. They obeyed stiffly, like tools left out in frost. "Wren, there are thousands of them. Maybe tens of thousands. Every hour I rest is an hour the Veil works on them. You saw what happened to the transept names." She had seen it, and could not stop seeing it: the far wall's inscriptions fading even now, letter by letter, the Veil working patiently through forty years of stone, erasing at the speed of erosion. When a name went completely, its owner frayed at the edges — she'd watched it happen to a man that morning, watched his grey outline lose its seams, watched him spend his last coherence pressing his hands together in thanks for having lasted long enough to be asked his name.

"I saw," Wren said quietly. "But dead people don't need you alive more than they need you whole. Maren said that." Maren's name landed between them like a dropped knife. They had left her in the capital with her oath and her stitched map and her warning — *the Door Out opens soon, be there when it does* — and every night here was a night subtracted from the road ahead.

"The Door can wait a week," Juniper said.

"What if it can't?"

"Then it waits anyway, and I'll have done what I could." She pulled her hand free, gently, and picked up the graver again. "Go back to sleep, Wren."

The child did not go back to sleep. She sat watching instead, chin on her knees, and after a while she began to help in the only way she could — singing. Softly, tunelessly, a Vellish lullaby whose words Juniper half-knew and could not say how. The choir caught it up. Hundreds of dead voices carrying a children's song through a drowned nave while a bleeding woman carved their neighbors' names into bone: it should have been horror. It was the closest thing to peace Juniper had known since the knock at her door.

* * *

On the fourth night, the dead began to argue.

It started small — a ripple in the consensus, two currents moving against each other in the grey flood. Then Wren sat up abruptly, listening, her face going tight.

"They're fighting about order," she said. "The ones from the capital quarter want to go next because the Veil is faster there. The farmers want to go next because they're already mostly gone. And the ones from the harbor—" she paused, listening harder, and her voice dropped— "the harbor ones say it doesn't matter what order, because there isn't time for everyone. Even if you don't sleep. Even if you don't eat. Even if your hands fall off."

The truth of it moved through the nave like cold through floorboards. Juniper kept carving — *Hesta Marun, weaver; Hesta Marun* — but her strokes had gone uneven.

"How many can I do?" she asked. "In a night, truly. Best case."

Wren did the arithmetic the way children do, lips moving, and arrived at a number too honest to soften. "Maybe ninety. If you don't bleed so much."

"And how many are left?"

Silence, except the choir, which had gone quiet in the way of crowds awaiting a verdict.

"More than ten thousand," Wren said finally. "The carver before you — the guildwoman — she told them, at the end. She said she'd written four thousand in her lifetime and the country held a million souls, and she apologized to every single one she couldn't reach, one at a time, for eleven years. That's why the choir knows how to ask. She taught them to take turns."

Juniper set down the graver. Her hands, freed of their task, began to shake — a fine, continuous tremor, the tremor of a mechanism pushed past tolerance. Ten thousand names. Ninety a night. She could do the division herself, and the quotient was years, years she did not have, kneeling in a sunken church while the Door Out opened once a generation and closed again, and Corvane's writs crept after them like frost across a window, and Wren — small, spentable, irreplaceable Wren — grew older by the hour in a country being eaten from the edges inward.

For the first time since she had taken up her grandmother's tools, Juniper Ravensworth hated the work.

Not the dead. Never the dead. She hated the arithmetic — the same arithmetic, she realized with a lurch, that had ruled every ledger she'd ever kept. Debts exceeding assets. Claims exceeding capacity. Somewhere in the Guild of Impossible Things there was a shelf of ledgers full of entries just like this one, balances that could not be made to balance, and the guild's whole grim genius was knowing which debts to pay and which to carry forward marked *outstanding*, and she had thought herself different from the merchants, she had thought her trade was mercy, but here was mercy itself itemized and rationed, here was a queue of ten thousand souls and one pair of ruined hands, and no entry in any book could make the sum come out fair.

A grey shape detached itself from the congregation and drifted close. An old man — the counter-weigher, she recognized him, the shopkeeper who had spent four days refusing his turn so that others might go first. He knelt at the base of the altar steps, which meant he lowered himself into the grey flood to his knees, and he folded his hands the way supplicants do.

He spoke, and this time Juniper heard him directly, without Wren's translation, the Vellish arriving in her mind already converted — her inheritance doing its secret work, the language her family had bought their lives by abandoning rising up in her like water finding an old channel:

*Carver. We know the number. We have always known the number.*

"Then you know I can't—"

*We know.* His grey face managed something like a smile. *We are not asking you to save us. We are asking you to keep us. There is a difference, and the difference is everything.*

"I don't understand."

*The Veil unmakes,* he said. *But a name written in bone and carried past the Veil's edge cannot be unmade. It can only be lost. So we do not ask for all of us. We ask for enough of us. A remnant. A seed. The woman who taught us to take turns — her bones went out of this country in a peddler's pack, forty years ago, and wherever those bones are, those four thousand names still exist. Still count. Still wait.* He raised his face to her, and around him the whole congregation had gone still, listening, ten thousand dead hanging on the theology of one dead shopkeeper. *Fill your satchel, carver. Fill it past reason. Fill it until you cannot lift it. Carry what you can and let the rest trust you, and that trust will be enough, because it is all anyone has ever had.*

Juniper looked at the pile of finished bones — ninety, a hundred and twenty, she'd lost count — each one a small white archive, each one a country in miniature. Then at her satchel. Then at her hands, still trembling, still seeping.

"How do I choose?" The question tore out of her rougher than she intended. "Who gets to be the seed? Tell me the rule. Give me the rule and I'll follow it."

The old man considered her for a long moment, and around the nave the choir murmured like wind through wheat, and the answer, when it came, was passed voice to voice to voice until the whole cathedral held it at once, gently, the way one holds something breakable:

*There is no rule. There was never a rule. Choose as a mother chooses — not fairly, not completely, but with everything you have.*

* * *

She chose for two more nights.

She chose children first — not because their souls weighed more but because a name uncarried for forty more years would be a longer exile for them than for the old, and she hated herself for the logic and used it anyway. She chose the beekeeper Sefa Ilves twice, once in bone and once cut into the underside of her own boot sole, because some names you want touching your skin. She chose the harbor families who had said it didn't matter, because she could not bear to prove them right. She chose at random when choosing by merit became unbearable — eyes shut, hand out, letting the dead arrange themselves against her palm — and discovered that randomness was its own kind of prayer.

Her hands bled through the bandages Wren wound from a petticoat. The blood got into the letters and she stopped fighting it; the dead assured her, through Wren, that names written in the blood of the living held fastest of all, and she decided not to examine what it cost them to know that.

On the sixth night, near dawn-that-didn't-exist-down-here, she carved the last name her satchel could hold — a girl of nine named Tuuli, who had liked to catch frogs — and wrapped it in cloth and laid it on top of the hoard: two hundred and eleven bones, a nation of seeds, heavier than any ledger she had ever carried.

Then she stood, and her knees screamed, and the whole congregation of the unburied — the thousands she could not take, the thousands who would fray and fade in the dark behind her — did not wail or accuse.

They sang.

The lullaby, all together, hundreds of grey throats, filling the drowned nave to its broken pinnacles, and Wren translated the last verse through her tears because Juniper's Vellish failed her exactly where it mattered most: *Sleep, little seed, in the box of the world. Someone is coming. Someone remembers the road.*

Juniper climbed the seven terraces with two hundred and eleven names on her back and her hands wrapped like a beggar's, and at the top, in the ordinary grey daylight of a country without a name, she looked down at the silent green hollow that held everything she could not carry —

— and saw, on the rim of the Downtaking, standing in the grass where no footprints led to them, three figures in undyed grey, faceless, patient, waiting with the composure of creditors.

They had not come for the dead.
