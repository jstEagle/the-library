## 21. Anger, Carved

The dead had finished speaking an hour ago, and the silence they left behind was worse than anything they'd said.

Juniper sat in the ruined sacristy of the sunken cathedral with her grandmother's ledger open across her knees, and she did not move. She did not move for a long time. The candle she'd lit from a stub of altar-wax guttered in air that tasted of stone and old incense and something else now — something sweetish and wrong, like flowers left too long in a closed room. The smell of a bargain kept.

*Sold,* the bones had said. *Rosalind Ravensworth sold the name. Sold it whole, root and branch, the true name of Velland given to the Veil's makers in exchange for three lives carried out through the crack before it sealed. And the Ravensworth fortune grew from that payment like a pearl around a wound.*

She had thought, when she first heard it, that she would weep. She had braced herself for weeping the way one braces for a blow — shoulders up, breath held, eyes shut against the coming sting.

But no tears came. What came instead was heat. It started low, somewhere beneath her ribs where her gift lived, that place where the dead's voices pooled like water in a cupped palm, and it rose and rose until her hands were shaking and the ledger trembled on her knees and the candle flame bent sideways as if flinching from her.

"Say it again," she whispered to the bone laid out before her — a femur, long and pale, belonging to a woman who had died inside the Veil forty years ago and waited all this time to be heard. "Say it again so I know I didn't dream it."

But the dead were quiet now. They had said what they came to say. They had pressed their truth into her carving-hand and left her alone with it, the way you leave someone alone with a diagnosis, and Juniper sat in the ringing silence and felt the heat climb higher.

Her grandmother. *Her grandmother.* The woman who had taught her to hold a graver. Who had wrapped Juniper's small fingers around the bone stylus when she was six years old and said, *the dead speak softly, little wren, so you must carve loudly.* Who had smelled of beeswax and dried lavender and had sung — had *sung*, God help her — half-remembered lullabies in a language she'd claimed was nothing, nonsense sounds, a game they played.

Vellish. Those lullabies had been Vellish. Every one.

And the fortune. The townhouse in the capital with its seven chimneys. The apprenticeships bought, the guild shares purchased, the ledgers bound in leather so fine it seemed to drink the light. All of it built on a single transaction, entered nowhere, denominated in everything: one country's true name, surrendered, so that three people could walk out of the erasure untouched while everyone else stayed inside to be forgotten.

Three lives. Her line. Bought with a nation.

Juniper stood up so fast the ledger slid to the floor and lay there, splayed, its pages breathing in the draft.

"No," she said aloud, to no one, to the stones, to the woman who had been dust for thirty years and whose ghost she could not even properly summon because the dead of this place were thick as fog but her grandmother's ghost was *elsewhere*, was comfortable, was perhaps sitting in some warm parlor of the afterlife counting interest. "No. You don't get to have done that. You don't get to have been *her*. You held my hand. You taught me—"

Her voice cracked and the crack made it worse, made the heat spike into something white-edged, and suddenly she was moving, kicking over the candle-stub, plunging the sacristy into grey half-dark where the stained glass windows — shattered, salt-eaten, their saints' faces worn to smudges — caught the last light and threw it back broken.

* * *

Her tools were in the satchel by the door where she always kept them. Roll of oiled leather, tied with a cord her grandmother had braided. Inside: nine gravers of graduated width, two burnishers, a small saw of Vellish steel that had come down through four generations, a whetstone worn into a valley by a century of thumbs, and the stylus. The bone stylus. Grandmother's stylus, grandmother's mother's stylus, back and back into the erased country like a root under a wall.

She picked up the roll and untied the cord and unrolled it on the altar slab, and the tools gleamed up at her in the gloom, faithful, familiar, each one shaped to her grip by years of use, and she hated them. That was the terrible thing. She hated them the way you hate a gift that turns out to be stolen goods.

"You knew," she said to the stylus. It lay in its channel of velvet, pale as a fingerbone, which is very nearly what it was. "You knew what you were. You knew what I was holding every time I carved a name. And you let me think it was a *gift*."

Her hand closed around the nearest graver.

It would be easy. That was the horror of rage, how practical it made everything. She could snap the thin ones like matchwood. The heavier ones she could grind against the altar stone until their edges were ruin. The saw she could bend until the steel gave its small dying note. And the stylus — the stylus she could press down against the slab and lean her whole weight on until it went to powder, powder she could scatter among the ruins where no ledger would ever gather it again.

No more hearing the dead. No more being the instrument of a country's grief. No more inheritance. Let the gift die with the lie that had carried it.

"Juniper."

Wren stood in the doorway.

Juniper hadn't heard her come in — of course she hadn't; the child moved like weather, like something you noticed only after it had already changed the room. Wren was barefoot still, though Juniper had long since stopped trying to keep shoes on her. Her dark hair was loose and her eyes in the half-light were enormous and steady, and she looked at the roll of tools, at Juniper's raised fist, at the graver clutched in it, and she did not look afraid.

That was somehow the worst part. A child should have looked afraid.

"Go back to sleep," Juniper said, and her voice came out ragged, scraped raw on the inside. "I'm — it's fine. Go back to sleep."

Wren did not go back to sleep. Wren came forward across the ruined floor, stepping without looking around the fallen masonry the way she stepped around everything, as if she had a map of the world drawn on the inside of her eyelids, and she stopped at the altar and looked down at the tools.

Then she looked up at Juniper, and asked a question in Vellish.

Juniper understood it. That was the wound within the wound — she understood it without wanting to, the way you understand your own name. *Why are you breaking your hands?*

"They're not my hands," Juniper said. "Don't you understand? Nothing is mine. My name isn't mine. My trade isn't mine. My grandmother sold—" and her voice broke again, worse, and she hated that too, hated that grief kept interrupting anger like a creditor at a door. "She sold the country, Wren. Your country. Everyone in these bones, everyone in the choir downstairs, everyone who ever sang those stupid lullabies — she traded them for *us*. For me. I am the change she got back. I'm the coins in her pocket."

Wren tilted her head. Then, with the grave deliberateness she brought to everything, she reached out and touched the femur lying on the altar — the dead woman's bone, the one who had told them — and said something soft in Vellish, and put Juniper's free hand flat against it.

The dead woman spoke. Not in words this time. In feeling. A warmth like sun through a window. An image pressed gently against the inside of Juniper's mind: a kitchen. Bread. A child laughing. And beneath it, unmistakable, patient: *it was not your doing, carver. It was not your hands.*

"But it was her hands," Juniper whispered. "It was her hands and her blood and I have her blood, I have her *hands*, look at them—" She held them up, trembling, ten fingers shaped exactly like the fingers that had signed away a nation. "Everything I've ever carved, every goodbye I've delivered, every debt I've balanced — I did it with stolen instruments. I'm not a guildswoman. I'm a fence. I'm laundering a country."

The rage crested again, and this time she let it.

The first graver snapped cleanly, a dry little sound in the dark, and the sound was almost a relief. She took up the second and bent it against the altar edge and it resisted, steel remembering its temper, and she bore down until her wrist screamed and it gave with a crack that echoed off the vaulting. The third. The fourth. The burnisher rang against stone like a struck bell, a bright wrong note in a house of the dead, and somewhere below her feet she felt the vast congregation of the unburied stir and turn toward the sound like a field of flowers turning toward a storm.

"Let them hear!" she shouted at the floor, at the whole drowned country underneath her. "Let them hear what their rescuer is! Let them hear the granddaughter of the woman who sold them!"

She reached for the stylus.

Wren's hand got there first.

Small, quick, certain — it covered the bone stylus in its velvet channel the way a bird covers its egg, and Wren looked up at Juniper with an expression that was not pleading, was not fear, was not any of the things that might have been argued with. It was simply *no*. The plainest word in any language, spoken without a mouth.

"Move your hand, Wren."

Wren shook her head.

"It's the thing itself. Don't you see? It's the actual thing she used. It probably — it might have been *in the room*." Juniper's voice climbed. "It might have touched the paper. The contract. The price. It's filthy, it's the filthiest object I own, and I've held it every day of my life and loved it—"

Wren lifted the stylus out from under her own palm. Held it up between them, turning it slowly, letting the grey light move along its length. Then she pointed — at the stylus, at Juniper, at the femur, at the floor where the dead waited — and spoke a long sentence in Vellish, low and level, and though Juniper caught only fragments of the grammar she caught all of the meaning, because meaning was the one language she had never needed translated.

*A tool does not choose what it writes. A hand does not choose the blood in it. But the writer chooses. The writer is choosing right now.*

"I don't want to choose," Juniper said, and it came out small and awful, the truth under all the fire. "I want to smash it all and be nobody's heir. I want it to stop being my debt."

Wren stepped closer. Took Juniper's right hand — the shaking one, the carving one — and turned it palm-up, and set the stylus in it, and folded Juniper's fingers closed around it with both of her own small hands, pressing them shut, holding them shut, her chin tucked, her whole slight weight committed to the gesture.

Then she looked up, and said one word. Not Vellish this time. Plain trade-tongue, learned in a few months of living beside Juniper, spoken carefully, the way a child carries water:

"*Carve.*"

The word went into Juniper like a graver going into bone — resistance first, then a clean deep channel.

Because that was it, wasn't it. That was the whole of it. Rosalind Ravensworth had used her hands to sell. These hands could use themselves to pay it back. The debt was real — the ledger didn't lie, ledgers never lied, that was the one holy thing about them — and a real debt could be entered, itemized, worked down. Anger that smashed the tools paid nothing. Anger that picked up the tools and wrote with them —

Anger, carved, became resolve. Her grandmother had taught her that too, in her way, without knowing she was teaching it. *The dead speak softly, little wren, so you must carve loudly.*

Little wren. She had called Juniper that. And now there was an actual Wren standing in front of her, holding her fists closed around the instrument of repayment, and Juniper understood that the country had sent her not a burden but a collections agent.

A laugh came out of her — cracked, wet, half sob, but a laugh. Wren frowned at it, suspicious, and Juniper knelt and pulled the child against her shoulder and held on, and Wren permitted it, the way she permitted all affection: stiffly, briefly, and then with a sudden fierce total surrender that undid Juniper completely.

"I'm sorry," Juniper said into her hair. "I scared you."

Wren pulled back far enough to shake her head once. *You didn't.* Then she pointed at the broken gravers scattered on the altar slab, and her frown deepened into something almost stern, and she said a short phrase in Vellish that Juniper understood perfectly: *and what do you intend to do about that?*

"Grind new edges," Juniper admitted. "They're steel. Steel forgives. That's rather the point of it." She gathered the broken pieces, laying them in the leather roll like soldiers in a tomb. Three gravers gone past saving; she'd buy more, or better, learn to forge them herself, from ore dug out of honest ground. The rest she could hone back to truth on the whetstone worn by a century of thumbs — including, she noted, and did not flinch from noting, her grandmother's thumb. The thumb was part of the tool. The tool was part of the work. The work was hers now, whatever it had been before.

Last, the stylus. She held it up. Bone, pale, forty years complicit and four hundred years older than that.

"You stay," she told it quietly. "But you work for me now. And the wage is this: every name I cut with you goes into the ledger twice. Once for the dead, and once against the debt. We're going to balance it, you and I. Line by line. However long it takes. However much it costs."

The stylus, being bone, said nothing. But the cathedral around her exhaled — a long settling of stone and dust and forty years of held breath — and from below, faintly, the choir of the unburied began again, not begging now, not pressing, just singing. Low. In Vellish. A working song, maybe, or a psalm, or possibly — and Juniper chose to believe this, chose it the way you choose a road — a song about morning.

* * *

They did not sleep again that night. Juniper sat on the altar steps with the ledger across her knees and worked by candlelight, and Wren curled against her side with her head under Juniper's arm, asleep within minutes, trusting in a way that Juniper intended to deserve.

She turned back through the pages to the beginning of her own book — the first ledger she'd ever kept, age eleven, in her grandmother's house, in her grandmother's hand guiding hers. *Debts of the Guild of Impossible Things, being a true account.* And there, on the flyleaf, in that same beloved hand, an epigraph she had read a thousand times and never once understood:

*All accounts settle. The question is only who is holding the pen when they do.*

"She knew," Juniper murmured. "At the end. She knew it would come to me."

She dipped her pen. Below the dead woman's testimony, below the terrible arithmetic of the founding payment, she wrote in her clearest bookhand:

*Item: one country's name, taken. Debtor: Rosalind Ravensworth, deceased; debt inherited by J. Ravensworth, living. Status: acknowledged. Terms: repayment in kind — names restored, one per entry, until the ledger balances or the ledger-keeper fails.*

*Interest accrues in memory.*

She blotted it. She looked at it for a long time. Then she took up the stylus and a fresh rib-bone and began, softly, so as not to wake the child, to carve the first name of the night's second shift — and that was when she heard it.

Footsteps above. On the broken flagstones of the nave, careful, deliberate, too heavy for a ghost and too measured for a wanderer. More than one pair. And threaded through the sound of boots, faint but unmistakable, drifting down through the collapsed roof into the house of the dead: the dry rustle of paper. Guild writs. Being read aloud.

Someone had found them. Someone had brought paperwork.
