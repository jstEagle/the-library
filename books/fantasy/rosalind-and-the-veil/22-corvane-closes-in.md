## 22. Corvane Closes In

The writ arrived at dawn, as the best paperwork always did — carried by a courier who smelled of wet wool and fear, sealed in grey wax that was not quite wax. Master Corvane broke it over his breakfast of black tea and dried figs, and read it twice, and smiled in the way he smiled at ledgers: not with pleasure, but with recognition.

*By order of the Standing Committee on Resonant Goods,* it began, *and pursuant to Precedent 114-K (Reckoning Era), the item designated WREN, minor, female, last verified speaker of a suppressed tongue, is hereby declared recoverable property of the Guild of Impossible Things.*

Recoverable. As though she had wandered off like a spoon.

Corvane set the writ down beside his cup and looked out the window of his office at the guildhall's inner courtyard, where the humming doors stood in their long ranks, each one tuned to a different absence. He had worked in this building for thirty-one years. He knew every door's pitch the way a musician knows his scales. The door that had been Juniper Ravensworth's exit — forged entry, forged exit, a forgery good enough to fool the hall itself for nearly two days — still sang slightly flat. He could hear it from here, if he listened. A small wrongness. A debt unpaid.

He did not blame her for the forgery. That was the thing his colleagues never understood about him, the thing that made them call him cold when in truth he was only precise: Corvane did not deal in blame. Blame was an expense with no return. What he dealt in was value, and value, properly audited, forgave everything.

Juniper Ravensworth had stolen guild property. Fine. She had also, in doing so, raised that property's value considerably. A runaway last speaker was worth three times a compliant one — the brokers paid premiums for items with provenance, with history, with *resistance*. Every mile she ran added to the invoice. In a sense, he owed her.

In another sense, and this was the sense that governed, he would find her, take the child from her, and deliver both the girl and the girl's escort to the parties who had standing orders, and he would do it without malice, without haste, and without a single unnecessary cruelty, because unnecessary cruelty was waste, and waste was the only sin Corvane had ever believed in.

He rang the small brass bell on his desk. His clerk appeared — a nervous young man named Pell whose hands always shook slightly, which Corvane had never once remarked upon, because remarking upon it would be cruel, and cruelty was waste.

"Pell. Two things. First, pull the file on Precedent 114-K. I want the original affidavits from the Reckoning era, all forty-one pages, and I want the valuation schedule for resonant goods, current edition."

"Yes, Master Corvane." Pell wrote it down, though there was no need; the boy simply found comfort in writing things down. Corvane permitted it. Comfort was cheap.

"Second," Corvane said, "find me the wardens."

Pell's pen stopped. "The — the grey ones, sir?"

"The grey ones," Corvane agreed. "Three of them. No more. They unnerve the staff past three, and besides, they work best in odd numbers. Something to do with symmetry. Tell them the fee is negotiable but the timeline is not. I want the item recovered within the fortnight."

"And the Ravensworth woman, sir?"

Corvane considered his tea. It had gone cold, which happened whenever he thought about Juniper Ravensworth, though he had never established causation and refused to speculate. "The woman is a separate ledger," he said. "A talented carver, a competent bookkeeper, and now a thief. There are buyers for talent even disgraced. Note her as *recoverable, condition open*."

* * *

The wardens came at dusk, because they always came at dusk, sliding into the courtyard like fog deciding to have opinions. There were three of them, and Corvane had met them before, on other retrievals, other erasures-in-progress, and he had learned their habits the way one learns the habits of weather: not with affection, but with respect for what they could do to your plans.

They were tall. Or they seemed tall; height was one of those qualities they borrowed from whatever room they stood in. Their faces were smooth as the backs of spoons. When they spoke — rarely, and only to confirm terms — their voices came from somewhere just left of where they were standing.

"You have the writ," said the middle one, which was how Corvane distinguished them, since none of them would answer to names.

"I have the writ," Corvane said. "And I have something better. I have the trail."

He led them into his office and unrolled his working papers across the desk: the ledger extracts, the ferry tolls, the market reports from four towns along the western road. He had spent three days assembling it, and it was, he felt, some of his finest forensic accounting.

"Here." He tapped a line. "A memory-toll collected at the Graywater ferry, nine days ago. The ferryman records every payment in his own private book — memories don't leave receipts, you understand, so he keeps a ledger of what was paid, in case anyone comes back demanding refunds. The entry reads: *one face, maternal, wept for.* Paid by a woman traveling with a child who spoke to him in no language he recognized."

The wardens did not nod. They simply became slightly more present, the way a room becomes more present when a lamp is lit.

"The child spoke to the ferryman," the middle warden said. Not a question. Confirmation. Somewhere behind its spoon-face, something had turned over.

"She speaks to everyone," Corvane said. "That is the entire point of her, and the entire problem. She is a last speaker walking through country that has been taught to forget her language exists. Every word she says out loud is a small tear in the Veil's work. The brokers want that stopped before it spreads. I intend to see it stopped profitably."

He laid down the next sheet. "From Graywater, the road forks. North goes to the salt flats — nothing there, no cover, no water, no reason. South goes through the hill country toward the old borderlands. Toward Velland." He said the name flatly, without reverence, the way one says *the old quarry* or *the flooded mine*. "Which is, of course, exactly where a sentimental woman and a homesick child would go. People run toward what they've lost. It's the most reliable instinct in commerce."

"And you have sent riders ahead?"

"I have sent no one ahead," Corvane said. "I am going myself."

This produced the closest thing to surprise the wardens were capable of: all three of them oriented on him at once, like compass needles.

"You'll forgive the observation," said the middle one, in a voice like paper being folded, "but masters of guilds do not walk the mud."

"Masters of guilds," Corvane said, "do not get to make the mistakes that apprentices make, and this retrieval has already accumulated two errors I intend to prevent a third. Error one: the Ravensworth woman was underestimated. She is a bookkeeper. Bookkeepers know where everything is hidden, including exits. Error two: the child was treated as cargo. Cargo can be handled roughly. This cargo talks, and everything it says costs someone. I will handle the negotiation personally, because I am the only person in this guild who knows what the item actually is — and therefore the only person who knows what it is worth, and therefore the only person who will not damage it through ignorance."

He gathered his papers, squared their edges, and tied them with cord. "We leave within the hour. You will travel behind me, at a distance, and you will not unmake anything until I give the word. Is that understood? Nothing gets erased ahead of schedule. Erasure ahead of schedule is called vandalism, and vandalism depresses prices."

The wardens absorbed this. Then the middle one said, quietly, in that voice from just left of itself:

"You speak of them as goods. But you flinch when you say the old name."

Corvane paused, cord half-knotted.

"I flinch at nothing," he said. "I adjust my grip. There is a difference, and the difference is money."

But the wardens watched him the whole time he finished tying the knot, and he had the uncomfortable sense — rare in him, unwelcome, noted and filed — that they could see all the way through the merchant's coat to whatever wore it.

* * *

They made better time than he'd expected, which was itself unsettling. The wardens did not ride horses; horses would not carry them. Instead they simply *were* wherever the road bent, appearing at each milestone as though they had always been standing there waiting for the rest of the party to catch up, and Corvane rode between these apparitions in a carriage he'd hired with six horses and a driver who took payment in advance and asked no questions, having been paid extra specifically not to.

On the third day out of the city, they passed through a village where the innkeeper swore — cheerfully, confidently, with the wholehearted conviction of a man repeating what everyone knew — that there had never been any country to the west at all. Just hills. Just mist. Just bad land nobody wanted.

"Never a country," the man said, wiping mugs behind his bar. "You're thinking of the duchy maybe, up north. West is nothing. Always been nothing. My grandfather hunted those hills, said there wasn't so much as a ruin."

"Your grandfather," Corvane said mildly, "was born in Velland."

The mug stopped mid-wipe.

For one moment — one measurable, invoiceable moment — the innkeeper's face did something complicated. Something moved behind his eyes like a fish under ice: a shape, a shadow, a flicker of *almost*. His mouth opened. His throat worked. And then the moment passed, the ice closed, and the man laughed and said, "Funny name. Never heard it. West is nothing, friend. Always has been."

Outside, the wardens stood in the yard, motionless, and Corvane had the distinct impression they were satisfied. That was the work, he understood. That was what the Veil's wardens *did*: not only erase, but patrol the edges of the erasure, smoothing the seams, tamping down the little fish-shapes under the ice. Every village they passed through came out the other side of their passage a little more certain of its own ignorance.

It was efficient. It was thorough. It was, Corvane admitted to himself, watching them stand in the inn-yard like three holes cut out of the evening, one of the most impressive operations he had ever been adjacent to.

So why did his hands feel cold?

He climbed back into the carriage and took out his working papers and reviewed the trail again, because reviewing figures calmed him, and because there was a discrepancy he kept circling and refusing to name.

The discrepancy was this: the trail was too clean.

Ferry tolls, market sightings, a hay-buyer's report, a witness at a shrine who described "a woman with ink-stained fingers and a child who hummed in a dead tongue" — every entry corroborated, every date sequenced, every stop logical. Juniper Ravensworth was a careful traveler. Too careful. She was a bookkeeper running from the most powerful trade guild in the known world, and she was leaving a trail readable by any first-year clerk.

Unless, said the part of Corvane's mind that had made him rich, she wanted to be followed. Unless the trail was bait, and the trap was somewhere south, in the hills, in the old borderlands, where the roads narrowed and the villages forgot things and a woman with a bone-carver's gift and a guild-trained mind had chosen her ground.

Or unless — and here his pen stopped moving altogether — she wasn't running a trap at all. Unless she was running a *ledger*, and every clean entry was deliberate, and what she was really doing was leaving a record. A paper trail in the oldest sense: proof, preserved against erasure, that a child had passed this way speaking a forbidden tongue. Every witness they interviewed was a witness she had made. Every innkeeper's flicker of almost-remembering was a deposit in an account she was building.

Corvane sat in the swaying carriage and stared at his own beautiful, damning evidence and understood, with the particular horror of an accountant finding arithmetic where he expected sentiment, that she might be using *him*. Using his pursuit. Using the wardens themselves — because wardens, patrolling the seams, forced the forgetting to happen in front of witnesses, and witnesses who watch forgetting happen start asking why, and questions are the one thing the Veil cannot fully digest.

She wasn't fleeing the net, perhaps. She was teaching it to catch questions.

"No," Corvane said aloud, to the empty carriage, and the word sounded strange in his own mouth. "No. She's a carver and a clerk. She's frightened and tired and in over her head. Sentiment makes people stupid, and she is merely being sentimental about a child."

The carriage rattled on through the darkening hills. Behind it, at the edge of the lamplight, the three wardens kept pace without feet, without sound, without faces, and the seams of the world closed smoothly behind them, village by village, name by name.

* * *

On the fifth day they reached the milestone.

It stood at the fork where the south road entered the true border country: a squat stone post older than any kingdom currently claiming the land, its carved inscription worn to suggestion. Corvane had seen it drawn in old surveys. It had once marked the edge of Velland. The surveyors' notes called it *the Last Honest Stone*, because unlike maps and ledgers and oaths, stone sometimes kept what it was told.

Someone had recently carved into it.

Corvane dismounted and knelt, and his breath went shallow in a way he resented deeply.

Fresh cuts, made with a fine graver by a skilled hand: not letters, exactly. Ledger-marks. A column of tally strokes in the old mercantile notation that every guild-trained bookkeeper learned and almost no one outside the guild could read. He translated automatically, the way one translates one's mother tongue without effort, and what the strokes said was:

*Item: one child, one carver, one debt.*
*Status: in transit.*
*Value: appreciating.*
*Followed by: three shadows and a man who used to have a grandmother.*

His eyes caught on the last line and stayed there, the way a boot stays in deep mud.

He had never told anyone about his grandmother. It was not in any file. It was not in any ledger. Forty years ago — the year of the Reckoning, the year the maps went blank — a woman had stood in a doorway in a city that no longer existed and put a coat on a small boy and told him, in a language the boy was never allowed to speak again after that night, *forget the word for home and they cannot take it from you.* He had forgotten it. He had become excellent at forgetting it. He had built an entire life on the load-bearing principle that some things are safer erased, and he had risen, and prospered, and never once — not once in forty years — had he written that woman's name anywhere.

And now it was carved into a milestone in his own professional notation, in Juniper Ravensworth's hand, addressed to him by name, though she had signed nothing and needed to.

*He knows,* the carving said, without saying it. *He knows what he is carrying.*

Behind him, the wardens had gone very still. Stillness, in them, was attention. One of them drifted closer to the stone, and Corvane heard himself say, sharply, before he had decided to say anything at all:

"Don't touch it."

The warden paused.

"It's guild notation," Corvane said, rising, dusting his knees, reassembling his voice into its usual dry inventory. "Guild property, effectively. Defacing guild marks is a fineable offense. We'll log it. We'll bill her." He was aware, distantly, that he was talking too much, and that the wardens did not blink, because they had no lids to blink, and that the middle one was looking at him with the whole featureless front of its face in a manner that suggested it had just audited *him* and found discrepancies.

"We're close," he said instead, climbing back into the carriage. "South road. Hill country. She'll need shelter within two days — the child can't sleep rough indefinitely, and neither can a woman raised behind guild walls. There are settlements along the way. Farmsteads. Villages." He picked up his papers. His hands were steady. He made them be steady. "Some of those villages, I'm told, are full of people who gave up their memories voluntarily to be safe. People who chose the Veil's peace over their own pasts."

The carriage rolled south. The milestone fell away behind them, its fresh-cut tally strokes catching the last light like a wound catching sun.

And far ahead, down the narrowing road, in a valley where the mist behaved strangely and the fields answered to names no living map admitted, smoke rose from the chimneys of a village that had chosen to forget — and Corvane caught himself hoping, with a cold and unfamiliar dread, that Juniper Ravensworth would not stop there.

Because he had seen what happened to people who surrendered their memories to be safe. He saw one every morning, shaving.

And if she reached them first — if she showed them what remembering cost, and what it bought — then the net he had spent five days drawing tight around a stolen child might close instead around the one thing he had spent forty years making sure no one could ever hand him back.

The wardens walked behind the carriage, patient as interest accruing, and the road went down into the mist.
