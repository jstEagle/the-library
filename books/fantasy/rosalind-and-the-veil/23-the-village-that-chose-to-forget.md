## 23. The Village That Chose to Forget

The village had no name on any map, which by now told Juniper everything she needed to know about it.

It sat in a fold of hills where Velland's old borders should have begun — she could feel it in her teeth, in the faint ache of the bone stylus against her hip, in the way the dead pressed close and then, strangely, fell back. They did not crowd her here. They hung at the edge of her hearing like people lingering outside a lit window, unwilling to come nearer.

"They're afraid," Wren said quietly, walking beside her. The girl had not spoken much since Maren's map had brought them down out of the high passes. "Not of you."

"Of what, then?"

Wren considered the question with the grave patience she gave all questions, as though each one were a stone she must turn over before setting it down. "Of being remembered wrong," she said at last. "Or of being remembered right."

The road into the village was swept clean. That was the first odd thing. Every other settlement they'd passed since crossing the river had worn its forgetting like weather — doors left unlatched because no one could recall why they'd once been barred, fields gone to bramble, wells filled with stones and no memory of wishing. But this place was tidy. Whitewashed walls. Wood stacked square. A bell rope hanging straight in a tower that had no bell.

And everywhere, small signs that someone had worked very hard to make the forgetting look like peace.

Juniper counted them as they walked: a lintel with a carving chiseled off but the outline still ghosted beneath the paint; a garden planted in rows too regular for anyone's pleasure, as if the planter had followed instructions rather than love; a well with a new wooden cover, and nailed to it, a slate bearing a single word in careful capitals: *FORGET.*

At the bottom of the slate, smaller: *We chose this. Do not pity us.*

"I don't like it here," Wren said. Her hand found Juniper's sleeve — she had taken to doing that in strange places, anchoring herself to cloth rather than flesh, as if even touch were a currency she was careful not to overspend.

"Neither do I," Juniper said. "But we need food, and Maren's map says there's a road east from this valley that doesn't appear on any guild chart. Roads that don't appear are usually roads someone paid to hide." She paused. "Someone with something worth hiding."

They found the inn by its smell — bread, real bread, yeast and woodsmoke — and Juniper's stomach made the decision her caution would have argued against. Inside, the common room was warm and half-full, and every head turned when Wren crossed the threshold.

Not at Juniper. At Wren.

Juniper had grown used to a certain kind of looking over the weeks of their flight: suspicion, greed, the flat appraisal of people deciding whether a child was trouble. This was different. This was the look of people seeing a ghost walk in wearing shoes. An old woman by the fire put her knitting down and did not pick it up again. A man at the corner table stood, thought better of it, sat, stood again, and finally left his cup untouched and went out into the cold.

"He looks like Tomas's girl," someone whispered, and then, horrified at their own words, clapped a hand over their mouth as if language itself might betray them.

The innkeeper was a broad, gray woman with forearms like a smith's and an expression of practiced blankness. She set down bread and stew without being asked and said only, "Two coppers. No questions asked here, and none answered. House rule."

"We'll pay the rule gladly," Juniper said, and set down four.

The woman looked at the coins. Looked at Wren, who was eating with the deliberate thoroughness of a child who had known hunger and did not intend to know it again. Something moved behind the innkeeper's face — quick, suppressed, like a fish under ice.

"You'll be wanting beds," she said. "There's a room at the back. I'll send my boy up with water." And then, so low Juniper almost missed it beneath the clatter of the room: "Don't let her speak the old words after dark. Not here. Please."

*Please.* The word cracked the woman's blankness down the middle, and through the crack Juniper saw something that made her breath catch: grief. Old, walled-up, load-bearing grief, the kind people build their whole lives around and call by another name.

"Whose words?" Juniper asked softly.

The innkeeper's jaw tightened. "No one's. There's no such country." She took the coins and turned away, and her shoulders were rigid as a fence post, and Juniper understood that she had just watched a woman hold a door shut from the inside.

* * *

The room at the back was clean and narrow, with two beds and a window overlooking the village green. Juniper unpacked her tools while Wren curled on the far bed, watching her with that unblinking attention that had stopped unnerving Juniper somewhere around the third week and become, instead, a kind of companionship.

"There are bones here," Wren said.

Juniper's hands stilled. "You can tell?"

"The way you can tell rain is coming." Wren touched her own wrist, where the little carved beads sat — beads Juniper had made her early on, each one holding a fragment of the night the Veil came. "Under the ground. Under the church floor. A lot of them. And they're quiet, but it's not resting-quiet. It's *holding*-quiet. Like children told to be silent or else."

Juniper felt the truth of it before the girl finished speaking. Her gift had been a murmur all her life, a tide of the dead at the edge of things; in Velland's half-country it had become a choir. Here it was neither. Here it was a held breath, hundreds of them, sustained for decades.

She took out a plain rib-bone — uncarved stock, bought honestly from a butcher in the last named town — and laid it on the table between her palms. Just to listen. Just to take the temperature of the place.

The bone spoke, and what it said was a name.

Half a name. A shape of a name, the way a footprint is the shape of a foot. It rose up through the bone like a bubble through dark water and broke against the inside of Juniper's skull, and behind it came not a voice but a *hole*, a silence shaped exactly like a person, and the silence was screaming without sound because sound required a name and the name had been taken.

Juniper dropped the bone. It rolled off the table and under the bed, and the screaming-shape-silence cut off as if a door had closed on it.

"Juniper?" Wren was on her feet.

"It's nothing." Her hands were shaking. She pressed them flat to the table. "Wren, how many people live in this village?"

"I don't know. Sixty? Seventy?"

"And how many graves are under the church?"

Wren tilted her head, listening in whatever way she listened. "More than sixty," she said slowly. "More than seventy. There are graves under the graves. Graves under the *garden*." Her eyes widened. "Juniper — nobody here has died recently. Nobody here has died in forty years. But the ground is full."

Full of the dead who had surrendered more than their memories. Full of the dead who had surrendered their names — and a person who lost their name, Juniper knew now, did not pass cleanly through death. They stuck. They lingered, nameless, in the soil of the place that had erased them, holding their silence like children told to be silent or else.

A knock at the door made them both jump.

It was the innkeeper's boy, as promised, with a jug of water — but behind him, half-hidden in the stairwell shadow, stood a man. Tall, stooped, maybe fifty, with the kind of face that had been handsome once and had since been lived in hard. He held his hat in both hands, wringing it, and he was staring past the boy at Wren with an expression Juniper had seen only once before: on the face of a drowning man watching a rope.

"Da," the boy said. "I brought the water."

"Good lad. Go down. Mind the stew." The boy went. The man did not move, except to step into the room, and Juniper saw that his hands were shaking worse than hers had been.

"You're the bone-woman," he said. His voice was rough, half-swallowed, the voice of a man who had spent years rationing his own words. "The carver. People in the valley talk, even people who've sworn not to. You carve names into bone and the dead can—" He stopped. Started again. "My name is Aldric. My name is Aldric Voss—"

He caught himself, appalled, as if the surname had escaped a cage.

"—Aldric. Just Aldric. Everyone here is just Aldric, just Marta, just Bren. We gave the rest up. We *chose*—" The word snagged. He said it again, louder, like a man repeating a lesson. "We chose this. Forty years ago, when the grey ones came through the valley offering terms, we chose. Give us your memories of the country and the Veil passes over you. Every house that signed lives still. Every house that refused—" He gestured vaguely east, toward the empty hills, toward the villages on Maren's map that were no longer on any map at all.

"You signed," Juniper said.

"I signed." He said it the way a man says *I ran*. "I signed for all of us. For my wife. For my son." His eyes went to Wren again and Juniper saw him physically flinch from what looking cost him. "For my daughter."

The held-breath silence beneath the church shifted. Juniper felt it turn toward the doorway like flowers toward sun.

"She was six," Aldric said. "Her name was—" His mouth opened. Nothing came. His mouth opened again, working, and what came out was a dry clicking sound, and then tears, sudden and furious, running down a face that seemed astonished by them. "Forty years," he whispered. "I have tried to say her name for forty years and it isn't *gone*, do you understand? It's *walled off*. I can feel the wall. Some nights I stand at it and press my forehead against it and I know — I *know* — there's a whole person on the other side. A little girl who called me Da. Who had a laugh like—" His fist clenched. "Like something. Like something I can't have back."

"Why come to me?" Juniper asked, though she already knew, and dreaded knowing. "If the Veil took the memory, I can't return it. I'm not a thief of the Veil's stores. I carve what remains. I keep what's given me. I don't manufacture."

"I'm not asking you to give her back to *me*." Aldric crossed the room and knelt — knelt, a gray-haired man on a stranger's floor — and held out his hands, palms up, the way one begs or prays. "I'm asking you to carve her name into bone. Whatever's left of it. The shape of it. The hole where it was. Let me hold it. Let me hear it once before I die, even if hearing it costs me—" He swallowed. "They told us, when we signed. They told us the terms. If we ever seek the names again, the protection ends. The Veil will finish what it started. Every soul in this village who signed becomes fair game. My wife. My son. My neighbors. All of them, for one name."

The room was very quiet. Downstairs, faintly, the ordinary sounds of the inn went on — dishes, voices, a chair scraping — the sounds of people performing ordinariness as a discipline, as devotion, as armor.

"Do you understand what you're asking?" Juniper said. "Not for yourself. For everyone."

"Yes." He said it instantly, terribly, without hesitation, and that was the worst part. "That's why I haven't asked for forty years. That's why I've pressed my forehead against the wall and walked away, every night, every night, every night." He looked up at her, and his eyes were red-rimmed and absolutely steady. "But then she walked in." He nodded at Wren. "A child, barefoot, out of the east, with the old words in her mouth. And I thought: the terms are already breaking. The Veil is already hunting her — I saw the grey ones on the ridge two nights past, following your road like smoke follows fire. The bargain is dying anyway. And if it dies — if it dies regardless — then I want my daughter's name. I want it out of the ground and into the light. I want to say it once, aloud, before whatever comes."

Juniper looked at Wren. Wren looked back, and there was something in the girl's face Juniper couldn't read — not fear, not pity. Recognition, perhaps. One child traded through a crack, meeting the father of a child who had never gotten a crack at all.

"Your daughter," Juniper said carefully. "Is she buried under the church?"

"No." Aldric's voice frayed. "She's not buried anywhere. When we signed, the grey ones took the memories *out* of us — and the parts of us that lived in those memories went with them. My daughter wasn't killed. She was *unwritten*. There's no body. There's no grave. There's a space in this village where a girl should have grown up, and everyone walks around it, and no one knows they're walking around it, and the ground under the church is full of everyone's unwritten dead because a nameless soul can't cross over. They stay. They stay and they wait and they hold their silence because that's the last thing they were ever told to do."

*Holding-quiet,* Wren had said. *Like children told to be silent or else.*

Juniper looked down at her own hands. Carver's hands. Ledger-keeper's hands. Hands that had spent a lifetime balancing accounts between the living and the dead, and had always believed the ledger was honest — that she recorded what remained, that she stole nothing, that her gift was a service rendered fairly on both sides of the veil between worlds.

But standing in this room, above a churchyard packed with the unwritten, she understood for the first time that some debts could not be balanced. Some entries could not be carried forward. Some names, once sold, stayed sold — unless someone was willing to buy them back at a price no ledger could itemize.

One name against seventy souls. Mercy against safety. And the man kneeling on the floor had already done the arithmetic forty years ago and was doing it again now, and arriving, both times, at his daughter.

"I won't decide tonight," Juniper said at last. "And I won't decide alone. There are others whose existence hangs on the answer — including hers." She nodded at Wren, and Aldric bowed his head as if struck. "Sleep. Tomorrow I'll go down among your dead and ask them what they want. It's their silence too. Maybe — maybe they've kept it all these years not because they were told to, but because they were *waiting*. Waiting for someone who could carry the names out safely. Waiting for a ledger."

Aldric rose slowly, an old man's rise, though he cannot have been older than Juniper's mother would have been. At the door he paused.

"Bone-woman. If you ask them — if you truly ask them — you'll hear it. Under everything. Even the ones who aren't mine." His voice dropped to almost nothing. "They all say the same thing. Every night, into the ground, into my dreams, into the walls. Three words. I can't translate them anymore — the words went with the rest — but I remember the *shape* of them, the weight. And the weight is: *we were here.*"

He left. The latch clicked. Outside, the wind moved through the bell-less tower, and somewhere below the floorboards, beneath the garden rows, beneath forty years of chosen silence, the dead of the village turned in their sleepless holding and — Juniper would have sworn it — leaned closer.

* * *

She did not sleep. She sat by the candle with her tools laid out and her grandmother's torn ledger page beside them, and she read the recurring word until it blurred: *price, price, price.*

Near midnight, Wren sat up in her bed.

"Juniper." The girl's voice was strange — not frightened, not dream-touched. Clear. Awake-clear. "Come to the window."

Juniper joined her. Below, the village green lay silvered under a thin moon, and at its edge, where the road climbed east toward the erased capital, three figures stood in a row. Grey. Faceless. Still as fence posts.

Not moving. Not yet. Simply waiting, the way creditors wait, the way winter waits — patient, certain, already counting what they were owed.

"They're not here for the village," Wren said softly. "The village already paid." Her hand rose to the beads at her wrist, and in the moonlight Juniper saw that the girl was trembling — not with fear, but with something larger moving underneath it, something rising like water behind a dam. "They're here for me. And Juniper — I think I finally remember why."

She turned, and her eyes in the dark were wide and ancient and six years old all at once.

"I know where the Door Out stands," Wren said. "I was born close enough to hear it breathing."
