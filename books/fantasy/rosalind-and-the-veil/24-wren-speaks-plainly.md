## 24. Wren Speaks Plainly

Wren had been counting the days the way Juniper counted coins — carefully, in little stacks, so that none of them could roll away and be lost.

Forty-one days since the ferryman's boat. Thirty-eight since the fields that answered to old names. Twelve since the sunken cathedral where Juniper had carved until her fingers bled, and Wren had sat among the dead with her hands folded like a good girl while hundreds of voices pressed against her skin, wanting things, wanting everything, wanting her to speak.

She was tired of being a thing that wanted things.

Tonight they slept in the loft of a barn that might have been Velland once, or might only have been near it. The beams were black oak, older than any tree Wren had ever seen standing, and when she laid her palm flat against them she felt a faint hum, like a word held in a closed mouth. Juniper said it was nothing. Juniper said a lot of things were nothing lately, in the careful voice she used when she was lying kindly.

The moon came through a gap in the slates and lay across the floorboards like a bone someone had set down and forgotten.

Wren got up. She was very good at getting up quietly; she had learned it before she learned anything else, back in the place where quiet was how you stayed alive. She gathered her beads from around her neck — the ones Juniper had carved for her, each one holding a scrap of somebody — and held them in both fists, and went down the ladder one rung at a time, testing each step before she trusted it.

Juniper was asleep by the banked fire, curled on her side with her ledger bag under her arm even now, even asleep, the way other people held pillows. Her hands were bandaged. The bandages had small brown spots on them, like seeds.

Wren stood over her for a long time.

*Tell her,* said the beads, in a hundred small voices at once. *Tell her tell her tell her.* They always talked like that, the dead. Like starlings. Like weather.

*I'm going to,* Wren told them, crossly, inside her head where no one but Juniper could hear, and Juniper couldn't hear this, not really, not yet.

Because here was the thing. Here was the thing Wren had been carrying since the night of the smoke, since the nightmare that had filled their shared room with remembered burning, since Juniper had held her and touched the beads and seen fragments — only fragments, pieces of a picture with its middle torn out. Juniper thought Wren had been born outside the Veil and carried in. Juniper thought Wren was a refugee who had escaped, the way her grandmother had escaped, the way Maren had escaped.

Juniper was wrong, and the wrongness was starting to hurt them both, because you cannot find a door if you do not know which wall it is in.

Wren sat down on the floor across from the sleeping woman, cross-legged, beads in her lap, and waited for morning the way she had once waited out a whole winter in a root cellar: patiently, hungrily, listening to the dark.

* * *

Morning came grey and smelling of rain that hadn't fallen yet. Juniper woke the way she always did — all at once, hand already reaching for the ledger bag — and found Wren sitting three feet away, watching her.

"You'll ruin your eyes," Juniper said, voice thick with sleep. "Staring at people."

"I don't blink enough," Wren agreed. "Mama said so."

It was the first time she had said *mama* out loud in forty-one days. She watched the word land on Juniper like a stone dropped into still water, rings spreading outward through the woman's whole face.

"Your mama," Juniper said carefully. She sat up, wincing at her hands. "In your dreams. The city that burned."

"Not dreams." Wren turned a bead between her fingers. It was the smallest one, the one carved from the rib of a woman whose name meant *morning*. "Memories. There's a difference. Dreams are made up. Memories are what's left when everything else is taken away." She had learned that sentence somewhere — from the dead, probably, or from the bones themselves. It sounded older than she was. Most of what she said sounded older than she was. People found it unsettling. Juniper had mostly stopped finding it unsettling, which was one of the reasons Wren had decided to tell her.

"Wren." Juniper rubbed her face. "If something's been frightening you—"

"Everything frightens me," Wren said. "That's not what I want to talk about." She took a breath, the big kind, the kind you take before diving into cold water. "You think I ran away from the Veil. You think I got out the same way the others got out, before it sealed. Through the edges. Over the river. That's what Maren thinks too. That's what everyone would think, if there were anyone left to think it."

"Aren't you?" Juniper asked. And then, because she was Juniper, and because she had spent her whole life learning to read the true shape of a question underneath its words: "No. You're not. Are you."

"No," Wren said.

* * *

The rain began while she talked, soft against the slates, filling the silences the way rain does — not erasing them but making them into a shape you could rest inside.

"I was born inside," Wren said. "Not near inside. Inside inside. In the capital — the real one, the one that isn't on maps anymore because there isn't a map to be on. In the house behind the chandlery on Candlemakers' Row, third door from the corner, where the step is worn down in the middle because everyone who ever lived there stepped in the same place for two hundred years." She said it fast, all in one breath, because if she stopped she would start crying and she had decided not to cry until afterward. "I know the smell of it. Tallow and wet stone and the bread they baked on Thursdays that had caraway in it. I know the sound of the bells. There were nine bells and one of them was cracked and nobody ever fixed it because the crack made it sound like it was laughing, and the whole city loved that bell best. Its name was Little Merrow."

Juniper had gone very still. Stillness was a thing she was good at; Wren had seen her sit motionless for hours over a piece of bone, waiting for the name inside it to show itself. But this was different. This was the stillness of someone trying not to make a noise that would stop a child from talking.

"The Veil came when I was four," Wren went on. "Or — no. That's wrong. The Veil came before I was born. Everyone says it came all at once, one terrible night, fire and forgetting, and maybe it did come like that for the ones who ran. But for us — for the ones who stayed — it came slowly. Like frost coming up through a window. First the far roads got hard to remember. Then the names of the towns next door. Then the words started going out of people's mouths — you'd reach for *bread* and find only the shape of reaching. My mother wrote words on slips of paper and put them in jars, all over the house, so we could open a jar and remember what a spoon was called. What Thursday was called. What *love*—" Her voice caught. She turned the bead faster. "What love was called."

"What was it called?" Juniper whispered.

"*Sennet.*" The word came out of Wren whole and warm, and she felt it leave her like a bird leaving a cage she'd kept it in too long. "Love was *sennet*. And the country — the country's true name, the deep one, the one the Door wants — I know that too. I've always known it. That's why they sent me."

"They," Juniper said. "Who sent you?"

"My mother. And the others. The ones who were left." Wren looked down at her fists, at the beads strung between them, each one a person, each one a name Juniper had given a resting place. "You have to understand what it was like at the end. The Veil wasn't eating us all at once. It was eating the edges first, and then the middle, and the middle was where we lived, and by the time I was six the city was mostly gone — not destroyed, *unmade*, streets turning to grey nothing when you looked away from them too long — and the people who were left had figured out the truth, the terrible truth, which is that the Veil wasn't a punishment that had been done to Velland. It was a mouth. And mouths close."

She looked up. The loft light made her eyes look older than eight, older than eighty, older than the black oak beams.

"There's a Door Out. You already knew that — the dead told you, at the bone-yard. *The Door Out stands where the name stood.* Everyone thought that was a riddle. It's not a riddle. It's directions." She tapped the floorboard beside her, twice, deliberate. "The name of the country used to be spoken from the palace steps every year, on Founding Day, by whoever wore the crown. The whole city came to hear it. The name lived in that stone the way heat lives in a hearthstone after the fire goes out. So that's where they built the Door. Under the palace. Under the erased capital. It's been there the whole time. It's there *now*."

"And it opens," Juniper said. Her voice had changed — Wren heard it go thin and careful, the voice of a woman walking on ice. "Maren said it opens once a generation. Soon."

"Soon," Wren agreed. "And here's the part I should have told you forty-one days ago, and didn't, because I was afraid, and because the dead told me not to, and because I wanted—" Her breath hitched. "I wanted to be a girl in a barn for a little while longer. Just a girl. Not a key."

Juniper crossed the space between them and knelt, ignoring what it must have cost her bandaged hands, and took Wren's face in her palms the way you hold something that might fly apart.

"Wren. Whatever you are, you're a girl first. That's not negotiable. I'm putting it in the ledger."

Despite everything, despite the weight in her chest, Wren almost laughed. "You can't put me in the ledger. I'm not inventory."

"You're family," Juniper said. "Family goes in ledgers. That's the whole point of them." But her thumbs were trembling against Wren's cheeks, and her eyes were wet, and she said, "Tell me the rest. The sending. How did you get out, if the Veil had sealed?"

* * *

So Wren told her, and the telling was the hardest work she had ever done, harder than walking, harder than silence.

"It was cracking," she said. "The Door. As the Veil finished swallowing, as the last of the country went grey, the Door started to crack along its seams — like an egg, my mother said, like an egg that something inside is pushing out of. And the cracks let out little winds, and the winds smelled like outside, like rain on roads that still had names. And my mother — my mother who had jars of words on every shelf — she carried me down through the cellars, past the chandlery, down the old stairs under the palace, and the dead were all around us by then, thousands of them, thick as fog, and they were singing. Not sad songs. *Directions.* They sang me to the crack."

She was shaking now. Juniper pulled her in, and Wren spoke into her shoulder, muffled, fast, because stopping was no longer possible.

"I was the last child born inside. Do you understand? Everyone younger than me was already unmade, or never born at all, because the Veil doesn't just erase the past — it erases the *future*, it reaches forward and unhappens things. I was the newest thing in the whole country. The freshest memory it had. And a child born inside the Veil carries the country in her the way an egg carries a bird. My mother pushed me through the crack and the crack was closing and it took — it took—" Her breath broke entirely, and for a moment she was only a small girl sobbing, and Juniper held all of her, rocking, saying nothing, because there was nothing to say that wasn't a lie.

When Wren could speak again, her voice was scraped hollow.

"It took my growing. I came out eight years old and I will always be eight years old, more or less, because the years I should have lived on the other side got spent squeezing through. And it took most of my memories — that's why they're broken, why they come as smoke and fragments and nightmares instead of whole. My mother kept the important ones safe for me. She put them somewhere. She—" Wren lifted the beads in her fist, and the smallest bead, the one carved from the rib of the woman named *morning*, seemed suddenly to weigh as much as a stone. "You carved these from bones the dead gave you. But you never asked why the dead chose *those* bones, out of all the bones in the yard. Did you?"

Juniper went white. "No," she said slowly. "They simply... offered them. Pressed them at me. I thought it was grief. I thought they wanted to be remembered."

"They did," Wren said. "But they also wanted to give me back what the crack took. Every bead you've carved me holds a piece of my own memory, Juniper. My mother arranged it before she — before the Veil finished. The dead have been carrying my childhood for eight years, waiting for a bone-carver with Ravensworth hands to come along and give it a shape I can wear." She opened her fist. "That's why I can't say the country's true name yet. Not properly. Not all the way through. The name is in the beads too — the biggest piece of it — and I only have it in pieces. But when the Door opens, I won't need to carry it whole. I'll need to *give* it. Speak it into the lock. And speaking it will spend me, the dead say. All of me. A name that big needs a whole person to carry it through a mouth-shaped lock, and I am—" she spread her small hands, "—not very much person."

The rain had stopped. In the wet quiet, Juniper's breathing was ragged.

"No," she said. Flatly. Finally. The way she said things she had entered in a ledger in ink. "No. We'll find another way. There's always another entry. There's always—"

"That's what I need to tell you," Wren said, and her voice steadied, because this last part was not hers to be frightened of; it was only hers to deliver, like a letter, like a debt. "There isn't another way. But there is a *next* way. The Door opens soon, Juniper — the dead feel it opening the way old people feel a storm in their joints. And the road to the erased capital runs through country where the Veil is thickest, and we are being followed — you know we're being followed, the grey men, the wardens — and I can feel them getting closer every day, like cold coming under a door." She reached out and took Juniper's bandaged hand, careful of the hurts. "So we have to go *now*. Today. Before I finish becoming brave enough to be afraid."

Juniper looked at her for a long moment — at the small girl with the old eyes and the string of borrowed childhoods around her neck — and then she did the thing Wren had hoped for and dreaded in equal measure. She reached for her ledger bag, drew out the book, licked her thumb, and turned to a fresh page.

"Destination," she said, writing aloud as she went, her voice rough but level. "One erased capital. One Door Out, located beneath the palace, access via cellars behind the chandlery on Candlemakers' Row, third door from the corner. One key, aged eight, non-negotiable, family." Her pen paused. "Terms of payment: to be negotiated, and I warn you now, Wren Ravensworth, I intend to negotiate *hard*."

"Ravensworth," Wren repeated. It was the first time Juniper had given her the name out loud. It settled over her like a coat that almost fit.

"You'll grow into it," Juniper said, closing the book. Then she frowned, tilting her head toward the barn's great doors, where the morning lay pale and silent. "Wren. When did the birds stop?"

Wren listened. The rain had ended, and the world beyond the barn was utterly, perfectly still — no rooks, no sparrows, no wind in the wet grass. Only a faint grey movement at the edge of the field, tall and faceless, patient as frost.

"They're here," Wren whispered, and closed her hand around her beads.
