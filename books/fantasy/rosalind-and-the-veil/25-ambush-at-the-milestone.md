## 25. Ambush at the Milestone

The milestone stood where the road bent toward the river, a squat grey finger of stone worn smooth by centuries of cartwheels and weather. Juniper had noticed it the evening before, when the light was kind, and had thought nothing of it. A marker was a marker. Roads were full of them.

She thought differently about it now, in the grey hour before dawn, with Wren asleep against her shoulder and the mist lying on the fields like something drowned.

Because the milestone had no number on it.

She had looked twice, three times, running her thumb over the stone's face in the dark. Every milestone she had ever passed in her life bore its distance to some town or crossing — *Twelve miles to Harrow Ford*, *Nine to the Salt Bridge* — carved deep so that no rain could take it. This one was blank. Not eroded. Blank, the way a page is blank when someone has scraped it clean with a knife. And around the scraped place, faint as breath on glass, she could feel the same pressure she felt near Wren's carved beads: a hum, a leaning, as though the stone remembered having said something and was straining to say it again.

The Veil did not only erase countries, she understood now. It erased the roads that led to them. It erased the distances. Somewhere under her palm, this stone had once told travelers how far it was to Velland's border towns — and it had been made to forget.

"Juniper." Wren's voice, small and sleep-roughened. The girl had woken without moving, the way she always did, eyes open before her body admitted it. She was looking at the milestone too. "It's wrong."

"I know."

"It used to say something." Wren slid out from under Juniper's arm and stood, barefoot in the wet grass despite every argument Juniper had ever won about boots. She pressed both hands flat against the stone and closed her eyes, and her lips moved — shaping sounds too soft for Juniper to catch, though some part of her caught them anyway, the way one catches a tune half-remembered from childhood. "There was a village," Wren whispered. "Aldenmere. It was four miles that way." She pointed east, into the mist. "The milestone said so. Four miles to Aldenmere, and Aldenmere had a bell tower you could see from here, and on feast days they rang it wrong on purpose because the priest liked the sound of mistakes."

Juniper felt the words go through her like cold water. Not because of the village. Because of the way Wren said *used to*. A child of perhaps nine years, speaking of a country forty years dead as if she'd walked its roads yesterday — which, in the manner of things inside the Veil, perhaps she had.

"And now?" Juniper asked gently.

Wren took her hands off the stone. Her fingers left no prints in the damp. "Now there's just road," she said. "Road going nowhere, because everywhere it went is gone."

They broke their fast on hard bread and the last of Maren's honey, and Juniper tried not to think about how little of the honey remained, or about the old woman who had pressed the jar into her hands three days ago along with the stitched map, saying, *I'm too old for roads. But I remember all of them.* Juniper had believed her. She had believed the map too — believed it enough to trust her life and Wren's to its inkless stitches, its towns marked in knots of thread, its rivers in lines of blue silk. Maren had sewn Velland out of her own memory, stitch by stitch, an act that must have cost her more than any oath-breaking, and had done it smiling, with flour still on her apron.

Juniper checked the map now, holding it low, though there was no one on the road to see. According to the threads, they were two days from the river. Two days from the ferryman and his terrible toll. Two days from the country that was and wasn't.

She should have known two days was a luxury the Veil would never grant them.

* * *

The wardens came at midmorning, out of a stretch of road that had been empty a moment before.

Juniper heard them first as a silence — not the absence of sound but the presence of it, a silence with weight and texture, like wool packed in the ears. The birds stopped. The wind stopped. Even the stream beside the road seemed to run quieter, apologetic. Wren went rigid against her side, and Juniper followed the girl's stare to the crest of the low hill behind them.

Three figures stood there. Grey, all of them, grey as the mist they'd walked out of, and shaped like people the way a glove is shaped like a hand — correctly, but with nothing inside. They had no faces. Where faces should have been there was only smoothness, the smoothness of stone worn by water, and yet Juniper knew, with a certainty that lived in her bones rather than her mind, that they were looking at Wren.

"Wren," she said quietly. "Behind me. Don't run. Running is a thing they follow."

The girl obeyed, pressing close, her small fists knotted in Juniper's coat. Juniper's own hands were not steady, but they found the satchel strap, the bone-handled knife, the small carved pieces she carried always now — a rib inscribed with a name, a knuckle-bone blank, the stylus. Her gift had been quiet since they crossed the last inhabited mile; the dead of these nameless fields kept their distance, thin and scattered. There was no choir here to call on. There was only her, a child, and three faceless things descending the hill at a pace that never hurried and never slowed.

"What do they want?" Wren breathed.

"You," Juniper said. "What you carry. What you are." She had learned enough from Maren, from the elders' terror, from the dead themselves: the wardens unmade spoken memory. They were the Veil's hands, its brooms, its clerks. And Wren was a living ledger of everything the Veil had spent forty years trying to burn.

The first warden reached the bottom of the hill.

And then, from somewhere behind them — from the direction they had come, from the road back toward the capital and everything sane — came a voice.

"*No.*"

It was an old voice, cracked and ordinary, and it cracked further on the word, because the woman speaking it knew exactly what the word cost. Juniper turned, and her heart did something complicated and painful, because there on the road stood Maren — Maren who had said *I'm too old for roads*, Maren in her shawl and her broken shoes, breathing hard from a journey no woman her age should have attempted, carrying nothing but a walking stick and a fury Juniper could see from thirty paces.

"Maren, no—" Juniper started forward. "*Go back.*"

But the old woman was already walking past her, past Wren, straight down the center of the road toward the wardens, and her voice rose as she went, and what came out of her was not Common at all.

It was Vellish.

Juniper understood it — that was the horror of it, she understood every word, words she had never been taught rising in her like water finding an old channel — and she understood, too, what Maren was doing. The oath. The sworn silence, enforced by the Veil itself; break it and be unmade. Maren had held that silence for forty years. Forty years of biting her tongue in market squares, of singing only in her head, of letting her grandchildren grow up tongue-tied and safe. And she was spending all of it now, in one breath, for a child she had known for five days.

The first warden stopped. The other two stopped. Even the Veil, it seemed, paused to listen.

Maren spoke the way she had sewn the map — steadily, lovingly, naming things. She named the road they stood on, and the road gained a name; Juniper felt it settle into the stones beneath her feet like a bone settling into flesh. She named the stream, and the stream ran louder. She named the milestone — *four miles to Aldenmere, and Aldenmere's bell tower, and the priest who loved the sound of mistakes* — and the blank stone under Juniper's earlier touch seemed to warm, to strain, almost to speak.

She was rebuilding the world aloud. Stitching it back with her voice the way she'd stitched it with thread. And the wardens stood frozen before her, faceless heads tilted, and Juniper realized with a lurch that they could not stop her — that the Veil's own law bound them as it bound her, that an oath freely broken must be permitted to break, that this was the price and the price was being paid and even the Veil's clerks had to stand aside while the account settled.

"Maren," Juniper whispered. "Maren, stop, please—"

But the old woman had turned, then, just for a moment, to look back at them. At Juniper. At Wren, whose face was wet and whose mouth was open around words she didn't know how to say. And Maren smiled — smiled the way she had smiled handing over the honey jar, warm and unhurried, a woman finishing a piece of work she'd started long ago.

"*Listen,*" she said, in Common now, plain and clear, so there could be no mistake. "Remember what I say. Remember me saying it. That's all any of us are, girl — what someone remembers. So remember."

Then she drew one breath, the deepest of her life, and began the sentence she must have been carrying for forty years, the sentence the whole silent generation of Velland had been carrying, and she gave it to the morning in her own mother's tongue:

"This land is called—"

The wardens moved.

Not toward her. Into her. All three at once, folding around her like grey water closing over a stone, and where they touched her she came apart — not bloodily, not painfully, that was the unbearable mercy of it — she came apart the way fog comes apart, the way a name comes apart when everyone agrees to stop saying it. Her shawl drifted down empty. Her walking stick clattered onto the road. And the last of her, the very last, was her voice, continuing the sentence in fragments that grew thinner and thinner, syllables scattering like birds:

"—called Vel— called— called—"

And then there was no Maren. There was a shawl, a stick, a jar of honey rolling slowly off the roadside into the grass, and three grey figures standing motionless among the leavings, their work done, their faces smooth as ever.

Juniper did not remember screaming. She found herself on her knees with her throat raw, so she must have. Wren was sobbing against her, great silent child-sobs, and Juniper gathered her in and held her and watched the wardens over the girl's head with a hatred so pure it frightened her.

The wardens turned. Began, unhurried, to advance.

And that was when Juniper understood the second thing Maren had bought with her life — the thing the old woman had known and she had not. Because the road had a name now. Maren had given it one, in the moments before the end, and names were doors, and doors opened both ways. Along that newly-named road, thin as smoke but coming, coming fast, came the dead — the scattered, distant dead of the nameless fields, drawn by the sound of their language spoken aloud, by the first Vellish sentence the air had held in forty years. Juniper felt them arrive the way one feels a crowd enter a room behind one's back.

She snatched up the stylus. She pressed it to her own forearm, to the skin over the bone, and she spoke — haltingly, terribly, in an accent that would have made Maren laugh — the fragment Maren had left behind.

"*Called,*" she said. "*This land is called.*"

The dead came through her like a flood through a broken weir. She did not carve; there was no time to carve. She simply opened, and let them pour, and whatever the wardens met as they reached her was not a tired woman and a weeping child but a country — hundreds of voices speaking at once, in Vellish, the names of fields and rivers and bells rung wrong on purpose, a wall of remembering that hit the grey figures and staggered them. Wardens unmade spoken memory. Perhaps they could be unmade by it too. They recoiled, folded, thinned — and Juniper, not waiting to learn whether it would hold, hauled Wren up and ran.

They ran east, the way the milestone had pointed once. They ran until the road bent and the wardens were out of sight and Juniper's lungs burned and Wren's hand was crushing hers. Behind them, faint across the fields, she thought she heard the dead still singing the unfinished sentence, passing it voice to voice like a bucket line against fire:

"*Called. Called. Called.*"

Holding the place open. Holding the space where the word would go, until someone came who could finish it.

* * *

They stopped at dusk in the shelter of a ruined kiln, and Juniper built a small fire and did small necessary things — bandaged Wren's cut feet, warmed the last of the bread — because grief does not excuse you from keeping a child alive, and because her hands needed tasks or they would shake apart.

Wren sat wrapped in Maren's shawl. It was far too big for her and she had pulled it around herself like a cocoon, and she stared into the flames with red-rimmed eyes.

"She knew," Wren said at last. "When she came. She knew what it would cost."

"Yes."

"Why?"

Juniper fed a stick to the fire. She thought of the honey jar, of the map with its patient knots of thread, of an old woman sewing a country out of her own head by lamplight. She thought of ledgers, and debts, and what it meant to pay one.

"Because some accounts," she said finally, "you don't get to choose whether to settle. You only get to choose when. She chose now, while she still had something left to pay with." She looked at the shawl, at the small fierce face above it. "And because of you. Because you're the last page of a book everyone else was forced to burn. People will do terrible and wonderful things for the last page of a book, Wren. I would know. I've spent my whole life selling pages back to the grieving."

Wren was quiet a long time. Then she reached into the shawl and brought out something Juniper hadn't seen her take: the walking stick. Maren's walking stick, which the girl had snatched up from the roadside without anyone noticing, without anyone knowing why.

"It's ash wood," Wren said. "From a tree in Velland. She told me once — the first day. Everyone's stick came from the home tree of their village. Hers was from Aldenmere." Her jaw set, and she looked suddenly much older than nine. "I'm going to carry it. Until we get there. And then I'm going to plant it, and it's going to grow, because it isn't really dead, it's only been waiting, like everything else."

Juniper looked at the stick — at the smooth handle worn to the shape of an old woman's grip — and felt her throat close. She reached out and closed the girl's fingers back around it.

"Then we'd better get there," she said.

That night, in the ledger she kept now in her head as surely as in her books, Juniper entered the debt. *Maren of Aldenmere. Paid in full: one voice, forty years held, one sentence unfinished.* And below it, in the space where a creditor signs, she wrote the only thing left to write, the thing Maren had told her to write with her dying breath:

*To be remembered.*

She slept badly, and woke before dawn to Wren shaking her, wide-eyed, pointing east through the kiln's broken arch.

The mist had lifted. And there, far off across the fields where no river should have been visible from so many miles away, the morning light had found water — a long bright ribbon stretching north to south as far as the eye could see, glittering like a seam of silver sewn through the land.

The river. Velland's edge. Two days early, and waiting.

And on the near bank, small in the distance, a figure stood beside a moored ferry-boat, watching them come.
