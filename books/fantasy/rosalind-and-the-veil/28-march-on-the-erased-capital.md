## 28. March on the Erased Capital

They buried Maren's boots.

There was nothing else to bury. Where the old woman had stood, mid-sentence, mid-sacrifice, there was only a smooth patch of road dust shaped vaguely like a woman lying down, and even that was already going soft at the edges, already being forgotten by the ground itself. Juniper had knelt and pressed her palms to it and felt nothing — no ghost, no echo, no residue of a soul. The Veil did not leave bodies behind when it unmade someone. It left absences, and absences weighed nothing, and that was somehow worse than any grave she had ever dug.

So they buried the boots, laced together, in the shade of a milestone that read *Velland — 40 miles* in letters no one but Juniper could see anymore. Wren laid a sprig of rowan on top, because Maren had carried rowan, and then the child stood very straight and said something in Vellish, low and formal, the way you'd speak to a judge or a mountain.

Juniper didn't ask what it meant. She suspected it meant goodbye, and she wasn't sure her heart could hold the translation.

* * *

They walked north through country that pretended not to exist.

That was the only way Juniper could describe it. The land itself had not been destroyed — the Veil did not destroy, it *declined*, it *omitted*, it performed the long slow trick of looking away until a thing wore through like fabric rubbed thin. The hills were still hills. The river valleys still held water. But the names were gone from everything, and without names the land seemed embarrassed to be seen, hunching into fog, blurring its own edges, offering itself only in glimpses, like a servant who has been told the house has no need of him.

Wren walked ahead. She always walked ahead now. The child moved through the fog the way other people moved through familiar streets — certain, unhesitating, her bare feet finding paths where Juniper saw only bracken and stone. When the way forked, Wren did not pause. When the fog thickened to wool, Wren simply kept walking, and the fog, grudgingly, made room.

"She remembers," Juniper said to the dead who traveled with them, because by now she had stopped pretending she spoke to herself. "She's not leading us. She's *returning*."

The dead did not answer in words. They never did, not exactly. They answered in pressure, in temperature, in the faint sweet-rot smell of old incense that meant agreement. There were more of them every day. On the first night after Maren's unmaking there had been perhaps a dozen pale shapes keeping pace at the edge of the firelight, patient as cattle. Now Juniper counted them sometimes, out of an old ledger-keeper's habit, and lost count past two hundred. They came out of hedgerows and well-shafts and the collapsed doorways of farms that had no farms attached to them anymore. They came because Juniper carried bone, and bone was the only paper left in Velland on which anything could be written.

She had filled forty-one ribs since the ambush. She worked as she walked, which the dead found acceptable if not ideal, carving names in the old tight script Wren pronounced for her syllable by syllable. Each finished name went into the satchel against her spine, and each name made the satchel heavier in a way that had nothing to do with weight. Some nights she woke with the whole bag humming against her back like a hive of small, patient bees.

"You're doing it wrong," Wren said on the third morning, watching her carve.

"I'm doing it the way I've always done it."

"That's what's wrong." The child crouched beside her, solemn as a tiny magistrate. "You carve for people who are gone. Here, everyone is still *here*. You have to carve like they're listening."

"They're always listening."

"No." Wren put her small hand flat over Juniper's knuckles, stopping the blade. Her fingers were cold; they were always cold; Juniper had stopped remarking on it. "In the capital, they listened. Out here, they're waiting to be heard. It's different. Grandmother Maren said—" The child stopped. Her face did something complicated and young. "Grandmother Maren said the difference between a graveyard and a country is whether anyone is still saying the names out loud."

Juniper looked down at the rib in her hands, half-finished, the name of a miller's daughter dead four hundred years, and understood that she had been writing letters to the dead all this time when she should have been reading aloud.

She started over. And when she spoke the name as she carved it — *Sela, daughter of the mill at Vorn-water, who loved plum wine and hated her own singing* — the fog around their camp drew in close, close, closer, until it pressed against the firelight like faces at a window, and somewhere in the grey a voice that was not quite a voice said, softly, *yes, that one, that is how she was,* and Juniper wept a little and did not stop working.

* * *

The villages began on the fourth day, or rather, the places where villages had agreed to stop being villages.

They came upon the first one at dusk: a cluster of roofless houses around a crooked well, the stones still warm, the hearths still black with recent ash, though Wren said — hesitantly, testing her own memory against the world — that no one had lived here since before she was born. A sign hung by the road, blank on both sides, its paint surrendered. In one doorway, Juniper found a child's chalk drawing of a sun, faded almost to nothing, refusing entirely to disappear.

"Say it," Wren whispered. "If you can find it. Say the name and it will come back."

"I don't know the name."

"Ask them."

So Juniper sat down in the middle of the dead village, took out a fresh-carved rib, and held it up like a candle, and asked. The dead crowded in — not the escort this time but the local dead, the ones who had stayed when the living were made to forget, clinging to their doorframes and wells like barnacles to a hull. They pushed their knowing at her the way drowning men push at a rope. The name arrived in her mind whole and heavy: *Ostrevin.* Village of Ostrevin. Forty-one souls at the last honest census. Famous for goose-fat candles and a bell that rang flat.

She carved it. She spoke it. And the village *breathed.*

It was the only word she ever found for what happened next. The roofs did not rebuild — the Veil was too strong for that, too thorough — but the stones remembered being walls. The well remembered being deep. The chalk sun brightened by one impossible degree. For the space of ten heartbeats, Ostrevin stood in the dusk as a place rather than a rumor of a place, and the dead inside it made a sound Juniper would carry to her own grave: not a cheer, not a sob, but the sound a house makes when a family comes home, the small settling of wood and hope.

Then the fog closed, because the Veil noticed, and the name slid off the world again like water off oilcloth. But the dead of Ostrevin followed them out onto the north road, and they did not stop following, and Juniper understood that she had just recruited an entire village.

"We can't save them all," she said to Wren that night, harsher than she meant, because the sound of the settling house was still in her ears. "I can't stand in every ruin in this country speaking names until I drop. That's not rescue, that's — it's a drop in the ocean, it's—"

"It's a ledger," Wren said.

Juniper stopped.

"Every entry counts," the child said, in the flat reciting tone of someone quoting scripture learned young. "You told me that. First week. You said a debt written down is a debt that can't pretend it was never owed. This is the same. You write the name, the name exists somewhere, and the Veil hates that. The Veil wants everything unwritten." She looked up, and her eyes in the firelight were the exact grey of the fog. "You're not saving villages. You're keeping accounts."

Juniper lay awake a long time after that, listening to two hundred dead breathe their non-breath around the fire, and thought: *I am the last ledger of a country, and I am walking into its capital to file the final entry.*

It should have terrified her. It did terrify her. But underneath the terror, steady as a double-entry column, ran something else — the particular calm of a woman who finally knows her vocation. She had spent her whole life carving other people's griefs into bone and handing them back. This was the same work. It was only the scale that had changed.

* * *

On the sixth day the road gave up pretending and became a procession.

They crested a hill in the grey morning and looked down at the valley road, and the road was full. Not of the living — the living had abandoned these miles decades ago, and the few farmsteads they passed stood empty with their doors respectfully shut, as if the houses themselves had stepped out to give the funeral room to pass. No: the road was full of the dead. Hundreds now, perhaps a thousand, streaming up out of ditches and orchards and the sunken lanes of forgotten hamlets, falling into step behind the small figure of Wren Ravensworth-Vellish as though she were a standard-bearer and they her ragged, luminous regiment.

They did not walk like ghosts in the stories. They walked like pilgrims. Old men with their hats in their hands. Women carrying bundles that were certainly nothing and certainly everything. Children — and this was the thing that broke Juniper's composure daily — children running up and down the column, playing tag through each other's insubstantial bodies, shrieking silently, alive in every way except the one that mattered.

At the head of the column, just behind Wren, walked three figures who never strayed far: a broad-shouldered man with a soldier's ruined jaw, a thin woman whose hands were stained the permanent blue of a dyer, and a girl of perhaps sixteen who had died with her hair braided for a dance. They had appointed themselves Wren's honor guard in the first days, and no one had elected them, and no one disputed them.

"The dyer speaks," the soldier rumbled one evening, by way of announcing that the dyer wished to speak, which was how communication with the rank and file worked — the dead borrowed each other's mouths the way poor families borrowed cups. The dyer's voice came out of the soldier's ruined jaw, thin and reedy, a mismatched instrument. "*Lady Ledger. Three miles on, the road forks. The left fork is the true road. The right fork is the Veil's road — it looks newer. It will look newer. Do not take it.*"

"Newer roads are lies," Juniper said. "Noted."

"*Everything new here is a lie,*" the dyer said, with the weary authority of the very old. "*The Veil cannot build. It can only mislabel. Remember that when you reach the city. The city will show you streets that gleam. Those streets go nowhere. The real streets are the broken ones. The real streets are the ones you must name to walk.*"

* * *

The capital announced itself by silence.

They smelled it first — or rather, they smelled the absence of it. Cities have a smell, coal smoke and horse dung and bread and drains, and the land before the erased capital had none, a clean blank odorlessness like the inside of a sealed jar. Then they felt it: a pressure on the ears, a flattening, as though the whole basin ahead of them were held under deep water. Even the dead grew quiet. The great column slowed, bunched, and stopped at the lip of the valley like animals scenting a fire.

Below them, where maps once showed a city of ninety thousand souls — where Wren's fragmented memories showed towers and a river-harbor and a palace with a thousand windows — there was a shallow bowl of grey mist and, showing through it in patches, the suggestion of foundations. Streets that were almost streets. Walls that were almost walls. A skyline that existed the way a word exists on the tip of the tongue: present, known, unreachable.

"There," Wren said. Her hand found Juniper's and gripped it hard enough to hurt. "The palace is there. Under the middle. Under the middle of everything." Her breathing had gone quick and shallow. "The Door is under the palace. I know because I remember the stairs. I remember the stairs because I fell down them."

"You fell down them?"

"When they were taking me to the crack. To send me out." The child's voice had gone small and terrible. "Everyone was running. The Veil was coming down like snow. My mother carried me and my mother's sister carried the box of names, and the stairs were dark, and I fell, and my mother didn't stop, because there wasn't time to stop, because—" She stopped herself the way you'd slam a door. "Under the palace. That's where we're going."

Juniper crouched to her level. Around them the thousand dead waited in absolute patience, and the fog below churned like something digesting.

"Wren. Once we go down there, the Veil will know exactly where we are. There's no crowd to hide in, no border to cross. It's the center of the thing. Do you understand me?"

"Yes."

"And you're not afraid?"

Wren considered this with the gravity she brought to all questions, which was one of the hundred things about her that undid Juniper daily.

"I'm afraid," she said at last. "But Grandmother Maren spent her whole life being safe and quiet, and in the end she got one sentence, and she spent it on us. So being safe isn't the point." She tugged Juniper's hand. "Come on. The streets won't stay named unless somebody says them."

* * *

They entered the city at the old west gate, and the gate existed because Wren said it did.

"*Vessengate,*" the child said, standing before a gap in a wall that was mostly absence, and the word came out of her in Vellish first, round and ancient, and then the air took it and *made it true*: stone assembled itself out of insistence, arches remembering their curve, and for a moment they stood under a real gate with a real keystone, and the dead poured through it in a silent flood, home after forty years, touching the walls with their hands.

But the Veil answered.

It came as cold. Not weather-cold — *judgment*-cold, a cold with intent behind it, rolling down the unnamed boulevards in visible waves that bent the fog into shapes almost like figures. Where it passed, Wren's spoken names frayed and died. *Vessengate* sagged. The rebuilt arch softened, slumped, began forgetting its own curve, and Juniper saw with horror that the battle would be exactly this, street by street, name by name, a war fought entirely in the present tense.

"So we don't stop talking," she said, and pulled out her knife and a fresh rib, and began to carve as she walked, and to speak as she carved, loud, in her plain guild-accented Vellish, every name she had collected in forty-one ribs and forty-one nights: *Sela of the mill at Vorn-water. Ostrevin, famous for candles. The dyer Anthe, whose blue never washed out. The soldier Brannoc, who held the east bridge.* Each name hit the fog like a struck match. Each bought twenty feet of real street.

And the dead took it up. That was the miracle she had not dared hope for. A thousand voices that had been silent for two generations began, raggedly, haltingly, to say their own names, their streets, their city — *this was the Tanner's Row, this was the fountain where I kissed her, this was my mother's window, third floor, the blue shutters* — and where they spoke, stone remembered. The column became a moving island of city, a traveling parish of the restored, advancing boulevard by boulevard through the grey, while the Veil's cold pressed in from all sides like a sea testing a seawall.

Wren walked at the very front, one small hand raised, calling intersections into being. *Korvane Square* — and Juniper flinched at the name, at the ugly coincidence, and the dead laughed their soundless laugh and corrected her gently: *not Korvane, Korevanne, older, better, the merchant families stole their names from our squares and never knew it.* *The Long Stair. The Fishmarket. Weeping Bridge, called that for the rain in the arches, not for sorrow, we were happy here, lady, remember we were happy here.*

They made two miles before sundown. Two miles of screaming stone and resurrected street, and at the end of it, on a rise of rubble that had once been — Wren said, in a voice gone completely quiet — the Palace Steps, the ones she had fallen down, they stopped and looked at what remained.

Ahead of them the fog was different. Thicker. Older. It did not churn; it *waited*. And in its heart, dim as a lamp under blankets, something white glowed — a shape like a threshold, like a doorway, like a mouth about to speak.

Between them and it, arranged across the ruined square in perfect, patient ranks, stood the grey figures. Wardens. Hundreds of them. Faceless, still, and all of them facing the small girl with the bare feet.

Beside Juniper, Wren's cold little hand slipped into hers.

"Don't stop saying the names," the child whispered.
