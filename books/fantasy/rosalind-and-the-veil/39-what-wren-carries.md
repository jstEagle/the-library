## 39. What Wren Carries

The schoolhouse had been a granary once, before the Veil, and the grain-smell still lived in its beams — faint and sweet, like something remembered by the wood itself. Wren stood at the front of the room on a morning in late spring, in the returned capital of Velland, and counted her students the way she had once counted cracks in the guildhall ceiling: seven children, ages four to eleven, sitting on benches that smelled of new-cut pine because everything here was new-cut pine, or new-laid stone, or new-thatched roof, the whole city rising out of its own absence like grass after fire.

Seven children, and every one of them born after the Door closed.

That was the thing that made her chest ache when she let herself think about it plainly, which she tried not to do during lessons because thinking about it plainly made her voice go strange and the smallest girl, Tamsin, would stop mouthing along and just watch her with those grave brown eyes that saw too much for six years old. Every child in this room had been born into a country that did not exist. They were the first generation of Velland in forty years, and they knew nothing, and they knew it, and they came anyway — trudged through mud streets still half rubble, past houses being raised by hands that remembered raising them without remembering how — to sit on hard benches and learn the tongue their grandparents had been sworn to swallow.

"Say it again," Wren said. "Slowly. *Vess-ah-loren.*"

"*Vess-ah-loren*," the room said back to her, ragged, earnest, half of them getting the second syllable wrong in exactly the same way, which meant they'd learned it wrong from each other first and she'd have to unpick it gently, the way you unpick a knot you don't want to break.

"*Loren* is soft," she said. "Like water going over a stone. Not like — " she made the hard sound they'd been making, and they laughed, because hearing your own mistake said back to you by someone who loved you is one of the oldest jokes there is.

Outside the granary's tall windows, the capital went on rebuilding itself in the particular way of places that had come back from somewhere else. The Veil had torn eight months ago. Eight months of names returning to things: the river answering to *Sorrel* again, the great ruined palace giving up its true name the way a sleeper gives up a dream, reluctantly and all at once. Streets existed now where streets had not, because people walked them and called them by name, and the country — patient, starved, remembering everything — grew to meet the calling.

Wren had a theory about that, one she kept mostly to herself. She thought the country had never fully gone. She thought the Veil was a lid, not an erasure, and underneath it Velland had gone on existing the way a seed goes on existing in dry ground — diminished, waiting, holding its shape in miniature. That was why the ruins rebuilt when she spoke. That was why the fields answered to old names. The land had been listening all those years for anyone who could still say the words.

She had been the only one. For a while.

Now there were seven voices in a granary, stumbling through *vess-ah-loren*, and by autumn there would be more, because word traveled in the returned country faster than carts, and mothers brought their children from towns whose names had only come home last month, and grandmothers came too, sat at the back on stools they brought themselves, lips moving silently, following along, weeping quietly into their shawls in the particular way of people tasting something they'd been told they'd never taste again.

Wren had learned to teach around the weeping. You didn't stop. You didn't make it strange. You just kept saying the words, slow and soft as water over stone, and let the weeping be part of the lesson, because it was. Grief was one of the conjugations of joy in a language like this one. She was fairly sure no grammarian had written that down yet. She would, eventually. Someone should write the grammar of Vellish for people who hadn't been born inside the Veil, and she supposed, looking at her own small hands — scarred now, ink-stained, steady on the slate — that someone was going to be her.

The bell she'd hung by the door — a real bell, cast in Sorrel-foundry iron, donated by a ferryman who never explained why he felt he owed her — rang the end of the morning lesson, and the children exploded into motion the way children do everywhere, in every country, under every sky, and Wren stood in the wash of their noise and thought: *this is what I was spent for. This is what it bought.*

She had not died speaking the name. That had been the fear, the price everyone assumed, the arithmetic Juniper had refused to accept. Instead Wren had poured the true name of Velland into thousands of pieces of carved bone, scattered it across so many small memorials that no single Veil could ever swallow it whole, and the spending had happened anyway — but differently. Not her body. Not her memory entire. Something subtler, something she was only now, months later, beginning to map in herself.

She could still speak Vellish. Of course she could; it was the tongue she'd thought in before she had words for thinking. But the language had changed shape in her mouth since the Door. It was no longer only hers. It was becoming everyone's — common, shared, a living tongue instead of a last breath. And in exchange, in trade, in the old guild sense of the word, some other door in her had opened: she dreamed the country now. Not her fragments, not the smoke and the running and her mother's hand pulling hers through a crack in the world. The *whole* of it. Nights, she walked streets that stood complete, saw the palace with its roof on, heard the cathedral choir with all its voices living. She was the only person alive who remembered Velland whole, and she carried it the way a granary carries grain — not to keep, but to give out, season by season, handful by handful, until the sowing was done.

She was teaching the children the market-day song when the door opened and the light changed, and she knew without turning who stood in it, because Juniper Ravensworth had a way of arriving that was less like entering a room and more like a ledger being opened to a marked page.

* * *

Juniper came twice a year now, traveling up from the south where the guildhall stood under its new rules. Wren always knew the visits were coming a day or two early, though she'd stopped trying to explain how. It was a bone-thing, probably. Some residue of the gift that had passed between them in the dark under the palace, when Juniper had offered her whole claim on existence as collateral and the Veil, in tearing, had left threads of itself in both of them. Wren dreamed Juniper's arrival the way she dreamed the whole country: simply, factually, the way you know weather is coming in your knees.

Today Juniper looked well. Older — they were both older, eight months and a war and a resurrection will do that — but well, her hands healed from the bleeding nights in the sunken cathedral, her face carrying the particular calm of a woman who has audited her own soul line by line and found the debts, finally, dischargeable. She wore traveling clothes and dust and stood just inside the granary door with the awkwardness of a woman who has never once in her life known what to do in a room full of children.

"You're in my seat," Wren said, grinning, and pointed to the stool at the back where the grandmothers sat. "That one's Maren's."

Something crossed Juniper's face at the name — grief and gratitude in their old entwined braid — and she went and sat on the dead woman's stool, and Wren turned back to her students and finished the verse.

Afterward, when the children had scattered to their dinners, the two of them walked out into the street together. The capital at midday was a noise Wren never tired of: hammers, cart wheels, someone arguing cheerfully about the price of nails, a dog with opinions. All of it in Vellish now, mostly — the language had come back to the adults in patches, surfacing like fish, words they'd been made to forget rising unbidden when they needed them. The oath was broken. The Veil was torn. The tongue was loose in the world again, and it sounded like this: ordinary, argumentative, alive.

They walked to the river and stood on the bank where the ferryman's boat used to cross, though there was a bridge now, half-built, being raised by men who sang work songs in a language two generations had been forbidden. The Sorrel ran green-gold in the spring light. Somewhere under it, Wren knew, lay the old toll-stones, the place where Juniper had paid her mother's face. They didn't talk about that. They never talked about that. Some entries stayed in the ledger even after the debt was discharged, as record, as witness, as the shape of what a person had survived.

"How many now?" Juniper asked.

"Thirty-one. Counting the ones who come and go." Wren skipped a flat stone; it jumped three times and sank. "And forty-some grandmothers at the back who think I don't notice them learning too."

"They notice you notice."

"I know." She grinned. "It's the best part."

Juniper watched the river. Her profile had gotten quieter over the years, Wren thought — the competent loneliness worn down into something softer, something that let itself be accompanied. "I brought the ledgers," Juniper said. "The old ones. Grandmother's. And the archive copies Corvane sent. I thought — the school should have them. Eventually. When the children are ready."

Wren went still, the way she went still whenever the past handed her something heavy and waited to see if she could hold it. "You're giving me Rosalind's books?"

"I'm giving the *country* Rosalind's books. You're just the shelf they're standing on." Juniper almost smiled. "Besides. You're better at carrying things than I am. Always were."

They stood a while longer. Then Juniper said, casually, in the tone of someone asking about the weather, "Say something. Anything. In it."

And Wren understood, because she always understood Juniper, and her heart did the complicated folding it did every visit, because she knew what was coming and she knew there was no kindness in sparing either of them from it.

So she said it. Softly, facing the water, the way she'd say anything precious: "*Sorrel, sorrel, vessa-loren i malen tor* — " the river's name, the country's name, a blessing her mother had said over bread —

And beside her, Juniper Ravensworth, who had once heard hundreds of dead through a single carved rib, who had read Vellish ledgers in her grandmother's handwriting, who had walked the erased capital naming streets back into existence — Juniper listened to the words with a polite, warm, entirely blank expression, the way you listen to a language you have never heard.

"It's beautiful," Juniper said. "I can hear that it's beautiful. I can't — " She stopped. Started again, steadier. "It's sounds. Lovely sounds. Water over stone, you'd say. But it doesn't *mean*. Not anymore."

"No," Wren said gently. "Not anymore."

Eight months ago, walking out of the tearing Veil, Juniper had paid her collateral. The bargain struck at the Door had taken her gift — the hearing, the bone-voice, the communion with the dead — and it had taken, quietly, without announcement, her understanding too. The language drained out of her the way color drains from a sunset, gradually and then completely, until one morning she'd picked up her grandmother's ledger and found it full of lovely, meaningless marks. She had told Wren about it on the first visit, matter-of-fact, the way she reported everything, prices and losses alike. *I'm deaf to it now. All of it. Consider it itemized.*

But knowing a loss in advance is different from standing next to it on a riverbank while a child speaks the tongue of your ancestors and it passes through you like wind through an open window, touching nothing.

Wren took her hand. Juniper let her — that was new, that letting, and Wren treasured it the way she treasured the bell and the bridge and the smell of grain in old beams.

"Do you want me to translate?" Wren asked. "Every time? I could. I'll always — "

"No." Juniper said it quickly, then softened it. "No. Don't. That's not — " She looked out at the water, and her jaw worked, and Wren waited, because you learned patience in a country that had waited forty years. "When you translate it, it becomes information. When I can't understand it, it stays — " she searched for the word, this woman of ten thousand precise words, and found it, and said it wondering, "— it stays *theirs*. The country's. Not mine to carry anymore. I carried it long enough. I'm — glad. Do you understand? I'm glad I can't understand it. It means it's free of me. It means it belongs to the living now, and the living aren't me."

And that was when Juniper began to cry.

Not the way she'd cried in the cathedral, bleeding over names. Not the way she'd cried at the ferry, paying. This was none of the griefs Wren had catalogued in her — this was something Wren had no entry for, tears with no debt behind them, no price, no exchange. Just a woman standing in the sun beside a river with its right name, listening to a language she could no longer read, and weeping because the not-understanding was itself the proof that everything had worked. That the erasure had been undone so thoroughly that even its witnesses were released from it. That Velland no longer needed Juniper Ravensworth to remember it, because Velland remembered itself.

Wren put her arms around her and held on, this tall strange woman who had stolen her out of a guildhall through humming doors, who had refused to let a child be spent, who had written *debt assumed* in a ledger under the eyes of a god-shaped silence — held her and felt her shake and thought, with a clarity that felt like the old bone-sight: *she's crying the way the grandmothers cry. She's crying because it tastes like something she was told she'd never taste again. Except for her, the taste is the loss itself, and it's sweet, and she can't explain that to anyone but me, and I'm the only one alive who'll understand, and that's all right. That's more than all right. That's what family is for.*

"You did it," Wren said into her shoulder, in Trade-tongue, so there'd be no mistaking it, no veil of beauty over the plainness. "Whatever it costs you to stand there and not know — you did it. It's real. It's *real*, Juniper, and you're the reason, and if the price of that is standing in the sun listening to a pretty song you can't parse, then you got the best bargain anyone ever struck with a Veil, and I will carve that on your headstone myself someday, far away from here, when you're very old and very annoying."

Juniper laughed wetly against her hair. "Itemized," she managed. "You'd itemize my headstone."

"Line by line. With interest."

They stood there until the crying was done, and the river ran, and across the water the half-built bridge caught the light like a sentence half-spoken, waiting for the rest.

* * *

They ate dinner that night at the house Wren shared with three other teachers above the chandler's shop, and afterward Juniper spread the old ledgers on the table — not to read them, she couldn't read them, but to look at them, the way you look at a photograph of someone you loved. Wren translated what Juniper asked about, and only what she asked about, honoring the terms they'd set on the riverbank. Mostly Juniper didn't ask. Mostly she just turned the pages with careful fingers, reading her grandmother's handwriting as pattern rather than meaning, and whatever she found in the shapes of it, she kept behind her eyes.

Near midnight, Wren woke to lamplight still burning downstairs and found Juniper at the table with the oldest ledger open, the one from the year of the Reckoning, and a piece of scrap paper in front of her covered in Juniper's own small, exact script.

"What are you doing?"

"Copying," Juniper said. "By shape. I can't read a word of it, but I can copy the letterforms — muscle memory, maybe, or maybe some of Rosalind is still in these hands." She tapped the page. "I'm making a key. A cipher-key. Word by word, position by position, comparing against the passages you've already translated for me. If I map enough of it — " She glanced up, and there was something in her face Wren hadn't seen since the Door: that ledger-light, that hunger for a balanced column. "I won't ever *understand* it. I know that. But I might be able to *index* it. Build the country a finding-guide to its own memory. A card-catalog for a nation that lost its card-catalog." She smiled, tired and crooked. "Turns out you can take the girl out of the guild — "

"— but the guild stays in the girl," Wren finished, and sat down across from her, and pulled a blank page toward herself. "All right. Tell me what the first word-shape looks like, and I'll tell you what it says, and we'll build your key together. But Juniper — " she caught her hand across the table, over the ghost-writing of a dead traitor and a dead saint who were somehow the same woman, "— when the key is done, it goes to the school. To the children. Promise me. No more Ravensworth vaults. No more private ledgers. Everything this family ever carried goes into the open air where the whole country can reach it."

Juniper looked at her for a long moment. Outside, somewhere in the rebuilt dark, a night-watchman called the hour in Vellish, the words rolling down the streets their grandfathers had named.

"Everything," Juniper said. "I promise. Starting tomorrow — starting with the strangest entry in the whole book." She turned the old ledger toward Wren and pointed to a line near the back, pressed hard into the paper, written in a hand that shook. "This one. I've stared at it for eight months and I can't even guess. The letterforms are wrong, Wren. They're not quite Vellish. It's as if whoever wrote it was — translating *into* the language from something older. Or hiding something inside it."

Wren leaned over the page. The lamplight pooled in the grooves of the furious, frightened script, and as her eyes moved along the line, the granary-warm feeling of her language shifted, cooled, went strange — because Juniper was right, the letters were Vellish and yet not, bent around some older shape beneath them the way a glove bends around a hand, and the hand underneath, half-glimpsed, made the skin of Wren's arms rise.

She knew that hidden shape. She had seen it once before — white bone, under a ruined palace, shaped like a mouth.

"Wren?" Juniper said quietly. "What does it say?"

Wren did not answer. Because the line wasn't a sentence at all, not really. It was a name — a name older than Velland, older than the Veil, a name that the Door Out had been built to lock away rather than open — and it was signed at the bottom, in the shaking hand, with a single word:

*Rosalind.*
