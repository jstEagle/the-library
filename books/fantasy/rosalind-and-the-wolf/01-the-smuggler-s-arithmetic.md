## 1. The Smuggler's Arithmetic

There were three ways to price a road, and Hazel Oyelaran had learned all of them before she was old enough to legally drink in any of the four countries she regularly broke the law of.

The first way was the Crown's way: a road cost whatever the toll said it cost, plus whatever the tax said it cost, plus whatever the customs officer's mood said it cost. This was, in Hazel's professional opinion, not pricing at all but theater — a number dressed up in a uniform and taught to shout.

The second way was the guild's way: a road cost what the guildmaster said it cost, minus whatever you could shave off by knowing a shortcut he didn't. Corvo called this *fraternity*. Hazel called it arithmetic with extra steps.

The third way was her own, and it was the only one that had never once lied to her. A road cost exactly what it could take from you, weighed against what it could give, divided by how much of either you'd survive to collect. Weather was currency. Distance was currency. The mood of a mountain pass in late autumn was very expensive currency indeed, and tonight the Grey Saddle was spending like a drunk lord.

Hazel stood at the mouth of the pass with her collar up and did the sums.

Wind out of the north-northwest, which meant it would funnel through the Saddle rather than over it — bad for lanterns, worse for the mule, who was named Turnip and held theological objections to wind. Snow line sitting low, maybe two hundred feet above them, maybe less; snow meant silence, and silence meant she couldn't hear riders on the switchbacks below. Moon: three days past full, waning, rising late. Which gave her roughly two hours of honest dark, and honest dark was worth more per ounce than anything in the packs.

And the cargo? Forty pounds of contraband so boring it was almost funny. Salted river-eel, taxed to absurdity in the lowlands because some duke three generations dead had once lost a fishing dispute. Somewhere downstream, a man would pay real money for eel that hadn't been bled through a ledger first. Somewhere upstream, a fisherman would eat this winter because of it. Hazel liked jobs like this. Nobody got hurt, nobody got saved, everybody just paid too much for fish. It was smuggling as it was meant to be: honest dishonesty.

She clicked her tongue at Turnip, and they went up.

* * *

The Grey Saddle earned its name honestly. From below it looked like a saddle thrown across the shoulders of the mountains, grey stone under grey sky, and from above — well, nobody sensible looked down from above. The path was old, older than the toll roads, older than the kingdoms that built the toll roads, though if you asked anyone official, the path had no age at all, because officially it did not exist. Officially, the mountains here were empty. Officially, the passes had always been empty. There was a particular kind of emptiness the Crown was fond of, Hazel thought — the kind you maintained with maps that showed nothing and archives that burned conveniently and a silence you could hear if you stood still long enough.

She didn't stand still long enough. Standing still in the high passes invited thoughts, and thoughts were overhead costs.

Instead she counted. It was what she did instead of praying, and it worked better. Steps. Switchbacks. Breath counts between gusts. She kept the numbers the way other people kept saints: reliably, privately, without expecting them to love her back.

Turnip objected to the third switchback, as was traditional. Hazel fed him the dried apple she'd been saving, as was also traditional, and told him, "You are a bad business partner," which was untrue and understood to be untrue by both parties. Turnip was, in fact, her best business partner. He never drank the profits, never talked to guild scouts, and had never once asked her why she lived the way she lived, which put him ahead of every human being she'd known since she was fifteen years old.

Because that was the thing about the mountains, see. They didn't ask. That was the whole appeal. Down in the valleys, people asked questions the way weather happened — constantly, and mostly at the worst possible time. *Where are you from, Hazel? Who taught you your letters, Hazel? Why does a girl who reads three languages walk eel over the Saddle when she could be a clerk somewhere warm?* And underneath those, the question nobody asked out loud but everyone carried in their face like a coin they were waiting to spend: *Who did you leave behind, and do they know where you are?*

Up here there was only the arithmetic. Wind: costly. Path: sound. Cargo: dull. Profit: adequate. Self: unexamined, thank you, and staying that way.

She was halfway across the saddle proper, the world dropped away on both sides into blue-black nothing, when she felt the letter.

It had been riding against her ribs for eleven days. She knew its weight the way she knew the weight of her own knife — precisely, unwillingly, and without ever choosing to. It had come to the waystation at Hollowmere in a bundle of ordinary freight, tucked inside a false bottom of a tea chest, addressed in handwriting that had no business being able to find her.

*R. Fairweather* had written the address. But the hand itself — round-bellied letters, ink pressed hard enough to emboss the paper — that hand belonged to Rosalind. Hedge-witch. Herb-wife. The woman who had taught Hazel her letters, her numbers, her herbs, her stars, and — though Hazel would have bitten her own tongue off before admitting it — most of the arithmetic she used nightly to stay alive. Names are load-bearing, Rosalind used to say. Everything true has weight. Write carefully.

Hazel had called it superstition at fifteen, loudly, in front of company, because at fifteen you are an idiot with excellent diction. Then she had packed a bag in the dark and walked out of the cottage at the edge of the fen, and she had been walking ever since, in a wide circle that never quite came back around.

Eleven days the letter had ridden against her ribs. Eleven days she had done the sums on it and come up with the same answer every time: opening it cost more than leaving it sealed. A sealed letter was just paper. An opened letter was a debt. And Hazel Oyelaran did not carry debts. She carried freight, and freight you could set down.

"You're doing the thing," she said aloud, to herself, because the wind made talking to yourself feel less like madness and more like conversation. "The thing where you argue with a piece of paper."

The piece of paper, being load-bearing, said nothing. But it sat there against her sternum, patient as a creditor, and Hazel found herself doing something she despised: slowing down. Stopping, actually, right there on the spine of the world, with Turnip blowing steam and the snow line glittering above like a promise someone else intended to keep.

She took the letter out.

It was smaller than she remembered. Letters usually were. In your head, a letter from the person you abandoned grows to the size of a door; in your hand, it's barely enough paper to wrap a pastry. The seal was plain tallow wax, no stamp — Rosalind had never owned a stamp, said heraldry was for people afraid of being recognized as themselves. One word was written on the outside, beneath the address, in that round pressing hand:

*When you're ready.*

Which was, Hazel thought with something between fury and admiration, the single most manipulative thing a person could write on an envelope. No threat. No plea. Just permission, offered in advance, which is far harder to refuse than a demand. A demand you could spit at. Permission just sat there, being reasonable, being kind, being *patient*, and patience from Rosalind had always been the worst weapon in her arsenal because it never ran out.

Hazel turned the letter over twice. Held it near her face. Got as far as sliding one thumbnail under the wax.

Then the wind shifted, and her ears — trained by ten years of passes to catch the wrongness in any sound — caught it: a rider, far below, moving fast on the low road. Alone, by the rhythm. Heavy horse. Not guild; guild riders traveled in pairs and complained audibly. Not Crown patrol either; patrols moved slow and sang badly. Someone alone and urgent, in the foothills, at night, heading toward the only waystation within six miles.

Her waystation.

Hazel put the letter away — unopened, still sealed, still weighing exactly what it had weighed, which was everything — and did new sums. Rider: one. Direction: hers. Hour: wrong. And the hour mattered, because nobody traveled to a smuggler's waystation at the wrong hour unless they wanted something they couldn't buy in daylight.

"Come on, Turnip," she said. "Work to do."

Turnip, who had heard the rider too and held opinions about strangers, followed anyway, because that was the arrangement: she supplied apples, he supplied loyalty, and neither of them discussed the terms.

* * *

The waystation at Hollowmere wasn't on any map, which was the point of it. It had been a shepherd's shelter once, then a shrine, then a ruin, then — after Hazel had spent two seasons of profits and most of a summer's swearing on it — a snug stone house with a good chimney, a deep cellar, and a door that locked from the inside with iron honest enough to make a burglar reconsider his career. She'd furnished it sparsely and sensibly: one bed, one cot, one table, chairs enough for a card game, and a shelf of books she pretended were for reading and actually were for pressing flowers and hiding things between pages.

She got the fire low, the lamp lit, the eel hidden, and the kettle on, because whatever came through that door, hospitality was cheaper than hostility and bought better will. Then she sat with her knife loose in its sheath and her boots on, and waited, and told herself the fluttering under her ribs was the cold.

It was not the cold. It was the letter, doing what letters did: making the present feel provisional. Because that was the trap of a thing like that, sealed or otherwise — it implied a future in which it got opened, and a future in which it got opened implied a version of Hazel who had gone home, and a version of Hazel who had gone home implied a version of Hazel who had stayed, and stayed, and learned the herb-lore properly, and never learned to count breaths between gusts because she'd never needed to hide from anyone.

Ridiculous. She was twenty-six years old. She had a trade, a mule, a roof, and a clean ledger. She owed nothing to anybody, which was the closest thing to freedom the world sold, and she'd paid full price for it, and she was not going to let one envelope with a kind word on the outside make her feel —

Three knocks at the door. Slow. Small.

That was wrong. Everything about it was wrong. Grown travelers knocked quick and guilty. Guild men knocked in the guild rhythm, four beats. Soldiers knocked like they were punishing the door. Nobody knocked slowly and softly except children, drunks, and the dead, and the dead generally preferred windows.

Hazel rose, took the lamp, and opened the door on the chain.

The storm had arrived while she wasn't looking — it did that in the high country, arrived like a debt collector, all at once and without manners — and standing in the white churn of it, barefoot on her threshold, was a child.

Small. Seven, eight, maybe. Hood soaked flat, hair plastered down, feet bare and blue on her frozen doorstep and — this was the part that snagged in Hazel's professional assessment like a burr in wool — entirely unafraid. Not shivering-brave, not numb-shocked. Unafraid, the way the mountains were unafraid. The child looked up at her, through the lamplight, with eyes that seemed to hold more night than the sky behind them, and spoke.

The words were nothing Hazel knew. The sounds were old and round and strange, vowels that rolled like stones in a streambed, consonants soft as moss. Any scholar in the lowlands would have shrugged. Any priest would have crossed himself. Any guildsman would have reached for a blade.

But Hazel Oyelaran, who had spent nine years of her childhood at a hedge-witch's knee pretending not to learn, caught one word in the middle of it — one small, round, load-bearing word — and her whole careful ledger of a life tilted sideways.

Because she knew that word. Knew it the way you know a song from before you can remember knowing songs. Rosalind had said it a thousand times, over bread and over graves and over Hazel's own head at night like a blessing: the old tongue, the first speech, the language of a country that the maps had agreed, all together, to forget.

The child said it again, patiently, pointing one small bare foot at the gap beneath the door, at the warmth spilling out, and Hazel understood — the way you understand a debt coming due — that she was being asked, in a language three centuries dead, for shelter.

Behind her, the fire popped. Against her ribs, the letter lay sealed and silent and patient as ever.

And out in the storm, far below on the black road, the heavy rider was still coming.
