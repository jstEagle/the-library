## 2. A Knock at the Wrong Hour

The storm came up the pass an hour after dark, the way storms did in the Thornfell country — all at once and without apology, like a debt collector who'd been waiting politely down the road.

Hazel Oyelaran heard it coming before she saw anything: a low grinding hush in the pines, the sound of a great deal of snow deciding, collectively, to fall. She was in the middle of her evening arithmetic — three coppers for lamp oil, four for the wood she hadn't chopped yet, eleven owed to Marta at the crossroads for flour, minus the two silver pieces sewn into her left boot that were not for spending but for *leaving*, should leaving become necessary — when the first gust hit the waystation hard enough to rattle the shutters on their hooks.

"Right," she told the room. "Everyone inside."

The room contained herself, a kettle, and a cat named Debt who had adopted her two winters ago on the strict understanding that he would never be fed on schedule. Debt opened one eye, assessed the situation, and went back to sleep on the only warm chair. Everyone inside, then, except Debt.

She did the rounds anyway, because rounds were what kept you alive in the high passes. Shutters hooked. Chimney damper set against backdraft. The stable door barred with the good bar, not the decorative one — she'd learned that lesson the expensive way when a mule named Collateral had pushed the decorative one open and spent a night eating an entire winter's worth of hay while looking at her with an expression of profound grievance. The contraband in the cellar floor was already tucked under its false boards; nobody knocked on a smuggler's door during a storm looking for brandy, they knocked looking for warmth, and you could give them warmth without giving them inventory.

That was the whole of smuggling, if you wanted to be honest about it, which Hazel tried very hard never to be. It wasn't daring. It wasn't cunning. It was arithmetic done quickly in bad weather. Every risk had a price, every price could be paid or avoided, and the only unforgivable mistake was forgetting that you yourself were also a line item — one whose value dropped considerably the moment you got sentimental.

She was banking the fire when the knock came.

Three knocks. Small ones. The kind of knocks made by a knuckle rather than a fist, or possibly by someone who had never been taught that doors answered better to fists.

Hazel went still, poker in hand, and ran the hour through her head. It was past midnight. No honest traveler walked the Thornfell passes after dark, and no dishonest one knocked — dishonest ones picked locks, or whistled the guild's three-note signal from the tree line, or simply let themselves in and apologized afterward over your own tea. A knock at this hour, in this weather, from something small enough to use a knuckle, sat so far outside her arithmetic that for a moment she couldn't price it at all.

Which, she would reflect later, should have been warning enough.

She lifted the bar and opened the door six inches, wind snatching the heat straight off her face, and looked down.

A child stood on her step. Barefoot. In the snow. In a storm that had already buried the fence posts to their tops. The child looked perhaps nine years old, though there was something about the face — a stillness around the eyes, the way the snowflakes landed on the dark hair and did not seem to hurry anywhere — that made guessing feel rude. They wore a coat that had once belonged to somebody much larger and much deader, buttoned wrong, and they stood with their hands loose at their sides as though waiting out of doors in a blizzard were a perfectly ordinary way to spend an evening.

"No," said Hazel, and started to close the door.

The child put one bare foot against the threshold — not blocking it, exactly. Just resting it there, the way you'd rest a hand on a table to indicate that the conversation was not yet concluded.

"It's a storm," said Hazel, who was aware even as she said it that she was arguing with a nine-year-old and losing. "You can't be out in it. You especially can't be out in it *like that*. Where are your boots? Where is anybody? Who sent you?"

The child tilted their head. And spoke.

It was not trade-tongue. It was not Lowmark cant, or court speech, or any of the border dialects Hazel had collected over eight years of guiding other people's illegal property through these mountains — and she prided herself on her collection. It didn't sound like any language she knew, and yet it sounded *familiar* in a way that made no sense, the way a smell can be familiar when you've never smelled it before. The words came rounded and deliberate, each one placed like a stone in a wall, and the child's voice under them was calm as deep water.

"I don't understand you," said Hazel, which was true, and then, because the wind chose that moment to shove her hair across her eyes and the child shivered — actually shivered, one small ripple from shoulders to bare feet — she heard herself say the fatal thing. "Oh, get *in*, then. Quickly. Before I come to my senses."

The child came in. Silently. Bare feet leaving small wet prints on her clean floor, which Hazel noted with the resigned air of a woman adding another line to tomorrow's ledger.

* * *

Getting the child thawed was straightforward work, and work was a comfort. Hazel got them into the chair — after a brief negotiation with Debt, who left with the affronted dignity of a cat who had been promised nothing and received less — and wrapped them in the good blanket, the grey wool one she'd traded a week of guide-work for and had never once regretted. She put water on for barley tea. She found the largest pair of socks she owned, which were hers, and therefore too big, and rolled them down over feet that were, disturbingly, not blue. Not even pink with returning blood. Just — warm. The child's feet had been bare in a killing storm, and they were warm as bread.

Hazel decided not to think about that. She was very good at not thinking about things. It was practically her trade.

"Eat," she said, setting down bread and dripping and the tea, and the child ate, neatly and completely, in a manner that suggested either excellent manners or genuine hunger, probably both. While they ate, Hazel studied them the way she studied everything: for value, for threat, for cost.

Threat: minimal. The child weighed about as much as her pack. Value: unknown, and unknowns made her itch. Cost: rising by the minute, because whoever this child belonged to would come looking, and people who lost children in the Thornfell passes came looking with torches and questions, and questions were the one commodity Hazel dealt in least.

"So," she said, in the tone she used for opening negotiations. "You've eaten my bread. That puts you in my debt, which around here is a serious condition. So you'll tell me your name, and where you're from, and how exactly you walked up a mountain in a blizzard without freezing, and we'll call it even."

The child looked at her. Considered. Then said a short word — two syllables, soft at the front, sharp at the end — and touched their own chest.

"That's a name?" said Hazel. "Say it slower."

The child said it again, patiently, the way you'd repeat something to someone who was being a bit slow but whom you liked anyway. *Wren.* Or something near it. Hazel's tongue stumbled on the middle of it both times, some small catch in the throat that the language seemed to require and hers refused to make.

"Wren," she settled on. "Fine. Wren. And where are you *from*, Wren?"

Wren pointed — not south, toward the villages, not west, toward Lowmark, but north and up. Into the high passes. Into the dead country. The direction where, according to every map Hazel owned and several she'd drawn herself, there was nothing at all.

"There's nothing up there," Hazel said. "Nothing but rock and weather and old stories."

Wren looked at her with an expression of mild, unblinking pity, and replied at length in that round, stone-placed language, and although Hazel understood none of it — not one word of it, she would have sworn this on her mother's grave, if she'd known where her mother's grave was — one word arrived in her ear and detonated quietly somewhere behind her ribs.

*Thornfell.*

Not the word itself. The word as any scholar or mapmaker would render it, the name of a country erased three hundred years ago, was a museum piece, a curiosity, the sort of thing Vane's inquisitors burned archives over. But this was the *other* pronunciation — the living one, the one with the breath in the middle of it, the one Rosalind had taught her twenty years ago in a kitchen that smelled of rosemary, making her say it over and over until she got the breath right, saying *names are load-bearing, Hazel, carry them properly or don't carry them at all.*

Hazel had called it superstition. She had called it worse than superstition, on the last day, with the door slamming. And she had spent eight years carefully pretending the lessons had never happened, the way you pretend a scar isn't there until something presses on it.

Now something had pressed on it. Something nine years old and barefoot, sitting in her chair, wearing her socks.

"Where did you learn that word?" Her voice came out lower than she intended.

Wren blinked at her, then answered in trade-tongue — the first words Hazel had heard from them in it, halting and oddly accented, each one chosen with visible care, like a person crossing a river on stepping stones.

"You said it. Inside. When you looked at me." A pause. "You said it wrong."

"I did *not*—" Hazel stopped. Closed her mouth. Opened it again. "I haven't said it in twenty years."

"You said it tonight." Wren took a sip of tea with the serenity of a judge confirming a verdict. "Wrong."

* * *

The storm did not break. Storms in the high passes rarely did; they settled in like relatives and stayed until the food ran out. By the second hour Hazel had accepted that her visitor wasn't going anywhere — wasn't going anywhere *in*, either, since the only road down was under six feet of new snow — and that the night would now proceed according to its own arithmetic, which apparently included children.

She gave Wren the cot and took the chair herself, boots off, knife within reach, out of habit rather than fear. Fear would have required believing the child was dangerous, and everything about Wren argued otherwise. The child had fallen asleep instantly, completely, with the untroubled totality of someone who had never once needed to sleep with one ear open. Hazel had not slept that way since she was fifteen. She watched the small shape breathe under the grey blanket and felt something in her chest do a thing she declined to examine.

Around the third hour, half-dreaming, she heard Wren talk in their sleep.

It wasn't talking, quite. It was more like — naming. Soft, unhurried, one word after another in that breathing language, and here was the thing Hazel would take to her grave and possibly beyond it: as the child murmured, the fire *settled*. Not died — settled, banked itself, drew its flames down into a low red contentment like a dog curling up. The wind outside, which had been screaming against the north shutter all night, changed its mind mid-scream and went around the house instead, polite as a caller redirected by a servant. And the frost on the window, which had been creeping inward all evening in jagged spears, stopped, hesitated, and withdrew — slowly, gracefully, the way ice melts when it is asked nicely.

Hazel sat in the dark with her heart going like a rabbit's and told herself: draft. Old house. Weather turning. There are explanations.

There were explanations. There were always explanations. That was the trouble with the world — it offered you one for everything, and the explanations were cheaper than the truth, and most people took the discount. Rosalind used to say that too, in that kitchen, in that voice: *the cheap explanation will bankrupt you eventually, love.*

"Shut up, Rosalind," Hazel whispered to the dark, and did not sleep again.

* * *

Morning came white and enormous. The storm had moved off eastward overnight — eastward, note, not onward, as if it had been seen off — and the world outside the window lay buried and blazing, sun on snow, the pines bowed under it, the whole pass silent as held breath. It was, objectively, beautiful. Hazel distrusted it immediately.

Wren was awake, had folded the blanket into a square so precise it could have passed inspection by a sergeant-major, and was standing at the window watching the light with an expression Hazel could only call homesick, which was absurd, because the child had come *from* out there, not toward it.

"Tea?" Hazel offered, because hospitality was cheaper than conversation.

Wren turned. And then, in careful trade-tongue, assembling the sentence like a child building blocks: "Thank you. For the door."

"The door?"

"You opened it." Wren touched the chest, the same gesture as last night. "Most doors, they are afraid. This one was not afraid. Because you were not afraid." A pause, and then, with the devastating directness of the young: "You were a little afraid."

"I was *not*—"

"You were a little afraid," Wren agreed kindly, "but you opened it anyway. That is why the frost went home."

Hazel stood with the kettle in her hand and looked at this child — this barefoot, impossible, self-possessed scrap of a person who talked about frost going home as though frost were a neighbor's dog — and felt her entire ledger develop a page she didn't know how to rule. Everything she knew about survival said: feed them, dry them, hand them to the nearest authority or the nearest buyer, whichever pays, and forget the color of their eyes. Everything she knew about survival had kept her alive for eight years and had cost her, along the way, nearly everything she'd ever pretended not to miss.

And underneath all of it, faint as a coal under ash, one word with breath in the middle of it, and a voice twenty years gone saying *carry it properly.*

"Right," said Hazel aloud, to the kettle, to the room, to whatever listening frost might remain. "Here's what happens now. The snow's too deep to travel for two days at least. You'll stay. You'll eat. You'll help chop wood, because nobody eats free under my roof, and yes, I know you're nine, the wood won't care. And when the pass opens—" she paused, hearing her own voice go strange, "—when the pass opens, we'll see. We'll see what the roads say."

Wren nodded solemnly, as though terms had been struck and witnessed, and then crossed the room and did something no one had done to Hazel Oyelaran in eight years, which was hug her, briefly, around the waist, with the matter-of-fact affection of a person greeting a fact rather than offering a sentiment. Then they stepped back, looked up, and delivered what was unmistakably a correction.

"You said 'we,'" they observed. "In trade-tongue. You said *we'll see*. That means two."

"It's an expression—"

But Wren had already turned to the window, watching the buried pass with those still, unhurried eyes, and far away down the southern slopes — too far to see, too far to hear, close enough that Hazel's spine prickled with a smuggler's instinct she'd learned to trust above scripture — something else was moving through the snow. Something with many boots.

Hazel reached for the kettle and began, quietly, to recalculate.
