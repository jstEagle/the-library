## 3. What the Guild Pays For Silence

The child slept in the woodbox.

Hazel had offered the cot. The child had looked at the cot, looked at the woodbox, and climbed into the woodbox, pulling the door shut like a lid on a pie. Hazel had stood there for a long moment with a blanket in her hands, feeling the particular foolishness of a woman whose hospitality has been declined by someone who cannot even say *no thank you* in any language she knew.

So: the woodbox. The blanket went through the gap under the lid, along with half a heel of bread and, after some negotiation conducted entirely in gestures — the child pointing at Hazel's lamp, Hazel pretending not to understand, the child pointing again with the patience of a customs officer — one of her two candles.

The storm had blown itself out by morning, which was typical of storms and of most things that arrive at your door in the night: dramatic on entry, gone before breakfast, leaving you to deal with what they'd dropped.

Hazel sat at her table with her ledger open and did not look at the woodbox.

She looked instead at the numbers, because numbers were honest. Ten days' food burned through in three, thanks to an unexpected guest. Two candles down to one. A week of guiding work lost to weather that had closed the high passes and would keep them closed until the frost let go. And in the corner of the ledger, where she kept the page flat with her thumb so it couldn't curl up and shame her, the figure she owed the guild on the boat passage from Saltmere, plus interest, plus the interest on the interest, which Corvo's clerks calculated with a creativity Hazel almost admired.

She did the arithmetic the way she always did, top to bottom, no sentiment. It came out the same as it always came out: behind.

Behind her, the woodbox creaked.

"I know you're awake," Hazel said, without turning around. "You've been awake since I put the kettle on. Nobody sleeps through me putting the kettle on. It sounds like a bear dying in a bucket."

Silence. Then the lid lifted an inch, and one dark eye appeared in the gap, regarding her with what Hazel could only describe as professional assessment. The eye took in the kettle, the ledger, the single candle stub, and the state of Hazel's boots by the door, and seemed to reach conclusions.

"Don't look at my boots," Hazel said. "My boots are fine."

The eye did not blink.

"They're *fine*."

The lid closed again. From inside came a small sound that Hazel was almost certain — *almost* certain — was laughter.

* * *

They came for her at midday, which was also typical. The guild never knocked at a reasonable hour; knocking at a reasonable hour suggested you had something to hide from daylight, and the guild hid from nothing except its own accountants.

There were three of them on the frozen mud outside the waystation: Brakka, who was wide as a doorway and had once carried a piano up a cliff face out of spite; Ferro, thin and grey as winter wire, who did the talking; and a third man Hazel didn't know, who stood slightly behind the other two with the studied blankness of someone being trained to stand behind people.

"Hazel Oyelaran." Ferro said it like he was reading it off a list, which he probably was. "Guildmaster wants a word."

"The guildmaster can write it down. I read slow but I'm cheap."

"He said you'd say that." Ferro smiled without investing anything in it. "He also said to remind you that your boat passage is now four hundred crowns, and that the passes are shut, and that a guide with no passes and four hundred crowns of debt is a woman with a lot of time on her hands."

Hazel leaned against her doorframe and made herself comfortable, because the first rule of negotiating with the guild was to be more comfortable than they were. "Ferro. You've come all this way through the snow to threaten me with arithmetic. I do arithmetic before breakfast. You'll have to bring something better."

"We brought something better." Ferro glanced past her shoulder, into the dim interior of the waystation, toward the woodbox. "We hear you've got cargo."

"You heard wrong. I've got a guest."

"A guest." Ferro tasted the word. "A guest who walked out of the Thornfell passes barefoot in a whiteout. A guest who doesn't talk. A guest that three separate people between here and Hollowmere have described as *wrong*, in the specific way things are wrong when they come out of Thornfell."

Hazel kept her face still. It was a skill she'd paid dearly to learn, back when she was learning it from Rosalind, who had called it *guarding the gate* and taught it alongside herb-lore and star-craft, back when Hazel had thought all of it was superstition dressed up as wisdom. Guarding the gate. Let nothing cross your face that you haven't chosen to send across.

"There's no such thing as Thornfell," she said. "It's a story grandmothers tell to keep children off the high roads."

"Mm," said Ferro. "And yet here you are, living at the edge of it, guiding through it, and here it is, sending you guests." He held up a hand before she could answer. "Save it. I'm not here to argue geography. I'm here because there's a buyer in Lowmark who pays very well for exactly the kind of thing you're pretending you don't have."

* * *

Inside, over tea that Hazel watered down on principle, Ferro laid it out.

The buyer had a name, or rather a set of initials and a shipping house, which in Lowmark amounted to the same thing. He collected extinct things. That was the phrase Ferro used, twice, with the care of a man handling someone else's fragile property: *extinct things*. Last speakers of dead languages. Seeds from orchards nobody tended anymore. A bell, Ferro said, from a village that had been unmade so thoroughly that even the bell's foundry records had rotted into blank paper.

"And the child?" Hazel asked. She kept her voice level. She was aware, distantly, of the woodbox against the far wall, and of how quiet it was, and of how she couldn't decide whether the quiet meant the child was listening or whether it meant something worse.

"Doesn't matter what the child is," Ferro said. "Matters what the child's worth. Five hundred crowns, delivered alive and whole to Lowmark within the month. Enough to clear your debt twice over and buy new boots."

"My boots are fine."

"Six hundred," said Ferro, "if you stop saying that."

Brakka laughed, a sound like a rockslide deciding to be friendly. Even the blank-faced third man's mouth twitched.

Hazel looked into her tea. This was the moment, she understood, where the real negotiation began, and it wasn't about crowns.

Here is what she told herself, in the space of about four seconds, while Ferro watched her with his winter-wire patience:

*It's a job.* That was the first thing. The oldest thing. Everything that crossed her table was a job — contraband, fugitives, letters too hot for the post, once, memorably, a live owl in a hatbox. She didn't ask what the goods were for. Asking was how guides got attached, and attachment was how guides got dead or, worse, got *slow*. The buyer wanted a child who spoke a dead language. Strange, yes. But collectors were strange; that was practically the definition. Somewhere in Lowmark was a warm room and a man who would pay six hundred crowns to sit and listen to words nobody else alive understood, and what was cruel about that? The child ate well. The child slept indoors. The child was, if anything, going *up* in the world, from a smuggler's woodbox to a collector's —

To a collector's what?

Hazel noticed that she had been about to think *cage* and had flinched away from the word, and she noticed herself noticing, and she did what she always did with thoughts that cost too much: she put them in the ledger column marked *later*.

"It's not enough," she said aloud.

Ferro raised an eyebrow.

"For a month on the road with a mute child through winter passes, with Vane's people crawling the foothills burning archives — you think I haven't heard? — and every scout from here to Hollowmere already gossiping about my *guest*." She ticked the risks off on her fingers, and the gesture was half performance and half genuine habit, because naming a risk out loud was how she made it smaller. "Road fees. Silence fees. And a danger fee, because if the Crown catches me with cargo out of Thornfell, they don't hang smugglers for wool or brandy, Ferro. They hang them for *words*. Eight hundred. Half up front."

"That's robbery."

"That's the rate. You knew the rate when you walked in. Corvo sets the rates; I just refuse to lose money politely."

Ferro looked at Brakka. Brakka shrugged, which from Brakka involved his entire upper body. Ferro sighed the sigh of a man whose employer had anticipated him, produced a purse, and counted out four hundred crowns onto the table in neat stacks, each stack squared with a little pat, because guild money always arrived looking tidier than it would ever leave.

"Half now," Ferro said. "Half on delivery. And Hazel —" He paused at the door, and for just a moment the list-reader fell away and something older and wearier looked out of his face. "The buyer's last acquisition. A woman who wove in a pattern nobody's woven since the old country. He kept her eleven years. She stopped speaking in the fourth. Died quiet in the ninth." He straightened his collar. "I'm telling you because you'll find out anyway, and I'd rather you heard it from someone who counts it a shame. Six hundred crowns says you don't care. Prove me wrong or don't."

Then he was gone, and Brakka was gone, and the blank-faced man was gone, and the snow outside had their footprints and then, slowly, didn't.

* * *

Hazel sat at her table for a long time.

Four hundred crowns in neat stacks. Her debt: gone. New boots: possible. A season of eating things that weren't barley: possible. All she had to do was walk a child south for a month and hand them to a man who collected extinct things, and the word *extinct* would sit in her mouth the whole way down the mountain like a stone she'd swallowed, and she would get used to it, because you got used to everything, that was the whole trick of staying alive out here. You got used to the cold, to the debt, to the lies you told yourself in a voice that sounded like arithmetic.

From the woodbox: a creak.

Hazel didn't turn around. "I know you heard all that. Your box has excellent acoustics. I've been meaning to complain about it for years."

The lid opened. The child climbed out, crossed the floor without a sound — bare feet on frozen boards, and how were the feet not blue, Hazel had checked twice in the night and the feet were warm as fresh bread — and stood beside the table, looking down at the money.

Not at Hazel. At the money.

Then the child reached out, and Hazel braced for grabbing, for the small grasping hands she'd seen in every market town, children who knew what coin meant before they knew their own names. But the child only touched the topmost crown, one fingertip, lightly, the way you'd touch something sleeping that you didn't want to wake. And then the child looked up at Hazel and said, in that language that predated every kingdom and every map and every law that had ever pretended the mountains were empty —

One word. Short. Soft.

And Hazel's chest did something complicated and unwelcome, because she knew the word. She hadn't known she knew it. It had been sitting in her for nine years, filed under *superstition*, next to everything else Rosalind had ever tried to teach her, and it surfaced now whole and warm and terrible.

*Debt,* the word meant. Or near enough. Not the guild's kind of debt — not the kind you counted and resented and cleared. The other kind. The kind you owed to someone who had fed you when you were hungry, and that could never be paid back, only passed on, forward, outward, like a flame handed from candle to candle down a dark road.

Rosalind had called it the oldest economy. Hazel had called it nonsense, at fifteen, with a packed bag and a slammed door.

The child withdrew the finger. The child looked at Hazel with those dark, patient eyes, and Hazel understood — with the sick clarity of a woman watching her own cleverness catch up with her — that the child knew exactly what had been agreed in this room. Knew the number. Knew the destination. Perhaps knew, in whatever way the child knew things, about the weaver who had died quiet in the ninth year.

"Well," Hazel said, and her voice came out steadier than she had any right to expect. "Don't make it weird."

The child tilted their head.

"I'm a professional," Hazel said, sweeping the crowns into her purse, not looking at them, looking anywhere but at them. "I take jobs. This is a job. In a month you'll have a warm room and regular meals and a man who'll listen to every word you say for the rest of your life, which is more than most people get, and I will have no debts, and everyone will be exactly where they chose to be." She stood, and shouldered her pack, and began laying out the maps she'd need for the southern route, smoothing each one flat with hands that were completely steady, thank you. "Lowmark. One month. Try to keep up."

The child watched her for a moment. Then the child went to the window, where the morning sun had finally broken through the storm clouds, and pointed south — down the long white shoulder of the mountains, toward the lowlands, toward Lowmark — and said another word.

That one Hazel didn't know. But she caught herself, later that evening, packing the last of the food, humming it. Just the shape of it, the rise and fall, a tune without meaning.

It sounded, she thought, like a warning.

It sounded, she decided, like nothing at all.

They left at first light.
