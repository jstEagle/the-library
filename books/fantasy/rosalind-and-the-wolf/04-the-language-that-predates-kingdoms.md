## 4. The Language That Predates Kingdoms

Before there were words for it, there was the smell of bread.

Wren did not know this yet — that memory could live in the nose the way a bird lives in an egg, whole and folded and waiting — but they knew the smell itself, knew it the way they knew their own hands: without needing to look.

Bread meant morning. Bread meant the baker's lane in Vell, where the ovens breathed heat through the shutters and the whole street woke up warm. Bread meant Da coming home with flour in his eyebrows like he'd been snowed on indoors, which was funny, which made Ma laugh until she had to sit down on the step, which was the best sound in any country, in any world, ever.

*Vell.*

The word came up out of Wren like a bubble rising through water. They held it carefully in their mouth. It fit there. It had always fit there, the way a stone fits in a streambed — not because anyone put it there, but because the water had spent so long moving around it that the shape of the absence became its own kind of presence.

That was what the old tongue was, though Wren didn't have those words either. The old tongue was the shape of everything that wasn't there anymore.

They lay on a straw pallet in a stranger's waystation while a storm leaned against the roof beams, and they thought about bread.

* * *

Here is what Wren remembered, in no particular order, because remembering was not a line for them but a weather:

A field of barley going gold at the edges first, like the sun was setting into it from underneath. Aunty Sef walking the rows with her skirts hitched up, calling the plants by name — not pet names, real names, the names that made them stand up straighter, and they did, Wren had seen it, the barley lifting its head when she spoke to it like a dog hearing its person come up the path.

A river called the Ossle, which was not a word for water but a word for *the water that remembers being rain*, and if you said it right, standing on the bank with your feet bare, you could taste last summer's storms.

A Door.

The Door was gray stone, older than gray, set into the mountain's shoulder above the high pastures, and it did not open for pushing or pulling or prying or wishing. It opened for asking. You stood before it and you said the guest-word — *hallowen*, which meant *I am arriving, please be glad* — and the Door would consider you, the way a hen considers a strange chick, and then it would open, and the air that came out smelled like bread.

Wren's grandmother had taken them to the Door once, when they were very small, small enough to be carried on her hip. She had not asked it to open. She had only wanted Wren to see it, to know it, to carry it.

"Someday," she'd said, tapping Wren's chest with one finger, right over the sternum, "this will knock from inside you."

Wren had laughed, because grandmothers said things like that, and also because her finger tickled.

* * *

Then the soldiers came, except they didn't come like soldiers, they came like weather, like a front moving down off the mountains, gray and total, and afterward nobody in Vell could say the name Vell.

Not wouldn't. Couldn't.

Wren watched it happen to Ma first. Ma was stirring the pot and she stopped, spoon in the air, and her face went smooth as a pond in windless weather, and she said, "Where are we again?" in the trade-tongue, in the flat gray tongue, and when Wren answered — answered in the old tongue, the round warm tongue, the tongue that tasted like bread and barley and the Ossle — Ma looked at them the way you look at a cat that has spoken.

"Where did you learn that?" she whispered. And then, worse, softer: "*What is that?*"

She couldn't hear it properly anymore. That was the thing nobody told you about erasure. It didn't take the country away all at once. It took the ears first. It took the part of people that could recognize home, so that even the ones who remembered their houses forgot why the houses mattered, and then the houses fell down or were pulled down or simply got renamed, and renaming, Wren learned, was a kind of pulling-down too, slower but just as thorough.

Da lasted longer. Da was stubborn the way oak is stubborn, which is to say he bent in every storm and then stood back up after, and everyone thought that meant he'd survive anything. He learned to say the trade-name of their town — it had a trade-name now, some gray syllable-cluster meaning roughly *tax station nine* — and he said it cheerfully, loudly, the way you say a curse word in front of children to show it has no power over you.

But late at night Wren would hear him in the yard, talking to the pear tree. And he wasn't talking in trade-tongue.

He was using the old words for the tree — the tree's true name, which meant something like *the one who holds up the sky a little bit so we can rest under it* — and the tree would lean toward him. Just slightly. Just enough. The way a plant leans toward light.

And Da would stop talking, and stand there with his hand over his mouth, and Wren, watching from the window, understood two things at once, the way children understand things, all at once and forever:

One: the words still worked. The erasure had taken the ears and the maps and the names off the signposts, but it had not taken the *working* out of the words. Words were load-bearing. Take away the wall and the beam still held up something, somewhere, somehow.

Two: Da was going to die of this. Not quickly. But the tree leaning toward him was a door opening in him, and doors that open in people let things out, and what was leaking out of Da was the last of his belief that any of this was survivable.

He was gone by the first frost. Not dead — Wren never found out if he was dead; dead was a word with a body attached, and Da left no body, just an empty coat and a yard where the pear tree dropped all its fruit overnight, unripe, as if in mourning, or protest, or both.

Gone the way a country goes.

* * *

After that, Wren walked.

They did not think of it as running, because running implies something chasing you, and nothing chased Wren. That was the terrible elegance of erasure: it didn't need to chase. It simply made the ground behind you stop having existed, so that wherever you stood, you stood at the edge of everything, and the only direction left was forward, into the gray tongue, the gray maps, the gray names.

But here is what the gray tongues and gray maps did not know, could not know, because knowing it required ears that had been plucked out three centuries ago:

The old tongue did not live in Wren's mouth.

It lived in the world.

Every word Wren had ever learned had been learned *from* something — from the barley standing up for Aunty Sef, from the river tasting of rain, from the pear tree holding up its little piece of sky. The language predated kingdoms, predated the Crown with its inquisitors and its burning archives, predated the very idea of borders, because it was not a language people had invented. It was a language the world had grown, the way a field grows barley, and you do not erase a field by changing the sign at its edge. The barley knows what it is. The barley has always known.

And now the barley — the fields, the stones, the rivers, the doors — had noticed Wren.

They noticed the way dogs notice. Silently, totally, with their whole bodies.

When Wren walked past a fence post, the post hummed, very faintly, a note like a struck wire. When Wren slept in a hay barn, the hay arranged itself around them more comfortably than hay had any business doing. Once, feverish with cold in a ditch outside a town whose name Wren refused to learn, they had mumbled the Ossle's name into the mud — just the river's name, just missing it, that's all — and the ditch had filled with clear water that tasted, impossibly, of last summer's storms.

Wren had drunk it and cried and not known which act came first.

* * *

So: the waystation. The storm. The woman with the guarded face and the clever hands who had opened the door at the wrong hour and then stood there looking at Wren like Wren was a coin of uncertain mint.

Wren had knocked because the door was a door, and doors deserved courtesy, and besides, the storm was winning.

The woman had spoken trade-tongue at them — *where's your mother, where are you from, can you speak?* — and Wren had shaken their head, not because they couldn't speak but because speaking trade-tongue felt like wearing someone else's boots: possible, but wrong-sized, and it made your soul walk funny.

And then the woman had said a word. One word. Half under her breath, half to herself, the way you say a thing you've sworn not to remember.

*Hallowen.*

*I am arriving, please be glad.*

Wren had nearly fallen over. Not from the storm-wind. From the word. Because the word was a key, and keys don't care how rusty they are — touch them to the right lock and everything inside wakes up — and somewhere in Wren's chest, the place where Grandmother had tapped and said *someday this will knock from inside you*, something knocked.

Someday, apparently, was now.

Wren had stepped past the woman into the warmth, and had done the only polite thing, the only correct thing, the thing every bone in their body insisted upon:

They had said thank you. In the old tongue. Properly. With the full weight of the gratitude-word, which means *you gave me something I did not ask for and I will hold it carefully forever.*

And the room had changed.

It was a small change. Wren barely saw it, they were so tired, so wet, so full of storm. But the fire sat up straighter in the grate. The draft under the door gave up and went home. The kettle, sitting cold on its hook, began — very softly, very shyly, like a child joining a song it only half knew — to warm.

And the woman, Hazel, though Wren didn't know her name yet, went very still, the way people go still when the floor moves and they're the only one who felt it.

Good, Wren thought, curling up on the pallet, pulling the blanket to their chin. Good. Let the room be glad. Let everything be glad. There has been enough gray.

Sleep came for them like the tide.

* * *

They dreamed of Vell.

In the dream the baker's lane was whole, the ovens breathing, flour-snow falling indoors, Ma laughing on the step, Da with his eyebrows white as a winter hedge. Aunty Sef walked her rows and the barley stood up to be counted. The Ossle ran bright and full of remembered rain. And above the high pastures, gray and patient and older than gray, the Door stood in the mountain's shoulder, waiting, the way good doors wait — not impatiently, not hungrily, just *willingly*, the way a held breath is willing.

Grandmother stood beside it, tapping Wren's chest. *Someday,* she said. *This will knock from inside you.*

"It's knocking now," Wren told her, in the dream, in the old tongue, and the words came out round and warm and tasting of bread.

And the Door — in the dream, in the dream only — shivered.

Woke.

The waystation was dark and quiet. The storm had worn itself down to a mutter. In the other room, the woman slept badly; Wren could hear her turning, turning, the way people turn when they are carrying something heavy in their chest and cannot put it down.

Wren lay in the dark and listened to the house settle around them, and noticed — with the mild, unsurprised attention of a child to whom impossible things had become merely Tuesday — that the floorboards beneath the pallet were humming. Faintly. A note like a struck wire. The same note the fence posts made, the same note the hay made, the same note the whole world made now whenever Wren's breath touched it.

The world was waking up around them, word by word, night by night.

And far away — farther than the storm, farther than the mountains, farther than the gray maps could measure — something else heard the humming too, and turned toward it, slow as a season, patient as a held breath.

Something with a crown it had never been allowed to wear.

Wren did not know this. Children rarely know what their voices summon.

They only knew that tomorrow, the woman would look at them again with that coin-of-uncertain-mint face, and Wren would have to decide whether to teach her another word.

They fell asleep smiling. It was, after all, a very good word.
