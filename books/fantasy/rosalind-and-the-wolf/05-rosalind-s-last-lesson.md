## 5. Rosalind's Last Lesson

The rain on the cottage roof had a rhythm to it, and Rosalind claimed you could read the year in it, the way other people read almanacs. *Early spring,* she'd say, tilting her head. *A wet one. The river will take the low field again.* And the river always did.

Hazel was fifteen that spring, and fifteen-year-olds know everything, which is a heavy burden, and she carried hers with all the grace of a mule carrying gravel.

"Say it back to me," Rosalind said. She was at the worktable, her hands buried in a bowl of dried nettle, not looking up, because looking up made Hazel sullen and Rosalind had learned to teach sideways, out of the corner of the morning, as though the lesson were an afterthought.

"There's nothing to say back. You were muttering at the nettles again."

"I was naming them."

"They're nettles. They know what they are."

Rosalind looked up then, and there was that expression — the one Hazel would spend years trying to forget and failing, because memory is a smuggler too and carries what it likes across borders you've closed. It wasn't hurt. That was the worst of it. It was patience, patient as a stone wall, patient as weather.

"Do they?" Rosalind said. "Do they know what they are?"

"They're plants, Ros. They don't know anything. They grow where they grow and sting whoever stumbles on them and that's the whole of it."

"Mm." Rosalind took a pinch of dried leaf and held it out on her palm. "This one grew by the crossroads. This one grew under the eave. Same plant, same dirt, same rain. But this one—" she tapped the crossroads nettle, "—I asked its name, and it answered, and now when I call it, it comes. Stings for me. Heals for me. The other one is just a weed in my basket."

"That's not how plants work."

"No," Rosalind agreed pleasantly. "That's how *names* work. The plant is just where they keep them."

Hazel threw up her hands, which at fifteen was her primary mode of argument. "You can't just say things like that and expect them to make sense."

"I don't expect them to make sense. I expect them to be true. Sense comes later, if it comes at all. Like most guests worth having."

Outside, the rain kept its rhythm, and the hens complained about it in the yard, and the world was exactly as small and exactly as safe as Hazel could not yet bear it to be.

* * *

Here is what must be understood about the hedge-witch's cottage at the edge of the Thornfell passes: it was the last house before the mountains stopped pretending to be anything but mountains, and everyone who passed it was going somewhere else. Peddlers. Pilgrims. Smugglers with honest faces and dishonest packs. Rosalind fed them all — there was always soup on, always bread, and if you stayed the night you woke to find your boots mended whether you'd asked or not. She called it hospitality. Hazel, even then, suspected it was something more like a habit of hope: that if you fed enough people going somewhere, one of them might tell you why anywhere was better than here.

It never worked. People ate, thanked her, and left. And every time the door shut behind them, Rosalind would stand a moment with her hand flat against the wood, feeling the draft of their going, and then turn back to her worktable as if nothing had happened.

Hazel watched her do it for eleven years before she understood she was watching grief practice.

* * *

The lesson that spring did not end at the nettles. Lessons with Rosalind never ended; they went underground like streams and surfaced days later somewhere unexpected.

Three days after the nettle argument, Hazel came in from the goat shed to find the table covered in paper. Not letters — Rosalind got perhaps two letters a year, both from the same apothecary in Lowmark, both bills. These were old pages, soft-cornered, copied out in a hand that wasn't Rosalind's, in an alphabet Hazel didn't know. Loops and strokes like bird tracks in snow, like frost deciding to be language.

"What's this?"

"Work," said Rosalind, which was her answer to most questions and meant *none of your business* and also *all of your business, eventually, if you'll let it be.*

"It's not trade-script."

"No."

"It's not Crown script either. I've seen Crown script on the toll notices."

"No," Rosalind said again, and this time she set down her pen and looked at Hazel straight, and something in the look made the hairs on Hazel's arms stand up, though she'd have bitten off her own tongue before admitting it. "Sit down, love."

Nobody sat down when told to sit down. Hazel sat down.

"This alphabet belongs to a country," Rosalind said. "And I'm going to tell you about it, and you're going to call it superstition, and then I'm going to tell you the rest anyway, because someone should carry it, and I am getting old, and you are stubborn in exactly the right direction."

"You're forty-two."

"And ancient," Rosalind agreed. "Practically a barrow-wight. Listen."

And so Hazel listened, arms crossed, chin set, wearing her skepticism like armor two sizes too big — and the terrible thing, the thing she would only understand much later, standing in a doorway with a mute child dripping stormwater on her floor, was that the armor fit perfectly. It had been made for her. She had helped make it.

* * *

"There was a country," Rosalind said, "where the mountains fold in on themselves above the high passes. You've walked its bones. Everyone who runs the Thornfell roads has. The switchback at Hollowmere — that was a market street. The lake at Sorrow Bridge — there was a town on its shore, with a bell tower, and the bell rang the hours, and people set their bread by it."

"There's no town at Sorrow Bridge. There's nothing at Sorrow Bridge. That's why it's good for moving cargo."

"Yes," said Rosalind. "Isn't it remarkable, how thoroughly nothing can be arranged?"

Hazel shifted. "What are you saying? Someone cleared it out?"

"Someone cleared out the *name* of it. The country was called Thornfell. Three hundred years ago, give or take a war. It stood where nothing stands now, and it spoke a language older than any kingdom on the maps, and when the kingdoms around it grew tired of a neighbor they couldn't tax or threaten, they did the sensible thing." Her voice was light. Her hands were not; they had gone still on the table, flat, the way she laid them on a sick animal. "They erased it. Not burned — burning leaves ash, and ash can be read. They unwrote it. Treaties rewritten, records amended, songs reworded, gravestones recut. Maps redrawn so smoothly that within four generations no one could point to the gap, because gaps are only visible from inside a border, and the border had been taught to forget it had ever been anywhere else."

"That's not possible. You can't unwrite a country. People remember."

"Do they?" Rosalind asked gently. "Name three things about your grandmother, Hazel. Your mother's mother."

Hazel opened her mouth. Closed it. She had a name — Maren Oyelaran — and a ring, which lived in a knot of cloth at the bottom of Hazel's pack even then, and beyond that, a shape in a doorway, and the smell of something cooking, maybe. Maybe.

"You see," Rosalind said, not cruelly, never cruelly, which somehow made it worse. "Erasure doesn't need to be perfect. It needs only to outlast the remembering. Three generations does it. Four, comfortably."

"So what's the alphabet, then? If they got everything."

"They missed some." Rosalind drew one of the looped characters toward herself, touched it without quite touching it, the way you'd touch a sleeping cat you hadn't been introduced to. "Names are load-bearing, love. That's the whole of hedge-craft, if you want it in one line. Take away enough names and the thing they held up comes down. But a country isn't a barn. You can't pull every beam. Some names went deep. Some names went into *people* — into bloodlines scattered over the borders before the end, families who married out, fled out, were smuggled out." A pause, delicate as a knife laid down. "Smuggled, Hazel. There have always been guides on those roads. Some of them carried more than cargo."

Hazel stared at the bird-track letters. "And these are — what? The leftovers?"

"These are the beams. Words in the old speech that still hold weight. Say one in the right place, in the right way, and the country remembers itself for a moment. Grass grows through three centuries of forgetting. Bells ring in towers that aren't there. Doors—" Rosalind stopped herself, and Hazel, fifteen and furious and paying attention to all the wrong things, noticed the stop and filed it away as proof of nonsense rather than proof of fear.

"Doors," Hazel repeated. "What doors?"

"Never mind doors."

"You brought up doors."

"I un-bring them. This isn't a lesson for — " Rosalind pressed her lips together, started again. "The point, Hazel, the entire point, is this: words are not noises. Words are *weights*. Every name anyone ever speaks is holding something up, or pressing something down. When you call a child stupid, you're laying a beam across her life and she will walk under it for sixty years. When you name a thing truly — its true name, the name it answers to — you're not describing it. You're *inviting* it. To be present. To answer. To come when called." She looked at Hazel then with an intensity that the girl mistook for drama and the woman would recognize, far too late, as a warning label. "So you hold your tongue like it costs money. Because it does. Everything you say is being spent somewhere."

"That's superstition," Hazel said. She heard her own voice go flat and hard, the way it went whenever Rosalind got close to the soft places. "Words are words. Nettles are nettles. Countries are countries, and dead ones stay dead, and no amount of muttering at baskets changes any of it."

Something moved behind Rosalind's eyes — not anger. Resignation arriving on schedule, like the river taking the low field.

"All right," Rosalind said quietly. "Then it's superstition. Will you learn it anyway? As superstition? The way you learned the herb-lore you call folklore and use every week?"

"No."

"Hazel—"

"No." She was on her feet now, and the chair skidded, and the hens outside went quiet as if listening. "I'm not you, Ros. I'm not going to sit in this kitchen until I'm ancient, talking to weeds and mourning people who died three hundred years ago in a country that nobody — *nobody*, Ros — has ever needed to exist. There's real work out there. Real coin. Real roads. Things you can count."

"Yes," Rosalind said. "Things you can count. That's the trouble with you, love. You think only what can be counted is real, and so you'll spend your life counting, and everything that matters will slip through the tally because it never had a number."

"Better than spending it talking to empty air!"

The silence after that had edges. Rosalind rose, unhurried, and began gathering the loose pages, squaring them, tying them with string — unhurried the way a person is unhurried when hurrying would mean admitting the wound.

"You'll want the packet someday," she said. "When you do, it's in the blue box under the floorboard by the hearth. Don't open it angry. Open it when you're ready to be wrong."

"I'm not wrong."

"No," Rosalind agreed, and smiled, and the smile was the loneliest thing in the room. "Not yet."

* * *

She left in the morning.

Of course she left in the morning. Fifteen-year-olds leave in the morning; it's practically liturgy. She packed in the dark while Rosalind slept — or lay awake pretending to sleep, which amounts to the same courtesy — and she took the road north with the dawn coming up gray behind the peaks, and she did not look back at the cottage, because looking back is for people who intend to return.

She told herself the leaving was practical. The passes paid better than goats. A guide with a head for routes and a face nobody remembered could earn in a season what the cottage saw in five years. It was arithmetic. Clean, countable arithmetic, the kind Rosalind sneered at and the world rewarded.

She did not tell herself — because telling yourself things is dangerous, you might hear — that she left because Rosalind had looked at her that morning over the porridge and said, mildly, as if remarking on the weather:

"You have the ear, you know. For the old speech. I've never said so because I hoped you wouldn't."

And Hazel had felt the words land in her like hooks, and had hated them, because a gift you didn't ask for is a debt you can't refuse, and Hazel Oyelaran had already decided, at fifteen, that she would owe nothing to anyone. Not to Rosalind. Not to a dead country. Not to a language that predates kingdoms and wanted, apparently, to live in her mouth.

She owed nothing. She counted everything. She left.

* * *

Years later — years of passes and payoffs and packages that whispered when customs officers shook them — the arithmetic held. It held beautifully. Right up until the night the knock came at the wrong hour, and a barefoot child stood in the storm speaking bird-tracks into the dark, and one word of it slipped past every lock Hazel had built and settled, load-bearing, in the exact center of her chest.

A word for *door*, she thought. Or possibly a word for *home*.

In the old speech, she suspected, with a sick lurch she refused to examine, they might be the same word.

And far to the south, in a foothill village whose archive smelled of tallow and fear, a man in a gray coat was asking very polite questions about unlicensed speech.
