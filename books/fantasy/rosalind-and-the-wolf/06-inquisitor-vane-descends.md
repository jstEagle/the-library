## 6. Inquisitor Vane Descends

The rain had followed Inquisitor Seraphine Vane down from the high passes for three days, as if the weather itself were trying to soften her, and she found this mildly insulting.

She rode at the head of six Crown troopers through the foothill country where the mapmakers' ink went thin and uncertain — a landscape of wet stone, stunted pines, and villages that seemed to apologize for existing. The locals called this stretch of road the Long Sorry. Vane had read that in a survey report and had privately considered it an honest piece of naming, which was more than could be said for most things in these hills.

"Another hour to Hollowmere, Inquisitor," said Corporal Brannic, riding up beside her. He was a broad, patient man with a farmer's hands and a soldier's posture, and he was the only one of her escort who spoke to her without flinching. "There's an inn. Dry, I'm told."

"I'm told a great many things," Vane said. "I've learned to weigh them."

Brannic smiled into his beard. He had been with her four years now, long enough to know that her dryness was not cruelty. It was load-bearing.

She was forty-one years old, and she had spent twenty-two of those years in the Office of Records and Rectification, which everyone in the lowlands called the Inquisition because it sounded better in songs. She was not a cruel woman. This was the thing her enemies never understood, and she had stopped expecting them to. Cruelty would have been easier — cruelty could be hated cleanly, dismissed, made into a story with a villain in it. What Vane carried was heavier than cruelty. It was conviction, and conviction does not let you sleep late or take bribes or look away.

She believed — with the calm, unshowy certainty of a woman who had done the arithmetic herself, twice, checking her work — that some words are wounds that never close. That a country erased from the maps three centuries ago had been erased *for reasons*, and that the reasons still held. That every syllable of the old Thornfellish tongue spoken aloud was a splinter worked back into flesh that had finally begun to heal.

And she believed, most deeply of all, in the mercy of forgetting.

She had seen what remembering did. She had stood in border villages where grandmothers taught children lullabies in a dead language, and she had watched those same villages, ten years on, tear themselves apart over names and grievances older than any living person's right to them. Grief was a fire, in her experience, and the Crown's great act of kindness — clumsy, brutal, sometimes unjust, yes — had been to smother it under three hundred years of official silence.

You do not thank the physician for the pain of the treatment. But you live.

* * *

Hollowmere smelled of woodsmoke and wet sheep, and its archive smelled of both of those things plus mildew.

Vane stood in the doorway of the village record-house — a squat stone building wedged between the mill and the shrine — and looked at the shelves with the expression of a woman reading a diagnosis.

It was not, technically, an illegal collection. That was the trouble with the foothills: everything here existed in the gray country between permitted and forbidden, where the law arrived late and left early. The village kept its records, as villages must — births, deaths, land disputes, the price of wool going back ninety years. All perfectly lawful.

But shelved among the lawful records, in the way such things always were, tucked and interleaved like weeds in a wheat field, were the other documents. A genealogy that traced certain families past the Founding Settlement and into a darkness before it, with names in the margins written in no alphabet licensed by the Crown. A bundle of letters, tied with ribbon gone the color of old tea, whose opening lines Vane could read well enough to know they were not in trade-tongue. And behind the genealogy, half-hidden, a slim hand-copied book of songs.

Songs. Vane's jaw tightened. It was always songs. Songs were how the splinter stayed sharp. You could burn a chronicle and the grief in it died; you burned a songbook and the grief simply changed lodgings, moving into throats, where no edict could follow.

"Inquisitor?" The village reeve hovered at her shoulder — a nervous man named Aldous Pell, round-faced, sweating despite the cold, wringing his hat as though it owed him money. "Is — is there something wrong with our records? We keep everything proper. Magistrate Halloway inspected us only two springs past—"

"Magistrate Halloway," Vane said, "inspected your ledgers." She drew off one glove, finger by finger, and touched the spine of the songbook. "He was not asked to look at what lives between your ledgers."

Pell swallowed. "Those are just — old family things, Inquisitor. Grandmother's letters. Nobody reads them. Half of us can't."

"No," Vane agreed quietly. "Half of you can't. That is precisely the tragedy I am here to prevent from becoming whole."

She turned to face him, and she made herself gentle, because gentleness was the harder discipline and because she meant it. This was the part of the work no one sang about: not the burning, but the explaining. The standing in cold rooms with frightened people and telling them the truth as she understood it, knowing most of them would hate her for it, knowing some of them — the tired ones, the ones who had buried children in feuds over inherited hatreds — might, secretly, be grateful.

"Master Pell. Three hundred years ago, there was a war. Not a small one. A war so bitter that when it ended, the Crown made a choice that has never been made before or since: it did not merely defeat a country. It retired it. Closed the account. Struck the name from every map, every ledger, every legal document, so that the quarrel could never be reopened by lawyers, because there would be nothing left to litigate." She let that sit. "Do you know why?"

Pell shook his head.

"Because the alternative was another war. Every generation. Forever. Hatred like that doesn't die of old age, Master Pell. It dies of starvation. And so the Crown starved it. Took away its food — its names, its records, its songs — and waited for it to starve." Her voice did not rise. It never needed to. "And it has nearly worked. Nearly. Do you understand what I am telling you? We are one generation — perhaps two — from finished. From a peace so complete that no one will even know there was ever anything to forgive."

"But then why come here," Pell whispered, "if it's nearly done?"

"Because of this." She lifted the songbook free of the shelf. It weighed almost nothing. That was the horror of it; the weight of the thing bore no relation to what it carried. "Because somewhere in these hills, someone is still feeding it. Someone taught their grandmother, who taught their mother, who wrote this down in case the starving ever ended. And last month, Master Pell, my office received a report from a wool-factor in Duncastle who heard — with his own ears — a child singing in the old tongue. A *child*. Born two hundred and eighty years too late, singing songs that should have been dust."

The room had gone very quiet. Even the rain against the shutters seemed to lean in.

"A child," Pell repeated faintly.

"Children are the whole point," Vane said. "Everything else is negotiable. I do not care about your grandmother's letters, truly — dead women's grievances are between them and whatever gods received them. But a child who learns the old speech is a wound being reopened in a body too young to remember why it hurts. And children grow up, Master Pell. Children with old words in their mouths grow into adults with old grievances in their hearts, and then we are back at the beginning, and the beginning cost forty thousand lives."

She set the songbook down on the reading table, alone, apart from the lawful records, the way a surgeon lays out the limb.

"You have a shrine here," she said. "A good one, from the look of it. Your priest buries your dead with the rites of the Concord. Those rites include a prayer of release — *what is finished, let it rest; what is forgotten, let it sleep.*" She met Pell's eyes. "That is not cruelty. That is the kindest sentence ever composed in our language. I am here to enforce it."

* * *

They burned the archive at dusk, in the mill yard, because Vane insisted on doing these things in daylight-adjacent hours, publicly, with witnesses, and never at night. Night burnings were for men who knew their work would not bear looking at. Hers would bear looking at. She made sure of it.

Not everything burned. She was precise — that was the reputation that preceded her through the foothills, the one smugglers and hedge-witches traded warnings about: *the Inquisitor who counts pages.* The births, deaths, land disputes, and wool prices went back on their shelves, dusted off, untouched. Only the interlopers fed the flames: the genealogy with its forbidden marginalia, the ribboned letters, the songbook, and eleven other items her clerks had catalogued that afternoon in neat, regretful columns.

Pell watched from his doorstep with his hat crushed in his fists. Several villagers stood with him. No one shouted. No one wept openly. An old woman near the well turned her back and walked away very slowly, upright as a fence post, and Vane noted her face — not for any list, but because she had learned long ago that the ones who refuse to watch are the ones carrying the memory forward, and a good inquisitor reads faces the way customs officers read cargo.

Brannic appeared at her elbow with two cups of something hot and alcoholic from the innkeeper, who had apparently decided that hospitality toward armed men was cheaper than resistance.

"You don't like it," Vane said, taking the cup. It was a statement, not an accusation. She valued Brannic partly because he never pretended otherwise.

"Don't like fires in villages," Brannic said. "Grew up in one. We lost the tithe barn once. Took eight years to feel right again." He drank. "But I'll say this, Inquisitor. You didn't burn the births."

"No."

"Noticed that. First time I've seen it done that way. Most of your office—" He stopped, with the tact of a man stepping back from a cliff edge.

"Most of my office burns everything," Vane finished for him. "Ash is easier than discrimination. Ash requires no judgment, only matches." She watched the letters curl and blacken, the ribbon catching last, flaring briefly green. "But ash is also indiscriminate, Corporal, and indiscriminateness is how offices become monsters. If I burn a man's grandmother's letters, he hates me, and in twenty years his son hates the Crown, and I have manufactured exactly the grievance I came to prevent. If I burn only the poison and leave the bread — if I can show these people that their own records, their own true history, was never the enemy — then perhaps one of them, someday, brings me the next songbook voluntarily. Because he understands."

"Do they understand?"

Vane was quiet for a moment. Sparks climbed into the wet dark and died.

"Some of them," she said. "The tired ones. There are always tired ones, Brannic. People who have inherited a grief they never chose and cannot put down, because no one will tell them it's permitted to put it down. When I burn these things, do you know what I am really burning?" She nodded at the fire. "Permission slips. Every old letter is a permission slip to go on hating. I am in the business of revoking permissions." She smiled, thin and genuine and terribly sincere. "One day there will be a generation in these hills with no permission slips at all. They'll be free, and they'll never even know what they were freed from. That is the mercy. That is all of it. We take the memory so they don't have to carry it."

"And the ones who want to carry it?"

"The ones who want to carry it," Vane said, "are the reason it has to be taken."

Brannic looked at her for a long moment — at the small, straight, absolutely certain woman silhouetted against a village fire — and drank his drink, and said nothing more, which was its own kind of answer.

* * *

They lodged that night at the inn, the Drowned Ox, whose sign depicted a drowned ox with an optimism Vane found almost touching. She took the small back room, declined supper, and sat by the window with her traveling desk open on her knees, writing her report to the Office by candlelight in her small, ruthless, beautiful handwriting.

*Item the first: archive at Hollowmere rectified. Eleven items destroyed, public, witnessed. Village cooperative. Reeve Pell shows no signs of retention.*

*Item the second: the wool-factor's report remains unsubstantiated. A singing child is not a singing child until found. Recommend continued surveillance of the upper passes. Smuggling traffic through the Thornfell roads has increased fourteen percent this season, per guild informants, and where smuggling thickens, so does the old traffic in relics, songs, and speech. Contraband is a river; memory swims in it.*

She paused over that last line, pleased with it despite herself, and was considering whether the Office would appreciate the metaphor when the knock came.

Two soft raps. Then, after a pause, two more — the established signal of her informant network in the foothills, which consisted, in its entirety, of a one-eyed peddler named Fitch who sold ribbons, needles, and information at roughly equal markups.

Fitch came in smelling of wet wool and cheap tobacco, glanced at the fire-warmed room with naked envy, and got down to business the way he always did: sideways.

"Cold night for burning," he said.

"Cold night for peddling. What have you?"

"What I have," Fitch said, settling onto the stool uninvited, "is interesting, and interesting costs double."

"Fitch."

"Half-double."

"Fitch."

"Regular rate," he sighed, "because you've got that look. Right. So. You know the guild runs the high passes — Corvo's people, contraband, the usual. And you know they use guides, local folk who know the old roads, the ones that aren't on maps." He tapped his nose. "Maps again, eh? Always maps."

"Get to it."

"There's a guide. Woman. Oyelaran, Hazel. Runs the Sorrow Bridge circuit, good as any of them, better than most. Left her mistress years back — some hedge-witch up past the snowline, name of Fairweather — bad parting, all very tragic, don't ask me the details because I'd make them up anyway." He leaned forward. "Here's the interesting part. Three nights ago, storm like the sky had a grudge. And into this woman's waystation door, out of that storm, walks a child. Barefoot. Alone. Unafraid — and that's the detail that gives me the shivers, Inquisitor, because no barefoot child in a mountain storm is *unafraid*, unless—"

"Unless," Vane said quietly.

"Mm. And the child talks. Or makes noises, anyhow. And the guild scouts who saw them come through — I bought two rounds and a lie apiece out of them — say the words weren't trade-tongue, weren't lowland dialect, weren't anything. Except." Fitch's eye gleamed. "Except the guide knew one of them. Knew it and went white as milk. And my contact in the guild says Corvo himself has since offered this Oyelaran woman a fortune — a *fortune*, mind — to deliver the child south to a buyer in Lowmark. Which means Corvo knows what the child is worth to somebody. And Corvo knowing a thing is worth money means the thing is worth more money than Corvo can imagine."

The candle flame stood perfectly still, as if listening.

"A child," Vane said. "Speaking the old tongue. In the company of a smuggler-guide. Bound for Lowmark, through the passes, under guild protection." She closed her traveling desk with a small, decisive click. "Fitch. Where is this waystation?"

"High up. Wolfmark Hollow, they call the valley. But you'll not catch them — they're a week gone already, and the roads up there eat soldiers for breakfast. Ask me how I know." He grinned. "Ask anybody."

"They will come down eventually," Vane said. "Everything comes down from the mountains eventually. Wool comes down. Tin comes down. Smugglers come down." She rose and stood at the window, looking north into the dark where the passes were, though the dark gave back nothing but her own reflection — a small straight woman with tired eyes, holding a candle against three hundred years of silence. "And children come down. Especially children bound for buyers. Buyers write letters, Fitch. Buyers pay in traceable coin. A child like that leaves a wake."

"That it does." Fitch pocketed his fee. "One more thing, gratis, because it's the part I like least. The scouts say the child hums, walking. Just little tunes, nothing you'd notice." His grin faded. "And where the child walked, Inquisitor — through the dead orchards along the Hollowmere road — the trees flowered. Out of season. Dead branches, thirty years barren, in full blossom overnight. My contact swears it on his mother."

Vane stood motionless for a long moment.

Flowering dead orchards. A voice that predated kingdoms, loose in the hills, in the keeping of a smuggler with a guilty conscience and a debt-ridden guildmaster's hand on her collar, rolling south toward the lowlands like a spark rolling toward a hayfield.

Three hundred years of silence, and somewhere above the snowline, a child was singing it awake.

"Corporal Brannic," Vane said, without turning from the window, and her voice was calm, and clear, and terrible in its gentleness. "Wake the men. We ride for Wolfmark Hollow at first light."

Behind her, Fitch paused in the doorway, pulling his hood up against the rain.

"Wolfmark Hollow's a hard road, Inquisitor," he offered.

"Yes," said Inquisitor Vane. "Most merciful roads are."
