## 7. The Road Through Hollowmere

The trouble with children, Hazel had decided somewhere around the second mile of the Hollowmere road, was that they asked questions.

Not Wren, though. That was the strange part. The child hadn't spoken a word of anything since they'd left the waystation — not trade-tongue, not the old language that made the back of Hazel's neck itch like a draft under a door. They just walked, barefoot in borrowed boots two sizes too big, humming.

It wasn't an annoying hum. That was the other strange part. It was one of those tunes that got into your teeth, the kind you caught yourself whistling an hour later without knowing where it came from. Hazel had already caught herself twice and stopped both times with the particular irritation of a woman who did not like being handled by a melody.

"Boots too big?" she asked, mostly to hear her own voice. "We can stuff them with straw at the next farmstead."

Wren looked up at her with those grey eyes — eyes the color of the sky over Thornfell's passes just before snow, which was a comparison Hazel resented making because she didn't know why she knew it — and shook their head. Then, after a moment, held up one finger. *Wait.*

Hazel waited. She was good at waiting. Waiting was ninety percent of smuggling; the other ten percent was arithmetic and lying.

Wren crouched, tugged off a boot, and tipped it. Out rolled a pebble, a beetle (alive, indignant), and a curl of birch bark. The child examined each item with the gravity of a magistrate reviewing evidence, put the beetle gently in the grass, kept the pebble, and returned the bark to their pocket.

"You're cataloguing," Hazel said. "You're a walking customs house."

Wren smiled. It was the first smile Hazel had seen from them, and it went straight past all her defenses like a coin through a slot. She scowled to cover it, which was her usual method, and walked on.

* * *

Hollowmere earned its name honestly. The road dropped through a valley where a lake had once been — you could still see its ghost in the shape of the land, the flat green basin, the reeds gone woody and wrong, the farms built on the old shoreline with foundations that sank a little every spring. The orchards down there had died decades ago when the water table shifted. Everyone said so. Black trees in rows, brittle as burnt matches, standing in the mist like an audience waiting for a play that got cancelled.

Guild caravans avoided Hollowmere in daylight. Too open, too flat, too many eyes from the ridge farms. But the ridge farms were empty this season — blight, or taxes, or both — and Hazel needed the miles. Lowmark was four days out if she pushed, five if she was honest, and she had promised Corvo's man delivery by week's end.

*Delivery.* She turned the word over as they walked. Not *handover*. Not *sale*. Delivery, like a parcel. Like flour.

Wren hummed.

The tune changed as they entered the valley. Hazel noticed it the way you notice weather — not all at once, but your bones know before you do. The melody had been something wandering and minor, a walking song. Now it settled. Deepened. Became the kind of thing you might sing over a cradle, or over a grave, if you were the sort who believed graves could hear.

They passed the first dead orchard an hour after dawn.

Hazel heard it before she saw it: a sound like knuckles cracking, dozens of small sharp reports, and she had a knife in her hand before her brain caught up — ambush, scouts, guild trouble — and then she stood there in the mist with her blade out like an idiot while an apple tree blossomed.

Not quickly. Not miraculously, in the way of temple stories, with light and trumpets. Slowly, the way real things happen. Buds swelling along black branches with a creak like an old door. Then white. Then petals, opening one by one in the cold morning air, out of season by three months and thirty years, while Wren stood beneath it humming and looking up with an expression of polite attention, as though the tree were a guest who'd finally arrived at a party.

Hazel sheathed her knife. Took it out again. Put it away properly this time, because fumbling made her nervous, and she hated being nervous more than she hated most things.

"That," she said, "is not possible."

Wren glanced back at her, and there was something in the look — not guilt, exactly. Apology, maybe. Or the patient expression of someone who has explained this before, to people who weren't listening.

The next tree opened. Then the next. Down the row, the whole dead orchard began to wake like a congregation standing for a hymn, blossom spreading through the black branches in a slow white wave, and the smell hit Hazel all at once — apple blossom in autumn, sweet and cold and utterly wrong, so wrong it made her eyes sting.

She did what any sensible smuggler would do.

She checked the ridgelines.

Because wonders were lovely, and wonders paid well in certain markets, but wonders in the open under a pale sky with witnesses on the heights were a noose with her name on the tag. She swept the mist along the eastern ridge, the western, the saddle to the north where the road climbed toward the shrine ruin—

Two figures. Horseback. Grey cloaks, no colors. Guild cut, the way guild men wore neutrality like a uniform.

Hazel moved without deciding to move. Her hand closed on Wren's shoulder — small, warm, startlingly solid — and she steered them off the road into the lee of a fallen drystone wall, down behind a hawthorn that had at least the decency to still be alive.

"Hum quieter," she breathed. "Or don't hum."

Wren looked at her. And here was the thing Hazel would turn over for nights afterward, the splinter working deeper: the child *stopped*. Instantly, completely, obediently — and the blossoming stopped too. Mid-bud. Half the orchard hung suspended between death and flowering, buds swollen and white-tipped and frozen, like a sentence interrupted.

Like the world had been listening to the child, and now it waited.

Hazel's mouth had gone dry. She was a woman who could quote freight rates for nine contraband categories from memory, who had once talked a toll-reeve out of a hanging with nothing but a forged bishop's letter and nerve, and she crouched behind a hawthorn with her heart going like a drum in a parade and thought, with terrible clarity:

*This is not cargo.*

* * *

The riders came down the switchback at an amble, the way men ride when they're bored rather than hunting. Hazel knew the nearer one — Dree, a lowlands scout with a face like a wet Tuesday and a habit of chewing willow bark until his jaw clicked. The other she didn't know. New blood. Young. He was staring at the orchard.

"Dree," the young one said. His voice carried in the flat valley air, and Hazel winced. "Dree. Look at the trees."

"I'm looking at the road."

"The trees are *blooming*."

"They're dead, is what they are. Been dead since my da was weaned." A pause. The click of willow bark against teeth. Then, slower: "...They are blooming."

Hazel pressed herself flatter against the cold stone. Beside her, Wren sat perfectly still, hands folded in their lap, watching the horses through the hawthorn twigs with the calm of a child watching ducks.

"Out of season," the young one whispered. "That's — Dree, that's not right. My gran used to say the Hollowmere orchards were cursed ground. Said the old country's under here somewhere. Said—"

"Your gran said a lot of things, and half of 'em got her neighbors questioned." Dree's voice had gone careful, the boredom stripped out of it. "You see anyone on the road?"

"No."

"See anyone *off* the road?"

A long pause. Hazel counted her knives without meaning to. Four on her belt, one in her boot, one sewn into her collar where a respectable woman would keep a brooch.

"Nah," the young one said finally. "Who'd be off the road? It's Hollowmere."

"Exactly. Nobody comes to Hollowmere. Which is why I don't like that the flowers do." The horses shifted. "Come on. We're due at the tollhouse by noon, and I want to be drunk before whatever-that-is finishes happening."

They rode on. Hazel watched them crest the far rise and drop out of sight, and she stayed crouched a full hundred-count after, because discipline was free and panic cost everything.

Then she sat back against the wall and laughed — one short, unamused breath of a laugh.

"Well," she told Wren. "That's that, then. Scouts talk. Scouts talk to sergeants, sergeants talk to factors, factors talk to Corvo, and Corvo talks to whoever pays him most, which this month might be me and next month might be the Crown. By Friday every ear between here and Lowmark will have heard about a miracle in Hollowmere, and every ear includes—" she tapped her own temple, "—the ones attached to grey coats. Inquisitors don't ride fast, but they ride *forever*."

Wren watched her with that grave attention, head tilted, and Hazel had the sudden uncomfortable sense of being the one catalogued. Pebble, beetle, birch bark, bitter woman.

"Don't look at me like that. I'm not the interesting one." She stood, brushed off her knees, and looked down the valley at the half-woken orchard. The buds hung frozen mid-swelling, neither dead nor alive, and it was somehow worse than either. "Can you... finish it? Or undo it? Whichever's less noticeable."

Wren considered the question with visible seriousness. Then they stood, faced the orchard, and — instead of humming — spoke.

One word. Two syllables, soft, round at the edges, a word shaped like a hand laid gently on a shoulder. Hazel felt it pass through her chest like warmth swallowed too fast.

The buds finished opening. All of them, all at once, a soft white roar of blossom down the whole row, petals shaking loose in the windless air — and then, gracefully, as if embarrassed by their own enthusiasm, the flowers let go. Every petal fell. The trees stood bare and black and dead again, honest about it, wearing a drift of white at their roots like snow that smelled of apples.

Wren turned back to Hazel and shrugged, a small economical shrug that said, clearly, in any language: *best I can do.*

Hazel stared.

Three centuries ago — everyone knew the story, the story was the only thing left — there had been a country called Thornfell. It had offended the Crown in some way history books declined to specify, and the Crown had done what crowns do: burned the records, renamed the roads, resettled the survivors until the name dissolved. There was no Thornfell on any map. There was no Thornfell in any registry. If you said the word aloud in a market town, people would frown and ask if you meant Thorndale, and the frown would have a little fear in it, and you'd learn to stop saying it.

Rosalind had said it freely. Rosalind had taught it to a fifteen-year-old apprentice in a kitchen that smelled of rosemary, drawing letters in spilled flour, saying *names are load-bearing, Hazel, they hold the roof up*, and Hazel had called it superstition and packed her bag.

And now here was a barefoot child in the middle of a drowned valley, politely asking dead trees to bloom and having them say yes, speaking the language that had no country left to live in.

Hazel did the arithmetic. It was what she was for. Cargo value: incalculable, therefore catastrophic. Witnesses: two, loose-tongued, guild-affiliated. Distance to Lowmark: four days. Probability of reaching the buyer clean: dropping by the hour. Probability of the Crown intercepting: rising. Probability that she could hand this specific small person over to a collector of extinct things and sleep afterward:

She didn't finish that sum. She noticed herself not finishing it, and that frightened her more than the riders had.

"Right," she said aloud, briskly, because briskness was armor. "New plan. We avoid the tollhouse, cut north through the shrine ruins, and you—" she pointed at Wren, "—do not speak, hum, whistle, sneeze, or *breathe musically* until we're clear of the valley. Understood?"

Wren nodded solemnly. Then reached into their pocket, took out the pebble, and offered it to her.

Hazel looked at it. A grey pebble, ordinary, damp. An absurd little gift.

"What am I supposed to do with this?"

Wren patted their own chest. Pointed at the orchard. Pointed at the pebble. Held it out again, insistent, and their expression was so earnest, so matter-of-fact, that Hazel took it before she could stop herself.

It was warm. That was the thing. The pebble was warm as a held hand, though it had come out of a boot in October, and it stayed warm in her palm while she stood there feeling like a woman reading a letter addressed to someone she used to be.

She put it in her pocket next to her mother's ring, which was the last thing she owned that wasn't owed to somebody, and told herself the arrangement meant nothing.

"Come on," she said. "Long way round. Keep close."

They walked north through the mist, past orchards that had briefly remembered being alive, and behind them — though neither of them saw it, and neither of them needed to — the young scout reined in on the ridge, looked back at the impossible white drifts under the black trees, and touched his cap-brim the way you do passing a grave.

By nightfall he'd told six people. By week's end, the story would reach three sets of ears that mattered, and one of them belonged to a man who burned archives for mercy's sake, and he would write one word in the margin of his report before sealing it.

The word was *key*.
