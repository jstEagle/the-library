## 8. Bargain at the Drowned Chapel

The chapel had been drowning for two hundred years and was in no hurry to finish.

It sat in a hollow below the old causeway road, half-swallowed by a lake that had crept up on it the way debts do — slowly, then all at once. The bell tower still stood above the waterline, its bronze bell green with rot, and the nave lay just beneath the surface so that on still days you could look down through water gone clear as glass and see the drowned congregation: pews, an altar, a stone font with rainwater and tadpoles sharing it amicably.

Hazel had used it as a meeting place three times before, because smugglers love a landmark nobody else wants and because the chapel's acoustics were a miracle — whisper at the altar and your voice arrived at the bell tower without ever passing through the air between. You could talk business with a man fifty yards away and neither of you would hear a footstep coming.

She stood on the chapel roof now, tiles slick under her boots, watching the light go long and gold across the water. Wren sat beside her, cross-legged, barefoot despite Hazel's best efforts, eating the last of the waybread with the solemn concentration of someone who considered food a serious subject.

"Don't drop crumbs," Hazel said. "Crumbs attract fish, fish attract fishermen, fishermen ask questions."

Wren looked at her with the flat, unblinking patience of a cat being lectured, and then — deliberately, Hazel would swear to it later — dropped one crumb over the edge of the roof into the water below.

"That's mutiny," Hazel said. "I want you to know I recognize it as such."

Something happened then that she would think about for years afterward, though she didn't understand it yet. The crumb touched the surface. The ripples went out. And the water *answered* — not with sound, but with a soft green shimmer that spread from the crumb outward in rings, and along the shallows below, the drowned weeds lifted and swayed as if a breeze had moved through them, though there was no wind and the surface hadn't stirred.

Wren said something in the old tongue. Three syllables, low and gentle, like a lullaby half-remembered. The shimmer brightened, warmed, and faded.

Hazel's skin prickled. She looked around the hollow — the reeds, the causeway, the darkening sky — all of it exactly where it should be, exactly what it should be. And all of it, for one breath, more awake than it had been a moment ago.

"What did you say?"

Wren pointed at the water, then at the crumb, then pressed both hands flat over their heart. A gift. A thanks. An offering given back to the world that made it.

"You thanked the lake," Hazel guessed. "For the bread."

Wren nodded once, gravely.

"Well." Hazel cleared her throat and found it tighter than she expected. "Don't do that in front of anyone."

* * *

Anyone turned out to be a problem, because anyone was already there.

She heard the whistle first — two notes, low-high, the guild signal for *friend approaching, count your fingers afterward* — and saw a lantern bobbing along the causeway above the hollow. She knew the walk before she knew the face: the rolling, unhurried gait of a man who had never once hurried anywhere in his life and considered hurry a form of bad manners.

"Odd Simeon," she said under her breath. "Of course. Of course it's him."

Wren tilted their head.

"He's a guild factor," Hazel explained, gathering the child close with one arm. "He prices things. That's his whole job. He looks at a cargo and tells Corvo what it's worth, and he has never once been wrong, which is the most frightening thing about him." She hesitated. "Second most frightening. He also hums."

The lantern came down the switchback trail, and Odd Simeon resolved out of the dusk: a broad, comfortable man in a coat that had been good twenty years ago and had decided to stay good out of loyalty. His face was round and mild, his eyes were not. Smugglers said Simeon could price a man's soul by looking at his boots, and they said it the way sailors say a ship is unlucky — jokingly, and absolutely believing it.

"Hazel Oyelaran." His voice was warm as bathwater. "Corvo said you'd come this way. I said, Hazel comes every way or no way, but she comes."

"Simeon. What brings the guild's finest arithmetic to a drowned church?"

"Arithmetic brings me everywhere." He stopped at the shore and set his lantern on a stone, and the light reached across the water and found them on the roof. His gaze went to Wren, stayed, and softened in a way that made Hazel's stomach drop, because Simeon never softened without calculating the value of doing so first. "And this will be the cargo."

"The cargo is a child who got separated from their people in the storm. I'm owed passage money for escorting them to kin."

"Mm," said Simeon. He crouched at the water's edge, unhurried, and looked into the flooded nave the way other men read ledgers. Then he straightened, brushed off his knees, and said, pleasantly: "Show me."

"Show you what?"

"Whatever it is." He smiled. It was a kind smile, genuinely kind; that was the horror of Odd Simeon. "You forget, girl, I priced the job. A mute child, lost, worth escort fees? Corvo doesn't pay escort fees. Corvo pays *retrieval* fees, and retrieval means somebody wants it back badly enough to bleed. So either you're lying to me, which would sadden us both, or—" he tipped his head at Wren, "—the goods are more interesting than advertised. Show me, and we'll keep this friendly."

Hazel ran the arithmetic. It came up short everywhere she tried it. Refuse, and Simeon reported to Corvo that she was hiding something, and the guild started pulling threads. Comply, and—

And Wren solved it for her, because of course they did. They had been listening with their head tipped, following tone if not words, and something in Simeon's voice — the pleasantness stretched thin over appetite — they clearly understood better than Hazel wished they could. They slid down the roof tiles to the eave, dropped the last few feet to the shingle, walked to the water's edge, and knelt.

They put one hand in the lake.

"Wren—" Hazel started.

Three syllables, spoken softly, almost apologetically, the way you'd speak to something sleeping.

The drowned chapel woke up.

Not violently. That was the thing Hazel couldn't explain later to herself, let alone to anyone else. The water simply *cleared*, top to bottom, mud and silt settling away as if the lake had been asked politely to tidy itself. Light came up out of the deep — a soft gold, lamplight-colored, though no lamp burned down there — and the whole nave became visible, every pew, every stone, and along the walls the painted saints, centuries drowned, their colors suddenly fresh as yesterday's work. And the bell in the tower, green and dead, gave off one note. Not rung. Just — remembered itself, briefly, the way a man asleep might smile at a name in a dream.

Then it all settled back. The murk crept in. The light sank. But the bell's note hung in the air a moment longer than any note had a right to, and while it hung, every bird in the hollow went silent, and the reeds bowed, all together, toward the child kneeling at the water.

When it was done, Wren took their hand out of the lake, wiped it on their borrowed shirt, and looked up at Odd Simeon with an expression of perfect, guileless trust.

Simeon did not move for a long time. When he finally spoke, his voice had changed — the warmth still there, but underneath it something older and colder, the voice of a man revising a number upward very fast.

"Well," he said. "Well, well, well."

"Simeon—"

"Escort fees." He laughed, short and disbelieving. "Oh, Hazel. Oh, you dear, doomed fool. Do you have any idea what you're carrying?"

"A child. Lost. Cold. Hungry."

"Yes," Simeon agreed mildly. "And I am going to be very rich, and you are going to be very paid, and neither of those facts is going to help you sleep." He picked up his lantern. "New terms. Corvo's buyer in Lowmark doubled the figure when he heard 'unlicensed speech.' After tonight I'd triple it, but I'm feeling generous, so I'll settle for double-plus. Which means, my dear guide, that your cut just changed too — and so did your obligations. The guild doesn't insure miracles for free."

"We had terms."

"We had terms for a *child*." He said the word gently, sadly, as if correcting a sum in a schoolboy's workbook. "This is not a child. This is a *country*. Or the last word of one, anyway, and words are worth more than children. Ask any king."

Hazel stepped down off the roof, landing between Wren and Simeon with her hand near her knife, knowing even as she did it that the knife was theater and Simeon knew it too. "What are you asking?"

"Passage tax. Guild law. Unusual cargo, unusual levy." He smiled again, apologetic, sincere. "Half your fee. Up front."

"Half my fee is—"

"Is everything you were getting. Yes. I know. I priced it." He began, unhurriedly, to turn away. "Or refuse, and explain to Master Corvo why the cargo is worth ten times what you claimed, and why you tried to cheat the guild of its share. Take your time. I'll walk slowly."

* * *

He walked slowly. He reached the second switchback before Hazel called out.

"Wait."

He waited, lantern swinging, patient as arithmetic.

"There's nothing here," Hazel said. "You know that. You've seen my ledger. I owe the guild for the winter stock, I owe Corvo for the pass-tolls he covered when the Vane business shut the roads, I owe the ferryman, I owe—" She stopped, because listing it aloud was its own kind of drowning. "My fee is already spent. It was spent before I earned it. That's how you built the contract, Simeon. That's how you build all of them."

"I know," Simeon said kindly. "That's why I take payment in kind instead."

"In kind. What does the guild want, my boots?"

"It wants," said Simeon, "what you own that isn't owed."

The wind came down the valley and moved the reeds, and the water whispered against the chapel stones, and Hazel stood very still.

There was exactly one thing.

Her mother's ring. Thin gold, worn nearly to wire on the inside from forty years of a woman's hands working rope and dough and Hazel's hair into braids. It had no gem. It had no value anyone honest would recognize. It lived on a cord around Hazel's neck because her finger had grown too big for it years ago, and she had not taken it off since the night Rosalind buried her mother and Hazel had stood at the graveside already halfway to leaving, and the ring was the only object in the world that belonged to Hazel Oyelaran free and clear — no debt, no lien, no guild mark scratched into it somewhere. Everything else she owned, she owned on sufferance. The ring was hers the way her name was hers.

She thought about saying no. She thought about the second switchback, and the third, and the long report Simeon would carry back to Corvo, and the threads that would start pulling — the waystation lease, the ferryman, the winter stock, all of it guild paper, all of it levers. She was good at arithmetic. It was the one lesson her old life had taught her well: everything is owed, and everything can be collected, and the only question is what you pay with.

"Give me a moment," she said.

"Take two," said Simeon generously. "Time is cheap."

She untied the cord with fingers that were steadier than she expected, which felt like a betrayal in itself. The ring came off into her palm, warm from her skin, light as a lie.

Wren was watching. Of course Wren was watching. The child looked from the ring to Hazel's face, and something passed over their expression — not understanding of the transaction, they couldn't know the arithmetic, but understanding of *her*, some direct unmediated reading of what the small gold circle meant, the way they seemed to read everything, the way the lake had read them. Their brow furrowed. They shook their head, small and definite. *No.*

"It's fine," Hazel said, and was surprised to find her voice worked. "It's just metal. It's just—" She laughed, one dry note with nothing behind it. "It's just the last thing I own, and apparently that was always going to be the price. Everything's owed eventually. My mother taught me that too, in her way. She'd have found it funny."

Wren did not find it funny. Wren crossed the shingle, took Hazel's hand in both of theirs — small hands, cold from the evening air — and closed her fingers back over the ring, and held them shut, and looked up at her with an urgency that needed no language at all.

"I have to," Hazel said gently. "You don't understand guild math. Nobody understands guild math. That's what makes it math."

Wren held on a moment longer. Then, slowly, they opened Hazel's fingers themselves, and pressed the ring flat into her palm one more time — and leaned down and breathed on it, one soft breath, and murmured a single syllable of the old tongue, so quiet Hazel barely caught it, a word like a hand laid on a shoulder.

Then they stepped back and nodded: *done.*

Nothing visible happened. Hazel looked at the ring. It was the same ring. Thin gold, worn smooth, no gem. And yet holding it now was like holding it the day after the burial, when grief had made every ordinary object ring like a struck bell — the weight of it suddenly exact, suddenly *present*, the way a room is present the moment you realize you'll miss it.

She didn't understand what Wren had done. She understood that it was a gift, and that gifts, in her experience, were the most expensive things there were.

"Simeon." She walked to the waterline and held the ring up so the lantern light caught it. "Here's your levy."

Simeon came back down the trail. He took the ring, weighed it on his palm — she watched him price it, watched the little flicker in his eyes as the number came back *nothing, worthless, sentimental scrap* — and then he paused. Turned it over. Held it closer to the light.

His eyebrows rose, very slightly, the way a man's rise when a ledger balances in a way it shouldn't.

"Hm," he said.

"What?"

"Nothing." He pocketed the ring, and his mildness returned like water closing over a stone. "Payment accepted. Terms amended. Half your fee restored, cargo delivered to Lowmark, buyer's agent is a man called Ferren Gault — gray house on Candlemaker's Row, knock twice and once. We leave the details to you." He hoisted his lantern and started up the trail, then paused, and spoke without turning around. "One piece of advice, free of charge, which you may price at what it cost you. That child thanked your lake tonight. Things remember being thanked. The mountains especially." He resumed walking, humming — of course humming — some old tuneless air. "Sleep well, Hazel Oyelaran."

The lantern bobbed up the switchbacks and out of sight, and the hollow went dark except for the stars and the last grey light on the water.

* * *

They didn't make camp in the hollow. Hazel wanted distance between herself and the chapel before dark, though she couldn't have said why — the place had been a meeting spot for years, safe as anything was safe. But tonight the drowned saints' gold light kept surfacing in her memory, and the bell's remembered note, and the reeds bowing, and she climbed the causeway road until the lake lay below and behind, and made a cold camp in the lee of an old boundary marker.

A boundary marker. She noticed it only as she was laying out the blankets: a squat mossy stone, older than the road, carved with a symbol she didn't recognize and half-did — a door, or a gate, or a standing stone, worn shallow by centuries of weather. Below it, letters in no alphabet she knew, though the curves of them nagged at her like a word on the tip of the tongue.

Wren touched the stone as they passed it. Stopped. Looked down at the carving for a long moment.

"What?" Hazel said. "What is it?"

Wren traced the symbol with one fingertip, careful, the way you'd touch a scar. Then they looked up at Hazel, and their face did something it hadn't done before — not wonder, not trust, not the grave patience that was their usual weather. Fear. Small, controlled, and utterly certain.

They pointed east, deeper into the mountains, toward the high passes where the maps went blank and the smuggler roads thinned into rumor. Then they pressed their palm flat against their own chest.

Then they pointed at the carving again.

Hazel followed the gesture and felt, absurdly, the hair lift on her arms. Because the stone — old, dead, moss-eaten stone — was warm. Faintly, barely, like a hearthstone hours after the fire, like something underneath the ground remembering heat. And as she watched, in the last light, the worn carving seemed to deepen, the lines sharpening, as if the mountain were recalling an old shape the way the bell had recalled its note.

Far off in the eastern dark, so far it might have been imagination, might have been wind, might have been nothing — the mountains groaned. One long, deep sound, stone settling on stone, the kind of sound Hazel had heard a hundred times in the passes and never thought twice about.

She thought twice about it now.

"Wren," she said quietly. "What's out there?"

The child looked east, and hugged their knees, and did not answer — and for the first time since the storm, Hazel found she wasn't sure whether the silence was because Wren couldn't speak her language, or because some things had no word in any tongue, or because the child was listening to something in the dark that answered before questions were finished.

The stone stayed warm all night. Hazel told herself it was geothermal. She had heard Rosalind use the word once, years ago, in another life, and she clung to it the way a drowning woman clings to driftwood, and she did not sleep, and in the morning the passes were waiting, and she took Wren's hand, and they went east.
