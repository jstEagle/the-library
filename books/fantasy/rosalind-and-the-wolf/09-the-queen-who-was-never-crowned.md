## 9. The Queen Who Was Never Crowned

The hollow beneath the mountains had once been a throne room.

Maren of Thornfell knew this because she had been crowned in it, in the way that mattered and in the way that did not. The way that mattered: her father's circlet on her head, the lords kneeling, the old tongue spoken over her like warm water poured from a great height. The way that did not: three hundred years later, no kingdom recognized it, no chronicle recorded it, and the stone floor where she had knelt to receive her crown was now the ceiling of a cave that did not officially exist, in a country that had been unwritten so thoroughly that even its ghosts had been issued a formal apology and then forgotten.

She sat on what remained of her throne — a chair-shaped absence in the rock, worn smooth by centuries of her sitting — and listened to the dark above her.

For a long time, the dark had said nothing. That was the punishment, really. Not the erasure itself; erasure, she had discovered, was almost restful. No taxes to collect. No border disputes. No ambassadors lying with tremendous skill about grain shipments. Just silence, and the slow company of her people, who repeated their last day the way a bell repeats its note: faithfully, endlessly, without knowing they were doing it.

But tonight — if it was tonight; time down here was a rumor — the dark above her said something.

It said a word.

Maren stood up so fast that three centuries of dust fell off her like an insulted cat leaving a lap.

"Say that again," she told the ceiling.

The ceiling, being a ceiling, declined. But the word hung in the stone anyway, the way smoke hangs in wool, and Maren walked toward it with her hands out like a woman walking toward a fire in winter.

*Thessaly,* the word had been. Or something near it. The old tongue was a living thing and it moved, the way rivers move, and you could not step in the same syllable twice. But she knew the shape of it. She knew the weight of it. It was the word for *come home*, spoken in the form one used for children, or for very small animals, or for kings who had wandered off during their own coronation feast, which had happened once, to her father, and was never mentioned again except at every dinner party for forty years.

Someone up there was speaking Thornfell's language.

Not reading it. Not reciting it from some smuggled scrap of parchment, the way scholars sometimes did before Vane's people found them and made examples. Speaking it. The way you speak the language you dream in. The way you speak the language your mother used to call you in from the fields.

A child, then. It had to be. The tongue did not survive in adults. Adults learned languages the way they learned everything else — carefully, defensively, with one eye on the exit. A child simply *was* in a language, the way a fish is in water, and this water had been drained from the world three hundred years ago, and yet here was a small voice swimming in it anyway, somewhere above her, on the mountain roads, calling *come home* in the grammar of cradles.

Maren reached the wall of the hollow and put both palms against it.

"Hello," she said, in trade-tongue first, because she was polite, and then in the old speech, because she was honest: *I hear you. Whoever you are. I hear you.*

The stone did not answer. Stone rarely did; it was the worst conversationalist in any kingdom, which was why she had spent her reign holding court in orchards. But far above — so far above that the sound arrived more as intention than noise — something shifted. A second voice. Lower. Rougher. Trade-tongue, clipped short, the accent of the lowland passes, the accent of someone who counted things.

And under both voices, faint as a hairline crack in a bell, a third sound. A working. Hedge-craft, old and homely, the kind of magic that smelled like bread and cut herbs. Someone up there was warding a campfire.

Three travelers. One of them spoke the dead language of a dead country as if it were alive, because as far as that child knew, it was.

Maren laughed.

It was not a nice laugh. She was aware of that. Three centuries of sitting in the dark will curdle anyone's laugh, and hers had started out merely imperious. But it was also, underneath everything, a hopeful laugh, and hope in the hands of the very patient is a dangerous implement.

* * *

"You're smiling," said Aldous.

Aldous had been her Lord Chancellor for eleven years before the end, and he had been dead for three hundred, and he had still not stopped disapproving of her posture. He appeared now at the edge of the throne room's absence, flickering the way he always did when he was pleased and pretending not to be — a tall smudge of a man with the ghost of a chain of office around the ghost of his neck.

"I am not smiling," said Maren. "I am strategizing."

"You are smiling like the day you declared war on Kettering over a bridge."

"That was also strategizing."

"That was a Tuesday," said Aldous. "You declared war before breakfast. Your father said you had the strategic instincts of a wet cat and the follow-through of an avalanche, and history has yet to prove him wrong on either count."

"My father," said Maren, "is not here to be quoted."

"No," Aldous agreed gently. "He went through. When the Door closed, he went through with the others who chose passage. You stayed."

"I stayed," said Maren, "because somebody had to keep the books."

This was true, and it was also not the whole truth, and Aldous knew both halves, because he had kept the other set of books himself, the ones a chancellor keeps in his heart where the queen cannot audit them. He let it lie. That was the trouble with dead chancellors: they had infinite patience and no leverage, so all they could do was wait for you to arrive at your own conclusions, which took centuries, and hurt the whole way.

"There is a child," Maren said, pressing her palm flat to the stone again. "Above us. On the high passes. Speaking the tongue."

"I felt it." Aldous's flicker steadied, which was how he showed alarm. "Half the hollow felt it. The baker woke up. Do you know what it means when the baker wakes up? He only wakes when the loop bends. The last time he woke was when Vane's grandfather burned the archive at Hollowmere, and he wept flour for a week. Flour, Maren. We don't have flour. He wept the memory of flour."

"The child bent the loop? From up there?"

"A word here, a word there. Like knocking on a table in another room." Aldous drifted closer, and his edges sharpened, which meant he was afraid and had decided to be useful instead. "The country heard it. All of us heard it. Three hundred years of quiet, and then a child says *thessaly* into the storm, and every soul in this hollow turned their head at once. Do you understand what that is like? To have a neck again, even for a moment?"

Maren understood. Her own neck ached pleasantly with the memory of turning.

"What else do you feel?" she asked.

"A witch-warding. Hedge-work, strong and kind. And a third mind — hard, counting, frightened of itself." Aldous paused. "That one I don't like. That one smells like ledgers."

"They all smell like ledgers," said Maren. "The living are mostly ledger. It's what holds them upright." She tilted her head, listening to the far-off voices again, thin as birdsong through six hundred feet of granite. "A hedge-witch, a guide, and a child who speaks the unmade tongue, traveling my mountains in winter. That is not a coincidence. That is a story. Stories come to countries the way weather comes to coasts. You cannot stop them. You can only decide whether to be the cliff or the harbor."

"And which are you planning to be?"

Maren considered the question honestly, which was more than most queens managed.

"Harbor," she said. "Obviously. Harbors get to keep things."

"Maren—"

"Don't 'Maren' me in that tone, Aldous. I have been dead for three hundred years and polite for two hundred and ninety of them. I am owed at least one decade of open ambition."

"You were never polite a day in your life. You were *courteous*. There's a difference, and it's the difference between a knife and a sword."

"Thank you."

"It wasn't entirely a compliment."

"It was entirely a compliment," said Maren, "and I'm keeping it."

* * *

She went down among her people, because a queen who does not walk among her people is only a statue with opinions, and her father had said that too, usually while hiding from petitioners in the orchard.

The hollow was not large, though it was larger than it looked, the way grief is. It held perhaps four thousand souls, which was perhaps a tenth of what Thornfell had been, the rest having gone through the Door on the last night, choosing exile in whatever lay beyond over existence as an echo. Those who remained had chosen to stay with their last day. Maren had never decided whether that was loyalty or cowardice, and she suspected the answer changed depending on whether she was thinking about them or about herself.

They were in the evening of it now, as they always were. The last evening. The wedding in the lower field, where the bride's mother wept the same seven tears in the same order, and the groom stepped on the same hem, and everyone laughed the same laugh, warm and real and sealed under glass. The bakery with its loaves forever rising, the baker forever turning toward the window where the first red light of the burning showed, forever not yet screaming. The schoolhouse where the teacher wrote tomorrow's date on the slate and the slate, mercifully, forgot it overnight.

Maren walked through them and they did not see her, because the loop did not include her; she existed slightly outside her own last day, the way a host exists slightly outside her own party. She touched the bride's mother's shoulder as she passed. The woman shivered and wiped a tear that hadn't fallen yet and would fall in exactly the same place, and Maren thought, as she always thought: *I will fix this. I will find the arithmetic that makes this end differently.*

That was the whole of her reign now. Not ruling — arithmetic. Somewhere there was a sum that undid the erasure. Somewhere there was a column of numbers in which Thornfell came back whole: the fields, the laws, the terrible poetry, the bridge she'd gone to war over (Kettering had built it back crooked out of spite, and she had loved it crooked). Every century she checked the sums again. Every century they came out the same way: the country could live again, but the living would have to pay.

That was the part the maps had gotten right, in their way, by leaving her out entirely. Erasure was not free. Neither was resurrection. Everything cost, and the only question — the eternal, filthy, royal question — was *who pays*.

The child's voice came again, fainter now, higher on the passes. Storm up there. She could taste the snow through the stone, cold and mineral, and under the snow the small bright syllables, and under those the guide's counting heart, and under *that*, steady as a hearthstone, the hedge-witch's working.

*Come home,* the child had said. In the cradle-grammar. To whom, the child probably didn't know. Words like that go out of a speaker like arrows out of a blind archer, striking whatever needs striking.

Maren stood in the middle of her looping kingdom, in the evening that never ended, and let herself want things. It was a discipline, wanting. She did it daily, like scales on a pianoforte, like drills with a sword. She wanted her fields back. She wanted her name in mouths that weren't echoes. She wanted the Door to stand open and the exiles to pour home and the maps to tear themselves out of shame. She wanted, with a patience that had gone past ferocity into something geological, to stop being the queen of a grave.

And here, at last, after three hundred years, came a key walking across her mountains on small bare feet, escorted by a guilty woman and a hedge-witch, straight toward the one threshold in the world that led anywhere at all.

If the child reached the Door, the child would go through, and the tongue would leave the world again, and the hollow would settle back into its long quiet until the mountains themselves wore down, and that would be that. Justice, of a sort. Mercy, even. The universe was very fond of offering her mercy. It had never once offered her a country.

But if the child came *down* — if the child were brought to the hollow, to her, before the Door — then the sums changed. A speaker of the tongue, inside the erased land, standing on the erased soil. The loop would bend. The Door might open *inward*. Thornfell could rise wearing new flesh, and the borderlands above — the towns and farms and names that had been built on the grave of her country like a house built on a body — well.

Well.

Aldous found her there, in the schoolhouse, watching the teacher write tomorrow's date.

"You've gone quiet," he said. "That's how the war with Kettering started. You went quiet for four days and then annexed a bridge."

"I'm doing arithmetic," said Maren.

"The kind where other people carry the cost?"

"All arithmetic is that kind, Aldous. That's what makes it arithmetic." She watched the chalk move. Tomorrow's date. Always tomorrow's. Never today. "There's a child above us who speaks the tongue. There's a Door that opens one way. And there is a road between them, and I am the country the road crosses, and everything that walks my roads walks through me." She turned, and the flickering light of the loop caught the ghost of a circlet that no longer existed on the ghost of a head that barely did. "Send my dream ahead of me. Find the guide — the counting one, the frightened one. She has a wound; I felt it when she passed over the old market square, a hole shaped like a person. Wounds are doors too. Everyone knows that. Even the living know that; they just pretend otherwise."

"And what will you offer her?"

"Everything she's lost," said Maren. "It's the only currency I have left, and I have a great deal of it. I've been saving."

Aldous looked at her for a long moment, three hundred years of disapproval gathering behind his flicker, and then — because he had loved her, and love is the one debt no chancellor ever gets repaid — he bowed.

"And if she refuses?" he asked.

Maren smiled the smile that had declared war before breakfast.

"Then I'll make the offer louder," she said.

Far above, through storm and stone, the child's voice rose once more — small, clear, unafraid — and every soul in the hollow turned toward it, four thousand faces tilting like flowers after a sun that had been dead for three hundred years, and the baker dropped a loaf that had never before been dropped, and the loop, for the first time in living memory, which is to say in memory at all, cracked a little wider.

Maren felt the crack run under her feet like spring ice.

"Hold on," she whispered upward, to a child she had never met, in a language only the two of them and the dead could speak. "*I'm coming.*"

And in a shepherd's hut on the high passes, a whiteout closing over the world, Hazel Oyelaran dreamed — for the first time in years — of a green country she had never seen, and a woman on a broken throne, smiling at her like family.
