## 10. Snowbound

### Hazel

The storm came down the pass like a debt collector — no warning, no negotiation, just white.

Hazel had been reading weather for eleven years, and she'd give herself credit for this much: she saw it coming a full hour before it arrived. She also gave herself credit for what she did next, which was to abandon the road entirely and cut uphill toward the shepherd's hut she knew was tucked into the lee of Crag's Shoulder, because the arithmetic was simple. A smuggler caught in a whiteout on an exposed road was dead. A smuggler in a hut was merely inconvenienced, cold, and out of schedule.

She hated being out of schedule. Schedules were how you stayed alive in her trade. But dead was deader than late, as her first guildmaster used to say, and so she hauled Wren up the goat track by the hand while the sky came apart above them.

The hut was exactly as she remembered it: low, stone, smelling of old smoke and older sheep. There was a hearth, half a stack of cut peat left by some shepherd with more foresight than sense, a straw pallet, and one blanket that had probably been gray since before Hazel was born. There was no lock on the door, because there was nothing in the hut worth stealing except shelter, and shelter belonged to whoever got there first.

"Home," Hazel announced, dumping her pack by the hearth. "Palatial, isn't it."

Wren stood in the doorway, snow crusted in their hair, and looked at the hut the way Hazel had once seen a merchant look at a chest of counterfeit coin — carefully, and with dawning disappointment.

"It's not *that* bad," Hazel said, though privately she conceded the child might have a point. She got the peat going, coaxed a flame, and within twenty minutes the room had achieved the particular amber gloom that passed for comfort in the high passes. She dug out her provisions: hard cheese, black bread gone harder, dried plums, and a twist of salt pork. Feast of kings.

Wren ate the plums first. Of course they did. Everyone ate the plums first.

Outside, the wind found its voice and began to scream. Snow hit the shutters like thrown gravel. Hazel listened to it with professional respect and did the sums: two days, maybe three, before the pass cleared. They had food for four if they were careful. Water from the melt-barrel outside, if she could reach it between gusts without becoming part of the landscape.

Which meant three days in a stone box with a child she couldn't talk to.

Hazel had survived ambushes, betrayals, one memorable avalanche, and a decade of guild politics. None of it had prepared her for the specific horror of enforced conversation with a small person who communicated primarily through looks of quiet judgment.

Wren finished the plums, licked their fingers with great thoroughness, and then pointed at Hazel.

"What?"

Wren pointed again, insistently, at Hazel's mouth, then made a shape with their own lips — an exaggerated, deliberate *word*. Then they pointed at Hazel again.

"You want me to talk?"

Wren nodded, with the air of someone confirming a diagnosis.

"I talk plenty," Hazel said. "Ask anyone. Ask the guild. They'll tell you I talk too much, usually when negotiating their rates downward."

Wren stared at her. The fire popped. The wind howled. And Hazel understood, with a sinking feeling, that this was not a request for conversation. This was a lesson plan. The child intended to learn trade-tongue, and had selected Hazel — God help them both — as instructor.

"Right," Hazel said slowly. "Well. Fair warning: my grammar is smuggler's grammar. If you go around saying *gimme* and *where's the profit*, people will know exactly which gutter raised you."

Wren blinked at her, unbothered, waiting.

Hazel sighed, picked up a piece of bread, and held it up. "Bread," she said clearly. "*Bread.*"

"*Bred,*" said Wren.

"Close enough. Bread." She handed it over. Wren took it, solemn as a priest receiving an offering, and repeated the word around a mouthful. Then they pointed at the fire.

"Fire."

"*Fir.*"

"Fire. You've got it. Fire." She pointed at herself. "Hazel."

Wren considered this for a long moment, head tilted, and then said something in the old tongue — a soft, layered word like water over stones — and pointed back at themselves.

"Wren," Hazel said. "I'm calling you Wren. It's shorter."

Wren shrugged, which Hazel was beginning to understand was the child's entire philosophy of names: they were other people's problem.

* * *

### Wren

The woman talked too much, but that was good. Talking was how the words lived.

Wren had been afraid, in the beginning, that words would die in their mouth. That was what the grey men wanted — the ones who burned the paper houses full of paper voices. Words needed mouths, needed ears, needed someone to answer back. A word alone in a head was a lamp in an empty house. It still worked. But nobody saw the light.

So Wren fed the woman words all afternoon, and the woman fed them back, and by the time the light through the shutter went orange and then grey, Wren had a whole pocketful: *bread, fire, snow, cold, sleep, no, yes, more, stop.* Some of them were ugly little words. *Stop* sounded like a door slamming. *No* was worse — flat as a dropped plate. But *more* was nice. *More* had a roundness to it, like a plum.

The woman taught with food, which Wren approved of entirely. Every new word earned a bite. Wren wondered if the woman knew she was doing it, or if it was just how she was built — everything in her life weighed and paid for, even kindness. Especially kindness.

When the dark came fully down and there was nothing to see but firelight, the woman stopped teaching and started doing something else with her face. Wren had learned faces early; they were the only language everyone spoke. This one was the thinking-face, the counting-face. The woman was doing sums behind her eyes. Wren watched her do them and waited.

Finally the woman said, "Your words. The ones you had when we met. Teach me one."

Wren sat up straighter. This was important. This was the opposite of the grey men, who took words away. Someone *asking* for a word — asking politely, the way you ask to borrow bread — that hadn't happened in a long time.

Wren held up a hand, palm open, and said: "*Selu.*"

The woman frowned. "What's it mean?"

Wren thought about how to say it. *Selu* meant the first warm day after winter, when the snow on the south slope went soft and you could smell the ground again. It meant that exact smell. There wasn't one word for it here. Wren pointed at the fire, then made a slow melting gesture, then pressed their hand flat against the floor as if feeling for warmth under it.

"The thaw?" the woman guessed. "First thaw?"

Wren nodded vigorously. "*Selu.*"

"*Selu,*" the woman repeated, and said it well, better than most of her own words, careful with the soft middle. Something in Wren's chest went loose and bright. A word spoken by a new mouth didn't die. It doubled. Somewhere, in whatever place kept count of such things, the tally went up by one.

They traded until the fire burned low. Wren gave: *selu* (first thaw), *harn* (the smell of rain coming), *mavet* (a song sung while working, any work), *lirre* (the space just inside a doorway where you pause before entering). The woman gave: *road, coin, trust* — which she taught with heavy irony — *hungry, warm, safe.*

Wren liked *safe* best. It was a small word but it fit well in the mouth, like a smooth stone. Wren rolled it around silently, practicing, saving it.

Later, wrapped in the one grey blanket with the woman on the pallet beside them, Wren lay listening to the storm batter the hut and felt something unfamiliar. Not fear. Not hunger. The hut was small and smelly and the blanket smelled like sheep, and none of it mattered, because there was a fire and a person who had asked for words instead of taking them.

Wren fell asleep mid-thought, halfway to a word for that feeling, and never found it. Maybe tomorrow. Maybe the woman would help.

### Hazel

Hazel woke at dawn to silence — the deep, woolly silence of a world buried — and to the discovery that she had acquired, at some point in the night, a child attached to her side like a burr.

She lay very still and considered the situation.

In eleven years of guiding contraband through these mountains, Hazel Oyelaran had maintained exactly one rule above all others: *nothing gets close.* Not clients, not colleagues, certainly not cargo. Cargo was weight and risk, measured in coin. You carried it, you delivered it, you forgot it. The moment anything got close, it became leverage — against you, or against itself, and either way somebody bled.

And yet here she was, pinned under ninety pounds of sleeping child, unwilling to move for fear of waking them, and annoyed at herself for being unwilling.

"Ridiculous," she whispered.

Wren stirred, opened one eye, and said, with terrible clarity, "*More?*"

"It's dawn. Breakfast is later. Go back to sleep."

"*More,*" Wren repeated, with the serene confidence of someone who had learned yesterday that this word produced results.

Hazel groaned, got up, and built the fire back. Breakfast was bread, cheese, and the last of the plums, which she split evenly and pretended not to notice Wren watching to confirm the split. Fair dealing. Even a smuggler had standards, especially with children, who remembered.

The storm had not spent itself. Through the gap in the shutters the world was a white roar, the kind of snow that erased distance and direction alike. Three days in this hut, then. Possibly four. Hazel inventoried her patience, found it thinner than her provisions, and decided to spend both carefully.

"So," she said, settling cross-legged by the fire. "Lessons. But fair's fair — you teach me too. One of yours for one of mine. That's how trade works."

Wren nodded at once, eager, and pointed at Hazel expectantly.

"Not yet — eat first." Hazel chewed, thinking. There was a question she'd been carrying since the waystation, since the first impossible word out of the child's mouth, and now, with nowhere to run and nothing to do, it finally worked its way up. "That night. At my door. How did you find me?"

Wren tilted their head. Then they set down their bread, held up one finger — *wait* — and searched for the words. What came out was a strange braided thing: trade-tongue nouns stitched with old-tongue shapes, gestures filling the gaps.

"*Roads... sing. Old roads. Your road—" Wren hummed three notes, low and winding. "—same song. I follow."

Hazel sat with that. "The passes sing."

Wren nodded, matter-of-fact, as though this were common knowledge, as though every child grew up knowing the mountains had music in them.

"They don't," Hazel said. "Sing. To anyone else. I've walked these roads my whole life and they've never sung a note."

Wren looked at her for a long moment, and then reached over and tapped her sternum, twice, gently, and said a word in the old tongue. Then they tapped their own chest and said it again, slightly different, and pointed back and forth between them.

"What's that mean?"

Wren frowned, hunting. Then they cupped their hands together, opening them slowly, like something unfolding. "*Same... seed,*" they tried. "*Different ground.*"

Hazel laughed despite herself. "We're the same seed in different ground? That's either very flattering or an insult to my family, and honestly I can't tell which."

But the laugh came out wrong, cracked in the middle, because underneath it something had shifted. Rosalind used to say things like that. *Names are seeds, Hazel-bird. Everything grows from what it's called.* Hazel had rolled her eyes at fifteen and packed her bag at sixteen, and here she was at twenty-seven, snowbound with a child who spoke in Rosalind's grammar without ever having heard it.

The old tongue and hedge-craft. Two languages she'd sworn off, converging on her from opposite ends of a mountain.

"Next lesson," she said briskly, before the feeling could get traction. "Numbers. If you're going to survive in the lowlands, you need numbers. People will cheat you otherwise." She held up fingers. "One. Two. Three..."

Wren matched her, finger for finger, and then offered their own counting words in exchange — a lilting run of syllables that Hazel made them repeat five times until she had it. By midmorning they had progressed to colors, body parts, and a spirited dispute over whether the word for the hut's smell should be *sheep* or *old smoke* or, per Wren's firm position, simply *home.*

"It is not home," Hazel said. "It's a hut. Home is—" She stopped.

Wren waited, patient as stone.

"Home is a cottage with a crooked chimney," Hazel said finally, to the fire. "With a garden that grows things out of season. With a witch in it who talks too much about names." She cleared her throat. "Anyway. Colors. This is *grey.* Say *grey.*"

* * *

By the second evening they had a vocabulary of perhaps two hundred words, a working pidgin of gesture and guesswork, and a game neither of them had officially agreed to: each would point at something, and the other had to produce the word for it in their own tongue. Points were awarded. Hazel was losing, which she attributed to cheating — Wren had a whole country's worth of words and Hazel had only ever had the lowlands' leavings.

"That's not a fair match," she protested, after failing to name the specific quality of light that came off snow at dusk. Wren had a word for it. Of course they did. "*Shirrel,*" they'd said, hands cupping the air, and Hazel had thought: *we don't even have a word for that, we just squint at it.*

The fire was burning down. Outside, the wind had dropped to a moan rather than a scream, which meant the storm was turning, not ending. Tomorrow, maybe. Hazel poked the peat and decided to say the thing she'd been circling all day.

"Wren. When we get to Lowmark—"

Wren looked up.

"—there are people there who will want to hear you speak. Important people. They'll pay me to bring you, and they'll be..." She hunted for a word Wren would know, and couldn't find an honest one. "...not kind," she finished. "You understand? Not kind."

Wren regarded her steadily. Then they said, in careful trade-tongue, each word placed like a stone in a wall:

"You. Sell. Me?"

The question landed in the hut like a dropped knife.

Hazel opened her mouth. Closed it. Eleven years of lies, and she'd built most of them fast and smooth, but this one stuck in her throat like a fishbone. Because it was Wren asking, and Wren had spent two days feeding her words for free, and somewhere in the last forty-eight hours the arithmetic had quietly changed denominations without her permission.

"It's a job," she said at last, and heard how thin it sounded. "Jobs end. You'll be... provided for. Warm rooms. Good food."

Wren didn't argue. That was the worst part. They just looked at her with those dark, unhurried eyes, and then they reached into the space between them and said one word in the old tongue — softly, the way you'd set something fragile down on a table.

Hazel didn't know it. But she felt it land anyway, the way you feel a change in weather before you can name it. The fire seemed to lean closer. The silence got heavier, warmer, sadder.

"What does that mean?" she asked, roughly.

Wren considered. Then they pointed at Hazel's chest, where the mother's ring hung on its cord beneath her shirt — the one thing she owned that wasn't owed. Then they opened their hand, palm up, offering.

"Gift?" Hazel guessed.

Wren shook their head slowly. Held up the word again, turning it over. Then, laboriously, in trade-tongue: "*Not-gift. Not-trade. Word-for... keeping-someone... in-you. After.*"

"After what?"

Wren touched their own chest. "*After they go.*"

The wind moaned against the shutters. Hazel sat very still, looking at the child who had just handed her a word for grief — a word her language apparently hadn't bothered to build — and understood that she was holding something she could not put a price on, and that this was going to be a problem.

"We should sleep," she said hoarsely. "Long walk tomorrow, if the sky clears."

Wren nodded and curled up against her side without asking, the way you curl up against something you've already decided is yours. Hazel pulled the grey blanket over them both and stared at the dying fire.

Through the gap in the shutters, far down the valley, she saw it and told herself she hadn't: a pinpoint of light moving against the storm's grain. Steady. Deliberate. The kind of light that traveled with purpose — lantern-light, and below it, if she strained, the faintest suggestion of many boots on the frozen road.

Nobody walked the Thornfell passes in a whiteout.

Nobody except the Crown.
