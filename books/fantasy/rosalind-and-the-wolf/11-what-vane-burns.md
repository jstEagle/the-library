## 11. What Vane Burns

Inquisitor Edric Vane had a theory about fire that he shared with no one, because it was the sort of theory that got men reassigned to coastal archives where nothing ever burned but fish.

The theory was this: fire was the most honest of all the Crown's instruments.

A sword killed in anger or fear or ambition — all messy things, all human things. A rope killed in ceremony. A cell killed slowly, by inches, while the prisoner's family stood outside counting the days. But fire killed cleanly, completely, and without appetite. Fire did not hate what it consumed. It simply refused to let a thing continue existing, which was, when you thought about it clearly, the kindest thing one could do for certain kinds of knowledge.

Vane thought about it clearly every morning. He considered clarity a discipline, like prayer, except that he'd stopped praying at nineteen and never missed it.

He stood now in the yard of the Hollowmere tollhouse with his hands folded behind his back, watching three of his clerks stack books in the snow. The books belonged to the tollhouse's little archive — tax ledgers going back eighty years, a surveyor's atlas of the foothills, and, wedged between them like a heresy hiding among receipts, eleven pages of a hand-copied songbook whose marginalia contained words in an alphabet that predated the Kingdom's charter by some centuries.

"Lead-singer's name?" Vane asked.

"Tomas Wicker," said Sister Anthe, his senior clerk, a small gray woman who wrote in a hand so precise it looked printed. "Toll-collector. Thirty-one years of service. Wife, two daughters. The marginalia is old — his grandfather's, we think. He may not even read the script."

"He kept it," Vane said. "That's enough."

Anthe made a note. She did not argue. In nine years she had never once argued with him, which Vane appreciated the way other men appreciated silence: as evidence of a well-run household.

The tollhouse keeper himself stood watching from the doorway, held there by two troopers, and Vane went to speak to him, because he always did. It was important. Not legally — the law required only the burning and the record — but morally, in the private ledger Vane kept that no auditor would ever see.

"Master Wicker."

"Lord Inquisitor." Wicker's voice was steady. His hands were not. "It's just songs. Old ones. My gran taught me the tune, I don't know what the writing says, I swear it on my girls—"

"I believe you," Vane said, and meant it, and watched the man flinch, because being believed by an inquisitor was somehow worse than being disbelieved. Everyone knew what belief led to. "That's rather the point, Master Wicker. You don't know what it says. None of us do. That is precisely why it must burn."

"That's mad," Wicker whispered.

"It's mercy," Vane said. "Consider. Somewhere in those pages is a word that has not been spoken aloud in three hundred years. If you spoke it — if your daughter spoke it, idly, at her loom, the way children repeat anything — do you know what happens? Nothing. Nothing happens, once. And then twice. And then a woman down the lane remembers her grandmother humming the same tune, and hums along, and within a generation there are forty people in this valley carrying a country inside their mouths. A country that was put down like a sick animal for reasons the Crown judged sufficient. Do you understand what I'm telling you, Master Wicker?"

Wicker said nothing.

"I'm telling you that I am saving your daughters from a grief they cannot imagine, by taking from them a book they never read." Vane smiled, briefly, and it was a real smile, which made it worse. "You'll thank me eventually. Most of them do. Not out loud. But they sleep well afterward, and their children grow up without ghosts, and that is the whole of my office. Sleep without ghosts."

He nodded to the troopers, and the fire was lit, and the archive went up with the soft greedy crackle of paper, and Vane watched until the last page curled, because watching was part of the discipline. Men who looked away from their own fires began, eventually, to enjoy them.

* * *

The Guildhall at Lowmark smelled of tallow, wet wool, and money that had been laundered through at least four lies before breakfast. Vane found it restful. There was something honest about a place that made no pretense of virtue.

Guildmaster Corvo received him in a room with a good fire, better wine, and three exits, because Corvo was a man who understood hospitality as a series of contingencies. He was broad, silvering, dressed like a successful merchant rather than a criminal, which was itself a kind of lie Vane almost admired.

"Inquisitor." Corvo poured two cups without asking. "We're loyal subjects of the Crown, whatever you've heard."

"You're criminals," Vane agreed pleasantly, sitting. "Which makes you useful. Criminals keep records too, Master Corvo. Worse ones than archivists — sloppier, more honest. Every route you run, every toll you bribe, every cargo you move — it's all written down somewhere, in a hand only you trust. And I have come to read it."

Corvo laughed. It was a good laugh, warm, practiced. "And if I decline to be read?"

"Then I burn your hall, arrest your captains, and read the ashes. You'd be surprised how much survives a fire, Master Corvo. Paper doesn't. Stone does. People do." Vane sipped the wine. It was very good. He noted it for the report — *subject attempted bribery via hospitality* — and drank more of it anyway. Discipline was not the same as joylessness. "But I'd rather not. Burning is for things that must be forgotten. Your guild is merely illegal, and illegality is negotiable. So. Negotiate."

For a long moment Corvo studied him with the flat professional interest of a man pricing a horse that might bite.

"What does the Crown want?"

"A child." Vane set down the cup. "Small. Dark-haired. Travels with a guide — a woman, Oyelaran, Hazel, formerly apprenticed to a hedge-witch named Rosalind Fairweather, formerly of the Thornfell marches. They'll be coming up the old drover roads toward Lowmark, because the passes east of Hollowmere are the only ones open after the early snows, and because your people control the waystations on that stretch. I want the route. Dates, stations, contacts. Everything."

"And if I give it?"

"Then your guild continues to exist, your tariffs remain unofficial, and the Crown's attention slides off you like rain off slate. I am not interested in smugglers, Master Corvo. Smugglers move goods. Goods stay moved." He leaned forward slightly. "Words are different. Words come back."

Corvo turned his cup slowly on its saucer. "There's a girl called Oyelaran on my rolls. Good guide. Best in the high passes since old Marta died. Took a contract three weeks back — unusual one. Cash up front, buyer in Lowmark, cargo description left blank." His eyes came up. "That your child?"

"Describe the contract's origin."

"Brokered through a factor named Dull. Factor works for a collector." Corvo's mouth did something complicated. "Marrow. Aurelius Marrow. The man buys extinct things, Inquisitor. Last letters of dead alphabets. Seeds of blighted orchards. He paid for a lock of hair from the last wolf shot in the Thornfell marches — paid more than the wolf's pelt would've gone for live."

Vane felt something cold settle behind his sternum, the particular chill of a pattern completing itself. A collector of extinct things, purchasing a living speaker of an extinct tongue. Of course. Of course it fit together the way plagues fit together: inevitably, and to no one's benefit but its own.

"The route," Vane said.

Corvo told him. All of it — the waystations, the drop at the drowned chapel, the shepherd's hut above the whiteout line where travelers sheltered when the weather closed. He gave it freely, smoothly, a merchant concluding a sale, and Vane wrote it all down in his precise clerk's hand and thanked him with genuine courtesy, and both men understood perfectly that each had betrayed the other in some way that would matter later. It was, Vane reflected, almost a friendship. The only kind he'd ever had time for.

At the door, Corvo paused. "One thing, Inquisitor. Free of charge, since we're partners now." His voice had lost its warmth entirely. "Oyelaran's good people. Guild people. Whatever she's carrying — if your office burns her, my captains will remember it. We look after our own."

"I know," Vane said. "That's why I'm here instead of at your chapel. Loyalty is a fine quality, Master Corvo. It's also a map." He smiled his real smile again. "Yours leads straight to her."

* * *

The smuggler's name was Ferren Boyle, and he knew too much, and the tragedy of Ferren Boyle — Vane genuinely considered it a tragedy, filed it that way in his heart alongside so many others — was that knowing too much was the whole of his crime.

They caught him at the drowned chapel, of all places, huddled under its sagging roof with a brazier and a jug, waiting to relay word up the drover road. Vane's troopers took him gently, because Vane insisted on gentleness in arrests; violence before questioning contaminated the answers.

Boyle talked immediately. That was the sad part. He sat on a fallen pew with his hands around the cup of tea Sister Anthe poured him — Anthe always poured tea for prisoners, another of the small decencies Vane permitted and privately relied upon — and he talked, eagerly, relieved, a man unburdening.

"—so I says to Dull, I says, that's no cargo, that's a *child*, and Marrow's people paying guide-rates like it's plate steel? And Oyelaran took it anyway, cash up front, which isn't like her, she haggles like a fishwife over everything—" He laughed, wiping his nose. "She's a good one, mind. Hard, but fair. You want to watch the chapel drop, that's where the toll goes up—"

"Master Boyle." Vane waited until the man looked at him. "Do you know why the child is valuable?"

Boyle shrugged. "Collector wants it. Rich folk collect strange things. I heard it talks funny. Old talk." He grinned, gap-toothed, guileless. "My nan used to say there was talk under the hills. Songs you shouldn't finish. Superstition, eh?"

"Yes," Vane said quietly. "Superstition."

He rose. Anthe rose with him. The troopers by the door shifted, and Boyle's grin faltered as understanding arrived late, the way it always did, walking into rooms after the conversation had already ended.

"Wait," Boyle said. "Wait, I told you everything, I cooperated, I—"

"You did," Vane agreed. "Beautifully. Every station, every contact, every date. You've been enormously helpful, Master Boyle, and I want you to know that your cooperation will be recorded, and your family will be told you died assisting the Crown." He drew on his gloves, one finger at a time. "But you knew about the child. You knew what she carries, roughly, and you knew where she's going, and you have a nan's worth of old songs in your head that you've never thought dangerous a day in your life. Which means you'll never think them dangerous tomorrow, either. Or next year. You'll hum them to your children. You'll teach the road-talk to new runners. And every word you carry is a seed, and seeds are patient, and I am not."

"You're going to kill me," Boyle said. His voice had gone very small. "For *talking*."

"For remembering," Vane corrected. "Talking is just how remembering spreads."

They did it outside, in the snow, quickly, with a sword rather than fire — Vane reserved fire for knowledge, not men; men were only vessels, and breaking a vessel was a smaller sacrament. Boyle went to his knees without being forced, which Vane noted with respect, and his last words were not a curse or a plea but a name — *tell my Nan's grave I finished none of 'em* — which Vane recorded verbatim in his journal that night, because he recorded everything, because the discipline of the record was the wall he built between himself and the abyss of enjoying any of this.

Afterward, Anthe stood beside him looking at the body, her breath steaming.

"You hesitated," she said. It was not an accusation. Anthe did not accuse. She observed, the way a scale observes weight.

"I did not."

"At 'Nan's grave.' Your pen stopped."

Vane looked down at his journal. The ink had indeed pooled, a small dark bloom at the end of the sentence, like a period grown diseased.

"He reminded me of someone," Vane said finally. "A boy in my village, when I was young. Before the second purge. He sang an old song at the well — half of it, the half anyone remembered — and within a month the whole valley was humming it, and within a year there were lights on the fells that hadn't been seen in living memory, and then the soldiers came, and it took eleven days." He closed the journal. "Eleven days, Anthe. Forty-one dead. For a song. I was seven. I hid in a root cellar and counted the smoke."

Anthe said nothing. She was very good at nothing.

"So yes," Vane said. "I hesitate. For exactly as long as it takes to remember why hesitation is a luxury purchased with other people's graves. Then I write it down, and I go on." He tucked the journal away and turned back toward the horses, toward Lowmark, toward the collector's gilded house and the child who would be waiting there, singing silently to herself in a soundproofed room, seeding nothing. Contained. Safe. *Mercied.*

"Set the pace hard," he told the troopers. "They'll reach Lowmark within the week, and I want to be standing in front of Marrow's door before Oyelaran collects her fee. A guide sells a cargo once, gentlemen. An inquisitor confiscates it forever. Let's see which of us loves order more."

He mounted, and paused, and looked back once at the chapel — at the drowned little church with its saintless niches, its moss-eaten stones, its bell that had rung for a parish that no longer appeared on any map. Something in the old script was carved above the door, worn nearly smooth. He could not read it. No one could. That was the point of him.

*Welcome, all who wander,* Rosalind Fairweather had once translated it, in a report Vane had read a dozen times and burned a dozen times, though of course paper keeps nothing and stone keeps everything, and the words were still there, still carved, still patiently welcoming, in a language the Crown had spent three centuries teaching the world to forget.

Vane turned his horse south and did not look again.

Behind him, the snow began filling in the troopers' tracks, erasing the road as they rode it, so that by morning there would be no proof anyone had passed this way at all — which was, he supposed, the closest thing to agreement the world ever offered him.

In Lowmark, a lamp was already burning in a gilded window, and beneath it, a child waited in a room built to swallow her voice, and neither the lamp nor the child knew yet that the most patient man in the Kingdom was coming to make them both unnecessary.

Hazel Oyelaran had perhaps five days.

Vane intended to use every one of them.
