## 12. Lowmark's Gilded Cage

Lowmark announced itself the way rich men do: too loudly and slightly too late.

Hazel crested the last hill an hour past dusk and there it all was — the river glittering like spilled coin, the terraced houses climbing their own vanity up the slope, the gaslamps of the Promenade burning so bright that the stars over Lowmark had apparently resigned in disgust. Somewhere below, a string quartet was playing badly enough to be expensive.

Beside her, Wren stopped and looked at the city for a long moment.

"Big," Hazel said, because Wren had been picking up trade-tongue the way a burr picks up wool, one word at a time, mostly the useful ones. *Big* had been among the first. Along with *food*, *no*, and — worryingly — *door*.

Wren said something back in the old tongue, soft, and the words did that thing they sometimes did: they landed on the world and the world listened. The gaslamps along the river road flickered, all together, like a row of courtiers bowing.

"Don't do that here," Hazel said quietly. "Please."

Wren looked at her. In the lamplight from below, the child's face was unreadable in the way of deep water — you could see something moving down there, but not what.

"I know," Hazel said, and hated how apologetic she sounded. "I know it's not wrong. I know it's just... talking. But this city eats anything that glows. So we keep it small until I've been paid, and then—" She didn't finish, because she didn't have an end for that sentence. *And then I walk away.* That was the end. She'd known the end since the drowned chapel, since her mother's ring left her finger, and she had made a professional discipline of not looking at it directly, the way you don't look directly at the sun or at your own reflection when you're deciding whether to do a bad thing.

Wren took her hand. Small fingers, cold from the pass, gripping with the absolute trust of someone who had never yet been dropped.

Hazel's stomach did something complicated.

"Come on," she said. "Let's get you fed before I ruin everything."

* * *

The buyer's house stood on Lantern Row, which was the kind of street where the houses were so old and so moneyed that they no longer needed to be beautiful; they could afford to be interesting instead. Ivy gone deliberately feral. A door knocker shaped like a raven with a suspiciously smug expression. The nameplate read **MADAME SERAPHINE QUILL & COLLECTIONS**, the ampersand doing a great deal of work.

The door opened before Hazel knocked.

"You're late," said Madame Quill.

She was perhaps seventy, built like a heron, dressed in dove-grey silk with a magnifying lens hanging at her throat on a chain, as if she expected the world to require close inspection at any moment. Her eyes were quick and pale and missed nothing, which Hazel noted professionally, because eyes that miss nothing are either your best friend or your worst client.

"The roads were snowed," Hazel said. "The cargo arrived intact. That's the whole of the arithmetic."

"Cargo." Madame Quill's gaze slid past her to Wren, who stood half-hidden in Hazel's shadow, and something moved through the old woman's face — hunger, yes, but underneath it something almost like reverence, which was somehow worse. "Oh," she breathed. "Oh, you *found* one."

"Found is a strong word. Found implies looking. This one found me." Hazel held out the letter of introduction Corvo had given her. "Half now, half on delivery. Guild terms."

"Guild terms are for guild goods." But she stepped aside, gesturing them in with a hand heavy with rings — and Hazel noticed, with the particular chill of a smuggler noticing inventory, that several of those rings were very old, and none of them matched any living kingdom's style. "Come in. Both of you. Dinner first. I don't transact with the unfed; they haggle badly and spill things."

The house inside was a museum that had eaten a home and was still working through the bones. Shelves of things under glass. A stuffed bird that Hazel was fairly sure no longer existed anywhere but in that case. A wall of keys — hundreds of them, labeled in a tiny meticulous hand: *Gate of Vell, lost 211. Locks of the Amber Court, melted. Door of Thornfell, unrecorded.*

Hazel's eye snagged on that last label and refused, for a moment, to move on.

"You collect extinct things," she said.

"I curate them," Madame Quill corrected, sweeping ahead. "Extinction is not an ending, dear girl, it is a *misfiling*. Everything that ever existed is still somewhere. It simply requires a person with sufficient patience and sufficient money to go and get it." She glanced back, and the lamp caught her lenses. "You would be surprised how often those two qualities travel together."

Dinner was excellent and Wren ate like a small polite avalanche, and Hazel ate less than she should have, because every course came with another question from their host — never about Wren directly, always sideways, always about *the region*, *the passes*, *the old families*, asked in the tone of a woman taking measurements. And Wren, who had learned perhaps forty words of trade-tongue, sat between them radiating the serene silence of someone who understood far more than they let on.

At last Madame Quill set down her fork.

"Well," she said. "Shall we?"

* * *

The room was on the third floor, behind two doors, and Hazel understood its purpose before anyone explained it, because Hazel had spent ten years reading rooms for a living and this one read like a confession.

It was beautiful, first of all. That was the trap of it. A wide bed with a quilt stitched in patterns Hazel almost recognized — patterns that tugged at some lesson she'd pretended to forget. A window, real glass, looking out over the lit sprawl of Lowmark. Books. A little stove. Toys, even — carved animals, a wooden puzzle-globe, things chosen with care by someone who had done research.

And on every wall, between the plaster and the paper, a layer of packed wool and lead sheeting. Hazel saw the seams. Saw the way the door closed with a soft, total *thump*, like a mouth.

A soundproofed room. A room built so that nothing said inside it could ever reach the outside world.

"It's for the child's protection," Madame Quill said smoothly, watching Hazel watch the walls. "There are people in this city who would pay a great deal to hear a voice like that — and others who would pay more to stop it. In here, the child can speak freely. Sing, even. No one will ever know." She smiled, and it was a genuinely warm smile, which made it worse. "Think of it as a sanctuary, Miss Oyelaran. The safest room in Lowmark."

The safest cage in Lowmark, Hazel thought, and said nothing, because saying something would mean admitting what she was currently doing, out loud, to a witness.

Wren walked into the middle of the room and turned around slowly, taking it in — the bed, the window, the toys, the walls that swallowed sound. Then the child looked at Hazel, and said, clearly, in careful trade-tongue:

"Hazel comes too?"

And there it was. The question, sitting in the middle of the beautiful room like a stone in a wedding shoe.

Madame Quill's smile didn't move. "Miss Oyelaran has been paid to deliver you safely. Which she has done. Admirably." A pause, delicate as a scalpel. "Hasn't she."

Every professional instinct Hazel owned lined up in neat formation and told her the same thing: *take the second half of the payment, say goodbye, walk out, don't look back, this is a job, jobs end, that's what makes them jobs.* Ten years of arithmetic, all pointing one direction. It had never once failed her.

"Wren," Hazel said. Her voice came out rougher than intended. "This is a good room. Warm. Safe. Better than most beds I've slept in, and I've slept in some spectacularly bad ones." She crouched, so they were eye to eye. "You remember the word for tomorrow?"

"*Tomorrow,*" Wren said solemnly. One of the forty.

"Aye. Well. Tomorrow I'll come back. Check on you. Make sure the old woman's feeding you properly." A lie. She heard it leave her mouth wearing the clothes of a promise, and hated herself with a fluency that was becoming, lately, almost routine.

Wren studied her face for a long moment — long enough that Hazel felt seen straight through, the way you can't hide anything from a child or a dog or a witch, three categories that are secretly the same category. Then Wren reached out and touched Hazel's cheek, once, lightly, the way you'd touch something to memorize it.

"*Tomorrow,*" Wren agreed.

Hazel got out of the room before her face could do anything unforgivable.

* * *

The second half of the payment was heavier than the first, which is a strange property of money and entirely consistent.

Madame Quill counted it out onto her study desk in broadcrowns and half-crowns and one small stack of old gold coins that Hazel didn't recognize and knew better than to ask about. Enough to clear her debts twice over. Enough to buy back her mother's ring from Brannic at the chapel, if he hadn't already sold it. Enough to disappear — properly disappear, south, over the sea, new name, new roads, the whole dream she'd been carrying since she was fifteen years old and walking away from a cottage with a hedge around it.

"There," Madame Quill said, pushing the last stack across. "Paid in full, and I'll thank you not to mention this transaction to anyone, ever, including me." She paused at the study door, and for a moment the curator's polish slipped and something older showed underneath — tired, and knowing. "You did a hard thing tonight, Miss Oyelaran. Most people who do hard things tell themselves stories so they can sleep. I won't insult you with one. Good night."

She closed the door gently. Somehow that was the cruelest part.

* * *

Hazel took a room at the Gilded Perch, the best inn on the Promenade, because if she was going to be wealthy and wretched she might as well be both comfortably.

The room was lovely. Featherbed, clean linen, a fire already laid. Through the window, across the rooftops, she could see — she realized with a lurch — the third-floor windows of Lantern Row, one of them glowing softly gold.

She unpacked nothing. She sat on the bed with the money in her lap and tried to do the arithmetic that had governed her whole adult life, and found that the numbers wouldn't sit still.

*Debts cleared.* Fine. That column worked.

*Ring bought back.* Except Brannic would ask where the money came from, and she'd have to say it out loud, and some prices you can't buy back anyway; a ring is just metal, what she'd actually handed over in that chapel was the last thing about herself she hadn't owed to somebody, and Madame Quill's broadcrowns didn't make change for that.

*Rosalind.* The letters sat in the bottom of her pack where they'd sat for years, unopened, each one a small accusation in Rosalind's tidy handwriting. She'd told herself she'd read them when she was settled. When she was safe. When she had earned — and there was the flaw in the ledger, wasn't it, the error she'd been carrying for a decade without auditing it: the belief that there was some sum of money, some distance, some number of successful jobs after which she would finally deserve to open a letter from the woman whose lessons she'd called superstition while storming out into the snow.

*Names are load-bearing,* Rosalind used to say, hands in the garden dirt, patient as stone. *You pull one out, girl, and you'd best know what it was holding up.*

Hazel had delivered a child to a woman who collected extinct things. She had personally carried the last living speaker of a murdered country up the stairs of a house full of stolen doors and set them down behind walls stuffed with wool so their voice could never again touch the air.

Somewhere below, the bad string quartet had given way to drunk singing. Somewhere across the roofs, the golden window burned. And somewhere in Hazel's chest, complicity — which had arrived that evening dressed as professionalism, all brisk and businesslike — began, slowly, to curdle.

She lay down. Got up. Lay down again. Counted broadcrowns like sheep. Did the thing where you tell yourself you'll feel differently in the morning, knowing full well you won't, because morning doesn't change what you did, it only makes you look at it in better light.

Around midnight she gave up, dressed, and stood at the window with her pack already on her shoulder — though going where, she couldn't have said. Back to the house? To do what, steal the child back from a legal sale she'd willingly made? Walk south with blood-money in her bag and call it a fresh start?

Across the rooftops, the golden window went dark.

Then — Hazel leaned forward, breath fogging the glass — it went dark again. Flickering. As though the lamps inside were bowing.

As though something in that sealed, silent, wool-stuffed room had spoken, politely, to the light.

Hazel was down the stairs and out into the cold before she had decided to move, and only on Lantern Row, hammering on the raven knocker with her heart going like a smuggler's clock, did she understand what she had already known for days, weeks, since Hollowmere, maybe since the storm:

She had never once believed Wren was cargo.

The door opened. Madame Quill stood there in a dressing gown, lantern raised, and her face was not angry.

Her face was afraid.

"You'd better come up," she said. "The room — the room is *gone*, Miss Oyelaran. There's a field where my wall used to be. And the child is standing in it, asking—" the old woman's voice cracked on the absurdity of it, "—asking the *night* whether it knows the way home."
