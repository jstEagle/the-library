## 14. Running Again

The knock came at the wrong hour again.

Hazel had learned, in her years on the passes, that there was a right hour for trouble and a wrong one, and the wrong one was always the same: the small black stretch before dawn when even the rats in Lowmark's walls took a moment to themselves. She'd been lying in the collector's guest room — paid for, comfortable, and shaped like a coffin that had ambitions — staring at the ceiling and doing arithmetic she didn't want to finish.

The payment sat in a lockbox under the bed. Forty crowns of it, gold, heavy, honest weight. The rest promised on delivery confirmation, which had happened at sundown, which meant the rest was already moving toward her account at the Guildhouse like water finding its level.

She had sold a child today.

No — she corrected herself, the way she corrected her ledgers, firmly and without sentiment — she had delivered cargo. The buyer had been respectable. The room had been *soundproofed*, the man had said, with genuine pride, as though soundproofing were a kindness. As though what he was keeping in it needed protecting from the world rather than the other way around.

Hazel rolled over. The mattress was stuffed with goose down and it felt like lying on someone else's apology.

*You'll sleep fine,* she told herself. *You've slept fine after worse.*

She hadn't, actually. But she'd gotten very good at the part where you lay still until morning came and made everything official.

Then: three knocks. Soft. Unhurried. The kind of knock that wasn't asking permission so much as announcing a fact.

Hazel was out of bed with her knife before her mind caught up to her body, which was, she reflected later, the most honest thing about the whole night. Her feet found cold floorboards. The corridor outside was dark and smelled of beeswax and old money.

Three knocks again. From the direction of the stairs.

Nobody knocked on the collector's stairs. The house had guards — two at the door, one who walked the perimeter whistling show tunes badly enough to constitute a second line of defense. Nobody knocked. You were either let in or you weren't here at all.

Hazel went down anyway.

* * *

Wren was sitting on the third step from the bottom, barefoot as ever, wrapped in the blanket Hazel had bought them at Hollowmere market — the blue one with the fraying edge that Wren had refused to give up even when the collector's people had offered silk instead. The child looked up at her with an expression of perfect calm, the way they always did, as though the world were a large and slightly slow animal that would eventually get where it was going.

Behind them, at the bottom of the stairs, stood the vault door.

It stood *open*.

Hazel knew that door. She'd watched them shut it, watched the collector run his hand along the seam the way a man pets a dog he doesn't entirely trust. Iron, banded, older than the house around it, salvaged from some ruin or other because collectors loved their iron with history in it. It had taken two men and a winch to close.

It now stood open maybe four inches, silent, without so much as a creak, like a mouth mid-sentence.

"How," Hazel said.

Wren said something in the old tongue. It was short, soft, almost apologetic. Then, in the careful trade-speech they'd been building together through snowstorms and long roads, word by word like stacking stones:

"The door. Was sleeping. I woke it. Politely."

"Politely."

A nod. Wren patted the step beside them, an invitation, and then — this was the part that undid Hazel, though she wouldn't admit it for years — moved over to make room, the way you do for family coming in out of the rain.

Hazel did not sit down. Sitting down felt like agreeing to something.

"You understand," she said, keeping her voice low because the guard-whistler had gone quiet somewhere outside, "that this is a problem. This is a *significant* problem. That man paid me forty crowns and owes me sixty more, and what he bought is currently sitting on his staircase wearing my blanket."

Wren considered this. "You sold me," they said. Not accusing. Just checking the arithmetic, the way Hazel herself checked arithmetic, and finding it stated correctly.

"I *delivered*—"

"You sold me." A pause. "For money. For your debt. To be not-in-the-room-with-the-quiet-walls."

There it was, laid out on the stair between them, plain as a ledger entry. Hazel opened her mouth to do the thing she did, the professional thing, the thing with categories and justifications arranged in tidy rows — and found the rows empty. Every justification she reached for had a hole in the middle exactly the size of a child in a soundproofed room.

"Yes," she said finally. "I did."

Wren nodded slowly, accepting this the way they accepted weather. Then they held up one small hand and, with great deliberation, spoke a single word of the old tongue into the space between them.

Hazel felt it go past her like a draft under a door. Felt it land somewhere in the house — in the walls, in the floor, in the bones of the building — and *settle*. Somewhere above them, a clock that had been running fast since Hazel arrived ticked once, audibly, and began keeping honest time.

"What was that?" Hazel whispered.

Wren smiled for the first time all night. It was a small smile, wry, and it looked suddenly and terribly familiar — it looked like Rosalind's, the particular smile of someone watching a lesson take root whether the student wants it to or not.

"It means," Wren said, "the house is listening now. Houses forget. I reminded it." They tilted their head. "Your word. For when you fix a road. *Remind* the mountain where the path goes."

Hazel stared at them.

"You've been learning my words too," she said.

"You talk," Wren said, "when you think no one is listening. About roads. About your mother's ring. About a witch." Another pause, surgical. "About being sorry."

"I have never in my life said I was sorry."

"Not with words," Wren agreed.

* * *

Here is what Hazel Oyelaran did next, and she would spend a long time afterward trying to make it sound more complicated than it was, because the truth was embarrassingly simple:

She went upstairs. She got dressed. She packed her kit — rope, flint, the good knife and the expendable knife, dried meat, the little tin of willowbark tea she pretended was for customers. She lifted the lockbox, weighed it in her hands for exactly three seconds, and set it back down under the bed.

Forty crowns. It could buy her out of Corvo's books twice over. It could buy a season of warm rooms and nobody asking questions. It could buy the version of her life she'd been constructing since she was fifteen years old and walked out of a hedge-witch's garden with her chin up and her heart nailed shut: the life where nothing was owed to anyone, where every transaction balanced, where you never had to be the person who stayed.

She left it under the bed.

Then she stopped, went back, and — because she was still, at core, a woman who kept accounts — took out ten crowns and stacked them neatly on the pillow. Payment for the blanket, she told herself. The blue one. Wren was keeping the blanket.

*That's not why,* said a voice in her head that sounded annoyingly like Rosalind.

*Shut up,* Hazel told it, and went downstairs.

* * *

Getting out of Lowmark was, ironically, easier than getting in. The city's gates opened before dawn for the fish carts, and fish cart drivers, in Hazel's extensive experience, could be bribed with coin, flattery, or in one memorable case a genuinely heartfelt discussion of herring migration patterns. She used coin. She was in a hurry.

Wren rode in the fish cart's false bottom between crates of sprats, which the child declared — once they were clear of the walls and could emerge reeking but free — to be "smelly but safe," which Hazel noted was also her own review of most of her life choices.

They walked. The road south out of Lowmark ran flat and gray through country that had been farmed so long it had forgotten how to be anything else, hedgerows trimmed to obedience, every tree planted on purpose. Hazel set a pace that ate distance without looking like flight, because looking like flight invited questions, and questions invited knives.

By midmorning she had done what she always did: run the routes, weighed the risks, priced the options. And by midmorning she had arrived, with the sickening clarity of a ledger that finally balances, at the truth.

Option one: report the escape. Walk into the collector's house, express astonishment, collect the reward for information leading to recovery. Sixty crowns plus gratitude plus the collector's ear, and the collector moved in circles where ears were worth more than crowns. Option one was sensible. Option one was the whole architecture of her life, load-bearing and familiar.

Option one required handing Wren back.

She looked over at the child, who was walking along the top of a low stone wall with their arms out, heel-to-toe, utterly unconcerned with ledgers. Where Wren stepped, she noticed — had been noticing since Hollowmere, if she was honest, and she was trying very hard lately to be honest — the grass grew a little greener. Nothing dramatic. No blossoms bursting. Just a stubbornness of green, a refusal of frost, following the child like a shadow made of springtime.

"They'll burn villages for this," Hazel said quietly. Mostly to herself. "Vane burns archives for *less* than this. What do you suppose they do to a living archive?"

Wren stopped walking. Looked at her. And said, in trade-tongue, carefully, each word placed like a stone in a wall:

"You are deciding. I can hear you deciding. It is loud."

"Sorry."

"Do not be sorry." Wren hopped down from the wall. "Be *certain*. There is a word for it. In the old tongue." They said the word. It sounded like a door closing gently, like a decision that did not need to be remade every morning.

"That's a useful word," Hazel managed.

"It is your word now too," Wren said simply. "I gave it to you. Words are gifts. That is what they are *for*."

And that — that was the moment, though Hazel would locate it later at half a dozen different points depending on how flattering she wanted the story to be — that was the moment the choice actually happened. Not on the stairs, not at the lockbox. Here, on a gray road full of obedient trees, when a mute-turned-speaking child handed her a word like a gift and she took it.

She turned south. Not east, toward the Guildhouse and Corvo's warm fire and the arithmetic that had governed her whole adult life. South. Toward the mountains. Toward the passes she knew better than her own scars.

Toward, though she didn't say it aloud yet, and barely admitted it even in the privacy of her own skull, *Rosalind*.

* * *

They made twelve miles before dark, holed up in a drover's shelter that smelled of centuries of sheep and had, in its favor, one wall and a roof that mostly agreed with each other. Hazel built a small fire, small enough to hide, and shared out the dried meat, and watched Wren eat with the solemn thoroughness of a child who had known hunger before knowing Hazel and intended never to be casual about food again.

"Where are we going?" Wren asked. It was the first time they'd asked. Children like Wren, Hazel was learning, didn't ask where; they asked *whether*, as in whether the person leading them deserved to.

"Away," Hazel said. "South, then west, into the Thornfell passes. There's—" She stopped. Started again. "There's a cottage. At the edge of the high country. A woman lives there. A hedge-witch. Her name is Rosalind."

"You knew her."

"She taught me. When I was young. Letters, herbs, the names of things." Hazel fed a twig to the fire. "I left. I was fifteen and I knew everything, which is the age at which everyone knows everything, and I told her her craft was superstition and her lessons were chains, and I walked out." A dry, unamused laugh. "She sent letters. I never opened them. I've been carrying them around for eleven years like an idiot, because throwing them away would have meant admitting they mattered."

Wren chewed. Considered. Then said, with the devastating economy of the young:

"You are going back."

"I'm going *south*," Hazel said. "South happens to contain a cottage. These are facts of geography, not sentiment."

Wren smiled the Rosalind smile again and said nothing, which was somehow worse.

Hazel lay back against the sheep-smelling wall and stared at the roof gaps where stars showed through. And she made herself do the thing she'd avoided doing since the stairs: she made herself look directly at what she was running toward, because she was done pretending she didn't know.

She was running toward an apology eleven years late. She was running toward a woman who might reasonably slam the door, and toward the possibility — worse than the slamming — that the door would open and Rosalind would simply look at her with those patient eyes and say *I know, love. I've known the whole time.* She was running toward the letters, which she had finally, two nights ago in a coffin-shaped bed, taken out of her coat and counted: nineteen of them, sealed, softened at the edges by years of her body heat, carried through storms and smuggles and every mile of the life she'd built specifically to prove she didn't need them.

She was running toward the Door, if Wren's fragments of memory meant what Hazel's gut said they meant. The threshold out of a country that had never legally existed. Which meant running toward Vane, and the Crown, and every inquisitor's lantern in the borderlands.

And she was running toward Corvo's displeasure, which was not a metaphor. Corvo did not send angry letters. Corvo sent people, and the people were professional, and the guild's books did not have a column for forgiveness.

Hazel tallied it all up, the way she tallied everything, and for the first time in her adult life the sum came out lopsided on purpose. Some things you carried because they cost too much to abandon and too much to keep, and you carried them anyway, and that was called —

"There's a word," she said into the dark. "In the old tongue. For carrying something that costs too much either way."

Wren was quiet a moment. "Many words," they said. "Which shape? Heavy-like-a-stone, or heavy-like-a-child-asleep?"

Hazel thought about it.

"Asleep," she said.

Wren taught her the word. It was soft, rounded, with a breath in the middle of it like a sleeping child's sigh, and Hazel repeated it until she had it right, and then said it once more silently, to herself, like a prayer or a promise or a name.

* * *

They were two days into the hills when Hazel saw the rider.

She spotted him the way she spotted everything — early, sideways, without turning her head. A lone horseman on the ridge behind them, not hurrying, holding a position that said *I see you and I want you to know I see you.* Guild colors nowhere visible, which meant guild, because Corvo's people dressed like farmers right up until they didn't.

"Hold my hand," Hazel said quietly. "We're about to be very lost."

She took them off the trail onto ground only she knew — a smuggler's cut behind a fallen scree, a path that existed because Hazel had made it exist, stone by argued-about stone, over six years of runs. Behind them, the rider did not follow. He didn't need to. He'd made his point.

That night, camped in a hollow with the fire buried, Hazel found the token wedged in her pack's outer pocket where it hadn't been that morning: a lead seal stamped with the guild's mark, and pressed into the soft metal beneath it, fresh, a single word in Corvo's blunt hand.

*DEBT.*

Not a threat. An invoice. The guild had sold her account — her debts, her obligations, her contract — and whoever held it now would come collecting with whatever methods they preferred. And Hazel had a very good guess who bought cheap paper from a guildmaster with a grudge and deep Crown pockets.

She turned the seal over in her fingers a long time. Wren watched her, and did not ask, because Wren understood invoices too, in the bone-deep way of children who had been priced before they'd been named.

"Well," Hazel said at last, and tucked the seal away, and looked south into the dark where the mountains waited like a held breath. "I hope you're home, old woman. Because we're coming, and we're bringing the entire kingdom's worth of trouble with us."

Far off, beyond three ridgelines, something answered — faint, wordless, unmistakable. A light in a cottage window, burning steady against the night, as if it had been left lit for eleven years by someone who never doubted, not once, that eventually the door would need to open.

Hazel looked at it until her eyes stung.

Then she shouldered the pack, took Wren's hand, and started walking.
