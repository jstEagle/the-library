## 15. The Guild Declares Debt

Guildmaster Corvo kept a ledger for everything.

He kept a ledger of tolls and tariffs, of favors owed and favors sold, of the exact weight of every bribe accepted by every customs officer between Lowmark and the passes — because an officer who took too little was either honest or planning to betray you, and both were problems. He kept a ledger of grudges, which was shorter than people assumed; he found most grudges unprofitable. And he kept, in a drawer locked with three keys that lived on three different people, a ledger of debts.

It was to this ledger that he turned on the morning after Hazel Oyelaran's cargo walked out of a soundproofed room in Lowmark and vanished into the rain.

"Read it to me," he said.

Brannick, his tally-man, cleared his throat. Brannick was a thin man with ink-stained cuffs and the moral flexibility of a willow branch, which was why Corvo liked him. "Oyelaran, Hazel. Age twenty-six. Entered guild service at nineteen as a pass-guide, provisional rating. Debts outstanding: training fees, forty crowns. Equipment advance, twelve crowns, four shillings. The winter she broke her ankle on Sorrow Bridge and we carried her down — board, splint, and physic, thirty-one crowns even. Interest compounded quarterly per guild custom." He licked his thumb and turned a page. "Current total: one hundred and six crowns, eleven shillings."

"And her assets?"

"One waystation lease, valued at sixty. A mule named Duchess, valued at eight, though honestly Guildmaster I've met the mule and I'd say four. Reputation—" Brannick hesitated.

"Reputation is not an asset," Corvo said mildly. "Reputation is collateral. Go on."

"Reputation rated *excellent* until last week. Now rated..." Brannick squinted at his own notation. "*Complicated.*"

Corvo smiled. It was not, he knew, a comforting smile, but he had never intended it to be. He was a broad man gone soft at the edges the way old rope goes soft — still strong underneath, but you had to trust the core of him, and most people wisely didn't. He poured himself tea. The tea was terrible. He drank it anyway, because a man who complained about small things would eventually complain about large ones, and Corvo had made a life's discipline of complaining about nothing at all.

"A hundred and six crowns," he said. "And she cost us a buyer worth how much?"

"The collector paid half up front. He wants the other half back. With interest. He used the word *breach of warranty*."

"He used it correctly, too. That's the worst part." Corvo set down the cup. "So. She owes us a hundred and six crowns, and she owes the collector whatever he decides a stolen miracle costs. And she has run — where?"

"Nobody knows. She didn't take the low road. She didn't take the river. My guess is the high passes, which in this season means she's either dead or very determined."

"She's very determined," Corvo said. "I trained her myself, more or less. Well. I signed the paper that let other people train her, which in the guild is the same thing." He rose and went to the window. Below, the guildhall yard was waking: mules being loaded, dice being thrown, a young thief named Pip attempting to steal from Pip's own partner, which showed initiative if nothing else. "Here's what I want understood, Brannick, by everyone, before anyone starts calling this personal."

"Is it personal?" Brannick asked, with the courage of a man who had already written his own eulogy several times and found it dull.

"No," said Corvo. "That's the whole point. Write it down."

* * *

The guild convened at noon in the long room above the counting-house, the one with the smoke-stained map of the mountain roads pinned to the wall — the real map, the one that existed nowhere else, because the Crown's maps were lies told in pretty colors. Forty-odd faces. Smugglers, fences, guides, two forgers pretending to be respectable merchants, and old Marta Voss who had smuggled salt before Corvo could walk and considered every meeting a personal affront to her nap.

Corvo stood at the head of the table and did not raise his voice. He never needed to.

"Some of you are thinking this is about the girl," he began. "Some of you are thinking it's about the child. Some of you — Marta, put your eyes back in your head — are thinking it's about the collector, and whether we can find a buyer less inclined to litigate. It is about none of those things. It is about arithmetic."

He tapped the ledger.

"Hazel Oyelaran owed this guild one hundred and six crowns. Small debt. We've forgiven bigger. If she'd lost the cargo to an avalanche, we'd have mourned, docked her wages, and taught her to swim out of the snow, metaphorically speaking. But she didn't lose the cargo. She *chose*. She looked at a contract signed in front of witnesses, weighed it against whatever was going on behind her eyes, and chose to break it. And then—" he paused, because the next part mattered, "—she did not come to us to renegotiate. That's the sin. Not the mercy. Mercy, we can price. Every one of you has shown mercy on a job, and every one of you has paid for it out of your own share like an honest woman. What you don't do is show mercy with *guild money* and then disappear into the mountains with the invoice unpaid."

"So we're collecting," said a voice near the back. Denner, muscle for hire, not bright but reliable in the way of a falling rock. "How much trouble can one guide be?"

"Denner," Corvo said pleasantly, "tell the room what you know about Thornfell."

Denner blinked. "Nothing. There's no such place."

"There isn't," Corvo agreed. "Three hundred years ago there was, and the Crown erased it so thoroughly that the word itself gives scholars nosebleeds. Our girl is guiding something out of that nothing. Something that makes dead orchards bloom and speaks a language older than the borders. The Crown has an inquisitor in the foothills burning archives over less. So when I tell you Hazel Oyelaran is now the most dangerous debtor on our books, I am not flattering her. I am telling you why we do this carefully."

Marta Voss yawned. "In my day we just broke thumbs."

"In your day, Marta, the Crown couldn't read our ledgers. Times have improved." Corvo let that sit. "Which brings me to business. The collector wants his money back and doesn't care from whom. The inquisitor wants the child and doesn't care through whom. And we want our hundred and six crowns plus costs. These three appetites, gentlemen and ladies, are about to be introduced to each other at dinner."

A murmur ran the table. Brannick, at Corvo's elbow, wrote *dinner* in the margin with a small private wince, because Brannick alone understood that Corvo meant it almost literally.

"You're selling her debt," Marta said. Not a question. Her voice had gone flat in the way of a woman watching a rule she'd helped write get applied to someone she'd once shared a campfire with.

"I'm monetizing her debt," Corvo corrected. "There's a difference. Selling is crude. Monetizing is policy." He spread his hands. "Look at the shape of it. The inquisitor's office has resources we can't match — soldiers, writs, the legal authority to make a person stop existing. We have something they can't buy: we know the roads, the safehouses, the habits of a woman who grew up inside our books. So we sell them the debt. They collect it their way, with their iron, and we take our percentage without dirtying a single additional thumb. The lawless world and the lawful world have always been competitors, friends. Today they become partners. It was inevitable. Both of us hate the same thing — a debt walking away free."

"And the child?" someone asked.

"The child," Corvo said, "is the inquisitor's problem now. We deal in currency. Whatever that creature is, it stopped being ours the moment it left the vault." He paused, and honesty — that rare visitor — made him add: "Though I'll note for the record that the vault door was found open, unlocked, and by all reports politely so, which bothers me more than I can price. Write that down too, Brannick. Under *anomalies*."

* * *

The sale itself took place three days later, in a room that smelled of lamp oil and cold stone, in the foothill town of Grayharbor where the last paved road gave up and became mud.

Inquisitor Vane arrived with two clerks and no visible weapon, which Corvo noted and distrusted on principle. Vane was younger than Corvo expected — neat, gray-eyed, with the particular stillness of a person who had resolved every argument they would ever have and won them all internally. The inquisitor sat, declined wine, accepted water, and placed a document case on the table between them like a chess player setting down a rook.

"Guildmaster. Your letter was unusually candid."

"I find candor cheaper than charm," Corvo said. "Charm requires wine, and your wine budget frightens me."

Something moved at the corner of Vane's mouth. It might have been amusement. It might have been a muscle spasm. Corvo decided to bill for it either way.

Vane opened the case. "Your terms. You transfer the Oyelaran debt — principal, interest, and collection rights — to this office, for a fee of fifty crowns and ten percent of recovered assets. In exchange you provide route intelligence, safehouse locations, and the names of any guild members assisting her. And in exchange for *that*, this office agrees to suspend its investigation into the guild's... provincial activities. For a period of one year."

"Renewable," Corvo said.

"Renewable," Vane agreed, "subject to good behavior. Which brings us to the clause I care about, Guildmaster, more than the money, more than the woman." The gray eyes came up. "You will not attempt collection yourselves. No knives, no ambushes, no clever little accidents on the passes. If Oyelaran dies at guild hands after signing this, I will consider the contract void and your activities unpardoned, and I will burn your organization down with the same thoroughness I bring to everything else. Do you understand me? This is not a threat. It is a schedule."

Corvo held the gaze and found, to his mild surprise, that he rather liked Vane. It was like looking into a mirror polished by a different craftsman — all the same discipline, none of the sentiment, and a genuine belief underneath that would have been touching if it weren't so expensive for everyone else.

"You think you're the good one," Corvo observed. "Between us."

"I know what I am," Vane said. "I erase dangerous things so that ordinary people can sleep. You sell dangerous things so that you can eat. One of us sleeps well."

"And the child? Does the child sleep well, in your arithmetic?"

For the first time, Vane hesitated. It lasted perhaps a second — a hairline crack across all that certainty, quickly mortared over. "The child is a hazard of unlicensed speech. Hazards are contained. Containment is kindness, compared to what happens when hazards go free." Vane signed. The pen scratched like a small animal. "Fifty crowns will be delivered tonight. Your intelligence packet by week's end. And Guildmaster — a professional courtesy. The woman you're selling has a reputation for getting out of things. Don't spend the fee twice."

"They all say that," Corvo said comfortably. "Every runner, every debtor, every clever girl in the history of clever girls. 'You don't know me. I'm different.' And they are, right up until the moment the mountains get hungry or the money runs out. Then they're just arithmetic again." He stood, extended his hand, and Vane shook it — a dry, precise grip, like being filed. "Pleasure doing business with the law, Inquisitor. First time for everything."

"Not the first time," Vane said quietly, gathering the papers. "Your guild has been selling people to governments since before either of us was born. The only novelty today is the paperwork." At the door, the inquisitor paused. "One more item. The debt ledger. I'll want the original entry. Signed. Dated."

"It's in the drawer."

"I know," said Vane, and left.

Corvo stood alone in the cold room for a while, listening to the rain start up against the shutters, and felt the peculiar emptiness that followed every good deal — the sense of having traded something weightless for something heavy and only noticing the heaviness afterward. Fifty crowns. A year's immunity. A clean conscience, or the guild equivalent, which was a balanced book.

"Anomalies," he said aloud, to nobody. "Vault door opens politely. Girl runs toward the one country that doesn't exist. Inquisitor buys a debt instead of sending soldiers." He shook his head slowly. "Somewhere in my ledgers there ought to be a column for things that don't add up. Brannick keeps telling me to make one. Maybe it's time."

* * *

That evening, in the guildhall, Corvo did the thing he always did after closing a sale: he walked the yard.

It was habit older than his office. When he'd been a runner himself — young, quick, certain the world owed him better margins — the old guildmaster had walked the yard after every deal, and Corvo had once asked him why. *"To see who looks at me differently,"* the old man had said. *"A sale changes you. Best to catch the change early, in yourself and in others."*

Tonight the yard was quiet. Duchess the mule stood in her stall, chewing with the air of profound grievance that was her permanent condition. Pip was teaching three smaller thieves a song about a customs officer's wife, which was at least educational. And by the tack-room door, arms crossed, waiting with the patience of furniture, stood Marta Voss.

"Out with it," Corvo said.

"You sold her." Marta's voice was gravel over a fire. "One hundred and six crowns of debt and a whole life besides, and you sold her to the man who burns libraries."

"I monetized—"

"Don't you *dare* use the nice word on me, boy. I taught you the ugly ones." She stepped closer. Old, yes — seventy if a day, hands like tree roots — but her eyes hadn't aged a season. "I ran with Hazel's mother, did you know that? Before the fever took her. Same look in both of them. Same way of standing in a doorway like the world should be grateful she entered it." She jabbed a root-like finger at his chest. "You've made betrayal into policy, Corvo. Clean forms, fair percentages, everybody signs. You know what that makes you?"

"Solvent," Corvo said.

"It makes you the Crown," Marta said. "With worse tea." She spat, with impressive accuracy, into the gutter, and stalked off toward her bunk, pausing only to add, without turning around: "When she comes back — and she will, girl's got her mother's luck and your stubbornness somehow, gods help the mountains — I'm sheltering her. Put that in your ledger."

Corvo stood in the dark yard a long while, rain dripping off the eaves, and did not write it down. Some entries, he suspected, were safer unwritten — though the thought arrived unbidden, unwelcome, and priced at nothing, that a ledger with blank spaces in it was no longer really a ledger at all.

Inside, Brannick was waiting with the night's correspondence. "Two items, Guildmaster. The collector's clerk confirms delivery of our fee-share. And—" he hesitated, "—a letter. No seal. Left at the Grayharbor post house addressed to you personally."

"From whom?"

"It's unsigned. But the hand is neat, and the paper is mountain-made, and there's a pressed spruce sprig inside." Brannick held it out at arm's length, as though it might hum. "Whoever wrote it knows your habits, sir. It says — well. It says that the debt you sold isn't yours to sell anymore. It says the old tongue has a word for a debt like that, and the word is *listening*. And it says—" Brannick swallowed. "It says the mountains have started forgetting where your safehouses are. All of them. Starting with the one in Hollowmere, which as of Tuesday appears to have never been built."

Corvo took the letter. Outside, far off in the black hills, something that might have been thunder rolled — except the sky was clear, and the sound, if you listened the way a smuggler listens, had the cadence of a language. Patient. Polite. Taking notes.

"Brannick," Corvo said slowly, "open a new ledger."

"What shall I title it, sir?"

He looked at the sprig of spruce on his desk, green and impossible in the dead of winter.

"*Losses,*" he said. "*Unexplained.*"
