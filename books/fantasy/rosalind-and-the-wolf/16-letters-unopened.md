## 16. Letters Unopened

The safehouse was a root cellar with pretensions.

Hazel had used it four times in six years — twice for cargo, once for a man the guild wanted found, and once for herself, when a job went wrong in ways that involved a goat, a tax collector, and a fire she still maintained was not her fault. It sat behind a collapsed shepherd's cot on the north face of Hollowmere, accessible only by a crawl through bramble that had opinions about clothing, and it smelled of earth and old turnips and the particular damp of a place that had given up on being anything but itself.

It was, in other words, exactly what she deserved.

She'd been there two days. Two days of listening to the wind work at the cot's broken beams like a patient carpenter, of rationing hardtack she didn't have enough of, of lying in the dark cataloguing every decision since Lowmark and finding each one worse than the last. Wren slept against the far wall, curled under Hazel's oilcloak, breathing the small even breaths of a child who could sleep anywhere because home was a word in a dead language and not a place with walls.

Hazel could not sleep at all.

*Her debts are sold to Vane's office.* Corvo hadn't even had the decency to send an assassin first. He'd sent paperwork. Somewhere in a ledger in Lowmark, her name now sat in Crown ink, and every road she knew — every smuggler's cut, every waystation, every friend who owed her or whom she owed — was a door Vane could knock on, and would, and the people behind those doors would open it, because that was what you did when the Crown knocked. You opened it, and you pointed, and you said *she went that way*, and you were sorry about it afterward, sincerely, over drinks.

She had spent her whole life building a web of small kindnesses and smaller debts, and Corvo had sold the whole thing for the price of not being executed himself.

"Stupid," she told the ceiling. "Stupid, stupid girl."

The ceiling did not disagree.

Wren stirred, made a sound — not a word, just a soft rising note, the way other children hummed — and settled again. Even asleep, the child hummed in that old tongue sometimes. Hazel had stopped flinching at it around the third night of the whiteout, somewhere between learning the word for *fire* and the word for *stay*. Now it just sounded like breathing. Like someone dreaming of a country.

And that was the problem, wasn't it. That was the whole rotten problem sitting in the dark with her like a third guest.

Because she'd known. Not everything — not the Door, not the queen, not any of the vast machinery of erasure grinding away beneath her feet — but she'd known *something*, and she'd known it for years, and she'd thrown it away in a doorway at nineteen with a pack on her shoulder and her mistress's lessons still ringing in her ears like church bells she'd decided were liars.

Hazel sat up in the dark.

There was a reason she hadn't thought about this in years, and the reason was that thinking about it required opening the satchel pocket she never opened, the flat one against her back panel, the one that held things that weren't cargo. She reached for it now the way you reach for a splinter you've been ignoring for a decade: knowing it will hurt, knowing it has to come out, hating yourself for leaving it so long that the flesh grew around it.

The letters were tied in a bundle with garden twine. Of course they were. Garden twine. Rosalind had wrapped a decade of correspondence in the same string she used for beans.

Nine letters. She counted them by touch before she dared count them by light. Nine letters across eleven years, arriving at waystations and dead drops and once, memorably, inside a hollowed-out wheel of cheese that had caused a great deal of confusion at a toll house in Marrow Ford. She had kept every one. She had opened none.

That was the trick she'd played on herself, all these years. Keeping them wasn't sentiment, she'd told herself. Keeping them was *prudence*. A guide never destroyed documents; documents were leverage; leverage was currency. Someday those letters might be worth something. That was the arithmetic. That was always the arithmetic.

But you couldn't spend a letter you'd never read, and she had known that too, and she had carried nine unopened letters through eleven winters because as long as they stayed shut, Rosalind was still back at the cottage, still waiting, still — and this was the part Hazel had never let herself finish the sentence on — still willing to take her back.

Unread, the letters were a door she hadn't closed. Read, they might be a verdict.

Outside, the wind changed its mind about its carpentry and started simply howling. Wren murmured. Hazel struck a match, cupped it, lit the stub of candle she'd been hoarding, and set the bundle on her knee.

"You're a coward," she informed herself quietly. "You've crossed the Sorrow Bridge in fog. You've haggled with Corvo face to face. You looked a Crown patrol in the eye and lied about your cargo with such conviction they apologized for asking." She turned the bundle over. The twine was stiff with age. "And you're afraid of string."

She untied it.

---

The first letter was the oldest — the paper gone soft and tea-colored, the ink faded to brown. Rosalind's handwriting had always been a scandal: enormous loops, letters that leaned into each other like drunk friends, margins full of little drawings. A sprig of something sprouted from the *h* in *Hazel*. A bee hovered over the salutation, drawn with more care than some kings got from their portraitists.

*My Hazel,*

*The goose has finally learned to respect the fence, which I attribute entirely to your absence, as she never respected anything else about this household including me. The garden is behaving. The weather is not. Mrs. Tallow's boy broke his arm falling out of the oak you used to climb, and I have told him it is haunted now, which he seems to find comforting rather than otherwise. Children are strange.*

*I am not writing to ask you to come home. I know better than to ask twice.*

*I am writing because I planted the winter beans where you said the frost wouldn't reach, purely to spite you, and you were right, and I want you to know that I hate it.*

*Eat something green. Write if you ever want to. Don't if you don't. The door doesn't lock anyway; it never has; you know this.*

*R.*

Hazel read it twice. Then she pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes and sat very still until the candle flame stopped swimming.

It was nothing. It was *nothing*. Beans and geese and a haunted tree. Eleven years ago, and the woman had written her a letter about *beans*, and signed it with a drawing of a rose instead of a name because she'd always done that, because *Rosalind* was too long for her own handwriting, and Hazel had taken this letter out of a dead drop, felt its weight, and put it in the flat pocket unopened because —

Because she'd been nineteen, and furious, and certain that any letter from Rosalind would be a lesson, and she had sworn off lessons. Because the letter might say *I told you so*, or worse, might say *I miss you*, and she'd had no idea what to do with either. Because it was easier to carry a sealed envelope than an open wound.

She picked up the second letter. Then the third. The candle burned down; she lit another off it without thinking, the way Rosalind had taught her, tilting the wicks together like two people leaning close to share a secret.

---

*My Hazel,*

*Year three, by my count, though I may be wrong; time moves strangely here, mostly sideways. The well needs a new rope and I refuse to buy one because you owe me a rope. This is now officially a debt. I am putting it in writing so there can be no dispute. One (1) rope, plus interest, the interest being news of you, payable whenever.*

*A thing happened in the spring that I will tell you about badly, because it does not translate. A peddler came through selling songs, the usual rubbish, and then he sang one I did not know, and it was in no language I know, and the garden heard it. Everything leaned toward him. The beans, the roses, the nettles — even the nettles, who lean for no one. When he finished, the rowan by the gate had flowered out of season, and he had not noticed, and I paid him double and asked where he'd learned it.*

*"My gran," he said. "From over the mountains. Before the maps changed."*

*Hazel, I am going to say a thing now that I should have said to your face, and I am sorry for the cowardice of paper, which is at least honest cowardice. There are words older than kingdoms. There are words that were here before the borders were drawn and will be here after, and they are not superstition, whatever you called them that last terrible week. They are load-bearing. The world stands on some of them the way a roof stands on rafters, and when people forget the words on purpose — when they pull them out — the roof comes down on whoever is standing underneath.*

*Over the mountains there was a country whose words were pulled out. You will not find it on any map, and if you ask, people will look at you kindly and change the subject, and that kindness is the cruelest thing about it. I knew a woman once, from there. Or from where it was. She taught me three words before she died, and I have kept them alive the way you keep a fire in wet weather, and I do not know why I am telling you this except that you are the only person I know who runs the passes, and the passes remember, even when the maps don't.*

*If you ever meet someone who speaks a language no scholar can name — be kind to them. That's all. Be kind, and be careful, and eat something green.*

*R.*

Hazel's hands had gone cold around the page.

She read it again. *Be kind to them.* Written eight years ago, in a cottage kitchen, by a woman who had never in her life written a wasted sentence, and Hazel had carried it against her spine through blizzards and border crossings while a barefoot child stood at her door speaking exactly, precisely, the language Rosalind had described, and Hazel had recognized one word of it — *one* — and known where she'd learned it, and known what it meant, and gotten the child fed and dried and delivered to the guild like a bolt of silk.

*One rope, plus interest, the interest being news of you.*

She owed a rope. She owed considerably more than a rope.

---

The middle letters blurred together and refused to. That was the cruelty of them — none of them were dramatic. None of them said *come home* in so many words, which somehow made it worse, because it meant Rosalind had understood her well enough to know that asking outright would slam the door, and loved her well enough to leave the door unlocked instead and mention, year after year, that it was unlocked, in the tone of someone discussing the weather.

*The door doesn't lock anyway; it never has; you know this.*

*The latch is broken, which I could fix, but I have decided not to, on principle.*

*I have left the lamp in the window on the nights the fog comes up the valley. No reason. Fog is treacherous. Even for people who aren't coming home.*

Letter six was shorter than the others, and the handwriting shook.

*My Hazel,*

*I was ill in the autumn. It passed. I mention it only because I promised myself I would stop pretending to you that I am immortal, and because if you are reading this at all, it means you are reading it late, and I would rather you were angry with me for telling you than angry with yourself for not knowing.*

*The garden misses you. I realize gardens cannot miss people. I am reporting what the garden reports.*

*R.*

Hazel laughed. It came out wrong, half a sob wearing a laugh's coat, and Wren stirred at the sound, and she froze, and the child resettled without waking, one hand curling loosely around the hem of the oilcloak.

She waited until the small breathing evened out again. Then she went back to the letters, because stopping now was unthinkable, the way putting down a rope mid-cliff is unthinkable.

---

The eighth letter was different. Longer. The handwriting steadier, the drawings absent except for one, at the bottom, that took her a moment to understand: a door, drawn simply, standing open, with a tiny figure in the doorway holding up a lamp.

*My Hazel,*

*I am going to tell you a story, and you may file it under superstition, and I will love you anyway, which is the arrangement we have.*

*Long ago — and I mean long, longer than the Crown, longer than the guilds, longer than the word 'Crown' — there was a country in the high passes. It had orchards and a chapel and a queen who was never crowned, because the crowning was scheduled for a day that never came. And there was a Door. Every country has one, though almost none know it: a threshold out, a way home, a hinge. And the country had a language, and the language was not decoration. It was maintenance. The people spoke their country into continuing, the way you'd mend a wall, the way I talk to the beans (stop laughing).*

*And then the maps changed. You know how borders move — you smuggle across their seams. But this was not a border moving. This was a country being unwritten. Not conquered — erased. And the language went with it, into hiding, into families, into lullabies, down and down, until it survived the way embers survive: in children.*

*Children, Hazel. That is the whole secret, and it is terrible. The tongue hides in children because children are the only ones who learn languages without being taught, and the only ones adults don't think to search. Every few generations, a child is born carrying more of it than usual. A key, if you like. The old women of my tradition called them door-children, and they did not envy them, and they did not abandon them, and if you ever meet one, I need you to hear this part clearly, so I will write it large:*

**THEY ARE NOT CARGO.**

*Forgive the shouting. Ink is the only volume I have.*

*They are not cargo and they are not weapons and they are not treasure, though every kind of vulture will tell you otherwise, and the vultures will pay well, and the paying well is how you'll know. A door-child belongs at the Door. That is all. That is the entire duty. You walk them home and you stand aside and you watch a country remember itself, and it is the finest thing a person can be present for, and I say this as a woman who has raised geese.*

*If you ever meet one — and I think, my dear, stubborn, infuriating girl, that someday you might — you will have a choice, and I cannot make it for you, and I would not insult you by trying. I will only say what I should have said at your door eleven years ago, when you left, when I let you leave, which I have regretted in the manner of a stone in the boot:*

*Names are load-bearing. So are people. So are you.*

*Come home when you can. The door doesn't lock.*

*R.*

Hazel set the letter down very carefully, the way you set down something that might go off.

The candle had burned to a thumb's width. She didn't relight it. In the grey pre-dawn seeping through the cellar's grate, she laid the nine letters out on the packed earth in order, oldest to newest, and knelt over them like a gambler reading a spread of cards she'd already lost on.

Eight years. Eight years of warning, delivered gently, in handwriting full of bees. *Be kind to them. They are not cargo.* And she had read the first line of the first letter — *I am not writing to ask you to come home* — and slammed the pocket shut on all of it, because the truth was she hadn't been afraid the letters would say *I told you so*.

She'd been afraid they'd say *I believe in you*, and that she'd believe it, and that believing it would mean owing something she couldn't pay in coin.

Well. She'd paid anyway, hadn't she. Paid in the worst currency there was. Paid a mother's ring to a chapel full of drowned saints. Paid Rosalind's garden and half her memory to a raid she'd run from. Paid a bridge, a country's worth of roads, every friend she had. And the interest on the original debt — the rope, the news of her, the lamp in the window — had compounded for eleven years into a sum she couldn't calculate, because it included a child sold to a collector, and a witch's memories burned like kindling, and every night Rosalind had sat alone in a kitchen talking to beans about a girl who couldn't be bothered to open an envelope.

"I'm sorry," Hazel said to the letters. It was insufficient. It was also true, which was more than most of her apologies managed.

Behind her, Wren sat up in the dark.

Hazel heard it — the rustle of the cloak, the small feet finding the floor — and turned. The child stood in the grey light, hair wild, watching the letters spread on the ground with an expression Hazel had learned to read during the snowbound week: not the blankness strangers mistook for emptiness, but attention. Total, unhurried attention, the kind most people gave to cathedrals.

Wren crouched. One small finger hovered over the ninth letter — the newest, the one that had arrived at the Marrow Ford drop only weeks ago, the one Hazel had shoved in the pocket unread because she'd been busy, because there'd been a job, because there was *always* a job — and tapped it twice.

"Yeah," Hazel said. Her voice came out gravelled. "Yeah, all right."

She opened the ninth letter. The paper was crisp; the ink barely dry; the handwriting steady and warm and, at the bottom, beneath the signature rose, a single line added in a different, shakier hand, as if written later, or written while crying, or both:

*P.S. — If you are reading this because everything has gone wrong, then listen: the passes above the Sorrow Bridge lead to a place the maps forgot on purpose. Bring the child. Bring yourself. I will put the kettle on.*

*The door doesn't lock. It never has. Come home, my Hazel. Come home.*

Hazel folded the letter along its old creases, and her hands were shaking, and she let them.

"Wren," she said. "Pack up. We're going to see a woman about a rope."

The child smiled — quick, bright, understanding more than any child should — and began folding the oilcloak with the grave precision of someone packing for a journey they had been waiting their whole erased life to take.

Above the cellar, the wind dropped all at once, the way it does when a storm has finally decided to let someone through.
