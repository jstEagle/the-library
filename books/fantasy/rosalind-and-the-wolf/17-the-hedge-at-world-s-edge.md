## 17. The Hedge at World's Edge

The cottage was exactly where Hazel had left it, which was the first insult of the day.

Seven years. Seven years of roads that moved, bridges that rotted, passes that renamed themselves every winter — and Rosalind's hedge stood at the end of the drover's track as if it had been waiting, patient as a debt. The hawthorn had gone up and out and sideways in that time, a green rampart taller than Hazel remembered, threaded with things that were not quite roses and smelled like rain on hot stone. Wren stopped dead when they saw it.

"Thorn," they said, in trade-tongue, and then said something else in the old speech, soft and wondering, and one of the not-quite-roses opened.

Hazel's stomach did something complicated. "Don't do that," she said. "Not here."

Wren looked up at her. They'd learned enough trade-tongue in the snowbound weeks to argue now, which Hazel had thought was progress until moments like this one, when it became a liability. "Flower happy," Wren said carefully. "I say hello."

"It's not about the flower." Hazel shifted her pack higher on her shoulders. The strap had worn a groove into her collarbone over seven years and three hundred miles; she knew its weight the way she knew her own pulse. "It's about — look, there are people who listen for that sound. We're close enough to the lowlands that Vane's riders come through twice a month. You say hello to a hedge and I have to explain to an inquisitor why his horse just grew apples. Can you — can you just be quiet until we're inside?"

Wren considered this with the gravity of a magistrate. Then they reached up and took Hazel's hand, which they had started doing somewhere around the second week, without asking, the way small children take things that have apparently been offered, and said nothing more.

Which was worse, somehow. The quiet gave Hazel's own thoughts room to stretch out and be heard.

*You could still turn around,* those thoughts said. *There's a shepherd's track two miles back that drops into the Vale road. From the Vale you could be in Lowmark by market day. Corvo's men think you went north. Vane thinks you don't exist anymore. Nobody is looking at a hedge at the edge of the world.*

But her feet kept walking, because seven years ago her feet had walked the other way down this same track, and she had promised herself then that she would never come back, and promises made by stupid frightened fifteen-year-olds were, she was discovering, the kind that came due with interest.

The gate was a gap in the hedge with a latch that stuck. It had always stuck. Hazel hit it with her hip in the old spot, hard, out of pure muscle memory, and it swung open exactly as it always had, and she hated how well her body still knew this place. Her hands knew where the water barrel stood. Her eyes went first to the kitchen window, out of habit, checking for lamplight, checking for —

Rosalind was in the garden.

She was kneeling among the bean rows with dirt to her elbows, and she looked older — of course she looked older, seven years, everyone got older — but it was more than that. There was grey coming in at her temples that hadn't been negotiated for, and her hands moved slower among the plants, and when she heard the latch she didn't startle or reach for anything sharp. She just turned her head, and looked at Hazel across forty feet of beans, and said:

"You're standing on my shallots."

Hazel looked down. She was standing on the shallots.

"I — sorry." It came out smaller than she intended. She had rehearsed nothing on the whole journey here. Two hundred miles of road and she had composed no apology, no explanation, no opening line, because some part of her had believed she would never actually arrive, that the road would go on being walked forever, and now she was here, crushing alliums, and everything she might say sounded either too large or too small.

Rosalind rose. She wiped her hands on her apron with unhurried thoroughness, taking her time about it, and Hazel understood that she was being given time too, and resented it, and was grateful for it, both at once.

Then Rosalind's gaze slid past her shoulder and found Wren, and everything changed.

Hazel watched it happen — watched her old mistress's face go through recognition, disbelief, and something very like fear, all in the space of a breath. Rosalind came around the bean rows fast, apron flapping, and dropped to her knees in the dirt in front of the child without a glance at Hazel at all.

"Oh," she said softly. "Oh, look at you. Barefoot again, I'll wager. Are you hungry? Don't answer, of course you're hungry, children are always hungry." She was already pulling off her shawl, wrapping it round Wren's shoulders, and Wren — who had bitten a guild scout in Lowmark, who trusted no one further than arm's length — let her do it, and leaned in, and said something long and liquid in the old tongue.

Rosalind went absolutely still.

"What," Hazel said. "What did they say?"

"They said—" Rosalind's voice had gone strange. "They said the door remembers me. Which is a peculiar thing for a child to say, unless—" She looked up at Hazel then, really looked, and Hazel felt seven years of unopened letters pressing against her ribs like a hand. "Where did you find them, Hazel Oyelaran?"

"The waystation. Storm night. They just — appeared." She heard how thin it sounded. Everything she'd done since sounded thin when spoken aloud to this woman. "Rosalind—"

"Inside," Rosalind said, standing, brisk again, the wonder folded away somewhere behind her eyes. "Both of you. Wash your hands. There's bread on the board and soup that's been on the fire since noon because I had a feeling, and I want it noted that my feelings are never wrong and nobody in this family ever listens to them."

"We're not family," Hazel said, before she could stop herself.

Rosalind paused in the doorway. For a moment Hazel thought she'd see the hurt land — wanted to see it, maybe, wanted proof that the wound ran both directions — but Rosalind only said, "No. Not yet. Soup's getting cold," and went inside.

* * *

The kitchen smelled like thyme and woodsmoke and seven years of the same life lived steadily in the same room, and Hazel sat at the same scarred table under the same hanging bunches of dried herbs and felt like a ghost attending her own funeral. Wren ate like a small wolf, which Rosalind clearly approved of, ladling out seconds and thirds and asking questions in a low even voice that required no answers: how old are you, where is your mother, does anything hurt. Wren answered none of them in words but held up their bowl for more, which seemed to satisfy her.

Hazel ate slowly and watched them together and felt something in her chest that she refused to name.

Finally Rosalind set down her spoon. "Show me your hands, little one."

Wren showed her. Rosalind turned them over, palm and palm, frowning at the lines, and then she did something Hazel recognized from years ago — pressed her thumb hard into the center of Wren's palm, right over the life-line, and held it there, and murmured three syllables that weren't in any language Hazel knew.

The lamp flames all leaned toward Wren at once. Every candle in the room, the fire itself, bending like grass in wind, toward a child sitting quietly with soup stains on their chin.

Rosalind snatched her hand back. She stood up so fast her chair went over backward, and Hazel had never once seen Rosalind knock anything over, not in all the years of lessons and potions and careful hedge-witch precision, and the sound of it going down was the loudest thing Hazel had ever heard.

"No," Rosalind said. "No, no, no." She knelt again, took Wren's face in her two dirty hands, gentle as anything, and looked into their eyes for a long time. Whatever she saw there drained the color out of her face by degrees, like water out of a cracked bowl.

"Rosalind." Hazel was on her feet. "What is it? What's wrong with them?"

"Nothing's wrong with them." Rosalind laughed, and it was the worst sound Hazel had ever heard, because Rosalind never laughed like that, high and thin and frightened. "Nothing's wrong with them. That's rather the problem. Do you know what they are?"

"A mute kid who talks in a dead language. A cargo. A — I don't know. Trouble."

"They're a key." Rosalind was still holding Wren's face, and Wren bore it patiently, watching her with those dark unbothered eyes. "A Door-key. The Door, Hazel. *The* Door. The one the maps forgot along with everything behind it." She released Wren gently and stood, gripping the table's edge. "And it's closing. Has been closing for three hundred years, and the last of its hinges is this child's voice, and when their voice finishes changing—" she gestured vaguely at all of Wren, the smallness of them, the childhood of them, "—it shuts forever. And everything still caught on the other side stays caught."

Hazel's mouth had gone dry. "Caught on the other side of what? There's nothing on the other side. Thornfell's a story. It's empty hills."

"Is it?" Rosalind looked at her, and there was no triumph in it, no *I told you so*, which somehow made it unbearable. "You've traveled with this child for two months. You've seen what happens when they speak. Orchards in January. Hedges blooming. Tell me again how empty the hills are."

"I saw tricks. Weather. Hedge-craft."

"You saw a country trying to remember itself." Rosalind pushed past her, went to the dresser, and began pulling open drawers with sudden fierce purpose — chalk, salt, a bundle of dried rowan, a blackened brass compass whose needle spun lazily and pointed at nothing, or at everything. "Three centuries ago the Crown couldn't beat Thornfell, so they unwrote it. Pulled the name out of the world like a splinter. But you can't kill a country with grammar, Hazel. You can only shut it away. And someone built a Door so that what was shut away could someday come home — and they keyed it to blood, to the old speech, to a child. Always a child. And the Crown has spent three hundred years making sure no such child ever survives to speak."

Hazel thought of Vane burning the village archive. Of the executed smuggler. Of the word *unlicensed speech*, said in that mild reasonable voice.

"How long?" she asked. "Until it closes."

Rosalind stopped rummaging. Her back was to Hazel, and when she spoke her voice had gone quiet and level, the teacher's voice, the one that meant the lesson was going to cost something.

"By my reckoning, and the reckoning isn't mine alone — the rowan's been flowering out of season for a year, the compass hasn't pointed north since spring, and last month the bees swarmed at midwinter — I'd say we have until the child stops growing. A year. Perhaps two." She turned around. "Less, if the Crown finds us first."

The kitchen was very silent. Wren, sensing the shape of the conversation if not its content, had gone still, spoon down, watching them both with the terrible attentiveness of children who have learned that adults' silences contain weather.

"So," Hazel said. Her voice worked. That was something. "So we take them to the Door. Through the passes. I know the passes."

"The passes that Vane is searching. With the guild's route-maps, bought and paid for." Rosalind set the compass on the table between them, needle spinning its mad slow circles. "Yes. I suppose we do."

"*We.*"

"Yes, we." Rosalind finally looked at her straight on, and her eyes were red-rimmed and furious and tired, seven years tired, and Hazel realized with a lurch that the anger had been there the whole time, banked under the practicality like coals under ash. "You don't get to vanish for seven years and then walk in my kitchen with a Door-key and a Crown price on your head and expect me to say *no*. This is my hedge, my country's memory, my — " she stopped, jaw tight, and started again, quieter. "You were the worst apprentice I ever had. You argued with everything. You called names superstition while writing your own name into every ledger from here to Lowmark. You broke my heart, Hazel, thoroughly and comprehensively, and I have grieved you once already, and I will be damned if I stand in this garden and watch the Crown do to another child what it did to Thornfell while I could lift a finger to stop it."

The words landed one after another like stones dropped down a well. Hazel stood in the splash of lamplight and could not make herself speak.

Wren solved it, the way Wren solved most things. They slid off their chair, crossed the floor, and took Rosalind's hand in one of theirs and Hazel's in the other, and looked up at them both, and said, in careful, accented, hard-won trade-tongue:

"Family later. Door first."

Rosalind made a sound that was half laugh and half sob. "Yes," she said, squeezing the small hand. "Yes, exactly that. Family later. Door first." She looked at Hazel over the child's head, and the fury in her face had settled into something harder and more durable — not forgiveness, nothing so finished as forgiveness, but a truce, drawn up in a kitchen, witnessed by a child and a spinning compass. "We leave tomorrow at first light. Tonight you'll tell me everything, from the storm onward, and I mean *everything*, and you'll finally read those letters, because I know you carried them, Hazel, I can see the shape of them in your pack, and I won't ask twice."

Hazel's hand went involuntarily to the side pocket of her pack. Eleven letters. Seven years of letters, corners soft with handling, never opened.

"Tomorrow," Rosalind said again, softer now, almost gentle, and it was somehow the most frightening thing she'd said yet. "Tonight, the letters."

Outside, the wind came down off the mountains and moved through the hedge, and every not-quite-rose in it opened at once, silently, in the dark, as if the garden itself had been listening — and had heard, at last, footsteps coming home.
