## 18. Three Teachers

The trouble with teaching, Rosalind had always found, was that you had to know what you knew.

She discovered this on the third morning of their strange household, standing at her worktable with a mortar in one hand and no idea what to put in it. For forty years she had thrown together charms by instinct — a pinch of this, a word of that, whatever the hedge offered up. It worked. It had always worked. But Wren sat across the table with the terrible patience of a child who had once watched an entire country be unwritten, waiting for her to explain *why* the ash of rowan-bark settled a fever, and Rosalind found she could not say.

"Because it does," she tried.

Wren blinked at her. Then, slowly, they raised one small hand and made the gesture Hazel used at the waystations when a customer was being foolish: palm out, fingers spread, the universal sign for *let's not insult each other*.

"Traitor," Rosalind said fondly. "You've known her nine days."

Wren's mouth twitched. They were learning trade-tongue in fragments — Hazel drilled them over breakfast with the merciless cheer of a woman who had once talked her way past three border guards using nothing but a cheese wheel and a forged bill of lading — but their face did most of their talking now. Rosalind was coming to read it the way she read weather. The set of the shoulders meant *I am thinking*. The slow blink meant *you are wrong, and I am being kind about it*. And the particular stillness they fell into sometimes, hands flat on the table, eyes gone somewhere far away — that meant the old tongue was moving through them, and the world was listening.

That last one she watched for the way other people watched for storms.

"Right," she said, and pulled a stool over, and sat down across from the child like a student herself. "Let's begin again. Why does rowan settle a fever? What do you see?"

Wren considered. Then they reached into the little pouch at their belt — Hazel had bought it for them in Hollowmere, soft leather, dyed the green of new leaves — and drew out a twig of rowan, snapped fresh that morning. They held it up between two fingers.

And spoke.

One word. Two syllables, round and low, like a stone dropped in deep water. Rosalind felt it in her teeth. The twig shivered — not moved, *shivered*, the way a dog shivers off rain — and its bark roughened, darkened, took on the grain of something older than itself. When Wren set it down, it looked like it had grown on a tree a hundred years dead.

"That," said Rosalind, "is not what I asked."

But she understood, all the same. She picked up the changed twig and turned it in her fingers. In Thornfell's tongue, apparently, you did not say *rowan settles fever*. You said the rowan's own name — its true name, the name it had before anyone needed it to be medicine — and the wood remembered what it was, and the remembering did the work. Names as load-bearing. She had told Hazel that once, in this very kitchen, and been laughed at.

Well. Not laughed at. Worse. Hazel had looked at her with that flat, careful politeness and said, *that's very nice, Rosalind*, the way you'd speak to someone who believed the moon was made of cheese, and packed her bag that same night.

Rosalind set the twig down before her hands could shake.

"You're going to be the death of me," she told Wren, "and I mean that as a compliment."

* * *

Hazel came in from the garden at midday with dirt to her elbows and an expression Rosalind recognized from twenty years ago: the look of a woman who has found something she cannot steal and is furious about it.

"There's a ward-stone under the gooseberries," Hazel said. "It's older than the cottage. Older than the *hedge*. It's got writing on it, and I can't read it, and it's warm."

"It's warm because I fed it yesterday."

"You *fed* it."

"Honey and barley. What did you think wards ran on, spite?"

Hazel opened her mouth. Closed it. Rosalind watched her decide, visibly, not to ask how much of the last twenty years of quiet competence had run on honey and barley, and file the question away instead for some future reckoning. That was Hazel all over. She kept accounts even when she wasn't keeping accounts.

"The point," Hazel said, "is that if there's a ward-stone under your garden, there are more on the road. And if there are more on the road, then somebody built a network through these mountains once, and if somebody built a network, I want to know where it goes." She wiped her hands on her trousers and looked at Rosalind with the expression that had, in another life, preceded every one of her best schemes. "Teach me the letters."

Rosalind put down her spoon.

"No."

"Mum—"

"Don't 'mum' me. You left. You spent—" she counted, because counting hurt more precisely, "—nine years telling everyone who'd listen that names are just sounds, that my craft was superstition, that letters are only marks, and now you want the alphabet because it's *tactically useful*—"

"Yes," Hazel said simply. "Obviously. That's how I learn things. I learn things when they're useful. It's not a character flaw, it's how I stayed alive."

And there it was — the old argument, worn smooth as a river stone from years of rolling between them. Rosalind felt it rise in her throat, the whole speech, the whole grief. Nine years of unopened letters sat in the chest under the stairs like a debt.

But Wren was watching them both from the corner, chin on their fists, with the grave attention of someone watching weather turn. And something in Rosalind — something that had been clenched since the night Vane's fire lit the southern sky — said: *not now. Not this argument. Not today.*

So instead she said: "Fine. But you learn it properly. All of it. Including the parts that are superstition."

Hazel grinned, quick and crooked, and Rosalind hated how glad she was to see it. "Wouldn't have it any other way."

* * *

They made a school of it, after that. There was no other word for it.

Mornings belonged to Hazel. She taught Wren trade-tongue at the kitchen table with a slate and a stick of chalk, and she taught it the smuggler's way — not the words first, but the *uses*. How to haggle. How to lie with your eyebrows. How to count coins behind your back so a stranger couldn't follow the arithmetic. Wren turned out to be a natural, which surprised nobody; a child who could speak a language that predated kingdoms took to haggling like a duck to rain. By the fourth day they had talked Hazel out of two extra portions of supper and a promise of new boots, and Hazel had let them, and had looked so proud about losing the negotiation that Rosalind had to leave the room briefly to compose herself.

Afternoons were hers. She taught Wren the craft the way her own grandmother had taught her, hands over hands: which moss drank pain and which moss caused it, how to talk a fever down, how to listen to a hedge until it told you where the foxes denned. And Wren — here was the marvel — Wren learned it *backwards*. Where Rosalind would gather and brew and hope, Wren would speak a small word to the sickness itself, politely, the way you'd ask a guest to stop putting their boots on the table. Half her remedies worked better with a syllable added. A few stopped working entirely, which was humbling in a way she chose not to examine too closely.

And evenings — evenings belonged to the letters.

She wrote them large on the slate, the old Thornfell script, the angular beautiful letters that had survived nowhere except in ward-stones and in the memory of children who should never have carried them. Hazel sat with her slate and copied them with the fierce concentration she usually spent on lock mechanisms. Wren corrected them both, gently, endlessly, with the patience of someone whose bones remembered what their teachers were only borrowing.

"That one's upside down," Rosalind said on the fifth evening, tapping the slate.

"It's *fine*."

"It's the letter for *threshold*, Hazel. You've written it lying on its side. As things stand you've written *door lying in the road*, which is either nonsense or a very specific warning."

Hazel squinted at it. "Could be useful. A door lying in the road stops traffic."

"*Hazel.*"

"I'm just saying, if we're making a smuggling code out of a dead alphabet, ambiguity is a feature—"

And Wren laughed.

It was a small sound, startled out of them, half a hiccup — and both women went silent as if a bird had landed between them. Because in nine days they had heard Wren cry, and sleep-talk, and sing one broken fragment of a lullaby in a language of hills that no longer existed. But laugh —

Wren clapped a hand over their own mouth, looking as astonished as either of them. Then, cautiously, they took the hand away and did it again. A real laugh this time, rusty and bright, the sound of a gate opening after years shut.

"Well," said Rosalind, when she could speak. Her eyes were doing something embarrassing. "Now you've done it. Now they'll never stop."

"I regret nothing," said Hazel, who was also wiping her face with great dignity. "Best deal I ever closed."

* * *

On the sixth evening, the plan finally came together — because of course it was Hazel who assembled it, spreading a map across the table and weighting the corners with the salt cellar, the honey jar, and Wren's boot.

"Here's what we know," Hazel said, in her ledger voice. "Vane burned Millbrook. Vane interrogated Corvo. Vane knows my route and roughly where I went to ground. Which means Vane knows, or will know within the week, that the trail ends somewhere near a hedge-witch's cottage in the middle of nowhere. We have days. Maybe less."

Rosalind nodded. She had felt it coming for days herself, in the way the hedge went quiet, in the way the ward-stones drank their honey faster. The mountains were holding their breath.

"So we don't wait to be found. We move first. But—" Hazel tapped the map, at the snarl of lines where the high passes began, "—every route I know through those mountains, I know because the guild mapped them and the Crown tolerates them. Both of those facts are about to become fatal. We need roads that aren't roads. Ways through that don't exist on any map, guild or otherwise." She looked up. "Old ways."

"Wren," Rosalind said quietly.

Wren had gone still — the deep stillness, the listening stillness. Their hands lay flat on the table. After a moment they lifted one finger and traced, slowly, along the map, a line that matched nothing Hazel had drawn. It wandered away from every pass, every bridge, every marked track, up through blank paper where the cartographers had written only *elevation* and given up.

"What's that?" Hazel asked.

Wren looked at the line. Looked at Hazel. And said, in careful, halting trade-tongue, assembling the sentence like a child carrying water:

"Road... remembers. If... walk it... right."

"If you walk it right," Hazel repeated. She stared at the blank stretch of map for a long moment, and Rosalind watched her do the arithmetic — risk weighed against risk, the currency she thought in — and watched, for the first time in nine years, the sum come out to something that wasn't a price. "Right. Then we learn to walk it right. Rosalind — your ward-stones. Do they mark the old road?"

"Some of them will. The network predates the erasure. Whoever laid them was keeping something safe, not keeping something out." Rosalind was already reaching for her coat, already feeling the old excitement kindle in her chest, the feeling of a working assembling itself. "If we can wake them — if Wren can greet them properly —"

"—then we've got a smugglers' road three centuries older than the smugglers," Hazel finished, and grinned, and it was the grin of a woman planning a heist against history itself. "Three teachers. Witchcraft, smuggling-craft, and the oldest tongue there is. Between us we might actually pull this off."

Wren looked from one of them to the other. And held up their slate, on which they had written, in wobbly new-learned trade-tongue letters:

*THREE TEACHERS. ONE SCHOOL. WHO IS THE PUPIL?*

There was a silence.

"Honestly?" said Hazel. "All three of us. Simultaneously. Forever."

Rosalind laughed — really laughed, for the first time since the letters had started arriving unanswered all those years ago — and got the honey jar down for the ward-stones, and outside the window the first flakes of the season's snow came drifting down through the blue dusk, soft as ash, patient as anything.

She did not yet know that she would pay for this evening with half her memory. She did not know that the hedge was already whispering warnings she would refuse to hear, or that the warmth in her kitchen was the exact size and shape of the thing she was about to lose.

She only knew that the child was laughing, and the witch was scheming, and the runaway was home — and that for one bright, borrowed week, against all sense and all arithmetic, they were simply a household.

The snow kept falling. Somewhere south, a man who believed erasure was mercy was reading a map.

And under the gooseberries, the old ward-stone drank its honey, and waited.
