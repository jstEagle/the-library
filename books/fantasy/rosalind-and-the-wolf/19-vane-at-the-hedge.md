## 19. Vane at the Hedge

Inquisitor Second-Class Edmure Vane had burned forty-one archives, three chapels, one very stubborn milliner's shop that had been hiding a printing press under its hat forms, and — though this was not entered in any ledger — one memory of his mother singing. He did not count the last one. It had been an accident of proximity, and he had decided long ago that accidents were simply order arriving early.

He rode up to the hedge-witch's cottage in the grey hour before dawn with twelve soldiers, two fire-pots, and a warrant signed by a man who could not have found this valley on a map if you paid him. That was rather the point. The Crown's warrants were like its roads: they went everywhere except where they were needed most.

The cottage sat low against the hillside as if it were trying to be mistaken for one more fold of land. Its garden was the first thing wrong about it. Vane had seen gardens in his career — neat rows, obedient cabbages, the whole vegetable kingdom arranged in ranks like a well-drilled militia. This garden was a riot. Beans climbed things beans had no business climbing. There was a rosebush growing through the middle of what appeared to be a deliberately collapsed wheelbarrow. Herbs he had only read about in confiscated herbals grew in cheerful defiance of every planting diagram ever drawn.

"Looks abandoned," said Sergeant Brakel, squinting through the pre-dawn murk.

"It looks *lived in*," Vane said. "That is worse."

Because here was the thing Vane knew, the thing that kept him awake on campaign nights while younger men snored: unlicensed speech was never loud. It did not announce itself from towers. It hid in kitchens. It grew in gardens. It was taught by women to children over bread dough and mending, passed hand to hand like a coin too small to notice until someone tried to buy something with it and discovered it was worth a kingdom.

Somewhere ahead of him, three days' ride and closing, was a child who spoke Thornfellish. A child whose words unmade borders. Vane had read the reports from Lowmark — the escaped cargo, the vanished guide, the collector's soundproofed room standing empty with its lock politely open. Politely open. He had turned that phrase over for two days. Locks did not open politely. Locks were broken or picked or burned. Politeness implied conversation. Conversation implied that the lock had *answered*.

And the trail led here, to a hedge-witch named Rosalind Fenner, who had been flagged eleven years ago and left alone because she seemed harmless — because she grew vegetables and mended neighbors' fences and once, according to a village record Vane had personally un-burned just to read it, taught children their letters using "funny old words" for practice.

Funny old words. The Crown's clerks had laughed at that entry and filed it under *trivia*.

Vane did not laugh at trivia. Trivia was where countries went to die.

* * *

They surrounded the cottage in the professional silence of men who had done this before, and Vane knocked on the door. Not kicked it. Knocked. He had rules. One of them was that you knocked first, because a door opened willingly told you more than a door broken down, and because — though he would have denied this under torture — his mother had raised him to knock.

The door opened on the third knock. A woman stood there, sixty-odd, grey-haired, wearing a shawl and an expression of profound, almost theatrical inconvenience, as though Vane were a traveling salesman and it was barely seven in the morning.

"Yes?" she said.

"Inquisitor Edmure Vane," he said, and showed the seal. "I have a warrant for the search of this premises and the detention of any persons found within."

"How nice for you," said Rosalind Fenner. "Do come in, then. Mind the cat. She bites officials on principle."

There was no cat. Vane looked anyway, because a witch announcing a cat was either a lie or a warning, and both were information.

Inside, the cottage smelled of bread and dried herbs and woodsmoke. It was, Vane noted with professional displeasure, exactly the kind of place that made soldiers hesitate. Brakel was already looking at the mantelpiece with the soft expression of a man remembering his grandmother. Dangerous. Sentiment was how searches failed.

"You've had visitors," Vane said. It was not a question. The hearth held three cups' worth of rings, still damp. Two of them were small.

"I have tea with the postman most mornings," Rosalind said. "He's a lovely boy. Terrible handwriting."

"The guide Hazel Oyelaran. And the child."

"Mm." She poured herself tea from a pot that had been sitting cold, which meant she'd been expecting them since roughly midnight, which meant she had chosen to be caught mid-tea rather than mid-flight. Vane filed that away and felt the first cold thread of unease go down his spine, because people who chose to be caught were people with plans, and plans in a hedge-witch's cottage were usually made of stranger materials than iron.

"They came through," Rosalind admitted, settling into her chair as if this were a social call. "Left yesterday evening. You won't catch them."

"Madam, I have twelve soldiers and fresh horses."

"And they have the deep passes and a head start and, if half of what I suspect is true, a country's worth of luck." She sipped her tea. "You look tired, Inquisitor. Have a cup. It's chamomile. Very calming. Good for men who burn things."

"We do not *burn* things," Brakel said from the doorway, stung. "We *destroy unauthorized records*. There's a procedure."

"There usually is," Rosalind said gently, and something in her voice made the sergeant go quiet and slightly pink.

Vane let the exchange happen. While they talked, he walked the room. He touched nothing — another rule; witches salted things, hexed things, booby-trapped things with consequences that took weeks to declare themselves — but he looked. Bundles of herbs labeled in trade-tongue. Bundles labeled in nothing he recognized. A child's wooden bird on the windowsill, carved badly and lovingly. A second cup, chipped, sitting upside down to dry.

And on the table, a stack of letters. Old ones, soft-cornered, tied with string. The topmost bore an address in a hand Vane knew from the guild records: *Hazel Oyelaran, Waystation Nine, the Thorn Road.*

Returned. Unopened. Years of them, by the look of the stack.

Vane felt something twist behind his ribs that he refused to name. Here was the whole sad architecture of it: the woman who wrote, the girl who wouldn't read, the child caught between them like a shuttle between looms. Erasure, he thought, is mercy — but this wasn't erasure. This was just ordinary human cowardice, the kind no warrant could reach.

"Where are they going?" he asked.

Rosalind smiled at him. It was a good smile. Warm. It reached her eyes.

It was also, Vane realized approximately one and a half seconds later, a distraction.

* * *

The garden went first.

There was no flash of light. No thunderclap. What happened was quieter than that and infinitely worse: the garden simply began to disagree with itself.

The bean vines unwove. Not wilted — *unwove*, each tendril retracting into its seed with the tidy precision of a sentence being erased word by word. The roses folded inward, petal by petal, until they were buds, then knots of green, then nothing but a rosebush-shaped absence with a wheelbarrow growing back out of it, whole and unbroken, as if it had never fallen. The soil itself seemed to sigh. Somewhere in the ranks of herbs, a plant Vane had never been able to name stopped existing so thoroughly that even his memory of trying to name it slid away like water off glass.

"What is she doing?" Brakel shouted, backing toward the fence. "Sir! What is she—"

"She's paying," Vane said, and his voice came out flat and strange.

Because he understood it then, all at once, the way you understand a debt when the collector arrives. Names are load-bearing. He had heard that phrase in a burned book once, in a margin, in a dead scholar's handwriting, and dismissed it as poetry. But the garden had been *worked* — decades of small workings, weather-charms and growth-spells and protections layered into every root like mortar into a wall — and Rosalind Fenner was calling in every charm at once, spending thirty years of patient magic in ninety seconds, buying one thing with everything she had.

Buying time.

"Take her!" Vane snapped. "Now, before—"

Two soldiers moved toward the cottage door. Rosalind set down her teacup with a small, precise click, looked Vane directly in the eye, and said a word.

Not a spell. A word. Four syllables, maybe five, in a language that made the air feel older. Vane's training screamed at him to cover his ears and he did, a half-second too late, and the word went through him like cold water through cloth.

And half of Rosalind's face went *absent*.

She didn't fall. She sat there in her chair, upright, breathing, watching him with eyes that had suddenly gone vague as pondwater, and when she spoke again her voice was hers but the furniture of her mind had been rearranged.

"I'm sorry," she said pleasantly. "Were we talking? I feel we were talking. There was something important — there was a girl — there was..." She frowned, groping. "There was a garden. Wasn't there? I had a garden. Did I have a garden?"

"Ma'am," Brakel said helplessly, "you've got a — there's a yard out back, it's all mud now, just mud and a wheelbarrow—"

"A wheelbarrow!" she said, delighted, as if he'd brought her a gift. "How useful. You can carry such a lot in a wheelbarrow."

Vane stood in the doorway between the ruined garden and the ruined woman and felt the first crack run through the edifice of his certainty. This was not resistance. Resistance fought. This was a woman who had looked at the price of escape — her memories, her life's work, half of everything she was — and paid it without blinking, the way you'd pay a toll.

What kind of cause was worth that toll?

*The right kind,* whispered something in him that sounded appallingly like his mother singing, and he put the thought down the way you'd put down a hot coal.

"Search the house," he said. "Carefully. Then bind her hands — gently, Sergeant, *gently* — and we'll take her to the foothill garrison. She'll be treated well. She'll be *questioned* well. Whatever she knows about the passes, whatever she taught the Oyelaran woman, we will recover it."

Rosalind watched them wrap the rope around her wrists with mild interest, like a spectator at someone else's picnic.

"You're a careful young man," she told Vane. "You knock first. Your mother taught you that."

He went very still.

"I can always tell," she said, smiling her warm smile with half a mind behind it. "The ones who knock. They think the knocking makes it civilized. It doesn't, dear. But it does mean there's a door in you somewhere, and doors—" she paused, and the vagueness crossed her face again like a cloud, "—doors are my business. Were my business. Whose business was it?"

Nobody answered her. Outside, the garden was mud and a wheelbarrow, and the wheelbarrow was the only thing in the valley that remembered what had grown there.

* * *

They found the tunnel at dusk.

It started under the hearthstone — of course it did; hedge-witches and their hearths — and ran downslope through packed earth, old and dry and lined with roots that had grown around it rather than through it, as if even the trees had agreed to keep the secret. It emerged in a bramble-choked hollow a quarter mile below the cottage, opening onto goat tracks and a streambed and, eventually, the high passes.

Too late. Hours too late. Vane stood at the tunnel mouth with a torch and did the arithmetic he hated doing: a head start of nearly a day, a guide who knew these mountains better than any soldier alive, and a child whose words opened locks *politely*.

"Set a watch on the tunnel," he said. "Send riders to Sorrow Bridge and the Hollowmere fork. And get me the guild contact in Lowmark — Corvo. If anyone can price where a smuggler runs, it's the man who owns her debts."

Brakel hesitated. "And the witch, sir? Garrison's asking what charge to write down."

Vane looked back up the slope toward the cottage, where lamplight had been lit — one of the soldiers lighting lamps for a prisoner, because the prisoner had asked nicely, because even bound and half-unmade she had a way of making men want to do small kindnesses for her.

"Unlicensed speech," he said. "Practices forbidden arts. Endangered the realm." The words came out rote and correct and, for the first time in nineteen years of service, slightly sour in his mouth. "Write it properly, Sergeant. We are not savages. We follow procedure."

"Yes sir."

That night, camped at the tree line, Vane dreamed of his mother singing. He woke with the melody gone and the loss sharp, and lay staring at the stars and telling himself the familiar catechism: *erasure is mercy, order is kindness, a border is a promise kept.*

Three valleys north, on a track so old it had forgotten its own name, Hazel Oyelaran dragged a stumbling child uphill through the dark, and did not look back, and did not stop, because stopping was a luxury and guilt was a luxury and the only thing she owned outright now was the next mile.

Behind them both, in a garrison cell smelling of straw and lamp oil, Rosalind Fenner slept the sleep of the half-remembering, and dreamed — the guards swore afterward she must have been dreaming — of a garden coming back.
