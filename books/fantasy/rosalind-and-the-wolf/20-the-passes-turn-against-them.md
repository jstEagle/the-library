## 20. The Passes Turn Against Them

Hazel had a rule about mountains: you didn't argue with them, and you didn't thank them either, because both invited attention, and attention from a mountain was like attention from a tax collector — it always cost more than you'd budgeted.

For eleven years she had known the Thornfell passes the way other people knew their own kitchens. She knew which scree slope lied about its footing and which one told the truth grudgingly. She knew that the third switchback above the Grey Stair had a cairn that some long-dead smuggler had built two stones too tall on purpose, so that anyone who trusted it blindly would walk off the edge of a shelf no wider than a coffin. She knew where the wind slept at noon and where it woke up mean.

She was, she would have said without false modesty, the best guide in the high country. Possibly the only guide in the high country, if you didn't count goats, and goats were unreliable contractors.

And now the mountains were forgetting her roads.

It started small, the way all disasters do — politely, almost. On the second morning out of Rosalind's burning hedge, Hazel led Wren up toward the Shepherd's Cut, a notch in the ridge she could have walked blindfolded, drunk, and being chased, in roughly that order of urgency. She crested the shoulder of the hill, looked down into the cut —

— and there was no cut.

There was a shoulder of rock, smooth and unbroken, running on for half a mile as though the mountain had never been anything but that. Hazel stood there with her mouth open, doing the arithmetic every smuggler does when reality stops adding up: *Am I wrong? Am I lost? Or is the world wrong?*

She checked the sun. She checked the cairn — the little stack of stones she herself had built four years ago, still faithfully marking a pass that no longer existed. The cairn stood there looking embarrassed, like a signpost to a town that had burned down.

"Huh," said Hazel.

Wren looked up at her. The child's face was pale under the wind-scour, and there was something new in it — not fear exactly, but the particular guilt of someone who has knocked over a cup and can't yet admit it. Wren's hands moved in the small, urgent gestures they used when trade-tongue failed: *me, sorry, me.*

"You didn't do this," Hazel said automatically, because that was what you said to children. Then she looked again at the unbroken rock, and at Wren's hands, which had been glowing faintly at the fingertips since dawn — a glow like the last coals of a fire nobody remembered lighting — and revised her statement. "All right. You did *some* of this. But we're going to agree it was an accident, because I cannot handle a confession right now."

Wren nodded solemnly, which was somehow worse than an argument.

* * *

Here was the trouble, as Hazel pieced it together over the next day, talking it through aloud because Wren couldn't talk back and Hazel's own silence had started to feel like a room with the door bricked over:

The old tongue didn't just name things. It *held* things. That was what Rosalind had spent years trying to pound into her stubborn skull — names are load-bearing, take the name away and the wall comes down. And Wren, walking deeper into the bones of Thornfell with every mile, was speaking more and more of that language in sleep, in dreams, in the little involuntary syllables that escaped when they stumbled or laughed. Every word was a word that had been buried three hundred years ago along with everything else. And the mountain, hearing its own dead language rise up out of a child's mouth, was apparently responding the way a man responds to his mother's voice after decades abroad: by forgetting where he'd been in the meantime.

The paths were going first because the paths were the newest things. Smuggler's roads were only a few centuries old — young, shallow-rooted, held in the mountain's memory the way a nickname is held, casually, without conviction. Thornfell itself had been here for thousands of years before any kingdom drew a line around it. When the mountain began remembering *that*, the smuggler's roads were the first thing sloughed off, like a snake shedding skin it no longer needed.

Hazel tested the theory grimly. The Hollowmere road: gone, replaced by a stand of birches that had no business growing above the treeline. The Grey Stair: still there, but the switchbacks had multiplied — she counted nine where there should have been five, each one carved in a style older than the guild, older than the Crown, older than anything with a flag. The Widow's Traverse, the single most reliable route across the whole range, the road she'd run contraband down since she was sixteen: she found it, walked fifty yards along it, and then it simply ended in a wall of stone, mid-stride, as if the masons had been called away to lunch three hundred years ago and never come back.

"It's not erasing," Hazel said, crouched at the wall's base, touching the stone. It was warm. Stone at eight thousand feet in late autumn should not be warm. "It's *remembering harder*. My roads are getting crowded out."

Wren crouched beside her and pressed one small palm flat against the rock. For a moment nothing happened. Then the child made a sound — not a word, more a hum with intent behind it, a hum shaped like *please* — and the wall shivered. Not fell. Shivered, and grew a ledge. A narrow one, barely a hand's width, spiraling up and over like a stitch pulled through cloth.

Hazel stared at it. "That's not a path. That's a *suggestion* of a path."

Wren shrugged, in the universal gesture of children everywhere: *it worked, didn't it?*

"It did work," Hazel admitted. "I hate that it worked." She looked up at the ledge winding away into the cloud, and felt something settle in her chest that she recognized from bad winters and worse debts: the specific vertigo of competence failing. Her whole life, her whole *value*, had been built on knowing these passes better than anyone alive. Take that away and what was she? A woman with a rope, a knife, and a child who could move mountains but couldn't ask for water in any language Hazel spoke.

"We're going to die up here," she announced, "and it's going to be embarrassing. Everyone at the guild will say, Oyelaran, lost in her own mountains, imagine. They'll put it in a ballad. It'll be a *funny* ballad."

Wren patted her arm. The gesture was so precisely consoling, so exactly what you'd offer a frightened animal, that Hazel laughed despite everything — a short, cracked sound the wind took away immediately.

"All right," she said. "New plan. We stop navigating and start asking."

* * *

Asking turned out to be a strange business.

Hazel had spent her career treating the mountain as an adversary to be outmaneuvered, and now she found herself walking slowly, watching for the places where the stone seemed to lean toward them rather than away. Wren would stop sometimes, tilt their head as though listening to something very far underground, and then tug Hazel's sleeve and point — and there would be a route. Not always a good one. Once it was a frozen waterfall that required an hour of knife-work and two prayers Hazel didn't believe in. Once it was a hollow under a fallen slab where they waited out a storm pressed together like spoons in a drawer, listening to the wind try to peel the sky off the peaks.

But there was always a way. The mountain, whatever else it was doing, did not seem to want them dead. It wanted them *home*, maybe. Or it wanted Wren home, and Hazel came attached, like burrs on a coat.

On the fourth day, in a high valley where the snow lay blue and undisturbed, Hazel made a discovery that chilled her worse than the wind did.

Her own footprints were gone.

Not blown over — gone. The snow behind them lay smooth and white as fresh paper, ten steps back, twenty, all the way to the lip of the valley, as though they'd been dropped there by the sky. Ahead of them, their tracks stretched out crisp and ordinary. Behind: nothing. The mountain was keeping only the present tense. It was forgetting them as fast as they passed through it, the way a dream forgets itself, and Hazel stood in the middle of that white silence feeling the hair lift on her arms.

"Wren," she said carefully. "Look back."

Wren looked. And Hazel watched the child understand it — watched the small shoulders drop, the chin crumple, the breath go ragged — and realized with a lurch that this was different from the roads. The roads were Thornfell's memory crowding out the newcomers'. This was Wren's power reaching further than the child wanted it to, spilling out sideways, eating even the evidence of themselves. Wren was becoming so much a part of this place that the place was absorbing them, dissolving their trail, their past, their *outside-ness* — pulling them in like a tide pulls a swimmer who has swum too far from shore.

"No," Hazel said, kneeling in the snow and taking the child's face in her mittened hands. "No, none of that. Listen to me. You are not disappearing. You are standing right here, being extremely cold and extremely annoying about my cooking, and I am not losing you to a *mountain*. Mountains don't get to keep people. I've been dodging that my whole life and I'm not starting now, especially not with you."

Wren blinked at her. Then, slowly, deliberately, the child reached out and dragged one finger through the snow between them — and wrote.

Hazel had taught Wren trade-tongue letters during the snowbound week in the shepherd's hut, back when the world was simpler and consisted entirely of one hut, one storm, and a vocabulary built from soup. The letters in the snow were wobbly but unmistakable:

**HAZEL**

"That's me," Hazel said, her voice coming out rougher than intended.

Wren pointed at the name. Then at Hazel. Then drew a circle around it, firm and complete, like a fence, like a ward, like a promise.

"Right," Hazel managed. "Well. Obviously. That's just basic cartography."

They walked on. The snow behind them stayed blank. But Hazel noticed, and pretended not to notice, that Wren glanced back less after that — as if the name in the snow had been proof enough, filed away somewhere the mountain couldn't reach.

* * *

By the sixth day Hazel had stopped trusting her maps entirely, which for her was less an inconvenience than a small death. She'd drawn those maps herself, over eleven years, in a cipher of her own devising, annotated with notes like *loose rock, liar* and *good ambush spot, avoid Tuesdays* (guild scouts kept schedules; it was one of their many failings). Now she sat on a wind-scoured ledge turning the pages and finding them useless — not wrong, exactly, but *orphaned*, describing roads that no longer connected to anything, a country of ghosts drawn in her own careful hand.

"This is what Vane wants," she said suddenly, the thought arriving whole. She looked up at Wren, who was gnawing stoically on dried apple. "You understand that? This is the whole argument. He burns archives because he thinks forgetting is mercy. And look—" she gestured at the blank snow, the vanished roads, "—you're doing it by accident. Just by *being loud in your sleep*. Every word out of you takes another piece off the map. If he saw this, he wouldn't need to convince anyone. He'd just point."

Wren had stopped chewing. The child set down the apple with great care, the way people do when they're buying time to feel something, and then looked at Hazel with an expression far too old for the face carrying it. One hand rose and pressed flat against Hazel's sternum, over the heart, and the other hand pressed against Wren's own chest, and then Wren pointed at the blank snow behind them and shook their head, slow and fierce.

*Not the same,* the gesture said. *I am not him.*

"I know," Hazel said. "I know you're not. But sweetheart, intentions are worth nothing on a mountain. Ask any avalanche." She sighed and pulled her cloak tighter. "Which means we have a problem, because the only thing I know how to do is find roads, and the roads are leaving faster than I can find them, and we've got — what, four days' hard walking to the Door if the Door still exists, which, given recent trends, I'm choosing not to think about."

Wren tilted their head. Then the child did something new: took Hazel's hand, turned it palm-up, and traced a shape in it with one finger. Slowly. Twice. Making sure.

Hazel knew the shape. She'd known it since she was fifteen and pretending not to listen to Rosalind's lessons, doodling it in the margins of her thoughts while she planned her escape. It was the old-tongue character for *door* — Rosalind had been very fond of it, very insistent about it, drawing it in spilled flour and river mud: a threshold, a line that is also an opening, depending on which side of it you're standing.

"You want me to learn," Hazel said. "You want me to *speak* it."

Wren nodded.

"Wren, I once called hedge-craft 'weeds and wishful thinking' to your face— well, to Rosalind's face, you weren't born yet, but the principle stands. I am the least magical person in this entire mountain range. I once tried to bless a shipment of brandy for luck and the blessing came out as a sneeze."

Wren's expression did not change. It was the immovable expression of a child who has decided something, and Hazel had learned in six days of blizzards and vanishing roads that there was no force on earth — guild, Crown, or geology — equal to it.

"Fine," Hazel said. "Fine. Teach me the door-word. But if I sprout warts or start glowing, I'm blaming you, and I want it noted that I said so."

Wren smiled — the first full smile in two days — and took Hazel's hand again, patient as snowfall, and began.

* * *

They made camp that night in a hollow beneath an overhang, and Hazel dreamed.

She dreamed of a hall lit green-gold, the light coming from nowhere and everywhere, the way light comes in deep water. There was a woman in the hall, tall and crowned, or wearing what had been a crown once and was now something sadder — a circlet of shadow with thorns where the jewels should be. Her face was beautiful the way winter is beautiful, which is to say it would kill you eventually and look magnificent doing it.

She did not speak. She didn't need to. The dream itself was the message, laid into Hazel's mind like a seal pressed into wax:

*I know what you have lost. I know what you gave away. I can give it back.*

Hazel woke with her heart slamming and frost on her eyelashes, and Wren was already awake, sitting bolt upright, staring into the dark with eyes gone wide and coal-bright — and somewhere far below them, down in the roots of the mountain, something that had been waiting three hundred years had just said her name.
