## 21. The Queen's Offer

The dead do not sleep, but they remember sleeping, and it is from that remembered shape — the hush, the dark, the slow tide of breath — that I learned to enter dreams.

I had been a queen for eleven years before the erasure, and a ghost for three hundred after, and I will tell you which taught me more about ruling. A crown teaches you to speak to crowds. Death teaches you to listen to one person at a time, in the dark, when their guard is down and their heart is honest as an open till.

The girl's dream was a road.

Of course it was. They always are, the ones who live by walking. I have entered the dreams of farmers and found endless fields; of bakers, ovens warm as wombs; of a tax collector once, whose dream was a ledger so beautiful I wept, though perhaps that says more about me than him. But guides and smugglers dream of roads — roads unrolling ahead, roads crumbling behind, roads with someone waiting at the end of them or no one, depending on how cruel the day has been.

Hazel Oyelaran's road was snowed under. She was standing on it in her dream with her hands in her pockets, looking at the drifts, and even asleep she wore the expression of a woman doing arithmetic she did not like.

I let myself be seen the way you let light into a room: gradually, so as not to startle.

She startled anyway.

"Right," she said, before I had said a word. "Either I'm dreaming, or I've finally eaten something off a corpse. Both are possible. Give me a moment."

*You may take several,* I said. *It is only a dream.*

"That's what worries me." She looked around at the snowfield, at the mountains rising behind us like the shoulders of something sleeping. "This is Thornfell, isn't it. The passes. Before—" She stopped herself, the way people do when they nearly say a word they've been taught not to say. Three centuries of careful forgetting, and still the shape of the silence was there, worn smooth as a river stone. "Before whatever happened here."

*I happened here,* I said. *Or rather, I failed to. Sit, if you like. Dreams are kinder to the seated.*

She did not sit. She was, I could already tell, a woman who would stand through her own funeral if sitting meant conceding anything. Instead she folded her arms and studied me with the frank appraisal of someone pricing a horse.

"You're the queen," she said. "The one Wren talks about. In the old tongue." Her mouth worked around the words of my language — badly, haltingly, the accent of a woman who had learned them recently and against her will, and yet. And yet. There was a hedge-witch somewhere behind this girl's history who had planted those syllables deep, back when Hazel was young enough to pretend she wasn't listening. Words are patient. They wait in the soil longer than the gardener expects. *The queen under the mountain.* "Wren's dreams have you in them too. The child won't say much about it."

*Then the child is wiser than either of us.*

"Low bar," said Hazel. "What do you want?"

Direct. Good. I have never had patience for the long dance of pleasantries, and death has burned what little remained. I gathered the dream around us the way I had once gathered a cloak, and showed her.

* * *

I showed her Rosalind.

Not the hedge-witch as she is now — half-her memory spent like a purse emptied to buy two children a night's escape — but whole. I reached into the country's remembering, into the great loom of Thornfell where every erased thing is kept, and I drew out the shape of what Vane's raiders had taken: the garden first, then the years, then the names. I showed Hazel her old mistress laughing at some joke neither of them would ever recall, hands earthy to the wrist, teaching a fifteen-year-old runaway the word for *guest* in a language older than kingdoms.

"This isn't real," Hazel said, but her voice had gone rough, and she could not look away. "Rosalind gave those memories up. You can't just—"

*I can restore them. Every one. Not a copy, girl — the true thing, drawn home out of my country's keeping. Your witch will wake tomorrow knowing everything she knew, plus everything she has learned since. Memory is only another kind of border. Borders are my trade.*

"And my name?" she said, and now I heard it, the small hungry crack beneath the flatness. "You said—"

*I know what the guild has done to you. Corvo sold your debts to the Crown like a man selling a lame horse, and now every road south has your description nailed to its posts. I can unmake that too. Names are only words, and words are only doors. I was a queen; opening doors was the least of my offices. By spring, Hazel Oyelaran will be no one's debtor and no one's quarry. You will walk into Lowmark and buy back your mother's ring from whatever pawnshop holds it, and the clerk will smile at you, because clerks smile at free women.*

She was silent a long time. The snow fell around us without landing, the way snow does in dreams, and in the distance — I did not show it to her, but I let her hear it — a bell rang in a village that had been ash for three hundred years.

"And the price," she said finally. "There's always a price. Smuggler's arithmetic. Nothing crosses a border free."

*Clever girl.* I let the dream soften, let the false-Rosalind fade gently rather than cutting it, because kindness costs nothing in a dream and buys so much. *You know the price already. You have known it since you saw me in the child's dreams and did not run.*

"Wren," she said. "You want me to bring Wren to you instead of the Door."

*I want you to bring the child home,* I said, and I made the words true, because they were true, and because a lie offered to a liar is wasted craft. *That is all any of this has ever been. A mother asking for her country's children to come home. The Door sends Wren through to — what? What lies beyond it, guide? Have you asked? Has the witch told you? A threshold out of a country that officially never existed, opening onto what, exactly?*

"I don't know," Hazel admitted. It cost her; I watched it cost her. Competent people hate admitting ignorance the way misers hate parting coin.

*No one knows. That is the honest answer, and I honor you for giving it. But I know what lies behind me.* I turned, and I let her see — not the ruin, not the hollow where my body had starved while my city starved faster, but the echo-country as Wren's voice had woken it: orchards in impossible blossom, a wedding party forever reaching its final dance, a baker drawing loaves from an oven that never quite finished heating. The last evening of Thornfell, looping like a fly in amber. *Every soul my kingdom lost is here. Not ghosts. People. Stubborn, grieving, ordinary people, living their last day over and over because no one would write them down and let them rest. The child can change that. The child can make us real again — alive again, in the world, with fields and winters and taxes, God help us, and mornings. All I ask is that the child come to me first. Let me ask properly. Let me kneel to a child, if kneeling is what it takes. Is that so monstrous?*

"It might be," Hazel said quietly. "Depends what 'real again' costs the borderlands. Depends what happens to everyone living between here and Lowmark if Thornfell comes back with borders."

Ah. There it was. The wound in my argument, and she had found it in four sentences, this barefoot smuggler with frostbite scars on her fingers. I had spent three centuries perfecting my grief and she had undone its hemming in a moment.

*You sound like the witch,* I said, and was surprised to find fondness in it.

"People keep telling me that." She rubbed her face with both hands. When she lowered them she looked, for the first time, simply tired — a tiredness older than this dream, older than the road, maybe older than her name. "Look. I'm going to be honest with you, because you're dead and honesty seems rude to withhold from the dead. If you'd come to me six months ago and offered me a clean name and Rosalind's memory back, I'd have carried you Wren in a sack and called it business. I'd have slept fine."

*And now?*

"Now I've heard the kid hum dead orchards back to life. Now I've seen what Vane does to archives. Now—" She stopped. Started again. "Now I know what it's like to be the person someone waits up with a lamp for, and I can't un-know it. So I'm not saying no. I'm saying I don't trust anyone's arithmetic anymore. Including mine. Including yours."

*That,* I said, *is the wisest thing anyone has said to me since my death, and I include the archbishop who apologized at my funeral for having backed the wrong succession.*

That surprised a laugh out of her — short, rusty, real. Even in dreams, laughter is a door. I pressed my advantage gently, the way you press a bruise to learn how deep it goes.

*Then hear my terms, and take your time answering. Bring the child to me before the Door closes. Let Wren choose — truly choose, with both futures laid out, not smuggled, not sold, not saved by someone else's guilt. If the child chooses the Door after hearing me, I will not bar the way. On my crown, on my grave, on the bones of everyone I failed: I will open the road myself and stand aside. And whether the child comes to me or goes through, you will have your name cleared and your witch made whole. Either way, Hazel Oyelaran. That is not a bargain. That is a gift with a request attached, and I am old enough to know the difference.*

She stared at me for a long moment. Snow that never landed. A bell that never finished ringing.

"Why?" she said. "Why be generous? Queens aren't. I've met the lowland kind."

*Because I have been dead for three hundred years,* I said, *and in all that time, no one has spoken to me as anything but a monster or a myth. You priced me like a horse and then refused to buy. Do you know how restful that is?* I let the dream begin to thin around us, the mountains going soft at their edges. *But I will tell you a truth, since you dealt fairly with me. The Door is closing. Your witch knows it; I feel it in the roots of my country like a tooth going bad. Whatever choice is coming, it is coming soon, and soon after that there will be no choice at all — only a sealed grave and a child locked on the wrong side of it. Do not let the child be locked anywhere, guide. You of all people should understand what that means.*

"What's on the other side of the Door?" she asked again, sharper now, catching at the fading dream like a woman grabbing a sleeve. "You must know. Tell me what's—"

*Ask the child,* I said, as the snow took me. *Ask the child what home means. I have wondered for three hundred years, and the dead are poor questioners — we only ever get answers in our sleep.*

* * *

I woke — the word I use for it — in the hollow beneath the mountain, in the dark that smells of stone and old smoke, and I found my hands shaking.

Ghosts' hands do not shake. We have no blood to chill, no breath to catch. What shook was older than anatomy: the memory of shaking, preserved like everything else in this country, and for the first time in three centuries I resented the preservation. Grief, at least, loops cleanly. Hope loops ragged.

Because I had lied to her. Not in the terms — the terms stand, my oath stands, I will open the road myself if the child refuses me. But in one thing, one small thing, the size of a keyhole.

I told her I wanted Wren to choose. True. I told her I would kneel. True — God forgive me, true. But I did not tell her what I have begun to suspect as the child's voice grows stronger in my country's soil, day by day, word by word: that Wren is not merely a key to the Door.

Wren may be the Door. Or the last of what made it. And if the child passes through to whatever lies beyond, my country does not close gently behind them.

My country ends.

Far above me, faint as a memory of a memory, I felt the echo-orchards stir, and heard — or dreamed I heard — a child's voice asking someone, in a language older than kingdoms, a single word.

The word for *home*.
