## 22. Wren Asks the Question

The fire had burned down to that stage where it was more memory than flame, and Wren was watching Hazel the way Wren watched everything now — openly, hungrily, the way a person watches a well when they have only just learned what thirst is.

They were in the lee of a fallen stone, somewhere in the high passes where the mountains had begun forgetting their own names. The wind came through in long sighs. Somewhere below them, a path that had existed yesterday did not exist tonight, and Hazel had spent an hour being quietly furious about it before giving up and making camp.

Wren had been counting words.

That was a habit now, a private game. Trade-tongue words learned: *fire. Bread. Cold. Go. Stay. Home.* That last one Hazel used often, always carelessly, the way people use things they have never had to think about. *We'll make for home ground. The hut's home enough. Home is behind us now.*

Home. Wren turned the word over like a river stone, feeling all its sides.

In the old tongue there were eleven words where trade-tongue had this one. There was the home you slept in. The home your dead slept in. The home you fled from. The home you were carried toward. The home that was a door left open. The home that was a door shut forever. Wren knew all eleven. Knew them the way you know your own hands.

But the question underneath the word — the one no language had a tidy word for — that one Wren had been carrying for days now, up through the forgetting passes, past the bridge that was no longer a bridge, and it had grown heavy as a full water-jar.

Tonight, Wren decided, it would be set down.

* * *

Hazel was mending a strap by firelight, working the awl with quick, competent little jabs, her whole body doing the thing it did when it didn't want to think: work. Wren had catalogued this too. When Hazel's hands were busy, her face went honest. It was the only time her face went honest on purpose.

"Home," Wren said.

Hazel's awl stopped. "Mm?"

"What means." Wren patted the ground between them, then gestured at the dark all around, the whole black country of it. "This. Not this." A pause, hunting. "What means... when—" and here Wren's careful new vocabulary ran out of road, so Wren did what Wren always did when trade-tongue failed: reached into the old tongue, found the word for *a country that has been made to never-have-been*, and said it.

The fire guttered. Just a little. Just enough.

Hazel flinched. "Don't do that."

"Do what?"

"You know what." Hazel set down the strap. Her voice had gone flat, which Wren had learned meant she was holding something down with both hands. "Words do things out here. You know that better than anyone."

"Yes." Wren leaned forward. This was important. This was the whole point. "So. What means home — when country is..." and again the old tongue rose up, offering its terrible precision, and again Wren swallowed most of it and gave Hazel only the shape of it: "—when country is grave."

* * *

Here is what happened inside Hazel Oyelaran in the space of about four seconds, though Wren could not have known it then and would only piece it together much later:

First, she reached for a lie. She was very good at lies; they were, in a sense, her profession. The lie ready to hand was a warm one, a kind one even: *Home is wherever you're safe, sprout. Home is people, not places.* She'd heard it in songs. It cost nothing. It would put the child to sleep.

Second, she opened her mouth to say it.

Third, she looked at Wren — really looked, the way she avoided doing — and saw a child who had walked barefoot out of a storm, who had asked a lock politely to open, who had watched orchards blossom at their humming and understood it as *greeting*, who had lost not a house but a *country*, an entire nation of fields and bread-ovens and grandmothers, erased so thoroughly that the maps had healed over like skin — and she saw that child waiting, with complete trust, for an answer.

And fourth, Hazel thought: *I lied my way out of Rosalind's cottage at fifteen, and I have been lying ever since, and every lie has cost me something I cannot buy back, and this child deserves better than my best work.*

She closed her mouth. Opened it again.

"That's a hard question," she said finally, which was true, and also a stalling tactic, and Wren waited with the patience of someone whose people had kept a Door for three hundred years and knew all about waiting.

* * *

"Home is where your bed is," Hazel tried. "Where your things are."

Wren shook their head slowly. "Things burn. Bed burns. Country burned." A small, terrible shrug. "Still home. Why?"

"Because—" Hazel rubbed her face. "Because you say so. Because you decide it. Home is... a thing you choose."

"Choose." Wren tasted the word. Then, gently, with the devastating literalness of the very young: "I choose Thornfell. Is not here. Is not anywhere. Maps eat it." The old tongue flickered at the edge of Wren's voice again, and Hazel said, quickly, "Don't," and Wren held it back, but the wind through the pass changed key anyway, a low note like a struck bell, and both of them sat very still until it passed.

"You see?" Hazel said, half laughing, not amused at all. "You see why we don't say things here."

"I see why you are afraid of saying things," Wren corrected. "Different."

Hazel stared at the child across the fire. "How old are you? Actually?"

"Nine winters. Maybe ten. Counting is different where I am from." Wren hugged their knees. "You are avoiding."

"I'm what?"

"Avoiding. Your word. I learn it from you. You avoid the guild. You avoid the queen's dreams. You avoid letters—" Wren stopped, because Hazel had gone very still, and some instinct older than either language told Wren this was a wall, not a door.

But Wren had come too far to stop at walls anymore.

"My teacher," Wren said instead, quieter. "Before. In the echo-country. She says: a question carried too long gets heavy. Heavy questions, you must set down or they crush the carrier." Wren looked at Hazel with those eyes that had seen a country die twice, once in fire and once in ink. "I carry this one since Hollowmere. Since the blossoms. Everyone looks at me like I am miracle or cargo or key. Nobody asks me the question I ask myself every night when you sleep."

"And what's that?"

Wren spread their hands at the darkness, at the mountains, at the whole indifferent world.

"If home is gone — if it is *grave* — then where do I go? What does the word mean, for me? You say 'home' easy, like bread. So tell me. Teach me. What means home, when your country is a grave?"

* * *

The fire popped. Somewhere above them, snow hissed off a ledge.

Hazel was quiet for a long time. Long enough that Wren began to think the answer would simply never come, that the adult would do the adult thing and change the subject, and Wren was preparing, with nine-or-ten-year-old dignity, to accept this defeat —

— when Hazel started to laugh. It wasn't a nice laugh. It was the laugh of someone who has just caught themselves red-handed.

"I can't answer that," Hazel said. "Do you want to know why? Because I don't have one. I've been running since I was fifteen. I had a home — a real one, a good one, a cottage with a hedge and a woman in it who taught me that names hold things up — and I called it superstition and I walked out, and I've spent eleven years telling myself I left because I was too smart for her. And then—" she gestured vaguely backward, toward the passes, toward the cottage, toward everything, "—then I read her letters. All of them. In one night, cornered like a rat, and every single one just said *come home, Hazel, come home when you're ready.* Eleven years of letters. Eleven years of open door. And I couldn't even open the envelopes."

Wren said nothing. This was not silence as absence; it was silence as room. Wren had learned that from the echo-country too — that sometimes the kindest thing you can give a person is a space big enough to fall apart in without hitting anything.

"So when you ask me what home means," Hazel said, "you're asking the one person in these mountains least qualified to answer. I've got a bed I pay for by the week. I've got debts in three towns and a price on my head in two. My mother's ring is in a drowned chapel. Everything I own fits in this pack, and half of it's stolen." She looked at her hands. "I'm not a person who knows what home means, sprout. I'm a person who knows exactly what it costs to lose one."

The wind sighed. Wren considered this with great seriousness.

"That is an answer," Wren said at last.

"It's a confession."

"Same thing, maybe. In my language." Wren scooted closer to the fire, closer to Hazel, close enough that the warmth was shared between them like bread. "My teacher says also: a bad truth is worth three good lies. You gave bad truth. Is good."

"Gee, thanks."

"You're welcome." Wren's mouth did the thing that was almost a smile, the first one in days. Then it faded, and the heavy question came back, because setting a question down is not the same as answering it. "But still. I need the word. I need it to mean something, or the Door—" Wren's voice caught. "The Door opens onto home. If home is nothing, if home is grave, then the Door opens onto nothing, and I walk into nothing, alone, and everyone here forgets me like the maps forgot my country, and—"

"Hey." Hazel moved. It was sudden, graceless, entirely unlike her — she crossed the space between them and took Wren by the shoulders, and her hands were shaking slightly, and her voice was rough. "Listen to me. Listen. No one forgets you. Not while I'm breathing. You hear me? I forgot somebody once. Somebody who waited for me with a lamp in the window for eleven years. I know exactly what forgetting costs, from both sides of it, and I will *not* do it again. Not to you. Whatever happens at that Door — whether it opens onto wheat fields or ash or nothing at all — you go knowing that someone remembers you. Someone carries you. That's not nothing. That might be the only thing that isn't nothing."

Wren stared up at her. In the firelight, the child's eyes were wet and furious and fierce.

"That is home," Wren said.

"What?"

"What you just said. Someone remembers you. Someone carries you." Wren pressed a small fist against their own chest. "In the old tongue, that is one of the eleven. *The home that is carried.* I thought it was the saddest one. The one for exiles. But maybe—" the fist uncurled, pressed instead against Hazel's hand where it gripped Wren's shoulder, "—maybe it is the truest one. Maybe all the others grow out of it. House-home, country-home — those are just the carried-home, planted somewhere. And when the planting place burns, the seed is still carried."

Hazel's breath went out of her slowly, like air from a punctured wineskin.

"Your teacher sounds very wise," she managed.

"She is dead. Or looping. Both." Wren shrugged, the terrible casualness of children. "She taught me the eleven homes. You taught me the twelfth."

"There is no twelfth."

"There is now. I just said it." Wren yawned suddenly, hugely, the way children do, grief and revelation both being exhausting. "Twelfth home: the one someone makes by refusing to forget you. I will teach it to my country. When I get through the Door. Even if it is ash. Especially if it is ash."

* * *

They settled down to sleep after that, Wren curled against Hazel's side without asking permission, because some things transcend language, and Hazel lay awake a long while, staring at the dying fire, one hand resting on the small rising-and-falling back.

*Carried-home,* she thought. *Eleven years of an open door, and a nine-year-old had to explain it to me.*

She was nearly asleep when Wren spoke one last time, drowsy, half-dreaming, in the borderland between the two tongues:

"Hazel. The dreams. The queen. She comes again tonight — I feel her walking in the snow, coming up the mountain. She wants to ask you the same question I asked." A pause. A small, cold shiver against Hazel's side. "But she will offer to pay for the answer. Be careful what your home costs, Hazel. Some prices look like gifts."

Hazel opened her eyes.

Far up the pass, where the stars should have been, the snow was moving against the wind.
