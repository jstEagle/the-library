## 23. Corvo's Ambush

There were three things every smuggler learned before they were allowed to carry anything worth stealing: how to read a road, how to read a man, and how to lie about both.

Hazel had learned a fourth thing on her own, somewhere in the years since — how to read the silence of birds.

The birds had gone quiet an hour ago, and not in the way they went quiet for weather or wolves. Weather-quiet was a hush, a held breath. Wolf-quiet was local, a hole in the sound where something moved. This was neither. This was the quiet of birds who had been paid to leave.

"They're ahead," she said.

Wren looked up from the flat stone they were using to practice letters, chalk stub in hand. They had gotten good at trade-tongue in the weeks since Lowmark — good enough to argue, which Rosalind said was the true sign of fluency, and good enough to be sarcastic about it, which Hazel privately considered her finest teaching. *Ahead,* Wren signed back, then added the old-tongue gesture that meant roughly *obviously*, because even a child raised outside of time could recognize when Hazel was stating the plain truth as though it were wisdom.

"Don't give me that look. Ahead could mean fifty yards or five miles, and those are very different problems."

Wren considered this, then pointed at the sky, then at the trees, then made a small gesture like wings folding — *they left all at once, close by.*

"…Five hundred yards," Hazel admitted. "Fine. You're better at this than me. Don't let it go to your head."

She crouched and did what she always did when the arithmetic turned ugly: she counted what she had. One child who could unmake borders but couldn't yet unmake a crossbow bolt traveling at speed. One knife, one hatchet, one coil of greased rope, three days of food, and a purse containing eleven coppers and a button she was fairly sure was valuable but had never had time to check. Against her: the Sorrow Bridge guild chapter, which meant Corvo himself, because Corvo never sent men to do a message's job.

And behind them — she glanced over her shoulder at the grey notch of the pass they'd climbed out of — Vane's soldiers, a day back and closing, moving faster than soldiers had any right to move through country that was actively trying to forget its own roads.

Ahead: Corvo. Behind: the Crown. And straight on, past the bridge, the high forgotten passes where the maps went blank and the mountains began remembering Thornfell.

"Right," Hazel said. "Options."

Wren sat up straighter. They liked options. They liked being included in the arithmetic; Hazel suspected it made them feel less like cargo, and she knew — because Rosalind had said it to her once, gently, in a way that had lodged under her skin like a splinter — that a person who felt like cargo eventually acted like it.

"One," Hazel said, holding up a finger. "We go off-road, climb the scree, come down on the far side of the bridge. Takes two days. Corvo's scouts know these slopes better than I do, so we'd likely meet them halfway, tired, on ground of their choosing."

Wren shook their head immediately.

"Two." A second finger. "We go back toward Vane and try to slip past the soldiers in the dark. Which is insane, and I want you to say it's insane so I don't have to."

Wren signed something brisk and rude that they'd almost certainly learned from her.

"Thank you. Three." She hesitated. "Three is we talk to him."

Wren tilted their head. *Talk?*

"Corvo. He's a monster, but he's a monster with a ledger, and ledgers can be argued with. Maybe. Once." She stood, brushing grit from her knees. "But I don't think he came to argue. I think he came to make an example, because the guild doesn't like it when people run, and it *really* doesn't like it when people run carrying something the Crown wants. It makes us look unreliable."

*You aren't unreliable,* Wren signed, with the fierce simplicity only children and the recently fluent can manage.

"I sold you once, love. Unreliable is my best quality."

Wren's face did the thing it did whenever Hazel mentioned Lowmark — a quick flinch, quickly mastered, followed by the stubborn refusal to hold it against her. They crossed the space between them and took her hand, which was their way of ending arguments they were winning.

"All right," Hazel said softly. "Bridge, then. But we do it my way, and my way has rules. Rule one: everything I own is a tool. Rule two: nothing I own is precious. Rule three—" she squeezed their hand, "—when I say *down*, you get small, and when I say *run*, you don't look back. Not once. Promise me."

Wren promised. Children promise easily; that was one of the many ways they were braver than adults.

They came down through the pines to the river valley in the last gold light of afternoon, and there it was: the Sorrow Bridge, named — according to the guild, who named everything after money or misery — for the tolls it had extracted over four centuries. It spanned the gorge in a single lurching arc of stone and old timber, wide enough for two mules abreast, patched in nine places with nine different generations of desperation. The far side vanished into the high passes, into country no map troubled to draw.

Corvo was waiting at the near end, which told Hazel he'd expected her to try to talk. Twelve men fanned along the railing and among the rocks above — she counted without moving her eyes, the way you count coins someone might be watching you count. Crossbows, mostly. Two swords. Old Maren on the rocks, who owed Hazel a debt from the Fenwinter run and would hate every minute of what came next, which was useful; hatred of one's own work made a man slow.

"Hazel Oyelaran." Corvo's voice carried across the water like it owned it. He was a broad, silvering man dressed like a prosperous merchant, because that was what he believed evil should look like: respectable, well-fed, patient. "You've cost me a buyer, an inquisitor's goodwill, and two good people who got too curious about your route. Do you know what that adds up to?"

"Let me guess. More than eleven coppers and a button."

"It adds up to collection." He spread his hands, warm as a hearth. "But I'm a reasonable man. The child walks to me, you walk away, and the debt dies with the season. That's charity, Hazel. That's me being soft."

"You're right," Hazel called back, walking forward, hands loose and visible, voice easy. "That *is* soft. Here's my counteroffer." She stopped ten yards out, close enough to talk, far enough that the crossbows would have to lead her if they wanted her. "You let us cross, and I don't tell you what I did to the third pier."

Something flickered in Corvo's face — not fear, Corvo didn't frighten, but recalculation. "You've done nothing to the third pier."

"No," Hazel agreed pleasantly. "Not yet. But I spent last night down in the gorge while the child slept, and I'll tell you what I found. That bridge is older than the guild, older than the Crown, older than whatever story you tell yourself about why you're the villain instead of just the bookkeeper. The piers are hollow. The mortar's older than mortar should be. And the whole beautiful thing is standing out of habit." She shrugged. "I've got a hatchet, a vial of lamp-oil, and no respect for history. So. New arithmetic. The child crosses free, or nobody uses this bridge again, ever, and you explain to the chapter why the Sorrow crossing died on your watch."

It was a bluff. It was ninety percent bluff, wrapped around ten percent truth, which was the correct ratio for all great lies. There was no vial of lamp-oil. There was no sabotage. But Corvo was a man who thought in assets, and a bridge was an asset, and men who think in assets can always imagine losing them.

"You'd strand yourself on the far side," Corvo said slowly. "Nothing back there but dead country and dead ends."

"Dead country doesn't send crossbowmen."

For a long moment the only sound was the river, going about its business in the gorge below, indifferent to everyone's plans. Then Corvo sighed, and Hazel's stomach dropped, because she knew that sigh. It was the sigh of a man concluding a calculation, not opening one.

"Maren," he said, without raising his voice.

On the rocks above, Maren shifted his crossbow — not toward Hazel, but toward the slope behind her. Toward Wren.

"New arithmetic," Corvo said. "You're standing between me and the child. I only need one of you to move."

*Down.*

Hazel was already moving as she shouted it, dropping into a roll as the first bolt cracked past where her chest had been, and the world went loud and simple the way it always did — no past, no debts, just the next three seconds, over and over, forever. She came up running, not toward the bridge but along it, toward the nearest support post, hatchet already out of her belt.

"Stop her!" Corvo's composure cracked clean down the middle, and underneath it was just a man shouting. "The post! Stop—"

A bolt slammed into the timber beside her head, close enough to spit splinters against her cheek. Another took her sleeve. She reached the post — ancient black oak, iron-banded, humming faintly under the bridge's weight like a plucked string — and swung.

The hatchet bit. Again. Again. Wood chips flew. Behind her boots thundered on the planks, twelve men discovering that charging across a narrow bridge at one woman with an axe was a math problem with no good answers.

"Wren!" she screamed. "The rope! The far anchor!"

She heard rather than saw it — the whip-crack of the greased rope paying out, the child's small determined grunts. That rope had been rigged that morning, quietly, while Corvo's scouts watched the road instead of the gorge, because Hazel had known — had known since the birds went quiet — that talking was a door she'd knock on only so she wouldn't regret never knocking. The rope ran from the bridge's far rail down to a boulder on the gorge's edge, and it was looped, and it was ready, and it was the single honest thing in today's entire performance.

The post groaned. Cracked. Held, barely.

"Hazel—" Wren's voice, thin with fear.

"Not yet!"

More weight on the bridge now — six men, seven, Corvo himself bellowing at them to hurry, because Corvo understood, finally, exactly what she was doing, and understanding it wouldn't stop him. The bridge swayed. Below, the river waited, white and patient, four hundred years of smugglers' bones tangled in its bed.

Hazel put everything she had left into one last swing.

The post went with a sound like a giant clearing its throat, and the bridge — proud, patched, impossible old thing — leaned, shrieked, and began to come apart in sections, planks peeling away into the gorge like pages torn from a book. Men screamed and grabbed and scrambled. Maren, bless his slow, guilty heart, had frozen on the rocks instead of firing, and would spend the rest of his life telling himself that was mercy rather than cowardice, and Hazel hoped he managed to believe it.

"Rope, Wren! Now!"

The line snapped taut. Hazel threw herself off the collapsing span, caught the rope, and swung — a wild, screaming arc out over the ruin of the bridge, over the heads of the men clinging to the far section, over the drop — and hit the scree on the far side rolling, coming up bruised and breathless with Wren already beside her, small hands still gripping the rope's end.

Below and behind, the Sorrow Bridge finished dying. What remained sagged into the gorge in a long grey sigh, and the gap it left was forty feet of empty air over white water, and Corvo stood at the broken edge with his fine coat and his ruined ledger, staring across at her.

"This isn't over," he called. His voice had gone quiet again, which was worse. "Debts don't drown, Hazel. They wait. And there's no way back from where you're standing. Nothing back there at all."

Hazel looked at the gap. Then at Wren, chalk dust still on their fingers, alive, whole, glaring across the gorge at Corvo with a fury that made her absurdly proud.

Then she picked up the hatchet, cut the rope, and dropped it into the river.

"No way back," she agreed. "Good."

They climbed. The path rose steeply from the river into pine and shadow, and behind them Corvo's voice faded, and then the men's voices, and then everything except wind and their own breathing. Hazel set a hard pace until the light failed, then a harder one after, because she wanted the gorge and everything in it well behind them before sleep.

They made camp in the lee of a boulder shaped like a sleeping animal, and shared the last of the bread, and Wren fell asleep mid-chew, the way children do when the day has been too large for them.

Hazel sat awake a while longer, doing the final sum.

Every trick spent. Every coin, every contact, every clever lie — gone into the gorge with the bridge. No way back to the lowlands, not for years, maybe never. Ahead lay only the high passes, the unmapped country, the place where the roads forgot themselves and the mountains remembered something older. The place Rosalind's letters had whispered about. The place the Door was supposed to be.

No way back but forward.

She pulled her coat over Wren's shoulders and looked up at the stars, which seemed closer here than stars had any right to be, and brighter, and arranged in patterns she half-recognized from lessons she'd pretended to forget.

Far off, beyond the ridge, something glowed — faint, steady, wrong for a campfire, wrong for anything. Like lamplight seen through a very old window.

"Wake up tomorrow," she murmured to the sleeping child. "We're nearly somewhere."

The glow pulsed, once, gently — almost like an answer.
