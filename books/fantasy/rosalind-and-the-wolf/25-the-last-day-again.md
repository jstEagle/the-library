## 25. The Last Day, Again

The sun over Thornfell did not set. It only leaned.

Hazel noticed it first as a wrongness in her bones — the particular ache of a day that had been going on too long, the way a road feels after you've walked it twice. The light came slantwise and gold across the valley, the light of an hour before dusk, and it had the quality of light that has been asked to stay past its welcome and has agreed out of politeness.

"It's always this hour," Wren said. They had been speaking more trade-tongue since the bridge, their accent still strange, each word placed down carefully like a cup on a saucer. "The last hour. It never finishes."

"Never?"

"Never." Wren's small hand found Hazel's sleeve, not her hand — Wren did not take hands yet, not quite, but they took sleeves, hems, the edges of things. "It's kind of them to keep trying."

"Kind," Hazel repeated. She looked at the valley below them — orchards heavy with fruit, wheat standing in rows like a congregation, smoke rising from chimneys in thin, unhurried threads — and felt something turn over in her chest. "Wren, how long has it been the last hour here?"

Wren considered this with the gravity of a child who had learned that adult questions deserve real answers even when the real answer is unbearable.

"Three hundred years," they said. "Give or take a supper."

* * *

They came down into the village as the bells began.

That was the other thing about the hour: it had a sound. Somewhere up the hill a church — or what had been a church; the tower was crowned with something older than any cross, a ring of standing stones wearing the building like a coat — swung its bell for evening service, and the note rolled down through the streets and was caught by the walls and held there, humming, so that the whole village seemed to be a struck instrument that no one would let finish ringing.

People were in the streets. That was the part that undid Hazel's expectations. She had braced for ghosts, for grey wailing shapes, for the horror-movie arithmetic of the dead. Instead there was a woman beating a rug off a balcony rail, three children chasing a dog around a well, an old man arguing cheerfully with a fishmonger about the price of eels.

"They're not suffering," Hazel said slowly. "They don't know."

"They know," Wren said. "But knowing doesn't hurt here. It's like — " Wren searched for the word, and when trade-tongue failed them, they said it in the old speech instead, two syllables that made the air go briefly warm and smelled, absurdly, of bread. Then they tried again. "Like a dream where you know you're dreaming. You can be sad in it. But the sadness is soft."

A woman looked up from her rug-beating and saw them — strangers, mud-caked, one tall and wary and armed with nothing but a smuggler's knife and a bad conscience, one small and barefoot and glowing faintly at the seams, the way a coal glows under ash.

"You'll want supper," the woman called down, in the old tongue, and though Hazel understood perhaps one word in five, she understood *supper*, because some words are universal. "There's a wedding at the mill. Everyone comes to the wedding. Even strangers." A pause, and then, with the mild curiosity of someone asking if they'd come far: "Are you from before or after?"

Hazel opened her mouth. Closed it.

"After," Wren said gently, for both of them.

"Oh," said the woman, and her face did something complicated and brief — a flicker of grief, gone as fast as it came, like a fish surfacing — "then you'd better eat while there's eating to be done."

* * *

The wedding was at the mill, and it was ending.

That was the thing Hazel could not stop seeing, once she'd seen it. Everything here was mid-farewell. The bride stood at the mill door in a dress the color of new cream, laughing at something her sister had said, and her laughter had the bright, brittle edge of laughter that knows. The groom held her hand the way you hold a bird you intend to release. Guests drifted toward the tables loaded with food that never diminished — Hazel watched a man take a honey cake from a platter, and the platter stayed full, and the man ate his cake with the expression of someone who has eaten that same cake ten thousand times and will eat it again tomorrow, and who has made his peace with it, mostly, on good days entirely.

"They're saying goodbye," Hazel whispered. "All of them. Forever."

"The country was erased on a Tuesday," Wren said. "In autumn. This is the evening before. Everyone knew the soldiers were coming — everyone knew what erasure meant, by then. So they spent the last day doing the last things." Wren pointed with their chin at the bride. "She married him anyway. There wasn't time for anything longer than a wedding, so she chose a wedding."

"And it never ends."

"No. And it never ends." Wren's voice caught on the shape of it. "That's the mercy and that's the cage. I don't think you can have one without the other. I asked the Door once. It didn't answer. Doors are rude."

Despite everything — despite the queen's dream still coiled somewhere behind Hazel's sternum like a hook waiting for flesh — despite three hundred years of grief pressing down on this valley like snowpack — Hazel laughed. It escaped her sideways, startled out of her.

"Doors are rude," she agreed. "I've met a few."

* * *

The baker found them before the supper did.

He was a round man with flour to his elbows and an air of tremendous purpose, and he came striding out of his shop bearing a tray of loaves, and he stopped dead when he saw Wren, and the tray trembled.

"You're one of the speaking ones," he said. His voice went careful, the way voices do around the very ill or the very holy. "We heard you coming over the bridge. The whole valley heard you. The river changed its mind about which way to run, just a little, just for a moment." He knelt, flour and all, and looked up at Wren from below, which is a posture Hazel had seen pilgrims take and had always found embarrassing, and found now, unbearably, moving. "Say something. Anything. Say it in the old way. We only get to hear it when the world outside forgets us harder."

Wren crouched too, so they were eye to eye, and said — softly, in the language older than kingdoms — a sentence Hazel didn't understand but whose meaning arrived in her chest anyway, the way music arrives: *You are remembered. I am sorry it took so long.*

The baker wept. Not dramatically. He wept the way men weep when they've been holding it for three centuries: quietly, continuously, without changing expression, flour dusting his wet cheeks into paste, while around him the wedding went on and the bell went on and the sun leaned and leaned and did not fall.

Then he straightened up, wiped his face on his shoulder, and said, "Right. Supper. You can't grieve on an empty stomach, my mother always said, and she said it every single evening for forty years until it came true, so it must be true." He pressed a loaf into Hazel's hands. It was warm. Of course it was warm. Everything here was warm, everything was mid-act, nothing had ever been allowed to go cold.

"What's your name?" Hazel asked him.

"Tomas." He smiled, and the smile had three hundred years in it. "Tomas Breadson, if you believe the census, which was invented by a clerk with no imagination. My mother was Marta. My wife is Ilse. My daughter—" He stopped. Started again. "My daughter was six."

Hazel waited. She was good at waiting; it was half her trade.

"She's at the wedding," Tomas said. "She's a flower girl. She's been a flower girl for three hundred years. She throws the petals and the petals land and then it's the moment before throwing them again, and she is happy, do you understand me? She is *happy*. She runs and laughs and the dog chases her and none of it ever hurts her because the hurting part came later, and the later part never arrives." His voice did not break. It simply went very level, the way water goes level before it goes over a ledge. "So when you ask me whether I want my country back — and everyone asks, eventually, the living always ask the dead what they want, as if we're a resource — I want you to know that I have thought about it every evening of the last evening, and I still don't know. Do you understand? I don't *know*. And anyone who tells you they know — anyone with a crown or a creed or an army — is lying to make their arithmetic easier."

* * *

They ate at the wedding feast, because refusing would have been its own cruelty.

Hazel sat between a grandmother who told the same story four times and laughed at it four times as though it were new, and a young soldier of Thornfell who had died — no, who had *stopped*, here on the last evening, before ever facing the armies that unmade his country — and who spent the meal trying to teach Wren a drinking song and getting the words slightly wrong each time and being delighted about it.

The food was endless and ordinary and perfect. The wine tasted of the year it was made, which was the last year, which meant it tasted of everything.

Rosalind — who had come over the bridge half-remembering herself, who moved through this place like a woman reading a letter from her own handwriting — sat very still for most of the meal, watching, and Hazel watched her watch, and understood that Rosalind was doing what Rosalind always did: learning. Cataloguing. Taking the country into herself the way soil takes rain.

"You're crying," Hazel said quietly.

"I'm memorizing," Rosalind corrected. Her voice was rough. Half her memory was ash on a hedge-witch's hearth, bought with a working that had unmade her garden, and what remained she guarded like coin. "Someone has to carry the recipe for that bread out of here. And the song. And the way that grandmother says 'wasn't it' — there's a grammar in it that isn't written anywhere, Hazel, it exists nowhere else in the world except in her mouth, and if no one carries it—"

"Then it dies twice."

Rosalind looked at her. Something passed between them that had no words in either language: the old wound, acknowledged, not healed — healing was going to take years and probably an argument — but *seen*.

"Yes," Rosalind said. "It dies twice. So we carry it once, at least."

* * *

Later — later being a flexible term in a country with one hour — Hazel walked.

She couldn't help it. The guide in her wanted maps, and the thief in her wanted exits, and the girl in her — the fifteen-year-old who had run from the only person who ever loved her without wanting anything back — wanted to understand what she was walking through. So she walked the lanes of the village while the eternal gold light lay along the walls like a sleeping animal.

She passed the rug-beating woman, still beating the rug. She passed the argument about eels, still unresolved, both parties still enjoying it. She passed a schoolhouse where a teacher wrote letters on a slate, reached the bottom of the lesson, erased it, and began again, and the children — Hazel stopped at the window and watched — the children raised their hands at exactly the same moments, asked the same questions, laughed at the same joke about the letter that looks like a little house. One small boy in the back row turned his head, met Hazel's eyes through the glass, and winked.

Her skin crawled. Then, immediately after, it ached.

Because that was the thing, wasn't it. That was what Vane's office had never understood, burning archives in the foothills, calling erasure *mercy*. Erasure didn't mean these people stopped. Erasure meant they *continued* — continued perfectly, continued forever, a country preserved at the moment of its death like a fly in amber, every joy genuine and every joy doomed to be its own echo. The Crown hadn't killed Thornfell. The Crown had *trapped* it. Had taken a living people and turned them into a memory of themselves, and then burned the memory, and called the ash peace.

And the guild — her guild, her family, her arithmetic — had run contraband over these mountains for three hundred years and never once wondered what was underneath the passes. Never once asked why the roads curved where they curved. She had walked over a buried nation her entire career the way you walk over a grave, except the grave was awake, and singing, and setting a place for you at supper.

"Hazel." Wren had appeared beside her, silent as their namesake. "You have the face."

"What face?"

"The face you make when the numbers stop working." Wren took her sleeve. "You had it at the chapel, when you gave away the ring. You have it now."

Hazel crouched down to the child's level. The gold light made them both look like figures in an old painting.

"Wren. When we get to the Door. When you go home." She made herself say it plainly; the child deserved plainness; everyone in this valley deserved plainness and had gotten a lie instead. "Is this what home is? Is it — is this what's waiting for you? An evening that never ends?"

Wren was quiet for a long moment. Down the lane, the flower girl threw her petals; they landed; the moment reset; she threw them again, laughing, laughing, laughing.

"My mama used to say," Wren said at last, in trade-tongue, choosing each word like a stepping-stone, "that a story isn't finished when it stops. It's finished when it means something. Maybe this country stopped. But it hasn't finished." Their small hand tightened on her sleeve. "Maybe that's what I'm for. Not to open the Door. To finish it."

Before Hazel could answer — before she could decide whether that was hope or horror, or whether it was both, which was looking increasingly like the honest answer — the light changed.

Not much. Anyone else wouldn't have noticed. But Hazel had spent her life reading skies, and she read this one the way she read a mark left on a fence post: instantly, and with a cold drop in her stomach.

The sun had moved. One degree, maybe less, toward the ridge. Toward setting.

Around them, the village had gone still. The rug-beating woman stood with her hand flat on the balcony rail. The eel argument hung unfinished in the air. In the schoolhouse, chalk hovered above slate. Three hundred years of practiced, gentle forgetting — and every soul in Thornfelt had felt it, the same heartbeat, the same thought arriving in ten thousand minds at once:

*The hour is ending.*

And beneath their feet, deep under the mountain, something vast and sorrowful and patient turned its attention toward the valley — toward the Door, toward Wren, toward the two small figures standing in the lane — and Hazel felt it the way you feel a storm front: in the teeth, in the old scars, in the animal brain that predates language.

"She knows," Wren whispered. Their hand slid from Hazel's sleeve to, finally, her hand. "Hazel. The queen knows we're here."
