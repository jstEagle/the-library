## 26. The Queen in Her Ruin

I had been a queen for eleven years before the erasure, and I have been a ruin for three hundred since, and I will tell you which of the two taught me more about arithmetic.

A queen learns sums early. How many bushels of barley against how many mouths. How many soldiers a harvest can feed before the harvest itself starves. How many truths a crown can afford to tell and how many it must bury in the cellar under the good wine. My tutors called this statecraft. My mother called it *counting what matters*. I was good at it. That was the whole tragedy of me: I was very, very good at counting.

And then one morning there were no more mouths to count, because there was no more country to count them in.

* * *

The echo-Thornfell keeps its own hours, which are no hours at all. The sun here sets at the same angle forever — that last evening, the evening we did not know was our last, when the light came slantwise through the orchards and everything smelled of woodsmoke and ripening apples. It has been setting for three hundred years. It has never finished setting. There is a lesson in that too, if you are patient enough to learn it, and I have had nothing but patience.

I felt the child coming the way a drowned man feels the shore.

It began as a wrongness in the loop. Old Marta, who has baked her loaves four hundred thousand times without once finishing them, paused at her window and looked east. The wedding party on the green — the bride who always reaches the end of her vows and begins again, sweet-faced, bewildered, reaching them again — went quiet mid-syllable. Even the river hesitated, and rivers in my country were never known for their hesitation.

Because somewhere past the Sorrow Bridge, past the collapsed stone and the guild-blood drying on it, a child was walking into Thornfell speaking the old tongue, and the old tongue is not a language. I want to be clear about that, because the Crown's scholars never understood it and their burning archives suggest they preferred not to. The old tongue is a *remembering*. It is the sound the country makes when it thinks about itself. And Wren — small, barefoot, storm-born Wren — speaks it the way other children breathe.

They had come home. Or they had come to the grave; from inside, the distinction is finer than you would think.

* * *

I dressed for the meeting, insofar as a remnant dresses.

My body is a matter of some negotiation these days. On strong days I am solid enough to cast a shadow, though the shadow is the wrong color — a deep thorn-purple, the purple of bruised dusk, and my people learned not to remark on it. On thin days I am mostly suggestion: the shape of a woman in a high-collared gown, the impression of a crown that was never crowned because the coronation was scheduled for the morning after the world ended. I keep the gown. Vanity, perhaps. Or perhaps only this: if I am to haunt my own country, I intend to haunt it *properly*, with posture.

I chose the hall of audience for our meeting, and then I laughed at myself — a dry, rusted sound, like a gate that has not been oiled since the erasure — and changed my mind. What was I going to do, receive a mud-streaked smuggler and a child of prophecy across a throne dais? I had done enough theater for one lifetime and three centuries of afterlife besides.

Instead I met them in the kitchen.

Old Marta's kitchen, in the governor's house by the square, where the loaves rise and rise and never brown. I sat on the worn bench by the hearth — my bench, once, on the evenings I escaped my counselors to eat bread and salt with the bakers, because a queen who cannot sit in her kitchens is a queen counting the wrong things — and I waited.

They came at the hour-that-is-not-an-hour, escorted by two of my echo-folk who had broken their loops to guide them, looking around themselves with the wide eyes of the living in a dead place. The woman first: Hazel Oyelaran, and I knew her name before she gave it, because the country whispers now, and it had been whispering about her for weeks. A smuggler's guide. A runaway apprentice. A girl who left a hedge-witch and a warm cottage to go count coins in the dark, and who had lately begun, visibly, painfully, to regret the arithmetic.

And behind her, holding her hand — that detail I noted, and treasured, and set aside to grieve over later — the child.

Wren looked at me and did not flinch.

Do you understand what that means? The living who enter Thornfell flinch. They flinch at the light that won't finish dying, at the baker who smiles with bread-ghosts on her hands, at the bride beginning her vows again in the square like a music box with a cracked tooth. But Wren looked at me — at the purple shadow, at the uncrowned crown, at all my three hundred years of careful haunting — and said, in the old tongue, the word for *hello*, which is also the word for *I see you*, which is also, in its deepest root, the word for *you are still real*.

I had not heard it in three hundred years.

I confess I forgot, briefly, every argument I had prepared.

* * *

"Sit," I said, in trade-tongue, because I would not make the child translate for my comfort. "Both of you. Marta—" and here the old baker turned, flour on her hands, her face doing something complicated that looped faces should not be able to do, "—Marta, love, could you manage tea?"

"She can't finish anything," Hazel said. Her voice was rough. Smuggler's voice, guard-post voice, but underneath it something rawer. "You told me that. The loaves never—"

"The loaves never finish," I agreed. "But she can pour tea. Pouring is a beginning. We have plenty of beginnings here." I smiled, and I felt my face do it properly, which surprised me. "That is rather the problem."

Marta poured. The tea was real — or as real as anything here, which is to say it tasted the way memory tastes, better than the thing itself ever did, and slightly of grief. Wren drank theirs with both hands around the cup, solemn as a magistrate. Hazel held hers and did not drink, which told me she was smarter than her reputation, or at least more frightened, which in my experience amounts to the same thing.

"You're the queen," Hazel said.

"I was never crowned." I said it lightly. Three hundred years of practice makes it light. "The ceremony was set for midsummer morning. The erasure came at midsummer night. The Crown's historians will tell you Thornfell rebelled, or trafficked with powers, or simply never existed — they can't agree, which is the point of the exercise. The truth is smaller and worse. We were inconvenient. We sat on a border someone wanted clean, and we spoke a language that made borders embarrassed of themselves, and so we were unwritten. Maps, records, gravestones, mothers' memories. All of it. All of us." I turned my cup. "I was mid-letter to my sister when it happened. I remember the ink lifting off the page. Do you know what it looks like, when words unwrite themselves? Like smoke deciding to go home."

Hazel's hand had gone, without her seeming to notice, to the child's shoulder. Protective. Instinctive. Interesting.

"And you stayed," Hazel said.

"Where would I go? I *am* the staying." I let that sit, then waved it away. "No. You didn't cross a collapsing bridge and spend your last coin and your last trick to hear an old ghost philosophize. You came because the child asked to come, and you came because — well. We'll get to why you came. Let me tell you what I want first, and I'll try to tell it honestly, because I find honesty is the only currency still worth anything in a ruined country."

I looked at Wren. Wren looked back, and in their eyes was the whole of the old tongue, patient as seed-time.

"When we were erased," I said, "we did not die. I want that understood. Death would have been a mercy, and the Crown was not feeling merciful. We were *folded*. Set aside, like a letter half-read. This place is the fold — the crease down the middle of a country that was taken out of the book. My people live their last day here, over and over, because it was the last day they had and no one told them they could stop. The bride never finishes her vows. Marta never finishes her loaves. And I—" I spread my hands, purple-shadowed, insubstantial at the edges, "—I never finish being queen of a country that officially does not exist."

"But the Door," Hazel said quietly. "The echoes can leave. It offered."

"It offered." Yes, I knew. I had stood at the threshold and watched the Door open like a wound learning kindness, and watched my people walk toward it, and I had felt the thing I have spent three centuries refusing to feel, which is hope, which is infinitely more painful than grief. "Some will go. Many. Perhaps most. And I will stand at that Door until the last of them passes through, and I will wave them out like a hostess seeing guests to their carriages, and I will mean every wave." I set down my cup. "And then I will turn around and look at the empty country behind me, and I will ask you the question I brought you here to answer."

The fire ticked. Somewhere far off, the bride reached the end of her vows and began again.

"Wren can undo it," I said. "Not partially. Not the way the child's little wonders have been undoing it — orchards blossoming, paths remembering themselves, borders sweating. Wholly. The old tongue at full strength, spoken at the Door, spoken *inward*: every map rewritten, every record restored, every grave given back its name, every mother's memory returned intact. Thornfell whole. Thornfell alive. Three hundred years folded up and shaken out, and my people waking in their beds the morning after midsummer as though the nightmare were only a nightmare." I heard my own voice climb and made myself level it. "I have wanted this longer than your grandmother's grandmother has been dead. I am not asking you to imagine wanting it. I am telling you it is the only thing left in me that isn't ash."

Hazel was very still. "At what price?"

"There it is." I almost smiled. "The smuggler's question. Everything has a price; you've built a life on knowing that. Good. Then let's do honest sums, you and I, because I have counted every cost of every choice for eleven years and three hundred, and I will not insult you with a bill hidden in the sleeve."

I told them.

To unfold Thornfell, the fold must be paid for. A country taken out of the book must be written back in, and writing requires ink, and the ink is *presence*. The old tongue does not create; it remembers, and remembering displaces. For Thornfell to stand whole and living again, the land must be taken back — the true land, the old borders, the passes and valleys and river-meadows that the Crown parceled out to settlers' grandchildren who have never heard the name Thornfell except as a curse or a lullaby. Those settlements stand on our fields. Those towns stand on our towns. And when the fold unfolds, what is folded cannot share the page.

"The borderlands," Hazel said. Her voice had gone flat, the flatness of someone reading a ledger they already dread. "How many?"

"Eleven thousand," I said. "Perhaps twelve. Farmers, mostly. A mill town. Two market villages. Children among them, yes — I will not soften the number, softening numbers is how the Crown erased *us*." I leaned forward. "And before you decide I'm a monster, let me show you the rest of the sum, because I promised honest arithmetic and I keep my books."

I showed them. In the old tongue, which Wren could follow and Hazel could only feel — I spoke, and the kitchen filled with what I have carried: the names. All of them. Every soul of Thornfell, forty thousand and more, each one a thread of light in the air between us, and the threads were singing, quietly, the way a house sings when everyone in it is asleep and safe. The miller's daughters. The choir. The man who repaired clocks and was late to everything including, in the end, his own erasure. Forty thousand souls folded into a crease, dreaming one evening forever.

"That is what the Crown spent," I said. "Forty thousand lives, unwritten, to tidy a border. No one called *that* murder. No one tried the accountants. No one even wrote it down — that was the entire art of it. So when you weigh my twelve thousand against their forty thousand, weigh also this: the world has already ruled that such spending is acceptable. It merely ruled against me. I am only proposing to move the decimal."

* * *

Hazel did not answer at once. I respected that. Quick answers are for people who haven't understood the question.

She looked at Wren instead, and Wren looked at the threads of light still hanging in the air, and reached up one small hand and touched them, and the threads sang louder, and I saw the child understand — really understand, in the bone-deep way of the old tongue — exactly what I was offering and exactly what it cost. Wren looked at me then with an expression I recognized, because I had worn it myself, at twenty, over ledgers of barley and soldiers.

Pity.

A child, pitying me. I found I minded it less than I should have.

"You loved them," Wren said, in trade-tongue now, carefully, the words new-built and precious, like a child carrying water in cupped hands. "All of them."

"Every one."

"And you'd pay twelve thousand strangers."

"They aren't strangers to me," I said. "That's the terrible part. I've read the census rolls of the borderlands — the Crown kept them in the same archive where they kept what remained of ours. I know their names too. I've had three hundred years with nothing to do but read." I closed my eyes. "Yes. I would pay them. And I would spend every year afterward standing in the market square of a resurrected country, carving twelve thousand names into a wall with my own hands, and I would not ask forgiveness, because I would not deserve it. I would only ask to be permitted to grieve them honestly, which is more than the Crown ever permitted anyone to grieve *us*."

The fire ticked. Marta stood by the oven with her unfinished loaves, watching me with flour-white hands and an expression I could not read, because her face had been looping too long to hold new expressions, and yet she was trying. She was trying to look at me the way she used to look at me across this kitchen, thirty years old and crownless and stealing bread.

"Do you know what I was going to say," Marta said suddenly, and her voice cracked the loop like ice cracking on a spring pond, "at midsummer? Before. I was going to tell you the second batch was for the coronation. I never got to tell you. It was going to be honey bread. Your favorite."

"Marta—"

"Honey bread," she repeated, stubbornly, wonderfully, a baker to the last and past the last. "So whatever you're selling these poor people, majesty, you sell it fair. That's all I'll say. Honey bread." And she turned back to her oven, and the loop took her gently by the shoulders and set her kneading, and I sat in my ruin and understood that I had just been scolded by the only subject whose opinion had ever mattered.

* * *

"So," I said to Hazel, when I could speak steadily again. "There is my case. Whole. No sleeves, no hidden clauses. Resurrection for a ransom. The oldest bargain in the world, and I am old enough to offer it without blushing." I rose, and my purple shadow rose with me, and I saw Hazel measure it — the distance to the door, the weight of the child's hand, the odds. A smuggler to the marrow. I liked her for it, God help me. "You needn't answer tonight. Walk my country first. See the wedding that never ends, the baker who never finishes, the evening that has been setting for three hundred years. See what erasure actually *is*, from the inside. Then tell me whether twelve thousand living strangers outweigh forty thousand sleeping dead — and tell me, too, whether you believe any ledger written by conquerors deserves to be the final word."

Hazel stood. She had not drunk her tea. She took Wren's hand.

"And if I refuse?" she said.

Ah. There it was. The question under all questions, the one she'd carried in since the bridge, since Lowmark, maybe since a cottage door she'd walked out of at fifteen with her pride and nothing else.

"Then I will be your enemy," I said, and made it gentle, because it was true and truth deserves gentleness. "Not tonight, and not gladly. I will grieve you while I hunt you, Hazel Oyelaran, and I will carve your name on the wall too, with the others, and write beside it: *she was kind, and she was wrong.*" I inclined my head, uncrowned, unhurried. "But you should know one more thing before you sleep under my roof tonight. While we've talked, my remnant has been moving. The ones who broke their loops for you — they were the merciful ones. The others are coming down out of the high ruins, and they do not pity, and they do not pause, and they have been waiting three hundred years for someone to open this country's front door."

Outside, far off in the eternal evening, the church bell began to ring.

It had never rung before.
