## 28. Flight Through the Echo

Hazel had been hunted before. She'd been chased out of three baronies, one duchy, and a wedding — the last one her own fault, involving a borrowed horse and a groom who turned out to be a tax collector's son. She knew how pursuit worked. Pursuit was arithmetic: your legs against theirs, your knowledge of the ground against theirs, your luck against everyone's.

But nothing in her arithmetic covered being chased by people who were already dead, through a country that kept replaying its own last evening like a song stuck in a music box.

"Left," Wren said, tugging her sleeve.

She went left. Behind them, across a wheat field that rippled with no wind, the queen's dead came on — not running, never running, just walking with that terrible patient certainty of people who know the world will eventually do what they want. There were perhaps thirty of them. They wore the clothes of Thornfell's last day: a shepherd's coat, a scholar's gown, an apron dusted with flour. Their faces were kind. That was the worst part. They looked like neighbors coming to borrow a cup of sugar, if neighbors could walk through hedges without disturbing a leaf.

"They're not angry," Wren said. It wasn't quite trade-tongue yet; it was the shape of trade-tongue poured into the old speech, and Hazel understood it the way you understand a melody hummed by someone else. "They're sad. Sad is worse."

"Sad I can work with," Hazel said, breathless. "Sad can be lied to. Where are we going?"

Wren pointed. Not at anything — at a direction, the way you'd point at home from a hilltop. Toward the Door, presumably. Toward the thin place where a country that didn't exist touched a world that insisted on existing anyway.

The wheat field ended at a lane, and the lane ran between hedgerows heavy with late blackberries, and everything was lit in the amber of an evening that had been ending for three hundred years. Hazel had grown up on Rosalind's stories about load-bearing names, and she'd spent fifteen years proving them nonsense, and now she was fleeing through a memory the size of a nation and she still couldn't quite make herself say *I was wrong* out loud. She filed it under things to do later, assuming there was a later.

"Hazel." Wren had stopped. "Listen."

She listened. Under the crickets — crickets, in a dead country, because even echoes kept their small comforts — she heard bells. A village bell, ringing the hour. And under the bells, voices. Singing.

"The wedding," Wren said. "The one that always ends."

* * *

They found the wedding by following lantern light down a lane that bent wrong, the way lanes did here when they felt like it. The village square was strung with bunting gone soft-edged at the seams, the way everything here was soft-edged, as though the whole country had been washed too many times. A fiddler played on the steps of a well. Dancers turned in slow circles. The bride wore a crown of meadowsweet and laughed at something the groom said, and Hazel watched her laugh and thought: *she has laughed at that exact thing four thousand times, and every time it is new to her.*

The dancers saw them. The music didn't stop — it never stopped, Hazel understood that now — but the dancers slowed, and turned, and looked.

One of them, an old woman with a shawl the color of rust, stepped out of the circle. She looked at Wren for a long moment, and then she knelt, which made Hazel's hand go to her knife until she realized the old woman was kneeling the way you kneel to a child, to be at eye level.

"You're one of the speaking ones," the old woman said. Her voice had the same doubled quality as Wren's — two languages braided. "We wondered if any were left outside."

"She's being hunted," Hazel said. "By the queen's — by the walking ones. We need a way through."

The old woman looked at Hazel then, and her gaze was not unkind but it was thorough, the way Rosalind's used to be, taking inventory of a person's whole history in one pass. "You're the living one," she said. "The guide. We've heard you described. You sold the child once."

"I bought her back," Hazel said.

"Mm," said the old woman, in the tone of a woman filing something away for later, and Hazel recognized that tone too, because it was her own.

Behind them, up the lane, the walking dead appeared — thirty figures in last-day clothes, moving with their terrible patience. The dancers didn't scream. They simply stopped dancing, all at once, like puppets whose strings had been gathered in one hand, and turned to watch them come.

"They'll ask us to step aside," the old woman said. "They always ask. It's politer than it sounds. And then we will step aside, because we always have, because that is what our evening is. We dance, the soldiers come, we step aside, the bell rings, we begin again." She smiled, and it was a real smile, tired around the edges. "It has been a very long evening, child. Do you know how long?"

"Three hundred years?" Hazel guessed.

"Longer," said the old woman. "Every time is longer than the last time. Time stretches, in a loop. Like bread dough. You knead it and knead it and it gets thinner and thinner, and eventually—" she spread her hands, "—eventually you begin to wonder what would happen if you just *stopped*. If you let the loaf be lumpy."

The lead walker reached the edge of the square. He was the shepherd Hazel had seen in the field, and up close his kindness was unbearable, the way a portrait's eyes follow you. He inclined his head to the old woman.

"Sister Maren," he said. "The child must come with us. The queen asks it."

"The queen asks," Maren repeated. She looked at Wren. She looked at the sky, which was doing its eternal trick of being sunset forever without ever committing to dark. Then she did something Hazel had never seen anyone do in this country, or any country.

She sat down. Right there, in the middle of the square, on the cobbles, gathering her skirts, like a woman deciding she'd been standing long enough.

"No," said Sister Maren. "I don't think I will."

* * *

For a heartbeat nothing happened, because nothing had ever happened here. The loop had no instructions for refusal. The fiddler's bow hung mid-stroke. The bride's laugh died unlaughed, which seemed to pain her more than anything else could have.

Then the shepherd frowned — actually frowned, the expression arriving on his face like weather from a foreign coast — and said, "Sister, you must. It is the evening. It has always been the evening."

"And I have always stepped aside," Maren agreed. "And my knees hurt, and my feet hurt, and I have watched that girl marry that boy eleven thousand times, lovely as it is, and I am *tired*, Tam. I am tired in a way that has nothing to do with dying, because I already did that part." She patted the cobblestones beside her. "Sit down."

"I cannot—"

"You can. I just did. Watch — it's easy." She demonstrated again, extravagantly, settling her shawl.

And Tam the shepherd, who had walked through hedges and across rivers and over the graves of his own grandchildren without disturbing so much as a dewdrop, looked at the empty cobbles beside a stubborn dead woman, and his face did something complicated, and he sat down.

Hazel grabbed Wren's hand. "Now," she breathed. "While they're — whatever this is."

But Wren didn't move. Wren was watching the square with wide eyes, and tears running freely down their face, and when Hazel pulled, Wren pulled back.

"No," Wren said. "Look."

Hazel looked. Down the lane, the other walkers were stopping, one by one, like a river encountering stones. A woman in an apron set down a basket she had apparently been carrying toward somewhere for three centuries. A soldier — one of Thornfell's own last soldiers, armor gone brown as autumn — leaned his spear against a wall and rubbed his shoulder, an ordinary gesture, an impossible gesture, because nothing here had ever ached before. Aching meant time passing. Aching meant change.

"They're choosing," Wren whispered. "Do you understand? Nobody ever chooses here. The evening just... happens to us. Over and over. But she sat down first, and now they're all—" Wren's voice cracked. "All this time I thought the country was asleep. It isn't asleep. It's *waiting*. For someone to go first."

The baker emerged from his shop, flour to his elbows, carrying loaves he would never finish baking, and instead of returning to his oven he handed them out — to the dancers, to the walkers, to Hazel and Wren, pressing warm bread into hands. "They're going stale otherwise," he said, bewildered and delighted, like a man discovering a new color. "Eleven thousand evenings and I never once thought to just give them away."

The bride lifted her crown of meadowsweet off her own head and put it on Wren's. "You're going somewhere," she said. It wasn't a question. "Take the flowers. They only exist tonight, and tonight seems to be — hm." She glanced at the sky, frowning at it the way you'd frown at a clock. "Tonight seems to be ending."

And it was. Hazel felt it in her teeth, in the soles of her feet: the amber light deepening, the crickets faltering, the whole preserved evening beginning, at long last, to do the thing evenings do. Somewhere overhead the first true star of three hundred years pricked through.

"It's the Door," Wren said suddenly, gripping Hazel's arm hard enough to bruise. "When I open it — when the country lets go — everything here goes with it. Or goes *through* it. I don't know which. I didn't know it would feel like this. I didn't know they'd be *awake*."

"Hey." Hazel crouched to eye level, the way Maren had. "One catastrophe at a time. Right now the catastrophe is those." She nodded up the lane, past the sitting walkers, where the square's mercy ran out: more figures on the ridge, and these were not sitting down. These moved with purpose, and at their center burned a cold silver light that Hazel recognized from nightmares she hadn't known she was having.

The queen's remnant. The part of the queen that grief had sharpened instead of softened.

Maren struggled upright, took Wren's face in her hands. "The back way," she said rapidly. "Through the tithe barn, along the millrace — the paths here forget themselves, but I never do, I've walked it every evening since the end. Go now. And child—" she caught Wren's sleeve, "—when you reach the Door, whatever you choose, choose it *for us too*. Not just for yourself. We'd rather be finished than be kept. Remember that some of us would rather be finished."

"I'll remember," Wren said, in the old tongue, and the words landed on the square like rain on dry ground, and every dead soul in it shivered — with pleasure, Hazel thought, or grief, or both, the way you shiver at a piece of music.

Then they ran, through the tithe barn, along the millrace, blackberry stains on their fingers and warm bread in Hazel's pocket and a crown of meadowsweet on a child's head, while behind them a whole country practiced, for the first time in three centuries, the radical act of doing something different.

* * *

The millrace path climbed. The false sunset finally, grudgingly, gave way to a night with actual stars in it, and Hazel found she wanted to cry and didn't have time, and filed that under later too. The *later* pile was getting tall.

Wren stumbled. Hazel caught them.

"Tired?" she asked, and then felt stupid — of course tired, the child had been running since Lowmark, since before Lowmark, since the beginning of everything.

"Not tired." Wren leaned against her, small and burning-hot, the way a lamp is hot. "Full. All those words tonight. All those choices. I'm carrying them." A pause. "Hazel. When we get to the Door. Will you still be there after I go through?"

Hazel looked at the path ahead, which climbed toward a pass she could not see and could no longer trust her maps to describe, and answered honestly, which was still a skill she was learning: "I don't know yet. But I'll be there *at* it. That I promise."

"That's enough," Wren said, and yawned, and kept walking, and Hazel carried the weight of that sentence the rest of the climb.

They came over the shoulder of the hill and stopped.

Below them, in a bowl of mountain dark, stood the Door. Even from here Hazel could see it was wrong — smaller than legend, older than stone, a threshold standing free with neither wall nor house, and around its frame the air shimmered like heat above a road. But between them and it, campfires. Dozens. The guild's green-lit lanterns. The Crown's white-and-gold banners. Vane's vanguard, arrived ahead of them somehow, or waiting all along.

And above the fires, on the threshold stone itself, a figure in a half-remembered garden-dirt-stained dress, leaning on a hedge-witch's staff, holding a ward-line against the dark with both hands.

"Oh," Hazel said, her heart doing something her arithmetic had no column for. "Oh, you *impossible* woman."

Wren put the meadowsweet crown straighter and took her hand.

"Come on, then," Hazel said. "Let's not keep her waiting."
