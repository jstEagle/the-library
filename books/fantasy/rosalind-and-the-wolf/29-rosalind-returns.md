## 29. Rosalind Returns

The road had forgotten itself three miles back.

Rosalind felt it the way you feel a word sitting on the tip of your tongue — a shape where something used to be, and nothing in the shape. She had walked this pass as a young woman, before Hazel was born, before any of it: the shepherd's cut above Hollowmere, the switchback that locals called the Cat's Stair because it went up sideways and complained the whole way. She knew it like she knew her own kitchen. And now her boots told her there was grass where the Stair should climb, and her memory insisted there was stone, and between the two of them she was left walking on faith and a hedge-witch's stubbornness, which had carried her through worse.

Half of her memory was gone. She had spent it, the way you spend coin you'd rather keep, buying Hazel and the child their escape from Vane's raiders. She remembered the garden burning — not burning, *unmaking*, row by row, the chamomile going first, then the bee-skeps, then — she pressed at the gap and it pressed back, smooth and blank as an egg. Whatever she had fed into that working, she no longer owned it. Her own grimoire had pages she couldn't read anymore. Her handwriting, and no idea what she'd said.

What remained was enough. It had to be enough.

She remembered Hazel. That was the strange arithmetic of it — she had lost whole seasons, recipes, the name of the neighbor two valleys over who owed her a ladder — but Hazel had survived intact, every sharp edge of her, every kindness buried under the sharp edges like a knife wrapped in a knife. Perhaps because Hazel was the wound. You don't lose the wound. The wound is load-bearing.

*Names are load-bearing,* she had taught her, once, in the kitchen, over bread that Hazel had salted wrong out of pure contrariness. *Say a thing true and it holds. Say it false and everything built on it sags.*

Hazel had called it superstition and left anyway, and Rosalind had let her go, because you cannot chain a person to being understood. But she had written. Forty-one letters. She'd counted them on the walk up, the way you count stitches to keep your hands steady. Forty-one letters, thirty-nine unopened when last she heard, and now here she was, an old woman climbing a mountain that was forgetting how to be a mountain, to say in person what the post had failed to carry:

*I'm not angry. I was never angry. I was frightened, and I let the fright do the talking for eleven years, and I would like my girl back, please, if she's still taking visitors.*

Also there was the small matter of the Door at the top of the world closing forever, and a child who was its key, and an inquisitor with four hundred soldiers somewhere behind her. Small matters. Hedge-witches live on small matters. A poultice here, a charm there, and occasionally the salvation of a nation nobody remembers.

* * *

She found the first soldier at dusk, or he found her boot, tripping over it in the dark and swearing with the particular eloquence of a man who has marched all day.

"Sorry," Rosalind said pleasantly, and put him to sleep before he finished standing up. Not a killing sleep. She'd done enough spending this week. He folded into the bracken like laundry.

His comrades were camped in the hollow below — a vanguard, maybe sixty strong, torches banked low, and among them she saw the grey-and-white of Crown livery and the black of Vane's office both. They were bivouacked at the mouth of the last pass, which meant they were waiting. Which meant Hazel and Wren were close. Which meant Rosalind had perhaps an hour to be clever, and her cleverness these days came with gaps in it, like a book with pages torn out by a child who liked the pictures.

She sat down on a rock, took out her flask of nettle tea, and thought.

Sixty soldiers. One witch with half a memory and a bad knee. The mathematics were not encouraging, but then, Rosalind had never been much for mathematics. Mathematics said a hedge-witch could not raise a ward-line across a pass. Mathematics said you could not fight an empire with nettles and nerve. Mathematics had also said, once, that Thornfell could simply be erased, and look how well that was going for everyone.

What she needed was not strength. What she needed was *attention*. Soldiers are honest creatures, bless them; they look where they're pointed. So she would point them.

She began to work, and the work hurt, because she was drawing again on the account she'd already overdrawn. Every working since the garden had cost her something she couldn't name afterward. This one, she suspected, would take the smell of rain — she could feel it loosening even as she cast, that green petrichor sense she'd had since girlhood, draining out through her palms into the pass. Fair trade. She could learn weather from her skin like everyone else.

Above the vanguard, the mountainside began to glow. Not fire — nothing so crude. It was foxfire light, cold and blue-green, and it moved in shapes: great slow sigils turning in the air like the lights some folk swore they saw over deep water. And beneath it, faint but unmistakable, a sound — singing, many-voiced, in no language any living kingdom spoke.

The camp erupted. Torches up, horns blowing, sixty men pointing their attention exactly where she wanted it: up and away from the narrow goat-track on the far side of the hollow, where a woman and a child might just possibly be trying to slip past toward a Door.

Rosalind watched them scramble, sipped her tea, and permitted herself one small, wicked smile. Illusion was the humblest of the arts. Anyone can make a thing appear. It takes real craft to make sixty armed men believe in nothing whatsoever.

Then a horn sounded from the goat-track side, and her smile died.

They hadn't sent everyone up the mountain. Of course they hadn't. Vane wasn't a fool; Vane was many terrible things, but never that. A dozen soldiers had held the track, and now they were moving, and through the dark came a sound Rosalind knew in her bones though she hadn't heard it in eleven years: Hazel's voice, raised not in fear but in that flat, fast smuggler's cadence, buying time with words the way other people bought it with steel.

Rosalind put down her tea.

"Well," she said to the night, to the mountain, to whatever was left of the woman who'd taught names to hold things together. "Late is late. But late is not never."

She went down the hill at a pace her knee would bill her for tomorrow.

* * *

Hazel had her back to a boulder and a knife in each hand, and she was losing beautifully.

That was the thing about her girl — she fought the way she haggled, giving ground only at a profit, keeping the enemy talking, always angling toward the exit. Two soldiers down already, disarmed and groaning, not dead — Hazel didn't kill unless she had to, another inheritance she'd deny to her grave. But twelve was too many for knives, and the soldiers had learned, spreading wide, shields up, content to wait. Waiting was the one weapon Hazel couldn't haggle with.

And behind her, pressed into the rock's shadow, stood Wren — small, barefoot as ever, watching the soldiers with that terrible calm children have when they have seen too much to be frightened of the ordinary kind of danger.

"Hazel Oyelaran!" the sergeant was shouting. "Inquisitor's orders! Stand down and the child comes to no harm!"

"He means it, you know," Hazel called back, conversational, circling. "He genuinely believes that. That's the worst part."

"You're surrounded—"

"I'm *adjacent*," Hazel said. "There's a difference. Ask anyone I've ever sold anything to."

It was, Rosalind thought, descending on them out of the dark like a moderately wrathful grandmother, the most ridiculous conversation she had ever heard conducted under threat of death, and she loved her beyond all measure.

She hit the ward-line mid-stride — hers, thrown up in the last second, a lattice of old speech older than the Crown, older than Vane's office, older than the lie that had eaten a country. It rang out across the hollow like a bell made of winter. Every soldier within it stopped as if the air had turned to honey. Not frozen — slowed, mired, their own shadows suddenly heavy as chains.

Then she stepped through the gap she'd left herself, and stood beside Hazel, back to boulder, shoulder nearly touching shoulder.

"Hello, love," she said. "You're out of flour. Also you're surrounded. I've fixed the flour problem; the other one we'll do together."

For one heartbeat Hazel stared at her, and Rosalind watched eleven years cross her face — the leaving, the letters, the garden, all of it — and watched her do what Hazel always did with feelings too large for the moment: she shelved them.

"You're supposed to be half a country away," Hazel said.

"I'm half a *memory* away. Different thing entirely." Rosalind flexed her fingers. "Twelve of them?"

"Twelve. Maybe fourteen. I stopped counting when it stopped being flattering."

"Mm. Take the left. I'm better on the left since—" she gestured vaguely at her own head, "—the renovation."

"What did you lose?"

"Ask me later. If there's a later. Left, Hazel."

And Hazel — miracle of miracles, grace of graces — did not argue. She just moved, knives out, and Rosalind moved with her, and for the first time in eleven years they fought as they had once cooked: without discussion, without apology, each knowing where the other would be.

It was not elegant. Rosalind was seventy-one and fighting with borrowed time; Hazel was fighting with a bruised rib she thought Rosalind hadn't noticed (she had; mothers notice). But it was *together*, and together was worth ten of elegance. When a shield-boss swung at Hazel's blind side, Rosalind's ward caught it with a sound like a struck fence-wire. When two soldiers rushed Rosalind's flank, Hazel was simply *there*, low and fast, taking the knees out from under them with a smuggler's lack of ceremony. Back to back, literally, shoulders touching, turning as one wheel.

Wren, meanwhile, had climbed onto the boulder, and was doing something Rosalind felt before she understood.

The child was speaking to the soldiers' weapons.

Not commanding. Never commanding — Rosalind had learned that much in the cottage, in those few fierce weeks of study, three teachers bent over one table. The old tongue didn't command; it *addressed*. And Wren was addressing the swords and shields and spear-butts in the language of their making, saying — Rosalind caught fragments, and the fragments were courteous, absurdly courteous — *you were iron once, and you were ore before that, and before that you were the mountain's own bone, and none of that was war.*

One by one, weapons sagged. Blades drooped like tired flowers. A spearhead quietly became, in the sergeant's horrified grip, a very nice piece of ornamental ironwork, suitable for a gate.

"Oh, that's *cheating*," Hazel said, delighted.

"It's *polite*," Rosalind corrected. "There's a difference. Ask anyone you've ever sold anything to."

Hazel laughed — actually laughed, in the middle of it, bright and startled, the laugh of a girl of fifteen who'd salted the bread wrong on purpose — and Rosalind felt something in her chest come unstitched that had been sewn shut for eleven years.

The soldiers broke. There is only so long men will fight when their weapons are apologizing to them. They fell back up the track in good order, dragging their drooping arms, and the hollow was theirs, and the night was quiet except for Wren, still on the boulder, murmuring thanks to the stones.

* * *

Afterward there was blood to see to — mostly theirs, a little of Hazel's — and a fire to build small and shielded, and tea, because Rosalind traveled with tea the way armies travel with banners.

Hazel stitched the gash on Rosalind's forearm with smuggler's thread and smuggler's hands, quick and rough and gentler than she'd ever admit. Neither of them spoke for a while. The fire crackled. Somewhere above, the false lights of her illusion still wheeled, keeping the main body of the vanguard busy chasing ghosts.

"The letters," Hazel said finally. Not looking up from the stitching.

"Forty-one."

"I read them. All of them. In a ditch outside Sorrow Bridge, of all the—" She exhaled. "You knew about children like Wren. You knew and you wrote to me and I—"

"Yes."

"That's it? *Yes?*"

"Hazel." Rosalind waited until the girl — the woman, thirty years old and still somehow her girl — looked up. "I have lost the smell of rain, the recipe for elderflower wine, and the face of a man I once intended to marry. I am not spending what's left on a quarrel. If you want to apologize, apologize by staying alive until morning. That's the whole of the fee."

Hazel's jaw worked. Her eyes were wet and furious about being wet. "You're impossible."

"I'm your teacher. Same thing." Rosalind nodded at her arm. "Tie that off. And then come sit properly; my left side is cold and I've decided that's your fault."

Hazel tied it off. And then — stiffly, the way a cat returns to a house it swears it abandoned — she came and sat, shoulder against shoulder, exactly where she'd fought, and Wren wriggled into the gap between them as if the space had been measured for a child all along, and the fire burned small and gold, and no one said anything else for a long time, because nothing needed saying. Reconciliation, Rosalind reflected, was mostly carpentry. You didn't announce the joint. You just fitted the pieces and bore the weight together until the wood forgot it had ever been separate.

"Wren says the Door is a day's walk," Hazel said at last, voice low. "Maybe less. It's closing, Rosalind. Closing for good. And Vane's got four hundred men between us and it, minus the dozen whose swords are currently garden ornaments."

"Then we'd best start early," Rosalind said, "and walk rudely."

Wren looked up at her then, and said something in the old tongue — soft, questioning. Rosalind caught perhaps half of it, and the half she caught made her go still.

"What?" Hazel said. "What is it?"

"She asked," Rosalind said slowly, "whether I'm the keeper now. Whether I've come to stay." She looked up the pass, toward the place where a country that had never existed kept its one remaining door. "And I find I don't know the answer. But I know I'm not leaving the two of you to knock alone."

High above, on the ridge, torches had begun to move — not the vanguard's. More of them. Many more, coming down from the heights in a long disciplined line, and at their head, unmistakable even at this distance, walked a figure in black who did not carry a torch, because Inquisitor Vane had never in his life needed light to see what he believed.

"There's our rude morning," Hazel said, and reached for her knives.

Rosalind reached instead for Wren's hand, and stood.
