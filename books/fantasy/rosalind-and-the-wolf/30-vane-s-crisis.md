## 30. Vane's Crisis

Inquisitor Edric Vane had burned forty-one archives in his career, and he had never once enjoyed it.

This was a fact he kept strictly to himself, the way other men kept mistresses or gambling debts. His subordinates believed—were meant to believe—that erasure came to him as easily as breathing, that he stood in the smoke of burning parish records and felt nothing but satisfaction at a job done cleanly. The truth was that he had trained himself not to enjoy it, and the training had taken eleven years, and some mornings it still required effort.

Order was kindness. That was the creed. He had watched what happened when every village fancied its own saints, its own histories, its own little grievances dressed up as holy truth: feuds, heresies, wars over nothing. A country that remembered everything bled. He had seen the ledgers. He had *made* the ledgers.

But standing now inside Thornfell—not Thornfell the rumor, not Thornfell the cautionary footnote in the Crown histories, but the actual country, whole and lit by an evening sun that had set three hundred years ago—Edric Vane found his creed developing a crack down the middle like a dropped bell.

It was the bread that did it.

* * *

They had crossed the threshold stone at dusk, thirty-one soldiers in Crown grey, boots striking cobbles that should have been dust. The echo-country had received them without alarm. That was the first wrong thing. Vane had expected resistance, wailing ghosts, the land itself rising up. Instead there had been lamplight. Music from somewhere uphill. A smell of woodsmoke and roasting meat that made two of his younger men stop dead in the road and say, quietly, "That's my grandmother's recipe," before catching themselves.

Vane had ordered them forward. They had gone. But they had gone *slowly*, the way men walk toward something they'd rather look at.

Now he stood in the market square of a town whose name he knew—he knew all the names; knowing names was his profession—and watched a baker pull loaves from an oven for the thousandth time, perhaps the ten-thousandth time, and set them out, and begin again.

The baker was not a ghost. That was the second wrong thing. Ghosts were pale, translucent, given to moaning. This man was ruddy and solid and had flour on his forearms and a smudge of it on his nose, and when Vane approached him with six soldiers at his back—the way one approaches anyone, professionally—he looked up with the mild irritation of a working man interrupted.

"You'll want to wait," the baker said. "They're better cooled. My wife insists, and she's been right about bread for forty years, which is more than I can say for most things."

"Forty years," Vane repeated.

The baker beamed, entirely untroubled. "Give or take."

"Give or take *what?*"

The baker considered this with genuine care, as though it were a question of philosophy rather than arithmetic. "Well," he said at last, "take, mostly." And he handed Vane a heel of bread—handed it to him, pressed it into his hand with the unselfconscious generosity of a man who has fed strangers ten thousand times—and turned back to his oven, because there was always another batch.

Vane stood holding the bread.

He did not eat it. He would tell himself later that this was discipline, and it was partly true. But mostly it was because eating it would make it real, and if it was real—if the bread was real, and the flour on the man's arms was real, and the wife who had been right about bread for forty years was real—then the ledger in his saddlebag, the one that listed the population of Thornfell under a single column headed ASSETS REMOVED, was also real, and every number in it had flour on its hands.

He put the bread in his coat pocket instead, which was somehow worse.

* * *

The wedding was happening again.

He heard it before he saw it—fiddles, laughter, someone singing badly and joyfully—and rounded a corner into a lane strung with ribbons, where guests spilled from doorways and children chased each other between the legs of dancing adults. A bride in yellow stood on a chair making a toast, and Vane stopped, because he had read the account of this wedding. It was in the last archive he'd ever burned, a parish record from the final autumn of Thornfell, and the entry had ended mid-sentence, because the archivist writing it had been interrupted, and the interruption had been Vane's office, three hundred years later, arriving with oil and torches.

"—and so I said to him, Bram, Bram, you great fool, you can't propose to a woman while she's elbow-deep in a sheep—" The bride paused, frowned slightly, as though hearing an echo of herself, and started the toast again. "—and so I said to him, Bram, Bram, you great fool—"

"It loops," said Sergeant Calla Mennick, beside him. She had come up quietly. She was his best soldier and the only one who ever asked questions she shouldn't, which was why he valued her and why he had twice passed her over for promotion. "I've been watching twenty minutes. Same toast, same joke, same sheep. Every few minutes. They don't know."

"They're echoes," Vane said. "Residues. Not people."

"Yes, sir." She didn't move. In the firelight a small girl ran past them both, laughing, trailing ribbon, and Sergeant Mennick turned to watch her go with an expression Vane recognized, because he wore it himself sometimes in the dark, before he put it away where it couldn't be promoted either.

"That child," Mennick said, "was laughing at me. At *me*, sir. She hid behind your leg when I walked past. Residues don't hide behind legs."

"Standards, Sergeant."

"Sir." She saluted. It was immaculate. Everything about her was immaculate except her eyes, which were doing something mutinous and grieving at the same time. Then she said, very quietly, in a tone he had only ever heard from men about to do something unforgivable: "My grandmother came from a border village. Near Hollowmere. When I was small she used to sing songs nobody else knew, and when I asked where they were from she'd look at the window first. Always the window first. Like checking whether it was safe to remember." She straightened. "Permission to continue the sweep, sir."

"Granted."

She went. Vane stood in the wedding light with his hand in his pocket, curled around a heel of bread going slowly stale, and understood that he had just lost her, wherever she marched, whatever she reported. Some cracks you could mortar over. Some you could only stand next to and pretend were load-bearing.

He knew a great deal about pretending that. He had built a career on it.

* * *

By full dark he had assembled his officers in the square, and the count was bad. Thirty-one had crossed the threshold. Twenty-two stood before him now. Of the missing nine, four had simply vanished into the lanes—deserted, presumably, swallowed by a country that offered bread and music and no orders at all. Two he had found sitting in a churchyard, talking softly with a sexton who had died before their grandfathers were born, and he had ordered them back to formation and they had obeyed, but their faces had the look of men returning from a place they intended to revisit. Three more—

Three more were Corporal Hesk's problem.

Hesk found him after midnight, at the edge of the square, where Vane stood alone watching the baker's windows go dark—or appear to go dark; the evening here never actually ended, it merely pretended to, courteously, the way a host pretends not to notice when a guest has stopped drinking.

"Inquisitor." Hesk's jaw worked. He was nineteen, the youngest man in the column, recruited from a workhouse orphanage that Vane's own office had funded, which Vane tried not to think about, and failed. "Sergeant Mennick says—I'm to tell you—there's a group forming. Going to the Door. To help the witch and the child. Mennick's with them, and Ossler, and Tam, and—" He swallowed. "And they asked me to ask you not to shoot."

"Not to *shoot.*"

"To let them go, sir. Or—" The boy's voice cracked and steadied itself with visible effort. "Or to come too. They said—Mennick said you'd want the option stated plainly, sir, because you were always fair about options."

Vane looked at him for a long moment. Nineteen years old, offering treason with a courtesy that could only have been learned from Mennick, who had learned it from watching him.

"Corporal," Vane said finally, "do you know what I did to the smuggler who knew our route?"

"No, sir."

"Neither does anyone else now. That was the point." He let the silence sit. "You are asking me to state an option plainly, so I will: if you join them, you are a traitor, and the sentence is death, and I will carry it out personally if we meet again on the wrong side of a drawn line. Do you understand?"

"Yes, sir." Hesk didn't run. That was the unbearable part. He stood there trembling and didn't run, and then he said, "Sir—may I say one thing? Not arguing. Just—one thing."

"Say it."

"The baker gave you bread, sir. I saw. You've still got it." The boy nodded at Vane's pocket, where the heel sat like a stone, like evidence, like a verdict deferred. "Nobody's thrown anything away in three hundred years here, sir. Not even us. We got given bread too, coming through. Everybody gets given bread." His voice went very young. "Sir, what kind of error has manners?"

Then he went, at a walk, because even deserting, he wouldn't run from Vane, and Vane stood in the false dark of a country that had been erased for being inconvenient and discovered that his hands were shaking.

He took out the bread.

He ate it.

It was good. It was warm still, impossibly, after hours, warm as the moment it was given, and it tasted of nothing he could name except the specific flavor of being wrong in a way that could no longer be filed, cross-referenced, or burned. Order was kindness. He said it to himself the way a man says a prayer he has stopped believing in but cannot yet put down, because putting it down means admitting what his hands have been full of all along.

* * *

Dawn—which was to say, the evening again, gently restarting, the lamps relit, the baker already elbow-deep in dough—found Vane walking toward the Door with the twelve soldiers who remained.

Twelve. Out of thirty-one. He performed the arithmetic the way he performed all arithmetic, cleanly and without flinching, and the number told him exactly what it told any competent officer: that he had lost this country the moment he entered it, not to force but to hospitality, which no drill could counter and no regulation addressed. There was no manual section for *the enemy baked for us.*

He would hold the Door anyway. Not because the mission was right—he was no longer certain, and the uncertainty sat in him like a swallowed coal—but because certainty was the last thing he owned that still worked, and a man who abandons his last working tool in enemy country does not get to call himself anything but lost.

Besides. There was a child at the end of that road who spoke a language older than kingdoms, and kingdoms were all Vane had ever served, and if the language was older than the kingdom then the kingdom had been lying to everyone including him, and he needed—to see. To see it with his own eyes. To stand across the threshold stone from a mute child and a runaway guide and a half-ruined hedge-witch and hear, from the source, whether mercy really could open doors that armies couldn't.

Ahead, through the last of the lanes, he could see the Door: a pale seam of light in the mountain's flank, figures gathered before it. He raised his hand and the twelve halted behind him, and he noticed—as one notices the thing that will define everything after—that his soldiers had formed up around him without being asked, but loosely, facing outward, as if protecting him from the country rather than helping him take it.

And then the wind changed, and brought him a sound from the threshold: a child's voice, high and clear, speaking a word he did not know, in a tongue that made the stones themselves lean in to listen.

Vane's hand went to the warrant in his breast pocket—the old warrant, the erasure order, the paper he had carried through forty-one burnings—and found, beneath it, a crumb of bread he had missed, and could not bring himself to brush away.

"Forward," he said, and his voice held, because it always held; that was the tragedy of him, that it always held.

Twelve soldiers moved toward the Door. Behind them, in the lanes of a country three hundred years dead and baking still, the evening began again, and the fiddles struck up, and somewhere a bride began a toast about a great fool and a sheep, and no one—no one at all—threw anything away.
