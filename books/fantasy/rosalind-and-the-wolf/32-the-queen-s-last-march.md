## 32. The Queen's Last March

The dead of Thornfell did not sleep, exactly. They *rehearsed*.

Marenne had learned this in the first century of her haunting, when she caught the baker's ghost kneading dough at midnight in a kitchen that had burned down before his grandchildren were born — not because he was compelled to, but because tomorrow's loaves mattered to him, and tomorrow was the only day he would ever be given. The whole country rehearsed its last evening the way an actor rehearses a role: with love, with precision, and with the quiet terror that one night the curtain simply would not rise again.

She stood now on the balcony of a palace that existed only because everyone who remembered it agreed it should, and watched her people prepare for an evening they had performed eleven thousand times.

Below, in the square, the wedding party was assembling. The bride — Sefa, a weaver's daughter who had died at nineteen and would be nineteen forever — was being pinned into her dress by her mother, who had died three years after her and had spent every looping evening since re-pinning the same hem. The groom waited by the fountain with the nervous dignity of a man who has proposed eleven thousand times and been accepted eleven thousand times and still flushed each time as though it were the first.

"Your Majesty." Old Corin, her chamberlain, materialized at her shoulder with the particular silence of a man who had been dead so long he'd forgotten how to make noise doing it. "The scouts report movement at the threshold. The witch and the child have reached the high road. And the others—" he hesitated, "—the others are coming too. The Crown. The guild. All of them."

"Then everyone is coming to my country," Marenne said, "which is more visitors than Thornfell has had in three hundred years. Perhaps I should arrange refreshments."

Corin did not laugh. Corin very rarely laughed anymore; grief had sanded the humor off him the way water sands stone, patiently and without malice. "You know what I mean to say, Majesty."

"You mean to say that I am about to do something terrible."

"I mean to say that you are about to do something you will not be able to take back. There is a difference. Terrible things can sometimes be survived."

She looked out over her kingdom. It was beautiful. That was the cruelty of it — the erasure had preserved them like flowers pressed in a book, every rooftop and hedgerow rendered in the colors of memory, which are always richer than the colors of fact. Somewhere beyond the mountains, the borderlands teemed with living thousands: farmers, children, a whole generation who had never heard of Thornfell and would go on not hearing of it if Marenne simply let the world keep its lie.

*Let the world keep its lie.* That was what the hedge-witch would counsel. What the smuggler woman had already declared, standing in the ruin of the great hall with her chin up and her voice shaking only a little: *you cannot pay for one country's life with another's death.*

Marenne had refused her. Refused her, and felt something crack in the refusal — not her resolve, but something adjacent to it. Some part of her that had hoped, for one mad moment, that the girl would say yes, and thereby relieve her of the burden of arithmetic.

"The child," she said. "Wren. Tell me again what the watchers saw."

"They spoke to the Door itself, Majesty. Politely. As one speaks to a host." Corin's voice went strange. "And the Door listened. It has begun to open. Not just for the child — the Door is offering passage to all of us. Every soul in the echo. Passage out. To somewhere else. Somewhere living."

There it was. The mercy she had not asked for, delivered by a mute child and a runaway guide, wrapped in the old tongue like a gift in paper she could not afford.

"And where does the Door lead, Corin? Have any of your watchers seen?"

"No, Majesty."

"Neither have I." She turned from the balcony. "A door that opens onto nothing known. A country we might walk into and find ourselves strangers in — no soil that remembers us, no names on any register, ghosts arriving in a land of the living with nothing to offer but our grief." She smiled, and knew it was the smile her courtiers had dreaded for thirty years of her reign, the one that meant a decision had been made and all debate was now decorative. "No. I did not spend three centuries keeping this country alive so that it could emigrate."

* * *

She gathered her remnant in the throne room at dusk, because dusk was when the echo ran brightest, when the last sun of the last real day poured through windows that had shattered three hundred years ago and somehow still held light.

They came — perhaps four hundred of them, out of the many thousands the echo held. Her soldiers, or the shapes her soldiers had left behind. Her ministers. A handful of farmers who had followed the drums out of curiosity, which in Marenne's experience was how most history got made.

She had dressed for it. Full regalia, the antlered crown that had never touched a living brow — she had died before her coronation, which was why the histories, had any survived, would have called her the queen who was never crowned. She had worn the crown anyway for three centuries. Memory, she had long ago decided, outranked protocol.

"My people," she began, and then stopped, because the speech she had prepared — the one about glory and restoration and the debt the living owed the dead — tasted of ash in her mouth. She had given that speech to herself every night for three hundred years, and tonight, facing their faces, she found she could not give it to anyone else.

So she told them the truth instead.

"I am going to ask you to march on the Door," she said. "Not to pass through it. To seize it. To hold it open — or rather, to hold it *shut* until Wren comes to me, and speaks the words that will make Thornfell whole again. Real. Living. Restored to the maps and the memories it was scraped from." She let that stand in the air, gleaming, the way a jeweler lets a stone stand in the light before naming its price. "And to make it real, the borderlands must be unmade. The villages there. The farms. The people in them."

Silence. The particular silence of a throne room full of dead people deciding whether their queen has gone mad, or whether she went mad some time ago and they were simply polite about it.

An old soldier near the front — Brennick, who had held the eastern ford for six hours against impossible odds and then died holding it for eternity — shifted his weight. "Unmade," he said carefully. "You mean killed, Majesty."

"I mean returned to the state Thornfell was put in," Marenne said. "Erased. Which is what was done to us, and which we all spent three centuries agreeing was the cruelest thing that can be done to a country." She heard herself, and winced inwardly, and kept going, because a queen who flinches mid-sentence loses the sentence. "I am not pretending this is clean. I am telling you it is just. We were erased without consent. I propose to un-erase us. The cost falls on those who inherited the theft."

"The children in those villages didn't inherit anything," said a voice from the back. A woman. Marenne searched the crowd and found her — Talla, the miller's wife, who had died in the plague year and whose loop was simpler than most: she walked from her house to the mill and back, and the walking was enough. "My granddaughter's granddaughter lives in Hollowmere, far as anyone can reckon it. I've watched the roads change around her village. She never stole my mill. She doesn't even know I existed."

"Talla—"

"I'm not refusing, Majesty." Talla's voice was steady, and that was worse than anger. "I'm asking you to say it plainly. Say: we will erase children so that our children can be un-erased. Say it in those words and see how it sits in your mouth."

Marenne sat with it. In her long life and longer death she had learned that the moments you most wanted to rush past were the ones that contained everything.

"I will say it plainly," she said at last. "We will erase children so that ours may live. That is the price. I believe it is too high. I also believe it is the only price on the table, and I have spent three centuries watching every other door close." She spread her hands. "If any of you can show me another way — a word, a working, a mercy I have missed in three hundred years of looking — I will kneel to you in front of this entire court and beg your pardon. Until then, I ask you to march with me. Not command. Ask."

And here was the thing she had not prepared for: they wavered.

Not all of them. Brennick squared his shoulders and stepped forward with the grim relief of a man finally given orders after a long peace. Half the soldiers came with him. But Talla stayed where she was, and others stayed with her — the miller's wife, two of the weavers, old Corin himself, who looked at his queen with three centuries of love in his face and said, quietly, "Someone must stay to keep the loops running, Majesty. If the country empties, it unravels. You know that."

It was true, and it was also a kindness dressed as logistics, and Marenne loved him for both.

"How many stay?" she asked.

"Enough," Corin said. "Enough to keep the bread rising and the wedding going. Enough that whatever happens at the Door, there is still a Thornfell for someone to come home to." He paused. "Even if home is only ever this evening."

She descended from the dais and stood among them, crown and all, and one by one those who would march came forward and took her hand — a dead queen's hand, cold as riverbed stone — and those who would stay stood apart with their heads bowed, not in shame but in blessing, the way family stands at a harbor.

Talla was the last. She took Marenne's hand in both of hers. "Bring them home," the miller's wife said. "Whichever them you can. Even if it's only yourself."

* * *

They marched at moonrise, through streets that glowed with remembered lamplight, past windows where the looping dead paused in their eternal evenings to watch them pass. The baker set down his dough and stood in his doorway and did not wave, because waving would break the loop, but he stood, and his standing was a kind of wave. The wedding party turned as one to watch the soldiers go, and Sefa, the bride forever nineteen, lifted her bouquet and held it aloft — not throwing it, just holding it up, the way you salute something you hope survives you.

Marenne rode at the head on a horse that had been dead longer than most nations, along roads that remembered her weight fondly. Behind her, perhaps two hundred souls. Ahead, the high passes, and the Door, and the gathering storm of the living: the Crown's black-and-silver column descending from the north, the guild's knives slipping through the scree from the east, and somewhere between them all, a smuggler woman and a hedge-witch and a child who spoke to locks like a guest at a table.

Corin had stayed behind. His last words to her had been characteristically practical: "If you win, send word. If you lose, send word. If you become something we wouldn't recognize—" he had almost smiled, "—send word anyway. We'll adjust the loop to include it. We're good at including things. It's most of what we do."

Now, climbing into the thin cold air where the echo thinned and the real mountains began, Marenne felt the strangest sensation: fear. She had not been afraid since she died. Fear required stakes, and the dead have none — nothing can be taken from someone who has already lost everything. But tonight she had something to lose again. Tonight she had a chance, and chances are heavier than crowns.

At a switchback below the summit, her outriders halted, and Brennick came back down the line at a dead run.

"Majesty. The Door." He was breathing hard, though breath was a habit he'd kept purely out of sentiment. "It's opening. The child is speaking to it. And the witch — the hedge-witch — she's holding the ward-line alone against the Crown's vanguard. They'll break through within the hour. Everyone will reach the threshold together."

"Together," Marenne repeated. Of course. Fate had always had a taste for the theatrical; she recognized a staged finale when she marched into one.

She drew her sword — not because she expected to use it, but because a queen's last march deserves a drawn blade — and raised it, and the moonlight ran down the steel like water finding its old channel.

"Thornfell!" she called, and her voice carried the way voices carry in high places, further than it should, further than physics allows, because the mountain itself had once been hers and had never entirely forgotten. "Three hundred years we waited in the dark and called it patience! One more hour! One more mile! Come — we march for home!"

The remnant roared, and the sound rolled down the passes ahead of them, and far below — she would learn this later, from a watcher's report delivered by a bird that had been dead since the plague year — the hedge-witch at the ward-line heard it and understood what was coming, and said a word that her student had once called superstition and now knew was load-bearing.

The queen's last march had begun. There was no road back from it, and she had stopped looking for one somewhere around the second century.

What she did not yet know — what no one at the Door yet knew, not the witch, not the smuggler, not even the child with the old tongue bright on their tongue — was that the Door had heard Talla's blessing too, in the way that Doors hear everything said in their country. And it was already deciding, with the slow courtesy of ancient things, whom it would admit.

And whom it would refuse.
