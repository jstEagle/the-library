## 36. The Queen Stays

The Door stood open, and the world beyond it smelled of rain.

Maren of Thornfell — who had been crowned in a barley field by a priest with shaking hands and never once sat the throne her father built, because the throne was ash before the oil dried on her crown — stood at the threshold and breathed that smell in. Rain on lowland clay. Woodsmoke from someone else's hearth. The green, wet, indifferent breath of a country that had never heard of hers.

She could walk through. That was the miracle of it, the unbearable gift of it. The Door did not weigh worthiness; it only asked if you were going home or leaving it, and either answer was permitted. Wren's word — *guest*, spoken in the old tongue with a child's careful lisp — hung in the air like a struck bell that refused to finish ringing. Beyond the threshold, the lowlands waited: roads, markets, kings who would need convincing, or kings who would not bother to be convinced at all but would simply find a use for a dead queen with a living face.

She could live again. That was what she had wanted. She had told herself so for three hundred years, down in the hollow beneath the mountains, in the dark where her country's last evening played itself out over and over like a song caught in a music box. *I want to live again.* It had been true. It was still true. Truths did not stop being true just because they became inconvenient.

The trouble — and Maren had three centuries to become an expert on trouble — was the arithmetic.

She had always been good at arithmetic. Her father said so. Her father said many things, most of them now ash along with his throne, but that one had survived because it was useful, and useful things survive erasure better than beloved things do. She had done the sums in the dark under the mountain. One country restored against some thousands of the living displaced, unsettled, unmade. She had carried the numbers like coins in her pocket, jingling them whenever her resolve went quiet. *A fair price,* she had told herself. *A fair price for everything we lost.*

And then Hazel Oyelaran — smuggler, runaway, a woman whose whole soul was a ledger of debts — had looked at her across the ruin of the palace court and said, in the flat voice of a merchant refusing a bad trade: *You cannot pay for one country's life with another's death.*

It should not have stung. Queens are stung by flattery, flattered by insults; it is the ordinary observations that get under the skin. But the girl had said it the way you'd correct a child's sums, without malice, without even much heat, as though the error were obvious and only politeness had delayed mentioning it. And Maren had stood there in her own echo-court, among her own looping dead, and done the arithmetic again, honestly this time, the way you do it when the tax collector is watching.

The numbers did not balance. They had never balanced. She had known it all along, the way you know a load-bearing wall is cracked and choose, night after night, not to put your ear against it.

* * *

The echoes were leaving.

That was the strangest part, the part she would try to describe later to Rosalind and fail, because language — even the old tongue, even the tongue that predated kingdoms — had been built by people who expected the world to stay put. The wedding party by the river had stepped through first, bride and groom hand in hand, and instead of the wedding ending as it always ended — as it had ended four hundred thousand times, the groom called away to the walls, the bride waiting, the waiting becoming the marriage, the marriage becoming the grief — they walked into the lowlands together and kept walking, and the wedding simply... continued. Somewhere past the threshold, it was still happening. Somewhere past the threshold, no one had yet been called to die.

The baker came next. He had been baking the same loaves since the last evening, always interrupted, always one tray short of finished, and he had crossed the threshold carrying his peel like a pilgrim's staff, and Maren had watched him go and thought: *he will finally taste his own bread.* She found she needed to sit down on the steps of her unfinished throne to think about that properly.

One by one, then in families, then in rivers of them, the people of the echo were choosing. That was the word Wren used, when the child paused at Maren's side, small hand tucked into hers with the frank entitlement of children everywhere. *Choosing.* Not fleeing. Not being released. Choosing — which meant that until this moment they had not been choosing, which meant that the loop had been a kind of cage, which meant that Maren, who had told herself for three centuries that she was keeping them safe, keeping them whole, keeping their last day warm and lit and unending rather than letting it go cold —

Well. A queen learns to hold two truths. It is the least of her training.

"You could come," Wren said. The child's trade-tongue had grown fluent these last weeks, quick as a magpie picking up shine, but the old words still came easier, and *come* in the old tongue was a lovely word — it meant *come toward the fire*, specifically, the way you'd call a child in from the fields at dusk. "There's room. The Door says there's room."

"The Door," Maren said, "is very generous lately." She smiled so the child would know it was a joke, mostly. "Tell me, little key. If I walk through — what happens here?"

Wren considered this with the gravity of a scholar and the attention span of a wren, which is to say the child looked at the emptying square, the shuttered stalls, the bell tower where the bells had been ringing the same alarm for three hundred years and had finally, an hour ago, stopped — and shrugged.

"Home," Wren said. Which was not an answer, except that it was. Home would happen here. Home would remain, a country with no one in it, a last day with no one left to live it, a memory with no rememberer.

"And if I stay?"

Wren looked up at her then, and Maren saw the thing she had been afraid to see: the child was sorry for her. Three hundred years of rage and grief and terrible arithmetic, and a mute child from God-knew-where patted her hand and was *sorry for her*, and it undid something in her chest that had been holding since the walls fell.

"Then somebody remembers," Wren said. "That's what Rosalind says. Somebody has to stay and remember, or it's like it never — " The child frowned, hunting for the word, and found it in the old tongue, naturally, because the old tongue had a word for everything the new languages had burned: *it becomes unwritten.*

* * *

Rosalind found her at dusk.

Not the real dusk — the echo's dusk, the eternal final evening, the light going amber and forgiving over the rooftops exactly as it had gone on the last day, forever. But the hedge-witch came walking up the processional road with her staff and her shawl and her half-memory (Maren could see the gaps in her, the way you can see missing teeth in a smile; Vane's raid had taken whole seasons from the woman, and she wore the absences like loose clothing), and she sat down on the steps beside the queen without ceremony, the way you'd sit beside any tired person at any well.

"They're all through," Rosalind said. "Nearly all. Hazel's counting stragglers. She counts everything — did you know that? I think she was born doing sums she didn't want to do."

"I know the type," Maren said.

They sat. Below them, the echo-city lay in its amber light, empty now, or nearly — a few echoes lingering at their doors like hosts reluctant to end a feast, deciding, deciding. The baker's ovens were cold for the first time in three centuries. Maren found the sight did not grieve her. That surprised her. She had expected to be gutted by the emptying of her country, and instead she felt — she searched for the word, and it arrived from the old tongue, as such words did — *harvested*. Not abandoned. Gathered in.

"Hazel thinks you'll go through," Rosalind said. "She has money on it, actually. With herself. She bet herself a bottle of plum brandy that you'd take the Door." A pause. "I told her queens don't gamble."

"Your apprentice owes me nothing, then. I'm staying."

Rosalind turned to look at her, and Maren braced for pity — the evening had been full of pity, it pooled around her like spilled wine — but the witch's face held something else. Recognition, maybe. The look of one keeper seeing another.

"May I ask why?" Rosalind said. "You don't owe me a reason. But I've watched people stand at thresholds my whole life, and the ones who turn back always have a reason, and it's never the reason they say first."

Maren laughed. It startled her; she hadn't laughed since the walls fell, and the sound came out rusty, like a gate opening after a long winter. "No, it never is. Very well. First reason: I am tired, witch. Three hundred years of being the last of something is enough to make anyone long to be the first of something else. If I walk through, I begin again. New country, new name, new griefs to learn. And I find — " she spread her hands, the hands that had signed the surrender no one ever read because everyone who could read it was dead — "I find I have already learned my griefs. I know them like my own teeth. Starting over would mean growing new ones, and I am too old, in soul-years, for teething."

"That's the reason you say first," Rosalind observed gently. "What's the real one?"

"The real one." Maren looked out over her city — her beautiful, impossible, erased city, every stone of it humming now with the Door's open mercy, every stone *written* again, legible, remembered. "When I was crowned, the priest said a thing I thought was nonsense. He said: *a kingdom is not its land. A kingdom is whoever carries it.* I thought it was the sort of thing priests say when the treasury is empty. But I have been a kingdom alone in the dark for three hundred years, hedge-witch, and I can tell you he was right. I carried it. Badly, sometimes. Alone, always. And now —"

She gestured at the threshold, where the last families of the echo were passing through, and beyond it, faintly, the sounds of the lowlands: Hazel shouting a headcount, Wren laughing, the ordinary racket of the living.

"Now there are others to carry it. The child will carry it out into the world — the child *is* the country, in a way I never managed to be. Your apprentice will carry it whether she likes it or not; she's already carrying it, you can see it sitting on her shoulders like a yoke she's decided to stop complaining about. And you —" she turned to Rosalind fully, and made herself say it plainly, because queens learn that plainly-said gifts are the only ones that land — "you have spent your life remembering things other people threw away. Hedge-craft. Forbidden alphabets. Names that were load-bearing. I have watched you for one day and I know your whole shape, Rosalind: you are a keeper. It is the oldest profession and the worst-paid."

"It pays in weeds and gossip," Rosalind agreed. "Mostly weeds."

"So I am asking." And here Maren did the hardest thing she had done since the surrender, harder than the refusal of the Door, harder than facing Hazel's flat-eyed arithmetic: she asked. Not commanded — she had no army left to command with, which was perhaps why asking had become possible — not bargained, not summoned, not any of the royal verbs. Asked. "Stay with me. Not as my subject. Not as my servant. As my colleague. My co-keeper. There is a whole country here that needs tending — not resurrected, I am done with resurrection, I have seen what it costs — but *tended*. The fields will keep growing their last evening's wheat. The houses will want roofs. The graves will want their names read aloud, regularly, the way you'd water a garden. Someone must live here and remember, and I have discovered that one rememberer goes mad in the dark — I have references."

Rosalind was quiet a long moment. In the amber light, Maren watched the witch's half-memory work, reaching for things and finding some and not others, and thought: *she knows about loss too. They all do. That is the whole sad guild of us.*

"You're asking me to move," Rosalind said at last. "At my age. Into a haunted country. With a queen."

"With a retired queen," Maren corrected. "I intend to abdicate within the week. A country of empty houses needs a gardener far more than a monarch, and between us, I have always been better at accounts than at ruling. You'll want help with the wards. I'm told I have a talent for borders."

That got the laugh she'd been fishing for — a short, startled bark of a laugh, the kind that escapes before dignity can catch it. "You're *terrible*," Rosalind said, delighted. "Three hundred years dead and you make jokes about borders."

"I have been saving them up. There was no one to tell them to." Maren let the humor settle, then set it aside, the way you set aside a tool. "I mean it, though. The asking. I will not pretend it costs me nothing to ask — pride is the last coin a queen carries and I am spending mine now. But I would rather tend this place beside a friend than rule it alone above a grave. And I think — I think you would rather tend it than grieve it. Am I wrong?"

Rosalind looked out at the city. The amber light was deepening toward the eternal dusk, and somewhere a lark — an echo-lark, the last one, the one that always sang at the very end of the last day — began its song, and this time, because no one was dying tonight, the song simply rose and kept rising, unfinished, unfinishable, going up and up into a sky that for the first time in three centuries was allowed to be only sky.

"No," Rosalind said quietly. "You're not wrong. But I have a condition."

"Name it."

"You let Hazel come visit. When she wants. No tolls, no royal protocol, none of this kneeling business. She'll pretend she doesn't want to come. She'll come anyway. That girl has been running from home so long she's forgotten homes can be *visited*." Rosalind's voice went soft and fierce at once, the way it must have gone over a hundred years of hedge-taught lessons. "Somebody has to teach her that. Might as well be a queen. She'll argue less with a queen."

"She argued with *me*," Maren said, "not six hours ago, in my own court, about arithmetic."

"Yes," said Rosalind, with unmistakable pride. "She gets that from me."

* * *

Later — much later, when the last echo had passed through and the Door stood closed and quiet, humming to itself like a hive gone to sleep — Maren walked her country alone.

She walked it as a keeper walks, not as a queen: checking the wells, reading the grave-names aloud in the old tongue, propping shutters, noting which fields would need what. The city was empty and it was not empty. Memory filled it the way lamplight fills a room — not solidly, but enough to see by. She had feared the silence. Instead she found the silence was full: full of the last evening, preserved now not as a cage but as a garden, tended, tended, tended.

At the palace steps she found Wren's parting gift chalked on the lowest stair, in a child's careful hand, in the old tongue: *someone lives here.* Not a spell. Just a fact, written down, the way facts become permanent when someone bothers to write them.

Maren sat down beside the words, took out the small knife she had carried since the walls fell, and — because a keeper's first duty is to leave the place better marked than she found it — added one line beneath, in her own hand, in letters three centuries old and brand-new:

*And she is not alone.*

Far off, past the border that no longer existed anywhere but in memory, she heard — or imagined she heard, though keepers learn not to distinguish — the creak of a waystation door opening, a lamp being lit, and Hazel Oyelaran's voice saying, in the tone of a woman who has finally stopped running and started waiting: *"Come in, then. You're letting the cold in."*

Somewhere out there, the next child was already walking toward that door.

Maren smiled, and went in to sweep the step.
