## 40. Rosalind and the Wolf

The first snow of the year came down the night before Rosalind arrived, which Hazel took as a personal affront from the weather.

She had spent three days stacking wood against the waystation's north wall, hauling it up from the split logs by the stream because her back had opinions now that she was thirty-one and had spent a decade carrying other people's contraband over mountains. She'd stacked it neat as an accountant's ledger. And then the sky had opened like a dropped flour sack and buried every stick of it under a foot of white, so that on the morning Rosalind's mule came clopping up the switchback road, Hazel was out in the drifts with a shovel, swearing with the fluency of a woman who could curse in four languages and the old tongue besides.

"You've missed a spot," Rosalind called from the saddle.

Hazel leaned on the shovel. "I've missed all the spots. The spots are gone. The snow ate them."

"Then you'll want to start again." Rosalind swung down with the creaking dignity of a woman who had recently turned sixty and decided to treat it as a negotiation rather than a defeat. She was wrapped in a coat the color of wet slate, and there was frost in her eyebrows, and when she smiled at Hazel across the white yard it did something to Hazel's chest that no amount of practice had ever managed to prevent.

"You could have waited for the thaw," Hazel said.

"I could have waited for a great many things." Rosalind handed her the mule's lead. "I didn't."

That was Rosalind all over. Hazel took the lead rope and thought, not for the first time, that she had spent fifteen years learning to read trails, weather, men's faces, ledgers, lies — and that none of it had ever been half so useful as learning to hear what Rosalind meant underneath what she said.

* * *

Inside, the waystation smelled of bread and pine smoke and the particular clean cold that came off stone walls in winter. It was not a large place: one long room with a hearth at each end, bunks along the walls for travelers who needed them, a table scarred by forty years of knives and cups and once, according to the previous keeper, a small riot. Above the great hearth hung the lamp — iron, green with age, its glass smoked amber — and beneath it, nailed to the beam, a board where Hazel chalked names.

Not cargo manifests. Not routes. Names. Some were real. Some were invented whole, six syllables of nonsense designed to be remembered wrong. All of them meant the same thing: *this door opens for you, and here is the word that proves you asked.*

Rosalind stood under the lamp a long while, reading the board, before she said anything at all.

"There are more than last time," she said.

"There are always more than last time."

"And fewer than there should be."

"That too." Hazel set two bowls on the table without asking whether Rosalind wanted stew, because Rosalind wanted stew; Rosalind had wanted stew since before Hazel was born, and would want it after the sun burned out. "Vane's office has stopped burning archives, if that counts for anything. They've started *collecting* them instead. Somebody high up decided dead languages were worth more filed than ashed. I don't know if that's progress or just theft with better manners."

"It's both," Rosalind said, settling onto the bench with a sigh that went all the way through her. "Most things are both. Sit down, girl, you're dripping on my floor."

"Your floor."

"I kept it alive for twenty years. That makes it mine in perpetuity. Ask any witch."

Hazel sat. Outside, the snow came down thick and unhurried, filling the world to the edges of the lamplight, and beyond the lamplight the mountain roads went dark and quiet, holding their secrets the way they had learned to again — gently, the way you hold a sleeping child.

* * *

They ate. Rosalind told her about the Door — the outer side of it, the side she tended now, a year and more since the battle at the threshold. The ward-line held. The echoes had gone through in ones and twos and then in dozens, a country's worth of people walking out of a loop into whatever came next, and the Door had closed behind the last of them soft as a book shutting. But doors that have been open once remember how, Rosalind said, and hers dreamed sometimes. On certain nights the stones around it hummed in a language Hazel knew now, syllable by syllable, thanks to a gift a child had pressed into her hands at the closing.

"On midwinter it sang," Rosalind said. "All night. I sat out there with tea until dawn listening. I understood perhaps a tenth of it."

"What was it saying?"

"Guests," Rosalind said. "It was saying *guests*. Over and over. A door is only ever saying one thing, if you listen long enough." She looked up at the lamp on its beam. "Yours says it too, you know. That's why you keep it lit."

"The lamp? I keep it lit because it's practical—"

"You keep it lit because a girl came to your door once, barefoot, in a storm, and you very nearly didn't let her in." Rosalind said it mildly, the way she said everything that cut deepest. "And because you didn't quite manage to become the person who doesn't let people in, however hard you tried. The lamp is your apology. To her. To yourself. To everyone you almost turned away."

Hazel looked into her stew. "You know, most people's old mistresses nag them about writing letters."

"I nag you about letters constantly."

"You do. This is worse. This is *interpretation*."

"I'm a hedge-witch, dear. Interpretation is the entire trade." Rosalind wiped her bowl with bread, then sat back and studied Hazel across the table with those pale eyes that had never once missed anything Hazel had done, including the things Hazel had successfully hidden from herself for years. "Besides. You asked me to come before midwinter. You never ask me for anything, so I knew it mattered. What is it?"

Hazel had rehearsed this. She had rehearsed it on the trail, at the hearth, in the privy, which was the sort of honesty she permitted herself these days. And now, with Rosalind actually sitting there, the rehearsal evaporated and left her holding the raw thing.

"There's a story," she said. "The one the title comes from. The one you used to tell. About Rosalind and the wolf. I ran away before you finished telling it. I've thought about that more times than I can count — that I left in the middle of a story. It seems like the kind of thing that should matter."

Rosalind was quiet for a moment. Snow ticked against the shutters. Then she pushed her bowl aside, folded her hands, and put on the voice — the old fireside voice, the one that had made even smugglers' children sit still.

"Well then," she said. "Let's finish it."

* * *

"When I was young," Rosalind began, "there was a village in the foothills — not this foothills, another, one that isn't there anymore, and I mean that plainly: the village is gone, and so is the valley, and if you walked there today you'd find a lake with a name nobody remembers giving it. In that village there lived a girl named Rosalind, because I am not clever enough to make up better names for myself than the ones I was given.

"Now, that village had a wolf. Or believed it did. Every winter, something came down from the high passes and took sheep. Sometimes a dog. Once, they swore, a child — though the child turned up in spring, living with a charcoal-burner two valleys over, having run away from a father who beat him. But by then the story had grown teeth of its own, and stories' teeth don't come out.

"So the village did what villages do. They named the wolf. They gave it a shape and a hunger and a malice, and they passed laws against it, and they paid hunters, and they wrote its name on the church door so God wouldn't mistake it for one of His. And every year the losses continued, because — and here is the part I was getting to, girl, the part you ran away before I reached — every year the losses continued because there was no wolf.

"There was a winter famine in the lowlands, and starving dogs come down off the farms. There was a neighbor with a grudge and a knife. There was poor husbandry and bad fences and a priest who liked the congregation frightened, because frightened congregations tithe. But the wolf — the wolf with the name on the church door — the wolf was never there at all. The village had spent thirty years defending itself against a thing it had invented, and in doing so it had failed to defend itself against everything real."

Hazel sat very still. The fire popped. Somewhere out in the dark, wind moved along the eaves like a hand testing a latch.

"They never found that out," Rosalind said. "Or rather — I found it out, when I was seventeen, following the tracks, which went in circles and ended at a shepherd's abandoned hut, because tracks that belong to nothing go nowhere. And I stood in that hut and understood that the danger had never been in the passes. The danger was the naming. The danger was a country so hungry for a simple enemy that it would build one out of shadow and then starve itself feeding it. The wolf was never the danger. The country that named it so — *that* was the danger."

"Thornfell," Hazel said quietly.

"Thornfell," Rosalind agreed. "Three hundred years ago a kingdom got frightened — of a language it didn't speak, a queen it couldn't crown, a border it couldn't hold — and so it did what frightened countries do. It named a wolf. It wrote *enemy* on the door where a neighbor's name should have been. And then it erased the name entirely, which is worse, because at least a lie on a door can be read and argued with. An erased name just leaves a draft coming under the door, forever, and everyone shivers and pretends the house isn't badly built." She shook her head. "Wren wasn't the wolf, Hazel. Neither was the queen. Neither was Vane, though Vane carried the leash. The wolf was the erasure itself. The naming of a whole country as *nothing*, and the thirty years — the three hundred years — spent defending that nothing instead of feeding anyone."

Hazel turned her cup slowly on the table. "I spent a long time thinking I was the wolf," she said. "After. Running contraband past the graves of a murdered country. Selling a child to a collector. I thought — wolf, that's me, that's the shape of me, write it on the door."

"You were never the wolf."

"No?"

"You were the girl who followed the tracks further than anyone else dared." Rosalind reached across the table and took Hazel's hand, and her grip was still strong, a gardener's grip, a grip that had held ward-lines against soldiers. "You followed them all the way to the Door. Most people stop at the hut."

* * *

Later, when the dishes were done and the fire was built up and Rosalind had claimed the bunk nearest the hearth with the unembarrassed entitlement of visiting royalty, Hazel stood at the window and watched the snow.

It was strange, she thought, how a life reassembled itself. Not the way you'd expect — not all at once, not with a single grand choice, though there had been a grand choice, at the threshold, with the Door standing open and Wren's hand small and cold in hers. No. It reassembled the way a trail reassembles after a rockslide: piece by piece, each stone finding the next stone, until one day you looked down and realized you were walking on a path again, and that it went somewhere, and that somewhere was not away.

She had testified nowhere. Filed nothing. Vane's office had offered her a pardon twice — once formally, once through an intermediary who left with a black eye and, Hazel maintained, valuable life lessons. She had declined both. Pardon implied she'd done something the Crown had the right to forgive. Instead she had taken the coin from the collector's household — extracted, with interest, by methods Rosalind pretended not to ask about — and bought the first waystation, and then the second, and then taught three other guides the old alphabet in chalk on the undersides of tables, and now there were nine stations on the high roads, and every one of them had a lamp and a board of names, and not one of them kept books the guild or the Crown could read.

Justice outside the law, Rosalind called it. Hazel called it *keeping the lamps lit*, which was the same thing but sounded less like a sermon.

A light moved on the switchback below. Hazel straightened.

Lantern-light, bobbing, slow, coming up through the storm from the low road — the low road, which no sensible traveler took in snow, which meant the traveler wasn't sensible, which meant the traveler was desperate, which meant exactly the kind of traveler the station existed for. Hazel watched a moment longer, counting the gait of the light the way she'd once counted the gait of horses for signs of stolen goods. One walker. Small. Stopping often.

She took down the lamp from its beam. The iron was warm from the fire. At the door she paused, hand on the latch, and felt the old reflex rise — the arithmetic, the risk weighed like currency, *what does this cost, what do I owe, who's watching* — and felt it, as she always did now, meet the other thing and lose. The other thing had a word in the old tongue. Wren had taught it to her at the Door, the last lesson, the gift inside the gift: the word for *guest*, which was also, in that older-than-kingdoms language, the word for *the reason a house exists*.

"Hazel?" Rosalind's voice, sleepy, from the bunks.

"Traveler on the low road," Hazel said. "Small one. Get the stew going."

She opened the door into the whirling white, lifted the lamp high, and waited — not running out, not hiding, just waiting, visible, warm, the way you wait for someone whose name you don't know yet but whose knock you've been expecting all your life. The light on the switchback paused. Wavered. Then came on, faster, stumbling toward her through the snow, and Hazel Oyelaran — runaway, smuggler, guide, witness, keeper of lamps — stood in her doorway with the door held wide, and said, in a language three centuries dead and more alive than any kingdom:

"*Welcome. Come in. You're expected.*"

And far above, past the storm, past the passes, on the outer side of a Door that dreamed in its sleep, something ancient and patient heard the old tongue spoken in welcome, and — if doors can be said to smile — smiled.
