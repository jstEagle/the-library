## 1. The Taster's Tongue

The pheasant remembered the orchard.

Mara felt it the moment the steam curled up past his face—sun-warmed windfalls rotting sweet under the trees where the bird had fed all autumn, and beneath that, older and fainter, the memory of the hands that had plucked it: a boy in the palace yard, fingers quick and bored, thinking about a girl in the laundry. The pheasant had no opinion about any of this. Food never did. It simply held what had happened to it, the way a riverbed holds the shape of water long after the water has gone.

Mara swallowed the taste, set down the silver fork, and said, "Clean."

That was the whole of his job, most days. One word, delivered flat, delivered fast. Clean. He'd said it eleven thousand times if he'd said it once, and in eleven thousand repetitions he had never once let his face change when the food told him something strange. That was the discipline. Not the tasting—the *not-tasting*, the practiced dullness, the performance of a man whose tongue was just a tongue.

"Clean," echoed Master Corvin from the head of the serving line, and the kitchen exhaled and began to move.

Vell Palace's kitchens occupied three vaulted floors cut into the hill beneath the east wing, and on a feast day they roared like a tide cave. Today was not a feast day, but the king was nervous, and nervous kings ate heavily, so the fires were lit before dawn and the scullery boys had been hauling water since the stars went out. Mara worked the small table near the passage to the royal apartments, apart from the main line, because that was how it was done. The taster ate alone. The taster touched nothing twice. The taster was, in the strictest sense, disposable, and everyone was very polite about never saying so.

He was twenty-six years old and had been the king's taster since he was nine, when the old taster—a whey-faced man named Doss who drank too much after hours—had retired rather suddenly into a grave. Mara's mother had sold him into the post for a purse and a promise, and the purse had been spent within a year, and the promise had been that the work would kill him young and painlessly, which was at least honest as promises went.

It hadn't killed him. That was the problem nobody knew about.

Because here was the thing Mara Nakamura had kept folded away for seventeen years, smaller than a coin and heavier than an anvil: the food didn't just tell him whether it was poisoned. It told him everything. The wheat in the bread remembered the field, and the miller's bad knee, and the night rats got into the granary. The wine remembered the vintner weeping over his dead wife among the barrels. Once, a plate of figs had shown him the merchant who carried them dying on a mountain road, and Mara had tasted grief so clean and enormous that he'd had to leave the hall and stand in the snow until his hands stopped shaking.

In Vell, magic didn't exist. Everyone agreed on this. The covenant—an arrangement so old that its reasons had worn smooth as river stones—pressed down over the valley like a lid on a pot, and whatever wildness had once lived in the world outside lived elsewhere now. The priests said the covenant was mercy. The crown said the covenant was law. The cooks said nothing at all, because cooks are pragmatists, and pragmatists know that talk of magic gets people burned or banished depending on which way the wind is blowing that season.

So Mara had learned early that his gift was not a gift. It was a symptom. And symptoms, in Vell, were hidden or they were fatal.

He managed it through habit and chemistry. Certain things dulled the sense—bitter greens, strong spirits, a tea Ilsa the head cook brewed him every morning without ever asking why he needed it, her broad back turned, her silence a form of loyalty he'd never dared name. He took his tea, he numbed himself to a whisper, and then he tasted for the crown with only the thinnest thread of the true sense left running underneath, enough to catch poison, not enough to be caught by anyone else.

Most days it worked.

* * *

"You're doing the thing," Ilsa said, appearing at his elbow with the silent bulk of a well-fed bear.

"I'm standing."

"You're standing *guiltily*. There's a difference. It's in the shoulders." She set down a covered dish and lifted the lid: quail in plum sauce, glistening, arranged with the geometric precision the king preferred. "Third course. His Majesty's in one of his moods, so chew slowly and don't die before the fish arrives."

"I never die before the fish."

"No," she agreed. "You have a talent for surviving, our Mara." Something in the way she said it made him look up, but her face was already turned toward the line, barking at a boy who'd let the butter sit too near the fire.

Ilsa Rask had run the palace kitchens for thirty years and had run them, rumor held, for two kings and one regency, outlasting ministers the way stone outlasts weather. She was the closest thing Mara had to a friend, which meant she knew exactly three facts about him: that he did his work, that he asked for the bitter tea, and that he had once, at fourteen, quietly vomited into the slop bucket rather than swallow a bite of lamb that had shown him something he refused even now to describe. She had never reported the vomiting. In Vell, that was practically a marriage vow.

He tasted the quail. Plum, fat, salt—and under it, the orchard again, and the hands of the cook who'd glazed it, and a flicker of the king himself, because the dish had been arranged in the room where His Majesty took his meals and rooms soaked things up like linen. The king's residue tasted of vinegar and sleeplessness. Nothing lethal. "Clean," Mara said, and the quail went up the passage on its little wheeled table, and he flexed his fingers and waited for the next.

The next came at midday, and it was the fish.

* * *

Later, Mara would try to reconstruct the sequence and fail, because the failure had happened in the space between two heartbeats and his memory of it was all edges. What he could hold onto was this:

The dish was a river trout, poached, dressed with brown butter and sorrel—one of the king's favorites, sent up from the cold room where the morning's catch lay packed in ice. It arrived at his table already plated, which was unusual; normally the fish came plain and was finished in front of him. But there had been a shuffle of orders, a visiting magistrate, a change in the seating, and somehow the plated dish had come down the passage first, and Mara, tired from a dawn start and already half-numbed by his tea, picked up his fork and tasted it before he thought to ask why it was dressed.

Sorrel. Butter. Salt.

And underneath, coiled in the flesh of the fish like a hair in milk: *bitterness*. Not the honest bitterness of sorrel, which has a green edge to it, a leafy brightness. This was a root bitterness, dark and oily, and threaded through it ran a memory that made Mara's stomach drop—the memory of a mortar and pestle, of gloved hands grinding something pale, of a woman's voice humming while she worked. The food remembered the poison going in. Food always remembered.

He put the fork down. The taste was already on his tongue, spreading cold along the back of his throat, and his body—the body of a man who had spent seventeen years ingesting small doses of everything the kingdom feared—registered it not as death but as *information*. Foxglove, maybe. Or something worse, something he didn't have a name for, because whoever had prepared it had been careful, and careful poisons were the kind that left room for deniability.

"Stop the fish," he said.

His voice came out wrong—too quiet, too late. The dish was already moving. Tam, the under-servant assigned to carry the third course that day, had lifted the trout onto his shoulder board and was halfway to the passage, whistling under his breath, a tuneless little sound he made when he was hungry. Tam was nineteen. Tam had a gap in his smile and a mother in the lower town he sent wages to every week. Tam had never once looked at Mara as anything other than a colleague, which in the palace counted as brotherhood.

"Tam—" Mara was on his feet. The word snagged somewhere in his chest. Because here was the arithmetic of his position, the arithmetic he had performed ten thousand times without letting himself see it: the taster's job was to eat the poison. If the fish was poisoned, the protocol was for *him* to eat it, and for the physicians to decide afterward whether his life was worth the antidotes in the royal stores. That was the covenant of his employment, unwritten but iron. A taster who warned was a taster who had somehow known. And knowing, in Vell, was the one crime worse than dying.

But Tam was already turning the corner into the passage, and the king's steward was calling for the fish, and there was no version of the next three seconds in which Mara both protected his secret and protected the boy.

He chose the boy. He would spend the rest of his life not being sure whether he'd chosen it fast enough.

"Tam!" He caught the passage in four strides, grabbed the shoulder board, and hauled it sideways. The trout slid, the plate tipped, and Mara did the only thing left: he swept the whole dish off the board with his forearm and caught the falling fish barehanded, and then, because a thrown dish raises questions and a taster eating raises none, he shoved the entire poisoned trout into his own mouth and swallowed.

The kitchen went silent the way a struck bell goes silent—all the noise stopping at once, ringing inward.

"Mara?" Ilsa's voice, sharp with a fear she'd deny to her grave.

He couldn't answer. The bitterness bloomed behind his sternum, and with it came the flood—the full, unnumbled memory of the poison, because he hadn't taken his second cup of tea and the dose was too large and too fresh. He saw the gloved hands. He saw the mortar. He saw, with terrible clarity, a sleeve: grey wool, court-cut, embroidered at the cuff with a sigil he knew—the crowned antler of the King's Hunt, the men who rode out beyond the valley's edge and came back with trophies and silence. Then the vision guttered, and the floor tilted, and Mara's last coherent thought was absurdly practical: *the emetic, third shelf, the blue jar, Ilsa knows.*

He was dimly aware of hands—Tam's hands, gripping his arms, keeping him upright. Of Ilsa shouting for the blue jar in a voice that rattled the copper pots. Of himself kneeling on cold flagstones, making his body give back what he'd taken, over and over, while the kitchen watched and the poison's memory drained out of him one burning wave at a time.

When it was over he was gray-faced and shaking, propped against the flour bins, and Ilsa was crouched in front of him with a wet cloth and an expression carved from granite.

"The fish," she said. "Talk."

"Bad," Mara rasped. "Get it out of the kitchen. Burn the plate. Nobody eats anything from the cold room until it's checked."

She looked at him for a long moment. Around them the kitchen had resumed its motion in that particular way kitchens have—everyone working twice as hard to avoid looking at the center of the trouble. Tam hovered at the edge of the frame, white as the flour sacks, holding the ruined shoulder board like a shield.

"You ate it," Ilsa said. "You saw it coming and you ate the whole cursed fish rather than let it reach the table. Why?"

Because a warning would have raised the question of how I knew. Because the taster's silence is the price of the taster's safety. Because I have spent my whole life learning exactly how much I can save without being seen saving it. He said none of this. He said, "Instinct."

"Instinct," Ilsa repeated, flat as a griddle.

"It's my job."

Something moved behind her eyes—not suspicion, exactly. Recognition, maybe, of a shape she'd seen before in other quiet people in other dangerous years. She stood, pressed the cloth into his hand, and said, loudly, for the kitchen's benefit: "The taster's stomach objected to the richness. He'll be fine by supper. Tam—fetch the physician's assistant for the paperwork, and someone get this floor scrubbed before the magistrate's dinner."

Tam knelt beside Mara instead. Up close, the boy smelled of ice and lamp oil, and his hands were still trembling. "You saved my life," Tam said. "That was going to the king's table. It was going to *me*, carrying it. If you hadn't—"

"Don't." Mara's voice cracked on it. "Don't make it a story. Just—don't tell anyone I knocked it off your board. Say you tripped. Say the plate slipped. It's better for everyone."

"Why is it better?"

Because stories travel upward in this place, and mine can't afford the climb. "Because I say so," Mara said, more gently than the words deserved, "and I've never lied to you, Tam. Have I?"

The boy searched his face, found whatever he found there, and nodded slowly. Before he went, he gripped Mara's shoulder once, hard, the way men grip when words won't hold the weight. And Mara sat alone against the flour bins with the taste of foxglove ghosting his throat and the image of the grey sleeve with the crowned antler burning behind his eyes, and understood that for the first time in seventeen years, the vigilance had failed—not his vigilance, but the other kind, the kind that keeps the self sealed. Someone in this palace wanted the king dead. Someone had walked past Mara's table with murder on a plate and hummed while they worked.

And he had swallowed it, and survived it, and noticed—as the tremors finally eased—that his survival had cost him something. The numbness from his morning tea was gone entirely, burned away by the antidote and the purge, and the kitchen was suddenly, overwhelmingly *loud*. The bread on the rack murmured of the mill. The onions wept their whole history. Every pot, every scrap, every crumb was talking at once, a babel he usually kept muffled to a whisper, and he sat in the middle of it with his hands over his ears that did no good at all, because the sense wasn't in his ears. It was in the blood. It had always been in the blood.

*Ilsa,* he thought, and then stopped, because for the first time he couldn't finish the thought. Couldn't finish asking her for the tea, the silence, the arrangement that had held his life together like twine around a bundle. Because the poison had come from inside the palace, and the palace was watching, and a taster who suddenly needed stronger doses of bitter tea was a taster asking to be noticed.

Across the kitchen, Ilsa met his eyes over the heads of the scullery boys. She held his gaze for three full seconds—longer than she'd ever allowed herself—and then she turned back to her ovens.

That evening, after the magistrate's dinner had gone up clean and the fires were banked, Mara climbed to his narrow room above the wine cellar and found, wedged under his door, a heel of dark bread wrapped in cloth. No note. No explanation. Just bread, and beneath the bread's ordinary memories of flour and fire, one extra layer, deliberate and unmistakable, pressed into it by hands that knew his secret and had known it for years:

Bitter herbs. Enough, steeped right, to last a week.

Mara sat on his cot with the bread in his lap and shook—not from the poison this time, but from the vertigo of being *known*, after a lifetime of being invisible. Somewhere below, the palace slept, and somewhere in it walked a poisoner with a crowned antler on their sleeve, and somewhere beyond the valley's edge, the blight crept closer to fields that had never heard of either.

He broke the bread and ate, and let himself, for one unguarded minute, taste everything.

* * *

The edicts went up at dawn.

Mara heard the criers before he saw them—hoarse voices cracking through the fog of the lower courtyard, and the peculiar hush that falls over a crowd when the words being read are words everyone feared and no one said. He pushed through the knot of servants gathered at the kitchen door, flour still on his sleeves from the night's baking, and read the notice nailed to the grain-store wall, the royal seal fat and red at the bottom like a wound.

By decree of the Crown: the ancient covenant wanes. The blight spreads beyond the valley. The realm endures a weakening of its protections, and weakness invites ruin. Therefore, be it known: any soul harboring the gift—any soul in whom the old magic stirs—shall present themselves for examination. Those who conceal the gift will be tried as enemies of the realm. Those who harbor them will share their sentence. Vigilance is the duty of all. Report what you suspect.

Below the formal text, in a clerk's hurried hand, someone had added the practical instruction: examinations begin at week's end, household by household, beginning with the palace staff.

Beginning with the palace staff.

Around Mara, the servants murmured and shuffled and avoided each other's eyes, each of them privately inventorying their neighbors, each of them aware of being inventoried. Tam stood close, reading over Mara's shoulder, and breathed, "Gods. What counts as the gift? My cousin swears her dreams come true sometimes—"

"Dreams aren't magic," someone said quickly, too quickly.

"They might be, though. Now."

Mara said nothing. He stood in the fog with the edict swimming in front of him and felt, with terrible clarity, the thing he had spent his whole life refusing to feel: that the lid on the pot was lifting, and everything underneath it—every hidden gift, every buried song, every secret like his—was about to rise into the light whether it wished to or not.

Behind him, in the doorway of the kitchen, Ilsa watched him read. He didn't need to turn around to know it. He could feel her attention the way he felt the memory in bread—steady, warm, and afraid.

Week's end, he thought. Seven days.

For seventeen years he had survived by being no one. Now the crown itself had announced that no one was no longer enough.
