## 2. Proclamation at Dawn

The edicts went up before the sun did.

Mara heard the hammering from the kitchen yard—three dull knocks of iron on oak, spaced like a heartbeat that had forgotten how to hurry—and he knew, before he even wiped his hands and stepped out into the blue-grey cold, that something had changed. Palace criers didn't post at dawn unless the king wanted the day to start afraid.

There were four boards nailed to the granary wall where the market notices usually lived. Notices about grain tariffs, about festival dates, about the reward for a stray dog that had bitten a tax collector. Those were still there, curling at their corners. The new boards had been placed above them, fresh-planed pine still bleeding sap, as if the old business of Vell could be shuffled down to make room.

He pushed through the small crowd of kitchen girls and stablehands and read them twice, because the first time his eyes refused to cooperate.

*BY ORDER OF THE CROWN OF VELL.*

*Let it be known throughout the valley and its holdings: the ancient covenant, by which our realm has been kept in peace and quietness these nine hundred years, is failing. The wards grow thin. The blight that has long gnawed at the outer provinces now advances toward the heartland. Fields sicken. Wells turn bitter. This is the price of decayed magic, and decayed magic is the price of neglect.*

*Therefore: any person found harboring the gift—the old blood, the touched blood, the singing blood—shall be brought before the magistrate and tried. Sheltering the gifted is treason against the realm itself. Reporting the gifted is a duty owed to every farmer whose fields still stand.*

*The hunt begins at first light. There will be no second proclamation.*

The crowd was very quiet. Kitchen girls who chattered through scaldings and knife-cuts stood with their breath steaming and said nothing at all. Old Tomas the carter read the board three times, lips moving, then spat into the frost and walked away without looking at anyone.

Mara read it a third time anyway. He wasn't sure why. Perhaps because reading it felt safer than believing it.

*The gift.* The words sat in him like a swallowed stone. Nine hundred years, the covenant had pressed the valley flat and silent, and in all that time the crown's position had been simple: there was no such thing as magic in Vell, only superstition in the outer provinces, and the covenant was a wall around a garden with nothing growing in it. Every child learned the catechism. *The valley is quiet. The valley is safe. Quiet is safety.* Now the same crown that had spent centuries denying the gift existed was hunting it like wolves hunted deer.

Which meant the covenant really was failing. Which meant—

His hand drifted, without permission, to his own chest.

"Mara." Ilsa's voice came from behind him, low and rough as a kettle coming to boil. The head cook was a broad woman built like a bread oven, with flour worked so deep into her knuckles it looked like age. She didn't look at the boards. She looked at him. "Yard's not going to sweep itself. And His Majesty's breakfast won't taste itself."

It was, on its face, an ordinary sentence. It was also, Mara understood, a rope thrown across a gap. *Come inside. Stop standing here where everyone can see your face doing whatever your face is doing.*

"Yes, mistress," he said, and followed her in.

* * *

The palace kitchens woke around him in their usual riot—fires stoked, copper pots scoured, the great spits turning already for the noon roast—but Ilsa drew him aside into the tasting room, the small stone chamber off the main kitchen where he performed his office. A taster's room was supposed to be austere: bare table, clean water, a bell to ring if he died. Ilsa had smuggled in a chair cushion over the years, and a jar of honey for the bitter mornings, and a little shelf of books she pretended were hers alone.

She shut the door. "You read it."

"Everyone read it."

"And?" She crossed her arms. "What's your face say that your mouth won't?"

"My face says nothing. My face is a professional instrument."

"Your face looks like a man reading his own death warrant and checking the spelling." She said it without heat, the way she said everything, and then she did something worse: she softened. "Sit down, boy."

"I have the morning meal to—"

"The morning meal can wait ten minutes while you sit down." He sat. She lowered herself onto the stool across from him, joints cracking, and studied him with those pale eyes that missed nothing that happened in her kitchens. "How long have you been building your immunities?"

The question landed strangely. "Since I took the post. Eleven years. You know that. Bitterleaf, arsenic in gradations, nightshade tincture—it's the whole discipline of the craft. A taster who can't survive a dose is a taster who dies learning."

"Aye." She turned the honey jar in her hands, not looking at him. "And how long have you been sleeping badly? Waking with your ears ringing? Tasting things that aren't on your tongue?"

The room went very still. Somewhere beyond the door, a boy shouted about onions, and the sound seemed to come from another country.

"What do you mean, tasting things that aren't—"

"Don't." Her voice cracked like a whip, then dropped again. "Don't do that. Don't do the blank face at me. I've fed you for eleven years, Mara Nakamura, and I've watched you eat, and I'm asking you plain." She leaned forward. "When you taste the king's soup, what else comes with it?"

He should have lied. He was good at lying; vigilance teaches you that. But it was dawn, and the edict was still nailed to his mind, and Ilsa was looking at him the way no one had looked at him in years—as if he were a person and not an instrument.

"Memories," he said quietly. "Sometimes. Not mine. The food remembers... things. Where it grew. Who cut it. Whether the harvest was frightened." He laughed, a thin sound with nothing in it. "That sounds mad."

"It doesn't." She said it fast and flat, and that speed frightened him more than anything else that morning. "And when did it start? Truly?"

He thought about lying again. He thought about it the way a man thinks about jumping.

"Two years ago," he said. "Maybe three. Small things at first. I told myself it was the immunities. Arsenic does strange things to the head—I'd read that. Bitterleaf gives you dreams. I built the immunity schedule tighter. More doses, smaller, regular. And the..." he gestured vaguely at his own skull, "...the noise got quieter. Manageable. I thought I was curing myself."

Ilsa closed her eyes. When she opened them they were wet, which was somehow the most terrifying thing he had ever seen in that kitchen.

"You weren't curing yourself, love. You were drowning it. There's a difference, and the difference matters now." She reached across the table and gripped his wrist, her floured fingers surprisingly strong. "My grandmother came from the hill farms, back when people still remembered the old songs under their breath. She had a touch of it. Nothing grand—she could tell if a cow was sick before the beast knew it herself. And she told me something I never thought I'd need to pass on." Her grip tightened. "The covenant doesn't kill the gift. It presses it down, like a lid on a pot. And folk born with it learn to live under the lid without knowing there's a pot at all. But when the lid starts to lift—when the covenant thins—the gift comes up. Slow. Like water finding cracks."

"The covenant isn't thin," Mara said. "The covenant is nine hundred years of quiet. That's the whole point of—"

"Read the board again, boy."

He had read the board. Three times. He had read it and filed it away as a political thing, a crown thing, a thing about power and fear and other people. Now, sitting in the tasting room with Ilsa's hand on his wrist, he let it become a personal thing, and it went through him like cold water through cloth.

*The covenant is failing.*

And for two years, his sleep had been broken. His ears rang at dusk. He tasted rain in bread baked miles from any rain, tasted grief in meat from animals slaughtered calm, tasted—once, terribly, and he had never told anyone—tasted smoke and screaming in a dish of pears that had come up from the southern provinces, and he had rung no bell, because the pears held no poison, only memory, and memory was not his office.

He had thought the immunities were working. He had built his little fortress of measured poisons and called the silence inside it health.

"It's not the arsenic," he said. His voice sounded far away. "The doses. They weren't suppressing a sickness. They were suppressing—" He stopped, because saying the word aloud in a palace kitchen, on the morning of the proclamation, felt like signing something.

"The gift," Ilsa said, for him, plainly, because she was Ilsa and she had never once flinched from naming a thing. "Aye."

"They'll try me." The words came out small. "If anyone learns. The edict says—"

"The edict says a lot of things." She released his wrist and stood, briskly, wiping her hands on her apron as though the conversation were a spill she could clean. "It says the crown blames the covenant's decay for the blight. Think on that a moment. Nine hundred years the covenant holds, and the moment it frays, the crown knows exactly whom to blame? Knows it fast enough to have proclamations printed and posted before dawn?" She snorted. "I've cooked for courtiers twenty years, boy. I know the smell of a fire that was set on purpose."

"You think the crown wants the gifted found."

"I think the crown wants *someone* found. Blight makes hungry people, and hungry people make angry ones, and angry ones need somewhere to put it." She moved to the door, then paused with her hand on the latch. "Taste the king's breakfast this morning like your life depends on noticing everything. Because it might."

* * *

The breakfast service passed in a haze.

Mara performed his office the way he always did—small spoonfuls, waited intervals, the careful cataloguing of each dish—but today every spoonful shouted. The barley porridge remembered the wet field it grew in and the bent backs that cut it. The honey remembered smoke, though there had been no smoke in the apiary; the bees had dreamed of fire, perhaps, or the beekeeper had. The winter apples from the walled orchard sang, faintly, of a tree planted by someone who had hummed while she dug—a tune Mara almost recognized, a tune that made his teeth ache.

He sat at his little table in the antechamber while the king ate beyond the doors, and he pressed his palms flat against the wood, and he tried to be a man with no gift in a valley with no magic, and he failed at both.

Because here was the arithmetic he could no longer avoid:

The covenant was failing. The edict said so, and the blight said so, and the food on his tongue said so, louder every season. And if the covenant was the lid, and the lid was lifting, then everything Ilsa's grandmother had said would follow, would follow. The pressed-down thing in him was rising. Not arriving—rising. It had been rising for years, patient as water, and he had mistaken its symptoms for illness and its suppression for cure.

He had spent eleven years guarding a king from poison.

He had spent the last three of them slowly poisoning himself to keep from hearing the world sing.

And the crown—the crown that fed him, housed him, defined the whole shape of his careful, hidden life—had this morning declared that what he was should be tried. Not healed. Not studied. Tried, as one tries a criminal, and the word carried its older meaning too, the way words do when power reaches for them: *tried*, tested, weighed, and found wanting.

A servant girl came to clear the king's plates. She was young, maybe sixteen, with chapped hands and a habit of humming under her breath while she worked—one of the reasons Mara liked her, though he'd never said so. Today she hummed as she stacked dishes, a wandering little melody with no name, and Mara watched the sound of it move through the room like heat off coals, and he saw—actually saw, with the sense he had spent three years drowning—the faintest shimmer in the air where the notes passed, like dust motes deciding to dance.

Then the shimmer guttered. Died. The covenant pressing down even here, even now, even on a kitchen girl's idle tune.

But it had been there. For half a second, it had been there.

The girl caught him staring and flushed. "Sorry, master Nakamura. Ilsa says I'm not to hum in the service rooms."

"Humming's fine," Mara heard himself say. "Hum whenever you like."

She smiled and hurried out, and Mara sat alone in the antechamber with the taste of applesong still on his tongue and the edict nailed up in his mind, and he understood that the question was no longer whether he had the gift.

The question was how long he had left before the lid came off on its own—in front of witnesses, in the heart of the palace, in the exact place the hunt would look first.

*They will try you,* the edict had said. *Sheltering the gifted is treason.*

That evening, Ilsa pressed a heel of dark bread into his hands at the end of shift, heavier than it should have been, wrapped in cloth, and held his eyes a moment longer than she ever had.

"Eat well," was all she said. "And sleep light."

He broke the bread open in his room that night, and inside the crumb, folded flat and greased against the steam of the ovens, was a scrap of paper with a map drawn in flour-dust and charcoal—a line leading up, out of the valley, toward the mountains.

And beneath the line, in Ilsa's blunt hand, four words:

*Find the ones singing.*
