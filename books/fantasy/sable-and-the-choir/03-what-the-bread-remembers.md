## 3. What the Bread Remembers

The loaf came up from the ovens at the wrong hour, which was the first strange thing about it.

Mara noticed because he noticed everything in the kitchens of Vell. That was his trade, or at least the shape his trade had worn: a taster's tongue, a taster's eye, a taster's patience. He knew the rhythm of the palace kitchens the way a priest knew liturgy. Bread went into the ovens before dawn so it could rest before the morning tables. This loaf—small, dark-crusted, set apart on a cooling rack like something ashamed of itself—had been baked mid-morning by no one on shift.

He should have ignored it. He had a tray to finish, six dishes to taste before they went up to the king's table, and Ilsa watching him with that flat gray stare she used when she suspected him of thinking.

Instead he touched it.

Not with his hands. With the other thing. The thing he kept folded up small inside himself like a knife wrapped in cloth, dull and hidden and never, ever drawn.

* * *

His gift, if you could call it that, was not much. The recruiters for the taster's guild had called it *sensitivity*, and written it down as a liability rather than an asset, and the covenant—the great dampening veil that lay over the valley of Vell like a lid on a pot—pressed it down further still. Under the covenant, Mara's gift was a whisper under water. He could feel what food remembered. Not see it, not hear it, just feel it: a faint emotional residue, like warmth left in a chair after someone rises.

Wheat from a good field felt patient. Wheat from a field where men had died felt heavy, and he'd learned early never to serve bread from those harvests to the king, because the king would eat and then be sour-tempered all day, and everyone would suffer for it. Honey from hives near the shrine tasted of candle smoke and calm. Salt carried grief across oceans and gave it to whatever it seasoned, which was why Mara always used less salt than any recipe called for, and why the court physician had once praised his "delicate hand."

No one knew. In thirty years of service, no one had ever known. The covenant kept his gift small enough to pass for skill, and skill was safe. Skill got you a room off the scullery and two meals a day and the right to grow old unnoticed.

But this loaf—

This loaf was loud.

* * *

He pressed two fingers to the crust, lightly, the way you'd touch a sleeping animal, and let himself listen.

It came in layers, the way memory always did. First the wheat: a field on the valley's southern slope, dry year, thin soil, but tended by someone who sang while she worked. He felt her song in the grain—not words, just the shape of it, rising and falling like weather. Then the milling: a miller with flour-burned hands and a bad knee, grumbling, content. Then the kneading, here in this kitchen, hours ago, by hands he didn't recognize—and under the kneading, something else. Something that made the back of his neck prickle.

A second voice. Woven through the baker's work like a thread pulled tight through cloth. Old. Deliberate. A melody laid into the dough the way you'd lay a blessing into a cradle.

Mara had spent his whole life feeling the faint memories of food, and he had never once felt anything like this. This wasn't residue. This was intention. Someone had sung *into* this bread, on purpose, knowing what a song could do.

And beneath the melody, faint as a watermark, an image rose in him—the first true image his gift had ever given him, not a feeling but a *seeing*:

A mountain. Granite shoulders against a sky the color of unpolished silver. Wind moving through pine, and on a shelf of stone above the treeline, figures standing in a half-circle, robed in undyed wool, mouths open. Singing. He couldn't hear them—the image had no sound—but he could feel what their song did. It held things. It held the mountain and the valley below it and the river and the fields, held them the way cupped hands hold water, and the holding was the point. The song was a wall and a roof and a promise, and every figure on that shelf of stone was giving something of themselves into it, willingly, note by note.

Then the vision guttered. The covenant pressed down. The kitchen snapped back around him—clatter of copper, hiss of fat, someone swearing at a stuck drawer—and Mara stood with two fingers on a cooling loaf and his heart going like a hammer.

Singers. On a mountain. Holding the world together with their voices.

He had heard the stories every child in Vell heard: that the covenant was old, older than the crown, that it kept magic sleeping in the valley so the blight beyond its borders couldn't take root here. Nobody sang anymore. Nobody needed to. The covenant simply *was*, like weather.

But bread didn't lie. And this bread remembered singing.

* * *

"You're pale," said Ilsa.

Mara snatched his hand back. She was standing at the end of the worktable with a paring knife in one fist and a turnip in the other, watching him with the expression she wore for apprentices who burned sauces—which was to say, without mercy, but also without malice.

"The loaf," he said. His voice came out steady, which surprised him. "Who baked it?"

"Nobody you need to worry about." She set the turnip down and began peeling it in one long unbroken ribbon, eyes on her work. "Old recipe. Out-of-date. I keep it for myself."

"It's been sung over."

The paring knife stopped.

For three full seconds the loudest thing in the kitchen was the drip of the ewer. Around them the staff moved and chopped and shouted, oblivious, wrapped in their own ordinary noise. Ilsa's face did nothing at all. That was the frightening part. Thirty years of reading faces across a tasting table, and Mara had never seen a face go so completely blank.

"I don't know what you mean," she said.

"Ilsa." He kept his voice low. "I've tasted for this court since I was nine years old. I know what food remembers. I know what people put into it." He swallowed. "There's a song in that bread. An old one. And there are singers in it. On a mountain."

Ilsa looked at him for a long moment. Then she glanced—quickly, expertly—at the scullery door, the pantry arch, the corridor to the hall. Checking sightlines. It was such a practiced motion that Mara's stomach dropped, because it was the motion of someone who had done it many times before.

"Come with me," she said.

* * *

She led him to the cold room, shut the door, and stood with her back against it, arms crossed, blocking it entirely. The hanging carcasses swayed gently above them. Breath fogged.

"How long," she said.

"How long what?"

"Don't." The word cracked like a whip, quiet though it was. "Don't do the innocent voice. You're the best taster this palace has ever had, and I have watched you for eleven years, and I have watched you *not notice things*. Do you understand me? Nobody tastes salt the way you taste salt. Nobody knows a dish is wrong before the poison is even in it. Eleven years, and you have never once been surprised by a flavor." Her gray eyes were wet-rimmed and furious. "How long have you had the gift?"

Mara's mouth was dry. Every instinct he owned screamed at him to deny it—to laugh, to shrug, to say *Ilsa, you're tired, it's just bread*. Denial had kept him alive for thirty years. Denial was the whole architecture of his life.

But she had said *gift*. Not curse. Not sickness. Gift.

"Always," he said. "Small. The covenant keeps it small. I feel what food remembers. That's all. Feelings. Nothing more. Until today."

"Until today," she repeated.

"Until that loaf." He wrapped his arms around himself against the cold-room chill, or maybe against something else. "Ilsa, who baked it? Where did the song come from? There were people in it—on a mountain—and they were holding something, some kind of—"

"Stop." She said it fast, sharp, glancing at the door again. "You don't say those words out loud. Not here. Not anywhere inside these walls." She pushed off the door and crossed to him, and suddenly her hands were on his shoulders, gripping hard, and her voice dropped to almost nothing. "The edict comes tomorrow. Dawn. Posted on every gate and church door in the city."

"What edict?"

"The covenant's failing. Everyone can feel it—the blight creeping closer every year, the wells going bitter in the outer villages. The crown needs someone to blame, and it isn't going to blame itself." Her grip tightened. "They're declaring a hunt, Mara. For the gifted. Trials. Confessions. You know what happened in Harrow Vale when they found the hedge-witch there."

He did. Everyone did. Harrow Vale had a new empty house with a scorched threshold.

"They'll test the household staff first," Ilsa went on. "Palace servants. Kitchen staff. They'll start with anyone whose work touches the king's table, because paranoia starts where the fear of poisoning lives." She laughed, short and terrible. "Which means they'll start with you."

* * *

Mara sat down on a chopping block because his legs informed him they were no longer interested in cooperating.

Thirty years. Thirty years of careful, invisible, meticulous survival. He had built his entire existence out of smallness—small gift, small opinions, small appetites, small footprint. He had made himself so unremarkable that even the covenant barely bothered pressing on him. And now, in the space of one loaf of bread, all of it was ash.

"I can't run," he heard himself say. "I don't know anything else. I've never been past the valley gates except with supply carts. I don't have money. I don't have—"

"You have tonight," Ilsa said. "That's what you have."

She turned away from him then, and busied herself at the far shelves, moving jars, shifting sacks, and it took Mara a moment to understand that she wasn't tidying—she was searching. When she came back she pressed three things into his hands, quick and businesslike, as if handing off a hot pan.

First, a cloth bundle, warm and dense. Bread. The same dark loaf, or its twin, plus a round of cheese and dried apples wrapped inside.

Second, a small drawstring purse that clinked. He stared at it. "Ilsa, I can't—"

"You can and you will. Twenty years I've been head cook of this palace. You think I haven't saved? You think I don't know exactly which jars nobody counts?" She closed his fingers over it herself. "Third."

Third was a scrap of oilcloth, folded small. Inside, drawn in charcoal in a firm, unhesitating hand: a map. The valley's southern edge, the old shepherd tracks, a river marked with a careful X, and then—up, up, into the granite teeth of the mountains—a route. At the top, where the lines converged on a shelf above the treeline, she had drawn a small mark. Not a cross. Not an X.

A circle with a line through it. Like a mouth, open. Like a note.

"There's a shrine at the river fork," she said. "Half-collapsed, nobody goes there. If you make it that far, wait there. Someone will find you." She met his eyes. "Someone who sings."

Mara looked down at the map, at the little open-mouthed mark on the mountaintop, and the vision rose in him again unbidden: robed figures on a shelf of stone, holding the world in cupped hands.

"The Choir," he whispered. "That's real. They're real."

"They're real, and they're few, and they're hunted worse than you'll ever be, so if you repeat that word inside these walls again I will personally drown you in the stockpot." But her voice shook on the last word, and her hands, when she took his shoulders once more, were trembling. "Listen to me. Tomorrow at dawn, testers come. You will not be here. You will be gone, and I will tell them you ran off with a scullery maid, because scandal is the only shield I've got left, and it's a good one. Nobody questions a man who runs for love."

"Why?" The question tore out of him before he could stop it. "Ilsa, why would you risk this? For me?"

She let go of him and stepped back, and for a moment the blank mask slipped, and underneath it Mara saw something old and tired and grieving.

"Because twenty-two years ago," she said, "a girl stood in that same cold room, and told her mother she could hear the onions weeping, and her mother reported her by nightfall, because that was the law, and it was the right thing, everyone said so." She wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand, briskly, like brushing away flour. "Her mother never forgave herself. Died asking forgiveness. So." She straightened her apron. "Some debts you pay forward."

Mara couldn't speak. He stood in the freezing room among the hanging meat and the fog of his own breath, holding bread and money and a map to a mountain, and understood that in thirty years of tasting everything, he had never once tasted kindness like this—kindness with a cost in it, kindness paid out of an old wound.

"Ilsa," he managed. "Thank you."

"Don't thank me. Run." She opened the cold room door and the noise of the kitchen flooded back in, ordinary and enormous. "Dusk. The east postern. Wear your oldest coat. And Mara—" she caught his sleeve, one last time, "—when you meet them, when you meet the singers—" 

"Yes?"

"Eat the bread on the way. All of it. Don't save any." Her mouth twitched, almost a smile. "It remembers better than you do. Let it teach you the road."

* * *

He went at dusk, as she said. The east postern, the oldest coat, the purse flat against his ribs. Behind him the palace towers caught the last red light, and somewhere up in them the king was eating a meal that someone else had tasted, and the covenant lay over the valley like a lid, and tomorrow at dawn the edicts would go up and the hunt would begin.

Ahead of him the mountains stood black against the dying sky, and high on their shoulders, invisible in the gathering dark, a shelf of stone waited where singers had once held the world together.

Mara touched the warm bundle of bread under his arm, and felt the old song sleeping in it, patient as wheat.

One way or another, he thought, he was going to find out what it remembered.
