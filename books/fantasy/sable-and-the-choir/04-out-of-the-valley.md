## 4. Out of the Valley

The map Ilsa gave him was drawn on a scrap of flour-dusted linen, and it was wrong in exactly one way: it made the journey look possible.

Mara studied it by moonlight at the treeline above the last farmstead in Vell, where the orchards gave up and the pines took over, and he tried to convince himself that a line of ink could carry a man over the Spine. The valley's edge was not a wall. It was worse than a wall. It was a gradual forgetting—the air itself thickening as you climbed, until your own thoughts came back to you muffled, like voices through a kitchen door. Every child in Vell grew up knowing the feeling without having words for it. You went up the mountain for wood or for goats, you felt the world go quiet and strange, and you came back down grateful, though you never said what for.

Grateful was the whole architecture of his life. Grateful for the roof, the wage, the small warm corner behind the palace kitchens. Grateful that no one had ever looked too closely at the taster who flinched from certain dishes—not because they smelled wrong, but because they *remembered* wrong.

He had the bread in his pack, still faintly warm after two days, which should have told him something about Ilsa's gift even if she'd never once let her hands glow where anyone could see. He had a skin of water, a knife, the coat with the palace livery picked off stitch by stitch in the dark of a hay barn. He had a map with one wrong thing on it.

And behind him, somewhere down in the sleeping valley, he had hunters.

He'd heard the dogs at dusk. Not close. Not yet. But the crown did not post edicts and then forget them, and a food-taster who vanished the same week the gifted were declared enemies of the realm was not a man who would be allowed to simply walk away. He'd spent eleven years learning to taste danger before it arrived. He could taste this one now, cold and metallic at the back of his throat, like a coin held too long in the mouth.

Mara folded the map, put it away, and started up into the pines.

* * *

For the first hour, climbing was just climbing. His legs burned; his breath smoked; the trail Ilsa's map promised appeared and disappeared under needle-fall like something shy of being followed. He counted his steps because counting kept the fear in a box. Four hundred steps, rest. Four hundred more.

It was somewhere past the thousandth step that he noticed the silence changing.

Not the silence of night woods—that silence has texture, insect-song, the tick of cooling stone. This was a different kind. It was the silence he'd lived inside his entire life without knowing it was a silence. The covenant's hush. He had been born under it, shaped by it, and he had never once heard its edges, the way a fish has never seen water.

Now, climbing, he began to feel the water thinning.

It started as lightness. That was the trap of it—how good it felt. A tightness behind his eyes he'd carried so long he'd mistaken it for his own face began to ease. The ache in his teeth faded. The constant low pressure, the sense of being pressed flat like a flower in a book, lifted one petal at a time. He stopped counting steps. He stopped needing to.

Then the bread in his pack spoke to him.

Not words. Nothing so kind. But he became aware of it the way you become aware of someone standing behind you in a dark room—a warmth, a presence, a low hum of *I was wheat, I was rain, I was the hands that cut me down*. Two days of baking and wrapping and carrying, and the loaf was still singing its short life to him, every stage of it, and he could hear all of it at once, and it was beautiful, and it was too much.

"No," Mara said aloud, to a loaf of bread, on a mountainside. "No, no."

Because it wasn't stopping at the bread. The pine trees next—their slow green patience, decades of it, pressing against his mind like faces at a window. The stones under his boots, older than kingdoms, murmuring their long mineral boredom. The wind coming over the Spine carrying tastes: snowmelt, juniper, far-off smoke, and underneath it all something vast and wounded that he understood, with a certainty that dropped through him like a stone through deep water, was the covenant itself. Fraying. Singing out of tune. Dying.

He had spent his whole life tasting what food remembered, and he had thought that was the gift. This was not the gift. This was the gift with the lid taken off, the gift as it must have been before the first king built his dam across the world—and it poured into him the way a river pours into a cracked bowl.

Mara went to his knees on the trail.

The vertigo was total. Up and down traded places. He gripped a root and the root gripped back, its awareness brushing his like a blind hand finding a face, and he sobbed once, a small humiliating sound, because there was nowhere in his body to put any of this. Eleven years of careful smallness. Eleven years of swallowing his own self a spoonful at a time, and now the mountain was pouring everything back in at once and he was the same cracked cup he had always been.

Somewhere below—far below, but not far enough—the dogs bayed.

The sound cut through the flood like a knife through dough. Dogs meant handlers. Handlers meant Wren's people, the king's hunters, men and women who read trails the way Ilsa read dough. And Mara understood, kneeling in the dark with the whole singing world crashing through him, that he could not fight and he could not hide, because hiding required a self to hide, and his self was currently being dissolved and remade by every living thing within a mile.

So he ran.

It wasn't running the way he'd ever run before. Each stride fed him new sensation—the ground's memory of every foot that had ever crossed it, the panic-smell of a fox den he passed, the cold bright attention of the stars themselves, which apparently had opinions—and he staggered between strides, weeping without deciding to, half-blind, following the trail upward because up was the only direction the map trusted. Behind him the dogs found his scent and announced it to the whole night, voice after voice joining, a harmony of pursuit, and some broken laughing part of him noted that even the hunt sang in parts.

The trees thinned. The air went knife-cold. And the world's song swelled to a pitch that was no longer sound but pressure, a hand closing around his skull, and Mara's last coherent thought was that he had made a terrible mistake, that the valley hadn't been a cage, it had been a bandage, and he had torn it off a wound that had never healed—

The shrine saved him without meaning to.

He didn't see it so much as fall into the edge of it: a ring of leaning stones at a bend in the trail, old beyond old, their carvings worn to suggestions. But the moment he crossed into the ring, the flood *eddyed*. The stones remembered something—devotion, maybe, or just centuries of travelers resting their burdens here—and that memory made a pocket of calm, a still pool in the rapids. Mara collapsed into it like a man falling into water after a burning.

He had enough sense left to curl small, to pull his coat over his head, to make himself a knot of ordinary flesh among the singing stones. The dogs were closer. Their voices bounced off the crags, triangulating. He lay in the shrine's quiet heart and shook, and the shaking was the only thing in the world that was only his.

The last thing he registered before the flood dragged him under was a smell cutting through pine and cold stone: woodsmoke, and under the woodsmoke, leather and steel and something bitter he couldn't name.

Someone was standing at the edge of the shrine, looking down at him.

He was too far gone to be afraid. That was the strange mercy of it. The flood took him mid-thought, and the thought was: *I never learned what my own voice sounds like when it isn't afraid.*

* * *

He dreamed of the kitchens.

In the dream everything was normal—the copper pots hanging in ranks, Ilsa's forearms dusted white, the great oaths of steam rising off the noon service—and Mara moved through it doing his work, lifting the silver spoon to his lips, tasting each dish before it went up to the king's table. But in the dream, when he tasted, he said what he tasted. Out loud. To everyone.

"This one remembers being happy," he told the scullery boys, holding up a spoonful of honeyed figs. "This one remembers the tree it grew on, and the year the tree almost died, and the gardener who talked to it every morning like a friend."

The kitchen went silent. Every face turned toward him. And he kept tasting, and kept telling, dish after dish, the whole hidden history of the table spilling out of him, and no one stopped him, and no one applauded either—they only watched, with expressions he couldn't read, while somewhere beyond the kitchen door the dogs bayed and bayed and bayed.

He woke because a boot nudged his shoulder. Gently, but with the unmistakable grammar of someone checking whether a thing was alive.

Mara opened his eye—the left one worked; the right was crusted shut—and the world resolved slowly into gray dawn light and a woman crouched beside him.

She was older than him by ten years or twenty, hard to say, with a face weathered down to its useful lines. A scar ran through her left eyebrow and kept going, and where the eyebrow should have continued there was nothing—an old wound, an empty socket, a leather patch worked smooth with handling. Her remaining eye was pale gray and it did not blink and it did not look away, and it looked at him the way he imagined hunters looked at tracks.

She had a crossbow slung across her back. She had a sword at her hip with the royal crest filed off the pommel, the ghost of it still visible as a shadow in the metal. She had, Mara noted with the detached clarity of the half-dead, hands that were very still, the way a person's hands are still when stillness is a discipline rather than a temperament.

"You crossed the veil," she said. Her voice was low, roughened, like a road well traveled. "Alone. At night. With no wards, no training, and—" she tipped her head, taking in his trembling, his crusted eyes, the way his fingers plucked involuntarily at the ground as if the soil were talking to them, which it was, "—no idea what you were walking into."

"I had a map," Mara said. His voice came out as a rasp.

"A map." Something moved at the corner of her mouth. It might have been the ancestor of a smile. "That explains it."

The dogs were gone—or distant, anyway, their voices thinned by distance and dawn. Mara tried to sit up and the world tilted generously, showing him the sky, the pines, the shrine stones, all of it still murmuring, though softer now, the flood settled into a wide slow river he could almost bear. Almost. He got himself upright against a shrine stone and found the woman watching him with an expression he recognized, because he'd worn it himself for eleven years across a tasting spoon: the careful face of someone deciding, very fast, what to do with information that could ruin them.

"You're from the valley," she said. Not a question. "Palace, by the look of you. They've picked the stitching off your collar."

"Taster," he said. There seemed little point lying to a woman whose sword had once been the king's. "Royal food-taster. Eleven years."

Something flickered across her face at that—quick, complicated, gone. "Eleven years," she repeated. "Swallowing poison for the man who posted those edicts."

"Mostly I swallowed nothing. That was the point." He wrapped his arms around his knees. The cold had gotten into him somewhere deep and set up housekeeping. "I'm not—I don't know what I am anymore. In the valley I could taste what food remembered. Just that. Small. Like hearing music through a wall. Then I crossed the line and it was—" He gestured helplessly at the mountains, the sky, the whole overflowing world. "Everything. All of it at once. I couldn't hold it."

"No one can. First time." She said it flatly, but something in the flatness was gentle. "The covenant doesn't kill the gift. It starves it. Cross the line and it eats everything it's been owed." She stood, in one economical motion, and looked down the trail the way he'd come. Listening. The dogs were silent now, and somehow that was worse. "They'll pick the trail back up by midmorning. Maybe sooner, if the fog burns off."

"Then I should keep moving."

"You should lie still and die quietly," she said, "if you're going to be stupid about it. You can't walk a mile in your state, and every step you take uphill makes the flood worse before it gets better. There's a place. Higher up. People who can teach you to hold it." She paused, and her one eye came back to him, and he watched her weigh something—watched the scales move behind her face, bounty on one side, whatever she saw kneeling in the shrine stones on the other. "People who sing."

The word landed in him strangely. Through the wide slow river of the world's murmur, he felt the shrine stones react to it—a faint harmonic, a tuning fork struck. *Sing.*

"Why are you helping me?" Mara asked. It was the question he should have asked Ilsa, and never had, and regretted. "You don't know me. I'm worth money to somebody. Probably a lot of money."

The woman was quiet for a moment. Wind moved through the pines above the shrine, and the stones hummed their old patient note, and somewhere very high a bird of prey turned circles on the thermal.

"I know the uniform you're not wearing anymore," she said finally. "I wore it longer than you did." She touched the filed-off crest on her pommel, once, like touching a scar. "My name is Sable. If you're coming with me, eat first. Bread travels better than courage."

She turned and started up the trail without waiting to see if he followed, and Mara sat alone a moment longer in the ring of humming stones, the pack open in his lap, Ilsa's loaf warm against his palms. It was still singing. Everything was still singing. But the song had edges now, banks to its river, and he thought—gripping the bread like a rope thrown to a drowning man—that maybe the drowning was survivable. Maybe there was a shore.

Behind him, far below, the first dog barked again.

Mara got up, and followed the one-eyed woman into the mountains.
