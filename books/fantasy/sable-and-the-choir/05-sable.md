## 5. Sable

The shrine had been empty for eleven years, and Sable knew because she was the one who'd emptied it.

She came back the way she always did—before dawn, before the light could make anything look like hope. The mountain path was slick with old frost. Her boots remembered it better than she wanted them to. Eleven years since she'd walked this ridge in a hunter's grey coat with a hunter's iron badge and a hunter's certainty that everything she did was justice. Now the coat was patched wool and the badge was a coin she'd hammered flat, and the certainty was gone, and what remained was this: a woman climbing to a shrine she'd desecrated, once a season, like paying a debt that never shrank.

The shrine was a ring of standing stones at the tree line, older than Vell, older than the crown, carved with spiral marks that the covenant's dampening had worn smooth on one side. She'd helped drag the altar stone down the slope herself. Wren had laughed while they did it. *Superstition,* Wren had said. *Rubble.* Sable had said nothing, which in those days was as close as she ever came to doubt.

Now the stones stood in their broken circle and the frost lay on them like a shroud laid by someone careless, and Sable sat on the fallen altar stone—the one she'd dragged, the one her hands still ached to remember—and waited for the sun.

That was when she heard him.

Not footsteps. She'd have heard footsteps; she'd tracked men through worse country than this for six years. What she heard was thinner than sound, a thread pulled through the cold air itself, coming from inside the shrine where no one should be: a note, wavering, held by someone who didn't know he was holding it. Unconscious singing. Covenant-trained breathing, if she trusted her ears, and her ears were the last part of her she still trusted completely.

Sable went still the way she'd been taught to go still—not frozen but *present*, weight settled, breath banked low. Then she moved, silent along the stones' blind side, and looked into the ring.

A boy lay crumpled against the eastern stone. Young—younger than she'd expected from the voice. Dark hair matted with sweat, valley clothes, valley boots, mud to the knee. He'd crossed the boundary line at night, then. Alone. And his body had taken the flood of returning magic the way a dry riverbed takes a flash flood: badly.

He was muttering. She edged closer, hand on her knife out of habit rather than intent, and caught the words.

"—too much, too much, it's all *singing*, why is everything—"

Then his eyes opened, and they found her immediately, the way prey finds the thing that's already decided about it.

"Don't," he rasped. "Please. I'm not—I didn't—"

"Shh." She crouched an arm's length away, knife still sheathed now, hands visible. Old habit from interrogations, repurposed. "You crossed the veil last night."

"I had to." His voice cracked on it. "They posted the edict. They're hunting anyone who—" He stopped himself, jaw tightening, and she watched him do the arithmetic every gifted person in the valley must have done since dawn of that proclamation: *how much do I tell the stranger with the knife.*

"You're burning up," she said instead. "You took the whole boundary surge at once. Most people cross slow, over days, letting it seep in. You jumped."

"I didn't know there was a slow way."

"No," she said. "You wouldn't have."

* * *

She built a fire small enough to hide and fed him willow bark steeped in snowmelt, and while he drank she studied him the way she used to study bounties: build, calluses, tells.

He wasn't a runner. Runners had a specific hunger in them, a lean forward even sitting still. This one sat like a man who'd spent years being careful in a small room. Kitchen-worker, maybe. Servant class. His hands were clean under the grime, nails trimmed, a cook's or taster's hands. And when he flinched at sounds, he flinched toward them first and away second—listening before fearing. That was a gift-bearing habit. That was a boy who'd spent his whole life hearing more than he was allowed to admit.

"Sable," she said finally, because he kept looking at her like a question.

"Mara."

"Mara what?"

"Just Mara." A ghost of something wry moved through the fever-glaze of his face. "It's safer."

"It's never safer," she said. "Names are only dangerous when you're hiding something worth finding."

He almost smiled. "What are you doing out here? At a shrine nobody tends?"

"Tending it," she said, "badly."

He slept after that, the hard sleep of the fevered, and Sable sat across the fire and did the thing she'd climbed the mountain to avoid doing, which was think.

* * *

Here was the arithmetic, laid out plain as a bounty notice:

The crown was offering forty crowns for unregistered gifted, sixty if they were adults with trained control, and the notices hadn't specified whether the finder needed to bring them alive. Sable had read the edict nailed to the waystation post two days ago, had stood in front of it in her hood while farmers muttered around her, and had felt the old machinery of herself begin to turn—assess, route, timeline, risk—before she'd shut it down so hard her teeth ached.

Forty crowns was a year of living. Sixty was a new name in a far province and a door that locked from the inside.

And here was the other column, the one without numbers:

Six years she'd worn the grey coat. Six years of following resonance-trails through the valley's villages while the covenant still hummed overhead, back when the hunt was quieter, politer—a knock at the door, a registry, a stern talk, a family quietly relocated to the capital "for assessment." Nobody disappeared, exactly. People just stopped writing letters. It had seemed clean. It had seemed like order.

Then the covenant had begun to fray, and the crown had gotten frightened, and frightened crowns sharpened things. The polite knocks became doors broken at night. The assessments became cages. And Sable had stood in a farmhouse kitchen outside Lowmere holding a lantern on a weaver woman who sang to her loom, who had never hurt anything in her life, and the woman had looked at Sable's badge and said, *You're one of us, aren't you? I can hear it in you. You hold your note so tight it hurts you.*

And Sable had arrested her anyway.

That was the night the coat came off. Not dramatically. She'd folded it. She'd left it on the barracks bench with the badge on top, squared at the corners, the way she did everything, and she'd walked out of the garrison and up into the mountains where the covenant's dampening thinned and her own buried gift—the tight-held note the weaver had heard—finally got to breathe.

She'd never told anyone what it was. Even now, alone, she only let it out in the smallest sips: a sense of harmony and dissonance in living things, a feeling for when a person's inner song was true or strained. Useless for bread. Useless for coin. Perfect for hunting people. That was the joke of it, the cruelty of it—that her gift had made her such a good hunter, and that the gift had known, all those years, how much she hated the work. She'd held her own note so tight it hurt. The weaver had been right.

The fire popped. Mara turned in his sleep and murmured something that sounded like *Ilsa*, and his lips shaped a word after it that might have been *thank you*.

Sable looked at him and felt the resonance come off him even unconscious—untrained, raw, enormous. Whatever he was, he wasn't a dabbler. He was the kind the crown feared most: strong, unregistered, and loose in the mountains with nothing between him and desperation.

She should turn him in. Every rational ledger said so. Forty crowns. Safety. An end to looking over her shoulder, because the hunters had never stopped looking for *her*—Wren among them, Wren most of all, Wren who had sent three messages in eleven years, each one the same: *Come home. All is forgiven. Come home.*

All was not forgiven. Sable knew exactly what forgiveness cost, because she'd priced it herself, once, for others.

* * *

He woke at dusk, lucid, embarrassed.

"How long—"

"A day. You needed it." She handed him broth made from the dried mushrooms she carried, and he cupped the tin in both hands like it was something holy, and she thought: *kitchens. Definitely kitchens.* "Tell me what you ran from. All of it. If I'm going to decide what to do with you, I want the whole weight of it."

"That's not frightening at all," he said, and there it was again, that flicker of dry humor under the fear, and she hated how much it reminded her of herself at that age, armor made of jokes.

So he told her. The palace kitchens. The tasting. Years of eating poison-adjacent food and building an immunity he'd thought was just a strong stomach, until the edict made him wonder if the immunity had been masking something else entirely. The loaf of bread he'd read like a letter—he described it haltingly, watching her face for the moment she'd decide he was mad, and she kept her face still because she'd heard stranger testimonies from saner-seeming people. The head cook who'd given him a map instead of a noose. The flight over the boundary line at night. The flood.

"When I crossed," he said, staring into the fire, "it was like—everything has a song. The rocks, the snow, my own blood. I've been deaf my whole life and nobody told me. And it wouldn't stop, and I couldn't find the—the handle on it, the off switch, and I just walked until I fell down here." He looked up. "Is that what it's like for everyone?"

"No," Sable said. "Most people's gifts are small. A warmth in the hands. A knack with animals. What you're describing—" She stopped.

"What?"

"What you're describing is a keeper's range." The words came out of her before she could weigh them, and once they were out she couldn't take them back, so she finished. "The old covenant-keepers could perceive the whole weave. It's in the training songs. I learned fragments, in the garrison, before they cut that part of the curriculum." Before they cut it because keepers were inconvenient, because keepers asked questions about where the keystone song had gone, though Sable hadn't understood that yet then—had only known that the officers went quiet and sharp whenever the old songs came up.

Mara set down the tin. "Keeper," he repeated. "That means something to you."

"It means the crown will pay more than sixty crowns for you," she said flatly. "And it means I should decide fast."

His throat moved. He didn't beg. She watched him decide not to beg, watch him gather the little dignity he had left and hold it in both hands, and something in her chest—the tight-held note, the one that hurt—shifted against its binding.

"Ask me," he said quietly. "Whatever you're weighing me against. Ask me, and I'll answer true. It's the only thing I've got."

* * *

She asked him three questions.

"Why did you run instead of registering?" Because some ran from guilt, and some ran from punishment, and the difference mattered.

"Because registering meant trusting the people who wrote the edict," he said. "I tasted for the king for nine years. I know what goes into the royal kitchen. Trust isn't on the menu."

"Do you hate the crown?" Because hatred made people reckless, and reckless made them dead, and dead made bad company.

"I hate what it's doing," he said slowly, working it out honestly, which was rarer than courage. "I don't know if I hate the crown. I served it. Some of the servants are kind. Some of the guards are scared boys. I think the crown is a machine and machines don't deserve hate, they deserve—" he frowned— "repair. Or replacing."

Last question. The one that mattered. She leaned forward.

"The weaver woman of Lowmere. Sang to her loom. Arrested four years ago, spring, by a hunter with a lantern." Her voice didn't shake. She'd practiced. "If I told you I was that hunter, what would you do?"

The fire cracked. Mara looked at her for a long time, and she felt her own gift open despite her, feel *his* song: terrified, exhausted, and underneath it a steady tone like a plumb line, true.

"I'd say," he said at last, "that you're telling me because you want me to say something that lets you forgive yourself. And I can't do that. But I can tell you what I know about tasting." He turned the tin in his hands. "Every day for nine years I put food in my mouth that might kill me, because someone had to stand between the poison and the king. It didn't make the king good. It didn't fix anything. But it was mine to do, and I did it, and then the day came when it wasn't enough anymore and I had to choose something bigger." He met her eyes. "Maybe your arresting her was yours to do, then. And maybe this is the bigger thing now. I don't get to decide that for you. Neither does she."

Sable sat back.

Eleven years she'd carried Lowmere like a stone in her boot, waiting for someone to either absolve her or condemn her, and this fevered kitchen-boy with keeper's ears had done neither—had handed the stone back to her and shown her where it fit: not behind her, dragging. Ahead. Weight to walk with.

"You shouldn't trust me," she said.

"Probably not."

"I'm going to help you anyway. Not because you're innocent—you're not, none of us out here is—but because the alternative is feeding you to the machine, and I'm done feeding the machine." She stood, joints cracking, and looked east toward the high passes where the trees grew wrong in ways only the gifted noticed, where the dampening thinned to nothing and the old songs still lived in the stone. "There are people who can teach you what you are. Singers. Exiles. They'll test you and probably annoy you. One of them will definitely insult your cooking."

"You don't know that I cook."

"You held that tin like it owed you money and you loved it anyway," Sable said. "Rest. We leave before dawn."

He lay back down, and just before sleep took him he said, "Sable? Why the shrine? You said you tend it badly."

She fed another stick to the fire and didn't look at him.

"Because I helped break it," she said, "and you can't mend a thing by avoiding where it broke. Sleep."

But she stayed up through the dark hours, listening to the boy breathe, feeling his great untrained song rise and fall, and thinking about the road ahead—because the pass they'd need to take wound past the waystation where the newest edicts were posted, and one of the notices she'd read there, half-hidden beneath the bounty lists, had been older, official, sealed with the king's own signet. A directive about *recovered archival materials*. About a *song*.

She'd thought nothing of it at the time.

Watching Mara sleep, she found she was thinking of it now.
