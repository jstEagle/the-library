## 6. The Choir Gathered

Sable walked like a woman who expected the mountain to betray her.

For two days she had led me up switchbacks that weren't on any map Ilsa's ink had touched, cutting across scree fields when the trail bent too close to the valley below, stopping at ridgelines to look back with her one eye narrowed against the wind. She spoke maybe forty words the whole time, and half of them were *drink*.

On the third morning she stopped at a wall of stone that looked exactly like every other wall of stone we'd passed, except that she put her palm flat against it and waited.

"Is something supposed to happen?" I asked.

"You'll know," she said. "If it doesn't, we were never here."

I was about to ask what that meant when I heard it — faint, threaded through the wind, so low I felt it in my teeth before my ears caught up. A hum. Not one voice. Many, braided, rising out of the rock itself as if the mountain had been hollow all along and only now remembered to breathe.

The wall didn't open. That was the strange part. We simply walked toward it, and the shadow of an overhang deepened around us, and then there was no wall at all — just a passage sloping down into warm dark, and the sound swelling around us until my chest ached with it.

"What is that?" I whispered.

Sable glanced back at me. In the dimness her scarred face looked almost gentle, which frightened me more than the dark did.

"That," she said, "is what you've been missing your whole life."

* * *

The amphitheater had been carved by hands that loved curves. Rows of stone seats descended in a perfect bowl toward a floor of pale wood, and above us the ceiling opened through a crack in the mountainside to a coin of blue sky. Moss grew in deliberate patterns down the walls. Someone had tended this place for generations.

And there were people everywhere.

A woman with silver hair sat cross-legged on the stage, tuning a lute by ear alone, each string plucked once and adjusted without testing twice. Two young men — twins, I'd realize later, though at first they seemed like one person reflected — moved between the rows relighting lamps, and as they worked they sang to each other in fragments, one beginning a phrase and the other completing it, so that their conversation sounded like a single mind thinking aloud. Near the back, an old man hunched over a table buried under paper, muttering, crossing things out, muttering again.

Maybe thirty people in all. Every one of them turned to look when Sable's boots hit the wooden floor.

I stopped dead. Everything in me that had survived twelve years in the palace kitchens said *small, quiet, invisible*. But there was nowhere small to be in that bowl of stone. The acoustics took my single footstep and gave it back to me three times, enormous.

"Sable." The silver-haired woman rose from her stool. Her voice carried without effort, the way water finds downhill. "You were gone nine days. You said four."

"The roads were bad."

"The roads are always bad. Who is this?"

Mara Nakamura, royal food-taster, second chair, eleven years of service. I opened my mouth to say it and found I couldn't. Not because it was a lie — it was true, every word of it — but because saying it here felt like introducing myself as a lockpick to a room full of safes.

"This is Mara," Sable said, watching me. "He crossed the veil three nights ago. He collapsed at the shrine of Vess. And he—" She hesitated. I had never heard her hesitate. "He heard the shrine singing before I told him it could sing."

The old man at the paper table looked up sharply. The twins went still, their half-finished phrase hanging unfinished in the air, and the silence where its resolution should have been made my skin prickle.

"Heard it how?" the silver-haired woman asked.

"With his ears," Sable said. "Like anyone. But he knew what it was. He said—" she glanced at me, almost apologetic, "—he said it sounded lonely."

"It did," I said. My voice cracked on the second word. I cleared my throat. "It did sound lonely. Like something waiting."

The woman came down the steps toward me, unhurried, studying me the way Ilsa studied dough — not judging, assessing, deciding what it might become. Up close she smelled of resin and woodsmoke. Her eyes were the brown of river stones.

"I'm Maren," she said. "I keep this place, such as it keeps itself. You're from the valley."

Not a question. "Yes."

"Then you've lived your whole life inside the covenant's shadow, and you've never once heard your own gift clearly. It will be muffled. Thin. Like hearing music through a wall." She tilted her head. "What is your gift, Mara of the valley?"

"I don't—" The denial rose automatically, smooth as poured cream, twelve years practiced. I swallowed it. These people had sung a mountain open for me. The least I owed them was the truth, or the shape of it. "I taste things. What food remembers. Where it grew, who handled it, whether anyone wished harm into it. It's small. It's nothing like—"

"Nothing like singing," Maren finished. Something flickered across her face — not disappointment exactly. Recalculation. "No. Probably not."

That night they fed me barley stew and bread that tasted, even dulled as my gift was behind whatever the covenant's residue still clung to me, of a hundred kitchens and a hundred hands, none of them afraid. I cried a little into the bowl and pretended it was the steam.

* * *

They tested me the next morning.

I want to say I understood what was happening, but I didn't. Maren simply appeared at my pallet in the alcove off the stage where I'd slept — actually slept, deeply, without listening for footsteps — and said, "Come. Stand on the floor. Sing."

"I can't sing."

"Everyone can sing. Some people just haven't been permitted to find out what happens when they do." She positioned me at the center of the pale wood, facing the empty seats, and the whole Choir began drifting in from the passages and alcoves until perhaps twenty faces watched me from the rising rows. My palms went damp. This was worse than the king's table. At the king's table, failure meant death, but it was a death I understood.

"Now," Maren said, standing beside me, "we're going to give you a note, and you're going to hold it. Don't make it pretty. Don't perform. Just hold it, the way you'd hold a rope over a drop."

Juniper — I'd learned her name the night before, the sharp-voiced young woman with the cropped hair who'd stared at me over her stew like I was a riddle she resented — snorted softly from the third row. "If he drops it, do we get to keep the rope?"

"Juniper," Maren said mildly, and Juniper subsided, but not before our eyes met, and hers said: *I'm watching you. Prove something.*

Maren sang a single tone, low and round, and nodded at me to take it.

My first attempt was, generously, a noise. My voice hadn't been asked to do anything but stay quiet for thirty-one years, and it objected to the assignment. The second attempt cracked. By the fifth, sweat ran down my spine and half the Choir had developed a fascination with their own hands.

But on the sixth try, something happened.

I stopped trying to place the note the way you'd place a cup on a shelf, carefully, from outside. Instead I did what I did with food. I listened for what it remembered. And the note — the note had been sung in this bowl for centuries, soaked into the wood and stone, and it was still there, underneath everything, patient as sediment. I didn't produce the pitch. I joined it.

The sound came out of me steady and full and utterly unfamiliar, and the Choir went quiet in a different way than they had before. Not bored-quiet. Attentive-quiet.

"Good," Maren murmured. "Hold it. Now don't let go when I do this."

She moved to a second tone above mine — a third, bright against my low root — and kept singing it, looking at me expectantly.

I didn't understand at first. Then I did, and the understanding landed in my stomach like a dropped knife. *Both. She wants both.*

"That's impossible," I said, and the note wobbled but held, which was its own kind of answer.

"Humans have one voice," called the old scholar from the back, rising, papers forgotten. His name was Ovid; someone had told me over breakfast, along with the words *disgraced*, *exiled*, and *don't get him started on coronation anthems*. "One set of folds, one fundamental. Split tones exist in throat-singing traditions, overtones coaxed out, but a true dyad — two independent pitches, equally voiced, simultaneously sustained—" He was already hurrying down the steps. "That requires resonance architecture no unaided body possesses. Unless."

"Unless," Maren said quietly.

"Unless the body isn't unaided," Ovid finished. He circled me like I was a manuscript with a disputed line. "Sing the lower note again. Both of you, together. And Mara — don't think about your throat. Think about whatever it is you taste."

So I thought about bread.

I thought about the loaf in Ilsa's kitchen, the wheat remembering rain, the miller's flour dusted with grief for his drowned brother, the yeast working in the dark like something faithful. I thought about how a flavor was never one thing — it was a chord of histories, and my tongue had always heard every strand separately, all at once, without effort, without confusion.

I stopped trying to make one note and let myself hear the two notes the way I tasted bread: as layers. As a history with more than one thread.

The second note came up out of me like water finding a crack.

I felt it happen more than heard it — a splitting somewhere behind my sternum, painless but profound, as if a door in my chest that had been painted shut for thirty-one years swung open onto a room I'd never known I owned. Two tones left my mouth at once, independent, equal, locked together like hands. The lower one mine. The upper one also mine. Both of them mine.

The amphitheater did something to the sound. The bowl caught our voices — mine doubled, Maren's threading beneath — and returned them changed, larger, layered with harmonics I wasn't producing and couldn't explain, as if the room itself had been waiting decades for exactly this interval and leapt at the chance to complete it.

The twins stood up.

They didn't speak — I would learn they rarely did, preferring to let their harmony carry what words couldn't — but they stood, both at once, mirror-smooth, and began to sing. They added a fourth and a fifth, and the chord thickened, and the moss on the walls seemed to shiver. The silver-haired lute player set down her instrument and simply listened with her eyes closed. Even Juniper had gone still, her chin lifted, her expression stripped of its usual armor.

And beneath it all, faint but unmistakable, the mountain hummed back.

I held the two notes until my breath ran out, and when the sound finally faded, the silence afterward was the fullest silence I had ever stood inside. Dust motes hung in the shaft of light from the ceiling crack like suspended applause.

Ovid broke it, voice shaking. "A keeper's interval. That is a keeper's interval. The last person documented to hold a true dyad was Keeper Ellin Voss, and that was—" he flipped through mental pages, "—ninety years ago. Before the decay accelerated. Before any of this." He gripped my shoulder with papery fingers. "Do you understand what you are?"

"A taster," I said weakly.

"You are a *keystone-shaped hole in the world*," Ovid said, "that has just started to remember its shape."

Murmurs ran through the rows. I caught fragments — *dyad*, *covenant-born*, *the valley*, *of course the valley* — and none of it meant anything to me except that the room's temperature toward me had shifted. Yesterday I had been Sable's stray. Today I was something else, and I couldn't tell yet if the something else was better.

Juniper came down the steps last. She stopped in front of me, arms crossed, and studied my face with those flat gray eyes.

"Two notes," she said. "At once. Without training. On your first morning."

"I didn't know I wasn't supposed to."

Something twitched at the corner of her mouth — not quite a smile, but the ghost of where one might eventually be built. "Of course you didn't. Valley-raised. Nobody ever told you the rules, so you never learned to be afraid of breaking them." She stuck out her hand, abrupt, as if the gesture cost her. "Juniper. I sing the parts nobody wants. High descant, mostly. It hurts."

"Mara. I taste what food remembers."

"That's the stupidest gift I've ever heard."

"Thank you," I said, and shook her hand, and this time the ghost of the smile actually arrived, brief as a struck match.

* * *

That evening Maren found me on the stage, running my fingers along the pale wood, trying to feel the centuries in it the way I'd feel them in bread. The amphitheater was nearly empty; the Choir had dispersed to supper and argument and the small labors of a hidden life.

"You felt it," she said, sitting beside me without ceremony. "When the second note came."

"I felt a door open."

"And now you're wondering what's behind the rest of them." She followed my hand to the wood. "This floor was built by the last generation of keepers. They raised it to resonate with the keystone song — the one that holds the covenant. When you sang today, the floor recognized something in you. So did the mountain. So did I." She was quiet a moment. "I won't lie to you, Mara. Voices like yours appear when the covenant is failing. They always have. A keeper rises when the lock begins to rust."

"Then the stories are true? The covenant is really dying?"

"The blight says so. The hunters say so. The king says so, loudly, and with a bounty attached." Her jaw tightened. "What the king doesn't say — what most of us only suspect — is *why* it's failing. Covenants don't rot on their own. They're maintained. Or they're robbed." She stood, brushing off her robes, and looked down at me with those river-stone eyes. "Rest tonight. Tomorrow, Ovid will want to talk your ear off, and Juniper will want to work you until you bleed, and the twins will want to feed you, because that's how they love people. Let them. You've spent your whole life being careful, Mara. You're among people who were careful too, right up until the day being careful stopped being enough."

She paused at the edge of the stage.

"One more thing. The dyad you held — the upper note. Did it feel like yours?"

I thought about it honestly. The lower note had been mine, rooted in my chest. But the upper one — the upper one had felt borrowed. Felt *remembered*. As if it had been living somewhere else for a long time and had only now come home.

"No," I said slowly. "It felt like it knew me better than I know it."

Maren held my gaze for a long moment, and whatever she saw there made her expression go carefully, deliberately blank.

"Then tomorrow," she said, "you should hear what Ovid knows about songs that outlive their singers."

Behind us, in the emptying amphitheater, the mountain hummed on — and for the first time, I noticed that its long, patient drone wasn't one note at all.

It was two. And the higher one was the note I'd held.
