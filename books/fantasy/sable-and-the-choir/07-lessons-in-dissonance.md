## 7. Lessons in Dissonance

The amphitheater had a name once. Ovid said it was carved into the mountain's shoulder four hundred years ago, when the covenant was young and the singers who kept it needed a place to practice without cracking the bedrock. Now the seats were moss-grown and half-swallowed by scree, and the stage was a circle of flat stone worn smooth by generations of bare feet.

Mara stood on that stone on his first morning and felt like a mouse standing in a hawk's nest.

"Again," Sable said from the front row.

He'd been trying to hold a single note for what felt like an hour. Not sing it—hold it, the way you'd hold a cup of water very still so nothing spilled. The Choir's craft wasn't performance. It was containment. Every note was a vessel, and into the vessel you poured intention until the air itself remembered what you wanted it to remember.

His note wobbled and died.

"You're swallowing it," said a voice from the third row, dry as old paper. "Like everything else, I'd wager."

The speaker was a woman about his age with copper-red hair cropped short and a scar splitting one eyebrow. She sat with her boots up on the seat in front of her, which no one else dared do, and she was eating an apple with the focused hostility of someone who found apples personally offensive.

"Juniper," Sable said, in the tone of a warning.

"What? I'm being encouraging." Juniper took another bite. "That was better than yesterday. Yesterday he sounded like a kettle dying."

* * *

The days found their shape quickly, and the shape was failure.

Mornings were breath-work with Sable: learning to pull air from the belly instead of the chest, learning that the Choir's singing began below the voice entirely, in something Sable called the root. "You've been tasting with your tongue your whole life," she told him, pressing two fingers against his sternum until he flinched. "This is where you taste with everything else."

Midday was theory with Ovid, who talked in spirals. Mara learned the covenant was not one song but a weave of them—a hundred melodies braided through the valley's borders, each one anchored to a keeper who could hold it steady. He learned the word *dissonance* meant something different here than it did in the palace choir loft he'd once sneaked past as a boy. In the Choir's grammar, dissonance was not error. It was tension held on purpose, the ache between two notes that made the resolution mean something.

"The crown teaches harmony as agreement," Ovid said, tapping a diagram scratched into slate. "Everything in its place, nothing questioning. But a chord where every note is identical isn't harmony. It's silence wearing a costume."

Afternoons were practice, and practice was public, because the amphitheater had no walls and the Choir had no patience for privacy. Mara would stand on the stage-stone and attempt exercises that the others had mastered as children, and they would watch, and he would fail, and then he would do it again.

It was the twin brothers, Hallow and Fen, who taught him the most by saying the least. They spoke only in unison—or rather, they spoke only in harmony, one a fifth above the other, so that even their ordinary speech sounded like a small strange hymn. They corrected his pitch by singing it back at him, wrong note for wrong note, until he heard his own error reflected and flinched away from it.

"They're showing off," Juniper said, dropping onto the bench beside him after one such session. "They love a new toy."

"I'm not a toy."

"No." She looked at him sideways, apple juice glistening on her chin. "You're a problem. You can hold two notes at once and you can't hold one. Do you understand how infuriating that is? Fen nearly wept. And Fen has never wept at anything except the death of his favorite goat, and that was Hallow doing the weeping while Fen sang the lament, because that's how they mourn."

Despite himself, Mara laughed—the first real laugh since the valley. It came out rusty and surprised him.

Juniper pointed the apple core at him like a verdict. "There. That. Whatever that noise was, keep making it. It's the first honest sound you've produced all week."

* * *

The gift itself was harder.

Sable's method was simple and merciless: eat, and tell us what it remembers. She brought him things from the Choir's stores—dried plums, hard cheese, a heel of black bread—and sat across from him with her arms folded while he closed his eyes and listened.

Food remembered the way river stones remembered water. A plum remembered the orchard's long drought, the farmer's hands shaking as he picked the last fruit. Cheese remembered a cellar and a cat and a girl humming. Bread remembered wheat fields and millstones and—

And sometimes, underneath, it remembered the valley. The dampened hush of it. The feeling Mara had carried all his life without naming: that the world behind the valley's rim was muffled, wrapped in wool, a song played through a wall.

Out here, on the mountain, the same bread tasted loud. Everything was loud. His gift, freed from the covenant's suppression, had swollen from a whisper to a shout, and some days he couldn't eat at all because every mouthful screamed its history at him.

"You're not listening," Sable said. "You're bracing. There's a difference."

"I know the difference. I just—" He set down the bread. "In the valley, my gift was a candle. Here it's a bonfire, and everyone keeps asking me to cook with it."

"So learn to bank it." Her one eye was steady on him. "You survived twenty-some years in the most dangerous kitchen in Vell by controlling yourself. Same skill. Bigger fire."

She was right, and knowing she was right didn't help, and that was the shape of most of his lessons those first weeks.

* * *

It was Juniper who finally broke him open—not with kindness, but with irritation.

They were alone in the amphitheater at dusk, working on a round called the Lattice, a three-part exercise meant to teach a singer to split attention. Mara kept collapsing the parts together. His voice wanted to be one thing; the Lattice demanded three.

"You're doing it again," Juniper snapped. "You're holding back."

"I'm trying—"

"No, you're *managing*. There's a difference, and I'm sick of watching you make it." She stood, pacing the stage-stone in tight circles. "Every exercise, you get ninety percent there and then you flinch. Like you're waiting for someone to punish you for succeeding. What are you afraid of?"

"That's none of—"

"It's entirely my business. If you crack during a working, it's my voice holding the line next to yours. So." She stopped in front of him, arms crossed. "What are you afraid of?"

And Mara, exhausted and homesick and tired of the taste of his own caution, heard himself say:

"I spent nine years feeding lies to a king."

The words fell into the amphitheater's bowl of stone and echoed back at him.

Juniper went still. "What does that mean?"

"I was the royal taster. Mara Nakamura, taster to the king's table. I ate poison so the crown wouldn't have to fear it. And before you say it—I know what the crown is. I knew. I watched them levy grain from villages that were already starving. I watched the hunt proclamations go up. I served their meals anyway, because serving was safe, because safety was the whole architecture of my life." His voice cracked on the last word. "I didn't leave because I grew a conscience. I left because they would have killed me. That's the truth of it. I'm not a hero who fled tyranny. I'm a coward who got lucky."

The silence afterward was enormous. Somewhere above, wind moved through the pines, and the sound seemed to lean in and listen.

Juniper sat down slowly on the stage-stone beside him.

"Do you know why I was exiled?" she said at last. "I sang at a wedding. A lord's wedding, in the lowlands, before I found the Choir. And mid-ceremony my gift slipped, and the bride heard—in the music—what her groom had done to his first wife. Everyone heard it. The whole hall." She turned the apple core over in her fingers. "They didn't exile me for the magic. They exiled me for the truth. Said I'd poisoned the occasion."

Mara looked at her.

"So when I say you're managing," Juniper said, "understand that I recognize the disease. I spent two years after that apologizing for having a voice. You spent nine years apologizing for having a tongue. We're the same sickness, you and I. The difference is I got angry about it and you got careful, and careful will kill you faster."

"Anger almost got you killed too."

"Yes." She smiled, sharp and sudden. "But look how interesting my scars are."

* * *

He confessed to the rest of them three nights later, because Juniper told him he had to, and because keeping it secret felt like carrying water in his hands up a long hill.

He stood on the stage-stone after the evening meal, with the whole Choir gathered in the crescent of seats—Sable, Ovid, the twins, Juniper, old Maren whose voice had gone gravelly but whose ear missed nothing, and a dozen others whose names he was still learning. He told them plainly. Taster to the king. Nine years. Fed the crown's table while the crown starved the valley's edge. Left not out of conscience but out of fear.

"I'm not asking you to trust me," he finished. "Trust is earned, and I haven't earned anything here yet. I'm just telling you what I am, so that whatever you decide about me, you're deciding about the truth."

The quiet stretched.

Old Maren spoke first, her gravel voice rolling up from the second row. "My son was taken in the second hunt. Twenty years ago. They never told us where." She said it without heat, a fact laid on the table like a stone. "You fed the men who did the taking."

"Yes," Mara said. "I did."

Hallow and Fen rose together, harmonizing even in silence, and walked out of the amphitheater without looking back. It was, Mara would learn later, not condemnation so much as abstention—they withheld judgment the way they withheld single notes, on principle. But that night it felt like a door closing.

Others stayed. Others asked questions, hard ones, and he answered them all, and when it was done maybe half the Choir nodded at him in passing and the other half looked through him, and both halves, he understood, were giving him exactly what he'd asked for: truth for truth.

Sable was the last to leave. She paused at the bottom of the stage-stone.

"You know what I was going to do with you, that first day at the shrine," she said. "For about an hour, I genuinely considered the bounty."

"I know."

"And then you were shivering and asking me if *I* was hurt, and I thought—there's a man who's been guarding something his whole life and never once guarded himself." She climbed the steps away from him, then paused again. "Maren's right. You did feed them. And you're right too—you ran for your life, not your soul. Both things are true. The Choir runs on both things being true. Get used to living in the dissonance."

* * *

Something shifted after that, though not all at once, and not for everyone.

But Juniper started sitting with him at meals, narrating the Choir's gossip like a war correspondent. Maren began correcting his breath-work with a gentleness she showed no one else. And one gray morning, working the Lattice for perhaps the hundredth time, Mara stopped flinching.

He didn't plan it. He simply reached the point in the exercise where the three parts diverged, and instead of pulling them back together into safety, he let them go—held the root note low and steady, let the second rise, let the third split off entirely, and found, to his astonishment, that he could stand inside all three at once. Not juggling them. *Being* them. Three doors open in one room, wind moving through.

The sound that came out of him was small and strange and utterly new: a chord with one throat.

Juniper dropped her apple.

"Say something," Mara managed, when the note finally released him, "so I know the world's still there."

"You absolute—" She was laughing, actually laughing, scrambling down the rows toward him. "Do you have any idea—Ovid! OVID! Get up here, you fossil, you have to see—"

Later, much later, when the amphitheater had emptied and the stars were out in their cold multitudes, Juniper sat beside him on the stage-stone and bumped her shoulder against his.

"For what it's worth," she said, "the twins came back. They watched from the ridge. They don't do that for anyone."

Mara looked up at the dark line of the ridge, where two silhouettes might or might not have been standing. "Then maybe I'm earning it."

"Don't get sentimental. It'll ruin your pitch." But she bumped his shoulder again and left it there, and they sat in the dissonance of the night—crickets and wind and the far-off groan of the glacier—all of it slightly, wonderfully off-key.

It was three days later that Ovid came down from the archive with a slate in his hands and an expression that drained the warmth out of the morning. He set the slate down in front of Sable, and Mara, reading upside down, saw a map of the lowlands with one village circled twice.

"Crops dying in rows," Ovid said. "But not the way crops die. Sable—the field hands say the wheat was *singing* before it failed. Off-key."

Sable looked up, and her eye found Mara's across the fire pit.

"Pack for travel," she said. "All of us. It's time he heard what the blight sounds like."
