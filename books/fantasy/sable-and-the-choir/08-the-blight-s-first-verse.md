## 8. The Blight's First Verse

Juniper had sung at three funerals before she turned fifteen, and she knew the sound a village made when it was already dead. It wasn't silence. Silence was clean, restful, the pause between verses. This was different. This was the held breath of people waiting for something worse to arrive.

The village was called Bracken Hollow, though the sign at its edge had been defaced so thoroughly that Sable had to confirm the name from her old hunter's maps. The fields around it were the reason they'd come: word had traveled up the mountain that the crops here sang.

Not metaphorically. Not in the way farmers' wives said the wheat whispered. The Choir's scouts—Ovid's correspondence network of disgraced archivists and one very talkative beekeeper—had reported it plainly. *The barley sings off-key. Then it dies.*

Juniper heard it before she saw it. They crested the last hill above the valley floor, Mara beside her with his pack straps creaking, and there it was: a thin, wavering tone rising off the fields like heat-shimmer made audible. A note that couldn't decide what it wanted to be. It slid between pitches the way a dying man slides between tenses—*I was, I am, I was*—

"Stop," Ovid said behind them. "Everyone stop."

They stopped. The twins, Bramble and Thorn, went still as fence posts, their shared attention narrowing to a point. Even Sable's hand drifted to the knife at her hip, though a knife was useless against a wrong note.

"What is it?" Juniper asked.

"It's not fraying," Ovid said. His voice had gone strange, flat, the scholar's voice he used when the facts were too large for feeling. "Fraying has a texture. I've read the accounts of the last decay, two hundred years ago. Fraying sounds like cloth tearing. Like threads parting." He pointed at the fields with a hand that wasn't quite steady. "That sounds like someone singing badly on purpose."

Mara made a small sound. Juniper looked at him. He'd gone pale—not the pale of fear, but the pale of recognition, the color draining out of a face when the body understands something before the mind consents to.

"You hear it too," she said.

"I taste it," he said. And then, because he was still Mara, still embarrassed by his own gift even after weeks among them: "Sorry. I know that doesn't make sense."

"It makes perfect sense," Ovid said. "Everything that grows carries the covenant in it. Every root is a thread in the weave. If you can read what things remember—" He stopped himself, visibly recalibrating, the way he did when he was about to ask someone for something he had no right to ask. "Would you? When we get down there. Would you read the soil?"

Mara looked at the fields. At the village beyond them, its chimneys smoking thin and gray, its lanes empty in the middle of the day. At the shrine at the crossroads with its offerings scattered by crows.

"Yeah," he said. "Yeah, I will."

Juniper walked the rest of the way down beside him and did not say what she was thinking, which was that he looked like a man walking toward his own execution and trying to be polite about it.

* * *

Bracken Hollow received them the way a body receives a leech—with wary, exhausted tolerance. An old woman named Maren met them at the well, arms crossed, eyes going over Sable's hunter's bearing, the twins' matched faces, Mara's soft hands.

"Tax collectors?"

"No," Sable said.

"Hunters?"

"No."

"Then what are you?"

"Singers," Juniper said, before she could stop herself.

Something moved through the gathered faces then—half a dozen villagers who had drifted close to listen, drawn by the novelty of strangers. It wasn't hope. Hope required belief in the possibility of good outcomes. It was more like hunger briefly remembered in a starving man.

"The crops started singing in the spring," Maren said. She spoke the way people speak when they've told a story too many times and it has worn smooth as river stone. "First just the east field. A hum. We thought it was bees. Then the whole valley floor, all at once, and the note was—" She faltered. Her hands, crossed so firmly a moment ago, unclenched and re-clenched. "My grandmother was a hedge-witch back when such things were only whispered about. Before the edicts. She said everything alive holds a note. That the old songs kept them in tune with each other. She said if you ever heard the fields singing wrong, you should run."

"Why didn't you run?" Thorn asked. Or Bramble. Juniper still couldn't always tell them apart when they weren't speaking together, which was most of the time now; they'd been rationing their harmony since the ford, since Wren, since the fracture.

"Where?" Maren said simply. "The blight's on every road south. The mountains are full of hunters. My husband's buried under the yew tree, and my daughter's under him, and my granddaughter—" She stopped. Started again, flatter. "We stayed. You stay until staying kills you. That's farming."

It was Mara who asked the question that mattered. Quietly, in his careful food-taster's voice, the voice he used for asking permission: "May I see the fields?"

* * *

He knelt in the east field and took a handful of soil like a man taking a handful of grain to check for rot, and Juniper watched him do the thing she had seen him do only twice before, always reluctantly, always wincing: he opened his gift all the way.

She felt it happen. Everyone did. The air changed pressure, the way it does before a storm. The twins swayed slightly, in opposite directions, correcting each other without touching. Ovid made a sound like a man reading a sentence he'd spent his life dreading.

And Mara—Mara closed his eyes and put the soil to his lips.

What he tasted, he told them afterward, in fragments, over two days, and Juniper assembled it later the way you assemble a shattered pot, matching edges, filling gaps with wax:

He tasted the covenant first. Not as an idea—as a flavor. A deep bass note underneath everything, older than the kingdom, holding every growing thing in Bracken Hollow in relation to every other thing. The barley knew the yew tree. The yew tree knew the well water. The well water remembered rain that fell before the crown existed. All of it woven, all of it keyed to the same fundamental tone, and the tone was—

"Failing," he said, kneeling there in the dirt with soil on his lips and tears running freely down his face, unaware of them, past caring. "It's failing note by note. Not fraying. Being *unpicked*. Someone is pulling threads. Deliberately. In sequence. There's an order to it, there's a—a melody missing, and everything else is straining to fill the gap and can't, and the strain is the singing, the wrongness is the singing, they're not cursed, they're *calling*, they're calling for the note that isn't there anymore—"

"Mara." Sable's voice, low and urgent. "Mara, come back."

But he wasn't finished. He swallowed, and Juniper saw his throat work, saw him take in more of it deliberately, greedily, a starving man at a feast of grief—and then he went rigid.

"There's a child," he said.

* * *

The child's name was Wren—no, that was the hunter, the name collided in Juniper's memory and she corrected herself—the child's name was Pip. Seven years old. Granddaughter to Maren, daughter to the grave under the yew tree. The village had been hiding her for four months.

"She started humming in her sleep," Maren said, in the dark of the cottage, while Pip herself sat on the bed with her knees pulled up, watching them with the flat, practiced blankness of a child who has learned that being interesting is dangerous. "Humming in her sleep, and the bread rose better on those mornings. And then the barley near our fence line grew a hand's height taller than the rest, and I knew. I knew what she was. So we taught her to be quiet. We taught her to be nothing." Maren's voice cracked on the last word and she pushed through it. "And then the king's men came through in autumn and asked questions, and I knew quiet wouldn't be enough forever."

Sable stood by the door with her back against it, and Juniper watched her face do the thing it did whenever children and hunters occupied the same thought: the scarred composure cracking along old fault lines. Sable had been a hunter. Sable had knocked on doors like this one. Whatever arithmetic she was doing behind her eyes, it was costing her.

"How long do we have," Sable said, "before they come back?"

"A fortnight. Maybe less. They left a notice." Maren handed over a printed sheet, and Ovid took it to the lamp and read aloud, his voice flattening further with each line: *"By order of the Crown, any person harboring a gifted individual commits treason against the realm. The gift must be surrendered for assessment. Assessment protects the gifted. Assessment protects everyone."*

"'Assessment,'" Juniper repeated. The word tasted like the blighted fields sounded.

"They took the miller's boy in the next valley," Maren said. "For assessment. He came back three weeks later and didn't speak for a month, and when he speaks now, he says the same sentence over and over. 'I sang where they told me.' That's all. 'I sang where they told me.'"

Silence in the cottage. Pip hadn't moved, hadn't blinked, had made herself so small and silent that Juniper kept losing sight of her against the dark bedding, and that, more than anything Maren had said, decided it.

Juniper looked at Sable. Sable looked at the child. And whatever argument might have happened—the risk, the numbers, the hunters, the ford still fresh in everyone's memory—didn't happen, because Bramble and Thorn stepped forward together, in unison, and said, as one voice:

"She comes with us."

* * *

They left Bracken Hollow at dawn, taking the high track north, away from the roads the hunters used. Pip walked between the twins, holding a hand of each, and Juniper watched the child slowly, cautiously, begin to hum.

It was barely anything. Three notes, wandering, uncertain—a child testing ice. But the land answered. Grass bent toward her in a wind that wasn't blowing. A lark descended and circled twice, keeping time. And the wrongness in the air behind them, the dying village's strained off-key cry, seemed—for a moment, just a moment—to ease, as if the smallest voice had given the broken chorus somewhere to lean.

Mara stopped walking. Juniper stopped with him.

"That's it," he whispered. "That's the answer. It's not one keeper. It never was. Look at her—one small voice, and everything leans toward it. The covenant was never meant to be carried alone. That's why it broke. That's why it's breaking. It was stolen and hoarded and bound into one anthem for one crown, and a song built for a choir can't live in a single throat—"

"Mara." Juniper caught his arm. "Breathe."

But he was looking at the child, at the lark, at the grass, and Juniper recognized the expression because she'd seen it in mirrors after her own exile: the look of someone whose grief has just found a job.

"We have to go to the capital," he said.

Sable turned around slowly. "No."

"The keystone song is there. In the anthem. Ovid proved it—we *know* it—"

"And the capital is a furnace, Mara. Hunters, wards, the whole weight of the crown between us and that gallery. We're eight people and a child. We go back, we dig in, we learn what you learned today, we find another way—"

"There is no other way!" It burst out of him louder than Juniper had ever heard him speak, and Pip flinched, and the lark scattered, and Mara immediately looked stricken—but he didn't stop. "You tasted what I tasted. You *heard* the fields. Every day we wait, another village starts singing wrong. Another child learns to be nothing. I spent ten years tasting that court's food and calling it survival. I'm done surviving. I'm done teaching children to be quiet."

The high track was silent except for wind. Sable stared at him, and Juniper watched the war behind her eyes—the hunter's caution against something older and less forgiving, the memory of every door she'd ever knocked on.

It was Pip who broke the standoff. She tugged the twins' hands and looked up at Mara with the frank, unnerving directness of the very young, and asked:

"If we sing the right song, will the fields stop crying?"

Mara knelt down to her level. When he answered, his voice was steady and terrible and certain.

"Yes."

Pip nodded once, satisfied, as though a bargain had been struck. Then she took his hand, and started walking north, toward the capital, toward the furnace, towing a grown man and two mute twins and the entire argument behind her like a small determined anchor.

Sable watched them go. Closed her eyes. Opened them.

"God help us," she said. "We'll need supplies."

Juniper grinned despite everything, despite the dread coiling cold in her stomach, and fell into step beside Mara.

Under her breath, so only he could hear, she began to hum the first phrase of the true keystone—the fragment Ovid had reconstructed from the archive, the melody the crown had stolen. Testing it. Learning it.

And far below them, in the fields of Bracken Hollow, the dying barley seemed to lean after them, listening.
