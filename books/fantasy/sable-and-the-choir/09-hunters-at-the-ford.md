## 9. Hunters at the Ford

The river told on them before anything else did.

Sable heard it at dawn, crouched over the water with her palms flat against a boulder, feeling the current's stutter where stones had been shifted by boots—many boots, days old but fresh enough for her to read like a ledger. Three men, two dogs, moving downstream toward the ford. They'd been waiting.

She straightened and looked back at the camp. Mara was asleep against Juniper's shoulder, both of them sitting up like they'd fallen that way mid-conversation, which knowing them they had. Ovid was already awake, of course, hunched over his notes with his spectacles fogged in the cold. The twins slept in perfect mirrored curls, breathing in unison, which still unsettled her after three weeks on the road.

They'd left the village four days ago with the child, Wren's name for her now though no one had claimed it yet—no. Not Wren. The child had no name anyone had given; the Choir called her Sparrow because she ate like one and sang like something much larger. She slept in the crook of Ovid's arm now, and Sable felt the old familiar arithmetic begin: five singers, one scholar, one child who could not run fast, and hunters between them and the high pass.

She had led hunts like this once. She knew exactly how it would go.

* * *

"Wake them," she told Ovid quietly. "No fire this morning. We cross at the narrows instead of the ford."

Ovid looked up, and she watched him do the same arithmetic and arrive at the same sum. "You're certain?"

"I can smell their dogs." That wasn't quite true. What she could do was remember being the dog-handler, remember how you positioned your lines at a crossing because a crossing is where fugitives get stupid—where the choice is taken out of their hands and all that's left is the water. "The ford is a trap. The narrows are fast water but shallow banks. If we go through the gorge we come out below their position."

"And if they've thought of that too?"

"Then I'll be very embarrassed."

He almost smiled. It was the most she got from him before noon.

Mara woke without being shaken—he'd developed that habit on the road, surfacing at any change in the camp's rhythm—and when he saw her face he didn't ask questions. He just started rolling bedding. She caught Juniper watching her with those sharp green eyes that missed nothing, and Juniper said nothing either, but the look lasted long enough to say: *you know these hunters. You know this hunt.*

Yes. She did.

* * *

The narrows were everything she remembered from the survey maps and worse in person. The river compressed between granite walls barely a spear's length apart, running white and loud, and the only crossing was a tumble of fallen stone that the water had spent a century trying to swallow. Sable went first, testing each block with her weight, calling the holds back over the roar. Ovid carried Sparrow strapped to his chest like cargo, which the child protested loudly until the spray hit and then she went silent and wide-eyed and gripped his collar with both fists.

Juniper crossed third, sure-footed as a goat and twice as smug about it. The twins went together, always together, one steadying the other in a way that made Sable's chest ache with something she refused to name.

Mara came last.

He was halfway across when the horns sounded.

Two notes, high and cutting, from the ridge above the ford—their original position—which meant the hunters had split their line, which meant Sable had been wrong, or half-wrong, which meant someone down there had read her the way she read rivers. She knew only one person who hunted like that.

"No," she said, to no one, to herself, to the woman she was about to see again for the first time in six years.

The second horn answered from downstream. Below the narrows. In front of them.

"Off the rocks!" she shouted. "Water side! Get low!"

It was already too late for clean tactics. The dogs came first—she heard them before she saw them, that deep belling bay that she'd trained into her own line years ago, a sound designed to freeze prey in place. Two brindle hounds broke from the alder scrub on the downstream bank and stopped at the waterline, pacing, unwilling to enter the current. Behind them came four hunters in grey-and-crimson, crossbows shouldered, spreading into the formation she could have drawn in her sleep.

And behind them, unhurried, came Wren.

* * *

Six years, and she walked the same. That was the thing that hit Sable hardest—not the scar through Wren's eyebrow that hadn't been there before, not the captain's clasp at her throat where there used to be a sergeant's knot, but the walk. That easy rolling stride, weight forward, hands loose. The walk of someone who had never once doubted she was the most dangerous thing in the valley.

"Sable Ashgrave," Wren called, and her voice carried over the water the way trained voices do. "Or whatever you're calling yourself now. The Choir's little mother."

"Captain Vess." Sable stood up slowly on the crossing stones, putting herself between the hunters' line and her people. "Or whatever they're calling you now."

Wren laughed. Some part of Sable hated how good it was to hear.

"You know how this goes," Wren said. "Four crossbows, two hounds, and you standing on wet rock in the middle of a river. I've read this page before. Usually it ends with somebody in irons."

"Usually it ends with somebody dead," Sable said. "That was the old book."

"The old book kept the realm fed." Wren tilted her head, studying the people behind her—Sable watched her catalog them the way she'd been trained to, threat assessment, escape routes, leverage. Her gaze snagged on Sparrow, strapped to Ovid's chest, and something moved behind her eyes that Sable recognized and wished she didn't. "A taster, a thief, a pair of songbirds, and a baby. You've gone soft, Ashgrave."

"I've gone accurate. There's a difference."

"Is there?" Wren took two steps closer to the water's edge. The hounds flanked her, quivering. "Because from where I stand, you're harboring gifted fugitives in a realm under edict, and every one of them is worth more than my yearly commission. I could bring you in alive. That's the offer. The crown wants the taster especially—there's a bounty on him with his own line item." She smiled, and it was the old smile, the one from the mess tent, the one that used to mean *we're on the same side of the joke.* "Come home, Sable. Bring the boy. Let the rest scatter. Nobody has to drown today."

Behind her, Sable heard Juniper shift, heard Ovid murmur something low to Sparrow, heard the twins' breath sync into that harmony they fell into under stress—a thin, keening note that made the nearest hound whimper and back a step.

And she heard Mara.

He had come off the rocks without her noticing, waded into the shallows at the bank, and knelt down with both hands in the water. His eyes were closed. His lips were moving.

Sable understood a heartbeat later what he was doing, and it nearly buckled her knees. He was tasting the river. Reading what it remembered—the dogs' passage upstream, the hunters' sweat, the oil on the crossbow strings, the whole recent history of the water—and he was singing it back. Quietly. Under the roar. A thread of sound pitched to carry exactly as far as a hound's ears.

The dogs went mad.

Not at the Choir—at each other. The nearer hound spun and snapped at its partner, then at the air, then at its own handler's boot, whining, circling, its tracking certainty shattered by a scent-song that said *prey everywhere, prey nowhere, prey in the water, prey in the sky.* The second dog bolted for the treeline. The handlers shouted, grabbed leads, lost them.

"What in the—" One of the crossbows swung toward the bank, toward Mara kneeling in the shallows.

Sable moved before she decided to. Three stones down the crossing, knife out of her boot and thrown before her foot landed—it buried itself in the crossbow's stock, splitting the wood an inch from the wielder's thumb. Not a kill shot. A statement.

"That's far enough," she said, and her voice came out with the old command in it, the voice that had once moved fifty hunters at a word, and she hated that it still worked, hated that the young hunter flinched, hated that Wren's hand dropped to her sword and stopped there.

For a moment nobody breathed except the river.

Then Wren laughed again, but differently this time. Lower. Almost wondering. "Your taster sings to dogs."

"He does a lot of things."

"You're protecting him." Wren's eyes flicked past Sable, doing sums. "All of them. You, who filed the report on the Marshfield cell yourself. Who signed the order that put eleven singers in the pits at Corren. You're standing in a river for them."

"Yes."

"Why?"

It was the question Sable had spent six years not answering, and here it was, standing in the water between them wearing a captain's clasp. She thought about lying. She thought about the speech she'd rehearsed in the dark of a hundred campsites, the accounting she owed.

"Because I counted," she said. "After Corren. I counted the names. Eleven. And I realized I couldn't remember a single one of their faces, because I never let myself look. I was very good at not looking, Wren. You taught me half of it." She held the other woman's gaze. "I'm looking now."

Something crossed Wren's face—fast, quickly killed, but Sable had known her too long and too well to miss it. Grief. Or its twin.

"Lower the bows," Wren said.

"Captain—" the youngest hunter started.

"I said lower them." And they did, and Sable watched the order cost Wren something, watched her weigh it and pay it, and understood that whatever happened next, a door had just opened that could not be closed again.

"We'll regroup at the ford," Wren said loudly, for her hunters' benefit. Then, quieter, pitched only for Sable across the water: "There are nine more lines in these hills by week's end. This was the polite one. Take your people east through the gorge and don't stop until the pass. And Sable—" her jaw worked, "—the bounty on the taster isn't the crown's idea alone. Someone at court wants him found *before* the coronation. Badly. Ask your scholar why."

She turned. The hounds—both of them, recovered now, confused, chastened—fell in at her heel. At the treeline she paused, half-turned, and for one unguarded moment she looked less like a captain and more like the sergeant who had once shared Sable's fire for seven years.

"You looked," Wren said. "Good for you."

Then she was gone, and the forest swallowed the crimson and grey.

* * *

Nobody spoke for a long moment. The river roared. Somewhere upstream a jay scolded the silence.

"She let us go," Juniper said finally, flat with disbelief. "Just—walked away. Four crossbows and she walked away."

"She didn't walk away." Sable retrieved her knife from the ruined crossbow stock—no, that was downstream now, lost to the current, fine, let the river have it. "She made a choice she'll answer for. Which means next time it won't be her leading the line against us. It'll be someone with nothing to forgive themselves for." She turned and looked at her people, all of them, taking the count she'd failed to take at Corren. "Move. Now. Gorge east, double pace. We talk when we're not standing in a killing ground."

They moved. But she felt the looks pass between them—Juniper to Ovid, Ovid to the twins—and she knew what they were saying. *She knew the captain. The captain knew her. The captain warned us.*

Saved by an old debt. Bound by one, too. Trust was a strange currency: Wren's mercy had just bought them their lives and spent a portion of theirs.

* * *

They made the gorge by midafternoon and camped wet and exhausted in a hollow beneath an overhang, no fire, cold rations. Sparrow slept like the dead. The twins took first watch, standing shoulder to shoulder, humming so softly it was more vibration than sound.

Mara sat down beside Sable at the hollow's mouth, where she could watch the dark mouth of the gorge and pretend she wasn't shaking.

"You sang to the river," she said.

"I tasted it. The singing just... happened." He rubbed his arms against the chill. "The dogs weren't hunting us anymore. They were hunting a lie I gave them. Is that—is that allowed? Am I allowed to do that?"

"It saved our lives. It's allowed." She glanced at him. He looked older than he had a month ago, or maybe just less hidden. "You knelt in open water in front of four crossbows to do it. That was either very brave or very stupid."

"Juniper says those are the same thing."

"Juniper's usually right, which is insufferable."

He almost smiled. Then he didn't. "What she said. About the coronation. About someone wanting me found before it." He turned to look at her, and Sable saw the fear in him and recognized it as her own, reflected. "Why would the crown want a food-taster badly enough to put a private bounty on him? What do they think I am?"

Sable looked out at the gorge, at the dark coming down between the walls like water filling a cup, and thought about Ovid's archive, about the keystone song, about the anthem sung at every crowning for three hundred years. She thought about a boy who had swallowed the king's table daily for a decade.

"I don't know," she lied, gently, because he wasn't ready, and because she needed one more night to decide whether she was ready to tell him.

Above them, on the rim of the gorge, a single note sounded—one horn, far off, brief, like a question asked into the dark.

Not Wren's call-sign. Something else. Something new.

Sable was on her feet before it faded.
