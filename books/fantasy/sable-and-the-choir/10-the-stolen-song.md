## 10. The Stolen Song

Ovid had spent forty years in rooms like this one, and he had learned that archives do not keep secrets. They keep evidence. The difference is that secrets want to stay hidden, while evidence only waits for someone stubborn enough to read it.

He was, by every measure that mattered to the crown, a stubborn man.

The archive beneath the amphitheater was older than the Choir itself—older, he suspected, than the valley's covenant, though proving that had cost him his chair at the Royal Academy and very nearly his neck. It was a low vaulted space cut into living rock, its shelves carved from stone rather than wood because paper burned and stone did not. Lamps burned low along the walls, their light the color of old honey. The air tasted of dust and cold iron and, faintly, of song—the way a bell tastes of bronze long after it has stopped ringing.

Mara came down the stairs behind Sable, moving carefully, the way people moved in Ovid's archive when they understood they were somewhere important. Good. He liked visitors who were careful. Careful people read slowly, and slow readers found things.

"You wanted to show me something," Mara said. His voice was still rough from the testing two nights past, when the twins had coaxed that impossible doubled note out of him and the whole amphitheater had gone silent as a held breath.

"I wanted to show you everything," Ovid said. "But we'll start with one thing."

He crossed to the deepest shelf, the one cut directly into the mountain's spine, and drew out a case of black slate. Inside lay a cylinder of clay, fired hard, incised with grooves that spiraled around it like a vine. A song-cylinder. There were perhaps nine in existence that Ovid knew of, and this was the oldest.

"This is a transcription," he said. "The first kind ever made. Before notation, before staves, before anyone thought music could be written down on paper, we pressed songs into clay. You play them with—" he gestured at the apparatus beside the shelf, a wheel and stylus assembly that looked like nothing so much as a torture device designed by a musician, "—this. The stylus reads the grooves. The resonator gives back what was pressed in."

"Whose song is it?" Sable asked. She stood with her arms folded, one eye on Mara and one on the door, because Sable never gave both eyes to anything.

"That," said Ovid, "is the question I have spent my life answering."

* * *

He set the cylinder on the wheel with hands that shook—not from age, though he was old enough for shaking to be excusable, but from the particular tremor of a man about to say aloud the thing he had only ever said to shelves.

"Before the covenant, there were singers everywhere. Every village, every hold, every river-crossing had its voices. The songs bound things—not in the crude way your hunters' tales describe, not binding people, but binding *together*. Bound the fields to the rain. Bound the bridges to the stone beneath them. Bound neighbor to neighbor so that oaths held without chains." He turned the wheel a quarter-turn, seating the stylus. "The covenant was the greatest of these bindings. One song, sung across the whole valley, that quieted wild magic into something livable. Not dead. Livable. Do you understand the difference?"

Mara nodded slowly. "The valley isn't empty of magic. It's *held*."

"Yes." Ovid pointed at him. "Held. Like a note. And a held note requires a singer. So: who sang it? Who kept the covenant for the first three hundred years of Vell's history?"

"The Choir," Sable said.

"The Choir's *predecessors*. The Keepers. And here is where every history book in the kingdom goes quiet." Ovid released the brake on the wheel. "Listen."

The stylus dropped into the groove. The resonator—a horn of beaten copper—shivered, and a voice came out of it.

It was a woman's voice, or had been; four thousand years had worn it thin as frost. She sang a rising phrase, seven notes climbing like a stair, and then a turn, and then a fall that resolved into rest. Simple. Almost plain. But Ovid watched Mara's face as it played, and saw what he had hoped to see and dreaded to see: recognition. Not of the melody—no one alive knew the melody—but of its *shape*. The shape of something that fit a hole you'd carried all your life.

"What is that?" Mara whispered.

"That is the keystone verse. The heart of the covenant song. Pressed into clay by a Keeper named Ashai, three generations after the covenant was first sung, because she was afraid it would be forgotten." Ovid stopped the wheel. "She was right to be afraid. Listen to what came next."

He lifted the cylinder away and replaced it with a second, newer, cruder pressing. This one played a fragment of melody—seven notes climbing, a turn—and then broke off mid-phrase, the groove gouged deep as if the hand cutting it had pressed too hard.

"The same opening," Mara said. "Almost."

"*Almost*," Ovid agreed, and the word came out of him like a blade coming out of a wound. "Now. Where have you heard that almost-melody, taster? Think. You served at the king's table for years. What music does the court make?"

Mara went very still. Sable unfolded her arms.

"The coronation anthem," she said flatly.

"The coronation anthem," Ovid said. "Which opens with seven ascending notes and a turn—which is to say, with the keystone verse, truncated, transposed up a third to sit better under a boy soprano's range, and *cut off before resolution*. Do you know why it cuts off?" He didn't wait. "Because whoever stole it didn't understand it. They took the melody the way a thief takes a sword by the blade—grabbing the bright part and bleeding on the rest."

* * *

He walked them down the aisle between the shelves as he talked, because he could not tell this story standing still. Forty years of fury needed motion.

"Aldric the First. The man the histories call Aldric Unifier, Aldric Peacebringer, Aldric of the Bright Crown." He pulled a scroll from its case and let it unroll across a reading table: a copy of a copy of a copy, but the earliest line still legible, in a hand like bird tracks. "This is a merchant's ledger from the last year before the crowning. Ordinary entries—grain, wool, salt. And here." His finger stopped on a line. "*Paid to the King's Men, forty silver, for the taking of the Singers' Stone and the silencing thereof.*"

"Silencing," Sable repeated.

"The Keepers kept their songs on stone tablets in a sanctuary at the valley's head. When Aldric decided he would be crowned—not elected, not affirmed, *crowned*, a word no ruler in Vell had used before—he needed a ceremony grand enough to make the title real. And his chroniclers admit, in language so delicate you could breathe on it and break it, that the anthem composed for the occasion was 'drawn from the old harmonies.'" Ovid laughed, and there was no humor in it at all. "Drawn from. Stolen from. The keystone verse was carried out of that sanctuary and rewritten as a fanfare, and the sanctuary was burned, and the Keepers were scattered or worse, and within a generation the covenant began to fray. Because a covenant is not a wall. It is a *song*. And a song cannot be owned. It can only be sung—or withheld."

Mara's hand had gone to his own throat, unconsciously, the way a man touches an old scar. "Every crowning since—"

"Has re-sung the theft." Ovid nodded. "Once a generation, the whole realm gathers to hear the corrupted verse performed with full pomp, and each performance drives the wedge deeper. The anthem doesn't renew the covenant—it *replaces* it, note by note, crowning by crowning. The blight outside isn't the covenant failing on its own. It's the covenant being strangled slowly, in public, to applause." He looked at Mara. "You've heard the anthem. Tell me. In all your years at court, did anyone ever seem *happy* singing it?"

Mara was quiet a moment. "They sang it beautifully," he said finally. "And it always felt like a funeral."

"There it is." Ovid spread his hands. "Four thousand years of evidence, and it fits together like a joint cut true. The crown took the song. The crown broke the covenant. The crown has spent every century since blaming the fraying on heretics and gifted children and anyone else convenient—because the alternative is admitting that the throne itself is the wound."

Sable had gone to the second cylinder and was looking at the gouge where the melody broke off. Her voice, when it came, was very even, which Ovid had learned meant it was anything but. "I ran hunts for eleven years. I put gifted people in wagons. I told myself it was law." She touched the broken groove. "It was a cover-up. I was the cover-up."

"You were a tool," Ovid said. "Tools can be picked up or put down. The hand that held you doesn't get that mercy."

* * *

Mara had drifted to the far shelf, where Ovid kept the fragments—the orphaned pages, the half-legible rubbings, the things that proved nothing alone and everything together. He was holding a rubbing taken from a sanctuary stone, charcoal on cloth, showing a circle of figures with their mouths open and lines running from their throats into a single knot at the center.

"What happens if it's sung right?" Mara asked. "The keystone verse. Whole. Unbroken."

Ovid came to stand beside him. Up close, the boy looked tired in a way that sleep wouldn't fix; the doubled note had cost him something, and Ovid suspected he hadn't told Sable how much.

"If it were sung whole, by a keeper's voice, at the seat of the theft?" He tapped the knot at the center of the rubbing. "The covenant reforges. Not restored—reforged. Restored means putting the old thing back, and the old thing belonged to dead Keepers and couldn't survive this age anyway. Reforged means new. Shared. Sung by many voices so no single throat carries it." He paused. "That part is my theory. The evidence supports it. But theory is all it is, because there's a problem."

"The anthem," Sable said from behind them. "The next crowning. If the heir sings the corrupted verse again—"

"In five months, yes. And every caged singer the crown holds will be forced to lend their voices to it, which will drive the wedge deeper than any crowning before, because stolen songs sing loudest when the stolen are made to carry them." Ovid met Mara's eyes. "There's another problem. The harder one."

"Tell me," Mara said.

"The keystone verse isn't just a melody. It's a *substance*. The old Keepers spoke of it as something you could carry—in the blood, in the bone, in the body's memory. That's how a keeper was made: not trained, *steeped*. Years of hearing it, humming it, tasting it in bread and water and wine until it lived in you." Ovid watched the boy's face carefully. "Which raises a question I've been turning over since the night the twins pulled that second note out of you. A food-taster, raised inside a dampened valley, with no training, no inheritance, no teacher—and yet you can hold two notes at once. That shouldn't be possible, Mara. Unless somewhere, somehow, the keystone already got into you."

The lamp nearest them guttered, though there was no draft.

Mara looked down at his own hands—the hands that had tasted ten thousand meals, that had lifted fork and cup to a mouth that had swallowed the king's table daily for fifteen years. Ovid saw the exact moment the arithmetic finished. Saw the color leave his face and come back wrong.

"The royal table," Mara said quietly. "The feast days. The coronation anniversaries, when they always served from the same gold service, wine from the same cellars, bread marked with the same seal—" He laughed once, short and hollow. "I've been tasting it for years. I've been *swallowing* it."

Ovid said nothing. There was nothing kind to say and no point pretending otherwise. The truth sat in the lamplight between them, heavy as the slate case: the boy was not just a singer. He was a vessel. And vessels, in the old songs, had two uses.

Above them, faint through the rock, the Choir began evening practice—a warm scatter of voices finding each other in the dark, the sound of people who believed they had time.

Mara closed his hand around the charcoal rubbing until the knuckles went white.

"They'll need me to sing it," he said. "Won't they. And the old songs say what else a vessel is *for*."

Ovid reached out and steadied the lamp, because his hands wanted something to hold.
