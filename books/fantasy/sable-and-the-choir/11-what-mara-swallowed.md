## 11. What Mara Swallowed

The archive smelled of dust and old ink and something else—something that made the back of Mara's teeth hum.

He'd been coming down to Ovid's archive every evening for a week now, ever since the scholar had begun teaching him the covenant's structure: how it was built like a song with load-bearing walls, how each verse anchored a region of the realm, how the keystone verse—the one that held all the others in tune—had gone silent two hundred years ago. Ovid talked with his hands as much as his voice, sketching diagrams in spilled flour on the stone floor because parchment was too precious to waste on a man who learned by erasing.

Tonight Ovid wasn't drawing. He was standing very still beside a shelf of wax cylinders, holding one up to the lamplight, and he had the look of a man who has just done arithmetic he wishes came out differently.

"Mara," he said. "Tell me again about the palace kitchens."

Mara set down the tray of bread and cheese he'd brought down—old habit, feeding people—and felt the first cold thread of warning slide under his ribs. "You've heard it all. Twelve years. I tasted everything that passed the king's lips. Every sauce, every wine, every crumb of ceremonial bread."

"The ceremonial bread." Ovid turned the cylinder slowly. "Coronation bread?"

"There's only been one coronation in my time. The king's. I was nine when—" He stopped. Recalculated. "No. Before that. There were feast days. Remembrance days. Days when they baked the old recipe, the one from the founding, and no one remembered why anymore except that the recipe said *always*. I tasted it every year. It tasted like—"

"Like what?"

"Like singing," Mara said, before he could stop himself. "Like a note held so long you forget it started. Like something underneath everything else, humming."

Ovid closed his eyes. "Sit down."

"I'd rather not."

"*Sit down*, taster."

Mara sat. The stool was low and the lamplight was yellow and Ovid knelt in front of him with the wax cylinder cradled in both hands like an egg, and Mara understood, in the way you understand a fall only after it's already happened, that whatever came next would divide his life into before and after.

"When we found you at the ford," Ovid said, "when Sable brought you in and Juniper tested your voice—you held two notes. You know what that means. Only covenant-keepers could do that. Only people who carried the keystone."

"You told me. A fluke of bloodline. Some distant keeper in my family tree."

"That's what I told you because it was the kind thing to assume." Ovid's hands were steady but his voice wasn't. "I've spent the week proving otherwise. Listen." He set the cylinder on a brass machine with a horn like a lily, wound the crank, and lowered the needle.

A voice filled the archive. Thin, warped by age, but unmistakably a voice—a woman's, singing a rising phrase that seemed to bend the lamplight as it went. And beneath her voice, woven through it, a second tone, lower, steady as bedrock. Two notes from one throat.

Mara knew the phrase. Not because he'd ever heard it sung. Because he'd *tasted* it.

His mouth flooded with the memory of it: the coronation bread, the founding-day loaves, the king's private stock of honeyed wine that Mara had been required to sip from every single morning for twelve years because the recipe was older than memory and the steward said tradition demanded it. All of it carrying that same undertone. That held, patient, buried note.

"What is this?" His own voice sounded far away.

"This is the last recorded fragment of the keystone verse. Recorded eighty years ago by a collector who didn't know what he had, from a woman who was the last living keeper and didn't know she was dying." Ovid stopped the machine. "And here is what I've spent seven sleepless nights confirming, because I kept hoping I was wrong."

He took Mara's hand. Turned it palm-up. From his sleeve he produced a needle—a singer's needle, thin as a hair, used for testing resonance in tuning forks—and before Mara could pull away, touched it to the pad of his thumb.

The needle sang.

Not a vibration. A *note*. Clear and small and horribly familiar, rising out of Mara's own blood like steam off water, and the lamp flame leaned toward it, and somewhere overhead in the amphitheater a sleeping singer murmured in harmony without waking.

Mara stared at his thumb. "What did you do to me?"

"Nothing. That's the point." Ovid's eyes were wet. "That's the whole terrible point. It isn't a gift you were born with, Mara. Or—it is, but it's been fed. For years. The crown didn't just steal the keystone song and bind it into their anthem. They couldn't keep it stable that way—it decayed, it leaked, it needed vessels. So they baked it into the royal table. They fed it to their tasters. Generation after generation of food-tasters, swallowing the covenant a crumb at a time, keeping it alive in their blood without ever knowing why the work killed most of them young."

The archive was very quiet. Somewhere above, wind moved through the mountain amphitheater and made the hanging bells murmur.

"They didn't know," Mara said. "The cooks. The stewards. They were just following recipes."

"No one knows anything. That's how it works. That's how it's *always* worked." Ovid sat back on his heels. "But you know now. And here is the part I can't make gentler, so I'll simply say it. The keystone in your blood is the strongest resonance we've ever measured outside the anthem itself. Stronger than the caged singers in the capital, because they're being drained daily. Strong enough—" he swallowed "—strong enough to reforge the covenant. If it were released."

"Released how?"

Ovid didn't answer. He didn't have to. Mara had spent twelve years around power; he knew how power talked when it wanted something. It talked about destiny. It talked about sacrifice. It dressed its appetite in ceremony and called the eating holy.

"No," Mara said.

"You haven't heard the—"

"I said no." He stood. His legs held, which surprised him. "I know where this goes. I've read the histories you gave me. Keepers died re-forging boundaries. Keepers died *holding* them. You're looking at me and doing arithmetic, Ovid. I can see you doing it. How much song is in me, how much it would take, whether one life balances against a whole realm going blind."

"It's not arithmetic," Ovid said quietly. "It's a door. And doors don't open themselves."

"Doors get opened by people pushing them." Mara picked up the bread tray because his hands needed to hold something that wasn't a needle or a truth. "Does anyone else know?"

"Sable suspects. She's known since the ford—she saw how the shrine stones rang when you passed them. She hasn't said anything because she's waiting for me to be wrong." Ovid rose too, slowly, an old man's knees cracking. "Juniper doesn't know. The others don't know. And I will tell no one until you decide what they may hear. That much I promise you."

"How generous." The bitterness came out sharper than he intended. "The scholar keeps the taster's secret. Just like the palace kept mine. Everyone deciding what I'm allowed to know about my own body."

"Mara—"

"How long?" He put the tray down. "How long do I have? If the other tasters died young—if that's the pattern—how long does the song stay a passenger before it starts driving?"

Ovid looked at him for a long moment, and whatever he saw in Mara's face made him choose honesty over mercy. "Years, perhaps. Decades, if you're lucky. But the resonance grows. Every year the covenant decays further, and the keystone in you strains harder against its silence. You've felt it. Don't tell me you haven't."

He had. The headaches after meals. The nights his heartbeat came in two rhythms, a beat and its echo, slightly out of phase. The way certain foods had begun tasting of places he'd never been—of high snowfields, of burning wheat, of a river running backward. He'd thought it was the gift waking up. He'd thought it was *him*.

It was the crown's stolen property, ripening in his blood like a debt accruing interest.

"I need air," Mara said, and left.

* * *

He climbed the spiral stair too fast and came out into the amphitheater's upper ring, where the night sky poured itself between the mountain peaks like something spilled and unrepentant. Below him, the Choir's fire glowed in the center of the stone bowl. Juniper was arguing with one of the twins about a chord progression, gesturing wildly, her braid coming loose. The foundling child, Wren-of-the-village—not that Wren, the other one, the small one—sat wrapped in Sable's cloak, watching the flames with the solemnity of someone learning fire for the second time.

Sable stood apart, as she often did, at the rim of the light. One eye on the dark passes. One eye, always, on the past.

They need you, Ovid hadn't said. They need what's in you. But the shape of it hung in the cold air anyway, and Mara looked down at the fire and the people around it and understood that he was no longer looking at a refuge. He was looking at a reason. Every kindness they'd shown him, every lesson, every shared meal—none of it changed, none of it was false, and yet all of it now cast a second shadow: these were people desperate enough to spend a life. Maybe even to spend *his*.

Betrayal dressed as destiny. He turned the phrase over and found it fit both directions. The crown had betrayed him first—fed him poison dressed as bread, dressed as duty, dressed as tradition. Now the Choir might dress his death as salvation, and mean it kindly, and it would still be a death.

Unless.

He pressed his thumbnail—the one the needle had kissed—to his sternum and felt the faint double pulse there, the note and its echo. *Strong enough to reforge the covenant.* Ovid had said it like a diagnosis. But Mara had spent twelve years learning what kings never bothered to learn: that ingredients are not meals. That a recipe is a choice. That the same knife that carves the roast can carve the rope.

If the keystone lived in him, then maybe it didn't have to be *spent* like coin. Maybe it could be *sung* like a voice—one voice among many, joined rather than consumed. Ovid's histories were full of keepers who died alone holding notes alone. They were notably silent on what happened when no one held anything alone.

Or maybe that was just what a man told himself on a cold mountainside when the alternative was despair.

"Mara."

Sable had come up the stair without sound, which was a hunter's trick she pretended she'd unlearned. She stood beside him at the rail, close enough to share warmth, far enough to grant escape.

"Ovid looks like a man who's confessed something," she said.

"He did."

"And you look like a man who's been sentenced."

Mara watched the fire below. "Did you know? At the shrine. When you found me."

Sable was quiet long enough that he thought she wouldn't answer. Then: "The stones sang when you crossed the threshold. Old covenant stones, dead for two hundred years, and they hummed like struck glass. I told myself it was the blight. I told myself a lot of things." Her jaw tightened. "I led hunts for six years, Mara. I learned to smell a bounty through stone walls. So yes. Part of me did the arithmetic the first night, while you were unconscious and worth more to me asleep than awake. And part of me decided I'd rather be hanged for sheltering you than paid for selling you. Both things are true. That's what being a person is."

"I could die," Mara said. Flatly, because circling it any longer felt like cowardice. "That's what's in me. That's what they need. And I don't know yet if I believe there's another way, and I don't know if I trust myself to keep believing it once they start asking."

Below, Juniper had won the argument—the twin was laughing, a rare event, a sound like coins dropped in a well. The child clapped. Sable's hand found Mara's shoulder, brief and hard, a hunter's grip that meant *stand up straight*.

"Then don't let them ask," she said. "Whatever you decide—decide it yourself, say it out loud, and make them live with hearing it. That's the only freedom anyone gets. I learned that too late to save anyone I hunted." She turned toward the stair, then paused. "For what it's worth: I don't think Ovid wants your death. I think he wants the covenant more than he wants to be right, and those are different hungers, and he hasn't noticed yet. Watch him."

She went down into the firelight, and Juniper looked up and called something mocking and warm, and the Choir gathered her in.

Mara stayed at the rail a while longer, alone with the double pulse in his chest, the crown's theft beating quietly inside him like a second heart. Twelve years he'd swallowed the kingdom's secrets and called it service. Now the largest secret of all had taken root behind his ribs, and everyone—from the dead king who'd devised the recipe to the gentle scholar measuring his blood—had plans for how it should be spent.

He thought of the loaf in chapter three of his own life, the one whose memory had shown him singers on a mountain. What the bread remembers. What the body keeps. What a voice can refuse.

Down in the firelight, Ovid emerged from the archive stair, brushing flour from his robes, and looked up—at the rail, at Mara—with an expression of careful, hopeful guilt.

And Mara made his choice about what to say next, and opened his mouth—

—and found that when he breathed in, the night air itself came into his lungs already harmonizing.
