## 12. Juniper's Bargain

Juniper had lied to better people than Sable. That was the trouble—she'd learned lying in a house where the truth got people sold, and she'd never quite unlearned it, even here, even among people who deserved better.

"I'm going down to Thornmarket for reed wax and salt," she said, pulling her hood up against the drizzle that came off the reservoir like a held breath.

Sable looked at her from across the fire pit, one eye catching the light, the other lost under the scarred ridge of her brow. "Take Tam with you."

"Don't need an escort for wax."

"Take him anyway." Sable said it mildly, which was how you knew it wasn't a suggestion. Sable's mildness was a blade wrapped in wool.

Juniper shrugged the way she'd watched Mara shrug—loose-shouldered, harmless—and felt something curdle in her chest at how easily she'd stolen his mannerisms. "Fine. He can carry the salt."

Tam was seventeen and eager and would believe anything she told him, because he was seventeen and eager. She believed nothing anymore except three things: that the blight moved faster every week; that the crown meant to burn the gifted before it admitted the blight existed; and that Mara Nakamura was going to walk into the capital alone if someone didn't get there first and find another way.

She loved him. She hadn't said it. Saying it out loud would have made the recklessness real, and Juniper preferred her recklessness the way smugglers preferred their cargo—in the dark, sealed tight, deniable until the moment of use.

* * *

Thornmarket smelled of fish brine and tallow and, underneath both, of the particular sourness that clung to places where the law was a rumor. The market squatted on the flats below the reservoir road, all canvas and mud and quick hands. Tam trailed her through the stalls clutching the coin purse like it might be stolen by the air itself.

"Wax first," Juniper said, because the best lies were built around true errands.

She bought the wax. She bought the salt. She let Tam haggle over dried plums while she drifted toward the far edge of the market, where the canvas gave way to leaning shacks and the stalls stopped selling anything you could name in front of a magistrate.

The sign above the third shack was a carved wren with a broken wing. Underneath it, in the shade, sat a woman mending a net with fingers like knotted rope.

"You're Orla's girl," the woman said without looking up.

"I'm nobody's girl."

"Orla's girl, then." The woman bit a thread clean through. "Name's Vetch. You want passage or gossip?"

"Passage. To Aurel. The capital."

That made Vetch look up. Her eyes were the color of pond ice. "Nobody goes to the capital this season unless they're summoned, sentenced, or stupid."

"Which rate is cheapest?"

Vetch laughed—a dry rattle, like wind through a dead reed bed. "You're Orla's girl all right. Your mother ran grain past the toll cutters for six years before they caught her. Never once paid full price for anything, including her own funeral." She set down the net. "What's the cargo?"

"A person."

"Singer?"

Juniper kept her face still. In Thornmarket, stillness was currency. "Why do you ask?"

"Because singers are the only cargo worth more than their weight in blood money right now." Vetch leaned forward, elbows on knees. "The hunt's posted new tariffs. Every ferryman, every cartwright, every innkeep between here and the capital walls is licensed to report the gifted. Passage costs triple what it did at midsummer. And I don't move anyone I can't look in the eye afterward and know they'll live long enough to be my problem again."

"I'm not asking you to move a singer."

"No," Vetch agreed pleasantly. "You're asking me to move you, so you can arrange something stupider yourself."

The rain thickened. Somewhere behind them, a bell rang the hour—two strokes off, the way Thornmarket bells always were, so no one could time a crime by them. Juniper counted the cost in her head: the Choir's winter stores were thin. Sable would notice missing coin. Everything about this plan was a wound waiting for a knife.

"How much?" she said.

"For you? Forty crowns. Half now, half on delivery to a chandler's cellar off Tanner's Row. You'll travel as my niece, deaf in one ear, simple in both. There's a grain barge leaving the day after tomorrow."

"And if I wanted passage for two more, later?"

Vetch went very quiet, which in a woman like her was the same as shouting. "Then I'd say you're not buying passage. You're building an exit. And exits from the capital, girl—" she shook her head slowly, "—exits are the one thing the crown has made truly expensive this year."

"Name a number."

"Sixty each. And I'd want names."

"No names."

"Then no bargain." Vetch picked up her net again, and the conversation was over as far as her hands were concerned.

Juniper stood there in the sour-smelling shade and understood, with a clarity that felt like cold water, that she was going to pay forty crowns she didn't have for a plan that didn't exist yet, for a man who hadn't asked her to, and that she would do it again tomorrow and the day after, forever, because love—she was learning—was mostly just fear wearing nicer clothes.

"I'll have the coin," she said. "Half now, half after. Same terms."

"Day after tomorrow. The barge loads at slack water. Come alone." A pause, thread bitten through. "And girl—if your one-eyed friend finds out you came here, remember I never saw your face."

"She won't find out," Juniper said, and hated how much the sentence sounded like a prayer.

* * *

Forty crowns. The Choir's common purse held sixty-three, and Sable counted it every seventh morning with the solemnity of a priest counting relics. Juniper knew because she'd helped twice, back when helping was innocent.

So she took it in pieces. Three crowns from selling her second cloak to a pawnbroker who didn't ask why a singer owned two. Five from a wedding—she sang two songs in a farmyard barn for a couple who couldn't afford a proper musician, and their joy was so uncomplicated it hurt her teeth. Eleven from the cache she'd buried beneath her sleeping mat against exactly this kind of day, coins her mother had pressed into her hand at fourteen with the words *never tell anyone you have these*.

Nine days of small thefts and honest work. Nine days of smiling at breakfast while Sable talked supply lines and Tam practiced his harmony thirds and Mara sat with his hands wrapped around a cup he never drank from, watching the mountain passes like a man reading a sentence he already knew ended badly.

"He's going to go alone," Mara's voice said in her memory—not Mara, actually; it was her own voice, arguing with itself in Mara's cadence. *He's going to go alone, and you're going to let him, and then you'll spend the rest of your life being the person who let him.*

On the ninth evening she told Sable she was going to check the snares on the south slope before the frost took the game trails.

Sable studied her for a long moment. One eye, seeing everything. "Take the good lamp," she said finally. "The south slope eats cheap lamps."

Guilt is a strange animal. It doesn't bite you when you expect it. It waits until you're walking away with the good lamp, warm in your hand, borrowed trust swinging gently at your side.

* * *

The deal went wrong in the way deals go wrong in Thornmarket: quickly, loudly, and with knives.

Juniper arrived at the barge at slack water with twenty-two crowns and a story about the rest following. Vetch took one look at the pouch and one longer look at Juniper's face, and whatever mercy lived in the pond-ice eyes closed like a shop shutter.

"That's not half."

"It's most of a half."

"It's a down payment on a lie." Vetch sighed and turned to the bargeman. "Put the crate aboard."

There was a crate. Juniper hadn't asked about crates.

"The arrangement changed," Vetch said, reading her face with professional patience. "New buyer came through last night. Crown factors. Paying fifty crowns a head for gifted cargo, delivered breathing, no questions. I told them I don't traffic singers." She met Juniper's eyes. "They told me they weren't asking."

Two men stepped out of the barge's shadow. They wore no colors, but their boots were government boots—good leather, hard heels, oiled against weather. Hunters' boots. Juniper had seen boots like that walk into her village square eleven years ago, and she had run then, and running had saved her, and she had spent eleven years telling herself the running was over.

"Girl," Vetch said quietly, "I'd run."

Juniper ran.

She went left along the waterline because right was the road and roads had horses. Behind her, boots on wet boards, a shout, the flat official bark of *stop in the name of the crown*, words that had followed her whole life like gulls follow a fishing boat. Her lungs burned with the cold air. Her feet found the old paths—drainage ditch, fence gap, the culvert under the toll road that her mother had shown her once, laughing, saying *this is how we beat them, little bird, this and nothing else*.

A hand caught her hood.

She twisted out of it, losing the hood, losing the good lamp—it cracked against stone and guttered—and losing, somewhere in the scramble, the pouch of crowns, which spilled silver across the mud like a handful of thrown stars. She heard the hunter stop to gather it. Greed buys seconds. Her mother taught her that too.

She came out of the culvert on the north side of the market, bleeding from one palm, soaked to the ribs, and stood gasping in the dark with the reservoir wind pushing her hair back and her heart slamming like a fist on a door.

Alive. Barely. Changed.

Because here was the thing she understood now, standing in the mud with her blood dripping pink into standing water: she hadn't been afraid in those last hundred yards. Not of the hunters. Of failing. Of Mara going to the capital unprepared, of the caged singers she'd heard rumors of, of a future in which she'd been careful and careful hadn't saved anybody. Fear had stopped working on her sometime in the culvert, and what replaced it was worse and better and would not be put back in its jar.

Love made people reckless. Fine. Then recklessness would have to make people ready.

* * *

She climbed the switchbacks through the night, refusing to stop, because stopping meant thinking and thinking meant feeling and feeling meant sitting down on a cold rock and crying, and she'd budgeted no time for that. By grey dawn the amphitheater's rim rose ahead of her, and the sentry stones hummed faintly under her palm—the covenant's old wards, recognizing a Choir voice.

Mara was awake, of course. He was always awake at dawn, sitting on the eastern tier with his hands open on his knees like a man listening for something underground. When he saw her come over the rim—muddy, hoodless, palm wrapped in a strip torn from her hem—he stood up so fast he nearly dropped his cup.

"Juniper. Where—your hand. What happened?"

"Fell."

"You don't fall. You've never fallen in your life. You trip other people and watch them fall." He was already crossing to her, already reaching for the ruined palm, and his touch was gentle in a way that undid her worse than any interrogation. "Where's the lamp? Where's the salt? Juniper, look at me."

She looked at him. His eyes were dark and worried and entirely undeserving of what she was about to do to his peace of mind.

"I went to Thornmarket," she said. "Not for wax. For passage. To Aurel. For you. Because you're going to go alone, and I decided you're not allowed to."

His face did several things at once, none of them small.

"And the crown has buyers in Thornmarket now," she went on, because once started, her mouth wouldn't stop. "Fifty crowns a head for gifted cargo, delivered breathing. They almost bought me instead. So the next chapter of this isn't whether we go to the capital. It's how many of us they catch before we get there. And I need you to help me figure out how to move faster than they do."

Behind them, gravel crunched.

Sable stood at the head of the stairs with the empty lamp oil tin in one hand and Juniper's pawn ticket in the other—found, no doubt, in the pocket of the cloak Juniper had sworn she'd lost. Her single eye went from the ticket, to the bloodied bandage, to Juniper's face, and Juniper watched eleven years of guarded mercy begin, very slowly, to close.

"South slope snares," Sable said, in a voice like a drawn wire, "must be very profitable this year."

Mara stepped halfway between them, cup still in his hand, and said, "Sable—"

But Juniper raised her chin and met the one-eyed stare dead on, because she was done running from women with better instincts than hers.

"We need to talk," she said. "All three of us. Before winter closes the passes—and before the crown's buyers start climbing mountains."
