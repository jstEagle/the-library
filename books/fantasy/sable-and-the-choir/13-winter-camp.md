## 13. Winter Camp

### Mara

The snow came on the third day and did not stop.

It buried the amphitheater's terraced seats in white, sealed the archive door under a drift, and turned the mountain into a held breath. The Choir had wintered here before—Ovid spoke of it the way sailors speak of storms they survived—but for Mara it was new: a week of nothing to do but wait, eat, and listen to Juniper complain about the cold in increasingly inventive ways.

"Third night of bean stew," she announced, stirring the pot with the air of someone punishing it. "If I dream about beans tonight, I'm defecting."

"You said that last night," said Tam, one of the twins.

"And I meant it then too."

"You dreamed about beans," said Rill, the other twin, "and you cried a little."

"I was moved by them," Juniper said, with dignity. "They were very good beans."

Mara laughed from his corner by the fire, where he'd been grinding dried herbs against the flat stone Ovid lent him. It was strange, how easily the sound came now. A year ago he would have swallowed it. In the palace kitchens you learned that laughter was a thing you rationed—too much drew attention, attention drew questions, questions ended careers or lives. Here nobody seemed to care who laughed, as long as you took your turn at the dishes.

He tipped the herbs into the stew and tasted the steam.

That was the other difference. He didn't have to pretend anymore. The broth remembered the juniper berries Sable had gathered two valleys over, remembered her cold fingers and her patience, remembered the smoke of the fire she'd built to dry them. It remembered rain on the roof of whatever shed she'd slept in. Food always remembered; he'd known that since he was small enough to hide it. But tasting freely, letting the memories arrive without flinching away—that was still new enough to feel like a small miracle every time.

"Stop making love to the soup," Juniper said.

"It's good soup."

"It's beans, Mara."

"It's *remembering* beans." He handed her a bowl. "There's a difference."

She took it, grumbling, but she sat down next to him instead of across the fire, which was its own kind of answer.

Across the camp, the foundling child—Pell, eight years old, all elbows and watchful eyes—sat apart from the others, as she always did, turning a spoon over in her hands. She hadn't spoken more than ten words since they'd pulled her out of the blighted village. But when she thought no one was looking, she held things up to her ear. Stones. Spoons. The handles of doors. Listening.

Mara knew what she heard. He'd been that child once, in a valley where listening got people tried.

He set down his bowl. "Pell. Come here a minute."

She looked at him the way she looked at everything—like she was calculating the cost of obedience. Then she came, spoon clutched to her chest like a knife.

* * *

### Pell

The taster-man's hands were warm. That was the first thing. In the village, hands were for work or for hitting, and sometimes both. These hands just opened, palm up, like a bird deciding whether to land.

"What do you hear?" he asked.

Pell looked at the fire. At the loud woman with the red scarf. At the old man with the books. At the one-eyed woman who had carried her through the snow without once asking her to talk.

"Everything talks," she said. It came out smaller than she meant it to.

"I know," the taster-man said. "Doesn't it?"

Her eyes stung. She blinked hard. Nobody had ever said *I know* like that—not the way grown-ups said it when they wanted you to stop crying, but the way you said it when it was true.

"The spoon says it was in a big house," she whispered. "With a lady who dropped it. And then soldiers came."

Mara nodded slowly. "Mine used to say things like that too. Bread mostly. Bread remembers everything." He picked up a smooth grey stone from beside the fire and held it out. "This one's been in the river a long time. Want to know what rivers sound like from underneath?"

She took the stone. Held it to her ear the way she always did, ready for the flood—the houses, the soldiers, the screaming, all the things that talked whether she wanted them to or not.

But this stone only hummed. Low and slow and round, like a voice singing under water. Like being held.

"How?" she asked.

"He told it to be quiet," said the old man, Ovid, settling onto the bench beside them with the creak of old knees. "Or rather—he asked politely. There's a difference between hearing everything and *listening*. The first is a wound. The second is a craft." His eyes twinkled behind the frost-fogged spectacles. "We can teach you the craft. If you'd like."

Pell looked at the stone. It hummed. She looked at the taster-man, who smiled at her like she wasn't broken, like she was just early.

"Okay," she said.

It was the longest word she'd given anyone in weeks. The loud woman with the red scarf made a noise like she was swallowing a cheer, and pretended to stir the pot.

### Sable

Sable took the watch at midnight because she never slept well anyway, and because the snow made the forest go quiet in a way that reminded her of ambushes.

Old habits. You could leave the hunters' guild, but the guild didn't leave you. Twelve years of reading tracks and reading men, and now she read snowfall for threats that weren't there, and counted exits out of rooms she loved, and woke with her hand closed around a knife hilt that hadn't moved from under her bedroll in either direction.

Footsteps in the snow. Soft ones. Deliberate.

"Mara." She didn't turn. "You walk like a man who's never had to sneak anywhere."

"I'm working on it." He settled onto the stone beside her, breath steaming. For a while neither of them spoke. The fire had burned down to coals; the snow kept falling, fat and slow, catching the ember-light like moths.

"You're not sleeping," she said finally.

"Neither are you."

"No. But I've had practice."

He was quiet a moment. Then: "Sable. What happens if we go to the capital? Honestly. Not the plan. The truth."

She should deflect. She was good at deflecting; she'd learned it from Wren, back when Wren taught her things instead of hunting her. But there was something about the boy—the way he asked, the way he waited, the way he'd spent years swallowing poison for other people and never once asked anyone to swallow anything for him—that made lying feel like a small theft.

"The truth," she said, "is that we're four people walking into a city of forty thousand, and the plan is for me to flash a badge I stole off my own corpse of a career, and for Juniper to pick locks faster than guards can walk. The truth is the plan is terrible."

"That's not what I meant."

"I know what you meant." She watched the snow. "You meant: am I afraid."

"Are you?"

"Yes." The word came out easier than she expected. Twelve years of not saying it, and it just—came out. "Every day since the ford. Every day since I saw Wren across that water and couldn't make myself put an arrow in her. Fear isn't new, Mara. I've been afraid since the day I realized what the hunts actually were. What I *did* in them." Her jaw tightened. "What's new is having something to lose. I led raids on strangers. Strangers are easy. You lot are—" She stopped. Started again. "You're not easy."

Mara was quiet. When he spoke, his voice was low. "I'm afraid I'll die there. In the hall. Holding the note." He turned his hands over, studying them like they belonged to someone else. "And worse—I'm afraid I won't mind. They fed me the song for eleven years, Sable. Maybe it's already mine to give. Maybe that's why I don't feel scared the way I should."

"Spare me the martyr arithmetic." She said it sharper than she intended, and didn't take it back. "Listen to me. Nobody pays alone. That's the whole point of a choir—you think I dragged us through a blight and a river ambush so you could do something noble and stupid by yourself? If your life gets spent reforging that covenant, it gets spent *by all of us*, together, or not at all. Those are the terms. Take them or argue with Juniper, and lose."

Something in his shoulders loosened. He almost laughed. "You rehearsed that."

"Every night for a week."

The snow fell. Somewhere below, an owl called, and the mountain answered with silence.

"Sable," he said. "Thank you."

"Don't thank me yet. Thank me when we're old and insufferable and telling this story wrong on purpose." She stood, stamped feeling back into her feet. "Now go sleep. Tomorrow Ovid wants to drill you on the third movement, and he'll use both twins on you, and you'll want your strength."

He went. She watched him pick his way back to the fire, careful in the snow, and thought: *not easy at all.*

### Juniper

Juniper couldn't sleep, which was normal, and couldn't stop thinking, which was less so.

She lay in her bunk in the singers' gallery with her boots on, staring at the rafters, doing sums. Distance to the capital: nine days' hard travel, six if the thaw held. Guards on the coronation route: unknown, but the crown had been hiring, everyone said so. Time until the heir's crowning: weeks, maybe less. And somewhere in the middle of all those numbers, a black-market smuggler named Corvane who owed her a favor and charged interest on favors like a moneylender with a grudge.

She'd sent the message before the snow closed the passes. No answer yet. That was fine. That was *fine*.

"You're doing the face," said Rill, from the next bunk.

"I don't have a face."

"You have a scheming face," said Tam, from the bunk above. "Your eyebrows conspire."

"My eyebrows are none of your business."

"They're everyone's business," the twins said together, in the harmony that still gave her chills if she let it. "They're plotting treason."

Juniper threw her pillow at the ceiling. It hit Tam's bunk frame. There was a pause, and then both twins began, softly, in unison, to sing the bean-stew lament—a little improvised aria of grief and legumes—and despite herself, despite everything, Juniper started laughing, silent and helpless, into her blanket.

Gods, she loved these idiots.

That was the problem. That was exactly the problem. She'd spent twenty-six years keeping herself light and mobile and unattached, a singer with a quick tongue and quicker feet, and now there were people whose snoring she knew by heart, a child who'd finally said *okay*, a scarred ex-hunter who made terrible tea, and a food-taster who held two notes at once like he'd been born holding them, and who was going to get himself killed in a marble hall unless somebody reckless did something about it first.

Somebody reckless. Somebody like her.

She stopped laughing. The twins, sensing the change the way they sensed everything, went quiet.

"Juniper," Tam said carefully. "Whatever you're planning—"

"I'm not planning anything."

"Your eyebrows," said Rill, "are writing a manifesto."

"Go to sleep."

They sang one more bar of the bean lament, softer, and then the camp settled into the long breathing quiet of snowbound places. Juniper stared at the rafters and did her sums again, and this time she added a new number: how many days until Corvane's answer came, and what she'd say to Sable when it did.

Nothing good. Nothing safe. Something necessary.

Outside, the snow went on falling, patient as a held note, covering the passes and the roads and the long dark way to the capital—covering everything except the thing growing quietly under it, waiting for spring.

Three mornings later, a raven landed on the kitchen ridge-beam with a scrap of paper tied to its leg, and Juniper read it twice, burned it in the stove, and went to find her boots.
