## 14. Wren's Offer

Sable heard the footsteps before she saw the lantern, and she knew the gait before she knew the light.

That was the trouble with training someone for six years. You learned the way they moved the way you learned a song—down in the body, past forgetting. Wren walked with her weight forward, heel-first on gravel to keep it from singing, and even now, even hunting her, she still walked like Sable had taught her.

Sable didn't turn around. She kept splitting kindling at the edge of the winter camp, axe falling in slow rhythm, and let the footsteps come to her.

"You're a long way from your patrol line," she said.

"Patrol lines don't exist anymore." Wren stepped into the firelight. She looked older than the last time Sable had seen her—at the ford, three weeks ago, when Sable had put a knife to her throat and then, unforgivably, hadn't used it. Thinner. A new scar through one eyebrow. But the eyes were the same: pale gray, patient, always reading the room before entering it.

"Neither do you," Sable said. "You're supposed to be dead. I told my people you were."

"I know. It was generous of you." Wren crouched across the fire from her, unhurried, hands visible. Old habit between them. *I'm not reaching for steel.* "It also made things complicated. Command doesn't believe in ghosts that leave witnesses alive."

"The ford wasn't mercy," Sable said. "It was arithmetic. Killing you would have cost me more than sparing you did."

"That's what I told them." Wren smiled, and it was the old smile, the one they'd shared over campfires in the years before everything broke, and Sable hated how much of her wanted to lean toward it. "They didn't believe me either. So here I am. Off the books. No patrol, no witness, no record."

"No record means no amnesty," Sable said. "It means a knife in the dark and a hole in the snow. Say what you came to say, Wren. You've never been good at circling."

Wren's smile faded by degrees, like a lamp running out of oil. She reached into her coat—slowly, telegraphing every inch—and drew out a folded paper sealed with black wax. The crown's seal. She set it on the flat stone between them without opening it.

"Amnesty," she said. "Full pardon. For every member of the Choir. Names struck from the hunt rolls. Passages home. Land grants, if they want them. The scholar keeps his archive. The twins keep their tongues." She paused. "The child keeps her childhood."

"And the price?"

"You know the price."

Sable set down the axe. Carefully. The way you set down something you might otherwise throw.

"Mara."

"Mara," Wren agreed. "Delivered alive, unharmed, to the capital before coronation eve. That's all. One taster for twenty-two lives. Even you can't call that a bad trade."

*Even you.* As if Sable were the kind of person who made trades like that. As if the arithmetic had ever been the point.

She picked up the paper anyway. Broke the seal. Read it twice, because the first time her eyes had slid off the words like water off grease. It was real. It was signed by the Lord Chancellor himself, not some provincial hunter-captain forging authority. It named the Choir—not as outlaws but as *persons of covenant interest*, which was a phrase Sable recognized from the old training manuals, from the days when the crown still pretended the gifted were a problem to be managed rather than a resource to be harvested.

"They're scared," Sable said slowly. "The blight's at the capital walls now. The anthem isn't holding. They need Mara because they think he's the keystone."

"They know he's the keystone," Wren said quietly. "There's a reason the hunt narrowed to him alone, Sable. There's a reason the bounty on him is ten times any other name on the roll. They've known since the archive theft last spring. He swallowed the song for fifteen years, one royal meal at a time. He's not a man to them. He's an instrument they misplaced."

Sable looked at the fire. Somewhere behind her, up the slope, the Choir's tents huddled against the wind. Juniper was on watch tonight—she'd traded shifts so she could be, because Juniper never slept well anymore, not since the black-market deal went wrong and she came back with frostbite scars on two fingers and something harder than frostbite behind her eyes. Mara was in his tent too, probably awake, probably teaching the child another chord by candlelight, because he couldn't stop teaching even when his hands still shook from what the ford had cost him.

Twenty-two lives. She counted them the way you count your own heartbeats in a bad moment. Twenty-two names, and she could bring most of them to mind with their faces attached. Ovid, who wept over burned books. The twins, who hadn't spoken a word apart since the day their village burned. Little Ash, who was seven, whose gift had gotten her mother killed.

One name against all of that.

"You already know my answer," Sable said.

"I know what you'll say." Wren leaned forward, elbows on knees, and for a moment the mask slipped and Sable saw the girl she'd trained, the one who'd once asked her, drunk on cheap wine after a hard hunt, *how do you decide who deserves to live?* And Sable had said *you don't decide, you follow orders*, and Wren had believed her, gods help them both, right up until the day she stopped. "I know what you'll say out loud. I'm asking what you'll say at three in the morning, when Ash coughs herself sick in the cold, when the food runs out, when the hunters stop being incompetent. Because they won't stay incompetent, Sable. I'm the best they have and they sent me alone, which means they have others coming who are worse and more numerous. This offer expires at coronation. After that—" She spread her hands. "After that there's no deal to make. There's just the hunt, and the hunt doesn't tire."

"You came here to save me," Sable said. "Not them. Me. Because you still can't stand the idea of me dying for something you think is stupid."

"I came here because you spared me at the ford and I owe a debt, and debts are the only law left that I trust." Wren stood. "Consider it. That's all I ask. Consider it honestly, the way you taught me to consider everything. Not the way you wish you felt. The way you feel."

She left the paper on the stone. She walked away the way she'd come, heel-first, quiet, and Sable sat alone with the fire and the seal and the arithmetic, and did exactly what Wren had asked.

She considered it honestly.

And the honest answer terrified her, because it took her four full hours to arrive at *no*, and four hours was longer than it should have been, and she knew—with the cold clarity of a woman who had once hunted people like these for a living—that anyone watching her face during those four hours would have seen something other than refusal.

* * *

She buried the paper under a cairn half a mile east of camp, wrapped in oilcloth, marked with a hunter's sign only Wren would read. Not destroyed. Not accepted. Held. A third option that was really just cowardice wearing a practical coat, and she knew that too, and she did it anyway, because destroying it meant closing a door and Sable had spent two years learning that closed doors got people killed.

She was coming back down the trail in the gray hour before dawn when she saw Juniper standing at the camp's edge, arms crossed, breath steaming, watching her with an expression Sable had last seen on the face of a woman deciding whether to report a comrade for theft.

"You were gone all night," Juniper said.

"I walk."

"All night. East. Toward the pass." Juniper's voice was flat, which was worse than angry. Angry meant heat; flat meant a decision already made. "I followed your tracks to the cairn. I watched you bury something. I watched you talk to yourself for four hours by a dead fire, except you weren't talking to yourself, because there were two sets of prints going in and one coming out, and the second set walks exactly like a hunter walks, because you trained her, didn't you? We all know the story. We all know who found you at the shrine, who you used to run with, who you let live at the ford."

Sable kept her voice level. "Juniper—"

"Wren." The name came out like a blade being drawn. "Wren was here. In the dark. Alone with you. And this morning you come back looking like a woman who's been arguing with herself and losing."

"What did the paper say?" That was Ovid, emerging from his tent with a lantern, robe thrown over his nightshirt, spectacles crooked. Behind him, others. The twins, moving as one. Tam with his hand already on his knife. And at the edge of the gathering, wrapped in a blanket against the cold, Mara—awake, pale, watching Sable with those terrible gentle eyes that always seemed to see straight through to the arithmetic underneath.

"There was no paper," Sable said.

"Don't." Juniper stepped closer. "Don't insult us. I read tracks before I could read letters. Someone offered you something in the dark and you sat with it for four hours instead of burning it, and now you're lying about it, and the only question left is what you were bought with."

"Nothing was—"

"Was it me?" Juniper's voice cracked, finally, heat breaking through the flat. "Is that what it takes? Was it my passage, my pardon? Did they offer to let us go home?" Her jaw worked. "Because I want to go home, Sable. I want it so badly I can't sleep. So whatever number they put on that paper, I understand it. What I don't understand is why you didn't bring it to us."

"Because it wasn't mine to bring!" The words tore out of her louder than she intended, loud enough to wake the whole camp, and she didn't care. "Because the moment I showed it to you, it would stop being a trap for me and become a wedge in all of you, and that's exactly what it was designed to be! You think the Chancellor sent Wren because he thinks I can deliver Mara? He sent her because he knows I can't—and he wants all of you to spend the next week wondering whether I can!"

Silence. Wind. The fire crackling down to embers.

Then Ovid, softly, terribly reasonable: "That is precisely what someone who had taken the offer would say."

Sable turned to look at him. The old scholar met her gaze without flinching, and there was no cruelty in his face, only grief—the grief of a man who had watched institutions rot from inside and could no longer look at anything, even a friend, without checking it for rot.

"You buried it," Ovid said. "You didn't destroy it. I've studied the crown's methods for thirty years, Sable. They never destroy leverage. Neither, apparently, do you."

"It's insurance—"

"It's a door you left open," Tam said. The twins spoke together, as always, one voice split between two mouths. "Doors open both ways."

Mara stepped forward then, and the crowd parted for him without seeming to decide to. He looked tired and small and steady, and when he spoke, his voice was the one he used in lessons, the one that could hold two notes at once.

"Sable saved my life at the shrine. She carried me three miles with dogs behind her. She faced Wren at the ford and chose us over her own safety, in front of witnesses, knowing what it would cost her." He looked around at the circle of faces. "If she wanted to sell me, I'd have been sold months ago, asleep, and none of you would ever have known. Whatever happened tonight, she brought back to us the only way she knows how—by standing here and taking our worst guess about her without raising a hand."

"She's not answering the question," Juniper said.

"I'm asking her not to," Mara said. "Not yet. Because I watched this camp almost tear itself apart over the twins leaving, and I watched it heal, and I know how fragile we are, and I think—" His voice caught, steadied. "I think whoever wrote that offer knew it too. Fear is their weapon. It's always been their weapon. It's cheaper than soldiers."

Juniper stared at him for a long moment. Then at Sable. Something moved behind her eyes—love and suspicion wrestling for the same ground, neither winning.

"Fine," she said at last, and the word was ice. "But I'm taking first watch from now until we move. And if you leave camp at night again, Sable—if you so much as step behind a tree—I will assume the worst, and I will act on it. I loved you like a sister. Don't make me learn what you are twice."

She walked away. Ovid followed. Then the others, one by one, peeling off into the gray dawn, until only Sable and Mara remained by the dead fire.

"They're wrong about you," Mara said quietly.

"Some of them are right about me." Sable looked at the cairn's direction, east, where the sun was coming up the color of an old bruise. "That's what frightens me. Wren didn't come to buy me, Mara. She came to break us, and she used the truest thing she had—that part of me did sit with that paper for four hours—to do it." She exhaled. "We can't stay here. Not after this. Suspicion festers in snowbound camps. We move for the capital soon, or we come apart before we ever get there."

Mara nodded slowly. Then he frowned, head tilting, the way he did when his gift brushed against something the rest of them couldn't hear.

"Sable," he said. "Last night. When you talked with her. What did she smell like?"

"What?"

"Humor me. Your gift reads the world differently than mine. What did she smell like?"

Sable cast back through the dark hours. Woodsmoke. Horse. Leather and cold iron. And underneath—

"Choir resin," she said slowly. "The incense we use in the amphitheater. Faint, but there. Fresh."

Mara's face went very still.

"Sable. Wren has never been inside the amphitheater."
