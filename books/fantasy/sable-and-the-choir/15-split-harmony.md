## 15. Split Harmony

The accusation came at dawn, the way accusations do—carried in on cold air before anyone had lit a fire.

Mara woke to voices in the amphitheater's upper ring, sharp and low, and lay still on his bedroll listening to the shape of them. He'd learned long ago that you could taste an argument before you understood it. This one had salt in it. Fear. The particular bitterness of people who had been betrayed before and were determined to be ready for it this time.

He climbed the stone steps into gray light. The Choir had gathered in a loose crescent around the fire pit, and at its center stood Ovid, holding a scrap of paper like it was something dead he'd found on the road.

"Someone spoke with Wren," Ovid said. His voice was careful, scholarly, which somehow made it worse. "Someone met her alone, two nights past, beyond the eastern ridge. And now we learn that the crown knows exactly where our winter camp lies."

Every eye went to Sable.

She stood apart from the others, arms crossed, her one eye steady and her empty socket turned toward the pale sky. She didn't flinch. Mara had watched her face down hunters, blight, and Juniper's temper without blinking, but he saw what the others couldn't—the way her jaw set a half-beat too late, the small tell of a woman deciding how much truth a room could hold.

"I met Wren," Sable said.

The crescent tightened. One of the twins made a sound—not a word, since the twins never used words alone, but a low harmonic note of alarm that his brother answered a third above it, so that between them they said *danger* the way other people said *fire*.

"You met her," said Halleth, the exiled temple singer, her voice cracking like ice underfoot. "Alone. Without telling us. And you expect us to believe nothing passed between you."

"I expect you to believe what I tell you," Sable said. "Which is that she offered amnesty for the whole Choir if I delivered Mara to the capital. And that I told her I'd consider it."

"You told her—" Halleth's hand flew to her mouth.

"To buy time," Sable said flatly. "To keep her from reporting our position while I figured out what she actually wanted. Which worked, by the way, for two full days, until someone started following me through the snow like a suspicious spouse."

"That would be me," Ovid admitted, not sounding sorry about it.

"And you saw enough to draw your own conclusions," Sable said. "Fine. Draw them out loud, then. Say what you're all thinking."

Nobody said it. That was worse. Silence has a flavor too, Mara thought—a greasy, unwashed flavor, the taste of a kitchen where nobody wants to be the first to name the rot.

It was the twins who broke it. They stepped forward together, always together, and sang a short descending phrase that Mara had learned to translate over the winter: *we cannot stay where trust is broken.* Then the taller one, Corvin, spoke for both of them, which he almost never did, his voice rusty as an unused door.

"We're going to Merrow's Ford. There are singers there who remember us. We'll wait out the thaw."

"You can't take supplies," Halleth said immediately. "We have barely enough as it is."

"We'll take our share," said the shorter twin, Darro, finding his own voice now, angry and cracked. "Our share, and no more. Count it if you don't believe us. Count everything. That seems to be the custom here now."

They moved through the camp with terrible efficiency, packing in silence, and no one stopped them, and Mara understood with a sick lurch that this was how choirs died. Not all at once. Note by note, voice by voice, each departure thinning the chord until what remained couldn't hold anything at all.

* * *

He found them at the eastern arch, shouldering their packs in the blue half-light before sunrise. Snow squeaked under his boots. Up close, the twins looked less angry than hollowed out, two halves of the same grief trying to stand upright separately.

"Don't go," Mara said.

Corvin smiled sadly. "You heard what happened."

"I heard what almost happened." Mara stepped into the archway, blocking it, feeling absurd—a food taster, blocking two grown men with a body like a fence post. "I've spent my whole life watching people leave rooms rather than say the hard thing in them. It doesn't work. It just means the room follows you."

"The hard thing," Darro said, "is that we don't know if Sable sold us. You weren't here when the last hunt came through. You didn't see what she led." He caught himself, jaw tightening. "That's not fair. You were there. In the valley. Being hunted. Just—differently than us."

"Then let me say the hard thing first," Mara said. "Sable met Wren because she's the only one of us who could. Because she carries that guilt like a stone in her chest and she'd carry it into the capital itself before she'd let one more gifted person be caged. I've tasted her cooking. I've tasted her fear. People can lie with their mouths. Nobody lies in bread."

The twins exchanged a look—one of those twin looks, whole paragraphs in a glance—and something in it softened.

"You really can taste everything," Corvin said quietly.

"Everything anyone ever put care into." Mara stepped aside from the arch. "Go, if you have to go. But take the road along the river, not the ridge trail. The ridge is exposed for three miles and Wren's scouts know it."

Darro hesitated, then gripped Mara's shoulder, brief and hard. When they'd gone, their footsteps fading down the frozen switchbacks, Mara stood alone in the archway and felt the amphitheater behind him like a mouth missing two teeth.

* * *

He called them together an hour later, all that remained of the Choir: Ovid, Halleth, Juniper, Sable, and little Wren-named-after-no-one, the foundling from the blighted village, who sat wrapped in three blankets and watched everything with eyes too old for her seven years.

"I'm going to the capital," Mara said. "Alone."

"No," said Juniper, instantly.

"You haven't let me finish."

"There's nothing to finish. It's a stupid plan and I reject it on behalf of everyone."

"It's the least stupid plan available," Mara said. "Think about it. The crown wants me. Specifically me—I'm the one carrying the keystone song in my blood, I'm the one who interrupted the anthem rehearsal plans depend on. If I walk in alone, I'm one fugitive. If we all go, we're a conspiracy, and conspiracies get everyone hanged. You stay here, you keep the child safe, you keep the archive safe, and when the covenant reforges—if it reforges—you're the ones who can teach the realm what it means."

He'd rehearsed it on the walk back from the archway, and it sounded even better out loud than in his head, which was exactly why he distrusted it. Good arguments, Ilsa the head cook had once told him, are like rich sauces: they exist to cover the flavor of something underneath.

The something underneath was simple. He could not bear for one more person to bleed for him. He had already been swallowed by the crown once, unknowing, note by stolen note in every meal he'd tasted. He would not be the reason the Choir bled out voice by voice.

"No," Sable said.

"One word? That's your counterargument?"

"My counterargument is that I've been a hunter, and I know exactly what happens to lone fugitives entering the capital on coronation week." Her one eye fixed on him. "There are checkpoints on every road. There are dogs that can smell fear through leather. There are informants paid by the head. You wouldn't reach the city walls. You'd die in a ditch outside Vellgate, and the covenant would die with you, and your sacrifice would buy nothing except a cleaner conscience for the rest of us. I won't allow it."

"You won't *allow*—"

"I'm the reason any of you are alive," Sable said, and her voice didn't rise at all, which was how you knew she meant it completely. "I know the roads. I know the checkpoints. I know which guards take bribes and which take trophies. Every day you spend arguing with me is a day you demonstrate why you need me."

"He's right about one thing," Juniper said slowly, and Mara's stomach dropped, because Juniper agreeing with him was historically a prelude to betrayal. "He shouldn't go alone. So he won't." She stood, brushing snow off her trousers, chin up, daring the circle to object. "I'm coming with him."

"Juniper—" Halleth began.

"You need someone who can pick locks, forge passes, and talk their way past a gate warden, and that's me. Also, and I say this with love, none of you can sing the keystone song, so if Mara collapses in a hallway somewhere, you'd all just stand around being sad at a very high standard of musicianship."

"I'm coming too," Sable said.

"You can't," Mara said. "Wren will be watching for you. The moment you show your face—"

"Wren will be watching for me whether I show my face or not," Sable said. "That's the trap she built. She offered me amnesty to make you all doubt me, and it worked, and the twins are walking to Merrow's Ford right now because of it. The only way out of a trap like that is straight through the middle. I go where the risk is, or the doubt wins forever."

Silence again—but different silence. Mara tasted it carefully. Underneath the fear there was something else, warm and stubborn, like yeast working in cold dough.

Ovid cleared his throat. "For the record, as the scholar of this company: splitting a choir of six into a garrison of three and a vanguard of three is strategically defensible and emotionally catastrophic, and I recommend it anyway. The child stays with Halleth and me. We are old, cautious, and excellent at hiding."

Halleth looked like she wanted to argue, looked at the child, and closed her mouth. Instead she crossed to Mara and took his hands, hers rough as bark. "Come back," she said. "All three of you. A choir missing voices isn't a choir. It's just people making noise in the dark."

* * *

They left at dusk, three against the snow.

The first mile was silent, the bad kind, each of them carrying words they hadn't said. Mara walked in the middle because Sable insisted on point and Juniper refused the rear, and he understood that the formation was itself an argument neither of them would speak aloud: *I don't trust you yet, but I'm choosing you anyway.*

It was Juniper who finally broke it, on the long climb toward the pass, breath steaming.

"For what it's worth," she said, not looking at him, "when you stood up this morning and volunteered to die alone in a ditch, I have never wanted to hit you more."

"Noted."

"You thought you were protecting us. You thought loneliness was a gift you could give." Now she did look at him, cheeks red with cold and something hotter. "It's not. It's just loneliness. I spent ten years being lonely in a city of two million people. It nearly killed me slower than any hunter will."

"I hear you," Mara said. "But understand what I was tasting, standing in that circle. Fear. Old fear. Everyone waiting for the next betrayal. I've been the poison in the king's cup my whole life without knowing it. I know what it does to a table when people start wondering which dish is tainted."

"So your answer is to remove yourself from the table."

"My answer was stupid," he admitted. "I'm working on better answers."

Sable snorted—a rare sound, precious as summer. "First honest thing anyone's said all day."

They crested the pass as the last light died. Below them, far off, the plains stretched black toward the distant smudge of the capital, where coronation banners were already going up, where caged singers were being marched to rehearsals, where a boy heir would soon bind a stolen song tighter around a dying land.

Mara felt it in his blood before he felt it in his ears: a faint wrongness on the wind, a melody carried a hundred miles and frayed to threads. The corrupted anthem, rehearsed nightly, pulling at the covenant's last fibers.

And beneath it—so faint he might have imagined it—something else. Voices. Many voices, singing against the grain, refusing.

"Do you hear that?" he whispered.

Sable's head came up sharply. Juniper went still beside him.

"Hear what?" Juniper asked.

Mara closed his eye and listened with the older sense, the one that read memory in bread and grief in soil. The sound resolved, thread by thread: the imprisoned singers of the capital, singing the corrupted verse as they were forced to—and inside it, hidden the way he had once hidden his gift in every meal he tasted, a second line. A true line. Someone down there was threading the keystone's fragments into the anthem, note by smuggled note, hoping someone would hear.

"They're fighting," he said. "In there. Right now. They're fighting the only way they can." He opened his eyes and found the others staring at him. "We have to move faster. They're running out of song."
