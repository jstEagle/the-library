## 16. The Capital's Caged Choir

The city of Vell's Reach announced itself by smell before it showed itself: coal smoke, tanneries, the sour breath of ten thousand chimneys. Juniper smelled it from the ridge road and felt her stomach turn, though she'd grown up two streets from the tanners' quarter. You could leave a city. The city never quite left you.

"Three days to the gates if we walk," Sable said, crouched beside her at the treeline. Below, the capital sprawled in its bowl like something spilled and left to set. "Two if we're stupid."

"We've been stupid before," Mara said.

He was crouched on Juniper's other side, hood up, hands wrapped in the plain wool bands the Choir used to muffle a gift that wanted to touch everything. He'd been quiet since the ford. Quieter since Ovid's archive. There was a particular silence a person made when they'd learned they might be a sacrifice, and Mara wore it like a second cloak.

Juniper had decided, somewhere on the mountain, that she hated that silence.

"You don't have to come past the walls," she told him. Not gently. She wasn't feeling gentle. "We need eyes on the anthem schedule, nothing more. Sable knows the guard rotations. I know the underways. You could wait—"

"With the horses," Mara said. "That we don't have."

"With the—fine. With your own thoughts, which is worse." She stood, brushing pine needles off her knees. "I'm saying you look like a man walking into his own funeral and I'd rather you didn't rehearse it."

Mara looked up at her. Even through the hood, even tired and half-starved, he had that steadiness that had unsettled her when they first met—the calm of someone who had spent years being poison-tested and had stopped flinching.

"I spent eleven years in that city," he said. "Eleven years tasting for a king who never once learned my name. If there's a place in those walls where my gift matters, I want to be standing in it."

Juniper opened her mouth to argue and found she didn't have an argument. That was the trouble with Mara. He kept saying true things.

* * *

They came in with the morning market carts, three faces among hundreds, and the city swallowed them without chewing.

Juniper led them down through the underways first—not the smugglers' tunnels she'd known as a girl, which were watched now, but the older veins beneath them, the ones the city had built over and forgotten. Drainage channels from a dynasty whose name nobody sang anymore. They walked ankle-deep in cold water while Sable counted junctions by feel and Mara held a shuttered lantern low.

It was Mara who heard it first.

He stopped so suddenly that Juniper walked into his back. "Wait," he whispered. "Listen."

She listened. Water dripping. Rats somewhere. The distant grind of the city above, wheels and hooves and the bell of the Assize Court tolling noon.

"I don't hear anything."

"That's what I mean." His voice had gone strange. "There should be noise down here. Echoes. But underneath it—" He pressed his palm flat against the wet stone wall, and Juniper saw him go rigid, the way he did when the world spoke too loudly. "Singing."

"Singing."

"Under everything. Under the whole city." He turned and his face in the lantern light was terrible. "Juniper. It's coming from below us. From *under* the palace district."

They stood in the dark of a dead dynasty's drain and listened, and this time Juniper heard it too—or thought she did. A thread of sound so deep and constant it had soaked into the stone itself, into the water, into their bones. Not a melody. A *function*. Something being sung the way a millwheel is turned: not for beauty, but because stopping would cost more than turning.

Sable had gone very still. Juniper knew that stillness. It was the stillness of a hunter recognizing a snare she'd once helped set.

"The Cisterns," Sable said quietly. "Under the palace. When I served, we were told they were flooded. Sealed. Nobody goes down." Her jaw worked. "We were told a lot of things."

* * *

Getting into the palace district took Sable two days and a favor owed by a gate sergeant who still believed she'd died honorably at the border. Getting *down* took Juniper a night, three lockpicks she couldn't afford to lose, and a lie about a burst cistern pipe delivered to a cellar steward who cared more about his wine than his duties.

They descended through a maintenance shaft behind the wine cellars, iron rungs slick with condensation, down farther than Juniper liked, down until the air changed temperature and texture and began, faintly, to *hum*.

Then the corridor opened, and Juniper stopped hearing the anthem as a thread and started hearing it as a wall.

It rose out of the dark ahead of them—a vast, flattened chord, hundreds of voices blended into one sound with all the life pressed out of it. No words. No melody. Just that endless sustained tone, worn smooth as river stone, filling the caverns the way water fills a well.

"Oh," Juniper said. And then, because the word was too small: "*Oh no.*"

The gallery ran along the upper rim of a chamber the size of a cathedral. Lanterns burned in iron cages at intervals—and between them, on tiered stone benches rising from a floor far below, sat the singers.

Hundreds of them. Men, women, children. Dressed in gray. Chained not by iron but by exhaustion, by guards with cudgels walking the aisles, by the simple arithmetic of nowhere to run. Their mouths moved in unison. Their eyes were anywhere else.

And Juniper understood, in one sick lurch, what she was looking at. The blight outside the valley, the crops singing off-key before they died, the covenant fraying note by note—and here, beneath the coronation city, the crown's answer. A choir of the stolen gifted, forced day and night to hold the corrupted anthem against the decay, a dam of human voices holding back a flood.

"This is the remedy," she whispered. "This is what the edicts were *for*. They weren't hunting the gifted to protect anyone. They were hunting them to *stock this place*."

Beside her, Mara made a sound.

It was small. Juniper almost missed it under the anthem's drone—a catch of breath, a crack. She turned and found him gripping the gallery rail with both hands, and tears running freely down his face, and his lips moving silently, following along.

He knew the song. Of course he knew it. Eleven years of tasting at the royal table; every feast, every ceremony, the anthem played a thousand times. But that wasn't why he wept. Juniper watched his face and understood that he was hearing what the song had been *before*—the keystone melody buried inside the corrupted verse, the way a body is buried inside a shroud—and hearing it sung by people who had been stolen, chained, and spent like coin to keep a kingdom comfortable.

"They can feel it," he managed. His voice broke apart. "Juniper, they can *feel* what they're singing. The covenant's shape. They're holding it and it's killing them and they know exactly what it is, and no one—" His hand found hers and gripped hard enough to hurt. "No one ever told them what they were carrying."

Below, a singer near the front—an old man, gray as his robe—stumbled. Lost the note for half a heartbeat. A guard stepped toward him, cudgel rising, and the old man scrambled back into the chord so fast it was like watching a drowning man fight for the surface.

Mara turned away and pressed his forehead against the cold rail and wept without sound, because sound was death down here, and Juniper held his shoulder and stared out at the gray ranks and felt something in her chest close from grief into purpose, the way a fist closes around a blade.

Sable stood apart, at the gallery's edge, and her face was carved from something older than stone.

"I trained some of them," she said. Nothing in her voice. Everything in it. "The testing protocols. The transport routes. I told myself the gifted were taken somewhere safe." She laughed once, a dry horrible sound. "Somewhere safe. I escorted them here myself, twice. I thought the Cisterns were flooded."

"Sable—" Juniper started.

"Don't." Sable held up a hand. Her eye was fixed on the tiers below, cataloguing, counting, the hunter's habit that would never leave her. "Count them. Learn the shifts. Find where they're kept between singings. We're not leaving this knowledge in this hole. Whatever it costs."

* * *

They mapped the Cisterns for six hours hidden in a disused observation alcove, and what they learned curdled everything.

Shifts of four hours. Singers rotated in and out, but the chord never stopped—hadn't stopped, by the tally marks scratched into the alcove wall, in years. Food brought down in buckets. A ward block for the ones who collapsed. And at the chamber's heart, on a raised dais, a single empty chair of black wood, banded in silver, facing the singers—a conductor's seat, or a throne, or both.

"It's a binding engine," Juniper murmured, copying the layout onto scrap by lantern-shielded light. "Ovid was right. They took the keystone, corrupted it, and they're making the stolen gifted hold the corruption in place by force of voice. Every crowning weakens the covenant, so they grab more singers. The hunt isn't panic. It's *supply*."

"And in nine days," Sable said, "they crown the heir. The anthem will be sung in the great hall above, the binding renewed, and every person down there stays in this hole until they die of it."

Juniper looked at the tally marks. Then at Mara, who hadn't spoken in an hour, who sat with his wrapped hands folded and his eyes closed, listening to the anthem the way a physician listens to a failing heart.

"Mara," she said softly. "Talk to me."

His eyes opened. What was in them frightened her—not despair, worse than despair. Recognition.

"I tasted for them," he said. "Eleven years. I swallowed their poisons so they could eat in peace, and I thought that was the worst thing about my life—that I was invisible, that I was a tool." He shook his head slowly. "But I was part of this, Juniper. My silence bought their dinners. My obedience kept me fed while they dragged people down here. I'm not different from them. I just got lucky."

"You are *not*—"

"I know what I was going to say." He stood, and something had settled in him, hard and bright and terrible. "I'm going to say it in nine days instead. In the great hall. Where they can all hear it."

Juniper grabbed his wrist. "Mara—"

But he was looking down at the singers again, at the old man who had stumbled and fought his way back into the chord, and his face was the face of a man who had finally found the thing worth breaking himself against.

"They've been singing alone," he said. "Every one of them, alone, all these years. Time somebody sang *with* them."

Above them, muffled through fathoms of stone, the bells of the palace began to ring the hour—and beneath the bells, beneath the anthem, beneath everything, Juniper felt the covenant shudder one note looser, like a knot giving way strand by strand, and knew they had nine days to learn how to hold a kingdom together with nothing but their voices and each other.
