## 17. Coronation Eve

The plan was three lines long, scratched in charcoal on the back of a laundry chit, and Mara had read it so many times he could see it with his eyes closed.

*One: Sable walks in the front. Two: Juniper opens the sky. Three: Mara sings.*

It looked worse every time.

They were crouched in the bell-loft of a tannery two streets from the coronation square, and through the slats of the louvers Mara could watch the city prepare for its own renewal. Banners of white and gold going up along the colonnade. Scaffolds wrapped in silk where masons had spent a month carving the new king's face into stone before he'd even worn the crown. Workmen hauling benches into the square by the hundred, then chairs, then—carried on shoulders like a relic—the great gilded gallery that would hold the anthem's singers.

The caged singers.

Mara had heard them last night, or thought he had. A thread of sound rising from the palace annex across the canal, thin as wire, cut off mid-phrase. He'd pressed his palm flat against the floorboards and felt it in his teeth: voices worn down to their last note, singing something almost right and entirely wrong. The keystone melody with its spine removed. The song the first king stole, broken into obedience, sung by prisoners because the crown needed the blight slowed and had no other tool left but theft.

Tomorrow they would sing it at a coronation. Tomorrow the binding would be renewed, fresh and strong and cruel, and the valley would stay shut for another generation, and the gifted would keep disappearing into rooms like the one across the canal.

*Three: Mara sings.*

He turned the laundry chit over. Blank. He'd been staring at it hoping the back would grow more instructions. It didn't work that way. It hadn't worked that way since the kitchen, since Ilsa, since the shrine, since any of it.

"You're doing the thing," Juniper said beside him.

"What thing."

"The thing where you read a blank piece of paper like it owes you money." She took it out of his fingers and tucked it into her sleeve. "It's a good plan. For us. Which means it's terrible, which means we're used to it."

"It's not a good plan."

"It's a *short* plan. Short plans are honest." Juniper leaned her shoulder against his, warm through the borrowed tanner's smock she still hadn't washed properly. She smelled of lye and woodsmoke and, faintly, of the black-market district she'd nearly died in, though she never talked about those three days and Mara had learned not to ask. "Sable gets stopped six times between here and the west door. That's six times the guards aren't checking galleries. I pick the lock while everyone's watching the front. You walk up the stairs like you belong there, because apparently that's a thing you can do now, and you stand next to forty chained singers and you open your mouth."

"And if my voice isn't enough?"

"Then we improvise." She grinned, and it didn't reach her eyes, and both of them knew it, and neither of them said it. That was its own kind of honesty.

Below them, the square swallowed another load of benches.

* * *

Sable came back at dusk smelling of horse sweat and official ink, and she brought dinner and bad news in equal portions.

"They moved the schedule," she said, dropping onto the loft floor and handing around bread and hard sausage without ceremony. "Coronation at dawn instead of noon. The heir's advisors want the binding renewed before the blight reaches the river districts. Which means our night just got shorter."

"How much shorter?" Mara asked.

"We move at the third bell instead of the fifth." She tore her bread with her teeth. "Also, Wren is commanding the honor guard."

Juniper stopped chewing.

"I saw her name on the duty roster pinned outside the quartermaster's hall," Sable went on, level, the way she said everything, as if the world were a document she was reading aloud. "She'll be at the west door. My door."

"Sable—" Juniper started.

"It's fine."

"It's not fine. Last time you two stood in the same field, you let her live and half the Choir decided you were a traitor for a season. Now you're going to walk up to her wearing your old credentials and ask her to believe you're coming home?"

"Yes."

"Why would she believe that?"

"Because part of it will be true." Sable looked at the louver slats, at the banners going gold in the last light. "I ran hunts for nine years. I know how a hunter thinks when a fugitive comes back. They don't think *trap*. They think *tired*. They think *finally*. People want to believe the ones they chased got tired. It flatters them." She brushed crumbs from her knees. "Wren most of all. She always wanted me to quit. She just wanted me to quit on her terms."

Mara watched her say it and understood, again, the size of what she was carrying. She wasn't walking toward the west door tomorrow. She was walking toward the version of herself she'd been fleeing since the ford, offering that woman up as bait, and praying the sacrifice held long enough for a taster and a thief to climb some stairs.

"If she doesn't believe it," Mara said quietly, "she arrests you."

"If she believes it, she arrests me anyway, later, after the anthem." Sable shrugged. "That's why it works. I only need an hour of being believed. I've been believed for less."

"That's not comforting."

"It's not meant to be. It's meant to be true." She finally looked at him, and her one eye held him the way it always did—completely, without mercy, without cruelty either. "Mara. Say the rest of it. The thing you're not saying."

He hadn't known there was a thing. Then he searched, and found it, sitting in his chest like a stone swallowed years ago.

"The covenant needs a keeper's life," he said. "Ovid's archive said it plain. To reforge what was stolen, someone has to pay. And I'm the one who carries the song in his blood. So tomorrow, when I start singing—if it starts taking me—I need you two to promise you won't do anything stupid."

Juniper laughed, one sharp note.

"No," Sable said.

"You don't get to—"

"No." She said it again, quieter, and it landed harder for being quiet. "You don't get to hand us your death in advance and call it a plan. We anchor you. That was settled at the winter camp and it's settled now. If the song wants a life, it can negotiate with all three of us, and we are *terrible* negotiators when we're scared, so it should be warned."

Mara felt something crack loose in his chest—not pain exactly. Closer to the moment bread rises, when the dough gives up holding itself tight.

"All right," he said.

"All right," she echoed, and passed him the last of the sausage, and that was the whole ceremony.

* * *

The third bell found the city asleep in the way cities sleep before festivals: badly, loudly, full of dreams about tomorrow.

They moved through the tanners' quarter in the blue hour, three shadows among the drainage channels, and parted at the mouth of Cooper's Lane the way they'd rehearsed—Sable straightening her old hunter's coat, the one she'd kept folded in the bottom of her pack for a year like a shed skin she couldn't stop carrying; Juniper flexing her picks and muttering the lock's imagined personality ("stubborn, vain, secretly lonely—you can always tell"); Mara standing very still, hands empty, heart going like a bird against a window.

Sable gripped his shoulder once. Juniper kissed his cheek, quick, like punctuation.

"Sing loud," she whispered.

Then they were gone into separate darks, and Mara walked alone toward the sound of forty broken voices.

* * *

The palace annex smelled of lye and lilies—the crown's idea of mercy, flowers for people it kept in cages. Mara reached the service stair as the fourth bell rang, moving at the pace of a man with an errand, because that was the whole trick of belonging: nobody stops anyone who looks like they're supposed to be there. Nine years in the royal kitchens had taught him that. Nine years of tasting other people's food, invisible at the elbow of power. He had hated that invisibility his whole life.

Tonight it was armor.

He heard the singers before he saw them. Through the iron door at the top of the stair, behind two guards playing dice by candlelight, the dawn rehearsal had already begun—a fragment of the corrupted anthem, rising and fraying, rising and fraying. Mara put his hand against the cold iron and his gift rose unbidden, the way it always did now, reading what things remembered.

The door remembered hands. Hundreds of them, locked and unlocked, fed and starved. The hinges remembered oil applied grudgingly. And beneath everything, threaded through the metal like a vein of ore, the door remembered the song—every rehearsal soaked into it, every cracked voice, every time someone sang the stolen verse correctly and wept afterward, quietly, so the guards wouldn't hear.

Mara stood with his forehead against the door and let himself weep once, briefly, silently, the way the singers did. Then he wiped his face and went down the stair to find Juniper's window.

* * *

The singers' gallery was a gilded cage built for show: a curved balcony suspended above the coronation dais, walled with carved screens so the realm could hear the anthem without having to look at the people singing it. The crown had spent a fortune making the cage beautiful. The locks, though—the locks were ordinary. Municipal. The kind Juniper had been insulting professionally since she was twelve.

Mara reached the base of the gallery stairs as the sky went grey at the edges, and found Juniper already there, flattened against the wall, listening.

"Well?" he breathed.

"She was right. Stubborn, vain, secretly lonely." Juniper held up her picks. "There's a guard ward-charm wired into the hasp, though. Crown work. Touch it wrong and it screams the whole wing awake." She glanced at him, and in the pre-dawn grey her grin finally reached her eyes, which somehow made it worse. "So don't touch it wrong."

"And if you touch it wrong?"

"Then you'll have about four seconds to decide what kind of story you tell with the rest of your life." She turned back to the lock. "Go stand where Sable told you. Watch the square. When the bells ring seven, the procession enters, everyone looks east, and I make this lock regret its choices. Singers file in at the eighth bell. You go up with them. That's the whole dance."

Mara took his place at the pillar's shadow and looked out over the square.

It filled as the light grew. First the poor, claiming bench space hours early because the coronation feast scraps would follow the ceremony. Then merchants, then minor houses, then the gray ranks of the guard, then the banners—white and gold, white and gold, everywhere, until the square looked like a field of some flower that had never grown anywhere honestly. At the far side, past the dais, Mara could see the west door where a figure in hunter's black stood at post: broad-shouldered, still, unmistakable even at this distance.

And approaching her, unhurried, a second figure in the same black, walking like she owned the ground and the ground owed her rent.

Mara's breath stopped.

Sable reached Wren. Even from here he could read the shape of it—the stiffening, the long pause, the two women standing closer than protocol allowed while the crowd flowed around them like water around stones. He couldn't hear words. He saw Wren's hand move to her sword hilt and stop. He saw Sable tilt her head, say something brief, and produce papers from inside her coat—her old credentials, her old name, her old life offered up on open palms.

He saw Wren take the papers. Read them. Look up.

Look, for one terrible moment, directly across the square—at the pillar, at the shadow where Mara stood—as if Sable's words had drawn her a map.

No. Impossible. Too far. Too many faces.

But Wren's gaze lingered a heartbeat too long, and then she handed the papers back, and stepped aside, and Sable walked through the west door into the palace like a woman coming home.

Mara exhaled for the first time in a minute.

At the pillar, soft as breath, Juniper spoke without looking up from the lock. "Bells in five minutes. Get ready, taster."

The first bell rang.

The crowd roared. The east gates opened, and the heir's procession came gold into the light, and somewhere above the dais, behind carved screens, forty stolen voices began to warm themselves for the anthem that would bind them all another generation—and Mara Nakamura, royal food-taster, keeper of a swallowed song, stepped out of the shadow and started climbing the stairs.
