## 18. The Anthem Interrupted

The gallery smells of dust and old gilt and, beneath it, the sour iron tang of fear that has soaked into the wood over years. Mara crouches behind the balustrade with Juniper's shoulder pressed to his and counts the singers below.

Forty-one.

He had hoped he was wrong. He had counted twice in the dark of the singers' gallery while Juniper worked the last lock, and both times the number came back the same: forty-one people in grey robes, chained at the ankle to a rail that runs the length of the stage, arranged like instruments on a shelf. The tallest of them stands at the center, a broad man with a conductor's scarred hands, and when the horns in the pit begin the coronation anthem's opening phrase, the man lifts his arms and forty-one voices rise to meet him.

They are good. That is the worst part. They are very, very good, and they have been made into this.

Below them, the coronation hall of Vell spreads out in tiers of white marble and blue banner-cloth, packed with every lord and lady and guildmaster the crown could summon. The old king sits near the front, small and yellowed in his furs, and beside him the heir — Prince Aldous, twenty-two years old, wearing a crown of unlit silver because the crowning itself will not happen until the anthem ends. The anthem binds him. The anthem binds everything.

Mara has heard this song his whole life from the outside: at festivals, at executions, at the annual renewal where the valley's dampening veil was re-tied like a knot in a rope. He never listened closely. You don't listen closely to the air you breathe.

Now he hears it, really hears it, and his stomach turns.

"It's wrong," he whispers. "Juniper, it's *wrong*."

"I know." Juniper's voice is flat as slate. She lies flat against the balustrade, one eye through the fretwork, her pick-kit still open at her knee. "Ovid told us. The keystone melody turned inside out. Every note inverted."

"It's not just inverted. Listen." Mara closes his eyes and lets his gift open the way Sable taught him, slow, careful, like opening a door onto wind. The music pours through him and he tastes it — because for him everything comes as taste, always has, since he was a boy in Ilsa's kitchen reading the history of a loaf off its crust. And what the anthem tastes like is ash and copper and something sweeter underneath, buried so deep you'd miss it if you weren't built to find the poison in the honey.

"The stolen song is still in there," he says. "Underneath. They didn't destroy it. They couldn't. They just—" he gropes for the word, "—*sealed* it. Wrapped it up in its own reflection."

"Then unwrap it." Juniper turns her head. In the gloom of the gallery her face is all angles and stubbornness, and there is something else in it too, something she'd deny under torture: fear. For him. "That's the plan. That's been the plan since the ford. You sing the true verse over their false one, loud enough, clean enough, that the hall hears the difference. That's all. One verse. Then we're gone down the east stair before anyone knows which gallery it came from."

"That was the plan when the plan involved all three of us leaving together."

"The plan involves all three of us leaving together *now*." Her hand finds his wrist and grips. "Mara. Look at me. One verse. Sable's already moving — see the west doors? She'll pull the guard rotation the moment you start. We go on your last note. Not after. On it."

Through the fretwork, far below and to the left, Mara catches a flicker of movement by the west doors: a big shape in palace livery, standing too straight, scanning the galleries with one eye that misses nothing. Sable, in a dead man's uniform, wearing her hunter's face. Somewhere in that crowd of two thousand she is the only person who knows what his voice can do, and she is betting his life on it anyway because there is no other bet left.

The anthem climbs toward its first turn.

Mara has spent his whole life being quiet. Quiet in the kitchens, quiet at the king's table, quiet about the flavors no one else could taste, quiet about the night a dish of quail whispered *bitter almonds* to him half a heartbeat too late. Quiet is how a food-taster survives. Quiet is how anything gifted survives in Vell. He learned silence the way other children learn to walk, and he is good at it, and every part of him that wants to live is screaming at him to stay crouched behind this balustrade until the anthem ends and the prince is crowned and the covenant is bound another generation tighter around the throats of everyone who ever hummed an honest note.

But he can hear the caged singers now. Not with his ears — with whatever he is. Their voices come up through the floor like heat, and each one carries its own flavor, and the flavors are wrong in a way he knows intimately: the taste of a person singing against their own nature, the way bread goes bitter when the baker weeps into the dough. Forty-one people pouring their lives into a lie because the alternative is the whip or worse, holding the blight back with the very song that caused it, and not one soul in this shining hall below knows or cares.

One small voice refusing to be silent. Juniper said it once, drunk on winter mead, laughing at him, and he'd thought she was mocking him. She hadn't been. She'd been handing him his name.

The anthem reaches the turn.

He stands up.

* * *

Later — in the days after, when people ask him, and they ask him constantly — Mara will try to explain what it felt like to step into the open space above two thousand heads, and he will fail, because the truth is that it felt like nothing at all. Like stepping off a curb. His body simply went ahead of his courage, the way it does, and then he was standing at the balustrade in full view of the royal box with his heart going like a drummer boy and the anthem swelling beneath him, and there was no more deciding left to do.

The corrupted verse begins. He knows it now, Ovid drilled it into him for weeks: the anthem's second movement, where the first king took the covenant's keystone melody and folded it backward on itself, note against note, harmony against harmony, until the song that once held the realms together became a knot that held them down. Below him, forty-one voices sing it flawlessly. The horns carry the inversion. The organ grinds underneath like millstones.

Mara opens his mouth and sings the truth.

It comes out smaller than he means it to. Of course it does — one untrained voice against an orchestra, one throat against a state. But he isn't singing to be louder than them. Sable taught him that. *You don't fight the current, taster. You put your stone in the river and let the river do the work.* He sings the keystone verse exactly as Ovid reconstructed it from fragments and memory and the notation hidden in the archive beneath the amphitheater, and he sings it *into* the anthem rather than against it, finding the gaps between the inverted notes the way water finds the cracks in a wall.

For four bars, nobody notices.

For four bars, his voice is just a strange overtone, a flaw in the acoustics, a bird got into the rafters. The conductor's scarred hands keep time. The prince smiles his practiced smile. The old king dozes in his furs.

And then the keystone melody finds the sealed thing inside the anthem and touches it, and the anthem *shudders*.

Mara feels it happen — feels it in his teeth, in the roots of his hair, in the place behind his sternum where his gift lives. The false verse is a locked box, and he has just sung the key, and somewhere in the architecture of the music a seal that has held for nine generations begins, very quietly, to crack.

A woman in the third tier drops her fan. A guildmaster stands halfway up and stays there, frozen, his mouth open. Down in the pit, a horn player misses her entrance — the first missed note in the anthem's performance in living memory, Mara would learn later, the first in two hundred years — and the gap where her note should be opens like a window, and through the window his voice pours down into the hall.

He stops hiding. There's no point anymore. He lets the gift come all the way up, the way it flooded into him the night he crossed the valley's edge, terrifying and enormous, and he doesn't try to contain it or aim it. He just sings, and lets the song remember.

Because that's what the keystone does. That's what it was always for. It remembers.

The hall begins to remember with it.

Mara watches it move through the crowd like frost across glass. It starts nearest the stage — a chambermaid sways and grips the rail, her eyes going wide and wet. Then a row of lords, sitting suddenly straight, faces draining. Then wider, tier by tier, outward and upward, two thousand people touched in the space of thirty seconds by something that is not quite sound. He knows what they're hearing because he tasted it once in a loaf of festival bread baked by a baker old enough to remember: the covenant as it used to be. Not a cage. A *promise*. Fields that sang back when you planted them. Borders that were handshakes instead of walls. Magic that belonged to everyone the way rain belongs to everyone. The memory of a world before the first king decided a kingdom needed a leash.

People are crying. All over the hall, people are crying without knowing why, and some of them are crying because they know exactly why — because they are sixty years old and they had forgotten, truly forgotten, that the world was ever any other shape.

The guards hesitate.

That matters more than anything. Mara sees it from above: the palace guard along the walls, the ones Sable couldn't fully draw away, half of them turning toward the gallery with hands on hilts — and stopping. Because they hear it too. One young guard by the royal box pulls his hand off his sword like the steel burned him and just stands there, tears running into his collar, listening to a song his grandmother might have hummed over a cradle before the hunts made humming dangerous.

The conductor keeps conducting. What else can he do? His scarred hands beat time for an anthem that is coming apart around him, and the forty-one chained voices beneath him waver, and then—

And then the tall man at the center looks up.

Straight up, past the tiers, past the banners, into the gallery, and finds Mara with his eyes. And Mara, singing, watching, sees the man's face change — sees grief and recognition and something ferocious and hopeful break across it like dawn over water. The man's mouth shapes a single silent word. Mara reads it easily; he has spent his life reading mouths across a paranoid king's table.

*The third verse.*

He knows. This stranger in chains, this conductor of the crown's stolen choir, knows the keystone song. Has known it all along, maybe, carrying it in his chest the way Mara carries it in his blood, waiting however many years for someone to give him permission.

The man raises his arms — not conducting now, *inviting* — and forty-one voices, ragged and weeping and magnificent, come in under Mara on the third verse.

The sound hits the hall like the valley breaking open.

* * *

Mara loses track of everything except the song.

This is the danger and he knows it and he cannot help it. Singing the keystone is like standing in a river of everything he has ever tasted — every meal, every memory soaked into every dish he ever carried to the king's table, and beyond that, deeper, the memories in the song itself, generations of keepers, the original singers whose voices first tied the covenant, and they are all singing *through* him now, or he through them, and the line between is dissolving beautifully, catastrophically.

His knees go soft. He grips the balustrade. The gilt is cold under his palms, and the cold is the only thing telling him where his body ends.

*Mara.*

Juniper's voice, or her hand — both, she's shouting his name and hauling at his arm, and he surfaces long enough to see her face inches from his, furious and terrified. "*Last verse! We go on your last note, that was the deal!*"

But the song isn't finished. The seal isn't broken — it's cracking, cracking, and if he stops now it will close again, and the prince will be crowned inside the lie, and forty-one voices will be chained to hold the blight back for another generation with their throats and their lives. He can feel how close it is. One more verse. The covenant's own words, the promise at the center of everything: *what was taken in secret shall be returned in sight of all.*

"In sight of all," he gasps, and Juniper goes still, because she knows the line too, everyone in the Choir knows it, it's carved over the archive door.

"Mara—"

"They have to *hear* it end, Juniper. If it ends wrong—" He turns back to the hall. Below, the anthem and the keystone are tangled together now, the orchestra faltering, the conductor weeping openly as he beats time for both songs at once, unable to stop either. The old king has risen from his seat, tiny and furious, shouting something at the captain of the guard, and the captain — Mara sees it clearly, will remember it clearly all his life — the captain does not move.

The final verse waits for him.

Mara takes the deepest breath of his life. It hurts. Everything hurts, his chest most of all, as though the song is using his ribs as a tuning fork. Far below, the tall conductor meets his eyes one more time and nods, once, like a man passing a torch across a chasm.

Mara steps up onto the balustrade itself — the whole gallery gasps, Juniper grabs for him and misses — and stands in the open air above two thousand people, above the crown, above the lie, one small figure in a borrowed coat with a taster's unremarkable voice, and begins to sing the ending of the true song of Vell.

The hall holds its breath to listen.

And in the silence between his first note and his second, Mara feels the covenant reach for him — feel it the way you feel a hook set behind the breastbone — and understands, with terrible clarity, exactly what the next chapter of this song is going to cost.
