## 21. After the Last Note

I woke to the smell of bread.

Not palace bread—palace bread smelled of nothing, of ovens scrubbed so clean they'd forgotten every loaf they'd ever made. This was mountain bread, dark and dense, and it remembered rain. It remembered the hands that kneaded it, a woman with flour in the creases of her knuckles, humming something low while she worked.

I opened my eyes.

The infirmary hall of the amphitheater had been built for singers, not patients, and it showed. The ceiling curved like the inside of a throat, and every sound I made came back to me softened, rounded, as if the room itself were listening kindly. Someone had draped a blanket over me that smelled of woodsmoke and juniper—Juniper's doing, probably; she'd been threatening for weeks to knit me something and this was worse than knitting.

"You're awake," she said from the chair beside my bed, where she was sitting with her boots on a second chair and an apple in her hand she clearly wasn't going to share. "Don't do anything dramatic. Sable's orders. You've done your lifetime quota."

"How long?"

"Nine days." She tossed the apple, caught it. "You held the note for eleven minutes, Mara. Eleven. Ovid says the old keepers managed four at most before the covenant took them. You're either the strongest voice we've ever had or the stupidest man alive, and honestly the evidence supports both."

My throat answered before I could—a raw, scraped silence where my voice should have been. I tried to say her name and produced something like wind through a cracked window.

"Yeah," Juniper said, gentler now. "The healers say it'll come back. Most of it. They say you spent yourself down to the wick and then kept burning anyway." She leaned forward, and for once she didn't have a joke loaded behind her teeth. "We thought we'd lost you. Sable held your hand through the worst of it and pretended she wasn't crying, which fooled no one, because Sable has never successfully hidden anything in her life except her own heart, and even that only from herself."

I reached for her hand. She let me take it, which from Juniper was practically an embrace.

* * *

They came to me over the following days, as my strength crept back and my voice returned one hoarse syllable at a time. The twins first, arriving together as always, speaking their single shared sentence: *"You held two notes when the world could hold only one."* Then Ovid, who sat by my bed for three hours reading aloud from his recovered archives—not the keystone song, never that, but the histories around it, the names of keepers across nine generations, as if he were returning them to the record after centuries of theft. He read until I slept, and when I woke there was a new page copied in his careful hand at my bedside: *Mara Nakamura, taster of Vell, keeper by blood and by choice.*

And then, on the twelfth day, Wren came.

She stood in the doorway of the infirmary like someone who hadn't decided she was allowed to enter. Hunter's coat, but the crown's badge had been cut off it—the fabric torn, not unpicked, torn in haste or fury—and beneath it she wore the plain gray of no allegiance at all.

"I heard you almost died," she said.

"Almost," I rasped.

"Pity. Would've saved me a speech." But she came closer, and I saw what the torn badge had cost her—there was a scar of brighter cloth where it had lived for years, shaped into her shoulder like a second skin. "Sable won't see me. I don't blame her. I'm not here for her forgiveness. I'm here because—" She stopped. Started again. "Because I spent six years hunting people like you, and I told myself it was law, and then I watched a food-taster nearly die in front of the whole court rather than let the law stand, and I understood that I had never once believed it was just. I just believed it was easier."

She set a satchel on my bed. It clinked faintly—papers, seals, the weight of official things.

"Warrants. Orders. Correspondence between the Lord Protector and the hunt's architects, including the directive to establish the singing prisons. Signed. Sealed. Dated." Her jaw tightened. "There are copies in three cities by now. If they come for me, it won't matter. If they come for any of you, it will."

"Why give it to us?" My voice was still gravel, but it carried. The room helped. Rooms like this always help the truth get out.

Wren looked at me for a long moment, and I saw the same thing in her face I'd seen at the ford, the moment Sable's blade stopped short of her throat: a woman standing at the edge of the person she used to be.

"Because you sang," she said simply. "In that hall, when the anthem started, everyone in the room remembered something they'd buried. I remembered a girl I arrested in the spring of my first year. She was fourteen. She'd made flowers grow out of season in a widow's garden, and I put her in chains for it, and I told myself it was law." She swallowed. "Her name was Tamsin. I never learned what happened to her. That's the least of what I owe, and I intend to start paying."

She left the satchel and walked out without waiting for absolution, which was, I thought, the most honest thing anyone had done in that palace in a hundred years.

* * *

The crown folded faster than any of us expected, though Ovid said we shouldn't have been surprised. "Regimes built on stolen songs have thin foundations," he said, tapping the archive shelves. "Take away the anthem and all that's left is men in expensive chairs realizing the music was the only thing holding them up."

The public pressure did the rest. Half the capital had heard the true keystone ring out over the corrupted verse, and the other half had heard about it by nightfall, and stories like that travel faster than soldiers. The caged singers were released in the third week—walked out of the singing-prisons blinking in daylight, some of them so long confined they flinched from open sky. The Choir met them at the gates. We didn't sing, not at first. We just made sure there were people there, and food, and no chains.

The Lord Protector resigned. Two of the hunt's architects were arrested using Wren's documents. The young king—heir no longer, crowned in a ceremony stripped of the stolen anthem, awkward and visibly relieved to be rid of it—issued a proclamation repealing the edicts. The word *gifted* appeared in royal language for the first time without the word *hunt* attached to it.

It wasn't justice. Justice would take years, maybe generations. But it was a door opening, and doors are how everything begins.

* * *

On the day they asked me what I wanted, I was well enough to walk to the amphitheater's edge and look out over the valley below.

The whole Choir had gathered—not summoned, just drawn, the way people gather when something is ending and beginning at once. Sable stood at my left, one eye on the horizon out of habit, the other on me out of something softer. Juniper at my right, uncharacteristically quiet. Behind us, the freed singers, the twins, Ovid clutching his notes, little Wren-named-Tamsin's-successors and foundlings and exiles, all of them waiting.

A herald had come up the mountain that morning with the king's own seal. Not a summons—an offer. Lands, titles, a position at court. *Name your reward,* the letter said. *The realm owes you a debt it cannot measure.*

"What do you want?" Sable asked me. Not reading the letter's question—asking her own, the real one. "Truly. Anything. After what you gave, no one would begrudge you."

I thought about it honestly, because they deserved honesty.

I thought about the palace kitchens, the taste of a hundred meals that remembered nothing, the vigilance that had been my whole life. I thought about the valley of Vell, where I'd been born under a veil that pressed magic flat as hammered tin, where I'd learned to hide before I learned to speak. I thought about the blight fields outside the walls, soil singing off-key, and the child we'd taken in whose gift had nearly gotten her killed. I thought about bread that remembered rain, and hands that kneaded it humming.

"I want to go home," I said.

Juniper stiffened. "Mara—"

"Not to hide." I turned to face them all, and my voice—still rough, still half-spent, but mine—carried the way it was learning to carry. "The valley's been shut behind its veil since before any of us were born. Everyone in Vell grew up the way I did: small, careful, afraid of the thing inside them. They think the world outside is the blight and the hunters and the crown. And now the veil is dissolving whether they're ready or not." I looked at Sable. At Juniper. At all of them. "So I want to show them the valley. All of it. I want the Choir to come to Vell and stand in the open air and sing, and let the people there see what a gift looks like when it isn't a secret. Let them see that the thing they've been hiding is the thing that saves them."

Silence, the good kind—the kind a room makes when it's listening kindly.

Then Sable smiled, slow and rare as sun through winter cloud. "A concert," she said. "Our first. In the place that taught you to be silent."

"The best place to learn otherwise," I said.

Juniper punched my arm, gently, which from her was practically a coronation. "Fine. But I'm choosing the set list, and if I hear one complaint about the ballad I wrote about you holding the note, I want it known that I suffered for that ballad."

Ovid was already scribbling. The twins hummed a chord that sounded, unmistakably, like anticipation. Somewhere behind us, the foundling child laughed at nothing, at everything, at the sheer fact of being alive and unchained on a mountainside in the spring.

I looked out past the amphitheater's rim, down toward the low green line of the valley where I'd spent a life being careful, and felt something I hadn't let myself feel since the night I climbed the wall and ran: hope. Not the desperate kind, the kind that keeps you moving with dogs at your heels. The settled kind. The kind that tastes like bread fresh from the oven, warm and certain, remembering rain.

The road home ran downhill the whole way.

For once, that felt right.
