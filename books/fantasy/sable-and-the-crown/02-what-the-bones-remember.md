## 2. What the Bones Remember

The boy slept in the corner of the workshop where Ada kept the shavings, curled among curls of elk antler and walrus ivory like something the sea had given up. He had not spoken since dawn. He did not need to. The token still lay on Ada's bench where he'd set it down at midnight, and it was still humming.

Not humming, exactly. That was the word his hands offered first, and his hands were honest, but it wasn't right. A hum filled a space. This sound *occupied* one. It sat in the workshop the way a guest sits in a room too small for grief — politely, unbearably. When Ada pressed his palm flat against the carved bone, he felt the vibration travel up through the meat of his thumb, into the wrist, into the long bone of the forearm, and there it stopped, as if waiting to be recognized.

He had carved forty years of voices out of dead things. Rib-bones of grandmothers who wanted their recipes remembered. Skulls of men who could not rest until someone knew where they'd hidden the deed to a field. Femurs of children — those he took rarely, and only when the voice inside was kind. Every piece spoke when he held it. That was the valley's quiet bargain: magic could not live here, could not cross the ridgeline, could not so much as flicker in a candleflame. But the dead were not magic. The dead were only memory with nowhere else to go, and memory, Ada had learned, was patient enough to wait inside bone.

The token's voice was not patient. It was *urgent*, and it was not speaking to him.

It was speaking to the carvings on his shelves.

All night they had answered it — softly, in their sleep-voices, the grandmother murmuring her recipe over and over, the field-deed man weeping his directions, all of them turning, in whatever way a voice turns, toward that small humming thing on the bench like flowers toward a window. And beneath their words, under all of them, ran a language Ada did not know: low, rounded, full of breath, a language built for cold air and long silences. The boy's language. The words he had spoken at the door last night while snow melted off his shoulders and his eyes held Ada's with the terrible calm of someone who has walked very far to be refused.

*Vhalen,* one of the skulls had whispered back, before Ada could stop listening. *Vhalen, Vhalen.*

He did not know the word. But he knew the skull that might.

* * *

Maren Ostergaard had died ninety-one years ago, and her skull had been carved by Ada's grandfather, and it was the oldest work in the valley — older than the church, older than the mill, older than any family name except its own. She had been, in life, a woman who remembered everything and forgave little, and death had not softened her. Her skull sat on the highest shelf, wrapped in grey linen, and Ada unwrapped it now with the boy watching from the doorway, thumbs hooked in the straps of a coat two sizes too big.

"I don't let living people hear her," Ada said. "She's rude."

The boy blinked at him. Then, deliberately, he sat down on the floor beside the bench and folded his hands, the way children do when they have decided to be permitted something.

Ada almost smiled. Almost.

He lifted Maren's skull in both hands. It was heavy the way old things are heavy — not with weight but with accumulation. Forty years of dust in the sutures. He closed his eyes, the way he always did, though the hearing had nothing to do with eyes; it was a courtesy, a closing of doors against the daylight world so the other one could speak.

"Maren."

A pause, the length of a held grudge. Then her voice, dry as seed husks: *The Iversen boy. Still carving. Still asking.*

"Someone came to my door last night." He turned the skull gently, the way you turn a face toward light. "A child. He speaks words no one knows. My carvings know them. They say a name — Vhalen."

The silence that followed was different from Maren's usual silences. Her usual silence was contemptuous. This one was afraid.

*Say it again,* she said.

"Vhalen."

*Lower your voice.* Which was absurd — she was a voice herself, wrapped in linen, on a shelf. But Ada lowered his anyway, and the boy leaned forward, and outside the wind moved through the pines with a sound like pages being turned by someone searching for a particular line.

*I was a girl when they burned the maps,* Maren said at last. *My father stood in the square in Halden and watched the criers read the decree. All record struck. All mention forbidden. The borders unmade. The roads renamed. Anyone who spoke the name aloud would be — * she paused, and Ada felt her choose the next word with care, which she never did — *corrected. Forty years ago this spring. You weren't born. Your grandfather was young, and he carved me that same year, and do you know what he asked me? Not about my husband, not about my grievances. He asked: what was erased? Because he said a valley without magic is a valley with room left over, and things hide in rooms like that. Things the Crown wants forgotten.*

"What things?"

*Countries,* Maren said. *Vhalen lay east of the passes, past the ridge where nothing grows. Rich in winter. Old in the way stone is old. They say the Crown didn't conquer it. Conquest leaves ruins, and ruins leave stories, and stories are seeds. So instead they did the other thing. They took it out of the world the way you take a splinter out — whole, and all at once, and leaving only the small wound behind to prove it happened.* Her voice thinned. *They called the ones who did it memory-eaters. Men and women in grey, who could swallow a name out of your mouth while you were saying it. Afterward, people remembered that there had been a country the way you remember a dream — badly, and less every year, until even the shame wore through.*

The workshop was very cold. Ada realized he had stopped breathing somewhere in the middle of it and made himself start again.

"And the language?" he asked. "The boy's language?"

*Whose else would it be?* Maren said. *That is Vhalen's tongue, or what's left of it walking around in a coat. Ask him his name, carver. No — * sharply, before Ada could move — *don't ask him here. Names are how they find things. Names are how they found us, the first time. If the Crown sent eaters after a whole nation, do you think they've stopped checking the wound? Forty years, and they will still come for anything that speaks. Especially something that speaks like that.*

The boy was looking up at the skull. Not at Ada — at the skull, at the linen, at the shelf, with an expression Ada had seen once before, on the face of an old woman listening to her own mother's rib-bone: recognition, and underneath it, grief arriving early.

Then the boy spoke. Two words, soft, in that round cold language, and every carving on every shelf answered at once — a rustle, a murmur, forty voices exhaling together like a congregation sitting down — and Maren's skull trembled in Ada's hands.

*Yes,* she said quietly. *That's the greeting. That's how they used to greet the winter. Tell him — * and here the oldest voice in the valley faltered, actually faltered — *tell him we're sorry. Whoever we are. We're sorry we forgot.*

* * *

Ada did not tell him. There was no time, and perhaps no way, and besides — the boy already knew. Children always know the shape of an apology before they know its words.

He fed the boy bread and dripping, and the boy ate the way travelers eat, fast and watchful, and Ada caught himself planning. Extra oil for the lamps. The good knife whetted. The back shutter that stuck, unstuck. His hands did these things while his mind ran ahead of them down dark roads, and he understood, with a carver's patience, that he was already building something — not a plan yet, just the shape of one, roughed out in green wood.

It was full dark when he heard the horses.

Not many. Three, maybe four, moving slowly up the valley road in the manner of riders who are in no hurry because they have never needed to be. The sound carried strangely in the frozen air, iron and breath and the creak of leather, and beneath it — Ada's blood went thin — beneath it was a silence that moved with the horses. A hole in the night's noise, shaped like men.

The boy heard it too. He went rigid, bread forgotten, and looked at the door with an expression Ada would carry the rest of his life: not fear. Recognition. The look of someone hearing a familiar voice through a wall.

Knuckles on wood. Three slow knocks, spaced like a sentence being dictated.

Ada put his hand on the boy's shoulder — felt the small bones under the coat, alive, warm, impossibly warm — and steered him back into the shadow of the shelves, among the sleeping dead. Then he opened the door.

Four riders stood in the snowfall. Grey cloaks, grey hoods, faces like faces seen through ice. Their horses did not steam. Behind them the valley lay white and ordinary and utterly silent, and Ada understood that the silence was theirs, laid over the land like a cloth.

"Goodman Iversen," the front rider said. Its voice was pleasant. That was the worst thing about it. "We're riding for the Crown, collecting reports of unregistered speech. Has anyone passed through your valley recently? Anyone who speaks oddly? A child, perhaps?"

The wind moved. Somewhere behind Ada, on a high shelf, under grey linen, the oldest voice in the valley waited to hear what he would say.

And Ada Iversen, who had spent forty years in a place where nothing magical could follow, heard himself answer — calmly, in the flat tone of a man discussing weather:

"No one's passed through. But you're welcome to warm yourselves, if you can."

The rider smiled, and behind Ada, in the dark of the workshop, the boy began, very softly, to hum.
