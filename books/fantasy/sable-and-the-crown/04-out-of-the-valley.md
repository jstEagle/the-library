## 4. Out of the Valley

They left before dawn, when the valley's cold was at its most honest.

Ada had packed in the dark the way he did everything — by touch, by habit, by the small certainties of a life built without wonder. Dried fish wrapped in waxed cloth. A skin of melted snow. His knife roll, because he could not imagine being himself without it: twelve blades of varying temper, each worn to the shape of his hand. And the bones. He had stood over the shelf of them for a long moment, listening to the murmur of the dead who lived there — old Marta in the knuckle of a bear, the drowned ferryman in a rib curved like a boat — and in the end he took only three pieces, wrapped soft in wool. The rest he left behind like a man leaving letters he would never send.

The boy waited by the door with his breath clouding and his eyes enormous. He would not go back inside. He had not gone back inside since the riders' lanterns had swept the road below, since Sable had come up the path with her sword still drawn and her face still carrying whatever it was she had decided on the walk up — that look of a woman who has set down something heavy and cannot yet feel her hands.

"His name," Sable had said last night, crouched by the fire, "is not written anywhere. I checked the writ twice. They call him *the speech*. That's all."

Ada had looked at the boy across the flames, at the small hands folded in his lap like an old man's, and thought: *they hunt a child the way you'd hunt a plague.*

Now they hunted him in truth.

* * *

The pass above the valley was called the Shepherd's Stair, though no shepherd had used it in living memory, and Ada knew it only as a place where the wind spoke in one long unbroken syllable. They climbed it in the blue hour, Sable first with the boy strapped against her back in Ada's spare coat, Ada last with the pack and the knife roll and the terrible awareness of how loud his own breathing sounded against the snow.

Below them, three points of light moved along the valley road. Lanterns. But they burned wrong — too white, too steady, guttering nothing in the wind. Ada had seen memory-eaters work once, from a distance, from safety, from the blessed ignorance of a man who believed magic could not reach him here. The light had been the same then. White as paper. Steady as a held breath.

"They'll reach your workshop by midmorning," Sable said without turning. Her voice carried strangely in the thin air, flattened of everything but fact. "When they find it empty, they'll read what's left. Bones remember, bone-carver. Your whole shelf of them. Every voice you ever kept."

Ada's stomach turned over slowly, like a stone rolling downhill. He thought of Marta's laugh in the bear's knuckle. Of the ferryman's endless counting of rivers crossed.

"They won't find much," he said. "I carve what people give me. Grief, mostly. Grudges. The dead don't say anything the living didn't already know."

"That's not true." Sable glanced back at him, and in the pre-dawn grey her face was all angles and frost. "You know it isn't. Why do you think they want the boy so badly? Because some things remember themselves."

The boy stirred against her back. He had been silent the whole climb — silent as always, silent as stone — but now his mitten came up and touched Sable's ear, once, gently, the way you'd touch something to be sure it was real. She went very still. Then she kept climbing.

Magic could not follow them. That was the valley's oldest law and its only mercy; whatever ran beneath the world elsewhere ran dry here, drained away by some ancient wound in the land that even the Crown's scholars had stopped pretending to explain. It was why Ada had been born here, married here, buried his wife here. It was why the dead in his carvings were only echoes, voices in wood and ivory, harmless as hearth-smoke.

But the boy hummed anyway. Ada felt it through the pack straps, through the wool, through his own ribs: a low note like a struck bell heard through a wall. Whatever the child was, the valley's silence could not quite smother it. And where the silence ended — at the ridge, at the high snowline, at the border of the map — Ada suspected the humming would become a shout.

He climbed faster.

* * *

They made the ridge by full light and dropped down its far side into country Ada had never seen, which should have been impossible, because he had walked every fold of these mountains since boyhood. But the maps in his head simply stopped at the crest, the way a sentence stops when the speaker forgets what he meant to say. Beyond lay a bowl of white slopes and black rock, wind-scoured, treeless, and utterly unnamed.

Sable called a halt in the lee of a fallen crag. She set the boy down, checked his fingers and nose for frostbite with a briskness that fooled neither of them, and shared out the fish and the water. While they ate, she kept her sword across her knees, unsheathed, and watched the ridgeline behind them.

"You could put that away," Ada said.

"No."

"I'm not going to—"

"I know what you're not going to do." She said it without heat. She tore a strip of fish with her teeth and chewed, watching the sky. "I spent eleven years learning exactly what people are not going to do right up until they do it. Eat your fish, carver."

He ate his fish. The boy ate his in tiny precise bites, watching them both with that grave attention that made Ada feel like a carving someone was studying for flaws.

It was after they resumed — Sable leading now, breaking trail through knee-deep drifts, Ada carrying the boy piggyback when the slope steepened — that the drawing began.

The boy slid down from Ada's back at the mouth of a shallow col and knelt in the snow. He swept it flat with both mittens, considered, and drew.

A door. Just that: a rectangle, a lintel, a handle rendered in three strokes. He pressed it deep so the dark earth showed through, then sat back on his heels and looked at it with an expression Ada had seen a hundred times on the faces of the recently bereaved. Recognition. Longing. The look of a man hearing his own name spoken in a crowd.

Then he moved ten paces and drew another.

By the time the light began to fail they had passed — Ada counted, because counting was how he held fear at arm's length — thirty-one doors. Some were tall and narrow, some round-topped, one vast enough to drive a cart through. One had a keyhole the size of the boy's fist, and beside it, scratched with terrible care, a symbol like a crown with the points broken off. The boy worked without stopping, without eating, without looking back at them, and the snow around each door seemed — Ada told himself it was the light, the angle, the exhaustion — seemed somehow *colder*, as if each drawing opened onto a draft from somewhere very far away.

"What is he doing?" Sable asked quietly. She had stopped pretending to watch the ridgeline. She was watching the doors.

"He's remembering," Ada said. The word came out of him before he understood it, the way the dead sometimes pushed words into his mouth while he carved. He crouched by the latest drawing and laid two fingers in the groove. Cold poured up his arm like water finding a channel. Not the cold of snow. The cold of a room long shut. "Or he's showing us the way out. Or—" He looked up at the boy, who was watching him with those enormous patient eyes. "Or there are more doors than one, and he doesn't know which one is real."

The boy reached out and touched Ada's wrist. His mitten was soaked through; his hand beneath was warm, fever-warm, and under the warmth Ada felt the humming again, closer now, almost words. Almost a name.

Behind them, far off along their own backtrail, a point of white light crested the ridge they had crossed at dawn.

Sable saw it too. She didn't swear, which frightened Ada more than swearing would have. She simply stood, rolled her shoulders the way she did before executions — he'd seen the motion once, years ago, from the edge of a crowd, before he'd known whose hand held the blade — and said, "Down-valley. Now. We move through the night."

"They can't follow us fast in this—"

"They aren't following us." She pulled the boy up onto her hip; he went willingly, arms around her neck, face tucked into the crook of it, and something in her posture changed when he did, softened and hardened at once, like steel quenched. "They're following *him*. Everything else is just weather to them. Move."

* * *

They moved. Through the col and down a frozen waterfall's spine, boots ringing on blue ice, the boy a warm weight against Sable's chest. Through a stand of dead pines bent all one way like men flinching from a blow. Through a night so clear the stars came down close enough to cut. Twice Ada looked back and saw the white light pacing them, patient, unhurried, keeping always the same distance — the distance of something that knows exactly where you are and is letting you tire yourself out.

In the smallest hour, in the shelter of an overhang where the wind finally lost its grip on them, Sable built a fire no larger than a cupped hand and fed it twigs one at a time. The boy slept between them, breathing in the slow deep rhythm of a child who trusts the people beside him completely, and Ada found that this trust — undeserved, unearned, given anyway — sat in his chest heavier than the pack.

"She chose exile over one last killing," he said, to the fire. "That's what they'll say about you, isn't it. In the reports."

Sable didn't answer for a long time. When she did, her voice was roughened, quieter than he'd ever heard it.

"The report will say I died. They always write it that way. It's cleaner." She fed the fire another twig. "Eleven years, carver. Do you know how many names I said aloud before the blade fell? You have to. It's the law. The condemned must hear their own name from the hand that kills them, so there's no mistake, so the record is true." Her jaw worked. "I said four hundred and six names. And every single one of them was a lie, because half those people weren't guilty of anything except being born in a country the Crown decided to forget. I just didn't know it yet. I was the eraser and I didn't know what I was erasing."

"And now?"

"Now I count backward." She looked at the sleeping boy. "Four hundred and five. Four hundred and four. Maybe if I get low enough, someday I'll reach zero and start again."

Ada wanted to say something — about forgiveness, about the difference between the hand and the order — but the bones had taught him long ago that comfort spoken too easily is just noise, and so he said nothing, and the silence between them was, for the first time, not unfriendly.

It was the boy who broke it. He woke all at once, the way he always did, no drifting, no blinking — awake as a struck bell. He crawled to the mouth of the overhang, looked out at the snowfield below, and went rigid.

Then he was drawing again, kneeling in the blue starlight, sweeping the snow with desperate strokes. Door after door after door, faster than before, faster than play or memory — and this time, as Ada and Sable watched, the drawings began to change. The rectangles grew lintels carved with script. The handles became rings, then knockers shaped like open mouths. And on the last one, largest of all, drawn directly in their path toward the high pass, the boy pressed his whole palm flat and held it there, trembling, until the snow around the outline crystallized into something that gleamed like frosted glass.

Beyond it, faint as a memory of a memory, Ada saw falling snow that fell upward. A street. A lamplit window. A city holding its breath.

"Sable," he whispered. "Tell me you see it."

She saw it. Her hand had gone to her sword hilt, and her face, in the starlight, had gone the color of the snow.

And down the valley behind them, unhurried, inevitable, the white light rounded the shoulder of the mountain and began, at last, to close the distance.
