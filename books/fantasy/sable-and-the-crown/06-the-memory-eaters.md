## 6. The Memory-Eaters

The sun did not rise in Vhalen, but it *dimmed*.

Ada had learned this over the days they'd wandered the frozen city — that the grey light pressing down between the spires was not the absence of dawn but its corpse, a light that remembered being morning the way the ghosts below remembered being alive. It came from everywhere and nowhere, pooling in the streets like milk gone thin. He had stopped trying to name the hour. Hours here were a courtesy the country could no longer afford.

He sat on the steps of what had once been a chandler's shop, whittling. The habit had crossed the mountains with him, one of the few things the valley let him keep even here, where magic pooled in the gutters and sang through the walls. The bone in his hands was deer rib, salvaged from a midden two streets back, and as his knife found its curve, he felt the dead stir in it — not the valley dead, with their small murmured grievances, but Vhalen dead, who spoke in long vowels and griefs the size of cathedrals.

*Cold,* said the bone. *We were cold at the end. But we were singing.*

"You're always singing," Ada muttered.

The boy sat beside him, knees drawn up, watching the street with those eyes the color of deep ice over black water. The boy did not fidget. The boy did not shiver, though the cold here went through wool and skin and settled in the marrow like a tenant. He simply watched, and sometimes his lips moved around words no living tongue owned, and when he did, the nearest ghosts would pause in their half-remembered errands — a woman carrying bread she never ate, a lamplighter lighting lamps against a dark that never lifted — and turn their faces toward him like flowers toward a sun that wasn't there.

Sable stood at the corner, sword loose in its sheath, her breath smoking. She had taken to standing watch at the mouth of every street they entered, though nothing in Vhalen had threatened them yet. Nothing except the country itself, which was dying by inches, and which watched them back with ten thousand borrowed eyes.

"Something's wrong," Sable said.

Ada looked up. "Something is always—"

"No." She turned, and her face had gone the color of the light. "Listen."

He listened. And understood.

The singing had stopped.

Not faded — stopped, all at once, the way a heart stops, the way a room goes silent when a door opens. Every ghost on the chandler's street froze mid-stride: the bread-woman with her loaf half-raised, the lamplighter with his taper burning to nothing in his fingers. They stood like figures in an unfinished carving, waiting for the knife to finish or release them.

Then, far off across the rooftops, came a sound Ada knew from the valley, from stories told in low voices near fires: a sound like pages turning, if pages were made of skin. A sound like a great soft mouth opening.

"The memory-eaters," Sable breathed. "They've breached the ridge."

* * *

They ran.

Vhalen's streets were built for processions and festivals, wide and straight and merciless to anyone fleeing down them, so Sable cut them sideways, through alleys choked with snow that had never melted and never would, past windows where faces pressed the glass from inside houses that had been empty for forty years. The boy rode against Ada's chest, wrapped in Sable's cloak, and Ada felt the boy's heartbeat — slow, too slow, steady as a drum in a funeral march — knocking against his own ribs.

Behind them, the sound grew. Not louder. *Closer*, which was worse, because it did not travel through air but through memory itself; Ada heard it inside his teeth, inside the bone shavings in his pocket, inside his own recollection of his workshop door, which suddenly seemed very far away and very thin.

"What do they actually do?" he shouted.

"They eat," Sable called back. "I saw it once, from outside. A village that sheltered a Vhaleni survivor. By morning there was a village there, and people in it, and none of them could say why they'd ever lived anywhere else. The eaters don't kill you. They take the part of you that knew."

Ahead, the street opened onto a square dominated by a fountain frozen mid-splash, water hanging in white ropes above a basin full of coins no one would ever wish on again. Ghosts crowded the square — dozens of them, hundreds, pressed together in the pattern of some festival Ada couldn't name, holding hands, mid-song.

And at the square's far edge stood the eaters.

There were three of them, and they were almost men. Tall, grey-robed, their faces smooth as eggs, with the suggestion of features drawn on afterward by someone working from a description rather than a memory. Where their mouths should have been, the air bent inward, the way heat bends above a forge. As Ada watched, the foremost of them raised one hand — gently, almost tenderly — toward the crowd of ghosts.

The song ended.

It didn't fade. One moment the square rang with a hundred voices; the next, silence fell like an axe. The ghosts did not scream. That was the horror of it. They simply became less — their outlines softening, their faces going vague as wax left near a fire, their hands still linked but no longer remembering whose hands they held. The frontmost eater stepped into the crowd and began, unhurriedly, to feed.

"We can't fight them," Sable said. Her voice was flat, the voice of a professional assessing a job. "Blades pass through. I tried, forty years ago, before I understood what they were. You run, or you hide, or you become something they've already eaten."

The boy stirred against Ada's chest.

He lifted his head, and his eyes had changed — not in color but in depth, as though Ada were looking down a well that went much further than a boy should go. His lips moved. Sound came out, and for the first time since the snowbound night at Ada's door, the words resolved themselves into something Ada's bones understood without translation:

*They are eating my hands.*

Ada stared at him. "What?"

But the boy only reached up and touched his own face, wonderingly, the way a man touches a mask. And Ada felt it then, through the bone token sewn into the boy's collar, through every carving in his pack, through the marrow of his own arms: a pulling sensation, a thread running out from the child in all directions, threads tied to every ghost in the city, to the frozen fountain, to the grey light itself, to the stones underfoot —

To everything.

"Oh," Ada said. The word came out very small. "Oh, no. You're not *from* Vhalen."

The boy looked at him.

"You *are* Vhalen." Ada's hands were shaking. Forty years ago the Crown hadn't just erased a country from maps and records. Erasure needed an anchor, a knot where all the cut threads could be gathered and hidden away — and someone, some desperate mother or king or bone-carver, had knotted an entire nation into a single unborn soul and smuggled it out through the winter door. "Every ghost. Every street. Every name. When they eat the last of your people, boy, there won't be anything left of you either. You'll be—" He couldn't finish it. *A child-shaped hole. An unregistered speech. Nothing.*

Across the square, the first eater finished feeding and turned its smooth face toward them. It had noticed the threads. Of course it had. The boy was the largest meal in the world, and he was standing in the open, wearing a stolen cloak.

"Run," Sable said.

* * *

They ran until the city's geometry betrayed them — Vhalen's streets rearranging themselves the way dreams do, delivering them to a district of narrow houses leaning together like conspirators, where the snow lay undisturbed and the ghosts, mercifully, had already fled or faded. Ada set the boy down behind a wall of stacked firewood and pressed his forehead to the child's.

"Talk to me. What does it feel like? When they eat?"

The boy considered. Then he took Ada's hand and pressed it flat against his own chest, over his heart, and Ada's gift did the rest — the bone-deep hearing that had never worked this well in the magicless valley, that roared to life here in the drowned country like a struck bell.

And he heard it. Underneath the boy's slow heartbeat: voices. Thousands upon thousands of them, layered like sediment, like rings in a tree. A woman naming her daughter. A mason counting stones. A king, maybe, or a baker — crowns meant little here. Each voice a citizen. Each citizen a memory the boy carried because no one else remained to carry it.

And some of the voices were *gone*. Not silenced — absent, leaving gaps in the sediment, holes shaped like people. Ada counted the gaps without wanting to and stopped at thirty.

Thirty souls eaten since the eaters breached the ridge. Thirty pieces of a child, swallowed.

As he listened, a thirty-first voice thinned, frayed, and tore away — and the boy flinched as if struck, his eyes going briefly, terribly blank, the way a page goes blank.

"He's unraveling," Ada said hoarsely, to Sable, to the wall, to anyone. "Every ghost they take, they take out of *him*. They don't need to catch him. They just need time and a city full of the dead." He gripped the boy's shoulders. "Listen to me. Can you hear me? Is there somewhere in this city they haven't reached? Somewhere the dead are safe?"

The boy's lips moved. The old language poured out, urgent now, frightened — and Ada caught fragments through the bone in his pocket, enough to assemble the shape of it:

*Below. Below the palace. The names are kept below. If the names are spoken, we cannot be eaten. A person with a true name is a door that will not open.*

"A door," Ada repeated. His mind was already moving, the carver's mind, finding the shape inside the material. "The archive. There's an archive under the palace, isn't there. Records. Registers. *Names.*"

The boy nodded, and for a moment his face was just a child's face — exhausted, terrified, trusting — and Ada felt something crack open in his chest wider than it had cracked the night the boy arrived at his door.

From three streets away, soft as turning pages, came the sound of the second eater beginning to feed.

Sable appeared at the mouth of the alley, snow in her hair, her hand white-knuckled on her sword's grip even though the blade was useless. "They're spreading out. Working the districts in order. We have until they reach the palace quarter — a day, perhaps less." She hesitated, and something moved behind her eyes, something she had been carrying since the executions, since the ledger, since the first lie she'd signed her name to. "Ada. There may be a way to save the names. I never told you why I really kept the ledger."

The third eater's feeding began, and beneath Ada's palm the boy's heartbeat stuttered — and a voice that had been singing in him for forty years went silent forever.
