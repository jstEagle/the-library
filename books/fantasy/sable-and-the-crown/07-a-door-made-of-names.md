## 7. A Door Made of Names

The way down was through the palace's drowned throat.

Ada had thought at first that the boy meant a cellar — some royal wine store flooded by centuries of meltwater — but as they descended the stair behind the throne room, the walls changed under his fingertips. The stone grew smoother, older, worked with reliefs so worn they were more suggestion than image: processions of figures, their faces rubbed to blankness, marching in lines toward something below. The boy's small hand stayed pressed flat against the wall the whole way down, and where he touched it, the carvings seemed to deepen, as if the stone remembered being young.

The water began at the ninth step.

It lay black and utterly still across the stairwell, a mirror without a sky, and Ada stopped with his boot an inch above it. In the valley, water had been honest — cold, quick, indifferent. This water watched.

"It won't take us," Sable said behind him. Her voice had grown strange since they'd entered the city proper — quieter, flatter, the voice of someone walking through her own guilt made physical. "It's not really water. It's what's left when you erase a country's name from every map. It has to go somewhere."

"Then how do we cross?"

"We don't." The boy tugged Ada's sleeve and pointed down.

Ada looked, and his breath went out of him in a long white plume. Beneath the surface — beneath the blackness — there were stairs. Thousands of them, spiraling into depths no lantern could plumb, and on every stair stood figures. Not ghosts exactly; ghosts, he had learned these past days, flickered and repeated themselves like moths against glass. These figures were still. They stood shoulder to shoulder in ranks that descended beyond counting, faces upturned, mouths closed, waiting with the terrible patience of things that had waited forty years already.

"They're names," the boy said, in that language older than kingdoms that Ada now understood the way one understands one's own heartbeat — not learned, but remembered. "The archive is made of them. Every citizen who ever lived in Vhalen is a step. You walk down on your own dead."

* * *

He did not remember deciding to step. He only knew that his foot found the first figure's shoulders, and the figure bore him without complaint, and the water closed over his boots without wetting them. The boy went ahead of him, light as a leaf on a current, and Sable came last, and Ada heard her breath catch each time she stepped on someone — heard her whisper *forgive me, forgive me, forgive me* in a rhythm that never broke, all the way down.

The archive, when they reached it, was a hall the size of a cathedral drowned in amber light. Shelves rose out of the gloom in tiers, but the shelves held no books. They held bones. Ribs and femurs and skulls, catalogued and labeled in a script that predated any kingdom Ada knew, arranged not by family or rank but — he understood this slowly, with a bone-carver's instinct — by voice. Each shelf held bones that had belonged to people whose voices sounded alike. Somewhere in this city, someone had believed that a person's name lived in the timbre of their throat, and had archived a nation accordingly.

And the bones were singing.

Not aloud. It was the hum he knew from his own workshop, magnified ten thousand times — the resonance of the dead held in living material, except here the material was dying too. As Ada walked the aisles, he saw whole shelves gone grey and silent, the bones crumbling to chalk at a touch. Whatever the memory-eaters devoured above, its hunger reached down here as well. The archive was starving.

The boy stopped at the hall's far end, before a wall that was not shelves but a single slab of black stone, tall as three men, seamless except for a hairline seam in the shape of a door. No handle. No keyhole. Only, carved around the frame in letters deep as finger-joints, words in the old language that Ada read without knowing how:

*HERE THE COUNTRY LEAVES. SPEAK US, AND BE SPOKEN.*

"A door," Sable said, coming up beside him. "There's actually a door."

"There's always a door," Ada murmured. His hand hovered over the seam. The hum came off it like heat off a forge, and within the hum he could hear — faintly, the way one hears rain beginning — voices. Thousands. Hundreds of thousands. All of them saying the same thing over and over, patient as the figures on the stairs: *our names. Our names. Our names.*

"The dead told me," the boy said. He was looking up at the door with an expression no child should wear — the expression of a country considering its own funeral. "In the snow, when I drew them. Every door I drew was this one. It opens for true names. All of them. Spoken aloud, by one mouth, in order, without stopping." He looked at Ada then, and his eyes were the grey of the sunless sky above the city. "Every citizen. Everyone who ever lived here. Forty years of erased births and erased deaths and erased everything between. One person has to carry all of it out loud."

"That's impossible," Sable said flatly. "No one remembers a nation."

"No," Ada said quietly. He was still looking at the carved words. *Speak us, and be spoken.* "But someone might have written one down."

* * *

They searched the archive while the light failed — though in Vhalen, Ada was learning, the light did not fail so much as grow tired. He worked shelf by shelf with his carver's hands, reading labels, feeling for the hum of intact bone, and the boy followed him touching each silent shelf as if he could warm it back to speech. Most of them he could not. The hunger above was methodical. It ate in the order maps had once been drawn: the coast first, then the river towns, then the capital's outer quarters. Whole districts of the archive were chalk dust and silence.

It was Sable who found the alcove.

She had drifted apart from them — she did that, in this city, pulled toward corners and stairwells as if by hooks set in her — and when Ada finally traced her silence to a recess behind the last tier of shelves, he found her standing very still, holding a metal box with both hands, the way one holds a wounded bird or a live coal.

"Sable."

"I know what this is," she said. Her voice came out sanded smooth, stripped of everything. "I've known since we crossed the ridge. I hoped I was wrong. I prayed, which is a thing I haven't done since I was a girl, and I'm telling you it didn't work."

She opened the box.

Inside, packed in oiled cloth gone stiff with age, lay a ledger. Not paper — vellum scraped thin as leaf-skin, bound in grey leather, and Ada knew grey leather; he had tanned enough hides in his life to know it came from no animal. It was the color of the memory-eaters' coats.

"You kept records," Ada said. Not a question. The shape of it was assembling itself in him with dreadful clarity. "An executioner keeps records. Dates. Sentences."

"Names." She turned the ledger so the open page faced him. Columns of script, tight and precise, in two languages side by side — the Crown's tongue and the old one, matched line to line like a translation waiting forty years to be needed. "That was the other half of my office. Nobody tells you that. An executioner doesn't just kill. An executioner *certifies*. The Crown required that every sentence be recorded with the condemned's true name — not the name on the registers, the true one, the one given at birth, because—" her jaw tightened— "because they said false names let the soul escape justice. So we wrote them down. All of them. And when the decree came, when they struck Vhalen from the maps, the executioners' ledgers weren't touched." She laughed, one dry note with no humor in it anywhere. "Do you understand? They erased a country and forgot to erase its hangman's paperwork. We were beneath notice. We were *tools*."

Ada took the ledger from her hands. It was heavier than it should have been, the way all true things are heavy. He turned pages. Names in their thousands, each with a date, a crime — most entries read simply *speech*, or *remembrance*, or the old word the boy used for *refusing to forget* — and each with the true name written twice, once to kill it in the Crown's tongue, once preserved in the language of the dead.

"This is most of them," he breathed. "Sable, this is—"

"Not everyone. I wasn't the only executioner, and I wasn't sent here until the third year of the erasure. There are gaps. Two years of the coast districts, the northern valleys—" She stopped. Started again, quieter. "I counted, on the ridge, before I decided to bring you here instead of turning back. If the door needs every name, the gaps will kill us. The door will open partway, maybe. Enough for one. Maybe not even that."

"But you kept yours." Ada looked up from the page and found her eyes. "You carried it out of the city when you fled. You've carried it for thirty years. You slept with it under your floor, didn't you. You couldn't burn it and you couldn't use it and you couldn't look at it, so you just — kept it."

"What else does a tool do," Sable said, "when it's thrown away? It keeps what it was made to keep."

The boy had come to stand beside them, and he laid his small hand flat on the open ledger, and the vellum warmed under his palm like skin. From somewhere deep in the archive — from the stairs of standing figures, from the singing shelves — a sound rose that Ada had not heard in all his days in this dead country. A sigh. Long and collective, the exhale of a nation that had been holding its breath for forty years.

Hope, Ada thought, and found that hope could feel like a wound. Because he understood, now, what the door demanded, and what the ledger offered, and the arithmetic between them. Someone would have to stand before that black stone and speak every name in this book, in order, without stopping — thousands upon thousands of names, hours upon hours, while the memory-eaters tore through the city above and the frost came down like a closing fist. And speaking them would cost. Everything the dead had shown him in the snow-drawings had a price marked on it. Doors in Vhalen did not open. They *traded*.

Above them, muffled by fathoms of stone and black water, came a sound like a great bell being swallowed.

All three of them froze.

The boy's head tilted, listening with whatever he listened with — that country-sized soul wearing a child's body. When he spoke, his voice was very small.

"They've found the palace."

Sable was already moving, sliding the ledger into her coat, checking the blade at her hip with hands that did not shake — executioner's hands, steady at the exact moment steadiness mattered most. "How long?"

The boy looked at Ada. "Until they reach the water? One night. Until they reach this room?" He shook his head. "The dead won't say. The dead are afraid to say."

Ada looked at the black door, at the carved command glowing faintly now, as if the stone itself had heard the ledger open. *Speak us, and be spoken.* Then at Sable, standing in the amber dark with thirty years of complicity bound in grey leather against her heart — the terrible, improbable truth that the Crown's own instrument of forgetting had become the last vessel of remembering.

"One night," he said. "Then we'd better learn every name in that book before morning."

"No," Sable said quietly, drawing the ledger out again, opening it to the first page, where the oldest entry waited in faded brown ink. "You don't understand yet. You don't *learn* them." She looked up at him, and in the drowned light her eyes were the color of the door. "You *become* them. That's the toll, Ada. Whoever speaks the names gives themselves to be spoken."
