## 10. Spring, Unremembered

The valley woke him the way a stranger wakes you — gently, and with no right to.

Ada Iversen opened his eyes to green light filtered through young leaves, to the smell of thawed earth and cut wood, to birdsong he could not name. He lay in a bed he did not remember making, in a house whose beams he had never planed, above a workshop that nonetheless felt like it had been waiting for him all his life. His hands were scarred in patterns he recognized without knowing their stories: knife-nicks along the thumbs, a pale burn across the left palm shaped like a crescent moon.

He sat up slowly, the way an old man does when he suspects the floor of holding opinions about him.

There was a boy asleep in the next room.

He knew this the way he knew his own heartbeat — not as memory but as fact, bedrock-deep, older than any question of how. He rose and stood in the doorway and watched the child breathe. Dark hair, a face still round with the last of childhood, fingers curled loosely around something at his throat. A token of carved bone, worn smooth as river stone.

When the boy woke, he looked up at Ada and said a word.

It was a word from no language Ada had ever studied, and yet it settled into him whole and warm, the way sunlight settles into a stone wall. It meant *father*. Or something near enough to father that the difference did not matter. Or perhaps it meant *the one who stays*, which was a kind of fathering too.

"Good morning," Ada said, because that was the only tongue he had, and the boy smiled as if the two languages had shaken hands somewhere behind the world where no one could see them do it.

His name was Tam. Ada knew this without knowing how. It was simply one of the things he carried, like the scars on his palms, like the way his fingers itched toward the carving knives each morning before he was fully awake.

* * *

The valley had a name, but it slid off him whenever anyone said it, like water off oiled wood. The neighbors were kind in the distant way mountain folk are kind — they brought bread when Tam had a fever, and never asked why the bone-carver's son spoke such strange syllables in his sleep, or why the carvings in Ada's window sometimes seemed to lean toward the boy like wheat toward sun.

Ada worked. That much of himself had survived whatever he had paid or lost or given away; he could feel the absence of the rest the way you feel a missing tooth, always probing the gap with your tongue. There had been another life. He was sure of it. There had been snow — the certainty arrived some mornings so strongly he would stand at his door watching the last grey ridges of winter retreat up the mountainside, half-expecting them to close over the pass like a mouth. There had been voices. Not the living kind. Voices that lived in bone, that spoke through the grain of antler and ivory and the long dense bones of cattle, and he had listened to them, he had *worked* with them —

But when he picked up a chisel now, the bones were only bones. Dense and silent and beautiful, holding nothing but the animal they had belonged to. He carved them anyway, because his hands demanded it, and because the work was good, and because some mornings the shapes came out of the blank material already finished — spirals, doorways, small figures with their arms raised — as though his hands remembered designs his mind had been forbidden to keep.

Tam watched him carve with an attention that went beyond a child's curiosity. Sometimes the boy would take a finished piece and hold it to his ear, listening, and then look up at Ada with an expression too old for his face — grief and patience braided together — and say nothing at all.

"What do you hear?" Ada asked once, kneeling to be level with him.

Tam considered the question with great seriousness. Then he answered in his old, old language, and Ada understood him anyway, the meaning arriving in his chest before it reached his ears:

*"Echoes. But they're going home."*

* * *

Spring deepened. The valley filled itself with ordinary miracles — lambs, mud, the first bitter greens, weddings in the low church where the priest had a voice like a cart on gravel. Ada learned the valley's rhythms the way an adopted child learns a family's faces: correctly, thoroughly, and always a half-step behind, always translating.

He told himself he was content. Most days he even believed it.

Then, on an evening in late spring when the light came through the workshop window thick and gold as honey, he found the token.

It was in the bottom of a chest he had never opened — or rather, a chest he had opened every day of his life here without ever once looking beneath the folded wool blankets, as though a hand inside his own had been holding the lid of that lower compartment shut. Tonight the hand let go. Perhaps it was the light. Perhaps it was that Tam was out gathering firewood and the house was empty enough to hold a truth.

Beneath the blankets, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff with age, lay a piece of carved bone no longer than his thumb.

It was a woman.

Not a portrait exactly — bone does not flatter — but a figure rendered with the economy of a master: shoulders squared, chin lifted, a blade held point-down at her side in a posture that was neither threat nor surrender but *witness*. Her hair was worked into long lines that might have been wind or might have been mourning-veils. And around the base of the figure, in letters tiny as frost, ran a ring of script in no alphabet Ada knew — and yet his eyes moved across it and his mouth almost shaped the sounds, almost, the way a sleeper almost speaks.

He turned it in his fingers.

It hummed.

Not a sound in the air — a sound in the bone, in the marrow of his own arm, rising through his wrist like water finding an old channel. And threaded through the hum, faint as a voice heard through a wall, through years, through the closing of a door —

*"Ada."*

He went very still. Dust motes hung in the gold light. Somewhere outside a thrush was ruining the silence beautifully.

*"Ada."* Again. A woman's voice, low and roughened, the voice of someone who had shouted orders and confessions and names, many names, so many names — *"You stubborn, quiet man. If this found you, then it worked. Then he worked. Listen to me, because I don't know how long the carving will hold my voice, and there are things you must — "*

The voice frayed. He gripped the token harder, pressing it to his ear like a shell, like a door held shut against a wind.

*"— don't grieve for what you gave. You gave it freely and it bought a life, and lives are the only currency that was ever worth anything. Do you hear me? The only — "*

Static, or snowfall, or the sound a country makes when it finishes dying.

*"— his name is Tam. Keep his name safe. Say it every day. Names are doors, Ada, and as long as someone says them — "*

And then, softer, so soft he had to close his eyes to catch it, a tone he somehow understood had never been spent on anyone else in all her hard and guilty and magnificent life:

*"I stayed so the names would stay spoken. You left so he would stay alive. Between us we made a fair accounting. Don't come looking for me. There's nothing left to find — only a witness at her post, and winters that don't end, and a country that thanks you both by forgetting itself a little more each year.*

*But Ada. Ada. When the boy laughs — when he says your word for father — listen to what it does to your chest. That's me. That's the part of me that walked out with you, because you couldn't carry the valley and I couldn't carry you, so we split the load like sensible traitors.*

*I loved you. I never said it while I had a mouth that worked properly, so I'm saying it into bone instead, where it will outlast both of us. Bone keeps what flesh is afraid to say.*

*Sable. My name was Sable. Remember that if you can't remember anything else."*

The hum faded. It did not stop — he could feel it still, faintly, under his fingertips, a pulse too slow to be a heartbeat and too steady to be an echo — but the voice withdrew, the way a tide withdraws, leaving the shape of itself in the sand.

Ada sat on the floor of his own workshop for a long time, in the failing gold light, holding a woman made of bone who had loved him in a life he could not enter.

He did not weep. Grief needs a place to stand, and everywhere he looked the ground had been sold from under it. What he felt instead was stranger and more durable: a fullness. As though a debt had been paid in his name by someone who never explained the terms, and the interest was this ache, this warmth, this unbearable tenderness for a world he had apparently crossed a country of death to buy.

The door banged open. Tam stood in it, arms full of firewood, cheeks red with evening cold, and took in the scene — the open chest, the old man on the floor, the token in his hand — with those too-old eyes.

He set down the wood. He crossed the room. He did not ask what Ada held, or where it had come from, or why his father's face was doing what it was doing. Instead he climbed into Ada's lap, the way he had done since before either of them could remember why it felt necessary, and pressed his ear against the token, and listened.

After a moment the boy looked up.

"She says your name," Tam reported gravely, in his language older than kingdoms. "She says it like she's keeping it warm."

"Yes," Ada managed.

"Is she coming back?"

Ada looked at the window, where the last light was going violet over ridges that had never known snow deep enough to seal a door. He looked at the bone in his hand, humming its slow faithful pulse. He thought of a ledger of names spoken into the dark by a woman who refused to leave her post, a country kept alive the only way a country can be — by being witnessed — and a door somewhere in the high passes that opened only in winter, that might, that *might*, if a man were foolish enough to climb—

"No," he said gently. "She's staying where she's needed."

Tam nodded, satisfied, and laid his head against Ada's chest.

But that night, after the boy was asleep, Ada took a fresh piece of bone from his stores — dense, pale, unmarked — and set it on his bench beside the chisels. His hands were already moving. They seemed to know the design without consulting him: a doorway, tall and narrow, its lintel worked with a ring of frost-lettered script.

And at the threshold of the little carved door, a figure. A man, mid-stride, walking toward winter.

Outside, far up past the treeline, the last of the spring snow lingered in the high shadows of the pass — and Ada found, watching it gleam in the starlight, that he was already wondering how long until autumn.
