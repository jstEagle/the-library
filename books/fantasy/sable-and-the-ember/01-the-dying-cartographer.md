## 1. The Dying Cartographer

Greyhollow has exactly three things worth mapping: the tavern, the toll bridge, and the way out. I've drawn all three so many times I could do them blindfolded, and some nights, after enough of Tam's rotgut, I have.

The rest of my trade lives in the Thornfells — smuggler roads, goat tracks, passes that don't officially exist because the Crown finds it cheaper not to know about them. I sell those maps to people who ask no questions, which in Greyhollow is everyone, and I sell them at prices calibrated to how nervous the asking looks. It's honest work, in the sense that everything in it is true and nothing in it is legal.

My stall sits in the corner of the Drowned Goose, wedged between a rain barrel and the wall where the plaster sweats. I'd been there since noon, haggling with a wool merchant over a chart of the Lowmire crossing — he wanted it for two coppers, I wanted five, we were both enjoying ourselves more than either would admit — when the door banged open and the cold came in wearing a man.

He was wrong in every particular. Wrong season for that thin a coat, wrong road for boots that muddy, wrong town for a face that gray. He crossed the common room like a man following instructions written on the inside of his eyelids, and he stopped at my stall and put one hand flat on my table as if to prove the world was still there.

"You're the mapmaker," he said. Not a question. His voice had gravel and blood in it.

"I'm a mapmaker," I said. "There's a difference. What can I draw you?"

He didn't answer. He looked at my charts — the neat rows of Thornfell passes, the coastlines, the tidy little Greyhollow with its three attractions — and something in his face did a thing I didn't like, something like recognition, or grief, or both sharing a coat.

Then his knees went.

I caught him mostly by accident, more out of the reflex of a woman who's spent years catching falling tankards than any nobility of spirit. He was heavier than he looked and lighter than he should have been, the way men get when they're bleeding somewhere I couldn't see. We went down behind the stall together, and Tam shouted something from the taps, and the wool merchant wisely decided his coppers were spent and left.

"Stabbed?" I asked, peeling back his coat. Underneath: a wound low on his side, bound with a strip of linen gone brown-black, soaked through twice over. Old work, badly done. He hadn't been stabbed today. He'd been walking on this for days, which told me two things: whoever cut him hadn't finished the job, and whatever he was carrying mattered more to him than stopping the leak.

"Not stabbed," he said, which was unhelpful and also a lie, but I let it go, because by then I'd stopped being a cartographer and started being the closest thing Greyhollow had to a surgeon, which is a job title earned entirely by owning clean cloth and strong drink.

I cleaned the wound. He took it the way soldiers take it — silent, jaw working, eyes fixed on the ceiling beams as if counting them. I stitched what I could reach. I made him drink half a cup of Tam's brandy, which is medically indistinguishable from setting fire to yourself, but it kept his hands from shaking.

"Who did this?" I asked, because I'm curious before I'm careful, which my father always said would be the death of me. He'd said it the way fathers say such things — meaning *don't be me*.

"Everyone," the stranger said, and almost smiled. "Eventually."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only kind I have left." He turned his head and looked at me properly for the first time. Gray eyes, fever-bright. "Lena Marchetti."

My hands went still on the bandage. "I didn't give you my name."

"No," he agreed. "You didn't."

There are perhaps six people alive who know my full name, and I could count the ones I'd greet warmly on fewer fingers. A stranger bleeding out behind my stall in the Drowned Goose should not be one of them. Every sensible instinct I own — and I own several, they're just badly trained — stood up and told me to finish the bandage, take my fee, and let the man find his own grave.

Instead I said, "How do you know it?"

"A friend drew you a letter once," he said. "You never got it. He kept it anyway. Said your name looked good in ink." His breath caught. "Said you'd have his hands."

I want to tell you I understood then. I didn't. Understanding came later, in pieces, the way it usually does, and by then it had teeth. In that moment all I felt was cold starting at the base of my skull, because my father had been missing for eleven years, and the last thing he'd left me was a drafting set and a note that said *keep drawing*, and I had built an entire stubborn life on not asking why.

"Your friend," I said carefully. "What was his name?"

The stranger closed his eyes. For a horrible moment I thought I'd lost him mid-sentence, which would be just my luck — the one man who knew something about my father, dying in a tavern famous for its ale and infamous for everything else.

"Tam," he rasped suddenly. "Water."

I got water. He drank. The color in his face moved around without settling anywhere, like weather deciding.

While I held the cup, his hand found mine — cold fingers, grip still surprisingly strong — and pressed something into my palm. Folded paper, soft at the creases, handled many times. I knew the weight of folded paper the way a mother knows her child's cry, and I knew before opening it that this was no ordinary chart.

"Don't read it here," he whispered.

"Then where—"

"Thirteen turns," he said.

"What does that mean? Thirteen turns of what? The road? The moon?" Because I'm a mapmaker, and turns mean switchbacks, and moons mean deadlines, and both meant someone had built a clock into my life without consulting me.

But he wasn't listening anymore. His eyes had gone past me, to the doorway, to something that wasn't there yet, and his breathing changed — shorter, wetter. I pressed harder on the bandage. Blood came through my fingers warm and unhurried, the way it does when the body has already filed its resignation.

"Hey. Hey. Look at me. Who's coming? Who do I give this to?" I shook his shoulder. "Give it to who?"

His hand rose — trembling, patient — and closed over both of mine, over the folded paper, pressing it against my chest like a seal on wax.

"Nowhere," he said. "That's the point. That's always been—" And the sentence ended the way roads end on bad maps: abruptly, without a destination marked.

He died in the Drowned Goose at roughly the hour the evening crowd began to arrive, and the crowd, being Greyhollow, pretended not to notice, because noticing things is expensive here. Tam helped me carry him out back. We buried him shallow, under the alder, because the ground was frozen and neither of us owned a shovel worth the name. I said words over him. I don't remember which ones. I remember thinking his friend — my father's friend, if that's what he was — had kept a letter eleven years and never sent it, and that seemed like the saddest arithmetic I'd ever heard.

* * *

I took the paper home because I couldn't decide what else to do with it, and indecision, in my experience, is just curiosity wearing a disguise.

My room above the chandler's shop smells of tallow and old ink, which suits me. I lit my lamp, sat at my drafting table — my father's drafting table, actually, scarred with compass points and one burn mark shaped like a crescent — and put the folded chart down in front of me.

I didn't open it. I want that on the record. For a long time — an hour, maybe two — I sat there and did not open it, because part of me, the part raised on my father's warnings and my own hard-won caution, understood that some papers change the shape of your life when unfolded, the way some doors only exist once opened.

Then I opened it, because I am my father's daughter, and his warnings never survived contact with my curiosity either.

It was a map. Of course it was a map. But it was wrong in every particular, wrong the way the stranger had been wrong — drawn in a style I recognized down to my bones, the fine hairline contours, the little hooked arrows for wind direction, the habit of labeling rivers in a slanted hand that leaned east like it was hurrying somewhere.

My father's hand. My father's style. Drawn with his tools, or their twins.

Except I knew these mountains, and these weren't them. The Thornfells were there, unmistakable, the great spine of them running north — but threaded through them were roads I had never charted, never heard of, roads that shouldn't exist. Valleys marked where I'd surveyed bare stone. A river where the Highfell dry wash runs. And at the center, high up past anything passable, a small circle of mountains surrounding a blank space, and in the blank space, in that slanted hurrying hand, a single word:

*Ember.*

I sat back. I told myself: forgery. Someone baiting a trap, using Dad's style because they'd stolen his drafts — and the thought of his drafts in a stranger's hands hurt worse than the wound downstairs had.

Then I looked away. Just for a moment — to feed the lamp, I think, or scratch my neck, some small human errand. When I looked back, the map had changed.

The river had moved. Not redrawn — *moved*, the ink crawling like something waking, the blue line sliding westward into a valley it hadn't touched before, and along its new bank a tiny notation had appeared that hadn't been there: *the ford drowns liars.*

I did the reasonable thing. I dropped it on the table, backed off my stool, and considered burning it. My lamp was right there. The grate was right there. One decision, cleanly made, and I could go back to selling passes to smugglers and pretending my father had simply walked out of the world without leaving anything in it but a burn mark shaped like a crescent.

I picked it up again instead.

Because here is the truth about me, the one I keep under the trade talk and the wry prices: I cannot leave an unread line alone. A map with a hidden road is a hook, and I have been swallowing hooks since I was six years old, tracing my father's contours while other children learned normal things. Whatever this document was — forgery, trap, miracle — it was drawn in the hand that taught me mine, and it knew my name, and it had waited eleven years to reach me, and I wanted, with a hunger that frightened me more than the moving ink did, to know why.

I spread it flat. I weighted the corners. I watched it for an hour and it did nothing, smug as a sleeping cat, and I understood that it only moved when I wasn't looking — which meant it was shy, or cunning, or ashamed, and none of those options comforted me.

Near dawn, too tired to be afraid anymore, I bent close and examined the margins, because my father always hid his best work in margins. And there, in the lower corner, so faint I'd taken it for a stain: a small sigil. A compass rose, yes, but with seven points instead of eight, and the seventh point broken off short.

I have that same mark burned into the handle of my drafting pen. My father cut it there himself, laughing, saying every mapmaker needs a signature the Crown can't tax.

The lamp guttered. Outside, Greyhollow was waking — carts, gulls, the toll bell — an ordinary morning arriving in an ordinary town where a man had died last night whispering numbers at me.

*Thirteen turns.*

I looked down at the map, and in the space below the word *Ember*, in ink that had definitely not been there when I lit the lamp, two new lines had appeared, small and precise and addressed, unmistakably, to me:

*Lena. Don't trust the ones who come asking. Trust the road.*
