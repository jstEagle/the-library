## 2. Ink That Remembers

I did not sleep so much as negotiate with sleep, and lost.

The stranger's map lay on my worktable where I'd left it, folded into quarters, weighted down with the brass inkwell I'd used since I was nine years old. Every sensible instinct I owned — and I own several, they're just badly paid — said to burn it. Feed it to the stove, sweep up the ash, charge someone for a nice map of the Greyhollow docks, and go back to being a woman whose problems could be measured in unpaid tabs and damp walls.

Instead I lit a candle at midnight and unfolded it again.

The paper was good stock. Old. The kind of vellum that costs more than my stall earns in a season, with that soft animal warmth to it even now, hours after its owner had gone cold in my back room. Old Marta had taken him out on a plank board an hour past dawn, and I'd paid for the burial myself, because nobody else was going to, and because I have this flaw where I do the decent thing and then resent it for weeks.

The roads were still there. That was the first wrong thing. When he'd pressed it into my hands, bleeding out against my counter, the chart had shown maybe half a dozen lines — smuggler tracks, the sort of thing I chart myself when the Crown's patrols aren't looking. Now there were dozens. Scores. Roads threading through the Thornfells like veins through a wrist, branching, doubling back, some of them crossing terrain where no road could stand. One of them ran straight up the face of Hollow Peak itself, which is not a thing roads do. Hollow Peak is a cliff wearing two smaller cliffs as shoulders. Men have died trying to climb it sober, and men don't attempt it any other way.

I told myself I'd misremembered. Grief does strange things to the witness, and I'd spent that whole terrible evening elbow-deep in a dying man's business. Memory is just a map you draw of your own life, and mine has always been drawn quickly and to scale only by accident.

Then the ink moved.

Not while I watched. It was better behaved than that. I looked away — one heartbeat, no more, just to rub the grit from my eyes — and when I looked back, a new road had opened through the Greymire lowlands like a wound deciding to heal in reverse. Wet. Shining. The ink hadn't dried. Ten minutes later it still hadn't dried, and I sat there with my finger hovering an inch above it, not touching, the way you don't touch a snake even when it's small.

"Right," I said aloud, to the empty room, because saying things aloud makes them either true or ridiculous, and ridiculous is safer. "You're going to explain yourself."

It did not. Maps never do. But around the eastern margin, in a hand smaller than the rest, finer, almost apologetic, letters surfaced the way drowned things surface: slowly, and all at once.

*Thirteen turns.*

The same words he'd whispered. His last breath, spent on my counter, smelling of blood and pine resin, and now written in his own careful cartographer's script on a map that was writing itself while I watched.

I want to tell you I closed it then. I want to tell you I'm the kind of woman who recognizes a door she shouldn't open. But I come from a long line of people who open doors, and my father used to say that a map is just a question asked carefully enough, and I have never once in my life left a question alone.

* * *

By the third hour past midnight I'd stopped pretending I was doing anything but reading it.

The roads weren't random. That was the second wrong thing, and worse than the first. They converged. Every track, every trail, every impossible line up impossible stone bent eventually toward a single point high in the Thornfells — a place marked not with the conventional symbol for a summit or a shrine, but with a small circle, and inside the circle, a flame.

Drawn in red. Everything else on the map was black iron-gall ink, the honest brown-black of a working draftsman. Except the flame. Red, fine as a hair, and it seemed — I know how this sounds — it seemed *warm*. Like looking at it too long made the rest of the room cooler by comparison.

And beneath the flame, in that same small apologetic hand, a sigil.

A circle bisected by a vertical line, with three short strokes descending from the crossbar like rain off a roof edge. I knew it. Of course I knew it. I'd known it before I could write my own name, because it was stamped in the corner of every practice chart my father ever made me copy, pressed into the wax seals on his letter tubes, scratched idly into tabletops when he was thinking. My father's drafting mark. His maker's mark. The signature he put on maps the way other men sign their sins.

My father had been missing for eleven years. Missing the way men who walk into the Thornfells are missing — which is to say, dead, though without a body you can keep the account open, and I had kept it open the way you keep a lamp lit in a window for someone who isn't coming. Not hope, exactly. Habit.

Now here was his mark, fresh-inked, on a document that drew its own roads, carried by a stranger who had died reaching for me specifically. Out of every map-stall in every border town between here and the coast, he'd crawled to mine.

Coincidence is a word cartographers use for patterns we haven't surveyed yet.

I traced the sigil without touching it, my shadow falling over the page, and that was when the third wrong thing happened, and this one I will be honest about: it took the floor out from under me.

In the lower corner of the map, in the space where a proper chart carries its attribution — *Surveyed and Drawn by*, the formal line, the line I write on my own work every day — new letters were surfacing. Slowly. Letter by letter, like something rising through deep water.

L. M. A. R. C. H. E. T. T. I.

*Lena Marchetti.*

My name. Spelled correctly, including the double T that everyone gets wrong, including the tax assessor, including the priest who buried my mother. My name, on a map drawn by a man I'd never seen before last night, in my father's hand, in my father's secret cipher of a signature.

I did not scream. I want credit for that. I sat very still for what felt like a year and what was probably forty seconds, and then I stood up, walked to the window, opened it, and breathed cold air until my hands stopped shaking, which took longer than I'd like to admit.

The town below was asleep. Greyhollow at night is a handful of roofs and a lot of dark, the mountains standing behind it like patient creditors. Somewhere out there, a man had died to bring this thing to me. Somewhere out there, whatever killed him might be looking for it still.

And somewhere under my sternum, dread was curdling into something I recognized from my father's stories about himself: obsession. The specific, greedy hunger of a person who has found a question with her family's name on it.

I went back to the table. Of course I went back to the table.

* * *

Dawn came grey and thin, and with it came the Crown.

I heard them before I saw them — boots on the boards outside, too many, too coordinated for this hour in a town where nothing opens before the second bell. I had just enough time to fold the map, shove it into the false bottom of my chart drawer — built for hiding exactly this sort of thing from exactly these sorts of people, thanks, Da — and arrange my face into the expression of a tired businesswoman before the door swung open.

Three of them. Inquisitors don't travel in pairs; pairs can be bribed symmetrically, or so the joke goes in border towns, quietly. The one in front wore the grey-and-scarlet of the Chancellery with the particular neatness of a man who irons his convictions. Forty-ish, clean-shaven, eyes like a ledger. He set a folded warrant on my counter with the gentleness of someone laying down a winning card.

"Mistress Marchetti."

They'd used my name. Before I'd given it. Before anyone had. My mouth went dry in a way that had nothing to do with fear of the warrant.

"We're looking for a traveler," he continued. "Passed through yesterday evening. Tall, grey-bearded, wounded." He let the pause sit there, letting me fill it, which is an interrogation technique and also just rude. "He would have sought out a mapmaker."

"Half the people who come through here seek out a mapmaker," I said. "It's the only reason my landlord hasn't evicted me."

"His name?"

"If he gave one, he didn't pay extra for me to remember it."

One of the others drifted toward my worktable, fingers trailing across my finished charts — the honest ones, the ones of river fords and pass elevations that I sell to drovers and pilgrims. I kept my breathing level and thought about the false-bottomed drawer the way you think about a lit stove when you're trying not to look at it.

"He was carrying documents," the lead inquisitor said. "Charts. Anything recovered from the deceased is property of the Crown under writ of seizure." He tapped the warrant. "You'll want to cooperate, Mistress Marchetti. Cooperation is noted."

"Noted by whom?"

"By everyone it matters to."

There it was. Not justice — bookkeeping. The Crown's mercy has always been an entry in a ledger, and I'd just been informed which column I was in.

"I buried a stranger this morning," I said. "He bled on my floor and died at my counter, and I paid old Marta to dig the hole because no one else would. If the Crown wants his belongings, the Crown is welcome to dig him back up." I leaned on the counter. "Unless the writ covers corpses. Does it? I've never seen that clause."

Something flickered behind the ledger-eyes. Recalculation. I was supposed to be frightened, and I was — genuinely, deeply, my knees were having a private conversation about giving out — but I have learned that the best way to hide fear is to be slightly unpleasant, and I am gifted at both.

He studied me for a long moment. Then he produced a small card, black, with the Chancellery seal pressed in grey wax, and set it beside the warrant.

"Inquisitor Hale," he said. "I'll be in Greyhollow for some days. The mountains are dangerous this season, Mistress Marchetti. People go into the Thornfells and don't come back." A thin smile. "It would be a shame if that happened twice to one family."

He knew about my father. Or he knew to imply it, which is nearly as useful and much cheaper.

They left. The door closed. I stood there counting my heartbeats until they slowed, and then I pulled the map from the drawer and unfolded it with hands that were almost steady.

The roads had multiplied again overnight. And along the westernmost route — a track I have personally walked and know for certain does not exist beyond the second ford — fresh ink was drying, spelling out a single line in my father's small, apologetic hand:

*Don't trust the grey coats. Trust the one who hesitates.*

I read it four times. Then I looked up, and through the frost-furred window of my stall, I saw a man across the street watching my door. Royal guard's coat, worn wrong, like a man wearing a costume of his own former life. He wasn't moving. Wasn't approaching. Just watching — and when our eyes met through the glass, he looked away first.

Like a man who hesitates.

I folded the map, and for the first time since midnight, my hands were completely steady.
