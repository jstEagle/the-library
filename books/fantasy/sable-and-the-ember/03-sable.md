## 3. Sable

I had been following a dead man for eleven days, which is the sort of sentence that sounds like poetry and feels like bad boots.

The dead man's name — or one of them; men who die in Greyhollow tend to have collected several — was Corren Vale, and he had been bleeding from somewhere beneath his ribs since before I picked up his trail at the Ashford ferry. He bled neatly. That was the first thing I noticed about him. He bound the wound every six hours by the look of it, changed his dressings, burned the old ones, and never once left so much as a spotted cloth behind for me to read. A careful man. A frightened careful man, which is worse, because fear makes careful people inventive.

I am not an inventive man. I am a persistent one, which is how I came to be standing in the rain outside a border-town tavern called the Drowned Bell, watching a cartographer's stall through a window fogged with other people's breath.

Let me explain what I was before I go further, because the rest of this story will not make sense unless you understand what I had been trained to be.

For nine years I wore the grey-and-gold of the Crown Guard. For seven of those years I was good at it. For two of them I was very good at it, which is different, and worse. The Guard does not want good men; it wants obedient ones, and it promotes the obedient ones who are also clever enough to make obedience look like initiative. I climbed. I took orders that sat wrong in my stomach and swallowed them like bad water, and I told myself that was what service meant. Then my brother Tam died enforcing an order that should never have been given, and I discovered that my stomach had opinions after all, and that I had spent nine years not listening to them.

Tam's order had concerned a village called Merrow's Ford and a tax collection that turned into something else. The official record says "civil disturbance, resolved." The unofficial record says forty-one people, of whom three were armed, none of whom needed to die, all of whom did. Tam was among them. He died holding a door shut against farmers, which is not how anyone wants to be remembered, least of all by the door.

I broke my commanding officer's nose over the report. This is frowned upon in the Guard. I was not hanged, because I had nine years of commendations and because Captain Aldric Voss — my commander then, my commander still, gods help me — argued that grief was temporary and training permanent. So instead of a rope I got disgrace: stripped of rank, sent to do the work no one wanted, tracking smugglers' roads and runaway clerks and now, apparently, dying strangers with secrets stitched into their coats.

Voss himself had handed me this commission. That should have told me something. Voss did not leave the capital for weather like this unless the prize was worth the mud.

* * *

Corren Vale died sometime in the night, in a room above the tavern where a mapmaker worked. I know this because I watched her find him. I had taken a room across the street — Greyhollow has exactly three rooms to rent and I had bribed my way into the only one with a view of hers — and when she went up the stairs in the morning carrying bread and a kettle, I watched through my spyglass as the door opened onto whatever the night had left of him.

She did not scream. I noted that. She set down the bread, set down the kettle, and stood very still for a long moment in the way of someone doing arithmetic they don't like. Then she checked his pulse anyway, which I respected — hope is a discipline some people keep even when they know better — and then she began going through his coat with quick, ungentle hands.

That was when I saw it. Even through glass, even through rain, even at distance: when she lifted the folded chart from inside his coat, the wax seal on its wrapping caught the lamplight and threw back a color I had seen exactly once before, in a vault under the capital, in a case that officially contained nothing.

Red-gold. Not the red of Crown wax, which is flat, institutional, the red of ledgers. This was red with fire in it, the way coals are red, the way the old banners of Thornfell were red before the kingdom fell and everyone agreed, very politely, to stop painting that color on anything.

The Ember's seal.

Officially, the Ember was a story. A bedtime story for loyalists, a cautionary tale for inquisitors: the last sovereign flame of the fallen house, the fire that could crown a king without a coronation, that burned only for rightful blood. Officially, the Crown's interest in it was archival. Unofficially, I had spent four months learning that the Crown's archival interests involved sealed wagons, hooded inquisitors, and questions asked of people who were never seen asking anything again.

And now it was sitting in the hands of a woman who mended maps for smugglers in a town whose chief export was rain.

My orders were simple. Voss had written them himself, which meant they were not actually simple: *Locate the bearer. Confirm the artifact. Retrieve intact. Do not engage if inquisitor presence detected. Report by courier, coded.* Retrieve. Not recover, not secure. Retrieve, the way you retrieve a stolen horse. Nobody in that phrasing was thinking about the bearer at all. Bearers were furniture.

I sat in my rented room with the spyglass balanced on the sill and watched her examine the map, and I felt the familiar cold weight of duty settle onto my shoulders like a wet cloak, and underneath it, faint but unmistakable, I felt Tam.

Not literally. I'm not a man given to ghosts. But every soldier carries his dead around like stones in a pack, and mine had a habit of shifting weight at inconvenient moments. Tam had died because an order said a door must be held, and the order had not cared what holding the door cost, because orders are written by people who will never stand in front of them. And here was another order, written in Voss's neat hand, that did not care what retrieving a map might cost the woman holding it — or, if the inquisitors arrived on schedule, as they always did, what confirming it might cost me.

Because that was the arithmetic nobody put in reports. If I reported the seal confirmed, the inquisitors would come to Greyhollow within days. Inquisitors did not retrieve things. They removed witnesses. I had watched it happen in Merrow's Ford, from the far side of a square, too junior to object and too honest to forget.

I wrote the report three times that evening.

The first draft was true: *Artifact confirmed. Seal matches Ember registry. Bearer is a local mapmaker, female, approximately thirty, no guard retinue visible.* It sat on the desk looking like a death warrant, because that is what it was, and I knew whose name would be on it.

The second draft was cautious: *Possible match, requires closer inspection.* This bought time. Time bought nothing. Voss would simply send someone else, someone hungrier, someone whose stomach had no opinions.

The third draft I burned in the candle flame, along with the other two, and sat in the smoke feeling like the world's largest coward and its smallest hero simultaneously, which is a sensation I recommend to no one.

* * *

I told myself I was only going to talk to her. Assess her. Determine whether she understood what she was carrying. There is a particular kind of lie you tell yourself that is so obviously a lie that telling it becomes almost a form of honesty — you both know what's happening, you and yourself, and you proceed anyway because the alternative is standing still.

She was at her stall in the morning market, and she looked, frankly, terrible. The kind of tired that sits behind the eyes and doesn't come out. She was working anyway, inking a coastline with the steady hand of someone who trusts her hands more than her sleep.

"Your lodger," I said. "Corren Vale. I'm sorry for your trouble."

Her pen did not stop moving. "He wasn't my lodger. He was a stranger who collapsed in my doorway."

"And you took him in."

"The alternative was the street." Now the pen stopped. She looked up at me with the flat assessment of someone who has spent her life measuring distances and has recently learned to measure men. "You're not from Greyhollow."

"No."

"You've got a soldier's shoulders and a beggar's coat, and you've been watching my stall from that window for two days." She nodded toward the Drowned Bell. "Your curtain moves when you drink."

I had been outmaneuvered by better spies than a tired mapmaker, but rarely so quickly, and never while feeling quite so much like a fool. "Captain Sable," I said, and gave her the name I'd kept when the Crown took everything else off me — my mother's joke-name, the black sheep of a family of greys, the one thing they couldn't strip. "Formerly of the Crown Guard. Currently of nowhere in particular."

"That's a lot of words for 'I'm following you.'"

"I was following Vale. You were where he stopped." I leaned in slightly, lowered my voice, and made the decision that I would spend the next several chapters — the rest of my life, probably — trying to deserve. "There are people coming for what he gave you. Not soldiers. Worse. When they arrive, anyone who's touched that map will be a question to be answered, and their kind of answering doesn't leave survivors."

Something flickered in her face — not fear, exactly. Recognition. The look of someone whose dread has just been handed a schedule.

"You've seen the map," she said. Not a question.

"A glimpse. Enough."

"Then you know what it is."

"I know what it isn't," I said. "It isn't yours, and it isn't safe, and keeping it makes you the most dangerous woman between here and the capital."

She studied me for a long moment, rain dripping off the awning between us, and I watched her weigh me the way she'd weigh a route on a chart — slope, exposure, likelihood of collapse. Whatever she concluded, she didn't share it.

"If you're lying to me," she said, "you're better at it than you look."

"I'm worse at it than I look," I said. "That's why I'm telling you."

It was the first time I'd spoken those words aloud — *worse at lying than I look* — and I want it noted, by whoever keeps accounts of such things, that I believed them when I said them. I had already lied once, in the burning of those reports, in the silence where a courier pigeon should have been. But there are lies of omission and lies of commission, and I told myself, with great care and no conviction whatsoever, that what I was doing fell into the former category.

That evening I wrote to Voss after all. One line, coded, technically truthful, and the finest piece of dishonesty of my career:

*Trail lost at Greyhollow. Bearer deceased. Artifact not located. Continuing search north.*

Three lies braided into five sentences. The bearer was alive. The artifact was found — just not by me. And the search, when it continued, would continue at my side rather than ahead of me, because somewhere between the second draft and the third I had understood that I was not going to hand this woman to the inquisitors, and that understanding had arrived not as a decision but as a fact, the way you eventually admit the tide has come in.

Tam would have laughed at me. He'd always said I'd end up choosing something stupid and human over something sensible and survivable, and here I was, choosing it a decade late and calling it a search.

I sealed the letter. Outside, the rain kept falling on Greyhollow, patient as a siege, and across the street a light burned in the mapmaker's window where a woman sat up late with a map that was older than her grief and newer than her fear, redrawing itself in ink that remembered.

I watched that light until well past midnight, and told myself I was keeping watch.

Two lies in one day. I was getting better at it after all.

That worried me more than the inquisitors did.

And then, three streets away, a wagon rolled into town flying no colors at all, and every dog in Greyhollow began to howl.
