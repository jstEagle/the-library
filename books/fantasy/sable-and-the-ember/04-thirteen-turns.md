## 4. Thirteen Turns

*Lena*

The inquisitors came to Greyhollow on a Tuesday, which I have always felt was an insult to Tuesdays.

I knew before the bells did. The map told me — it had been twitching all morning like a dog that smells a storm, its roads crawling toward the western edge of the parchment as though trying to escape their own borders. I'd learned three things about my inheritance by then: it moved when unobserved, it refused to be burned (I had tried twice; the flames bent around it politely, like courtiers declining an invitation), and it knew things about me I hadn't said aloud. My name had appeared in the corner in my father's cramped hand two nights ago. *Property of L. Marchetti.* As if I were a parcel. As if he'd known.

The knock on my stall's shutter was polite. Inquisitors are always polite right up until they aren't.

"Cartographer Marchetti." The man wore grey, which in Greyhollow passed for modesty the way a wolf passes for a large dog. "Inquisitor Vess. We're seeking a traveler who passed through recently. Tall, wounded, fond of maps."

"Fond of maps," I repeated. "In this town? He'll be hard to distinguish from the furniture."

Vess smiled without using his eyes. Behind him, two more greys were already moving through my stall with gloved fingers, lifting my finished charts, checking the backs of them. Checking for what, I wondered, and then stopped wondering, because the answer was folded against my ribs under my coat, warm as a stolen loaf.

"He died," I said. "The traveler. Bled out at my counter two nights past. I drew the body myself — well, I drew the *map* of where the body was found. That's the closest I come to medicine."

"May we see your recent work?"

Here is something no one tells you about lying: it is a craft, and like any craft, it improves with practice and collapses under scrutiny. I handed Vess a chart of the mill road. Ordinary. Honest. Every line drawn by my own hand and meaning exactly what it said. He studied it the way men study a face across a card table.

"You're the daughter of Aldous Marchetti," he said.

Not a question. The map against my ribs went hot enough that I felt it through wool and linen both.

"Never heard of him," I said, and watched Vess decide to believe me, or rather, decided to pretend to believe me, which in Greyhollow amounted to the same courtesy.

They left. I gave them ten minutes, because anything less would be suspicious and anything more would be fatal, and then I blew out my lamp, took down my sign, and walked out the back door into the alley where a disgraced royal guard was waiting with two horses and the expression of a man who has recently discovered that conscience is expensive.

"They'll be back," Sable said. "With fewer questions."

"I noticed."

"The map wants to go east. Into the Thornfells."

"Of course it does," I said. "It's been pulling at me since dawn. It's very rude about it, too." I swung up onto the horse he'd brought — a dour grey mare with the eyes of an accountant. "One condition. You tell me who you actually work for, and don't say the Crown, because the Crown tried to arrest me twenty minutes ago."

Sable looked at me for a long moment. He had one of those faces built for garrisons — square, weathered, honest-looking in the way that made you check your purse after talking to him.

"I work for a dead man's promise," he said finally. "That's the whole of my employment history now. Ride."

* * *

*Sable*

She rode like she drew maps — fast, precise, and with visible contempt for anything that got in her way. I kept half a length behind and told myself it was tactical.

We went up through the pinewoods on the old charcoal-burners' track, and somewhere past the second ridge the map's influence became obvious even to me: Lena stopped consulting it entirely and simply followed it, her horse turning at forks before she'd glanced down, as though the parchment were whispering directions into her ear. Roads appeared where I remembered none. A bridge stood over a gorge I'd crossed by rope-ladder five years ago, in service, in another life, carrying orders I still didn't talk about.

"This one wasn't here," I said, dismounting at the bridge. "I've taken this pass. There was nothing but scree."

"There's a bridge now." She crouched, touched the stone. "Warm. Like something walked over it recently and left the heat behind." She looked up at me, and there was a sharpness in her face that I was beginning to suspect was simply her face, permanently. "You've been in these mountains before. On Crown business."

"Yes."

"Hunting the same man?"

"Yes."

"And?"

"And he was better at running than I was at catching." I tightened the mare's girth longer than it needed. It's easier to confess to a saddle. "Three years ago I had orders to retrieve a document from a courier in these hills. I found the courier. He wouldn't give it up. My commander's solution was—" I stopped.

"Was?"

"My brother enforced the order. Not me. I was the one who carried the courier's body down afterward." I heard how it sounded and didn't correct it. Some truths you hand over crooked and let people straighten them themselves. "After that I requested border duty. Quiet postings. Then last month a stranger came to me bleeding, with a seal I recognized off a report I should never have read, and asked me to find a cartographer named Marchetti if he didn't make it to her first."

Lena was quiet for a while. Wind moved the pines below us, a long shushing sound, like the mountain telling everyone to keep their voices down.

"You lied to whoever sent you," she said. "About finding me."

"I reported that the trail went cold at Greyhollow."

"Why?"

Because your name was on the map in a dead man's handwriting and it felt like an instruction. Because every order I've ever followed perfectly has ended with someone carrying a body downhill. Because you look at that thing under your coat the way my brother looked at his commission, and I'd like to be present when one of you finally puts it down.

"Seemed tidy," I said.

She snorted. But she mounted up again, and we rode on together, two liars sharing a road, each of us pretending not to notice the other counting our bluffs.

* * *

*Lena*

We made camp in the ruin of a shepherd's hut that the map insisted existed, and did. Of course it did. I was starting to develop a professional jealousy of a piece of paper.

By firelight I finally sat down with the margins properly. My father had been a cipher-drunk old goat — I'd spent my childhood decoding his grocery lists — and once I looked, the pattern leapt out at me like it had been waiting, offended, for so long. Every third word along the border roads. Numbers dressed as place-names. *Thornwick. Ninefell. Turnstone.* Take the turns. Count the moons.

"Sable," I said. "How many moons does autumn have here?"

He looked up from sharpening a knife he clearly already considered sharp. "Thirteen. Why?"

"Because my father wrote a countdown." I spread the map flat on a stone between us, and I swear the ink brightened, eager, like a student who knows the answer. "Look. Each road ends in a number of turns. Thirteen roads, thirteen numbers, descending. And at the center—" I traced it with a fingertip, not quite touching. "Hollow Peak. There's a deadline written into the geography itself. Whatever the Ember is, wherever it's going, it has to arrive before the thirteenth moon. Or—"

"Or what?"

I read the cipher again, the last line, the one I'd been hoping said something else. "*Or it will be found.*" I looked up. "Not lost. Found. By both armies, simultaneously, apparently. My father built a hiding place with an expiry date."

Sable was silent. Firelight did him favors, filling in the lines around his mouth, making him look less like a man deciding whether to lie to me and more like a man deciding whether to stop.

"That's why the factions haven't simply torn the mountains apart looking for it," he said slowly. "They can't rush. They have to wait for the map to surface — wait for someone like you to carry it. The thirteenth moon isn't just a deadline. It's a trapdoor. Everyone converges at once."

"Wonderful. So I'm not a courier. I'm the fuse."

"You could put it down," he said quietly. "Walk away. Let it be found. Let them fight over it and leave you out of it."

I thought about saying yes. It would have been the sensible lie, and I was getting good at those. Instead I heard myself say: "He drew me into this. *Property of L. Marchetti.* If my father trusted me with his life's worst secret, the least I can do is find out why." I rolled the map up before it could see my face do anything sentimental. "Besides. Someone has to draw the road to Hollow Peak. No one else is qualified."

Sable almost smiled. It was a small, damaged thing, that smile, like a coin bitten to test if it was real.

"Get some sleep," he said. "First watch is mine."

I slept, which surprised me. Trust, it turns out, is mostly exhaustion with good posture.

* * *

*Sable*

I woke her an hour before dawn because the horses wouldn't settle, and because I'd spent the night learning something I didn't like: the mountains had grown quieter. Not peaceful-quiet. Held-breath quiet. Somewhere below us, in the dark, other people were climbing the same impossible roads, following the same impossible map's scent, and the map only had one original.

"We're being followed," I said. "Two parties, maybe. One behind, one flanking east."

Lena was awake instantly, hair escaping its braid, eyes already moving to the map tube. "Which means the loyalists found Greyhollow too."

"Or the Crown doubled back. Or both." I saddled the mare. "There's a junction ahead where the routes split. The map will want the high road. The high road is exposed for a full morning's ride."

"Then we take the low road and argue with the paper."

"It doesn't argue. It redraws."

"Then it can learn some manners." She swung up behind me on the mare — we'd lost the second horse to a scree slope and neither of us had mourned — and pressed the map tube between us like a shared secret, which, I supposed, it now was.

We rode down into the grey light, and at the junction, as predicted, the low road was there. New. Warm underfoot. It curved away beneath an overhang of rock, hidden, sensible, safe.

And pinned to a stone at its entrance, driven through with a knife whose hilt I recognized from a garrison I'd sworn an oath to eleven years ago, was a page of parchment. Fresh ink. My father's drafting mark, Lena's breath said beside me, gone strange.

But I was reading the words beneath the sigil, and they weren't a father's.

*To the cartographer's daughter,* it said. *Your mother's son is alive. Bring the map, and learn what happened to your father — from the woman who watched him die.*

Beside me, Lena had gone very still, in the way of people standing at the edge of something with no floor.

"That's not possible," she whispered. "My mother had no sons."

"No," I agreed, and reached slowly for the knife. "But someone wants you to believe it badly enough to walk into their hands. Question is — do we take the bait, or do we spend the rest of the ride wondering?"

The map, against my back, began to move.
