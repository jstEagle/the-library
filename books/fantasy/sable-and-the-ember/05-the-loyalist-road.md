## 5. The Loyalist Road

The map wanted us to walk into the ambush.

I want to be clear about that, because later — when Sable asked me how I could have missed it — I told him the truth: I didn't miss it. The road narrowed at a place called the Shepherd's Throat, where two cliffs leaned together like conspirators, and the ink had drawn the path straight through the pinch with a confidence that felt almost personal. My father's hand. My father's roads. And my father, wherever he was now, had never once in his life taken the direct route when a paranoid one existed.

So either the map trusted the ground, or it trusted whoever was waiting there.

We made camp above the Throat instead, on a shelf of granite with a view of the pass, and I spent the evening pretending to study the chart while actually studying Sable. He'd taken first watch without being asked, which was either courtesy or a way to avoid sleeping near me, and he sat with his back against a boulder and his sword across his knees like a man who expected his own reflection to attack him.

"You're staring," he said, without turning.

"I'm mapping you. It's what I do."

"Find anything?"

"You sleep with your boots on."

"That's not a finding, that's an observation."

"It's a finding about your character." I rolled the map closed before it could do anything clever. "Boots-on sleepers are men who've been woken badly before."

He considered that longer than the joke deserved. "Once," he said finally. "In Vellmark. We were—" He stopped. He always stopped at *we*, that pronoun with a graveyard behind it. "Go to sleep, Marchetti."

I didn't, particularly. But I closed my eyes, because the map did its best work when I wasn't watching, and because somewhere between the twelfth hour and the thirteenth I'd learned to listen for the sound of ink moving — a dry whisper, like a moth against paper — and I wanted to know if it would speak tonight.

It spoke.

I heard it just past midnight: that soft scratching, and beneath it something new, a second voice in the whisper, fainter, as though the map were talking to itself. Or being talked to. I opened my eyes and the fire had burned to coals and Sable was on his feet with his sword drawn, staring down into the pass, and below us, filling the Shepherd's Throat like water fills a cup, were torches.

Dozens. Moving without a single spoken word, which is more frightening than shouting. Armies announce themselves; this was something quieter and older, a column of people who had learned silence the hard way, in exile, in winter, in the years after a kingdom fell and its loyalists learned that grief makes poor armor but excellent discipline.

"Sable," I breathed.

"I see them." His knuckles were white on the hilt. "They're not Crown. No livery. No horns."

"They're not bandits either. Bandits would've hit us while we slept."

He looked at me then, and in the coal-light his face did something complicated. "Then they know exactly who we are," he said, "and they want us awake for it."

* * *

They came up the cliff face at dawn, which told me everything. Inquisitors would have surrounded us in the dark and made it a siege. Bandits would have come screaming. These climbed the rock in the grey half-light with ropes and patience, and by the time the sun cleared the eastern ridge there were thirty of them on our shelf, arranged in a loose crescent, and every one of them wore grey travel-stained clothes with no sigil at all — except that once you'd seen it, you couldn't unsee it: the cut of their coats, the way the older ones stood, heels together, hands clasped, like courtiers pretending very hard to be shepherds.

And at the crescent's center, a woman on a mule.

She was perhaps sixty, silver-haired, with a face like a weathered map herself — all contour lines and old creases — and eyes the pale grey of glacier ice. She dismounted without help, which surprised her escort, so it surprised me too, and she walked to our cold fire pit and stood over it as though reading ashes the way I read ink.

"Lena Marchetti," she said.

My name in her mouth sounded wrong. Not pronounced wrong — pronounced *owned*, the way you say a thing you've been keeping in a box.

"Do I know you?" I said.

"No. You knew my son." She turned. Up close she was taller than I'd guessed, and thinner, and there was a smell about her of woodsmoke and some bitter herb I'd later learn was feverfew, chewed against grief. "Tomas. Though I understand he gave you another name. He was fond of false names. He got it from his father."

The shelf went very quiet. Even the wind seemed to lean in.

"The stranger," Sable said carefully. "At Greyhollow."

"My son." She said it again, and this time I heard what lived under it: not the word but the weight, the way you set down something heavy you've carried a long way. "He wrote to me from the lowlands. Four letters in six years, each one shorter than the last. The final one said only: *If I don't come home, find the cartographer's daughter.*" Her ice-grey eyes settled on me. "You see my difficulty, Lena Marchetti. I have been looking for you since spring."

"He died in my arms," I said. Because it was true, and because I wanted to watch what the truth did to her face.

What it did was nothing, at first. Then the creases around her eyes deepened, all of them at once, like a page folding along every line ever drawn on it. "Did he say anything?"

"He said *thirteen turns*. He gave me a map."

"And you still carry it."

It wasn't a question, and I didn't answer it, and my hand — traitor, coward — drifted toward the satchel anyway, and she watched it move with the satisfaction of a woman confirming a suspicion she'd been feeding for months.

"I am Maren Vale," she said. "You will not have heard the name. That was rather the point of the name. But twenty years ago I was Mistress of the Royal Wardrobe, and before that I was a girl in Emberhold who watched the sovereign flame burn in the High Vault every morning of her childhood, and I am the last living soul who held rank in a kingdom that no longer appears on maps." A thin smile. "Present company excepted."

Beside me, Sable had gone rigid in a way I'd learned to read. Not fear. Recognition. He knew her, or knew *of* her, and the knowing hurt him.

"Your Majesty's household," he said quietly.

"Captain." She inclined her head a precise half-inch. "Or former captain. They tell me you fell from grace. They tell me a great many things about you, most of them written by people who never met you." Then back to me, and her voice changed — dropped its ceremony like a coat. "Girl. Come walk with me. Your guard may follow at ten paces, if it keeps him calm."

* * *

We walked the ridge above the Throat, she and I, with the loyalists melting away behind us and Sable trailing at exactly ten paces, counting, I'd have bet my drafting pens on it. Maren Vale walked like a woman who had spent decades making small distances feel long.

"Tomas was my youngest," she said. "My only one left. I sent him to find the Ember because everyone else I sent came back dead or bought, and he was clever, and he was mine, and I thought love would be better armor than training." She looked out at the mountains. "It wasn't. Love is terrible armor. It fits badly and shows everything."

"Why are you telling me this?"

"Because you're going to ask me about your father, and I'd rather we begin even." She stopped and faced me fully. "Edric Marchetti. Draftsman to the Royal Survey. The finest mapmaker the kingdom produced in a century, and the only man alive who understood the Hollow Peak's approaches well enough to hide a flame inside a mountain. You want to know where he is."

The world tilted slightly. I kept my feet through sheer professional stubbornness. "You know him."

"I fed him soup in the winter of the fall, when he came to us half-starved with a child's drawing folded inside his coat and swore us all to secrecy with a look that could have sealed a tomb." Her voice softened by a degree, no more. "He stayed with us eleven days. On the twelfth he went out alone toward Thornfell and never came back, and Tomas — who worshipped him, who'd followed him around the survey offices as a boy begging to hold the compasses — Tomas took it as a sacred charge. *Find what Edric hid. Finish what Edric started.* Those were practically the boy's prayers."

"What child's drawing?" I said, because my mouth chose it before my sense could intervene.

Maren looked at me for a long moment, and something in her face — pity, or its colder cousin, recognition — made me wish I hadn't.

"A map," she said. "Of a house. Drawn in a child's hand, with a child's certainty. A little house with a garden, and a road leading away from it, and the road simply... stops. Ends in blankness. And beneath it, in an adult's careful script: *some places are not for kings.*" She let that sit between us in the cold air. "He talked about you, Lena. Eleven days of soup, and most of it was you. How you'd found his old drafting mark and copied it into your own work without being taught. How you argued with his projections at seven years old. How he'd made one perfect thing in his life and it wasn't a map."

The wind came up the pass and I let it hit my face because my eyes were doing something I didn't permit in front of strangers. Somewhere behind us, ten paces back, Sable had stopped walking, and I could feel him not-approaching, giving me the lie of privacy the way he gave me everything else — grudgingly, completely.

"He's dead," I said. "Isn't he. That's why the map—"

"Your father is beyond my knowing. But the Ember is not, and the Ember is the kingdom, girl. Not a symbol of it. *Is* it. As long as that flame burns uncrowned, everything we lost stays lost — the laws, the names, the graves without stones. Put it back on a living head and the dead get their country back." She stepped closer, and up close her eyes weren't ice at all; they were tired, human, desperate. "Give me the map. I'll give you everything I know of him — every day of those eleven days, every word. You'll have your father back the only way anyone can have the dead back: whole, and told truly. And when the flame is crowned, when the kingdom stands again, there will be a place in it for the daughter of Edric Marchetti. A chair. A title. A *name* on a map, at last, instead of a name on a warrant."

"And if I say no?"

"Then I take it," she said, without heat, without apology, "and I lose the only honest thing I've done in years, which is asking."

* * *

She gave me until sundown. Her people made camp across the shelf with a courtesy that was worse than menace, sharing dried fish and bitter tea, and Sable materialized at my shoulder the moment the crescent opened, steering me to the far rocks with a hand at my elbow light as a draft line.

"She's telling the truth," he said quietly. "About the household. About your father. I remember the survey man — everyone did. And I remember what happened to the last courier who brought the Ember's route to the loyalist camps." His jaw worked. "Hale's inquisitors burned three villages looking for that courier. Villages, Lena. Full of people who'd never seen a map in their lives."

"So I give it to her and she leads a coronation, and the Crown burns a fourth village, and a fifth—"

"So you give it to her and you're free." He said it flatly, and I turned to look at him, because it was the wrong thing for him to say and he knew it was the wrong thing and said it anyway, watching me with those careful eyes. "That's what you want me to say, isn't it? Hand it over. Walk away. Let it be someone else's war."

"What I want," I said, "is for you to stop deciding what I should want."

"Then decide fast." He glanced at the loyalist fires. "Because I've been thinking about the Throat. About why the map led us straight through an ambush."

"It didn't lead us through. We camped above it."

"It led us *to* it." He turned to me fully, and the last light caught the scar along his jaw. "Think about it. Your father drew roads that hid things. What if this road wasn't hiding the Ember from the loyalists? What if it was delivering you to them? On purpose. Because there's something on this map that only opens for you — and he wanted you to meet these people, hear their offer, and refuse it with your eyes open. Refusal you choose is different from refusal you're forced into. Maybe he needed you to *know* what you were refusing."

I stared at him. "You've been reading my father's margins again."

"I've been reading *you*," he said, and then went slightly grey, as if the sentence had escaped without a permit, and turned away to check a blade that didn't need checking.

Sundown came. Maren Vale sent for me, and I went, and the map was heavy in my satchel like a sleeping animal, and the whole grey column stood waiting in the violet light with their torches unlit, patient as stone saints.

"Well, Lena Marchetti," she said. "Daughter of Edric. What will it be?"

I put my hand on the satchel flap. And beneath my palm, through the leather, I felt it — the faintest tremor, the dry whisper of moving ink, and underneath the whisper, unmistakable now, that second voice, that other hand, writing something new.

Writing, I realized with a chill that had nothing to do with the mountain air, in my father's hand:

*a road that leads nowhere — but only she can draw it.*

Maren saw my face change. "What is it? What does it say?"

I lifted the map out into the dying light. And where the road to the Shepherd's Throat had been, where the ambush and the offer and the crown had all been waiting — the ink was redrawing itself even as we watched, lines crawling like roots, erasing the pass, erasing the loyalist camp, erasing everything, and laying down instead a single new road that climbed past the edge of the parchment and off into the blank margin, going nowhere, going *beyond*—

"Oh," Maren Vale whispered, and her voice cracked clean down the middle. "*Oh, you treacherous, brilliant man.*"

Behind her, thirty exiles went for their weapons.
