## 6. What My Father Drew

There are three things a disgraced royal guard learns quickly: how to sleep sitting up, how to eat anything that doesn't fight back, and how to lie with a straight face. I had mastered all three by my twenty-fifth year. What nobody teaches you is what to do when the lying starts to cost more than the truth.

I was learning that now, at a fire no bigger than a cupped hand, while Lena Marchetti slept with her back against a stone that had probably been ancient before anyone thought to build kingdoms on top of it.

The map lay open across my knees.

She'd let me hold it. That was the thing that kept snagging in my chest like a fishhook — she'd looked at me this morning, this man who'd lied to her about his name for two days before she caught him at it, and she'd said, *You watch it tonight. You've got better eyes than me and I need to sleep.* Trust, or exhaustion, or some mixture of both that she hadn't examined too closely. With Lena, I suspected most of her decisions were made somewhere below the waterline, where she couldn't see them clearly enough to argue.

So I watched it. And the map watched me back.

* * *

You have to understand what this document was. It wasn't paper with ink on it. It was an argument that never ended. The roads shifted when you weren't looking — I'd verified that myself, twice, by marking a bend with charcoal and finding it moved a finger's width east by morning, patient as a tide. The mountains redrew their own shoulders. Rivers wandered like drunkards. Somewhere under all of it, faint as breath on glass, there were other lines: older roads, roads that had been erased from every honest atlas in Thornfell, surfacing and sinking again like drowned men.

Lena's father had drawn this. Aldric Marchetti, cartographer to a court that no longer existed, dead in a ditch outside Greyhollow with his life leaking out of him and his last work pressed into his daughter's hands. I'd known the name before I met her. Every guard who'd served the old regime knew it. He'd been the one who made the Crown's maps worth having, and then he'd vanished the same winter the Ember did, and everyone with a rank and a conscience had assumed the same two things: that he'd stolen it, and that he was dead.

Both correct, as it turned out. Just not in the order anyone guessed.

I turned the map toward the firelight and did what I'd been doing for three nights instead of sleeping: I read the margins.

Aldric Marchetti had written in cipher, but not a clever one. That was the first thing that had struck me, back in Greyhollow, when I'd glimpsed the sigil matching his drafting mark — a mountain cradling a flame, small as a thumbnail in the corner. A man who could hide a sovereign flame from two armies for thirteen years does not use a child's substitution cipher unless he wants it broken. Unless he wants it broken *by someone specific.*

By blood kin. That was the phrase I'd worked out on the second night, and it had sat in my stomach like a swallowed stone ever since.

* * *

Here is what I found, in the hours between the twelfth moon rising and the fire burning down to embers.

The margins weren't notes. They were instructions — but they only resolved if you read them against the map itself, line for line, cipher symbol to road. And the map, I realized around midnight, wasn't finished. It couldn't finish itself. There was a gap in it, a blank stretch beneath the Hollow Peak where the ink simply refused to go, and every night the self-redrawing roads crept closer to that blankness and stopped, like water reaching the edge of a cliff.

The final route — the true route, the one that ended at whatever vault Aldric had built into the mountain — would only appear if the last lines were *drawn*. Not revealed. Drawn. By hand.

And the cipher said whose hand.

*Vere sanguinis vincit* — the blood-line binds. Old court Latin, the kind they taught to pages and guards' sons so we could read proclamations without laughing. Beneath it, in plainer script, almost apologetic: *the lock must be turned by the maker's own hand, or the hand it made.*

I sat with that for a long time. Long enough for the fire to die to coals. Long enough for an owl somewhere above us to give up on its night and go silent.

Aldric Marchetti hadn't just hidden the Ember. He'd built a lock out of his daughter. The map would lead anyone anywhere — inquisitors, loyalists, kings, fools — right up to the edge of the blank space, and then it would show them nothing, because the final road didn't exist until Lena drew it. Her father had died knowing exactly what he was asking of her. He'd folded the chart into a stranger's hands with his last breath and sent it home to her like a letter, and the letter said: *you are the key, and I'm sorry, and I'm not sorry enough to undo it.*

Or maybe he was sorry enough. Maybe that was why the cipher was easy. Maybe he'd wanted her to understand, eventually, on her own terms, in her own time — wanted her to be angry at him properly, with all the evidence, instead of half-blind.

It didn't matter. Either way, he'd made his daughter the door, and every armed man in Thornfell was walking toward her.

* * *

"You're staring at it like it owes you money."

I nearly put my knife through my own thigh. Lena was awake, watching me across the dead fire, her hair flattened on one side and her eyes already fully sharp, which seemed deeply unfair. Cartographers, I decided, slept like soldiers. One ear always on.

"It might," I said. "Maps usually do."

"That's not what maps owe people." She came and sat beside me, close enough that our shoulders touched, which she did casually and I did not experience casually. "Maps owe you the truth about distance. Everything else is decoration."

"Spoken like a woman who's never used one to flee an inquisitor."

"I've fled three inquisitors with one. Ask it who's carrying whom."

I almost smiled. That was the trouble. That was the whole entire trouble, laid out plain: she made me almost smile, at four in the morning, on cold stone, with the Crown behind us and a loyalist army ahead of us and a dead man's guilt warm in my lap.

"Lena." I kept my voice level. "When we get to the Hollow Peak. What's your plan? For the map, I mean."

She was quiet long enough that I thought she wouldn't answer. "My plan," she said finally, "is to get there before either army does, and then decide. My father left it to *me*. Not to the Crown, not to the exiles. Me. Whatever he wanted done with it, he trusted me to work it out." She glanced sideways at me. "Why? You have a suggestion?"

"No," I said, and it was the easiest lie I'd ever told, and it went down like glass. "No suggestions. Your father's work. Your call."

Because what was I going to say? *Actually, the map can't be finished by anyone but you, which means your father chained the fate of a kingdom to your wrist, and also the moment you draw that final road, every blade in Thornfell will know exactly who holds the Ember, and I've known this for three days and said nothing because watching you figure it out yourself felt more honest than telling you, which is a lie I'm telling myself, too.*

Some lies you tell to protect people. Some you tell because the truth would make them look at you differently. And some — the worst kind, the kind I was steeping in — you tell because the truth would make them look at you *accurately*, and you're not ready to be seen that clearly.

* * *

We walked at dawn. The map led us up a trail that hadn't existed the week before — a switchbacking goat-path stitched along a ridge where the charts showed sheer cliff — and Lena navigated it with the map held loose in one hand, barely glancing at it, reading the mountain itself and letting the paper confirm what she already knew. Watching her was like watching someone talk to a parent. Familiar, fluent, faintly exasperated.

"The cipher," she said, halfway up the ridge. "You've been reading it at night. Don't look surprised, you hold the map like it's a confession. What have you got?"

"A lot of numbers," I said. "Road measurements. Distances between shrines. Your father was thorough."

"Mm." She stepped over a fallen pine, sure-footed, not looking back. "Thorough. That's one word for a man who encoded his grocery lists."

"He encoded everything?"

"Everything. I found one of his letters once, when I was twelve. Took me a month to crack it. You know what it said?" She laughed, short and dry. "*Buy more lamp oil. The girl reads after dark.*" She shook her head. "He hid love letters in supply requisitions. Hiding things was just how he breathed."

*The girl reads after dark.* Something in my chest turned over slowly, like a body in water.

"So if he wrote something important," she went on, "something that mattered — he wouldn't leave it plain. But he'd leave it *findable*. He always did. He wanted to be caught, eventually. By the right person." She stopped on the trail and looked back at me, wind pulling at her coat, the valley falling away grey and endless behind her. "Sable. If you find something in those margins — something big — you'll tell me. Right?"

The ridge hummed with wind. Below us, far off, a thread of smoke rose from the loyalist camp in the valley, thin and patient as a fuse.

"Yes," I said.

Third lie. They were getting heavier, the way stones do when you carry them uphill. I told it anyway, and smiled while I did it, because that was the fourth thing nobody teaches a disgraced guard: how to lie to someone you —

How to lie to someone you respect. That was as far as I let the sentence go. Love felt like another betrayal waiting to happen — hers to her father's memory, mine to every order I'd ever sworn, and worst of all mine to her, because everything I loved, I'd eventually failed. My brother. My commission. My own name. I wasn't going to add Lena Marchetti to the list by telling her the truth and watching her realize her father had made her a lock, and that the man she'd started trusting had known all along and kept his mouth shut to protect her from a truth that would only hurt her.

That was the story I was telling myself, anyway. Lies nest, like boxes. I was very good at boxes.

That night, camped in the lee of the ridge with the twelfth moon fat and yellow over the peaks, I took out the map one more time and held my charcoal pencil over the blank space below the Hollow Peak. Testing a theory. If the route truly needed her hand, mine should do nothing.

I drew a single line. The ink shivered, considered it, and erased it — smoothly, politely, the way a door closes in the face of someone without the password.

But the map *noticed*. In the margin, beneath the sigil of the mountain and the flame, new words surfaced in Aldric Marchetti's careful hand, addressed, I was suddenly certain, not to me at all:

*If you are reading this, you are not her. Put it down. She will come. She always comes home.*

And below that, smaller, fresher, in ink still wet:

*Two days behind you. Both banners. Run.*

I looked up at the darkening trail behind us and saw, very far below, torchlight beginning to move.
