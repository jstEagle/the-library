## 7. The Inquisitor's Mercy

*Lena*

The shrine appeared the way all good ambushes do: as salvation.

We'd been three days on the high road with nothing but goat trails and Sable's increasingly creative swearing for company, and when I saw the stone archway rising out of the fog — a little roofless chapel wedged into the mountainside like a tooth in a jaw — I actually felt relief. That's the thing about being hunted. You start mistaking traps for shelter because your legs have opinions and your brain has stopped voting.

"Old pilgrim rest," Sable said, scanning it the way he scanned everything, like the landscape owed him money. "Travelers used to leave offerings. Nobody's climbed this far since the war."

"Then we'll have it to ourselves."

I want that noted for the record: I said it first. Whatever happens next, history should know the optimism was mine.

Inside, the shrine was a single room open to the sky, its altar worn smooth by centuries of weather and knees. Someone had left a clay lamp on the ledge, long empty. The map — my father's map, my inheritance, my curse — had gone quiet in my satchel since the eclipse two nights past, its ink lying flat and still like a dog pretending to sleep. I'd almost convinced myself that was good news.

Sable built a fire small enough to hide from God, which struck me as ambitious given our recent luck. We ate hardtack and dried plums and didn't talk about the loyalists behind us or the inquisitors somewhere ahead, which left us the weather.

"You know what I miss?" I said, mostly to hear a voice that wasn't the wind. "Doors. Doors that close. Walls with four of them."

"I miss my brother's cooking," Sable said, and then looked surprised at himself, the way a man does when he trips over a truth in the dark.

"You've never mentioned a brother."

"There's a lot I haven't mentioned." He fed a twig to the fire. "He was better at it than me. Cooking. Most things."

I was composing a suitably wry reply — something about how that hardly seemed possible — when the light changed.

Not the firelight. The *other* light, the gray one, the one that comes off snow. It went white at the edges, and shadows moved where no shadows should be, and Sable was already on his feet with his hand on his sword, saying a word I won't repeat but will admit I agreed with.

They came through the archway in a line of six, gray cloaks, silver chains, faces like unpaid debts. And at their center walked a man who did not match them at all: broad, unhurried, with a traveler's beard going gray at the chin and the mild expression of someone who has come to collect on a bet you'd forgotten placing.

"Lena Marchetti," he said warmly. "And Ser Aldous Sable, formerly of the Crown Guard. What a shrine this is. All this history, and nobody visits."

* * *

*Sable*

I knew Hale before he introduced himself. Everyone who'd ever worn the gray knew of Inquisitor Hale, though most descriptions got him wrong. They painted him cruel. He wasn't cruel. Cruelty requires passion, and Hale had the temperament of a man doing arithmetic.

Six against two, and one of the six had a crossbow leveled at Lena before she finished standing. I put myself between her and it, an old habit arriving late to save nobody.

"Ser Sable." Hale studied me with something adjacent to fondness. "Your commander filed you missing eleven days ago. Desertion, technically. Though between us, the paperwork is flexible. Paperwork usually is."

"I'm not deserting. I'm completing the assignment."

"The assignment being?"

"Retrieval of the artifact." My mouth made the words while the rest of me watched the crossbowman's finger. "The stranger who carried it died in Greyhollow. The woman found it. I've been tracking her."

Lena turned and looked at me. It was a very thorough look. It took inventory of every lie I'd told since the tavern, and I felt each one get counted.

"Is that so," Hale said. He smiled, and it was worse than his not-smiling. "Then you'll be pleased to surrender it. Both of you can walk down the mountain. There's a cell with your name on it, Ser Aldous, but cells are temporary. Careers are long."

"And the Marchetti woman?"

Hale spread his hands, magnanimous as a festival. "The Crown has no quarrel with cartographers. She drew a map. Maps are paper. We are reasonable men."

Every man who says *we are reasonable men* is announcing the opposite, the way a merchant who swears a price is final is inviting you to haggle. But I said, "It's in her satchel," because the crossbow was steady and Lena was breathing wrong and I have one talent in this world, which is knowing exactly how much a body can survive.

Hale nodded to one of his people. The satchel came off Lena's shoulder gently, almost courteously, and the map was unfolded on the altar stone, pinned at four corners with daggers, and the ink lay there dead and ordinary, just roads and mountains and a sigil like a closed eye.

Hale looked at it for a long time.

"Do you know," he said finally, conversationally, "how long the Crown has known about the Ember?"

Neither of us answered. He hadn't asked a question; he'd started a sermon.

"Thirty-one years," he said. "Since the fall of Vaelmoor. We burned the palace records ourselves — officially. Unofficially, we read them first. We have known where the last sovereign flame was hidden for longer than either of you has been alive, and do you know what we did with that knowledge?" He tapped the map. "Nothing. We let the old king's loyalists chase rumors. We let smugglers sell false routes. Every few years some fool gets close, and we let the factions tear at each other over him, and while they're busy, the borders stay quiet. The peace holds. Do you understand? The Ember isn't a threat to the Crown. It's a *tool*. A bone thrown between two dogs so they never notice who holds the leash."

Lena's voice came out flat. "My father. Was he part of this?"

"Edric Marchetti." Hale said the name like a toast. "Our finest asset. He drew the false maps, planted the rumors, kept both sides running in circles for twenty years. Until, near the end, he stopped reporting." Something flickered across Hale's face — not grief, but the memory of where grief would go if he could afford it. "He decided the game was crueler than the prize. He tried to end it. He hid the flame where neither faction could reach, and he built a lock only his blood could open, and then he died in a ditch in Greyhollow keeping the secret out of his own mouth." Hale straightened. "So you see, Miss Marchetti, you aren't a fugitive. You're an *estate*. Your father left you to the Crown, and I've simply come to collect."

* * *

*Lena*

There's a particular silence that lives inside you after a sentence like that. Everything keeps moving — wind through the roofless shrine, fire popping, Sable's breath beside me — but inside, it's very quiet, the way a house is quiet after the person in it stops existing.

My father worked for them. All those years I spent angry at him for vanishing, for choosing his roads over his daughter, and he'd been theirs. And then he'd stopped. And then he'd died in front of me without saying a word, pressing a moving map into my hands and spending his last breath on two words: *thirteen turns.*

He hadn't abandoned me. He'd *armed* me. There was no version of that which hurt less.

"And if I give it to you," I said. "If I hand over the map and tell you everything he taught me. Then what?"

"Then you go home," Hale said. "Clemency. Full pardon. The Crown forgives the daughter for the father's apostasy. You keep your shop, your name, your life." He paused, delicately, a surgeon indicating the incision. "There is one small matter. Ser Aldous here. His orders were retrieval. Instead he lied to his commander, fled with the asset, and has been seen armed alongside a person of interest. The tribunal will need testimony. Say that he stole the map from you. Say he coerced your silence. One afternoon of your honesty, Miss Marchetti, and he hangs alone, and you walk free, and the Ember goes into a vault where it can threaten no one ever again. Isn't that cleaner? Isn't that *merciful*?"

I looked at Sable.

Here's what I saw: a man who had lied to his commander for me, lied to the loyalists for me, lied to me *for* me, standing in the rain-shadow of a ruined chapel with a crossbow aimed at his chest and not one word of protest in his mouth. He wasn't going to argue. He'd already done the arithmetic himself, and in his sums, I was worth more than he was. It was the most infuriating thing I have ever witnessed, and I once watched a man pay full price for a map of a lake that had dried up.

I thought about my father again. Twenty years of drawing lies for the Crown, and the one true thing he ever made was a map that led nowhere — made for me, locked to me, useless to every throne and army and reasonable man on this mountain. He'd spent his whole career serving power, and his last act refusing it. He'd chosen the road with no destination because every other road belonged to somebody else.

I am my father's daughter. It just took me until now to decide which parts.

"No," I said.

Hale tilted his head. "No to clemency?"

"No to the theater." I stepped forward, past Sable, close enough to see Hale's eyes, which were patient and bottomless as wells. "You don't want the Ember destroyed. You said it yourself — it's your bone, your leash, your peace. If I testify, you hang a guard who broke ranks, and you file the map in a vault, and in ten years some other fool finds some other rumor, and you throw the bone again. Nothing ends. Nothing was ever going to end. You didn't come up this mountain for justice, Inquisitor. You came for the *story*. The story where the Crown wins cleanly and everyone goes home believing it." I pulled the map's corner free of its dagger pin. "I'm not giving you the story. And I'm not giving you him."

The crossbowman shifted. Hale raised two fingers — *wait* — and studied me the way I study blank parchment, deciding what belongs there.

"You understand," he said softly, "that refusal has a price."

"So does everything. At least mine comes with the receipt."

For a moment — I swear this happened — Hale almost smiled. Not cruelty. Recognition. The look of a man meeting the child of an old opponent and finding the argument hasn't aged a day.

"Take them," he said. "Gently. They'll want their strength for the tribunal."

And that's when the ink woke up.

I felt it before anyone saw it — a warmth against my hip like a hand closing around mine — and on the altar, under six pairs of eyes, my father's map began to move. Roads crawled. Mountains folded. The closed-eye sigil opened, and every route on the page peeled itself away and redrew itself into a single line: one road, climbing, ending at a peak shaped like a hollow ring.

Hale stared at it, and for the first time in thirty-one years, the Crown's arithmetic failed him.

"That's not possible," he said. "That route doesn't exist. It *can't*—"

"It exists," I said, backing toward the archway with Sable's arm suddenly around mine, "for exactly two people. And you're not one of them."

The crossbow fired. Sable turned, took the bolt meant for my heart on his shoulder instead, and we ran — out of the shrine, into the fog, up a road that had no business existing, drawn by a dead man for a living daughter, with the thirteenth moon rising behind us like a door beginning to open.
