## 8. Ash and Ascent

The loyalists took the map at dawn, and they took it the way people take things they believe God has already signed for — politely, with apologies, and a knife held low where the apologies can't see it.

Maren — the woman who had once offered me my father's whole history in exchange for a piece of paper — didn't even look at me while her people did it. She stood at the edge of our camp on the glacier with the folded chart in both hands, holding it the way you'd hold a sleeping child, and said, "You were never meant to carry it, cartographer. You were only meant to bring it this far."

"That's not what the cipher says," I told her.

"The cipher says what we need it to say." She smiled then, and it was the worst thing I'd seen all winter, because it was kind. "Your father was a good man, Lena Marchetti. He would understand."

I have been robbed before. Twice by bandits, once by a business partner, once by a customs officer in Vell who confiscated an entire case of maps because he claimed the coastline was seditious. But being robbed by people who are certain they're right — that has a texture all its own. It doesn't feel like theft. It feels like being edited.

They left us the tent. They left us half the rope, out of some lingering decency or some practical arithmetic about how much shame Maren could carry up a glacier. They did not leave us food, or the climbing irons, or Sable's sword, which he surrendered without a fight when three crossbows found their argument very persuasive.

And then they walked south, toward the Hollow Peak, eleven souls and one stolen map, into the white.

* * *

Sable sat in the snow beside the collapsed fire ring for a long time before he spoke.

"I should have gone after them."

"With what? Your fists? They had your sword." I was wrapping my hands in strips of blanket, because frostbite is a slow negotiator and I wanted to open talks early. "Besides, we don't need the map."

He looked up. In the flat light of the glacier his face was all planes and shadows, a topography I could have charted blind by then. "The map burned into your head?"

"No. Better." I flexed my fingers inside the wrappings. "The map was never the route, Sable. It was the *invitation*. My father built the vault so that only blood kin could open it — you figured that out weeks ago, so don't make that face. The final road only appears if drawn by blood kin. Which means the map Maren stole is a beautiful, expensive piece of nothing. She can follow every line on it to the summit and find a door that doesn't know she exists."

"You knew." His voice did something complicated. "How long?"

"Since the shrine. Since Hale." I kept my eyes on my hands. "Since I watched you decode that margin note and then fold the map away like it was nothing, and lie to me with your whole honest face. You're bad at lying, by the way. You get very still, like a man standing very close to a cliff edge and pretending it's a floor."

"I was trying to protect you."

"I know. That's what made it infuriating." I finally looked at him. "Here's the part you're not going to like. I let you keep the secret. Because if I admitted I already knew I was the lock, then it became real. Then it wasn't my father hiding something from me anymore — it was my father *choosing* me, deliberately, as the last door of a dead kingdom. And I wasn't ready to be chosen. So I let you carry the guilt, and I carried the knowing, and we climbed along together like two mules loaded down with each other's baggage."

The wind came off the ice field and found every gap in my coat. Sable didn't say anything for a while. When he did, it was quiet enough that the mountain nearly took it.

"So no secrets left."

"No secrets left." I stood, tested my weight on the packed snow, and offered him my hand. "Which means from here on, when one of us is terrified, the other one gets to know about it. Fair warning: I'm terrified roughly always."

He took my hand and came up off the snow, and for one unguarded moment he almost smiled — the first one since Greyhollow, since before his brother's name started living behind his teeth. "Then we're evenly matched," he said. "I've been terrified since chapter one."

* * *

We climbed through the twelfth moon's eclipse.

I want to be precise about this, because precision is the only prayer I know how to say. The moon did not vanish. It went the color of old iron, then the color of a bruise healing, and the light it gave back to the glacier was wrong in a way that made the snow look like skin and the crevasses look like veins. We roped ourselves together with the half-rope the loyalists' decency had left us, and we climbed into that bruised light because there was nowhere else, and because the thirteenth moon was coming whether we were ready or not.

Sable led the pitches where the ice was steep. He'd been a royal guard; he climbed like one, economical and graceless and utterly reliable, and I followed in his boot-holes and hated him a little for how much safer it made me feel.

At the second camp, huddled in the tent with the storm screaming its one long syllable outside, he said, "Tell me about your father. Not the cartographer. Him."

I thought about refusing. Old habit. Then I remembered the bargain we'd struck on the glacier — no secrets left — and paid up.

"He hummed when he drew coastlines. Never when he drew mountains. Mountains he did in silence, like he was apologizing to them." I pulled my knees up under the blanket. "He used to tell me that a map is a promise you make to strangers. That every line you draw, someone might die trusting it, so you'd better be sure. And then he disappeared for four years and broke every promise he ever made, and I spent those years being furious at him instead of scared for him, because furious was easier." I paused. "He knew. He must have known when he drew that map that I'd find it. He wrote my name into the margins in cipher before I ever told the map who I was. He chose me and then he died without asking if I wanted to be chosen."

"And now?"

"Now I think maybe that's just what love looks like when it's in a hurry." I said it before I could stop myself, and then, because the bargain was the bargain: "That sentence wasn't about him."

Sable was quiet for long enough that the storm filled the space. Then: "My brother Tomas enforced a retrieval order once. A smuggler with a box of Crown letters. The order said bring the man alive. Tomas brought him alive. The man talked anyway, in a cell, and they executed them both — the smuggler for the letters, Tomas for having read one." His voice stayed level the way ice stays level over deep water. "I learned that day that duty is a story the powerful tell the dutiful. I've been telling myself a different story ever since. It's got worse prose but better characters."

"Does it have an ending yet?"

"It's got a cliffhanger." He banked the little stove. "Sleep, Lena. Tomorrow's the shoulder of the peak, and the thirteenth moon won't wait for either of us."

* * *

The last day, the mountain stopped pretending to be climbable and simply dared us.

We went up the couloir my father's cipher had described — *the throat*, he'd called it, in letters small enough to hide in a border flourish — a chimney of blue ice between walls of black stone, climbing with the half-rope and two iron pitons Sable had hammered flat and sharpened against a rock until they were less equipment than arguments. The eclipse-light followed us even in daytime now, the twelfth moon hanging pale and wounded over the southern ridge like a debt coming due.

Halfway up the throat, the rope went tight above me and I looked up to find Sable braced across the chimney, arm shaking, holding my whole weight plus his own on two pitons and a prayer.

"Lena." His voice was stripped down to studs and rivets. "If I fall, you go on. You're the lock. I'm just the—"

"Don't." I climbed faster than I've ever climbed, hands screaming, and came level with him, and put my forehead against his frozen hood. "Finish that sentence and I'll leave you here out of spite. You're not 'just.' You have never once been 'just.'" I breathed, and the breath fogged between us. "No secrets, remember? Here's mine: somewhere on this glacier I stopped doing this to survive and started doing it because the alternative was a world where you weren't in it. I'm not naming it. Naming it can wait until we're somewhere warm. But it's true, and now you know, and you can be furious at me for saying it at the worst possible altitude."

For a moment he was so still I thought the cold had taken him. Then he laughed — one short, cracked sound that the mountain swallowed immediately — and said, "Worst possible altitude," and reached past me to set the next piton, and we climbed.

* * *

We came out of the throat at dusk onto the summit shoulder, and the world below us was on fire with torchlight.

Two armies. I could see them from that height the way my father must have seen them in his mind's eye when he drew roads leading nowhere — the loyalists' camp strung along the southern col like a rosary of embers, and beyond them, lower, the disciplined cold squares of the Crown's inquisitors, whose fires burned white and even, like arithmetic. Between them, the glacier we'd crawled up lay silver and empty, our own trail already erased by wind, as if the mountain had decided we'd never happened.

"They'll converge by morning," Sable said. "Hale will let the loyalists break themselves on the approach and take the peak at the cost of nothing. It's what I'd order."

"It's what you'd have ordered," I said. "Past tense. You're unemployed."

Above us, cut into the summit rock, was a door.

Not a grand one. That was the thing that undid me — not a vault, not a throne room, but a plain stone lintel over a plain stone frame, the kind of door you'd find on a shepherd's hut, with no handle and no keyhole and no seam. And across the lintel, worn nearly smooth by decades of weather, was a drafting mark I'd known my whole life: my father's sigil, the compass rose with one broken point.

The twelfth moon slid fully into eclipse behind us, and the light on the stone turned the color of ember-coal, and the door — I swear this on every map I ever sold — the door began, very faintly, to glow from the inside, like paper held up to a flame.

Sable's hand found mine. No secrets left, so neither of us pretended it was for balance.

"It knows you're here," he said quietly.

Below us, the first horns of the loyalists sounded in the dark, thin and hungry across the ice. Above us, my father's door waited, glowing softly, patient as ink.

I stepped forward.
