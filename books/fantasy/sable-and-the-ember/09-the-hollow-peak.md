## 9. The Hollow Peak

**Lena**

The last stretch of the Hollow Peak was not a road. It was a wound.

My father had drawn it that way on purpose, I understood now — a scar of black ink across the final page of the map, jagged as a lightning strike frozen mid-fall. For years I'd assumed it was damage, water stain, the ruin of an old document. Cartographers see ruins everywhere; it's how we forgive ourselves for the blank spaces. But it wasn't damage. It was instruction. *Climb here, and only here, and know that the mountain will not love you for it.*

Sable climbed ahead of me, one hand finding holds in rock that shouldn't have held anything, and I followed the map's scar with my boots and my bleeding fingers and the Ember riding against my sternum like a second heart that had opinions about my pace.

Because that's what it had become, in the weeks since Greyhollow. Not a flame I carried. A passenger. It warmed when I despaired and cooled when I lied, and it had been very cold lately, because I had been lying to Sable about how well I could climb, and to myself about what I intended to do at the top.

Below us — far below, so far that the sound arrived late and flattened, like thunder remembered rather than heard — two armies were converging on the mountain's root.

I couldn't see them. The cloud deck had swallowed everything past the tree line hours ago. But I could feel them the way you feel a storm through the floorboards: Inquisitor Hale's Crown regulars marching up the western approach with their neat, murderous discipline, and the loyalists coming from the south with Maerin Voss at their head, carrying my father's map like a relic she hadn't earned. They would meet somewhere on the lower slopes. They would fight, or they would parley, or they would do that terrible thing armies do when they both want the same small thing — stand very still, pointing weapons at each other, while the weather decides.

It didn't matter. That was the strange mercy of altitude. From up here, every army was a rumor.

"Rest," Sable called down, and his voice came out thin, scraped by wind. "Lena. Rest."

"We're close."

"We've been close for an hour." He wedged himself into a notch of stone and reached back for me, and I took his hand because refusing it would have cost more breath than I had. His fingers were torn raw. So were mine. We were, I reflected, going to make a very unimpressive pair of legends, if anyone ever wrote this down. Which no one would, because everyone who might write it down was currently climbing the other side of the mountain to kill us.

"The vault," he said, when I'd hauled myself level with him. "Your father built it. You said the map shows—"

"The map shows a door." I pulled the chart out — carefully, the way you handle something that has been trying to talk to you all week. The ink had stopped moving. That frightened me more than the moving ever had. A living map goes quiet either because it is finished, or because it is waiting. "A door in the summit stone, drawn in his hand, with his mark. And beneath it, one line."

"Which says?"

I looked at him. The twelfth moon's eclipse had passed two nights ago, and we had climbed through its shadow like penitents, and there was nothing left between us now — not the secret of the blood-lock, not my confession that I'd known, not the ugly arithmetic of who had sacrificed what to get us this far. Stripped bare, as I'd promised myself. No secrets left.

Only the climb, and him, and the lie I was still keeping warm under my ribs.

"It says: *the door opens for the drawer's kin, and closes forever after.*"

Sable went still in the way he did before violence or grief, which by now were nearly the same gesture. "And after it closes?"

"After it closes," I said, "the mountain keeps what it's given."

He nodded slowly. He thought I meant the Ember. I let him think it. There are mercies that are also betrayals, and I had stopped pretending I knew the difference.

* * *

**Sable**

She was lying to me again.

I want it recorded — if anyone ever asks, which no one will — that I knew. Sixteen years a guard teaches you to read people the way Lena reads terrain: the small hesitations, the weight shifted off a bad ankle, the sentence finished too smoothly. She told me the door's inscription like a woman reciting a bill of sale, and her eyes went to the left, always the left, the way they did when she was holding something back behind her teeth.

I didn't call it out. That's the part I'll answer for, if there's anyone to answer to. I had spent my whole life believing that duty meant saying the true thing at the worst moment — and it had gotten my brother killed, saying the true thing, reporting the true thing, standing at attention while the true thing got him fed into the machinery of the Crown. So maybe I'd learned the wrong lesson. Or maybe the right one arrives late and wearing different clothes.

Below us, muffled by cloud, the first horns sounded.

Two notes, Crown-made, clean and cold. Then, a breath later, a third horn answering from the south — rougher, older, the loyalist call that half the exiled houses still kept oiled like heirlooms. They'd found each other on the slopes. Hale must have offered terms. Voss must have refused them, or accepted them, or done whatever a woman does when she believes her son died carrying a king in his chest and the king is finally within reach.

Either way, the noise of it climbed the mountain after us like something hungry.

"Lena." I touched her shoulder. "Whatever's up there — whatever your father built — remember that he built it to be *ended*. Not crowned. Not kept. Ended."

"I know what he built it for."

"Do you?" I turned her gently toward me. Wind took her hair sideways. "Because I've watched that flame work on you for three hundred miles. It shows you things. Your father's hand. Roads that don't exist. A kingdom redrawn in your own ink — don't look surprised, you talked about it in your sleep outside the shrine, and it was the most beautiful sedition I've ever heard. It offers, Lena. That's what it does. That's all it does. Everything it gives, it gives on loan."

"And what would you know about it?"

"Nothing." I said it flatly, and it was true, and it hurt like truth does. "Nothing except what happened to every man I served who touched power he didn't draw himself. My brother included."

Something moved behind her eyes — not anger. Recognition. She knew I was talking about myself, the way she always knew, and hated that she knew, the way she always hated it.

"The door opens for the drawer's kin," she said quietly. "That's me, Sable. Whatever happens up there, it happens to me alone. If I tell you to go back down—"

"You won't."

"If I tell you to go back down, will you?"

I looked at her. At the frost in her lashes and the map pressed against her chest and the whole ridiculous, impossible architecture of the last months standing between us — the raid, the road, the glacier, the night on the ice when she confessed she'd always known she was the lock and I confessed I'd hidden it, and we'd laughed, actually laughed, two liars with nothing left to steal from each other but the truth.

"No," I said. "I'm a disgrace to the uniform. Lying to commanders, abandoning posts, consorting with cartographers. One more dereliction won't change the ledger."

Her mouth twitched. Even now. Even here.

"Come on, then," she said. "Let's go disappoint everyone."

* * *

**Lena**

The door was exactly where my father had drawn it, which should not have surprised me and did anyway.

Summit stone, black with old fire, and set into its face a seam no wider than a knife blade — invisible until you stood where the map said to stand, at which point it appeared all at once, the way meaning appears in a cipher once you hold the key. Around it, cut deep and worn shallow, ran the drafting mark I'd known since childhood: the compass rose with one spoke broken, my father's signature, the one he used to doodle in the margins of my schoolwork when I was small enough to sit under his drafting table.

*Thirteen turns,* the dying stranger had whispered in Greyhollow. Thirteen turns of the moon. But standing there, palm against the stone, I felt the other meaning arrive like a key turning: thirteen turns of the *lock*. Of the mechanism. Of whatever waited inside my father's last work.

I put my hand in the seam, and the mountain opened.

Not dramatically. Doors in stories swing and blaze. This one simply stopped being closed — the seam widened, the dark inside breathed out warm, smelling of hot iron and cedar and, impossibly, of my father's workshop: tallow smoke and ink and the particular dust of vellum. I stepped through with Sable's hand hard on my shoulder, and the mountain closed behind us, and the horns below went silent as if the peak had put a hand over their mouths.

The vault was round and low and lit by nothing, and at its center, on a plinth of black glass, burned the Ember.

It was smaller than I expected. That's the cruelty of sovereign things — they're never the size of their reputation. A flame the size of a cupped hand, gold-white at the core, and it did not flicker, because flickering is doubt, and it had never doubted anything in its long burning. The last flame of a fallen kingdom. The thing two armies were killing each other to reach. It sat in the dark like a patient eye.

And then it looked at me.

I don't have better language than that. It looked at me, and it spoke without sound, and what it said was *yes*.

*Yes* to everything. Yes to the kingdom redrawn — and I saw it, God help me, I saw it whole: Thornfell mapped inch by inch in my own hand, roads where roads had never gone, bridges over gorges, towns named for my mother, borders drawn fair and generous because I would draw them fair and generous, wouldn't I? Yes to vengeance — Hale's theater of justice unmasked in front of the Crown itself, Voss's dead son avenged, every lie of the last twenty years dragged into light and made to account. Yes to restoration — my father alive in the flame's memory, his voice, his laugh, the weight of his hand on my head, everything the mountain had taken given back on terms I could almost believe.

*You are the lock,* it said, or sang, or simply was, in the way a sun is simply bright. *Locks open. What you keep, you keep forever. Draw me a kingdom, daughter of the drawer. No one else can. No one else ever could.*

And I understood — finally, fully, the way you understand a map only when you've walked the ground — why my father had filled his last chart with roads leading nowhere.

Not to hide the Ember. Anyone could hide a flame. He drew roads to nowhere because he had seen what I was seeing: that the Ember doesn't show you the future. It shows you a future — the one you want most, rendered so perfectly you can't see the frame around it. Every road it offered led somewhere beautiful, and none of them led anywhere real, because a kingdom redrawn in one person's ink is just one person's wish wearing a crown. He had spent his life mapping true things. He knew the difference. And he loved me — that was the part that broke me, standing in the dark with a god-sized candle purring at my wants — he loved me enough to make me the lock and trust me to turn it the right way, knowing the right way was the way that gave up everything.

Renunciation. The hardest act of love. He'd written it once in the margin of an old coastal chart, in cipher, and I'd thought it was a shipping note.

"Sable," I said. My voice came out steady, which surprised me. "Turn around."

"Lena—"

"Please. Whatever you hear. Turn around."

He looked at me for a long moment. Then he did the hardest thing I ever asked of him, harder than the glacier, harder than the lying: he trusted me, and turned.

I walked to the plinth. The Ember rose to meet my hands, warm as a sleeping child, and it showed me one last road — the longest, loveliest one, a lifetime drawn in gold — and I said the thing my father had built this room for me to say:

"No. You belong to no throne. Not theirs. Not mine."

And I drove my palms together around the flame, and gave it to the mountain.

The light went through me like a shout through a bell. I felt it leave — felt the kingdom-roads unravel, felt the vengeance cool, felt my father's voice fade to a warmth in my chest and then to memory and then to ash — and the black glass plinth drank the flame down, and the walls of the vault began to glow, seams of ember-light racing away into the rock, down, deeper than any map I will ever draw, into the roots of Thornfell where no army, no inquisitor, no loyalist, no daughter of anybody can follow.

The mountain sealed itself with a sound like a book closing.

Then dark. Then silence. Then Sable's arms around me, and my face against his ruined coat, and the two of us standing in an empty room at the top of the world, poorer than we'd ever been.

Far below, faint through miles of stone, I heard the horns stop.

Both of them. All of them. The war dying unborn, its prize gone to stone and fire, and neither army yet knowing that the thing they'd climbed for had just chosen, over every crown in the world, the dark.

Sable pulled back and looked at me — at whatever the giving had written on my face — and asked, very quietly:

"What do we do now?"

I looked around the vault, at the sealed door, at the long descent, at the rest of my unmapped life.

"Now," I said, "we learn to walk off the edge of the map."
