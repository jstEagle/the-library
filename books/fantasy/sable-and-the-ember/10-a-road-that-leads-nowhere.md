## 10. A Road That Leads Nowhere

The mountain did not roar when it sealed itself. I had expected roaring. I had expected the sky to split and both armies to fall to their knees and some great voice to pronounce judgment on the kingdoms of men. Instead there was a sound like a door closing in a house you thought was empty — a soft, final click of stone against stone — and then the snow on Hollow Peak settled back into its ancient shapes, and the wind picked up as if nothing had happened at all.

Below us, two armies stood in the dark with their torches guttering and their generals shouting at each other about who had lost what.

I had burned my maps that morning. All of them. Fourteen years of work — every smuggler road, every goat track, every secret pass through the Thornfells that I'd bled and frozen to chart — fed page by page into my father's little vault fire until the vault itself drank them down along with the Ember. The mountain took everything: the flame, the paper, the ink that remembered. It took my father's last secret and made it no one's.

Sable watched me feed the last sheet to the fire. It was a map of Greyhollow, ordinary as bread, the first map I'd ever drawn for coin. He didn't try to stop me. He just said, "Cold up here," which was his way of saying he understood, and we turned our backs on the summit and walked down through the twelfth moon's dying eclipse while two factions discovered, all at once, that they had nothing left to fight over.

You'd think they'd have been angry at us. You'd think someone would have come looking. But here is the thing about a war that dies unborn: nobody mourns it. The loyalists went home to their exile and told themselves the Ember had chosen silence over them, which was almost true. The Crown's inquisitors wrote reports saying it had never existed at all, which was also almost true. Inquisitor Hale — who let us walk out of that shrine because a dead Ember served him better than a captured one — shook my hand like we were merchants concluding a dull trade. "Justice unrecorded," he said, "is still justice." I didn't correct him. It wasn't justice. It was mercy, wearing justice's coat, and I've decided I can live with that.

We came down out of the Thornfells in spring, following roads that were on no map because I had burned every map they'd ever been on. Sable kept asking where we were going, and I kept saying "away," and eventually he stopped asking, because away turned out to be enough for both of us.

* * *

Saltmere smells of fish and tar and, in the evenings, of someone else's dinner, which is the smell I love best. We've been here eight months. There's a shop on Cordwainer Lane with a crooked blue door, and above it a sign I painted myself: **MARCHETTI & VANE, CARTOGRAPHERS**. Sable insisted on the ampersand. He said if he was going to be respectable, he wanted it in writing.

He is not respectable. He sweeps the shop and argues with customers about scale and once threw a drunk lordling into the street for mocking my prices, but he still sleeps with a knife under his pillow and still flinches at red wax seals. Some things don't seal themselves shut. You just learn to walk around them.

I sell maps of ordinary places now. Harbors. Coastlines. A very popular one of the road to the shrine at Wellmarket, drawn from memory by a man who used to guard shrines for a living. Nobody asks me about the Thornfells. If they do, I tell them the passes are closed, which is true, though not for the reason they imagine. The mountain keeps its own counsel now. Sometimes, on clear nights, I look north and swear I can see a faint warmth on the peak, like an ember banked under ash, patient as anything. My father drew roads leading nowhere because he understood something I only learned at the top of the world: that some things are safest in no one's keeping, not even memory's. Especially not memory's.

Business is good. People will always pay for maps of places they already know; it comforts them, seeing their small lives given borders and names. I understand that better than I used to.

There is one map I don't sell. It hangs behind the counter in a plain frame, and customers sometimes ask about it, and I always give the same answer: "Not for sale. Commission piece."

It is blank. Good linen paper, stretched flat, no coastlines, no roads, no compass rose. Just, in the lower right corner, walking off the very edge of the page, two small figures in ink — one tall, one shorter, close together, headed nowhere in particular. Sable thinks they look like us. I've never told him they are us. A cartographer is allowed her secrets; I got that from my father too, along with the steady hands and the habit of reading other people's margins.

Some nights, after we blow out the lamps, I stand in front of it and think about the stranger in Greyhollow who pressed a folded chart into my hands with his last breath. Thirteen turns, he whispered. I gave the flame thirteen turns and it asked for one more than I had, and somehow we both survived the arithmetic. I think he'd have liked Saltmere. I think he'd have liked the blank map most of all — a man who spent his life carrying a document that knew too much, finally handed a page that knew nothing at all.

Last week a girl came in, maybe sixteen, with ink-stained fingers and her father's drafting kit slung over her shoulder, and she looked at the blank map for a long time and then at me, and she said, "What's it a map *of*?"

"A road," I said.

"Where does it go?"

"Nowhere," I said, and smiled, and sold her a harbor chart, and did not tell her that nowhere is the finest destination there is, because you can never be followed there, and no one can ever take it from you.

* * *

I never used to notice doors. Twenty years a soldier teaches you to watch windows, rooftops, the crowd's hands — but doors are how people leave, and I had no talent for watching things leave. Then Lena Marchetti walked through the blue door of our shop every morning for eight months, and now I watch doors the way other men watch the tide.

She doesn't know I count them. She knows most of what I don't say, which is more than anyone's ever managed, but she doesn't know this. A man keeps one or two things folded up inside him, pressed flat like a chart, or he's got nothing left that's only his.

Tonight she's at the counter working late, inking a commission — the harbor at dawn, gulls rendered so precisely you can hear them — and I'm supposed to be closing the shutters, and instead I'm standing in front of the framed map again. The two figures at the edge. I asked her once, early on, careful as a man defusing something, whether she'd ever draw herself a new Thornfell. Whether the old roads called to her.

"They're not roads anymore," she said. "They're just where we walked." And she kissed me on the jaw, which is her way of ending conversations she considers finished, and went back to her ink.

But here's what I know, standing here in the lamplight while she hums off-key over her harbor chart: the figures on that blank map are walking *off* the edge. Not toward anywhere. Off. And I've started to wonder — quietly, the way you wonder about things you're not ready to want — whether somewhere out past the edge of the page there's another country neither of us has ever charted. Somewhere with no Crown in it, no exiles, no seals in red wax. Somewhere a disgraced guard could be just a man who sweeps a shop, and a cartographer could be just a woman whose father loved her enough to make her the lock instead of the key.

There's a ship leaving Saltmere in three days. Coastal trader, bound south past the maps' end. I saw the notice nailed up this morning and I've been carrying the thought around all day like a stone I haven't decided to keep.

Lena looks up from her work. She catches me staring at the frame, and her quill goes still, and I watch her read my face the way she reads a margin — quickly, completely, missing nothing.

"Sable," she says slowly, "what are you thinking?"

And I open my mouth to say *nothing*, the way I've said it ten thousand times, duty cracking under the weight of memory — except that's not true anymore, is it. Nothing died on that mountain along with the war. So instead I point at the two small figures walking off the edge of the page, and I say:

"I think they might be going somewhere. If we let them."

She sets down her quill. Outside, the tide is coming in, and somewhere past it, three days south, a ship is waiting with room for two passengers and no destination worth keeping secret.

"Show me," says Lena Marchetti, who once mapped every road in the Thornfells and burned them all, "somewhere that isn't on any map."

And for the first time in my life, I find I actually know the way.
