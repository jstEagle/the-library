## 1. The Valley That Forgets

The ink was the first thing Cyrus learned to fear, and the last thing he learned to love.

Master Ellery had told him that on his first day, when he was seven years old and his hand shook so badly he'd blotted three lines in a row. *Ink is honest, boy. It remembers everything you've ever done to it. Be kind to it, and it will be kind to you.* Then Ellery had counted Cyrus's breaths — aloud, in a flat ledger-keeper's voice, one through sixty — until the boy's hand went still from sheer terror of being counted wrong.

Nine years later, the counting hadn't stopped. It had simply become the shape of silence itself.

Cyrus sat at the long copying table in the scriptorium of the Temple of Quiet Hours, third window from the east wall, and copied the harvest tithes for the village of Lowmarsh into a fresh ledger. Barley, twelve sacks. Rye, nine. Wool, forty-one fleeces, two spoiled by rot. His pen moved without him thinking about it, which was the whole point. A scribe who thought about his letters made mistakes. A scribe who thought about anything made mistakes. The Temple paid for precision and nothing else, and Cyrus had learned to fold himself small enough that precision was all that remained visible.

"Breath," Master Ellery said, from somewhere behind him.

Cyrus exhaled. He had been holding it again. He did that when a line came out well — held his breath as if the words might smudge if the air touched them too soon.

"Fourteen this hour," Ellery said. "You were at eleven last week. You're getting worse."

"Yes, Master."

"Getting worse is a debt, Underhill. Everything is a debt. You know the arithmetic."

He knew the arithmetic. He had known it since before he could read it, which in the Temple of Quiet Hours was considered a kind of scandal — a child raised among ledgers who couldn't yet read his own account. His father had signed something before Cyrus was born. Nobody would tell him what. The abbot called it *provision*, the way the temple called the valley's borders *blessing* and its stillness *grace*, and every word was chosen the way a moneylender chooses which hand to shake with.

Cyrus dipped his pen. Barley, twelve sacks. The barley never changed. Nothing in the valley changed, and that was the entire design.

Outside the third window, the morning lay over the valley like a sheet pulled up to the chin. The Terraces of Lowmarsh stepped down toward the river in their neat green shelves. Smoke rose from chimneys in perfectly straight columns because there was no wind worth mentioning — there was never wind worth mentioning. Beyond the fields, the border stones stood in their ancient ring around the valley floor, gray and lichen-eaten, each one carved with a word no one could read anymore. The villagers called them the Quiet Stones. Children dared each other to touch them. Nothing happened when they did. Nothing ever happened here.

That was the point. That was the *mercy*, if you believed the abbot, and most people did because believing was easier than asking what the mercy cost.

Magic could not cross the border stones. Whatever else was true about the world outside — and travelers' tales said plenty, most of it terrible — none of it followed you in. No curses rode the mountain passes down into the valley. No debts of blood or bargain could be collected past the stones. The sold and the stolen came here to hide, generation after generation, and the valley forgot them kindly, and they forgot themselves, and their children grew up thinking the stillness was simply how the world was.

Cyrus had grown up thinking that too. He was twenty-six years old and he had never seen rain fall sideways. He had never watched a fire burn a color fire shouldn't burn. He had never once, in his entire counted and careful life, felt the particular vertigo of standing in a place where the rules might not apply.

But he had dreams. Everyone in the valley had dreams, though admitting it was poor manners. In his dreams there was a color he had no name for, deep and wet and moving, like light through water over something enormous. In his dreams someone was calling his name in a voice that wasn't quite a voice, more like a hand pressed flat against the inside of his skull. And in his dreams he always woke just before answering, with his heart going like a trapped bird and the taste of iron in his mouth, and he would lie in his narrow cell in the novices' wing and listen to the Temple bell count the hours and think: *there is something I am supposed to remember.*

Then morning would come, and Master Ellery would count his breaths, and the feeling would fold itself away like a letter into an envelope that had no address.

"Copies," Ellery said now, materializing at his shoulder the way he always did, silent as spilled flour. He took the finished page, held it at arm's length, and squinted at it with the expression of a man appraising a horse with a hidden limp. "Your descenders are lazy."

"They're consistent, Master."

"Consistent laziness is still laziness." He set the page down. "Again."

Cyrus began again. Barley, twelve sacks. Outside, a cart creaked along the river road, and he heard old Marta Halloway arguing cheerfully with her mule, and for one unguarded moment Cyrus envied the mule. The mule had never signed anything.

* * *

Midday meal was bread, hard cheese, and small beer, taken in the refectory in silence, because speech was permitted only after the noon bell and only then in low voices on matters of work. Forty novices and scribes sat in rows, and the sound of the room was chewing, and the clink of spoons, and the great clock in the tower ticking like a patient heart.

Brother Anselm sat across from him. Anselm was the closest thing the Temple produced to a friend, which meant he broke silence first.

"You've gone gray," Anselm murmured. "Ellery's been at you since dawn?"

"Since before dawn. The tithe rolls have to be done before the equinox audit."

"The audit." Anselm chewed thoughtfully. "You know what I heard? I heard the abbot's had the undercroft sealed. Third time this year. There are records down there nobody's supposed to look at anymore, and now there's a lock on them that isn't temple-made. Bought iron. From outside."

Cyrus kept his eyes on his cheese. "People say a lot of things."

"People say things," Anselm agreed, "and sometimes the things are true. My cousin works the gatehouse. She says a courier came through last month asking questions about old contracts. Contracts from before the current abbot. Contracts with names in them." He paused, delicately. "She says the courier asked about the name Underhill."

The taste of iron arrived in Cyrus's mouth, sudden and unexplained, as if his dream had leaked into the waking world for half a second. He put down his spoon.

"That's not funny, Anselm."

"I'm not laughing."

"What contracts? My father's been dead eleven years. Whatever he owed—"

"Whatever he owed," Anselm said quietly, "the Temple renewed it every year on your birthday. You knew that much, surely. Why do you think they keep you? Scribes can be hired, Cyrus. Debts are kept."

The noon bell rang. Around them, forty people rose in unison and returned to their work, and the silence closed over the conversation like water over a dropped stone.

Cyrus walked back to the scriptorium with his heart counting itself, breath after breath after breath, and understood for the first time that the counting had never been discipline. It had been inventory.

* * *

The afternoon passed the way all afternoons passed, in ink and arithmetic and the tick of the tower clock, but something had come loose in Cyrus and would not sit flat again. He copied the tithe rolls and his mind kept returning to the same image: his father's hand, some version of it, signing some document, year after year, *renewed, renewed, renewed* — and then stopping. Eleven years dead, and the renewals had continued without him. Which meant someone else had been signing. Which meant someone else had been *paying*. Or pretending to.

At vespers the abbot preached, as he always did, on the mercy of the valley. Father Abbot Oren was a soft-spoken man with soft hands and a voice like warm oil, and he spoke of the border stones the way other men spoke of saints.

"We are the kept ones," he said, hands folded over his belly, gazing out at the sea of bowed heads. "The world beyond our stones is a world of appetite. It takes and takes and calls the taking freedom. But here—" he spread those soft hands, "—here nothing can follow us. Not curse, not creditor, not the hungry dark. We gave up magic so that magic could never own us. Remember that, children. What we surrendered is the measure of what we were spared."

*Creditor,* Cyrus thought, sitting in the fourth row with his hands folded in imitation of everyone else. *He said creditor. Out loud. Like it was a blessing.*

After vespers, the Temple slept early, as temples of quiet hours must. But Cyrus did not sleep. He lay in his cell listening to the tower clock and watching moonlight move across the floor in a slow silver bar, and at some point he realized he had made a decision, though he could not have said when. Perhaps decisions in the valley worked the way everything else did — slowly, silently, without wind, until one day they were simply true.

He dressed in the dark. He took his scribe's knife, not for defense but because a scribe's knife opens things, and that seemed suddenly important. And he went down into the Temple, past the dormitories, past the refectory, down the stair behind the vestry that smelled of cold stone and older paper, to the door of the undercroft.

There was a new lock on it. Bought iron, from outside, exactly as Anselm had said. It sat wrong against the old wood like a foreign body.

Cyrus stood before it for a long moment, breathing — one, two, three, counting his own breaths now, for himself, in his own voice inside his own head — and it occurred to him that in twenty-six years he had never once opened a door he wasn't assigned to open. That his whole life was a corridor of doors opened by other people's permission.

His father had signed something. His name was written somewhere beneath a lock bought from outside, in a building that preached forgetting, in a valley built on the promise that nothing could follow anyone in.

Which raised the question he had never let himself ask, standing there in the dark with his knife in his hand and his heart going like a bird:

If nothing could cross the border stones —

What, exactly, had his father's debt been running from?

Down the corridor behind him, faint but unmistakable, came the sound of the gatehouse bell. Once. Twice. A third time — the alarm pattern, the one reserved for trouble at the border stone.

Someone, or something, had just arrived at the edge of the valley.

And in Cyrus's chest, beneath the fear, beneath the counting, something that had been quiet for twenty-six years lifted its head and listened.
