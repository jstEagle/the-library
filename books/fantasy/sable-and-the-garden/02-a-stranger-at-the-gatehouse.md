## 2. A Stranger at the Gatehouse

The border stone had a name, once. The abbot told me it was carved on the far side, in letters older than the temple, but no one from the valley had crossed to read it in living memory. On our side the stone was smooth and blank as an egg, worn down by centuries of hands that touched it without knowing why. Pilgrims did it. Children dared each other to do it. I did it every morning on my way to fetch water, because Master Hollis counted my steps and I liked to give him one extra thing to be wrong about.

That morning, someone was already touching it.

I saw her from thirty paces and thought at first she was a beggar kneeling in prayer — travelers sometimes came as far as the stone and stopped there, as if something in them understood they could come no further. But beggars don't kneel with their fingers dug into the earth like that. Beggars don't shake.

She was trying to cross.

I want to be precise about what I saw, because for years afterward I tried to make it into something less than what it was, and precision is the one discipline they gave me that turned out to be worth anything. The woman was not wounded in any ordinary way. Her left side — arm, hip, the whole flank of her — was coming apart. Not bleeding out, not torn. *Dissolving*, the way ink dissolves when you hold the page over steam: edges going soft and grey, substance lifting off her like smoke that couldn't quite decide to rise. Where her forearm should have been solid there was a suggestion of forearm, and through it I could see the border stone behind her, blurred, as if seen through dirty glass.

And the land was doing it to her. I understood that immediately, the way you understand a dropped cup is falling before you understand why. The valley was pushing her back out of itself the way a body pushes out a splinter. Every inch she dragged herself forward cost her something visible. Grey flakes of her drifted off and never touched the ground.

I should have run for the guards. That's what the temple taught: strangers at the stone are reported, not approached. The valley keeps us safe by keeping everything else out, and the first duty of anyone wearing a scribe's cord is to preserve the records, and the second is to preserve himself, in that order, because a dead scribe writes nothing.

Instead I said, "You can't come in."

My voice came out steadier than I felt. She lifted her head. Her face was young under the grime and the strain — younger than mine, maybe twenty-five — with dark hair plastered to her forehead and eyes so dark they seemed to have no boundary between iris and pupil. She looked at me the way a drowning woman looks at a rope thrown to someone else.

"I know," she said. "I'm not—" A cough bent her double. When she straightened, there was blood on her lips, ordinary red blood, which somehow made the grey dissolution worse, as if her body couldn't decide which way to fail. "I'm not trying to come in. I need it to *look* like I'm trying."

I didn't understand that either. I have since learned that this would become the defining condition of my acquaintance with Sable: understanding arriving late, always late, and always more expensive than expected.

"Then stop," I said. "Whatever you're doing, stop, and I'll fetch help—"

"There's no help." She laughed, or made a sound adjacent to laughing, and it cracked in the middle. "Look at me, scribe. Look at where I am." She gestured with her half-gone hand at the air around her, and I finally saw what I'd been refusing to see: the grey dissolution wasn't only at her edges. It was everywhere on her, faint as frost on a window, a shimmer over all of her skin, as if the whole woman were a drawing someone was erasing from the outside in. "I crossed too much of the world to die at the door. But I can't go back out there either. So I'll stay here, on the line, where neither side will take me, and I'll finish my work while I still have hands to finish it with."

"The guards—"

"*No.*" It came out flat and final, and then softer, almost gentle, which frightened me more. "Boy. What's your name?"

"Cyrus Underhill," I said, because the question had the shape of authority even though she had none, and ten years of temple training answered before I could stop myself.

Something moved across her face. I'd call it grief if grief ever looked that much like relief. "Underhill," she repeated. "Of course. Of course it is." And then, before I could ask what she meant by that, she pushed herself up onto her knees and held out her right hand — the solid one, the last whole part of her — palm up, fingers curled slightly inward, the way you offer something fragile to a stranger you're afraid will drop it.

"Come here," she said. "Please. I don't have long, and what I have to give you won't keep."

Every sensible instinct I owned told me to walk away. I want that on the record, whoever ends up reading this: I knew better. I was raised by an institution whose entire theology was *do not engage with the outside*, and I had spent my life being the kind of person who follows rules precisely because following them meant no one could demand anything else of me. Rules were the wall I lived inside, smaller than the valley, comfortable as a ledger line.

I walked toward her anyway. Later I would tell myself it was duty. It was not duty. It was the first honest curiosity of my life, and it has cost me nearly everything, and I have never once regretted paying.

* * *

Up close she smelled of woodsmoke and something metallic, like coins held too long in a hot fist. Up close, the dissolving was worse; I could see individual motes of her lifting away, and where they left, the skin beneath was pale and unmarked, as if the land were skimming her rather than consuming her — taking the surface of her first, working down toward whatever was underneath.

"What are you?" I asked.

"A courier," she said. "Was. The road out of Ashford took nine days and it took other things too. Don't ask me what I am now; I've been asking the same question since the ford at Marrow Bridge." She flexed her right hand, checking its grip the way an old man checks his knees before stairs. "Cyrus Underhill. Do you know what you are?"

"A scribe. Indentured. Third year of seven."

"That's what you *do*." Her voice had gone low and urgent, and she kept glancing past my shoulder at the valley behind me, at the fields and the temple roof and the bell tower, as if expecting pursuit. Maybe she was. "Do you know what you *are*? Has anyone ever told you? Your father, your mother, the abbot — anyone?"

"My father's dead. My mother I never met. The abbot tells me I'm a debt paid down yearly, same as the roof tiles."

Something in her expression closed, like a door shutting on a room where someone is crying. "Yes," she said quietly. "They would tell you exactly that. In exactly those words." She breathed once, deep, and I watched the breath cost her — watched the shimmer climb a finger's width up her wrist. "Then listen, because I only get to say this once, and the land is listening too, and it doesn't like me saying anything at all."

She pressed her right hand flat against the border stone and, with a motion I can only describe as *unsealing* — thumb dragging through something invisible, like breaking the skin on broth — drew out a folded square of heavy paper that had not been there a moment before. It was stained dark along every fold. Not with dirt.

With blood. Inked in blood, drawn in blood, written in blood — I knew ink, I had mixed a thousand pots of it, oak gall and iron and gum arabic, and this was none of those. This was blood worked until it behaved like ink, and it had soaked through the paper so thoroughly that the map on its face seemed lit from behind, veins of dark red tracing roads and rivers and ranges I didn't recognize, converging on a single mark near the center: a mountain, rendered in careful crosshatch, with a symbol beside it I had never seen and yet somehow knew meant *debt*.

"This is the way," she said. "Under Mount Vael. There's a court there, and the court keeps a registry, and the registry keeps—" Her voice snagged. She swallowed and started again. "It keeps names. Names given as surety. Pledged souls, scribe, that's what the court trades in, that's what the fire under the mountain burns for. And three days ago the payment on one of those pledges failed to arrive. One pledge. One name." She looked up at me, and her eyes were wet, and the wetness was evaporating off them almost as fast as it formed, pulled out of her by the patient grey hunger of the valley's edge. "Yours."

The word went through me like cold water through cloth.

"That's not possible," I said. "My debt is held by the temple. It's in the ledgers. I've *seen* the ledgers—"

"You've seen the copies they let you see." She pressed the map into my hands — actually pressed it, folded my fingers closed over it with her remaining strength, and her grip was astonishingly strong for a body coming apart. "Your father pledged you before you were born. Or he was made to pledge you; the distinction matters to the living and not at all to the court. Every year the temple sent the renewal. Every year, for nineteen years. This year the courier who carried the renewal never reached the mountain." Her mouth twisted. "I know, because I was sent to find out why. And I found out why, Cyrus Underhill, and the why is standing in front of me holding his own death warrant in both hands."

I looked down at the map. Blood-roads. Blood-mountain. My hands were shaking and the map shook with them, and somewhere very far away I heard the morning bell ring for first copying, and some obedient fragment of me flinched toward the sound, and that flinch — that small trained animal-flinch toward the bell — is what convinced her, I think, that I was who she needed. Someone owned. Someone who would understand a debt in his bones.

"They'll come for you," she said. "When the default registers, the court sends collectors. Collectors don't argue and they don't wait and they cannot enter this valley — nothing of theirs can, that's the one mercy of this place — but they can wait at the stone forever, and they can take anyone who walks out. You have a choice, and it's a filthy choice, and I'm sorry. Stay, and live small and safe until the day the fire under Vael gutters out — and it *is* guttering, boy, I've seen it, everyone under the mountain has seen it — and then the protection dies with it and the collectors walk in and take every pledged soul in this valley at once. Or go. Take the map. Go under the mountain and settle the account yourself, on your terms, before the terms are settled for you."

"I can't leave," I whispered. "I'm indentured. If I cross the stone—"

"If you cross the stone, you'll feel magic for the first time in your life, and it will hurt, and then it will be the truest thing you've ever felt." She smiled, and it was a terrible smile, lopsided because the left side of her face had begun to soften. "I know. I crossed it once too. Everyone who carries this map crosses it once."

"Who *are* you?" I asked again, and this time it wasn't fear in my voice. It was something rawer. Because looking at her — at the dark eyes, at the particular stubborn set of her jaw, at the way she held pain like a chore she resented having to perform well — I felt a recognition I had no vocabulary for. Like hearing your own footsteps in an empty house.

Her face did the thing again — grief shaped like relief — and she opened her mouth to answer.

And the valley decided it had been patient enough.

The shimmer surged. I watched it happen: a slow grey wave rolling up her from the ground, the land finally expelling the splinter it had tolerated at its edge. She gasped — not a scream, she was too disciplined to scream, and I would learn that discipline was the core of her, deeper than any name — and shoved the map hard against my chest with her last solid hand.

"Under the mountain," she said. "Find the registry. Find your name. And Cyrus — when you find it — read what's written *underneath* it. Not the debt. Underneath the debt. Promise me you'll read it."

"I promise," I said, because she was dying, and you promise dying people things.

"No." Her hand found my collar and pulled, and her face was inches from mine, and her eyes were the darkest thing in the valley. "*Promise me.*"

"I promise," I said again, and meant it, though I didn't yet know what I was promising, or what it would cost, or that I would spend a book's worth of miles learning both.

She let go. She sagged back against the border stone, and the grey took her gently after that, the way sleep takes the old — in stages, with pauses, with time between stages for looking at the sky. She used those pauses to watch me. Not the sky. Me. As if I were the view she'd crossed the world for.

The last thing she said, when her voice had gone thin as paper and her outline had gone soft as smoke, was not a warning or an instruction. It was quieter than either, and it is the sentence I have turned over most nights since:

"You have his hands," she said. "I always wondered if you would."

Then the wind came up the valley, as it does at that hour, and there was nothing against the border stone but a smear of grey dust already scattering, and a single dark drop of blood soaking into the ancient blank stone like the first letter of a word.

* * *

I stood there until the bell rang twice more. I know because I counted, the way I count everything, the way I was made to count. First copying. Second copying. Master Hollis would mark me absent. The abbot would send someone. Someone would find me standing at the border stone holding a bloodstained map, and they would ask me questions, and I would have to decide — in the next hundred breaths, maybe fewer — whether the institution that raised me was the thing that had saved me or the thing that had sold me.

Because here is what I did not say to her, what I could not say, what I carried up the path from the stone with the map burning against my ribs like a coal: I had *seen* my entry in the temple ledgers. Hundreds of times. Debtor: Cyrus Underhill. Surety: labor, seven years. Renewed annually.

But last night, cataloguing the archive's outer room, I had found a ledger I'd never seen before — older, bound in black leather gone green with damp, locked with a clasp that bore a symbol I hadn't recognized.

A symbol that meant *debt*.

The same symbol now crosshatched beside a mountain of blood on the paper in my hands.

I began to run.
