## 3. What the Ledger Says

The archives of the Temple of Quiet Hours were kept colder than any other room in the valley, and Cyrus had always assumed this was for the sake of the paper. Now, standing in the dark between the shelves with a stolen lamp cupped in his hands, he understood it was for the sake of the silence. Cold kept things still. Stillness kept things secret.

He had told Master Ellery he was ill. A stomach complaint, nothing contagious — the kind of lie that a man who counted your breaths would see through in an instant. But Ellery had only looked at him over his spectacles, that long gray look, and said, "Rest, then," in a voice so flat it might have been permission or a dare. Cyrus still didn't know which. He suspected he never would.

The map was folded against his chest, beneath his shirt, where its edges pressed into his skin like a second set of ribs. He had not slept. Every time he closed his eyes he saw the woman at the border stone again — Sable, dissolving, her hand slick with blood, her mouth shaping words that the wind took before they reached him fully. *Your name is written beneath the mountain.* He had spent one whole night telling himself she was mad, or dying, or both, and that dying people said all manner of things to whoever happened to be standing near them.

But he was a scribe. And a scribe knows that every debt leaves a mark on paper.

If his name was written somewhere, there would be a ledger. If there was a ledger, it lived here, in the cold, among ten thousand others.

* * *

The Temple of Quiet Hours did not call itself a bank. No temple ever does. It called itself a house of remembrance, a place where the valley's accounts were kept in trust — grain owed against harvests, labor owed against lodging, small honest debts that made a community cohere. That was what Cyrus had believed when he was nine years old and first brought down these stairs, his master's hand heavy on his shoulder, and told: *This is where we keep what people owe each other. Guard it well.*

He had guarded it for twenty years. He had copied contracts until the words lost their meaning and became pure pattern, ink-shapes flowing from his pen without passing through his mind. That was the trick of the place, he realized now, standing in the dark. You could know everything and understand nothing, if you were tired enough, obedient enough, for long enough.

The soul-contracts were not shelved with the rest. They were kept in the western alcove, behind a door of black iron that required two keys — one held by the abbot, one by the master of ledgers. Cyrus had neither key. What he had was twenty years of copying those contracts, and a copyist's knowledge of how things are filed, and the memory of a single afternoon three years ago when the iron door had stood open while the master of ledgers went to fetch more lamp oil, and Cyrus had seen, just inside, a shelf of boxes labeled not by name but by year.

Contracts renewed yearly. Filed by year. Which meant that whatever he was looking for, if it existed, would be in the current year's box — or in last year's, if no one had gotten around to renewing it.

The problem was the door.

He had spent the morning solving it, and the solution embarrassed him. The iron lock was beyond him; the hinges were not. Twenty years of damp cold had done what no thief could, rusting the lower hinge nearly through. With a pry-bar borrowed from the cellar stores and forty minutes of work that left his palms bleeding, Cyrus had bent the door far enough to reach through the gap. His arm was longer than most. He'd never once been grateful for it until tonight.

Now the box sat on the reading table before him, plain wood, stenciled with the year in the temple's spare numerals. Inside: perhaps sixty contracts, each a single sheet of vellum folded twice, each sealed with wax of a particular gray that Cyrus had mixed himself a hundred times — ash and tallow and a drop of consecrated oil. Gray wax for souls. He had always thought it was for funerals.

His hands shook as he lifted them out. Not all of them. Just enough.

He worked alphabetically by the surname written along each fold. Underhill. Underhill. He passed Halloway, Harrow, Ives, Kettering — and stopped.

There was no Underhill.

He checked twice. Then a third time, slower, unfolding each sheet to its first line, because sometimes names were entered wrong, transposed, misspelled by some tired clerk a decade dead. Nothing. Sixty contracts for the current year, and none of them his.

Which meant either Sable had lied with her last breath, or —

Or the contract had lapsed. Or been moved. Or been paid.

He sat back on the stool and let the cold settle into him. Think like a scribe, he told himself. Follow the paper. Contracts lapse; they don't vanish. A lapsed contract is annotated, cross-referenced, archived. There would be a record. Somewhere in this building there was a piece of paper with his name on it, and it would tell him what he was.

He began at the beginning of the box instead of the end, and this time he read the entries properly, not just the names. And on the fourth sheet, in the space where the surety's description should have been, he found something odd: a contract for a soul pledged as collateral, debtor's name given as *Underhill, T.*, surety described only as *one male child, born of the debtor's line*. Renewed yearly. The renewal clause filled the bottom third of the page, a column of dates going back and back — and Cyrus counted them, because counting was the one thing he was good at — thirty-one dates, each in a different clerk's hand, each initialed.

Thirty-one years. He was twenty-nine.

The last renewal date was four years old. After that, the column continued in a different rhythm — not yearly anymore but quarterly, then monthly, as though someone had grown anxious. And beside the final entry, in fresh ink, barely a season old, a single word in a hand Cyrus knew better than his own:

*Held.*

Not renewed. Not paid. Not forgiven. *Held.*

He knew that handwriting. He had learned his letters under it. He had watched it move across a thousand pages, patient and dry and exact. Master Ellery had signed the last annotation on his soul.

* * *

Cyrus did not remember deciding to go further. He simply found himself deeper in the alcove, pulling older boxes, working backward through the years with the lamp held high. Thirty-one years back, to the founding entry, and there it was at last — not in a box but in a folio bound in green leather, the oldest kind, the kind used for original instruments. He opened it with fingers that no longer felt like his own.

The contract was beautiful. That was the worst part. Whoever had drafted it — and the hand was fine, confident, almost loving — had treated the pledge of a child's soul as an occasion for craft. The clauses marched down the page in perfect order. The debtor: Tobias Underhill, of the valley, indebted to the Court Beneath Mount Vael for a sum specified elsewhere. The surety: his son, unborn at the signing, whose soul to stand as payment should the debt go unpaid within the debtor's lifetime. The interest accruing yearly. The renewal requirement, yearly, witnessed and sealed.

And at the bottom, in the same fine hand, a line that was not legal language at all:

*The court extends this courtesy in recognition of the debtor's service. Let the child be raised in comfort and ignorance, and let no demand be made upon him until the fire dims.*

Until the fire dims.

Cyrus read the sentence five times. He thought of the border stone, and the courier who had died against it, and the way the valley kept magic out the way a body keeps out a splinter — and he thought, with a clarity that felt like falling: *the valley isn't a refuge. It's a container.* Everything safe inside it, everything owing, held in stasis until the thing that powered the contracts outside began to fail. Until the fire dimmed. And the fire was dimming — Sable had come to say so, or he had always half-believed she had — and the moment it failed, every debt in the valley would come due at once, including the one written against his own breath.

His father hadn't sold him carelessly. His father had sold him *carefully*, with a courtesy clause, a kindness stitched into the paperwork like a coin sewn into a coat lining. Raised in comfort. Raised in ignorance. Raised by the temple, in fact — because who else in the valley would take a debtor's orphan and make a scribe of him? Who else would feed him, teach him, house him, count his breaths?

The temple hadn't sheltered him. The temple had *kept* him. He was not a ward of the Quiet Hours. He was an asset under management.

* * *

He heard the footsteps on the stairs and did not panic, which surprised him. Perhaps panic was a luxury of men whose lives were their own.

Lamplight preceded the abbot around the corner of the shelves — Abbot Coram, old and straight-backed, in traveling boots rather than slippers, as though he had dressed for this. As though he had known exactly which night Cyrus would come down here, and had simply waited.

"You bent the hinge," the abbot said. "I'll want that repaired."

"Master Ellery wrote *held* on my contract." Cyrus's voice came out level. He was almost proud of it. "Four months ago. Before the courier died at the stone. Before I knew any of this existed."

"Yes."

"That's all? Yes?"

"What would you like me to say?" Coram came closer, unhurried, and looked down at the green folio as a man looks at an old wound. "That I've carried this for thirty years and hated every day of it? That's true, and it changes nothing. Sit down, Cyrus."

"I'll stand."

Coram sighed and lowered himself onto the stool instead, which somehow made the room feel smaller. "Your father came to us before you were born. Not to the temple — to me, privately. He had dealings outside the valley, bad ones, the kind that follow a man home. The court held his paper. He begged me to intercede. I couldn't buy the debt — the temple doesn't traffic in souls, whatever you've just discovered down here — but I could do one thing. I could promise to raise the boy myself if anything happened to Tobias. Keep him inside the walls. Keep him ignorant, because ignorance was the term of the loan, and as long as the terms were met, the court wouldn't call it in." He rubbed his eyes. "It seemed merciful at the time. Most cruelties do."

"The terms are being met?"

"They were. Yearly, I paid the renewal fee out of the temple's own funds. A formality — a seal, a signature, a small sum sent to the mountain's agents at the spring fair. Thirty-one years of buying you another year." The abbot's mouth twisted. "This spring, the agents didn't come. The fair came and went and no one from Vael appeared, and no word has come since, and the road reports say the mountain's outer courts have gone quiet. I don't know if the debt is dead or if the court is dead or if something worse than either is happening. So I marked it *held*, and I told Ellery to watch you, and I prayed — for the first time in twenty years — that the walls would hold."

"And the courier?"

Something flickered across the old man's face. Grief, maybe, or guilt wearing grief's clothing. "She crossed the border dying. Whatever she carried, she carried it to you specifically. That tells me someone outside knows your name, Cyrus, and knows it well enough to spend a life delivering it. I don't know who. I don't know why. I know that if the court learns what she gave you, the temple cannot protect you — the temple cannot protect anyone from what's coming, and I am too old to pretend otherwise." He stood, joints cracking, and met Cyrus's eyes. "Burn the map. Stay inside the walls. Live out your life as a scribe, and when the fire dies — if it dies — perhaps the debt dies with it, and no one ever comes to collect."

"And if it doesn't die with it?"

"Then you'll wish you'd burned it."

Cyrus looked down at the green folio, at his father's careful, courteous theft of his entire existence, at the phrase *raised in comfort and ignorance* that had described the last twenty-nine years so precisely it might have been prophecy. He thought about obedience. He had been very good at obedience. It had been mistaken, all his life, for contentment.

"No," he said.

"Cyrus—"

"My name is written beneath the mountain. In your own archive. In my father's hand." He folded the folio shut and slid it back into its place with a copyist's neatness, because even now, especially now, order was the only prayer he knew. "You kept me the way you keep everything else in this building. Cold, and quiet, and filed. Well. I'm taking myself out of the collection."

He walked past the abbot toward the stairs, the map warm against his chest, his palms still sticky with blood from the hinge. Behind him, Coram said nothing — and that silence, Cyrus thought, climbing into the dark, was the loudest confession the temple had ever produced.

At the top of the stairs, a lamp was burning in the scriptorium, and Master Ellery sat at his desk with his spectacles pushed up and his counting-look fixed on the door.

"You're supposed to be ill," Ellery said.

"I'm cured," Cyrus said, and kept walking.
