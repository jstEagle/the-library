## 6. The Broker of Ashford Market

Ashford Market smelled of tallow, wet wool, and something sweeter underneath — a smell Cyrus couldn't name until he was close enough to see the source: braziers burning resin that made the air shimmer faintly gold. Memory-smoke. People paid to breathe it in and remember things they'd sold.

He had never seen so many strangers in one place. In the valley, everyone's face was a face he'd known his whole life; here, the crowd moved like water with a hundred currents, and every current had its own smell, its own accent, its own way of walking. Wren had melted into it the moment they passed the toll arch, promising — *promising*, she'd said, with her hand on her heart, which he already suspected meant nothing — to meet him at the fountain when the bells rang four.

That gave him three hours.

The map sat against his chest, wrapped in oilcloth beneath his shirt, warm as a sleeping animal. It had been quiet since the forest road began, but quiet wasn't the same as silent. He could feel it *thinking*. And he could feel other people noticing him thinking about it.

"First time at market?" A woman selling candied ginger watched him flinch away from a cart. She laughed without malice. "Walk like you own the ground. Predators read hesitation."

He tried. It half worked.

Wren had given him one piece of advice before vanishing into the crowd: *"If you want the map read, don't go to the scribes' stalls. They'll copy it and sell the copy before you've paid. Find the broker under the clocktower. The one who doesn't advertise."*

*The one who doesn't advertise.* Which, in Ashford Market, apparently meant the stall with no sign at all, tucked between a dyer's awning and a coffin-maker's shed, where an old man sat behind a table bearing nothing but a brass scale, a stoppered jar of ink-black smoke, and a single chair facing him across the wood.

No goods. No prices. No customers.

Cyrus almost walked past. Then the old man looked up, and his eyes were the pale gray of paper left too long in sun, and he said, in a voice like a page turning:

"You're carrying something written in blood. Sit down."

Cyrus did not sit. "How did you—"

"I'm a broker, boy. I deal in what people carry. You carry guilt, grief, and a document that wants to be read so badly it's practically shouting." He gestured at the chair again. "Sit. I don't bite. Biting leaves marks, and marks can be stolen."

Every instinct Cyrus had learned in the temple said walk away. Every instinct he'd developed since crossing the border stone said this man knew things, and knowledge was the only currency Cyrus currently possessed any hope of spending.

He sat.

"My name is Corvus." The broker folded his hands on the table. "Before we discuss your map, let me explain how this works, because you have the look of someone raised on ledgers, which means you think everything has a fair price. It doesn't. Everything has a *price*. Fairness is a rumor started by buyers."

"I understand debt," Cyrus said quietly. "Better than most."

"Mm. Then you understand that some debts are paid in coin, some in labor, and some in—" he tapped his own temple "—the only currency that appreciates. Memories. A memory of your mother's voice is worth nothing today and priceless in forty years, when she's dead and you'd trade a kingdom to hear it again. That's why brokers exist. We're not thieves. Thieves take from the unwilling. We simply stand where the desperate are walking and hold out our hands."

"What would reading the map cost?"

Corvus smiled, and there was something almost kind in it, which made it worse. "Let me see it first."

Cyrus hesitated. Then he reached beneath his shirt and unwrapped the oilcloth, laying the blood-inked map on the table between them.

Corvus didn't touch it. He leaned over it the way a physician leans over a patient, nostrils flaring, and for a long moment he said nothing at all. When he finally spoke, his voice had changed — lower, more careful, the way Master Ellery's voice changed when he found an error in a ledger that was *not* a simple error.

"This isn't courier-work. This is court-work. Blood-ink from Mount Vael itself." His gray eyes lifted. "Where did you get this?"

"A dying woman gave it to me."

"Dying." Corvus snorted softly. "Dying women don't cross three hundred miles of pilgrim road. Dying women get buried by the roadside. Someone *sent* her, and someone wanted this delivered to you specifically, which means either you're far more important than you look or far more trouble than you look. Possibly both." He straightened. "I can read it. The question is whether you can afford for me to."

"Name the price."

"One hour."

Cyrus frowned. "One hour of what?"

"Of your childhood. Any hour between your seventh year and your twelfth. I extract it whole — you won't feel it go, you'll just find a gap where it used to be, smooth as a pulled tooth. I keep it, I breathe it, I live inside it for as long as it pleases me, and you go on your way knowing exactly what your map says." He spread his hands. "Or you keep your hour and your ignorance, and good luck to you."

Cyrus stared at the map. The blood-lines seemed darker than they had a moment ago, as if the ink were listening.

"Why an hour of childhood?" he asked. "Why not coin? Why not labor?"

"Because childhood is the only time a person is ever completely honest," Corvus said. "Adults edit themselves even in their own heads. Children just... record." He leaned forward. "And because I've been in this trade sixty years, boy, and I can tell you're not asking what it costs. You're asking what it *feels* like. So I'll tell you. It feels like nothing. That's the horror of it. One day you'll reach for your father's face and find a smear where it used to be, and you won't know why, and you'll pay another broker to tell you, and he'll say *you sold it*, and you won't believe him."

Something cold moved through Cyrus's chest. But underneath the cold, quieter, was a thought he couldn't shake:

*I never see my father's face anyway.*

It had been eleven years since Aldous Underhill walked out of the valley to renegotiate a contract and never came back. Eleven years, and Cyrus's memory of him had worn down to a shape — tall, stooped at the shoulder, hands stained with ink — but the face itself had gone soft-focus, a smudge above a collar. He hadn't lost it to a broker. He'd lost it to time, to distance, to the slow erosion of a boy growing up without the man.

So what was one more loss? What was one hour, out of seven years of hours?

But then another thought, colder still: *What if the hour he takes is the last hour I have left of him?*

"I need to think," Cyrus said.

"Take your time. Time is the one thing I never charge for." Corvus produced a clay pipe from somewhere and lit it with a coal from the brazier, filling his corner of the stall with sweet smoke. "Though I'll note, for the record, that you came here knowing the price would be steep. Desperation dresses itself up as deliberation. I've seen it ten thousand times."

Cyrus stood and walked away from the stall, into the crowd, and let the market swallow him.

* * *

He bought a meat pie he didn't taste and ate it standing at the fountain, watching the water jump and fall, jump and fall. Around him the market roared — haggling, laughter, a fiddler murdering a reel, two children chasing a dog through the fish-stalls — and Cyrus felt the strange double vision that had followed him since the border stone. This world was louder, brighter, sharper-edged than the valley, and yet he moved through it like a ghost, untouched, unattached, belonging nowhere.

In the valley, he had belonged. That was the trap of it. Belonging had been the bars.

A shadow fell across the fountain. Wren dropped onto the rim beside him, smelling of horse and copper, a new scratch on her knuckles.

"You look terrible," she said fondly.

"I found the broker."

Her eyebrows went up. "Corvus? You found Corvus? He doesn't see anyone without an introduction. What'd he quote you?"

"An hour of my childhood."

Wren winced visibly. "Oof. Yeah, that's his rate for anything court-related. Blood-ink scares them all, and scared men charge double." She studied his face. "You're actually thinking about paying it."

"The map is useless if I can't read it. And the map is the only thing standing between me and—" He stopped. Between me and the mountain. Between me and whatever my father signed away. Between me and the truth.

"Between you and the mountain," Wren finished for him. She was quiet a moment, turning an apple over in her fingers, and when she spoke again her voice had lost its usual flippancy. "I sold my first memory at thirteen. My brother's funeral. I don't remember him dying anymore. I don't remember him *living*, really — just that there was a boy named Tam who shared my room, and that I cried, once, for reasons I can't recall." She tossed the apple, caught it. "People think the memories are the price. They're wrong. The price is that afterward, you can't remember *why anything mattered*. Gaps breed gaps. You start patching holes with guesses, and the guesses become the person you think you are." She looked at him sideways. "So. Is the map worth becoming a slightly different person?"

Cyrus opened his mouth to answer and found he didn't know.

The bells rang four. Wren stood, stretched, and said, "Fountain, tomorrow, dawn, if you're still you," and vanished into the crowd again, leaving Cyrus alone with the water and the question.

* * *

He went back at dusk, when the market was guttering down to embers and the braziers threw long shadows between the stalls. Corvus was still there, packing nothing into nothing, as if the absence of goods required careful storage.

"Yes?" the broker said, without looking up.

"One hour," Cyrus said. "My ninth year. Midwinter. There was a night my father let me stay up past midnight to watch him work. He was copying something — I don't remember what — and he talked while he wrote. About the letters. About how each one was a little door." Cyrus swallowed. "If you have to take something, take that. At least I'll know what I lost."

Corvus regarded him for a long moment, and something flickered behind the sun-bleached eyes — surprise, maybe, or respect. "Most people grab randomly. 'Take anything, just not the good parts.' As if I can't tell which parts are load-bearing." He set down his pipe. "Very well. The midwinter night. Sit still, breathe normally, and do not fight the extraction. Fighting turns a clean pull into a tear, and tears leave scars in places you'll spend years trying to itch."

Cyrus sat. Corvus unstopped the jar of black smoke, dipped two fingers of his right hand into it — the smoke clung to his skin like living tar — and pressed those fingers gently against Cyrus's temples.

"Closure," Corvus murmured, "is a story adults tell children. Nothing closes. It only gets filed."

The world went soft at the edges.

Cyrus felt the memory lift out of him like a splinter drawn from flesh — painless, precisely as promised, and precisely as horrifying as promised. For one suspended instant he was nine years old again, wholly and completely: the cold of the flagstones through his nightshirt, the guttering candle, the scratch-scratch-scratch of his father's pen, the rumble of his voice saying *every letter is a little door, Cy, and behind every door is a room nobody's swept in centuries*—

Then it was gone, and the gap where it had been was smooth as polished stone.

Corvus withdrew his hand, exhaled a thin ribbon of smoke, and looked briefly, genuinely tired. "Paid in full," he said. "Now. Your map."

He spread it flat on the table and bent close, and this time he did touch it — one fingertip, tracing the blood-lines the way a blind man reads. His lips moved silently. The brazier-light made the ink glisten, and Cyrus could have sworn the lines shifted under the broker's finger, rearranging themselves, though that was impossible. Maps didn't move.

Except this one had spoken to him in a dead woman's voice.

"It's a route," Corvus said at last. "Not to the mountain — around it, first. There's a waystation here—" his finger stopped at a mark in the western hills "—and then a descent shaft here, through the old slag-tunnels. The court sealed the front gates two hundred years ago. Debtors go in through the slag. Everyone does." His finger moved to a cluster of dense notation near the map's heart. "This part isn't geography. It's a schedule. Dates. The next one is..." He paused, and Cyrus saw his jaw tighten. "Eleven days from now. Something happens under that mountain in eleven days, and whoever drew this map wanted you there for it."

"Can you read the notation itself?"

"Some. It's collector's shorthand — the script Lady Asche's office uses for internal records." Corvus glanced up sharply. "You don't know that name. Good. Pray you never hear it spoken aloud within her hearing." He tapped the notation. "There's a word repeated throughout. *Vigil.* And a number attached to it that keeps changing, counting down. Whatever the vigil is, it's running out."

The dragon-fire. Cyrus didn't know how he knew — the knowledge arrived whole, unbidden, the way Sable's voice had arrived — but he knew. *The last dragon-fire gutters out, and the valley's protection dies with it.* The dying courier's words, or his own dread, or the map whispering through his blood.

"Thank you," he said, and reached for the map.

Corvus held it down with one finger. "One more thing. Free of charge, because I liked your father's voice." Cyrus went rigid. "Oh yes," the broker said softly. "Sixty years in this trade, boy. I know whose memory I'm breathing. Aldous Underhill copied contracts for the Temple of Quiet Hours, and he talked to his son while he worked because he was lonely, and he was lonely because he'd done something terrible and couldn't say it out loud. Men like that talk to children because children don't ask the right questions." He released the map. "Your father didn't abandon you. He went back to the mountain to try to buy you out. That much, the memory told me before I even tasted it — it was soaked through with guilt. Take the free advice: whatever you find under Vael, remember that he tried."

Cyrus took the map with hands that weren't quite steady. "You said the memory was soaked with guilt. Guilt about *what*?"

"That," said Corvus, relighting his pipe, "would be a second reading. And you've got nothing left I want." He smiled, thin and papery. "Go on. Eleven days. The road west is bad after rain."

Cyrus walked out into the darkening market, and the strangest thing happened: he reached for the midwinter night, the little doors, the scratch of the pen — and found the gap, smooth and clean, exactly where Corvus had promised. But around the gap, pressed against its edges like flowers kept in a book, other fragments survived. His father's cough. The smell of tallow candles. The weight of a heavy hand resting briefly on a small head.

And his father's face — not the memory of it, but the *fact* of it, suddenly sharp after eleven blurred years. Long jaw. Ink stain on the left cheekbone, always, because he wiped his pen the wrong way. Eyes that looked at Cyrus the way men look at fires they're afraid will go out.

Cyrus stood in the emptying square and wept, quietly and thoroughly, for a man he remembered better now than he had in a decade — because a stranger had charged him an hour of his childhood to make it possible, and because grief, he was learning, was the one debt that appreciated fastest of all.

When the tears stopped, the map was warm against his chest, and beneath his ribs, faint but unmistakable, a voice that was not quite his own whispered:

*West, Cyrus. Hurry. He's waiting for you — the part of him that's still mine.*

Sable. Speaking through the blood-ink, speaking through *him*.

Eleven days. He turned toward the western gate and started walking.
