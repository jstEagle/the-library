## 8. Wren's Bargain

Wren had sold the same memory three times before she learned to stop feeling bad about it.

The trick was to sell the copy, not the original. A memory-broker in Duncastle had taught her that for free — a rare kindness — explaining how you could hold a moment in your mind like a coin and press it into wax without losing the coin itself. The buyer got the shape of the thing: the warmth of her mother's kitchen, the smell of bread gone wrong, the sound of her brother laughing through a wall. They never knew it was a copy. Copies were all anyone bought or sold out here anyway.

The original she kept. That was the whole point of Wren. Everything else about her was negotiable.

So when the scout in the grey cloak sat down across from her at the Ashford market fire and said, *I hear you deal in childhoods*, she smiled the way she'd been taught by hunger, which is to say honestly enough to pass.

"Childhoods, first kisses, last words," she said. "Grief runs high this season. Joy is cheap. What are you carrying?"

"Nothing I want to sell." He turned his cup in his hands. Grey cloak, grey eyes, a dragon-scale pin at his collar worn dull on purpose — she noticed that, the deliberate dullness, the way men who wanted to be found wore things bright and men who didn't wore them dim. "I'm buying."

"Everyone says that until they hear the price."

"I've heard the price." He leaned in. Firelight did something unkind to his face, hollowing it. "I'm looking for a specific memory. Not one of yours. One that passed through your hands recently. A valley-man. Scribe's hands, ink under the nails, walks like he's apologizing to the ground. He came through the broker stalls yesterday. Paid with an hour of his childhood — everyone talked about it. An *hour*. Nobody pays in hours anymore."

Wren kept her face still. Inside, something small and cold began to turn over.

"What's he to you?" she asked.

"He's carrying something that belongs to the mountain. A map. Blood-inked. If he reaches Vael with it, a great many people suffer. If we take it from him gently, only he suffers." The scout smiled. It was a professional smile, sanded smooth. "You've traveled with him. Two days on the pilgrim road, sharing fires. Don't insult me by denying it; I watched you pitch your tent close enough to share warmth and far enough to watch your purse."

He had watched well. She hated him a little for it.

"And if I tell you where he sleeps?"

"Then you eat this winter." He set a purse on the table between them. It made no sound when it landed, which meant it was heavy. "And there's more where that came from. The Vigil pays its friends."

*The Vigil.* Dragon-cult. She'd heard the name whispered at three markets now, always the same way people say the name of a storm coming over hills — not with fear exactly, but with the particular respect owed to something that will arrive whether you pray or not.

She looked at the purse. She looked at the fire. She thought about Cyrus Underhill, who had spent two days flinching every time she reached toward his pack, and who had told her once, badly, around a mouthful of trail-bread, that he'd never owned anything in his life except a penknife and a debt.

She thought about how easy it would be.

That was the thing about Wren that she'd never managed to sand off, the flaw in all her careful copies: some part of her still checked whether the door she was opening led somewhere she could live afterward.

"He sleeps alone," she said slowly, buying time, watching the scout's eyes sharpen. "Behind the tannery row. But listen — if you touch him, the map won't be on him. He's a temple scribe. Temple men hide things in plain sight. You'll tear his pack apart and find laundry."

"Where, then?"

"That's the second question. Second questions cost double."

The scout laughed — a real laugh, brief and surprised out of him — and pushed the purse closer. "Double, then. Name it."

And that was when the hand closed on Wren's shoulder.

* * *

Later — much later, in the years after, when she told the story and always told it slightly wrong on purpose — Wren would say she never saw Cyrus move. That wasn't true. She saw him perfectly. What she hadn't believed, until that moment, was that he was capable of it.

He came out of the crowd's edge like a man stepping off a ledge he'd been standing on for days. No anger in his face. Worse than anger: certainty. He put his hand on her shoulder and his other hand flat on the table beside the purse, and he said, in the flat clear voice of a man reading aloud from a ledger, "This conversation is concluded."

The scout went very still. "Scribe."

"You know what I am."

"I know what you carry."

"Then you know I'd rather burn it than let it reach the mountain." Cyrus's thumb tapped the table once, twice. Wren felt his heartbeat through his hand on her shoulder — fast, rabbit-fast, but the voice stayed level, and she understood suddenly that the voice was the real thing and everything else was scaffolding. "Here's what happens next. You stand up. You walk back to whatever grey hole the Vigil keeps you in. And you report that the courier is paranoid, armed, and traveling with someone whose loyalty you couldn't buy — which will be true, because I'm about to make it true."

"She offered to sell you," the scout said. "You understand that. She was negotiating your location when you walked up."

"I heard the last of it." Something flickered across Cyrus's face then, quick as a fish under ice. "I've been listening for ten minutes. Long enough."

Wren's stomach dropped through the floorboards of the world. Ten minutes. He'd stood in the dark behind the broker stalls for ten minutes and listened to her price him out, and he had come forward anyway, and she could not for the life of her work out why, and that not-knowing frightened her more than the scout did.

"The Vigil doesn't forget faces," the scout said quietly.

"No," Cyrus agreed. "Neither do I."

It wasn't a threat, quite. It was worse. It was the tone of a man writing something down.

The scout weighed it — Wren watched him weigh it, the way a man weighs a bridge before trusting it — and then he rose, unhurried, collected his purse, and inclined his head with terrible courtesy. "Another time, scribe. The mountain is patient. It has to be. Its money's tied up in it." And he melted into the market crowd, grey into grey, gone.

Cyrus took his hand off her shoulder.

The absence of it was loud.

* * *

They walked. Neither of them suggested it; their feet simply agreed. Away from the firelight, away from the broker stalls with their jars of bottled afternoons, down the crooked lane behind the tanneries where the smell was thick enough to chew. Wren counted her own footsteps because counting was what she did instead of panicking.

At forty steps she broke. "I was going to warn him off."

"No, you weren't."

"I was going to take the purse and then—" She stopped. There was no end to that sentence that wasn't a lie. She tried another one. "I need the money, Cyrus. You don't know what it's like out here. Debts don't stay dead in the low country. They follow you. Mine have teeth. I sold memories to pay interest on interest until there wasn't much left of me worth collecting, and the Vigil pays better than anyone, and I thought—"

"I know what you thought." He stopped walking. In the dark between two tanning sheds, his face was mostly shadow and one thin stripe of lamplight. "I stood there for ten minutes, Wren. I heard the arithmetic. I'm a scribe. Arithmetic is my native language."

"So why?" It came out of her rawer than she meant it to. "Why step in? You should have run. You should have moved camp tonight. You should hate me."

He was quiet long enough that she heard the tannery vats breathing, that slow chemical sigh.

"When I paid the broker," he said finally, "for the hour of my childhood — my father teaching me letters, ink and ash on a slate — I thought I was buying a memory. That's what it feels like from inside. But that hour isn't mine anymore. It lives in a jar in Ashford Market, and anyone can own it, and I can't ever get it back unless I find whoever holds it." His voice did something complicated on the word *own*. "Do you understand? I know exactly what it is to be sold. I've been collateral since before I could walk. So when I saw you doing the selling instead of being sold—" He stopped. Started again, simpler. "I'm not saying it's the same. I'm saying I recognized the look on your face. The one where you're checking whether the door leads anywhere you can live afterward. I wear that look most days."

Wren discovered she was crying, which infuriated her. She hadn't cried since Duncastle, since the broker with the kind eyes had shown her the wax impression of her mother's kitchen and she'd realized the buyer would get the warmth and she'd keep the cold.

"Don't do that," she said. "Don't be decent at me. I had a plan for indecent."

"I know. I watched it."

She laughed despite herself, wet and ugly, and wiped her face with her sleeve like a child. "What do you want, Cyrus? Everyone wants something. Nobody steps in front of the Vigil for free."

And here is the thing Wren remembered for the rest of her life, the detail she never told wrong even when she told everything else wrong: he hesitated. Not the hesitation of a man deciding what to ask for. The hesitation of a man realizing he genuinely didn't know how to be owed.

"I want you to come with me," he said. "To the mountain. Because I'm going to die out here alone, and you're the only person I've met outside the valley who tells the truth badly instead of lying well, and I find I trust that more than I probably should."

"That's not a bargain. That's charity."

"It's a hiring. I'll pay you."

"With what? You gave your childhood to a jar."

"I have—" He patted his pack, and she saw him decide, in real time, to lie to her, and saw the lie die halfway up his throat, and admired him for both. "I have very little. But whatever I have at the end of this, you'll have a share. Sworn. I'll write it down if you want. I'm good at writing things down."

Wren looked at him for a long moment. The lamplight stripe crossed his eyes, and behind them she could see the ledger turning, the honest arithmetic of a man calculating what little he had and offering all of it.

"Sworn debts bind harder than written ones, out here," she said. "Everyone knows that. Writing can be burned. A sworn debt sits in you like a swallowed coal." She stuck out her hand. "So swear it plain. And then hear mine, because it goes both ways or not at all."

He took her hand. His grip was ink-stained and stronger than she expected.

"I, Cyrus Underhill, owe Wren of nowhere-in-particular a full share of whatever I win or reclaim at Mount Vael," he said, formal as scripture, scribe to the bone. "Sworn before witnesses, of which there are none, so sworn before the dark, which counts."

"*Which counts,*" she repeated solemnly, and then ruined it by grinning. "Right. My side. I, Wren, owe Cyrus Underhill a debt of freedom, purchased tonight against my will and my better judgment, and I'll tell you now that I intend to pay it in the most annoying possible currency, which is loyalty. You'll regret this. Everyone regrets my loyalty eventually. I hover."

"Noted," he said gravely. "The contract specifies hovering."

They shook on it, and the coal went down, and it sat warm under her ribs the whole rest of the road, and she never once managed to spit it out.

* * *

They made camp that night in the lee of a fallen waystone, farther from town than safety strictly required, because Cyrus said the scout would check the obvious places first and Wren said nothing because he was right. She built the fire small. He sat across from it with the map's case in his lap, not opening it, just resting his hands on it like a man resting his hands on a sleeping animal.

"Tell me about the Vigil," he said. "You've dealt with them before?"

"Once. Indirectly." She fed a twig to the flames. "There's a woman in Corvess — sells luck, real luck, the bottled kind — and she told me the cult started as debtors' kin. People whose families got pledged to the mountain generations back. They worship the dragon because the dragon is the vault. Every soul-contract under Vael burns on that creature's flame. Kill the fire, they say, and every debt comes due at once." She glanced up. "They think that's a threat. Some of us might call it a promise."

Cyrus had gone very still.

"Cyrus?"

"The contracts come due when the fire dies," he repeated softly. "All of them. At once." He looked at her, and in the firelight his eyes had the particular shine of a man seeing a door he'd been walking past for days. "Wren. How much time does the dragon have left?"

"The luck-seller said seasons. Maybe less. Why—"

But he was already pulling the map case open, already holding the blood-inked parchment up to the light, and the ink was moving, gathering, forming letters in a hand that was not any living person's hand, and the first word it wrote made Wren's skin crawl up her arms like ants:

*HURRY.*
