## 9. The Court Beneath the Mountain

They climbed for two days through country that had stopped pretending to be kind.

The pilgrim road gave out at a river the color of old pewter, and beyond it the land went up in long gray switchbacks — scree, then heather, then nothing but stone laced with snow that never quite melted. Wren walked ahead with her pack straps crossed over her chest like a harness, saying almost nothing, which was unlike her. The map said even less. It hung against Cyrus's ribs inside his coat, warm as a sleeping bird, and when it did speak it spoke only one word, over and over, as if it were saving its strength:

*Closer.*

On the morning of the third day they came over the shoulder of a ridge called, according to Wren, the Widow's Teeth, and Cyrus stopped walking so suddenly that she took three more steps before she noticed.

"Ah," she said, when she turned and followed his gaze. "There it is."

*There* was not a word large enough.

Mount Vael stood at the head of the valley like a fist closed around something precious. He had expected a mountain — the maps all promised a mountain — but no map had prepared him for the fact that the mountain was *hollow*, or that its hollowness was lit. The near face of Vael had been carved away in terraces, tier upon tier of galleries and buttresses and stairways cut into living rock, descending inward toward a core of light. Windows — thousands of them, tens of thousands — studded the stone like scales on a fish. Roads spiraled up the outside of the mountain from every direction, black with traffic even at this distance: carts, processions, columns of figures moving in slow lines toward gates that glowed faintly gold.

And at the center, deep in the mountain's throat, burned the light itself.

It was hard to look at directly. Not because it was bright — though it was — but because it seemed to look back. A red-gold radiance rose out of the mountain's heart and spilled up through the tiers of the city, gilding every window, every stairway, every face turned toward it, and Cyrus understood without being told what he was seeing. Every soul-contract ever signed under that light drew its power from it. Every pledge, every surety, every name written into a ledger somewhere below — all of it fed on that fire, and the fire fed on something else.

"It's dimmer than last year," Wren said quietly.

Cyrus blinked. "You've seen it before?"

"Everyone's seen it who travels the outer roads." She crouched, unpacking bread and the last of the smoked fish, and tore a piece for him without asking. "The traders talk about it the way farmers talk about weather. *Vael's burning low this season.* *Vael's gone orange.* My mother swore it used to be white when she was young. White as a star." She chewed, watching the distant glow. "Now look at it. Red at the edges. Like a coal an hour after the fire's dead."

He looked. She was right. The great light pulsed — slowly, arrhythmically, the way a heart beats when it is tired — and each pulse seemed slightly weaker than the last. Around the rim of the innermost chamber, where the light met stone, he could see darkness creeping inward like frost forming on a window.

Against his chest, the map stirred.

*That is why you were sent,* Sable's voice said, thin and clear, the way a bell sounds through a wall. *Not to pay your debt. To arrive before the paying stops mattering.*

"What happens when it goes out?" Cyrus asked aloud. Wren glanced at him; by now she was used to his habit of talking to his coat.

"You know what happens," Wren said. "Everyone knows. The contracts fail. The souls—" She stopped herself, which was also unlike her. She looked down at her hands. "The souls get recalled. Raw. All at once. Every person in the world who owes a piece of themselves to that court feels it come due."

"And how many people owe?"

Wren was quiet for a long moment. "More than there are stars over Ashford," she said finally. "Maybe more than there are people. Some folk carry two or three debts. Some carry parts of parts. You've seen the tinker camps — half my trade is brokering little slivers of memory between people who sold too much and need enough of themselves back to remember their own children's names." She wrapped the bread away with sharp, economical movements. "When that light dies, all of it comes home at once. Every fragment. Every lien. They say the last time a dragon-fire guttered out — four hundred years ago, in the eastern ranges — whole towns fell down screaming. People drowned in their own returned memories. Others just... emptied. Went vacant. The court rebuilt, of course. The court always rebuilds. That's the point of courts."

Cyrus stared at the mountain until his eyes watered.

He tried to count the windows and failed. He tried to imagine each one holding a person, and each person holding a debt, and each debt a splinter of someone's soul — a first kiss, a mother's voice, the smell of rain, the knowledge of how to forgive — and the arithmetic broke him somewhere around the third terrace. This was not a fortress. It was not even a city. It was a *ledger* made architecture, a machine built to hold the collateral of the world, and it was running out of fuel.

And somewhere inside it, filed and annotated and renewed faithfully every year until this one, was a page with his name on it.

*Your father's handwriting,* Sable murmured. *I remember it. I remember everything he wrote. He pressed too hard. The letters always bit through the paper.*

"I don't want to remember it," Cyrus said softly.

*Yes, you do.* A pause, and something almost gentle entered the voice. *That's the trouble with us.*

* * *

They made camp that night in the lee of a fallen pillar — a column of worked granite the size of a temple, lying on its side halfway up the ridge, carved with script so ancient the letters had worn to suggestions. Wren said it had rolled down from the mountain in some catastrophe no one remembered, which Cyrus found less comforting the longer he looked at it. Even the mountain shed pieces of itself here, at the edge of its gravity.

He couldn't sleep. He sat with his back against the pillar and watched the light in Vael's throat breathe — swell, fade, swell, fade — and found he could time his own heartbeat against it without meaning to. When the light swelled, his chest felt fuller. When it faded, something in him felt briefly, horribly *thin*, like a candle flame leaning in wind.

"Do you feel that?" he asked.

Wren lay with her arms behind her head, eyes open, watching the same light. "Everyone does, this close. It's the pull. Your soul knows where its paperwork lives." She said it lightly, but her jaw was tight. "Mine's in there too, if you're wondering. Two entries. One from when I was nine — my father sold a winter's worth of my memories to cover a toll. One from when I was nineteen, and stupid, and needed coin faster than honest work pays." She turned her head and looked at him. "So when we go down there tomorrow, understand what you're asking me to walk into. That place doesn't see people. It sees accounts. And I am a delinquent account, Underhill, twice over."

"I'm sorry," Cyrus said. "For asking."

"You didn't ask. That's worse, mostly." But she said it without heat, and after a moment she added, "Why do you think I came? Not for your pretty gratitude. If that fire dies while I still owe, I get everything back at once — every memory I sold, all of it, in one flood, with no broker to meter it out gentle. I've seen it happen. It kills most folk. So either we fix this properly, or I'd rather take my chances climbing back down and selling the rest of myself off slow." She looked back at the mountain. "Properly is harder. Properly means going *in*."

The map warmed against his ribs.

*Then let me tell you what proper looks like,* Sable said. *Listen carefully, both of you. There are three ways into Vael. The pilgrim gates on the south face — open to anyone with a debt to discharge, which means open to you, Wren, and fatal to Cyrus, whose entry will be flagged the moment his name touches a registry. The courier posterns on the north — sealed since I died, and I use that word loosely. And the clerks' road.*

"The clerks' road," Cyrus repeated.

*Every great ledger needs hands. The court employs thousands of scribes, clerks, tally-keepers, binders. They come up the western stair in shifts, through a gate called the Inkwell, and the gate does not check souls. It checks credentials. A seal, a hand, a working knowledge of contract-form.* The voice grew quieter, more careful. *You were made for ledgers, Cyrus. Whatever else they took from you at birth, they left the part that writes.*

He thought of the Temple of Quiet Hours. Of Master Ellery counting his breaths across the copying desk. Of ten years spent learning to make letters so uniform they might have been printed, to keep columns true, to write numbers that could not be disputed. He had believed it was a cage. He had resented every hour of it.

It had been training. Someone — something — had known, all along, exactly what work he would be needed for.

"That's obscene," he said.

*Yes,* Sable agreed. *Most true things are.*

Wren had propped herself up on one elbow, listening to his half of the conversation with the expression of a woman doing sums. "A clerk," she said. "You'd walk in the front door dressed as paper."

"Dressed as paper," Cyrus confirmed.

"They'll test you. The real clerks are tested — wax seals, countersigns, the whole liturgy. You can't fake—"

"I don't have to fake it." He heard his own voice come out flat and steady and was distantly surprised by it. "I've been keeping false ledgers since I was twelve. The Temple taught me double-entry bookkeeping for souls, Wren. They just didn't tell me what the souls were for. I know the forms. I know the seals — I've copied the Temple's own contract-marks hundreds of times, and the Temple learned its marks *from* Vael, everyone knows that, the Quiet Hours was founded as an outpost of the mountain court before it went quiet." He paused. "I know how they think. Debt-clerks think in columns. If I walk in thinking in columns, I'm invisible."

Wren studied him for a long moment. Then she laughed — short, disbelieving, almost admiring. "Look at you. Three weeks ago you flinched when a cart honked its horn."

"Three weeks ago I didn't know what I was worth," Cyrus said. "Turns out the answer is: a great deal, to very bad people."

* * *

Sleep came for him eventually, in shallow installments, and each time he surfaced the mountain was still there, breathing its failing light against the underside of his eyelids. In the small hours he dreamed — not of the mountain but of a desk, a high bright desk, and his own hands moving across paper with a fluency he had never possessed in waking life, writing names in a hand that pressed too hard, letters biting through the page. In the dream he knew every name. In the dream the names were all his.

He woke before dawn with the taste of ink in his mouth and Wren already standing, packing, silhouetted against a sky going gray at the edges.

Below them, the road to the western stair was already alive. Even at this distance he could pick out the shift-changes: lines of small dark figures ascending in single file, bearing lamp-cases and document-satchels, passing through a gate in the mountain's flank that glowed a dull, bureaucratic amber. The Inkwell. Ordinary people, going to ordinary work, inside the throat of the thing that owned the world.

*Today,* the map said. Just that. *Today.*

Cyrus stood, brushed the frost from his coat, and took one last long look at Mount Vael — at the tiers and the windows and the dying gold heart of it, at the scale of the machine that had held a claim on his soul since before he could speak. He had come expecting a monster. What he saw instead was worse: order. Bookkeeping. Ten thousand clerks keeping ten thousand columns, none of them evil, all of them complicit, the whole vast apparatus humming along on the simple assumption that debts must be paid and someone must collect.

Awe curdled in his chest and came out the other side as dread, and the dread hardened, as he watched the light flicker and dim, into something steadier.

Resolve.

"All right," he said to Wren, and to the map, and to whatever remained of himself waiting inside the mountain. "Teach me to be a clerk."

*No need,* said Sable, and for the first time since the forest, the voice sounded almost like laughter. *You've been one all your life. We only have to dress the part — and I know exactly where the courier caches left a seal worth stealing.*

Wren shouldered her pack and started down the scree toward the road, and Cyrus followed, down toward the light that was going out, one step at a time, into the ledger that knew his name.
