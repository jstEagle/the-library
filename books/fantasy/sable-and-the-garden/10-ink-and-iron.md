## 10. Ink and Iron

The outer court of Mount Vael smelled like a countinghouse that had learned to sweat.

Cyrus had expected grandeur — the ridge had promised it, all those towers carved from living stone, windows glowing with borrowed firelight — but what he found at the gate was queues. Hundreds of them, winding through the great iron doors and down the switchback road, people holding papers, holding children, holding each other up. Debtors come to renew. Debtors come to plead. Debtors come to surrender whatever was left.

"Name?" said the gate-clerk, a woman with ink-stained cuffs and eyes like coins rubbed thin.

"Cyrus Underhill," he said, and then, because Wren had drilled him on this for two days until her voice went hoarse: "Journeyman clerk, indentured to the Temple of Quiet Hours, here under writ of transfer to assist in the annual reconciliation."

He slid the forged letter across the counter. It was good work — his own work, which was the strange part. He hadn't needed to fake much. The temple's seal he'd copied from memory, having pressed it into wax ten thousand times. The abbot's hand he'd imitated the way you imitate a song you've hated your whole life. The only true lie in it was the purpose, and lies of purpose were the easiest kind, because no one checks a purpose. They check a seal.

The clerk read it twice. Her lips moved.

"You're from the valley," she said.

"Yes."

"They still send clerks out of there? Thought the Quiet Hours kept everyone." She looked up, and for a moment something moved behind the coin-flat eyes — envy, maybe, or its cousin, grief. "Nice work if you can get it."

"It has its difficulties."

"Don't they all." She stamped the letter without reading it again. "Reconciliation hall's down the third gallery, past the tithe cages. You'll want to report to Master Quillon. He'll give you a desk and a quota and hate you by sundown." She almost smiled. "Welcome to Vael."

He walked through the iron doors into the mountain's throat, and the sound hit him first: not roar, not music, but arithmetic. Thousands of quills. Thousands of ledgers turning. Somewhere beneath it all, so low he felt it in his teeth rather than heard it, a slow pulse — the dragon-fire, breathing out its long diminishing breath.

*It's dimmer than last night,* he thought, and didn't let himself think anything after that.

* * *

Master Quillon was a small man built entirely of disapproval, perched on a tall stool behind a desk the size of an altar. His office overlooked the reconciliation hall from a balcony of black iron, and below him three hundred clerks scratched away in perfect silence, because silence here wasn't peace. Silence here was policy.

"A valley scribe." Quillon pronounced it like a diagnosis. "They still teach the old notation out there?"

"Ledger-hand, covenant-hand, and the pre-Reform cipher."

"Hm." Something flickered across his face that might have been respect, quickly strangled. "We're short-handed. The fire dims, the contracts wobble, and every wobbling contract generates disputes, and every dispute generates paper." He gestured at the hall below as if presenting a wound. "You'll take registry overflow. Cross-checking surety entries against the renewal rolls. Can you do that without fainting?"

"I copy ledgers for a living, Master Quillon."

"Then you know how to keep your face still." He studied Cyrus a moment longer. "Good. That's rarer than penmanship." He waved a hand toward a stair. "Registry's two levels down. Tell the under-warden I sent you. And clerk—" Cyrus stopped. "Whatever you find down there, you file it. You don't *feel* it. Feelings are how collectors find soft clerks, and soft clerks end up in the ledger instead of beside it."

"Yes, Master Quillon."

"And Underhill." Quillon was already turning back to his own papers. "Welcome to Vael."

The same words. Cyrus wondered, descending, whether anyone in this mountain had ever said anything else.

* * *

The Registry of Souls did not look like a place where souls were kept. It looked like a library that had been punished.

Rows of shelves rose into darkness, each one crammed with folios bound in leather gone grey with handling. No titles. No ornament. Just numbers burned into spines, and the smell — old ink, old fear, the particular staleness of paper that has been wept on and dried and wept on again. Lamplight came from glass globes fed by thin copper pipes, and the flame inside each globe was amber, and it flickered in rhythm. The same rhythm as the pulse he'd felt in his teeth.

Every light in the room was drinking from the dying fire.

"Underhill. Valley transfer." He showed the stamped letter to the under-warden, a broad woman with keys at her belt and flour on her sleeves, absurdly domestic in the middle of all this grey. She glanced at it, grunted, and pointed him to a carrel near the back. "Overflow's in the third press. Renewal rolls are chained; ask if you need them unchained. Don't take folios off-site. Don't copy entries for personal use." She paused. "Don't look up your own name."

Cyrus kept his face still, the way Quillon had told him.

"Everyone does," the under-warden said, not unkindly. "That's why I say it. Rule keeps the ones who follow it honest and tells me who the others are." She walked away, keys chiming.

He sat down at the carrel and worked.

That was the thing nobody warned you about infiltration: most of it was just the job. For four hours he cross-checked surety entries against renewal rolls, and the work was genuinely absorbing, the way terrible things often are when they're well-organized. Each folio held a life reduced to columns: name, origin, nature of surety, date pledged, date renewed, rate of interest measured not in coin but in *years of remembering*, or *the color blue*, or *one's mother's laugh*. The court took payment in whatever the debtor could least afford to lose. That was the genius of it. Coin comes back. A mother's laugh doesn't.

He copied numbers. He initialed discrepancies. He kept his face still.

And all the while, in the pocket of his coat, folded small enough to sit over his heart, the map lay quiet. Sable had been quiet since the ridge — since Mount Vael had risen out of the dark like a held breath. He missed her voice and hated himself for missing it, the way you miss a splinter once it's out.

Near the end of his shift, the under-warden passed his carrel and set down a folio without comment. Overflow, third press. He opened it expecting more columns.

The label on the spine read **U**.

His hands went cold around the quill.

*She knows,* he thought. *This is the test.* But the under-warden was forty feet away, reshelving, her back turned, and there was no way she'd guessed — unless everyone guessed, unless the whole mountain ran on the assumption that sooner or later every clerk would come looking for himself. Maybe that was the real function of the rule. Not to prevent the search. To time it.

He should put it back. He should reshelve it untouched and go back to his quota and be a clerk among clerks.

He opened it.

* * *

The U folio was thinner than the others. Fewer names. The valley sent few debtors, because the valley forgot, and you cannot collect from someone who no longer remembers owing.

He found his father's entry on the fourth page.

**UNDERHILL, ALDERN. Surety: one soul, issue of the pledge, male, born in the valley year 712. Held in trust against principal and accrued interest. Renewal due annually at the autumn reconciliation.**

There it was. Not a metaphor. Not a story. Columns and dates and a clerk's neat abbreviation — *soul, 1, held* — as if he were a sack of grain in a tithe cage. Cyrus stared at the words *issue of the pledge* and understood, with a lurch, that the court had never thought of him as a person at risk of forfeiture. They had thought of him as inventory. Born already owned. The valley hadn't been his refuge. It had been his *storage*.

His finger traced the renewal column. Year after year, a mark in the same careful hand — his father's hand, the hand from the letters, the hand from the one memory the broker hadn't taken, a man hunched over a kitchen table writing by candlelight with his tongue between his teeth. Aldern Underhill had climbed out of the valley every autumn of Cyrus's childhood. Had crossed the border stone that magic couldn't cross — *how had he paid for that passage? what had it cost him?* — and walked up this same switchback road, and stood at some counter very like the one at the gate, and renewed the pledge. Kept the debt alive. Kept his son's soul technically his own to lose rather than forfeit.

Year after year after year.

Then Cyrus reached the last line, and stopped breathing.

The final renewal entry was blank. This year's mark was absent — he'd known that, had pieced it together from the archives, had fled the temple over that absence. But below the blank space, in the margin, in that same careful hand, smaller than the rest, written the way men write things they don't intend to be read:

*forgive me.*

Three words. Ink faded to brown. The paper around them slightly warped, as though water had fallen on it once, long ago, and dried.

Cyrus put both hands flat on the carrel to stop them shaking.

His father had known. Standing at some desk in this grey cathedral of arithmetic, Aldern Underhill had known he would not make the next climb — sick, or broke, or simply done — and he had written those two words in the margin of his son's soul, and then he had gone home to the valley and died, or worse, stayed, and never said a word of it aloud. Never explained. Never asked forgiveness in any way a boy could hear.

*forgive me.*

"You're bleeding," said a voice.

Cyrus looked down. His palm had clenched around the quill hard enough to snap the nib, and the broken shaft had driven into the meat of his thumb. Blood welled, bright and ordinary, and dripped — one drop, two — onto the open folio, onto the margin, onto the word *forgive*.

The blood sank in. The ink stirred.

Not spread. *Stirred* — the letters shivering like insects waking, the brown fading lines brightening, and for one heartbeat the handwriting lifted off the page and hung there, hovering above the parchment in a thin red script that was and was not his father's, and Cyrus felt the map in his pocket go hot as a coal, and heard — not with his ears, somewhere behind them — Sable's voice, sharp with alarm:

*Cyrus. Close it. CLOSE IT—*

Boots on stone. The under-warden, turning, keys beginning to chime.

He slammed the folio shut. The hovering script collapsed back into the page like breath sucked inward. He pressed his bleeding thumb against the cut to staunch it, shoved the volume into the gap on the shelf, and was reaching for his quills with a clerk's bland efficiency when the shadow fell over his carrel.

Not the under-warden.

A woman in grey silk the color of ash, flanked by two guards whose armor made no sound at all, which was somehow worse than clanking. She was older than she appeared and younger than she looked, and her eyes were the exact amber of the registry lamps — lit from inside by the same guttering fire.

"Well," said Lady Asche pleasantly, "a valley clerk. How industrious." Her gaze dropped to the shelf where the U folio sat, then to the drop of blood drying on the wood of the carrel, dark as a period at the end of a sentence. "We do so love industry here. It saves us the trouble of going out to find interesting people."

She smiled, and the smile did not reach the fire in her eyes, which had begun, very slowly, to dim.
