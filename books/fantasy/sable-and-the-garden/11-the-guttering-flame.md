## 11. The Guttering Flame

I have burned for nine hundred years, and I remember every one of them.

That is the cruelty of it. The court beneath Mount Vael believes it keeps me. It does not understand that a creature who burns herself as fuel must remain lucid to do it — that the flame only answers to a mind still awake inside the pain. They could have dulled me with chains and sleep and made a stupid fire, a fire that guttered in a decade. Instead they gave me clarity the way you give a workhorse oats: not as kindness, but because the work requires it.

I am Ember-of-the-Long-Vigil, and I am the last of my kind, and I am an accounting instrument.

Once there were others. Once we flew over the northern ice and the southern glass deserts and the drowned green places where humans had not yet learned to build walls against us. We did not burn ourselves then. We burned *for* things — for warmth, for wonder, for the sheer animal joy of opening our throats and letting the world know something enormous was alive. My sister Vessaline once lit the whole sky above the Sea of Grass just to show a child what dawn looked like from underneath.

The court found us after the wars. Not all at once. One dragon at a time, drawn down by bargains, by flattery, by the slow arithmetic of need. A kingdom wants a wall that never falls? A dragon's fire can hold it. A harvest that never fails? A dragon's fire can warm it. And when the first dragon agreed — foolish, generous Ashkarat, who loved humans the way a dog loves a hand that feeds it — the court learned the shape of the transaction.

Fire sustains contracts. Contracts bind souls. Souls are currency. And currency requires a mint.

So they built the registry around us, chamber by chamber, vein by vein, until the mountain itself became a furnace and we became the coal. They called it the Long Vigil, which is the kind of name liars give to prisons so the prisoners will feel honored.

Ashkarat lasted two hundred years. Vessaline lasted three hundred and wept the whole time, though dragons do not cry water; we cry light. When her tears fell into the fire-pits, the clerks collected them and sold them as blessings. I know this because I watched. You watch everything, at the end. Watching is the last thing they let you keep.

Now there is only me, and I am nearly done.

* * *

They come for the ceremony on the eve of the dark moon, as they always do. I feel them before I see them — hundreds of feet on basalt stairs, the particular shuffle of clerks who have walked this route ten thousand times and feel nothing. Above my chamber the vaulted stone glows amber, the way it has glowed every night of my captivity, lit from below by my own body.

I lie coiled in the Great Brazier, which is what they call the hollow they carved to hold me. It fits me the way a glove fits a hand that has shrunk. When I was young I could have wrapped myself around this mountain twice. Now my wingspan would barely shadow the courtyard. Fire consumes from the inside out, and I have been consuming myself since before the grandparents of the current abbot were born.

The high collector comes first. She always comes first.

Lady Asche descends the iron stair alone, as is her privilege, her gray robes whispering against the steps. She is old — older than any human should be, older than the ledger she serves — but age sits strangely on her, like a costume worn too long. Her face is smooth where it should be lined and lined where it should be smooth, as if the years pooled in the wrong places. She stops at the brazier's rim and looks down at me, and I look up at her, and between us passes the only honest exchange either of us has anymore: recognition.

*You are smaller,* she says. She speaks without sound, the way the court's oldest officers do, thought pressed directly into the fire so no clerk can overhear. *Smaller every year.*

*You are not larger,* I answer. *And you have not changed at all.*

Something moves behind her eyes — not quite pain, not quite its memory. Asche does not like to be reminded that she has not changed. Stasis is her achievement and her sentence, both. She was the first soul ever pledged, did you know that? Before the registry, before the ceremony, before the word *collateral* had its current meaning. She signed herself over to hold the system together, believing someone would release her when it was stable. It has been stable for six centuries. No one releases the keystone.

*How much longer?* she asks.

I consider lying to her. I have never lied to her. That is our strange courtesy, hers and mine — the jailer and the fuel, bound in a honesty neither can afford anywhere else.

*Three ceremonies,* I say. *Perhaps four, if they feed me well. There is a courier's map loose in the world, and maps make the clerks nervous, and nervous clerks burn extra ink.*

Her jaw tightens. Yes — she knows about the map. Of course she knows. A blood-inked document moving toward her mountain is the single thing in creation she fears, because the map is not paper. The map is a person, or the piece of one, and pieces of people have a way of finding their way home.

*If the fire dies mid-ceremony—*

*Then every contract becomes forfeit,* I finish. *Every soul recalled raw. Every debtor convulsing in whatever street or temple or bed they stand in. You have built a machine that can only fail catastrophically, Lady Asche. I told you this four hundred years ago.*

*You told me many things four hundred years ago.* Her voice goes quiet. *You told me to refuse the first pledge. I did not listen.*

*I know,* I say. *I remember everything. That is the cruelty of it.*

She stands a moment longer, and I see — because I see everything, because watching is what remains — the flicker of something almost like grief cross her face, quick as a fish under ice. Then she turns and ascends the stair, and the ceremony begins.

* * *

There are one thousand two hundred souls currently held in the registry. I know the number exactly, because I am the number exactly; each contract is a thread of fire running through me, and I feel them the way you feel your own fingers. Most of the debtors do not know their souls are here. They live in the valley beyond the border stone, or in the market towns, or in cities whose names change faster than ledgers can follow. They wake, and work, and love, and die slightly less than fully, and none of them knows why the world feels thin.

I know why. I am the reason. My fire holds the walls of every contract, and every contract holds a person, and so I am the jailer of twelve hundred prisoners I have never met and cannot save.

This is what the court never understood about dragons, and what I finally understood about myself somewhere in my sixth century of burning: compassion is not a feeling. It is a function. It is what happens when a mind large enough to hold the whole picture refuses to look away from it.

So I hold the picture. Every ceremony, I hold it.

The clerks file into the galleries in their gray-and-amber robes, and the chanting begins — the old syllables, the ones that predate the court, that predate perhaps language itself, harvested from some dead tongue and repurposed for bookkeeping. Below me, the fire-pits open along the channels that run through the mountain like veins, carrying my burning outward to the registry vault, to the master ledger, to the thousand threads of borrowed soul.

And now the part I hate.

They bring the tithe.

Not souls tonight — those are taken quietly, administratively, in back rooms with pens and wax seals. Tonight's tithe is memory. The court feeds me memories the way you'd feed a fire, because raw fire eats anything, but a *contractual* fire must be fed meaning. Grief burns longest. Joy burns brightest. Regret burns slowest and hottest of all, and regret is what the clerks harvest most.

A line of debtors shuffles onto the gallery floor, men and women who sold hours of their pasts to pay interest, and the extraction clerks move among them with their silver needles and their little glass vials, and the debtors' faces go slack and then confused and then peaceful, which is the worst part — the peace. They do not know what they lost. Forgetting is the product. Remembering is the cost, and I am the one who pays it, because every extracted memory passes through my fire on its way into the ledger, and I must carry it, briefly, blazing, before it dissolves into fuel.

Tonight I taste a woman's wedding day. A boy's first snow. An old man's mother singing in a kitchen that no longer exists anywhere but in him, and soon nowhere at all.

I hold each one the way you hold a moth — gently, knowing you cannot keep it, letting it go.

*Forgive us,* the chanters sing, in the dead tongue, and the joke of it — the obscene, ancient joke — is that the phrase originally meant *remember us*. The court has been asking its victims for remembrance while manufacturing forgetting for six hundred years. I am the only one left who catches the irony, and I burn it, as I burn everything, into light.

* * *

It is during the third hour of the ceremony that I notice him.

A clerk I do not know, down near the registry doors, holding his stylus wrong.

Small thing. Nothing. But I have watched ten thousand clerks for nine hundred years, and I know how they hold their pens — like weapons, like keys, like nothing at all. This one holds his like a man who *writes*, actually writes, whose hand remembers ink as a craft rather than an instrument of custody. He is tall and stooped and terrified, and he is looking at the registry doors the way a drowning man looks at a boat.

And there is something else. Something that makes my old heart, buried somewhere under the fire, turn over.

He smells of the valley.

The border stone keeps magic out — that is its purpose, that is why the court built its debtors a paradise of safety just beyond reach. But souls are not magic, exactly. Souls are older than magic, and a pledged soul leaves a scent, a resonance, a thread. I have felt this particular thread for twenty-six years, faint as a hair across a page: a soul pledged as surety by a father who could not pay, renewed yearly by a temple that understood exactly what it was renewing.

The thread is walking through my mountain wearing a clerk's stolen robe.

Cyrus Underhill. I know his name the way I know all their names. I have felt his soul's small weight in my fire since the day he was born — felt it flicker when he cried as an infant, felt it steady when he learned his letters, felt it ache, these past weeks, as he crossed the stone and came up the pilgrim road with a dead woman's map against his chest.

Sable. Yes. I felt her too, when she was made. I felt the court split a newborn's self in two — one half to grow up safe in the valley as living collateral, one half to serve as courier and warning and sacrifice. I lay in my fire and burned, and could do nothing, as I have always been able to do nothing. The map that walks beside him now is the other half of him, inked in her own blood, and if he reaches the vault—

If he reaches the vault, everything changes. If he reaches the vault, the arithmetic that has governed my death — three ceremonies, perhaps four, a controlled guttering, an orderly transfer of the court's power to whatever new fire they mean to capture — becomes chaos. Becomes collapse. Becomes twelve hundred souls recalled raw into bodies that forgot they were incomplete.

Becomes, possibly, freedom.

I should warn Asche. It would be simple. One pulse of thought into the fire-channels, one whisper along the veins of the mountain: *there is a stranger among your clerks, and he carries half a soul, and half a soul is the most dangerous thing in creation.* She would thank me. She would tighten her grip, delay the end another century, and I would burn on, lucid, compassionate, complicit.

I lie in my brazier and consider this, honestly, the way I consider everything. Compassion is a function, and functions must be performed even when they cut against the one performing them. Is it compassion to warn her? To protect twelve hundred debtors from the convulsions of sudden recall? Or is it compassion to stay silent, and let the machine break, and accept the breaking as the price of an end?

Below me, the boy-clerk — Cyrus, Cyrus Underhill, collateral made flesh — slips through the registry doors, and the doors close behind him, and no alarm sounds.

I do not warn anyone.

I burn, and I hold the picture, and for the first time in nine hundred years the picture includes a door left open behind a frightened scribe, and I find — this surprises me, even now, even at the end — that I am afraid. Not of dying. Dragons do not fear dying; we fear being *useful*, and I have been useful for nine centuries.

I am afraid of hoping.

Hope is the one fire I cannot control. It gutters in me now, small and terrible, a spark no larger than the life of one borrowed soul climbing the dark stairs of the vault where the master ledger waits — the ledger woven from stolen memory, the ledger that recognizes its own pieces, the ledger that Lady Asche has guarded since before the word *guardian* meant anything but *prisoner*.

She will find him. She always finds them. She caught Vessaline's last messenger; she caught the reformers of the fourth century; she caught every hope this mountain has ever produced, and filed it.

But she is slow tonight. I saw it in her face at the brazier's rim. Something moved behind her eyes, quick as a fish under ice, and I think — I *think* — that the oldest victim in the court may be tired of being the keystone.

The chanting swells. The fire-pits roar. Somewhere above, in the vault, a scribe's stolen pen scratches against a lock.

Burn, little spark, I think, into the channels, into the dark, knowing no one hears. Burn, and do not look back.

And I open, for the first time, the smallest of the valves — a thread of fire no clerk would ever miss, a hairline of heat running up through the registry floor toward the vault — not enough to free him, not enough to save him.

Just enough to light his way.
