## 14. Wren Walks Away

Wren had counted her debts the way other people counted their teeth — often, and with a certain grim satisfaction that they were all still there.

She kept them in a little book bound in green leather, soft as a tongue, that she'd traded three memories of her mother's kitchen to get. Not the whole kitchen. Just the smell of bread, the sound of the knife on the board, the way the light came through the east window in winter. Three small things, sold at three different markets, to three different brokers, so no one buyer could ever stitch them back together into something she'd miss.

The book held forty-one entries. Debts owed to her, debts she owed, and — this was the part nobody outside the road understood — debts that *were* her. The memory of her sister's laugh, pledged against a cart axle when she was fourteen. Her name as it sounded in her father's mouth, spent on a winter's food when the axle broke and the cart with it. Six years of her own face, because she'd never owned a mirror long enough to keep it, and mirrors cost.

People thought memory-brokers took your past. They didn't. They took your *collateral*, same as any lender anywhere. And like any sensible debtor, Wren had learned to live inside the shape of what was missing. You built your house around the hole. You hung your coat on the gap where the wall should be. You got very good, over eleven years, at not reaching for the thing that wasn't there.

So when Cyrus Underhill sat across their small fire on the last night below Mount Vael and said, in the mild voice of a man announcing he'd fixed a squeaky hinge, "I'm going to unmake all of it," Wren laughed.

Then she saw his face, and stopped laughing.

* * *

"All of it," she said. "Say that again. Slower."

"The ledger." He turned his hands toward the fire, palms open, the way he did when he was being careful with words — a scribe's habit, she'd learned, handling language like a page he didn't want to smudge. "The master ledger under the mountain. Every contract written in it. If Ember helps me reach the vault, and if Sable's half consents, we don't just free my soul. We burn the whole book. Everyone walks out owing nothing."

"Everyone walks out owning nothing," Wren said. "You mean the same thing, up there. You just haven't been poor enough to know it yet."

He flinched. She watched him flinch and hated how much she wanted him to.

"Cyrus." She set down the tin cup she wasn't drinking from. "Tell me what you think happens tomorrow morning. After the fire goes out. After the ledger burns. Tell me about the woman in Ashford who sold nine years of her daughter's growing-up to pay a physician, and now goes to the broker every month to *rent one hour back*. What does she wake up to?"

"She wakes up free."

"She wakes up with nothing to rent," Wren said. "And no daughter she can remember well enough to miss. You think the debt is the cage? The debt is the *shape*. It's the only reason she still climbs the stairs to that broker's stall. Take it away and you haven't opened a door. You've knocked down a wall she was leaning on."

"That's not—" He stopped. Cyrus always stopped before the cruel part of a sentence, which was the thing she liked best about him and the thing that made him impossible. "That's not an argument for keeping people enslaved."

"No," she agreed. "It's an argument for asking them first."

"I *am* asking. I'm telling you right now—"

"You're telling me the day before you do it!" Her voice cracked up an octave and she let it. Let him hear the eleven years in it. "You've had this plan since the vault, haven't you? Since the dragon. Since Asche. How many fires have we sat at since then, Cyrus? How many times did I ask what happens after?"

"You never asked what happens after."

"Because I thought *after* meant us walking down the mountain with your soul in your chest and my book still balanced! I did the arithmetic, scribe. I'm good at arithmetic — it's the one thing they can't take as collateral without taking everything." She pulled the green book from her coat and slapped it on the ground between them, spine up, like a dead bird. "Forty-one entries. Read me one. Go on. Read me the entry from the winter I was fifteen."

He looked at the book. He didn't touch it. "Wren."

"Winter I was fifteen. Cart's gone, father's coughing blood, and I trade the sound of my own name to a broker in Corrow Ford for six weeks of flour and tallow. That's the entry. Now tell me what I get back when you burn the ledger. Tell me what comes home to me."

"Your name," he said quietly. "The sound of it. In his voice."

"It doesn't come back!" She was standing now; she didn't remember standing. "That's not how it works and you know it isn't, you've seen the registry, you've read the contracts — the collateral gets *recalled*, raw, when a debt collapses. You said it yourself. Chapter seventeen of your own disaster. Debtors convulsing in the halls while their souls come home like swallowed knives. Eleven years, Cyrus. My collateral is scattered across four provinces in thirty different heads. Some of those brokers are *dead*. Where does my sister's laugh come home *to*?"

"To you. It should come home to—"

"*Should.*" She laughed, and it was an ugly sound, and she was glad. "There it is. The temple word. The valley word. Should." She crouched, snatched up the green book, and shoved it back inside her coat with hands that were not quite steady. "You know what the difference is between you and Lady Asche? Ask yourself honestly. She keeps a ledger woven out of stolen memories and calls it order. You want to burn a ledger woven out of stolen memories and call it mercy. Neither one of you asked a single soul what they wanted."

"That's not fair."

"No," she said. "It isn't. Welcome to the road."

* * *

She walked.

Not far — the pass below Vael narrowed to a shelf of black stone with a drop on the left that went down further than the eye cared to follow, and night was coming on, and she was furious but not stupid. She walked until the firelight was a smudge behind her and her breath came out in white flags, and then she sat on a boulder with her back to the mountain and did what she always did with a wound: priced it.

The anger was worth something. Anger was the one asset that appreciated. She'd learned that early — a tinker learns to sell the feeling of a thing when the thing itself is gone. She could carry this anger to market someday. *Genuine article, barely used, betrayed by a friend with good intentions, best offer.*

Except.

Except that at the border stone, months ago, when he'd stepped over it shaking like a wet dog and asked her why she followed him, she'd told him the truth: *because somebody finally owed me something that wasn't a piece of my head.* His debt to her — sworn on the broker's floor in Ashford, one hour of his childhood spent buying her freedom from that cult scout — was the first clean entry in the green book. No interest. No terms buried in the marginalia. Just: *Cyrus Underhill owes Wren. Freely given, freely sworn.*

And here was the arithmetic she couldn't make sit straight, no matter how she moved the numbers:

If he burned the ledger, her debts died. All of them. The ones crushing her and the ones holding her up, because up here on the road they were the same debts wearing different collars. The memory of her sister's laugh was collateral, yes — but it was also the only proof she had that she'd ever had a sister. The six years of her own face were pawned, but they were also *hers*, the only version of her anyone had ever kept safe. The brokers were thieves, but they were thieves with vaults, and vaults kept things better than poverty did.

Burn the book and she wouldn't walk out free. She'd walk out *empty*. A woman with a hole where her history was and no paper trail leading back to it. Free the way a stripped field is free.

And he knew. He had to know — he'd read the registry, he'd watched the ceremony, he'd stood in that hall while debtors screamed. He knew exactly what dissolution cost the small people, and he'd decided the sum was acceptable, and he hadn't put it to her, and that — that was the wound under the wound. Not the burning. The *deciding*.

Because she'd been decided-for her whole life. By brokers, by buyers, by weather and luck and the price of flour. She'd clawed out exactly one square foot of the world where she was the one who chose, and she'd built it out of a green book and a mule named Turnip and a rule she never broke: *never let anyone else hold your pen.*

And here was Cyrus, kind Cyrus, gentle scribe Cyrus, reaching for her pen with both hands, saying *trust me, I've read the fine print, I know what's best for everyone.*

The moon came up over the shoulder of Vael, and the mountain's glow caught it wrong — that deep red light bleeding up from the dragon-fire, staining the clouds like a bruise under skin. Dimmer tonight. She could see it was dimmer even from here. Whatever he meant to do, the clock was already running, and it wasn't waiting for her forgiveness.

Good, she thought. Then: *not good.* Then she pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes and sat with both thoughts at once, which was the most honest accounting she'd done in years.

* * *

He found her an hour later. Of course he did. He walked like a man approaching a shrine he wasn't sure he was allowed to enter, and he stood a respectful ten feet off with his hands in his sleeves, and he said, "I'm not going to apologize for wanting everyone free."

"Good," she said. "Don't. It would cheapen it."

"But I should have asked."

"Yes."

"And I'm asking now." He came closer, slowly, and crouched so they were nearly level, which she noticed, and resented noticing. "Not to change your mind. To hear it. If I could do it differently — if there were a way to unmake the ledger and give every piece of collateral time to find its way home, years maybe, a generation — would you stand with me? Knowing it costs you something either way?"

Wren looked at him for a long time. The red light of the dying fire moved on his face.

"That's not the plan, though," she said. "Is it. There's no slow version. There's the fire going out and the choice happening fast, and you'll be standing in the vault with two halves of yourself and a dragon's last breath, and nobody will be thinking about a tinker's green book."

"No," he admitted. "Probably not."

"There it is." She nodded, almost gently now, which frightened her more than shouting had. "You're a good man, Cyrus Underhill. Genuinely. You're the best man I've met on this road and the road is mostly wolves in coats. But good men with big mercies are more dangerous than greedy ones, because greedy men can be bought off and you can't. Your mercy doesn't negotiate. It just arrives, and everyone downstream drowns in it, and afterward you grieve beautifully."

She stood. Shouldered her pack. Turnip was tethered twenty yards down the shelf, and she whistled, and the mule's ears came up.

"Where will you go?" he asked. Standing too now. Not following. She gave him credit for that.

"Down," she said. "Away. There are people in Ashford who'll want warning — debtors, renters, the ones living month to month on borrowed hours. Somebody should tell them the flood is coming so they can climb onto whatever they can carry." She paused at the edge of the firelight. "That's not permission, understand. I'm not blessing this. I'm just refusing to watch it happen from close range."

"Wren."

"Don't." She held up a hand. "If you say my name in that voice I'll stay, and I can't afford to stay, and you can't afford for me to, because someone has to be out in the world remembering that your beautiful freedom has a body count. Consider it a debt collection. You owe me. I'm choosing how you pay."

He actually smiled at that — small, cracked, real. "Fair terms."

"The only kind I offer." She turned, led Turnip out onto the shelf, and swung up into the saddle. The drop yawned on her left, black and enormous, and the mountain glowed on her right like a furnace door left ajar, and between those two darknesses there was a narrow road and she took it.

Halfway down the switchback she stopped, because there was one more thing, and she'd regret it forever if she left it unsaid. She didn't turn around. She shouted it down into the dark instead, toward the smudge of his fire, toward the man sitting alone with his two souls:

"When you meet her in there — the other half of you — ask her what *she* wants! Not what the ledger needs, not what the dragon needs, not what mercy looks like from the top of the mountain! Ask the person! It's the only question anybody ever asks me last!"

Silence came back up the slope. Then, faintly, carried on the cold air: "I will."

She believed him. That was the worst part. She believed him completely, and she rode away anyway, because believing a man and trusting the world he was about to remake were two different entries in two different books, and only one of them was hers to balance.

Behind her, the light under Mount Vael flickered — guttered, steadied, guttered again — like a candle deciding.

She did not look back again. Looking back was collateral, and she'd paid enough.
