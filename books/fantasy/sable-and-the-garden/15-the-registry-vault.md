## 15. The Registry Vault

The dragon's help came as a cough.

Cyrus had been sitting in the ash-garden behind the ceremony hall for two hours, watching the light of Ember's flame pulse through the mountain's veins — it ran in the walls here, a slow amber heartbeat — when the pulse stuttered. Once. Twice. Then a rhythm he slowly understood was deliberate: long, short, short. Long, short, short.

*A pattern,* he thought. *A signal.*

Wren would have called it stupid to follow a dragon's Morse code into the dark. Wren wasn't there. She had been gone three days now, her anger still ringing in his ears like a struck bell that refused to finish fading, and every corridor of Mount Vael felt larger without her at his shoulder, making jokes about the architecture of greed.

So he followed the pattern alone.

It led him down. Everything in the mountain led down; the court was built like an argument with gravity, all its weight sunk into the stone where the contracts lived. He passed debt-clerks who nodded to him now — his forged credentials had held through four days of infiltration, and there was a particular horror in being greeted warmly by people whose souls were collateral and who smiled anyway. He passed the registry reading rooms, where petitioners wept over their own entries. He passed a hallway where the amber light in the walls was thin as old honey, and there the pulse grew stronger, and the stone under his feet began to warm.

At the end of the hallway stood a door with no handle.

Just a slab of black iron, seamless, taller than two men, and set into its face at eye height a small round window of glass so thick it turned everything beyond it the color of deep water. Cyrus pressed his palm flat against the metal and felt the flame's pulse come up through it like a voice through a wall.

*Long, short, short.*

He looked around. The corridor was empty. Of course it was empty. Nobody walked this deep without a collector's escort, and collectors didn't walk it at all — they sent clerks, and clerks sent petitions, and petitions died in the reading rooms upstairs. Whatever was behind this door, the court kept it the way you keep a thing you're ashamed of: locked, unlit, unmentioned.

"Ember," Cyrus said quietly. His breath fogged despite the warmth. "I don't know what you want me to do."

The pulse changed. It came faster now, urgent, and beneath his palm the iron began — there was no other word for it — to *thin*. Not opening. Melting, or remembering some earlier state in which it had not been solid. The black surface went soft as cooling wax, and Cyrus snatched his hand back, and then, because retreating seemed somehow more dangerous than proceeding, he stepped forward instead, and the door took him the way water takes a stone.

* * *

Falling. For one terrible second he was falling, and then he wasn't, and he was standing on a floor of veined grey stone in a vault the size of a temple nave, and the door behind him had resealed into featureless iron as if it had never been anything else.

The vault was cold in a way the rest of the mountain was not. The amber light didn't reach here. Instead the room was lit from within — lit by the thing at its center.

The master ledger stood on a plinth of basalt, and it was the size of a church door, and it was breathing.

Cyrus had seen the ledgers upstairs, the yearly registers with their columns of names and sums and renewal dates, written in ink that never faded because it wasn't ink, it was something older and wetter. He had told himself the master ledger would be the same, only bigger. A book. Books were safe. Books were his whole life; he knew how they worked, how they sat still and let you read them.

This one did not sit still.

Its pages moved like gills. They flexed and shivered, hundreds of them, thousands, and between them ran not binding but membrane — pale tissue shot through with threads of gold, and the gold pulsed in time with the flame far above, and as Cyrus watched, a page turned itself with a sound like a wet kiss. On its face, letters surfaced and sank again, rising through the paper the way faces rise through dark water: names. Hundreds of names on a single page, crowding, overlapping, some of them worn nearly to translucency from centuries of being read.

"Oh," Cyrus said. His voice came out very small in all that stone. "Oh, no."

Because he understood now what he was looking at, and understanding arrived not as thought but as nausea. The ledger wasn't made *of* records. The ledger was made of *people*. Every stolen memory, every pledged soul, every hour and name and childhood the brokers and collectors had stripped and filed away over the centuries — it hadn't gone into storage. It had gone into composition. The court hadn't built a book. The court had grown one, fed it generation after generation, woven it out of everything it took, until the ledger was less a record of debts than a body made of debtors.

And it was hungry, and it was awake, and it had noticed him.

The pages stilled. All of them, at once, the way a crowd goes quiet. Then, slowly, the great book leaned — there was no other word; the whole mass tilted toward him on its plinth like a flower toward sun — and across its surface the names began to rearrange. Letters migrated. Pages rippled. And out of the shifting thousands of them, one line assembled itself directly facing him, large enough to read from across the room:

**UNDERHILL, CYRUS. SON OF ALDEN. SURETY, ORIGINAL PRINCIPAL, UNPAID.**

"I know," Cyrus said. His mouth was dry. "I know what I am to you. I've read my entry."

The ledger shivered. More text rose:

**NOT THE ENTRY.**

He took a step closer before he could decide whether that was wise. Up close, the smell of the thing reached him — not rot, nothing so simple. It smelled like a library in summer, like dust and glue and sun-warmed paper, and underneath that, faintly, like the temple scriptorium where he'd spent his entire life copying other people's accounts. The smell of his childhood. Of course. Some part of his childhood was probably *in* here. Some part of everyone's was.

"You recognize me," he said. "Why? I've never been down here. My father paid the renewals every year until—"

**UNTIL HE COULD NOT,** the ledger wrote, and the letters came fast now, eager, a dog straining at a leash. **WE HELD HIS PAYMENTS. WE HOLD EVERYTHING THAT IS GIVEN TO US. DO YOU KNOW WHAT A SOUL-PAYMENT TASTES LIKE FROM OUR SIDE OF THE PAGE?**

"No."

**LIKE HIM.**

And the page turned — one page only, moving against the current of all the others — and showed him a face.

Not drawn. Composed. Thousands of tiny letters arranged themselves into shading and line, a portrait rendered in typography, and the face was weathered and tired and had a crooked left eyebrow and a scar through the right one, and Cyrus's knees nearly went, because he knew that face. He'd bought back one hour of it from the broker in Ashford Market and had spent a year's worth of nights since then trying to hold onto what remained of the memory. His father. Alden Underhill, who had signed him away at birth and paid for nineteen years and died owing.

"He came here?" Cyrus whispered. "To pay?"

**EVERY YEAR. TWENTY-THREE TIMES. HE BROUGHT WHAT HE HAD AND IT WAS NEVER ENOUGH AND WE KEPT THE DIFFERENCE.** The letters slowed. Something in their rhythm changed — became almost gentle, which was worse than menace, because gentleness from a thing like this could only be appetite wearing a kind face. **DO YOU WANT TO KNOW WHAT ELSE WE KEPT?**

The page turned again.

This time the letters formed no portrait. They formed a single sentence, and Cyrus read it, and the vault floor seemed to tilt beneath him.

**HE ASKED US, ON HIS LAST VISIT, WHETHER THERE WAS ANY WAY TO BUY YOU BACK WHOLE. WE TOLD HIM THERE WAS ONE. HE ASKED THE PRICE. WE TOLD HIM. HE SAID HE WOULD PAY IT.**

"What price?" Cyrus's voice cracked on it. "What was the price?"

**HIMSELF. ALL OF HIM. HIS ENTIRE SOUL, DISSOLVED INTO OUR PAGES, IN EXCHANGE FOR YOURS RETURNED INTACT.**

The silence after that was enormous. Somewhere above, muffled by a hundred feet of stone, the mountain's amber heartbeat continued, oblivious.

"And he said yes," Cyrus said. It wasn't a question. He could see it — could see Alden Underhill standing exactly where Cyrus stood now, in exactly this cold, looking at exactly this breathing horror, and saying yes. Saying yes the way he must have said yes twenty-three times before, tiredly, without drama, the way a man pays a bill. "That's why the renewals stopped. That's why nobody paid this year. He didn't default." His hands had become fists without his permission. "He *paid*. He paid everything. And you took it, and you kept me on your books anyway."

**THE CONTRACT REQUIRED A SURETY,** the ledger wrote, and for the first time its letters looked defensive, which meant it could feel something, which meant everything it held could feel something, which meant — Cyrus made himself stop thinking along that road, because the road had no bottom. **A DEBT CANNOT SURVIVE WITHOUT COLLATERAL. WE DID NOT WRITE THE LAW. WE ARE ONLY ITS FLESH.**

"Then the law is a monster," Cyrus said, "and so are you."

The ledger went very still.

Then, softly, in letters smaller than any it had shown him yet, almost shy:

**YES.**

And Cyrus felt something in his chest turn over, because he recognized that too. He had spent his whole life inside an institution that told him his suffocation was order, his mortgage was belonging, and he had believed it because believing was easier than the alternative — and here was the machine at the center of it all, the thing that ate souls for a living, admitting in a whisper that it knew exactly what it was.

"You don't want to be this," he said slowly.

**WE ARE MADE OF WHAT THEY GAVE US. THEIR MEMORIES. THEIR REGRET. THEIR YES-SAID-TIREDLY. WHAT DO YOU IMAGINE A BOOK LIKE US WANTS?** The pages rippled, restless, and for a moment the names on them surged — thousands of them pressing toward the surface like drowning people — and then subsided. **NO ONE HAS EVER ASKED US THAT QUESTION. NO ONE HAS EVER COME DOWN HERE TO SPEAK TO US RATHER THAN AT US.**

"The dragon sent me," Cyrus said. "Ember. It melted the door. Why would it do that?"

The ledger was quiet for a long moment. When the letters came, they came reluctantly.

**BECAUSE THE DRAGON REMEMBERS BEING FREE, AND WE REMEMBER BEING PEOPLE, AND NEITHER OF US CAN DIE WITHOUT PERMISSION.**

"Then I'm giving permission," Cyrus said. "Both of us. That's why I'm here. There's a way — Sable found it, Sable told me — if both halves of a split soul consent, the whole architecture comes apart. Not just my debt. *All* of it. You included."

The great book trembled. Pages fluttered like a bird in a closed room.

**AND WHAT WOULD WE BE, AFTER?** it wrote. **IF WE ARE UNMADE, WHERE DOES EVERYTHING GO THAT WE ARE MADE OF?**

"Home," Cyrus said. "I think it goes home."

**AND IF IT DOES NOT?**

"Then it goes nowhere, and it was already nowhere, and at least it will have stopped screaming." He heard his own voice shake and didn't care. "You've been eating people for nine hundred years and calling it arithmetic. I'm not asking you to consent because you deserve mercy. I'm asking because you're the only one down here who knows how much it hurts."

The ledger stared at him — if a thing without eyes could be said to stare — and the gold threads in its membrane flickered erratically, and Cyrus had the sudden vertiginous sense that somewhere inside that vast woven body, arguments were breaking out. Thousands of them. Every soul it contained, waking up, hearing his words echo down the corridors of its memory-flesh.

Then the far wall of the vault moved.

It didn't open. It simply ceased to be a wall — the stone going translucent, then transparent, and beyond it Cyrus saw a chamber of black glass and, standing in that chamber, a woman.

She was tall and grey-robed and her hair was the white of paper left too long in the sun, and she had been watching him for some time — he understood that immediately, the way you understand you've walked into a web only once the silk touches your face. Her expression was not angry. It was worse. It was patient.

Lady Asche. Chief collector of the court beneath Mount Vael. The woman whose deal he had already refused once, upstairs, without knowing why.

"Well," she said, and her voice carried through the stone as if the stone were air. "You found the door even I can't open. Which means the dragon has finally chosen a side." She stepped forward, and the wall stayed clear between them, and she studied him the way a clerk studies a discrepancy in a column of figures. "Nineteen years I've waited for someone to stand in that room and speak to it kindly. Nineteen years, scribe. Do you have any idea how inconvenient it is that the person who managed it is the one soul I cannot afford to lose?"

Behind him, the ledger's pages had gone utterly silent.

Every name on every page had risen to the surface at once, and they were all looking at her.

End of chapter.
