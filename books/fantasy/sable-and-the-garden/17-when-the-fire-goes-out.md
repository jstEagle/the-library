## 17. When the Fire Goes Out

*Ember-of-the-Long-Vigil*

I have burned for nine hundred years, and I know the arithmetic of my own body the way a miser knows his coins.

There is not much left. There has not been much left for a long time — a century of thinning flame, of the court's clerks coming to my chamber with their ledgers and their anxious faces, asking me to burn *just a little longer*, just until the next contract closes, the next soul collected, the next debt squared away so the great machine beneath Mount Vael could keep its perfect books.

They never asked what it cost me to burn. They only asked how long.

Tonight I am speaking with the scribe — the Underhill boy, the one whose name is written in the master ledger in his father's guilty hand — and I am aware, distantly, the way one is aware of rain beginning, that this is the last conversation I will ever have.

His mouth is moving. He is saying something about mercy, about the ledger remembering instead of counting, and I want to tell him: *hurry, boy.* I want to tell him that the negotiation he thinks is happening is already over, that every word spent on persuasion is a word taken from the fire, and the fire is all that stands between nine thousand sleeping souls and the raw dark.

But dragons do not hurry. It is the one vanity I have kept.

So I listen. And I let my flame gutter.

* * *

*Cyrus*

The light changed first.

Cyrus had been standing in Ember's chamber for what felt like hours — though time down here was a rumor, the air thick with ash and old gold and the smell of something burning that was also, impossibly, *thinking* — and he had learned to read the dragon-fire the way he'd once read candle flames in the scriptorium. A steady flame meant a steady night. A flicker meant Master Hollis approaching with his counting-breath and his disapproval.

The flame before him now did not flicker. It *shrank*.

It pulled inward like a fist closing, like a tide retreating from a shore it would never touch again, and in the space of three heartbeats the vast chamber went from furnace-orange to a bruised, dying red, and Cyrus could suddenly see the walls — really see them — the carvings of the First Bargain, the niches where contract-souls were stored in vials of smoked glass, thousands of them, glowing faintly like a city seen from a hill at night.

"Ember." His voice came out small. "Ember, what's happening?"

The dragon's eye, large as a temple door, rolled toward him. In it he saw himself reflected: a thin man in a borrowed clerk's coat, holding a map inked in blood, standing in the last light of the world's oldest fire.

"What is happening," Ember said, and her voice was gravel poured slowly, "is that you talked too long, little scribe. Or I listened too long. The blame is shared."

"No. No, you said there was time—"

"I said there was *some* time. You heard *enough* time. Debtors always do." Something moved in the depths of the eye that might have been humor, or grief, or both wearing the same face. "Listen to me now, because I will only say this once and the mountain is about to become very loud. The ledger in the vault below — the living one, the one woven from stolen memory — it is anchored to my flame. When my flame dies, the anchor fails. The contracts do not simply end, boy. They *tear*. Every soul bound in this mountain will be recalled to its owner raw and unransomed, and most of those owners are not prepared to receive themselves."

Cyrus's mind, trained on ledgers, translated instantly: *not an annulment. A default.*

"The vault," he said. "Asche is down there. Sable is down there. If I can reach the master ledger before the tearing finishes—"

"Then you may catch the souls mid-fall and give them somewhere gentle to land. Or you may fail, and they will scatter through the mountain like sparks through a burnt roof." The eye closed, opened. "Go. Run. And boy — when you stand before the ledger, do not let it speak first. It knows your voice better than you do."

The flame shrank again. And this time it did not stop shrinking.

Cyrus ran.

* * *

*Wren*

Wren had been halfway down the stair from the ridge-gate with her arms full of stolen candles — don't ask, the court's guards never checked a tinker carrying candles, it was the one universal law of fortresses — when the mountain screamed.

Not the rock. Not the wind through the vents. The *people*. From every corridor, every cell, every gilded debtors' gallery where the court's indentured souls sat out their terms in comfortable misery, came a sound she had heard exactly once before, in a broker's back room in Ashford, when a man tried to take back a memory that had been sold twice.

A sound like someone being handed themselves and dropping it.

She dropped the candles. Wax skittered across the stone. Down the corridor, a woman in a fine grey robe collapsed against the wall, clawing at her own chest as if trying to hold shut a door that was bursting open from inside, and light — actual light, silver-white, wrong — poured out between her fingers.

"Wren!" Someone grabbed her arm. It was Tam, the scullery boy who'd sold three years of his childhood to cover his mother's gambling debts, the one who smiled too easily because smiling was cheaper than crying. Except he wasn't smiling now. He was convulsing, his eyes rolling white, and the light was in him too, thrashing under his skin like a fish on a line. "Wren, it *hurts*, it's coming *back*, make it stop—"

"I can't—" Her hands hovered useless over him. She was a tinker. She fixed hinges and kettles and, once, memorably, a music box that played the same four notes forever. She did not know how to fix a soul arriving home without permission. "Tam, look at me, breathe—"

"It's *raw*, it's all raw, I can feel everything I sold, all at once—"

All across the gallery, the same scene, dozens of times over. Debtors folding double. Guards dropping their pikes to clutch their own heads — because of course the guards were debtors too; in the court beneath Vael, everyone was somebody's collateral. Somewhere above, faintly, through miles of carved stone, Wren could hear the great bell in the registry tower ringing wild and unpatterned, no rhythm to it at all, which frightened her more than anything else, because bells were supposed to mean something.

And under it all, growing louder, a sound like tearing cloth — vast, slow, continuous — as if the mountain itself were being unstitched.

*This is him,* she thought, with a clarity that cut through the panic. *This is what Cyrus is doing. This is what liberation sounds like from the inside.*

She had walked away from him for exactly this. She had told herself it was righteous, that he had no right to unmake the system she survived inside, and maybe that was still true, maybe it would be true tomorrow. But Tam was seizing on the floor in front of her, and righteousness didn't have hands.

She knelt, took the boy's face in hers, and did the only thing she knew how to do with broken things.

"Hold on," she said. "Hold on. I've got you. Somebody up there had better know what they're doing."

* * *

*Lady Asche*

In the vault, the master ledger began to scream in nine thousand voices, and Asche stood very still and let it wash over her.

She had heard it before. Once. Nine hundred years ago, when the first contract was struck and the first soul — hers — was fed into the binding, and the ledger learned its first word. Everything since had been variations. Interest. Renewal. Forfeiture. She had built the grammar of it, clause by clause, and told herself for nine centuries that grammar was a kind of mercy, that a soul given structure suffered less than a soul left loose in the dark.

Now the structure was failing, and the souls were loose, and they were screaming in her own voice — every one of them, because the ledger had learned language from her, and everything it held spoke as she spoke.

"Cyrus Underhill," she said to the empty vault, to the writhing shelves, to the map-fragment pinned under her palm where she'd caught it trying to crawl toward the ledger. "You are late."

The vault doors burst open behind her.

He came through them at a dead run, coat torn, ash in his hair, and stopped so abruptly he nearly fell — because the vault was not what he expected, or perhaps because she was not. Asche watched him take it in: the shelves of glass vials guttering like storm-lamps, the ledger itself heaving on its plinth like a beached thing, pages turning themselves, ink crawling off the parchment and hanging in the air in threads.

"You," he said.

"Me." She lifted her hand off the map fragment. It immediately began dragging itself toward the ledger, leaving a smear of old blood on the stone. "Your courier-piece has been trying to reach the book since the flame began to die. It knows what it wants to be. Do you?"

"Sable wants—"

"Sable wants what *you* want, that's the horror of it, boy, that's the whole architecture of the thing." For the first time in centuries, Asche raised her voice, and the vault flinched around her. "The court split you at birth because two halves consent more easily than one whole. One half to live safe in the valley and grow up believing himself free. One half to serve as courier, collateral, proof of concept. And now you stand here with both halves in one room while the anchor burns out, and you still think there is a version of this where nobody pays."

"There is." He stepped forward, over the map, past her, toward the heaving ledger. "One soul anchors it kindly. Two unmake it. Ember told me. Sable told me. Both halves consent, and it ends — not defaults, *ends*. Cleanly."

"And you believe a dragon and a scrap of talking ink." Asche laughed, and hated the sound, because it cracked in the middle. "I wrote the first contract, child. I know what these things cost. There is no *cleanly*. There is only *who*."

Above them, through the vault's high shaft, the light turned from red to grey.

The flame was out.

* * *

*Ember-of-the-Long-Vigil*

Nine hundred years, and the last of it spent listening to a scribe talk about mercy.

I do not regret it. Let the record show — let whatever ledger comes after me record it — that Ember-of-the-Long-Vigil, last of her kind, jailer by fuel and victim by design, chose to spend her final hour on a conversation rather than a scream.

The flame goes. It does not go out the way fires go out; it goes the way tides go, drawn back into some deeper water, and for one moment — one impossible, luxurious moment — I am not burning. I am only myself. Small. Cold. Nine hundred years of heat lifting off me like steam, and underneath: a creature. Just a creature. Tired beyond the vocabulary of tired.

Below me, the mountain fills with the sound of souls coming home badly.

Forgive me, little ones. I held the door as long as I could.

The dark takes me gently, which is more than I ever gave anyone.

* * *

*Cyrus*

The grey light lasted three seconds. Then the vault went black — true black, the black of a place that has never needed windows — lit only by the vials, thousands of them, flaring white as the contracts tore, and by the ledger, which rose off its plinth entirely, pages beating like wings.

Cyrus felt it find him.

Not see. *Find.* The way a hound finds a scent, the way a debt finds a debtor. The living book turned in the air and every page faced him at once, and written across all of them, in his father's handwriting, in Sable's blood-ink, in Asche's ancient careful script, was his own name — *Underhill, Cyrus — surety — renewed — renewed — renewed — FORFEIT* — and the word forfeit was still wet.

"Take my hand," he said. He didn't know to whom. Both of them. Either.

Sable's map pressed itself into his palm from below, warm as a living thing. And somewhere in the roaring dark of the vault, in the space between one heartbeat and the next, he felt the other half of himself arrive — not a voice, not yet, but a presence, familiar as his own reflection, standing beside him in the black.

*Together,* said Sable, everywhere at once. *Now. Before it remembers how to count.*

Cyrus reached for the ledger with both hands — one his, one hers —

—and the ledger reached back.
