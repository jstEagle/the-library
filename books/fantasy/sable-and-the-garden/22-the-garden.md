## 22. The Garden

The border stone had been a wall once. Now it was a threshold, and now it was soil.

Cyrus knelt in it on a morning in late spring, his knees damp through his trousers, turning earth that had never known magic and now could not remember being without it. The stone itself still lay beneath the garden beds — he could feel it with his palms when he pressed deep enough, smooth and cool as the inside of a wrist. The temple masons had offered to break it up and cart it away. He had said no. You did not unmake the thing you built your life upon. You planted over it, and you tended what grew, and you told the truth about where you were standing.

The garden ran along the old line of the ward for half a mile, a green seam stitched between the valley and the world beyond. Every plant in it was a name.

That had been Wren's idea, though she'd deny it if asked. *You want to keep memory?* she'd said, the first spring after the Unmaking, her hands already dirty, her eyes already scheming. *Don't write it down. Writing gets read by people who weren't there. Plant it. Make them kneel to find out who somebody was.* So they had. Each plot held a name spoken aloud at planting, worked into the roots like a blessing or an apology. Some plots held the names of debtors whose souls had come home too late to find anyone waiting. Some held couriers. Some held dragons — one plot, near the eastern end, held only *Ember-of-the-Long-Vigil*, and nothing would grow there but a single white flower that bloomed in winter, which everyone agreed was correct.

Cyrus had planted more names than anyone. He tried not to count them anymore. Counting was the ledger's habit, and the ledger was ash, and he was trying, year by year, to be a man instead of an account.

He turned another spadeful of earth and found a worm, fat and indifferent, and let it be.

"You're doing it wrong," said a voice behind him.

He didn't startle. After seven years he knew every footfall in this garden, and this one belonged to a girl of nine named Ilsa, daughter of the miller, who had appointed herself the garden's inspector sometime around her sixth birthday and had never been relieved of the post.

"The beds are supposed to be straight," Ilsa said, coming to stand beside him with her hands on her hips. She wore her father's old apron rolled three times at the waist. "Master Corvin's beds are straight."

"Master Corvin has a plumb line and forty years of practice."

"You have seven years."

"And a bad back." He sat back on his heels. "Besides, the names don't care if the rows are straight. They've been crooked their whole lives. Most of them."

Ilsa considered this with the gravity of a magistrate. She was one of the valley children born after the border fell — children who had never known the strange stillness, who thought it ordinary that weather came from beyond the ridge, that traders' songs drifted in from roads that used to dead-end at a stone. To them the old valley was a story told by adults who went quiet in the middle of it. Cyrus sometimes envied them so much it felt like grief.

"What are you planting today?" she asked.

"A name." He reached into the basket beside him and took out the small clay marker, unfired, its surface still bare. He hadn't written on it yet. That came last. "An old one."

"Whose?"

"Sable."

Ilsa frowned. The name meant something even to her — the way certain names in the valley had become less like names and more like weather, present everywhere, explained to no one. Sable's Gate, they called the western path now. Sable's Rest was the inn. There was a bell in the temple tower cast from the melted registry chains, and the children called it the Sable-bell because it rang at dusk, and no one had ever successfully corrected them.

"The lady from the stories," Ilsa said. "The one who was you."

"That's the one."

"But how can someone *be* you?" This was her favorite question. She asked it the way other children asked why the sky was blue — not because she expected an answer but because she liked hearing him try. "If she was you, then where were you while she was her?"

Cyrus set the spade down and looked out along the garden. Morning mist was burning off the far beds. A pair of travelers — pilgrims, probably, come to see the place where the last dragon-fire had gone out and everything changed — stood at the eastern gate reading the wooden sign Wren had carved: *WALK SLOWLY. THE DEAD HERE ARE ONLY MOSTLY GONE.*

"Sit down," he said, "and I'll tell you properly."

She sat, cross-legged in the dirt, apron and all. Dirt was not a thing anyone minded anymore. That was another change: the temple had spent two centuries teaching cleanliness of body as a metaphor for cleanliness of soul, and the garden had undone all of it in a single season. You could not work soil and stay spotless. The abbot — a round, kind woman named Ferren who had taken the office after Master Hollis retired to copy herbals by the sea — said the garden taught in one afternoon what the liturgy couldn't teach in a lifetime. Cyrus thought that overstated it. It had taken him rather longer than an afternoon.

"When I was born," he began, "there was a court under Mount Vael that kept its power in a very simple way. It kept debts. Not money-debts — soul-debts. If your family owed something it couldn't pay, the court would take a piece of you as surety. And the piece it took from my family was—"

"You," said Ilsa.

"Me. Or most of me. What it took was sent away, made into a courier, given a different name and a different shape and no memory of where it came from. That was Sable. She carried messages for the court for years before she understood what she was carrying, and when she finally understood, she stole a map written in her own blood and ran for the one place in the world the court's contracts couldn't follow." He touched the soil lightly. "Here. Over this stone. Magic stopped at the border then, you know. Nothing crossed. Not spells, not collectors, not debts."

"And she died here."

"At the gatehouse. Half-dissolved already — the land didn't know what to make of her, a piece of a valley boy walking around in a stranger's skin. But she lived long enough to put the map in the hands of the first person she saw." He smiled. "A scribe. Me. I didn't know yet what she was. I spent most of a year finding out."

Isla picked at a thread on her apron. "And when you found out — that she was you — what did you do?"

"I asked her what she wanted."

This was the part he never got right in the telling. There was no right way. The truth was that he had knelt in a vault full of screaming paper and asked the fragment of himself — the self that had been stolen, shaped, sent out, broken, and burned into ink — *what do you want*, and she had answered, and her answer had been the single most generous thing he had ever witnessed, and he had been the beneficiary of it, which made generosity feel like theft every time he remembered it.

"She wanted to stop being a debt," he said. "Not to die. Debts end when they're paid, and she wasn't paid, she was just... held. She wanted to go back into the world as something freely given. So we unmade the ledger together — her and me and a dragon and a tinker and a woman who'd been the court's first victim and its cruelest hand — and when it came apart, Sable went into the new book. Not as a name owed. As a name remembered. Everything the ledger knows now, it knows because she taught it mercy instead of arithmetic."

Ilsa absorbed this with her head tipped sideways. "So she's in the ground here?"

"In a manner of speaking. She's in the bell. In the paths. In—" he gestured at the mist, the beds, the slow green world, "—all of this, a little. Mostly she's in me. When she dissolved, some of her memories stayed behind. I carry them alongside mine. Her first snowfall. The weight of a courier's satchel. The sound the mountain made at night. Sometimes I dream things I never lived, and I wake up knowing they're hers, and I'm glad. It means she's not gone. It means she's *kept*."

"Do you miss her?"

It was the question adults never asked, because adults assumed the answer was too complicated, and it wasn't complicated at all.

"Yes," Cyrus said. "Every day. And every day I'm grateful, which is a strange thing to feel at the same time, but you get used to holding two truths. I was a man who'd been missing half of himself his whole life and didn't know it. Then I met the missing half, and she was braver than me, and kinder, and funnier, and she chose to spend herself so that thousands of strangers could go free. You don't get over that. You build a garden on it."

* * *

They planted the marker together. Ilsa insisted on writing the name herself, in careful charcoal strokes that would fade with the first hard rain and be rewritten, as all the markers were, season after season — memory kept alive by being renewed, never by being preserved. *SABLE*, she wrote, and beneath it, after a moment's fierce concentration, *WHO WAS ALSO CYRUS*, which was not the formula, but which Cyrus decided was better than the formula.

They pressed the soil flat with their palms. Somewhere down the row, the white flower on Ember's plot had opened despite the season, and the mist had lifted off the far fields, and beyond the ridge — visible now, always visible, the mountain wearing its ruined crown like an old scar — Mount Vael stood empty and open, its courts turned into schools and granaries, its vaults into seed archives. Lady Asche had gone west years ago, alone, carrying nothing, having refused every offer of company including Wren's, which Wren still brought up at least twice a year with genuine outrage. The last Cyrus had heard, Asche was teaching children to read in a fishing village that had never heard of the court, and had wept again, recently, at her own name — but softly this time, the way you weep at a song you're finally done grieving.

Wren herself lived four valleys over and visited when guilt or affection got the better of her, whichever came first. She still traded memories, but only her own, and only willingly, and she always came back to check that Cyrus hadn't straightened the beds.

The bell rang noon. Ilsa sprang up, suddenly all elbows and appetite, remembering chores or lunch or both.

"Will you tell it again someday?" she asked at the edge of the bed. "The whole story? About the fire going out, and the lady with the iron hands, and the dragon?"

"I'll tell it as many times as you ask."

"Good." She paused, and looked back at the fresh marker, and asked the question he'd been waiting for all morning, the real one, the one underneath the others:

"Who was Sable? Really. Just — who was she?"

Cyrus wiped his hands on his knees and looked at the name in the soil, at the crooked row, at the long green line running east and west over the bones of an old wall, and found that after seven years the answer came easily, the way true things do.

"Someone I was," he said. "And someone I got to meet."

Ilsa nodded slowly, satisfied, and ran off toward the bell-sound and the bread. Cyrus stayed a while longer in the warm dirt, one hand resting on the border stone beneath the soil, feeling it hold — not as a wall, not as a ward, just as a stone, patient and plain, keeping the garden steady while everything above it grew.
