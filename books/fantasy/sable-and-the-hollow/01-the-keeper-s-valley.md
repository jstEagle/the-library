## 1. The Keeper's Valley

The valley woke the way it always did: without magic.

Marguerite Ishikawa stood at her window in the grey hour before dawn and watched the mist come down off the western ridgeline, slow as poured milk. Below her, Lamplight Hollow — the valley folk had never bothered to name it anything grander — was a scatter of roofs and chimney smoke, orchards going gold at their edges, the millrace flashing where it caught the first light. A beautiful place. A prosperous place. The richest soil in three provinces, the elders liked to say, blessed by the vigilance of the Keeper's line.

Blessed. Marguerite turned the word over some mornings like a coin she suspected of being clipped.

She dressed in the dark out of habit: wool stockings, the grey dress with the deep pockets, her mother's old boots resoled twice and still too large. At the door she paused, as she did every morning, and pressed her palm flat against the lintel where a sprig of rowan hung dried to brown. No charm in it. There could be no charm in anything past the gate-line. That was the first law, the oldest one, the one written into the valley's bones: *magic does not follow you home.* Whatever crossed from the Hollow side stayed on the Hollow side, or it died at the threshold like frost in sunlight.

Twenty years, and she had never once tested whether that was true.

The path down from the keeper's cottage was worn into a pale ribbon through the bracken. She walked it without looking, her body knowing every root and stone, while her mind did what it did every morning — counted. Three hundred and eleven steps to the stream. The stream ran cold even in high summer; the elders said it came down from snowfields no map showed. She crossed on the stepping stones, fourth one loose, always loose, and began the climb toward the gate.

The Hollow Gate sat in a fold of the western hills where the land seemed to have been folded shut like a hand closing over something precious. From the valley side it looked like nothing at all: a ring of standing stones, seven of them, lichened grey and leaning like tired old men, around a floor of packed earth that no grass had grown on in living memory. Sheep would not graze there. Birds crossed overhead but never landed. The valley children dared each other to touch the stones and came back pale and quiet, unable to say why.

Marguerite had stopped being afraid of them when she was nine. She had started being afraid *for* them when she was twenty-five.

Her rounds were simple, and they were everything. She walked the outer ward first — a circle only she could see, marked by a faint coolness against her skin, like walking through the memory of water. Then the stones themselves, each one greeted by name, because her grandmother had named them and her grandmother before that, all the way back to the founders: Ash, Thorn, Briar, Hollow, Sorrow, Vigil, and the seventh, the keystone, which had no name anyone would speak aloud. Her grandmother had called it only *the Tooth*, and had said it with her mouth turned away, as if the stone might hear and take offense.

Last, the rite.

She knelt at the center of the ring and laid both palms on the earth. The words were in a language older than the valley's tongue, taught to her syllable by syllable until she could have spoken them asleep, and perhaps she often had. They were not a prayer exactly. They were closer to a promise, or a payment — *we remember, we maintain, we hold* — and as she spoke them she felt the seal beneath the earth answer, the way a great sleeping animal answers the hand that feeds it: a slow pulse, deep and vast, turning over beneath the packed dirt.

Every morning for twenty years, that pulse had been steady as a heartbeat.

This morning, it stuttered.

It was small. Anyone else would have missed it entirely — a hitch in the rhythm, a half-beat of silence where there should have been fullness, like a heart skipping in the night. Marguerite's hands went cold against the soil. She spoke the next line of the rite and felt the seal gather itself, smooth over the stumble, resume its long slow turning. By the time she finished, everything felt normal. Everything felt *right*.

Except that in twenty years, it had never once stumbled.

She knelt there a long moment after the last word faded, listening with her whole body. The wind moved through the bracken on the ridge. Somewhere below, the mill began its day. The stones stood in their patient ring, lichen-grey, silent, keeping whatever counsel stones keep.

"You felt it too," she said quietly, to the keystone. "Don't pretend you didn't."

The Tooth, naturally, said nothing. But as she rose and brushed the earth from her knees, she noticed — and this was new, this was *wrong* — that the moss on its northern face had died. Not faded. Died. A patch the size of her two hands, black and brittle as burnt paper, though there had been no fire, no lightning, no drought. She touched it and the crumble came away on her fingers like ash.

Magic cannot follow you home. She looked at the ash on her fingertips for a long moment, then wiped them carefully on a cloth and buried the cloth in her pocket rather than shake it loose inside the ward. Rules were rules. Rules were all she had.

* * *

The walk back down took longer than the walk up, because she let herself stop at the overlook — the one indulgence in a life otherwise scheduled to the minute — and look at the valley spread out below.

From here she could see how well it lived. The wheat already heavy in the fields, though it was barely midsummer. The orchards bent under fruit that neighboring valleys wouldn't see for another month. The millrace running strong, the smoke rising fat and white from chimneys that had known no lean winter in four generations. When traders came up the pass, they paid for valley grain in silver and called the place god-touched. The elders smiled modestly and said it was good husbandry and the Keeper's vigilance.

Good husbandry. Marguerite had been saying the words to herself lately in a different tone, letting them curdle.

Because here was the thing she had begun to notice, the way you notice a sound in the night only after years of sleeping through it: the covenant had rules, but the rules were not symmetrical. She was forbidden to leave the valley — fine, she had never wanted to. She was forbidden to love — harder, but bearable, or so she'd told herself, though there had been a young man once, a trader's son with ink-stained fingers, whom she had sent away before anything could grow, and whose letters she still kept in a box she did not open. But nowhere — not in the rite, not in the teachings, not in the crumbling ledger of her order that she had read so many times the binding fell apart in her hands — nowhere did it say what the valley gave *back*. What the seal *took*, and for whom.

A seal keeps something out. That was what she had been taught. But a seal also keeps something *in*, and a seal can be made to bleed in only one direction, and she had spent twenty years feeding a power beneath the earth without ever once asking where the blood went.

The prosperity had to come from somewhere. Nothing in this world came from nowhere. She had learned that at her grandmother's knee along with the names of the stones, and she had forgotten it conveniently for most of her life, because forgetting was easier than the question that followed: *if the valley is fed, what is being eaten?*

She stood at the overlook until the sun cleared the ridge and the mist burned off, and then she went down, because routine was the shape her discipline took, and discipline was the only inheritance her order had left her that she trusted.

* * *

The council met in the meetinghouse on the green, in a room that smelled of lamp oil and old varnish, and Marguerite attended every sitting as tradition required — the Keeper present, the Keeper silent, the Keeper honored. Seven elders sat behind the long table. Elder Corvane presided, a broad comfortable man with a voice built for harvest sermons and hands that had never touched the gate-stones. He was kind to her, in the way one is kind to a well that gives water: grateful, proprietary, and incurious about what happens underground.

"Keeper Ishikawa," he said warmly, when she asked to speak. "What news from the vigil?"

"The seal stumbled at dawn." She said it plainly, because she had learned that plainness was the only armor that fit her. "A hitch in the pulse. And the moss on the keystone has died — burned black, north face. I've seen nothing like it in twenty years."

The silence that followed lasted three seconds. She counted. She counted everything now; counting kept the dread in a cage.

Then Elder Wren, the eldest, a woman like a bundle of grey sticks, said, "Seasonal. These things fluctuate."

"These things have never fluctuated."

"Even mountains settle," said Corvane comfortably. "You'll perform the rite again tomorrow, and the day after, as your line has always done, and the valley will sleep soundly. Is there anything else?"

There were a dozen things else. That the ledger's later pages were missing, torn cleanly at the spine. That her grandmother had died muttering about *the ninth day* and no one would tell her what happened on the ninth day of what. That when she had asked, at nineteen, why the founders' records ended a generation before her birth, Elder Wren had looked at her with an expression she had never forgotten — not guilt, exactly. Something more like *inventory*. As if Marguerite herself were a line item being checked against a list.

"No," she said. "Nothing else."

She bowed her head as tradition required and sat through the rest of the sitting in silence, watching lamplight move on the faces of the seven people who were, on paper, her family, her order, her whole world. They talked about grain tariffs. About a roof needing slate. About the festival, six weeks hence, which the valley held every year in honor of the covenant — lanterns, honey-cakes, dancing on the green, gratitude to the Keeper who kept them safe.

Gratitude. It tasted different now. It tasted like a payment made to someone who wasn't expected to live long enough to spend it.

Walking home in the blue dusk, past windows glowing with the easy warmth of a valley that had never once gone hungry, Marguerite caught herself doing something new. She was rehearsing questions. *Where do the missing pages go? What did my grandmother know? What are you harvesting, and who is starving for it?* Questions she would ask no one, because she already knew the shape of the answers, the way you know the shape of a wound before you pull the bandage.

That night she climbed to the gate one final time, though the rite was done, though the round was finished, though every rule said the Keeper's day ended at sundown. She stood outside the ward — outside, deliberately, where the air was just air — and looked at the seven stones silvered by moonlight, at the dead black patch on the keystone's shoulder, at the packed earth that no grass would ever dare.

The seal pulsed beneath her feet, steady again, obedient again. Too steady. Like a liar who has practiced.

"I'm going to find out what you're for," she told it softly. "Whatever it costs."

And far beneath the earth, in the dark where the founders had buried their clever work, the seal turned over in its long sleep — and did not stumble this time, but *leaned*, faintly, toward her voice, like something that had been waiting twenty years to be spoken to.

She was halfway down the hill when she heard it: a sound from beyond the stones, from the far side of the fold in the hills where no human sound should carry. A footfall. A stagger. And then — thin, ragged, unmistakably alive — a man's voice crying out in a language she had never been taught, and somehow, impossibly, understood.
