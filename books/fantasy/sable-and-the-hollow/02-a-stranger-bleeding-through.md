## 2. A Stranger Bleeding Through

The ward woke her before dawn.

Not with sound — the Hollow Gate had never made a sound in twenty years of my keeping — but with a sensation like a tooth aching somewhere behind the world. I lay in my narrow bed above the gatehouse and felt it thrum through the floorboards, through the stone, through the soles of my feet when I stood, and I knew before I dressed that something had touched the outer ring.

Nothing touched the outer ring.

I pulled on my coat over my nightclothes because there was no time for propriety, took the iron lantern from its hook, and went down into the blue-black cold of the valley's edge. Frost furred the grass. My breath hung around me like a veil. The Gate itself rose ahead, as it always did: not a wall, not an arch, but a *seam* — a place where the air grew dense and faintly silvered, like breath on glass that never faded. The founders had built nothing visible. They had only marked the wound and taught my order to keep it closed.

The outer ward was the first of nine concentric bindings, a circle of standing stones half-swallowed by moss a quarter mile down the slope. In twenty years I had walked to those stones every morning and every night, laid my palm against the coldest one, and spoken the words my mother's mother's mother had spoken: *What is owed is held. What is held is whole.* And every time, the stone had answered with a hum like a struck bell heard underwater. Steady. Indifferent. Alive.

Tonight the stone was screaming without a voice.

I felt it through my palm — a stuttering pulse, arrhythmic, like a heart failing. And pressed against the far side of the stone, half in this world and half in whatever waits between worlds, was a man.

He was folded against the monolith as though he'd been thrown there by something with very little patience. Dark hair matted with blood. A coat that belonged to no tailor in the valley — cut long and close, dyed the color of wet slate, stitched with thread that caught my lantern light and gave back a color I had no name for. His left side was soaked dark from ribs to hip. Even as I watched, the frost around him curled away, melting in a perfect halo, as if his body were burning cold instead of hot.

Magic-touched. Profoundly, catastrophically magic-touched.

Every rule I had ever learned stood up inside me at once. *Never bring what the Hollow has marked across the inner line. Never touch what bleeds between. The valley is clean because we keep it clean.* I had recited those rules since I could speak. I had built my entire life inside them the way a snail builds inside its shell — and I knew, standing there in the freezing dark with my hand still on the shrieking stone, that a shell was also a kind of coffin.

He opened his eyes.

They were wrong. Not the color — though the color was wrong too, a grey like rain over iron — but the depth of them, as though someone had taken ordinary eyes and added a room behind them. He looked at me, and then past me, at the silvered seam of the Gate glowing faintly up the slope, and something in his ruined face unclenched with relief so naked it hurt to witness.

"Keeper," he said. His voice was gravel dragged through water. "You have to—"

"Don't talk." The words came out of me before I decided anything at all. That is the truth I would tell afterward, and tell again, and never quite believe: I did not decide. My hands were already moving, hauling his arm across my shoulders, and he weighed almost nothing, which frightened me more than the blood did. People who bleed that much should be heavy with it.

"The map," he gasped. "Don't let go of the—"

"I have you." I said it the way you say things to the dying, meaning comfort, meaning nothing. But my arm tightened anyway.

* * *

Getting him down the slope to the gatehouse was the longest walk of my life. He faded twice — once near the shepherd's cairn, when his knees simply stopped being interested in working, and once at the threshold itself, when some instinct older than thought made him flinch back from the doorway like a horse refusing a bridge.

"Inside," I said. "It's safe."

"It isn't," he managed, and laughed — a terrible, wheezing scrap of laughter. "For me, keeper, nothing has been safe in a hundred miles." But he came, because dying men do not argue with the only door offered.

I got him onto the cot in the gatehouse's back room — the room where keepers were born and, until now, where none had ever bled. I cut the slate-colored coat off him with my belt knife and threw it in the corner, where it lay looking less like clothing than like a sleeping animal. The wound beneath was worse than the blood had promised: three gashes, parallel, precise, running from his hip toward his heart. Claw-marks. Nothing in my valley made claw-marks like that. Nothing in my valley made claw-marks at all.

And here is where I should have stopped. Here is where twenty years of obedience drew its line in the floorboards. Because the gashes were not merely flesh-deep — they glowed. Faintly, along their edges, the way the Gate's seam glowed: a thin silver light, seeping, *hungry*. Whatever had torn him open had torn him open between, and the wound itself was a small breach, leaking the Hollow into my clean house.

The texts were explicit. Wounds of the between must be sealed with denial — salt, iron, the spoken refusal — even if the bearer dies. Especially if the bearer dies. I had read the accounts. Keepers had held strangers down on stones like this one and spoken them out of the world rather than risk the leak.

I heated water instead. I threaded a needle. And when my hands shook, I noticed that they shook exactly as they had shaken at nineteen, the night I sealed my first crack in the Gate alone, and I understood that the shaking had never been fear of the work. It had been fear of how much I wanted it.

His blood was strange — darker than mine, and where it dried on my fingers it glittered faintly, like ash mixed with mica. I cleaned the gashes and sewed them shut stitch by stitch, and the silver light fought me, crawling up the thread, trying to reopen what I closed. So I did the thing the texts forbade and the old songs only hinted at: I sang the binding backward. Not *what is owed is held* but its mirror, the verse my grandmother used to hum while shelling peas, the one the elders called superstition, the one in a language I had always assumed was just old valley dialect.

The light stilled. Then, slowly, it sank into the wound like water into drought-cracked earth, and the gashes closed to three pale seams, and the man beneath my hands breathed one long, shuddering breath and slept.

I sat back on my heels. My lantern had burned to a stub. Outside, the sky was going grey at the edges, and the Gate stood silent up the hill, and I had broken — I counted them with a kind of numb wonder — four separate laws before breakfast.

I hadn't decided to break a single one.

* * *

He slept through the day and most of the next night. I told myself I was watching him because he was a threat. I checked his fever eleven times because he was a threat. I fed him broth spoonful by spoonful when he surfaced, mumbling, into waking — because a threat that dies on your floor becomes a much worse kind of problem. Every justification I reached for was thin as onion skin, and underneath all of them lay the simple, unbearable fact: I had not been this close to another living person in six years, and my hands remembered warmth the way drowned men remember air.

On the second evening his eyes opened properly and stayed open, and found me sitting in the chair by the cot with a book I had not turned the page of in an hour.

"You sang," he said.

"You were delirious."

"In the old tongue. The keeper-line tongue." He pushed himself up on his elbows, wincing, and studied me with those deep, wrong, beautiful eyes. "Only two lines carry it now. One is dying in the Hollow." His gaze dropped to my hands, to the faint glitter still caught in the creases of my knuckles. "I'd begun to think the other was dead too."

My spine went cold. "Who are you?"

He reached, slow and careful, beneath the ruined ruin of his coat in the corner, and drew out a leather tube sealed with wax the color of dried violets. From it he slid a roll of heavy paper and spread it on the cot between us, weighting the corners with the candlestick and my abandoned book.

A map. But not any map of any country I knew. It showed the valley — my valley, rendered in fine, obsessive detail, the gatehouse, the nine rings of binding, the standing stones — and then it continued *past* the Gate, into a territory no valley map had ever admitted existed: a land drawn in shifting, silvery ink, forests and rivers and courts sketched in lines that seemed to move when I wasn't looking directly at them. And the labels ran in two scripts, interwoven like braided hair. One was the flowing, thorned alphabet of the Hollow.

The other was mine. The one my grandmother hummed. The one I had sung into his wound.

"That language died with the founder-keepers," I said. My voice sounded far away to me. "The elders say—"

"The elders say a great many things." He tapped the center of the map, where the Gate's seam was drawn not as a wall but as a *hinge*, with markings around it I couldn't read and understood, with sickening clarity, that I needed to. "My name is Sable. I crossed a hundred miles of the Hollow to find the last speaker of that tongue, because in nine days—" his hand flattened against the map, against the hinge, "—this opens. And everything your elders told you about why is a lie."

The fire ticked. The frost climbed the window. Somewhere up the hill, the Gate hummed its low, indifferent note, and for the first time in twenty years, I heard it as what it might have been all along:

Not a bell keeping time.

A lock, counting down.
