## 4. Nine Days

The map would not lie flat.

Marguerite had weighted its corners with river stones, smoothed it with the heel of her hand until the vellum warmed and softened, but still it curled at the edges like something trying to remember being a different shape. Ink in two languages crawled across its face — the founders' formal script she had studied since childhood, and beneath it, threaded through every line like roots under a road, a second hand. Older. Angled. Written in the alphabet her grandmother had whispered over once, drunk on plum wine, tracing letters in spilled sugar before the elders made her forget.

*Her bloodline's hand.* Sable had said it plainly, the second night, when fever had burned out of him enough to speak in full sentences. *This is your inheritance, keeper. Not theirs.*

She did not touch that thought yet. She touched the map instead, and read.

Nine days.

The breach date sat in the center of the gate's rendered architecture like a heart drawn into an anatomical study — a ring of figures, nine of them, each one a moon-phase glyph she knew from the ritual calendar. Eight were already dark. The ninth was filling now, silver bleeding into its crescent night by night, and when she had checked it against the sky that morning, the correspondence had been exact. The seal was not weakening randomly. It was counting down.

"You've gone quiet," Sable said from the hearth.

He was sitting propped against the stones where she'd moved him, because lying flat still pulled at the wound in his side. Three days of her stitching and salves and stubbornness, and he had color again — a sallow, hollow-boned version of health, but health. His eyes tracked her the way his kind's eyes did everything: too steadily, as if blinking were a courtesy he'd outgrown.

"I'm reading," she said.

"You're angry." He said it without accusation, the way he might have noted rain coming. "Your shoulders do this thing. Like wings folding wrong."

"My shoulders are none of your concern."

"No," he agreed mildly. "But the gate is."

She hated that he was right, and hated more that he'd learned to read her so quickly — three days, and he catalogued her like weather. She turned back to the map and forced herself through the founder's script line by line, translating aloud because Sable couldn't parse the formal register alone, and because hearing it in her own voice made it harder to pretend she'd misunderstood.

*"The covenant shall be renewed each generation by the offering of a keeper's life, willingly given, that the valley may know peace."*

There it was. The sentence she had been raised inside of, carved above the gatehouse door in stone so worn the letters had gone soft as old bread. She had walked beneath it ten thousand times. She had never once asked what the peace was *for*.

"Read the marginalia," Sable said quietly. "The second hand."

She found it — small annotations coiled around the main text like bindweed, in the angled alphabet she could only half-read. But half was enough. Half was a knife.

*Not for peace. For the mill-race. For the orchards that fruit twice. For the lamps that burn without oil.*

Marguerite stood very still.

She thought of the valley below the gatehouse — the green, generous, impossible valley. Wheat that ripened early and heavy. Apple trees that bore in autumn and again at midwinter, blossoms opening in snow. The lamps along the mill lane that needed no wick-trimming, no oil, no tending, burning with a pale gold flame the elders called *the founders' blessing*. She had grown up thinking the whole world was like that. She had grown up thinking magic simply *ended* at the ward-line, that the valley's abundance was the natural state of things and the Hollow beyond the gate was the barren exception.

The map said otherwise. The map said the valley drank.

Every blessing was a siphon. Every doubled harvest, every oilless flame, drew its sweetness through the seal from the realm on the other side — and the covenant's renewal, the keeper's death each generation, was not maintenance. It was a *payment schedule*. A tithe of lives to keep the tap running, dressed in sacred language so no one would look too closely at the pipes.

"They built it to fail," she heard herself say. Her voice came out level, which surprised her; she felt anything but level, felt like a hillside after weeks of rain. "The seal. It was never meant to hold forever. It's a ledger. When the balance runs low, the breach opens, the Hollow spills what's left, and then—" her finger stopped on the ninth glyph, the filling moon, "—then a keeper dies, the covenant renews, and the valley drinks for another hundred years."

Sable was silent for a long moment. When he spoke, his voice had an odd grain to it, roughened. "My people called it the Leeching. I thought it was a story mothers told. That the human realm grew fat by swallowing ours bite by bite." He looked down at his hands. "I came here hating you. All of you. And you didn't even know."

"I knew something was wrong." The admission cost her; she let it out anyway. "Twenty years of rounds, twenty years of ritual, and there was always this—" she pressed her knuckles briefly to her sternum, "—this splinter. Little things. The elders never walking the outer ward themselves. The way they changed the subject whenever I asked what happened to the keepers before me. My mother's records ending mid-sentence, forty years ago, the last page torn out." She laughed, and it came out wrong, dry and cracked. "I told myself the splinter was loneliness. That if I just kept the rituals perfectly enough, the feeling would go away."

"And?"

"It got sharper."

* * *

They worked through the night, and neither of them suggested stopping.

It was strange, Marguerite thought somewhere past midnight, how naturally conspiracy fit them. He held the lamp — one of the ordinary ones, wax and wick, because after what they'd read neither of them could bear the gold-flamed lamps' light in the room. She translated. He remembered. His knowledge of the Hollow's side of the architecture filled gaps in the founders' script the way water fills a footprint: the keystone chamber beneath the gate, the anchor-stones, the old words of binding that the fae court had preserved in mourning-songs for two centuries without knowing what they mourned.

"The reforging clause," he said, tapping a passage she'd marked. "Read it again. Slowly."

*"When the breach stands open, the covenant may be remade only by an offering of equal weight to that which was taken. As one life sealed the first bond, one life must seal the last."*

"One life," she repeated.

"One life," he agreed, and something crossed his face — quick, swallowed, gone — like a fish turning under ice. She saw it. She filed it away. There was a shape to his silences that she was beginning to recognize, a particular stillness he wore when he was carrying something he'd decided she shouldn't have yet. She'd seen it twice before: once about the court that sent him, once about the scar beneath his collarbone.

She let it go. Not because she wasn't suspicious — suspicion had become, in three days, her native climate — but because the night was short and the map was long, and whatever he was hiding, it sat in the same country as the truth they'd already uncovered. One secret among many. She would dig it out later, when her hands weren't full of the world ending.

What she noticed instead, as the candle burned down, was smaller. That he had started handing her things before she reached for them — the magnifying lens, the water flask, the second language glossary she'd copied out. That when she muttered a translation under her breath, he'd begun answering in it, haltingly, learning her grandmother's alphabet from her mouth. That at some point she had stopped thinking of him as *the stranger* and started thinking of him as *Sable*, the way you stop noticing a wound has closed because the skin around it has quietly become new skin.

"Here." His finger landed on a diagram near the map's edge — the gate itself, drawn in cross-section, and beneath the visible arch a second structure, inverted, mirrored. "The keystone isn't the stone. It's the *seam*. Where the two architectures interlock. Your founders built the lock; mine built the door. Neither side ever saw the whole design." He glanced up. "That's why the map's in two hands. Someone wanted the whole design to exist somewhere. Someone who knew both."

"A founder who regretted it," Marguerite murmured.

"A founder who was *both*," Sable said. "Look at the signature."

At the map's bottom corner, half worn away, was a mark she had taken for a stamp of office. Seen fresh, it resolved into a name written twice, overlapping — once in the founders' script, once in the angled alphabet. Two names. Or one name in two tongues.

She sat back. Outside, the wind had come around to the north, and it carried a smell she'd never caught on that wind before: cold stone and green rot and something sweet underneath, like fruit going over. The Hollow, breathing through the widening seam.

"Eight days," she said.

"Eight days," he echoed. Then, softer, almost to himself: "I used to think I'd feel glad. Standing here, seeing it proven. Vindicated." He shook his head slowly. "Instead I keep thinking about the mill lane. The lamps. Some child lit one tonight and felt safe."

"The safety was stolen goods."

"Yes. And it was still real to the child." He met her eyes across the guttering candle. "That's the worst thing I've learned in your valley, keeper. Not that your elders lied. That the lie *worked*. Everyone ate. Everyone slept warm. On the backs of people who are starving to death in the dark, and none of them — none of *you* — ever got to choose whether to be fed this way."

Marguerite looked at him for a long time.

Then she reached across the table, took his hand — the unwounded one, ink-stained now from copying glyphs — and set it flat against the map, over the ninth moon, and put her own hand on top of his.

"Then we choose," she said. "Both of us. Whatever the reforging costs, we find another way to pay it. Together. That's the whole design, isn't it? The part the founders never tried?"

His fingers curled under hers, careful, deliberate, and for a moment neither of them breathed. The candle flame leaned. Somewhere far below, in the deep architecture of the gate, metal groaned — or stone, or something older than either — a long settling sound, like a house shifting on its foundations.

Neither of them mentioned it. Neither of them moved their hands.

* * *

Dawn came gray and thin, and with it, hoofbeats on the switchback road.

Marguerite was at the window before she was fully awake, Sable rising behind her with a knife he had produced from nowhere and the fluid silence of his kind. Below, winding up through the frost-silvered orchards, came six riders in the gray-and-white livery of the valley council, moving at the unhurried pace of people who owned everything they surveyed.

At their head rode Elder Corvane, and even at this distance Marguerite could see the folded document in his gloved hand, sealed with the council's double-ring sigil.

"They're early," Sable murmured. "The quarterly inspection isn't for—"

"Eleven days." Her mouth had gone dry. "They never come early. They've never once come early in twenty years."

Below, the riders halted at the ward-line, and Elder Corvane lifted his face to the gatehouse windows — directly to hers, as though he had known exactly where she'd stand — and smiled.

It was not a comforting smile. It was the smile of a man reading a ledger whose numbers had finally come due.

"Keeper Ishikawa," he called up, his voice carrying unnaturally well on the still morning air. "We've come about the map. And about the company you've been keeping. Open the ward, Marguerite. The council wishes to *help*."

Behind her, Sable went very still, and said, in the tone of a man watching a trap finish closing: "They know I'm here. Which means someone told them." He paused. "Which means the elders have known about the breach all along — and they've come to make sure their keeper dies *on schedule*."
