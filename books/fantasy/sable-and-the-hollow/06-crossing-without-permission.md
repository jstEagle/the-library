## 6. Crossing Without Permission

The council hall kept its secrets in a cedar chest beneath the speaker's dais, and it kept only one guard at night — old Wren, who had once taught Marguerite to whistle through cupped hands and now pretended, in his dotage, not to see the world ending around him.

Sable crouched in the shadow of the hall's eastern eaves and watched the moon slide behind the mountain. Beside him, Marguerite breathed the way she did before every hard thing: slow, deliberate, as if she could ration her own courage.

"He'll be asleep by the second bell," she murmured. "He always is. He sleeps like a man who has already decided nothing is his fault."

"And you?"

"I don't sleep at all anymore." She said it lightly. It did not land lightly. "The map first. Then the gate."

They moved when the bell came — two strokes, soft as rain on stone. Sable went over the low wall while Marguerite drew Wren away with a knock on the granary door and a story about wolves near the upper pasture. He heard her voice, warm and easy, lying with the fluency of someone who had spent twenty years telling no lies at all and was discovering a talent for it. Something in his chest turned over. He would have to stop noticing things like that. There was no room, in what was coming, for noticing.

The chest's lock was iron and honest; his knife was neither. It gave. Inside, wrapped in oilcloth that smelled of incense and old decisions, lay the map.

He unrolled it there on the cold floor, because he could not help himself. Two scripts wound across the vellum like roots sharing one soil: the keepers' valley-tongue, and the older script beneath it, the one written in his bloodline's hand. The gate's true architecture sprawled between them — not a wall, as the valley believed, but a seam. A wound that had been stitched shut with a single life, year after year, generation after generation, while the founders grew rich on what leaked through the stitches.

Nine days. Fewer, now. Seven.

He rolled it up and ran.

* * *

Marguerite was waiting at the gate itself, of course. She had not gone back to her cottage for anything — not the salt-iron key, not her mother's ring, not the bundle of letters she had never sent to anyone. She stood before the Hollow Gate in the dark with her hands empty, and Sable understood, looking at her, that everything she owned had always been this: the seal, and her vigil over it.

"You should take something," he said. "Of yours. People carry something."

"I'm carrying you." She glanced at him, and the moonlight caught the dry humor in her face, the fear underneath it. "That's quite enough weight."

The gate was not a door. That was the first lie of it. From the valley side it looked like weathered standing stones in an arch, moss-eaten, half-swallowed by the hillside. But the map had shown them the truth, and now that Sable knew where to look, he could see the seams — hairline fractures in the air between the stones, leaking a light the color of deep water. The covenant hummed along them, a sound felt in the teeth more than the ears. His sister's voice, years ago, telling him how it felt from the other side: *like chewing on winter.*

"Once we cross," Marguerite said, "the valley can't follow. The magic can't live out here, and we—" She stopped. Started again, more honestly. "I don't know what it will do to me. To us."

"It will do what it does to everyone." He set his hand against the nearest stone. Cold, then warm, then cold again, like a thing breathing badly. "It will ask what you love most, and start taking it in installments."

"And you came out of there alive."

"I came out of there *fast*." He almost smiled. "There's a difference."

She stepped up beside him, shoulder nearly touching his. "Say it," she said. "Whatever you're not saying. Say it now, so I know its shape."

So he told her. Not all of it — not yet, not the part that sat in him like a swallowed stone — but enough. "I left the Hollow eleven years ago. My sister held the line of our house while I climbed over the dying to reach the gate. She called it treason. She wasn't wrong. I didn't leave to find help, whatever I've told myself since. I left because I wanted to live, and the Hollow had stopped offering that to anyone."

Marguerite was quiet a moment. "And now?"

"Now I'm going back." He looked at the seam-light. "Which is either courage or a very elaborate way of apologizing."

"Together, then," she said. "Courage or apology. We'll decide which later."

She took his hand. Her palm was calloused in the shapes of keys and ritual-tools, and it was steady, and he hated how much steadiness cost her, and loved that she paid it anyway.

They stepped through together.

* * *

Crossing felt like being remembered.

That was the only word Sable had ever found for it — the sensation of a place taking inventory of you, naming your bones, weighing what you'd carried away and what you'd brought back. The seam parted around them with a sound like tearing linen, and then the Hollow closed over their heads like dark water.

It was beautiful. That was the cruelty of it. Even starving — *especially* starving — the Hollow was beautiful the way a candle is beautiful as it gutters. Trees stood silver-barked and immense, their leaves translucent as stained glass, glowing faintly with hoarded light. Rivers ran clear as memory. And everywhere, threaded through the loveliness, the rot: orchards fruiting into ash, meadows gone gray at the edges like paper burning very slowly, a village of exquisite spired houses with no smoke rising from any chimney, doors standing open onto rooms where dust fell upward, drawn toward the sky that fed on nothing.

Marguerite made a small sound beside him. He watched her take it in — the keeper raised in a valley where magic couldn't follow, seeing for the first time what her whole life had been built to contain.

"It's dying," she said finally. "You told me. I thought— I don't know what I thought. That it would look like dying."

"This is what it looks like when something beautiful starves slowly enough to stay proud about it."

They walked. The Hollow knew him. He felt it in the paths that remembered his feet, bending slightly to meet him; in the hawthorn that dropped a blossom on his shoulder like an accusation or a welcome, he couldn't tell which. Eleven years, and the land had not forgotten him. That was the wound of homecoming: not that the place had changed, but that it hadn't changed *toward* him. It had simply waited, patient as a creditor.

Near midnight — if the Hollow kept such hours, which it did out of politeness — they reached the court.

* * *

The Court of Ashes sat in what had been a palace and was now a held breath. Its towers leaned like tired elders. Its great hall still glittered, chandeliers burning with captured dawn, but the banners hanging from the rafters were faded to the color of old bruises, and the courtiers gathered beneath them were thin in the way of things made mostly of intention.

They saw him coming from a long way off. Word traveled ahead of them through the Hollow the way cold travels through a floor.

At the far end of the hall, on a throne of white wood grown rather than carved, sat Vespera.

His sister had been twenty-three when he left. She was not old now — the fae aged like rivers erode, imperceptibly and all at once — but the leading of a dying people had carved her. She wore the regalia of their house, moth-wing gray and thorn-silver, and she wore it the way soldiers wear armor: correctly, and without comfort.

"Sable." Just his name. She let it hang in the hall until it rotted.

"Vespera."

"You bring a human keeper into the heart of the Hollow." Her eyes moved to Marguerite, cataloguing her — the valley wool, the keeper's posture, the absence of fear that was itself remarkable. "Eleven years, and you return with the enemy's last daughter and a stolen map. If this is an apology, brother, it has a strange architecture."

"The map shows what your own scholars suspected and couldn't prove. The founders built the seal to fail. The valley harvests us, Vespera. Every season of their prosperity has been paid from our realm's marrow. In seven days the breach opens on schedule, and they intend to let it."

Murmurs moved through the courtiers like wind through wheat. Vespera did not move at all.

"I know," she said.

The words landed in the hall and in Sable's chest simultaneously. "You *know*."

"We have known for sixty years. Do you imagine we sat here starving politely?" For the first time something cracked through her composure — fury, old and banked and terrible. "We sent three emissaries to the valley. Three. Their heads came back in boxes lined with valley lavender, so the smell would cover the rot. You were the fourth attempt, Sable. The last one we could afford. And you vanished through the gate and never sent word, and we spent eleven years believing you dead, or worse — believing you *comfortable*."

"I was trying to survive."

"Yes." She rose from the throne, descending the steps slowly, each footfall precise. "That was always your gift. The rest of us stayed and starved with dignity. You survived. Look at you — fed. Whole. Warm-blooded. You wear their world like a coat you intend to keep."

"That's not why I've come back."

"Then why?" She stopped before him, close enough that he could see the hunger in her, the literal hollowing behind her cheekbones. "Give me one reason to believe you that doesn't begin and end with your own salvation."

Beside him, Marguerite shifted — he felt her about to speak, about to spend herself defending him, and he caught her hand. *Not yet.* This reckoning was his alone.

"Because I know how to reforge the covenant," he said. "Not patch it. Not feed it another keeper's death. Reforge it — shared sacrifice, both realms bound equally, the theft ended. The map shows the keystone. I know the rite. And I came back to stand in front of the people I abandoned and offer it, knowing you have every right to refuse me."

Silence. Somewhere above, a chandelier flickered, its captured dawn guttering.

Vespera studied him for a long time. When she spoke again, her voice had gone quiet, which from her was worse than shouting.

"There is a detail missing from your generous plan, brother. One the map does not show, because the map was drawn by people who preferred not to write it down." Her gaze slid to Marguerite, then back. "Ask him, keeper. Ask him what the reforging costs. Ask him what he hasn't told you. And then decide whether the man who saved himself once can be trusted to save anyone else."

The hall went utterly still. Marguerite's hand tightened in his.

"Sable," she said quietly. "What is she talking about?"

Above them, the chandeliers dimmed, as if the Hollow itself were leaning in to hear his answer.
