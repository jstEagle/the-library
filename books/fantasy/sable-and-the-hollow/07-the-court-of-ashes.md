## 7. The Court of Ashes

The Hollow did not smell of rot, as I had been taught. It smelled of rain that had fallen on stone a hundred years ago and never dried — a sweetness gone stale, like fruit left in a locked room. We walked the last mile to the court through a forest of white trees whose bark peeled back in long curls, revealing wood beneath that was not brown but the grey of old ash, veined faintly with silver where something still lived.

Sable walked ahead of me, and I watched him the way one watches a person returning to a house where they once buried something. His shoulders had climbed toward his ears over the past day. He had not spoken since we crossed the gate except to name landmarks — *the ford, the cairn, the orchard* — each word dropped like a coin into water, sinking without echo.

"You grew up here," I said, because silence was worse.

"I survived here." He didn't turn around. "There's a difference."

* * *

The court received us in a hall built from the same white trees, their trunks still rooted, still growing upward into a ceiling lost in grey mist. No fire burned anywhere, yet light came from everywhere and nowhere, the way light comes through closed eyelids. The courtiers stood in ranks along the walls — tall figures in garments the color of moth wings, faces beautiful and starved, cheekbones sharp enough to have cut their way out of dying flesh. They did not breathe. I counted, out of some nervous habit my order would have beaten out of me, and none of them breathed.

At the hall's far end, upon a throne grown rather than carved from a single trunk, sat Sable's sister.

She was not what I expected. I had imagined someone severe, ancient, crowned in thorns. Instead she looked like Sable — the same dark hair going prematurely silver at the temples, the same watchful eyes — but worn down to wire. She rose when she saw him, and the whole court rose with her, a sound like wind moving through a wheat field made of bones.

"Vessan," she said. Not *brother*. A name, formal as a blade laid flat between them.

"Neriah." He stopped ten paces from the throne and bowed his head — not deeply. I understood then how much pride it cost him to bow at all. "I found the keystone's architecture. And I brought a keeper."

Every face turned to me. I felt their attention like cold water poured slowly down the back of my neck.

"I am Marguerite Ishikawa," I said, and my voice held, which surprised me. "Last keeper of the Hollow Gate."

"Last," Neriah repeated softly. She descended from the throne, her bare feet making no sound on the living floor. She circled me once, the way my grandmother used to circle a horse she meant to buy. "The valley grows fat while we starve. Your order built the seal to fail, to bleed us year by year into their fields and fountains. And now you come asking us to trust a keeper's word?"

"No," I said. "I'm asking you to help me break what my order built. That's not a keeper's word. That's a traitor's."

Something flickered across her face — not warmth, but its distant cousin. She looked at Sable. "And you. You left us. When the famine came and the court needed every blade, you slipped through a crack in the world and let the valley keep you alive. Why should I believe anything you carry is not poisoned by comfort?"

"Because I came back," he said quietly. "Knowing what you'd say. Knowing what you might do."

"Knowing what I *will* do," she corrected, "if this fails."

* * *

I told them everything. The map in two languages, the founders' design, the nine days — now eight — the elders who wanted me dead so their harvest could continue. I spoke until my throat was raw, and the court listened with the terrible patience of people who have waited a very long time for very little.

When I finished, Neriah sat again upon her throne and was silent for the space of ten heartbeats. Then: "You propose a new covenant. Mutual sacrifice. Two lives bound across the seam instead of one life spent."

"Yes."

"The old rite requires a willing death. A keeper gives everything; the gate drinks deep and holds for another age. It is clean. It is proven." Her eyes were pitiless. "Your rite has never been done. Half-lives, braided. You ask two realms to gamble their survival on an untested knot."

"I ask them to stop feeding on each other," I said. "One death buys eighty years. Then another keeper, raised in lies, dies alone in a valley that never loved her. I know exactly what that bargain costs, because I was raised to be its payment. There is a better knot. Let me tie it."

Silence. Somewhere above us, in the misted ceiling, a branch groaned.

"It cannot be done without proof," Neriah said at last. "Good faith must be shown, not spoken. The old law allows one trial." She rose. "Each of you will surrender your deepest anchor to the flame of the court. Whatever holds you to your shore — whatever you would turn back for — you place it in our keeping. If either of you refuses, or if either of you breaks before the trial completes, there is no covenant. Only war, and the gate torn open by force."

"And if we don't break?" Sable asked.

"Then the court believes you. Belief is the first thread of any knot."

* * *

They led us to a chamber beneath the hall where a fire burned that was not fire — a column of pale light rising from a basin of black glass, giving off heat but no color, warmth but no comfort. The Flame of the Court, Sable had called it on the walk down, and his voice had been strange when he said it.

Neriah stood at its edge. "Marguerite Ishikawa. Keeper. What is your deepest anchor?"

I thought of the valley. Of course I did. The terraced fields going gold in autumn. The bell in the gatehouse tower that I rang at dawn and dusk for twenty years, its rope worn smooth by hands — my hands, only my hands, always only mine. The ritual of my rounds. The smell of bread from the village ovens drifting up the hill, a smell I was never permitted to go down and share. My whole life fit inside that valley like a hand inside a glove, and even now, standing in a starving realm among the starving beautiful dead-alive, I loved it. God help me, I loved it still.

"The valley," I said. "My memory of it. All of it."

The words left me and became visible — I watched them go, a ribbon of gold light unwinding from my chest, from behind my eyes, threading toward the flame. And with it went the bell tower. The terraces. My grandmother's kitchen. The first frost I ever saw. The gate itself, walking its circuit at dawn, dew soaking my hem. Twenty years of mornings began to gutter like candles in a draft.

I hadn't understood. I had offered my anchor thinking I was offering a thing, a token, and the flame took it as a *taking* — it reached into me and pulled, gently, patiently, the way tide pulls a sandcastle, and I felt the valley dissolving out of me room by room, season by season, and I heard myself say *no, wait, not the gatehouse, not the bell, please—*

Because without those memories I would not know why I was fighting. I would be a woman standing in a strange land beside a stranger, holding a map she couldn't read, for reasons that would evaporate with the last golden thread.

"Marguerite." Sable's voice, far away.

I turned toward it. My own name sounded foreign. Who was Marguerite? Where—

He broke the trial.

I saw it happen. He stepped into the space between me and the flame — into the column of pale fire, which should have unmade him, which the court cried out as one voice to warn him against — and he seized the golden ribbon of my memories with both hands and *held*, arms shaking, skin smoking, hair lifting as though in a wind that existed nowhere else in that chamber. The flame roared soundlessly. The court was on its feet.

"Stop the trial!" Neriah's voice cracked like ice. "Stop it, you fool, you'll burn—"

"Then take mine instead," he said through his teeth, and I felt him open himself, felt the flame turn from me to him, and what poured out of Sable Vessan was not gold but silver — a river of it — his sister's face when they were children, the orchard, the ford, the cairn, every landmark he'd named on our walk and a thousand he hadn't, his whole childhood in this rotting beautiful realm offered up wholesale, freely, *instead*, so that mine would stay whole.

The flame drank him for three heartbeats. Four. Five.

Then Neriah struck the black basin with the flat of her hand and the fire died all at once, and Sable folded to the floor like a coat slipping off a hook.

I caught him before he landed. My memories flooded back into me all at once — the valley, the bell, the frost, the gate, all of it returned, every golden thread — and I knelt there on the cold glass holding him, weeping without any dignity whatsoever, saying his name over and over because I could remember it now, because remembering was suddenly the most precious thing in any world.

His eyes opened. They were unfocused, searching my face like a man reading a letter in poor light.

"Do I know you?" he whispered.

And then, before terror could finish forming in my chest: "No. Don't answer. I want to learn it again properly." A ghost of his old crooked smile. "Tell me everything. Start with the bell."

* * *

The court gave him a night to mend, and the fae heal well or not at all. By dawn he remembered the valley, the map, the mission — but the Hollow had gone quiet in him. He stood at the chamber's high window looking out at the white trees and said, wonderingly, "It's like looking at a stranger's photograph album. I know these are my trees. I can't feel them anymore." He touched the glass. "You're all I have of either world now, Marguerite. Isn't that a terrible thing to put on one person."

"We'll put it on two," I said. "That was always the point."

He laughed — a real laugh, startled out of him — and something in the room changed shape around that sound, and neither of us pretended afterward that we didn't know what it was.

* * *

We were summoned back to the hall at the hour when the false light burned brightest. Neriah awaited us on her throne, and beside her stood a courtier I had not seen before — older than the others, if such a thing could be read in those unbreathing faces, dressed in robes the color of deep water.

"The trial was broken," Neriah said. "By law, that voids the offering. By law, I should send you both away, or worse." She paused. "But the law was written by courts that assumed sacrifice flows one direction. What my brother did — taking your wound onto himself — is the oldest form of binding we have. Older than the covenant. Older than the gate." She rose. "The Court of Ashes accepts your terms. We will stand witness at the reforging."

Relief hit me like warm water, and I nearly missed what came next.

"But there is news," Neriah continued, and her voice went flat and hard, the voice of a commander. "A rider came an hour ago. From the gate." Her eyes found mine, and I felt the bottom drop out of the morning. "The seal is tearing, keeper. Not in nine days. Not in eight. The valley elders have begun forcing it open from their side — tonight."

The hall erupted into sound, and Sable's hand found mine in the chaos, gripping hard.

"How long do we have?" I asked.

"Hours," said Neriah. "Ride now, or bury both worlds."
