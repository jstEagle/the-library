## 8. The Breach Opens Early

### Marguerite

The Hollow had a smell she would never forget: wet stone and overripe fruit, sweetness rotting at the root. It clung to her hair now, her sleeves, the inside of her lungs. Three days since they had crossed without permission, three days of ash-pale courts and Sable's sister's flint-gray eyes, and still the smell followed her like a debt.

She was standing in the antechamber of the Court of Ashes when the gate screamed.

There was no other word for it. The sound came up through the floor, through the roots of the dead orchard outside, through the marrow of her — a long, tearing note like a harp string snapping under too much weight. Every fae in the chamber went still. Then every head turned toward her.

"No," she said, before anyone could speak it. "No, we didn't touch it. We've been *here*—"

But she already knew. She knew the way she knew weather coming, the way she had known for twenty years that the covenant was a lie built on a lie. She felt the gate in her blood the way she always had, that faint second pulse behind her heart, and it was *rending*.

Sable reached her first, crossing the chamber in four strides, his hand closing around hers. His fingers were cold. "Marguerite." Just her name, but it carried everything he couldn't say in front of the court.

Lady Vesperine rose from her throne of fused glass. Around her temples, the ash-crown flickered. "Keeper," she said, and the word was an accusation dressed as a title. "Your valley opens your gate."

"It isn't us—" Sable started.

"Then who?" Vesperine's voice did not rise. That was the terrible thing about her; nothing about her ever rose, and yet everything fell before it. "Who else knows the architecture? Who else has kept the maps, the rites, the keys? Your order built this seal, Sable. Built it to fail, we learned that much from your thief's map. And now, on the eve of its failing, it tears open from the *far side*."

Through the high windows of the court, the sky changed color. Not darkened — *changed*, the gray-green light of the Hollow bleeding toward a bruised violet, and beneath their feet the ground shivered with a rhythm that was almost, horribly, like footsteps.

Marguerite closed her eyes and reached for the gate the way she reached for her own heartbeat.

And saw it.

Not the tidy ward she had tended all her life, the ring of standing stones wreathed in morning mist above the valley. She saw the truth beneath it: the keystone, the great buried heart of the seal, and around it — *hands*. Human hands, old hands, hands she knew. Elder Corvane's voice came through the link like something spoken down a well, cracked and reverent:

"*In the name of the founders. In the name of provision. Open.*"

They were forcing it. The elders — the keepers-in-name-only who had taken her map and told her to die quietly for their comfort — were prying the gate open themselves. Not waiting nine days for the breach. Not mourning the covenant. *Harvesting.*

Her eyes opened. The whole court was watching her, and she found she wasn't afraid of them anymore.

"They're opening it," she said. "Corvane and his council. They mean to take the magic directly."

Vesperine went very still, the way a blade goes still before it moves. "Then your valley has just declared war on mine."

"Your valley," Marguerite said, and stepped forward, out of Sable's grip, into the center of the chamber where every cold eye could see her. "Listen to me. If the gate tears wide, what comes through will not be magic you can harvest. It will be *hunger*. Yours and theirs, loose in both worlds at once. I have tended that seal my whole life. I know what presses against it. You do not want it unpressed."

"And what would you have us do, keeper? Trust the line that starved us?"

"I'd have you come to the seam with me," Marguerite said. "Both of us. All of us. Because there are exactly two people in either realm who know how to close what's being opened, and one of them is standing right here, and the other one is holding my hand whether he admits it or not."

Behind her, Sable made a sound that might have been a laugh strangled at birth.

Vesperine looked at her brother for a long moment. Something passed between them — old wound, older love, neither willing to name it. Then the Lady of Ashes turned back to Marguerite.

"Go," she said. "Run ahead if you can. But understand, keeper: if your elders have broken the gate, my court will hold your line responsible. All of it." Her gaze flicked to Sable. "All of you."

* * *

### Sable

He had run through the Hollow before. He had been running through it, in one way or another, for thirty years.

This was different. This time the Hollow ran with him — or rather, unraveled around him, the rotting beauty peeling back from their path as though the land itself were flinching. He and Marguerite moved together through the dead orchard, past the glass-thin lake where drowned reflections swam upside down, up the long gray slope toward the place where the world wore thin. Behind them, faint but gaining, came the sound of the court mustering: horns made of hollow bone, voices like wind through winter reeds.

Ahead, the seam.

Even from a mile away he could see it wrong. The gate had always shown itself in the Hollow as a line of pale gold stitched across the air, neat as a hem. Now the stitching was pulling. Light poured through the gaps in shafts that bent where light should not bend, and through those shafts he could hear the valley — birdsong, a church bell, rain on slate — sounds that had no business here, spilling through like blood from a cut.

"They're using the harvest rite," Marguerite gasped as they climbed. "I can feel it. They think because the founders designed the gate to channel power outward, they can just — reverse the flow. Take instead of give."

"Can they?"

"The founders' design was never meant to be driven by hand. It's like—" she ducked under a branch of petrified hawthorn, "—like trying to steer a river by grabbing the water."

The last hundred yards were scree and silence. Then the seam was upon them, and Sable stopped, because there was nothing in his childhood nightmares, nothing in the war-songs of the remnant court, nothing in all his years of exile that had prepared him for seeing a gate die.

It stood where it had always stood, the twin arches facing each other across the thin place, one of living stone and one of living wood. Between them the air bulged outward like skin over a wound. And through the bulge, silhouetted against valley-light, stood figures in gray robes — six of them, seven — arranged in the harvest circle, arms raised, voices chanting in the old cadence he'd heard only once before, on the night he'd crawled through this same gate with a knife wound and a stolen map and no thought in his head but survival.

At the circle's center stood Elder Corvane, and in his hands, unrolled and pinned open by two acolytes, was the map. *His* map. Their map. Inked in two languages, one of them hers by bloodline, and Corvane was reading it aloud like scripture, wrenching syllables out of shape, and each mispronounced word made the seam bulge further.

"He's reading it wrong," Marguerite whispered. "He doesn't — he never learned the second language. None of them did. They let it die in our family because only the keeper needed it, and now he's *guessing*—"

The seam tore.

Not all the way. Not yet. But a hand's width of gap opened along the arches' inner edge, and through it came the first of them.

Sable would remember it afterward in fragments: a shape like a heron made of smoke and teeth. A thing that might once have been a fox, its coat gone to shadow, its eyes gone to embers. Small things first, quick things, the desperate minor spirits of the Hollow's margins — pouring through the gap like water finding a crack, tumbling onto valley grass, and then turning, confused, starving, toward the nearest source of warmth.

Toward the robed figures.

The chanting broke into screaming. From this side it looked almost gentle, the way the creatures flowed around the elders, curious as moths. Then one of the acolytes fell, and the scream that followed was not gentle at all, and the harvest circle scattered like a kicked anthill, and Corvane — Corvane held the map above his head and shouted words of command that only made the gap wider.

"They'll blame us," Sable said. He heard his own voice as if from far away. "Whatever happens next, the court will say the keepers' line opened the door."

"Let them say it while they help us shut it." Marguerite was already moving forward, toward the tear, toward the light and the smoke-shapes and the falling bodies, and her hand found his and pulled him with her. "Sable. The keystone. If we don't reach it before the gap widens past the arches, there won't *be* a keystone. There won't be anything to reforge."

He went. Of course he went. He had spent thirty years learning every way there was to run away from this place, and in the end it took only her hand to teach him the way back.

They crossed the threshold together, out of the dying Hollow and into the screaming valley, and the last thing Sable heard behind him was Lady Vesperine's voice, carried on bone-horns, giving the order he had dreaded since childhood:

"To the gate. And let nothing of the keepers' line survive to tell it otherwise."

* * *

### Marguerite

The valley was burning.

Not with fire — with *light*. Stolen magic, hoarded for two hundred years in the stones of the keepers' hall and the roots of the orchards and the blood of every family that had prospered on the covenant's lie, was boiling up out of the ground now that the seal no longer contained it. Wheat stood silver and writhing in the fields. The river ran backward for a hundred yards, confused. And everywhere, everywhere, the small hungry shapes of the Hollow moved through the dusk, drinking the light, growing larger as they drank.

An elder lay at the edge of the harvest circle. Marguerite knelt beside her without meaning to — Elder Wren, who had taught her her letters, who had smiled at her across twenty years of meetings and never once told her the truth. Wren's eyes found hers, and there was no apology in them. Only terror.

"You brought them," Wren whispered. "You went over and you *brought them*—"

"We came to save the gate. You opened it." Marguerite pressed her hands over the wound in the old woman's side, knowing it was useless, feeling the light leak out between her fingers like breath in winter. "Why, Wren? Why force it early?"

Wren laughed, and the laugh became a cough, and the cough became something quieter. "Because you weren't going to die," she said. "Nine days, and you were going to stand at the keystone and refuse. We could read it in you. So Corvane said — better we take it ourselves than lose it entire." Her hand clawed weakly at Marguerite's wrist. "We only ever wanted what we were promised."

"That's what they said in the Hollow too," Marguerite said, and watched the light go out of her.

She rose. Sable was already at the arches, his palms flat against the living stone, reading the keystone's agony the way she read it — through the blood, through the bond that was forming between them whether they willed it or not. Above them the gap in the seam groaned wider, and through it now came things that were not small: a stag of black water whose antlers dripped rivers; a choir of faces woven into one winged shape; and behind them all, patient as nightfall, something vast that the Hollow had kept caged at its own center until tonight.

The surviving elders had fled toward the village. The Hollow's host boiled at the threshold. And somewhere on the far side, gathering like a storm front, came the Court of Ashes with its bone horns and its judgment already written.

"Sable." Her voice was steady. She was surprised by that. "Tell me we can still close it."

He turned to her, and his face was lit from below by the leaking light of two worlds, and he was smiling — that crooked, exhausted, utterly stubborn smile she had first seen on a dying man in the mist.

"We can't close it," he said. "But we can *answer* it. Both of us. Right now, before the court arrives and before that thing comes through. Marguerite — the reforging doesn't need nine days. It needs two willing lives, offered *together*, at the keystone, while the breach is open enough to bind them into it."

"Offered how?" she asked, though her heart had already begun to answer.

"Half," he said. "Half a life each. Bound to each other, bound to both shores. No more keeper. No more theft. A covenant with two names on it."

Above them, the vast thing at the center of the breach shifted its weight, and the mountains of the valley leaned away from it.

Marguerite took his hand.

"Then we'd better hurry," she said, "before your sister arrives to kill us for doing it."
