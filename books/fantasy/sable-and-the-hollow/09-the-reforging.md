## 9. The Reforging

**Marguerite**

The keystone had been beautiful once. She could see it in the bones of the thing — the way the stone still remembered its shaping, curves worn into it like a face remembered by someone long dead. Now it hung in the seam between worlds, half of it valley granite, half of it Hollow obsidian, and through its cracked heart poured a light that was neither dawn nor dusk but the color of a wound left too long unbandaged.

Nine days had become eight, then seven, and now there were no numbers left at all. Only this: the breach yawning above them like a throat, and the keystone failing beneath her hands, and Sable's breath coming fast beside her in the cold that poured from both worlds at once.

They stood on a shelf of stone no wider than a grave — fitting, she thought, for what they meant to do here. Below, the valley spread gold and green in the last light she might ever see it by. Behind, the Hollow stretched its grey and hungry miles. And between them, the gate.

*Half a life,* the court had said. *Not a death. A division.*

Marguerite pressed her palm flat against the keystone's warm-cracked surface and felt it shudder, like a heart trying to remember its rhythm.

"Sable." Her voice came out steadier than she felt. "If I falter—"

"You won't." He took her other hand. His fingers were cold; they had been cold since the trial, since he broke the court's rite to shield her, since he'd wrapped his own memories of home around her like a coat against winter. "And if you do, I'll be there to catch the falling half."

She almost laughed. Almost. What came out instead was something smaller and more frightened and more true.

"I spent twenty years learning how to be alone," she said. "No one taught me how to be half."

"Then we'll learn together," he said. "It's the first lesson either realm has ever offered for free."

Above them, the breach groaned. The sound went through the stone shelf, through their boots, through their teeth. Somewhere down in the valley, she knew, the elders were moving — she had felt their intent like a bruise all day, the council chamber emptied, the confiscated map gone from its vault, old Wren Ashcombe's voice carrying that particular sanctimony he saved for acts of theft committed in the name of preservation.

They were coming. There was no time left to be careful.

Marguerite drew the knife.

It was not a keeper's blade — those had all been surrendered with her vows, or so the elders believed. This one Sable had forged in the Hollow's last green place, from iron that remembered being alive, and its edge sang faintly when it touched air that belonged to two worlds at once. She opened her left palm without ceremony. Twenty years of ritual had taught her that much: the body does the sacred work better when the mind stops interfering.

"Sable Ishikawa-ash," she said, using his full name, the fae syllables that made the air lean in to listen. "I offer half my life. Not to the gate. Not to the valley. To you — freely given, jointly held, never to be taken by force again."

The words were hers. That was the whole point. The old covenant had been written in blood that was spilled; theirs would be written in blood that was *chosen*.

His knife found his own palm in answer, and his blood ran dark as ink, dark as the map that had started everything.

"To Marguerite, last keeper and first partner," he said, and his voice caught — just once, just enough that she would remember it forever. "Half my life, freely given. Let neither world feed on the other. Let us feed each other instead."

They pressed their bleeding palms together over the keystone's broken heart.

* * *

**Sable**

The magic did not come like a wave. It came like a recognition.

He felt her half-life enter him the way you feel a word you've known in another language finally translated — sudden, obvious, rearranging everything it touched. Her twenty years of solitary rounds. The taste of valley bread. The specific loneliness of being the only person awake in a sleeping house. All of it poured into him, and in exchange he gave her the Hollow: the smell of ash-lilies, the weight of his sister's estrangement, the terrible beauty of a realm slowly starving itself elegant.

Their blood met on the keystone and the crack began to close.

He watched it happen with something like terror, because it was working — because for nine days he had carried the secret that the old rite required a death, and every alternative he'd imagined had ended in ashes, and here was the impossible arithmetic actually balancing: two halves making a whole that harmed no one. The keystone drank their joined offering and its fracture sealed like ice melting in reverse, and the breach above them began, slowly, agonizingly, to narrow—

Light flared behind them.

Sable turned, and his stomach dropped through both worlds at once.

Wren Ashcombe stood at the mouth of the passage with six of the council behind him, and they had not come empty-handed. They had come with the harvested magic of twenty years — hoarded, concentrated, coiled around their fists like luminous rope. The valley's stolen inheritance, weaponized at last.

"The gate stays closed," Ashcombe called, and his voice was almost gentle, which was worse than shouting. "As it has always been closed. As it must remain. You understand, child. Order matters more than mercy."

"He means to kill the keystone," Sable breathed. "If it won't seal, they'll shatter it and rebuild the old covenant — with your death as the anchor, since you're standing right here—"

Marguerite moved first. Of course she did. She stepped between him and the elders with her empty hand raised, blood still running, and said, "Wren. You know what happens if you break it now. Both realms bleed out through the wound."

"What happens," Ashcombe said, raising his fistful of light, "is that we survive. It is the only prayer our founders ever wrote, and I intend to honor it."

The magic left his hand like a spear.

Sable had one heartbeat to choose — shield her, or complete the binding, because his half of the rite still needed grounding, needed one more pulse of will to set it permanent — and in that heartbeat, someone else chose for him.

* * *

**Marguerite**

She saw the sister move before she understood it.

Vesha Ishikawa-ash had stood apart from the court all day, arms crossed, eyes like chips of the same obsidian as the keystone, trusting nothing and no one — least of all the brother who had once fled this realm and left her to inherit its ruin. Marguerite had expected her to watch them die, perhaps. Or to gloat.

Instead Vesha stepped into the light's path.

What she did then was not spellwork so much as grief given form. She opened herself to the Hollow's dying magic — all of it, the starving residue of a whole realm — and threw it outward in a wall between the elders' spear and the keystone. The collision was enormous. The shelf of stone screamed. Light ate light, and through the blaze Marguerite heard Vesha's voice, raw and furious:

"You want to save yourself by feeding on us? *Then choke on what's left of us!*"

"Hold the rite!" Vesha shouted back, not turning. "Finish it, brother! I have wanted to hate you for a hundred years — don't make me die doing it!"

Sable's hand found hers again, slick with both their blood.

The elders were pushing forward through the glare, Ashcombe screaming words of unbinding, and the keystone trembled under their palms, half-healed, wholly vulnerable. Marguerite looked at Sable — at this stranger who had bled through her wall and rewritten her life in nine days — and felt the terror rise up huge and cold in her chest.

And then, deliberately, she let it become something else.

Terror was just vow with the courage drained out. She poured it back in.

"Together," she said.

"Together," he answered.

They spoke the final words as one voice, hers in the valley tongue, his in the language of the Hollow, and where the languages met they made a third thing that had no name yet — a covenant written in shared sacrifice, in halves that refused to be wholes alone. The keystone flared white. The breach snapped shut like a book closing. And the binding raced up through Marguerite's arm, through her chest, through the place where her heartbeat lived, and settled there — doubled. Shared. *Held.*

She felt Sable's pulse beating inside her own ribs. Felt him feel hers.

Across the ruined shelf, the elders' magic guttered and died, unmade by a covenant older and truer than their theft. Ashcombe stared at his empty hands. Vesha sank to her knees, hollowed and grinning, alive.

And the gate — the gate did not vanish. It stood quiet between the worlds, its keystone whole, humming with two voices in one chord.

Marguerite laughed, or cried, or both, and gripped Sable's hand harder.

"It held," he whispered. "We held."

But far below, in the valley, bells had begun to ring — the alarm cadence, the one that meant the wards along the outer vale were failing — and the sound climbed toward them through the stone like water rising in a locked room.
