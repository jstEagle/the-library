## 10. Two Shores, One Gate

The first morning after the reforging, I woke to birdsong on both sides of the gate.

That was how I knew it had worked. Not the ache in my chest where half my life now lived in another body, not the silver thread I could feel running from my ribs to Sable's like a plucked string humming in the dark — but the birds. For twenty years the valley's dawn chorus had been thin and wrong, the way a room sounds when something has been quietly taken from it. The elders called it peace. It was only silence wearing a borrowed coat.

Now the song came through the archway itself, spilling over the threshold stones like water finding its old channel, and it did not stop at the ward-line. Nothing stopped at the ward-line anymore. That was the whole point.

I lay in the keeper's cot — *our* cot now, though we had not said so aloud; some things you let settle into place without naming them, the way silt settles into a riverbed — and listened to two dawns braided into one.

* * *

The gate no longer looked like what it had been. The keystone, reforged with our shared offering, had changed color: the old grey granite gone to a deep veined green, as if moss had grown inward instead of out. Where the seal once hummed with prohibition, it now breathed. You could stand beneath the arch and feel it move — an exhale toward the valley, an inhale toward the Hollow — slow and tidal, patient as anything that intends to outlive you.

The covenant we wrote is not written anywhere. That frightened me at first, until I understood that the old one had been written everywhere — carved into lintels, inked into ledgers, recited by rote to children too young to know they were memorizing their own sacrifice — and all that writing had never once made it true. Ours lives in the thread between us. It cannot be confiscated. It cannot be misread. If someone wants to know its terms, they must look at how we stand near each other, and read.

The terms are simple. Neither realm feeds on the other. What crosses, crosses freely or not at all. And no gate shall again be tended by one person standing alone against the dark, holding a door shut with the whole weight of a single life.

We were fools, my order. Brave, devoted, meticulous fools who built a lock that required a corpse for a key and called it holiness. Survival was never meant to be paid alone. I know that now the way I know the shape of my own hands — which is to say, I will spend the rest of my life relearning it, because twenty years of a lie does not leave in a season.

* * *

The valley took the loss of its stolen magic the way a body takes the setting of a broken bone: badly, and then gratefully.

The orchards fruited smaller that first autumn, and the wheat came up pale where the Hollow's seeped power had fattened it for generations. Old men stood in their fields cursing the fae, then cursing the keepers, then — more quietly, when they thought no one heard — cursing themselves. The council sent a delegation to the gate in the second month. I met them on the threshold stones, Sable a step behind me, and Elder Corvane looked at the green keystone and at me and could not decide which of us he resented more.

"You've left us weakened," he said.

"No," I told him. "You've been left *honest*. It feels the same at first."

They went back down the mountain. And here is what I did not expect: they began to learn. A farmer named Odile — her grandmother had once been passed over for keeper training because she asked too many questions — started planting the old way, the pre-covenant way, crop rotations and ash and patience. Others followed. The valley's children, who had never known hunger or wonder in equal measure, began coming up the mountain just to look at the arch, and I taught them what I should have been taught: not fear. Maintenance. Listening. How to tell a healthy ward from a tired one. How to knock.

Because the gate is a bridge now, and bridges have traffic.

* * *

On the far side, the Hollow greens.

It is slower there — rot that deep takes years to forgive — but spring came early to the western fenlands, and Sable swears the river that runs past his sister's court ran clear for three days in Thawmonth, the first time in living memory. His sister. Vessane still does not smile easily, and trusts less easily than that, but she holds the court together with iron and grief and a stubbornness I recognize because I married its twin. She sends envoys through the gate now, fae keepers trained in the old rites, and they work the eastern side of the arch while our people work the west, and twice a season we all meet in the middle — under the keystone, in the breath of the gate — and compare notes like any guild of any craft.

The first time Vessane crossed to the valley side, she stood very still for a long moment, looking at the wheat fields going gold under an honest sun, and said nothing at all. But she took a stalk of the wheat home with her. I have decided to believe it was a kindness.

Sable goes back and forth as freely as weather. Some days he is hollow-eyed with homesickness for a place that hurt him; some days he comes through the arch laughing at something a fae child said, carrying gifts — a lantern that burns cold light, a seed packet labeled in both our languages. He has nightmares about the trial still, about the moment he broke it to shield me, surrendering his own deepest anchor rather than let them take mine. I know what it cost him because I hold the other end of it. When he dreams, the thread pulls taut, and I wake, and I put my hand over his heart until the humming stops.

That is the covenant working. That is what it is *for*. Not the grand rite, not the keystone, not the courts and councils with their parchment and pride — but this: two people who each carry half a life, checking on each other in the dark.

* * *

Today we walk the threshold together, as we do every seventh day.

It has become ritual, though neither of us planned it. We start on the valley side at dawn, walk the length of the gatehouse, pass under the arch at the exact hour the tide of it turns — you can feel the inhale change to exhale under your feet, like stepping over the spine of a sleeping animal — and come out into the Hollow while the light there is still young. We inspect the wards. We leave offerings at the small shrines the fae keepers have raised: bread and salt on this side, dew-wine and a carved bird on that. We talk about nothing. Irrigation. Whether the eastern warden's apprentice is ready for her vows. Whether apples will ever take root in Hollow soil, and whether Sable's mother's grave has finally got moss on it, and whether moss counts as an answer.

This morning the mist is doing something lovely in the Hollow — lying in the fenland valleys like poured milk, burning off slowly, and the light comes through it in long gold shafts that make even the half-rotted trees look like cathedrals. Sable stops on the path and looks at it the way he always does, as if he needs to reconfirm, daily, that beauty can survive in a place that tried to kill him.

"It's greening," he says. "You can see it from the arch now."

"I know. I saw yesterday."

"Say it anyway."

"The Hollow is greening," I say, and he smiles, and the thread between us hums a note I have stopped pretending is anything other than what it is.

We walk on. Behind us the gate stands open — open, and held, which no one believed possible, which the founders' own blueprints swore was impossible, which took a dying stranger with a forbidden map and a keeper willing to burn twenty years of obedience to prove otherwise. Ahead of us the fen opens, mist lifting, water brightening, the ruined world putting on its green coat one reluctant acre at a time.

My life is half in each place now. Half my heartbeat belongs to this side, where the wheat grows honest and the children are learning to listen instead of fear. Half belongs to the other, where my husband's people are teaching their starving land to live again. I belong fully to neither shore.

I belong wholly to him.

And this morning, as we reach the midpoint of the bridge — the exact center, where the stone changes grain and the air changes taste — a sound comes up through the keystone that neither of us has heard before. Not the tide-breath. Not the hum.

A third voice. Faint, rhythmic, rising from somewhere deep beneath the foundations, older than the founders, older than either realm.

Sable looks at me. I look at Sable.

"Tell me you hear that," he says.

I do. And it sounds, impossibly, like knocking.
