## 1. The Valley That Forgets

The boundary stones did not like to be touched, but they liked to be neglected even less.

Sebastian Ashgrove learned this the way he'd learned everything that mattered — from his grandfather, who had learned it from his mother before him, back through a line of wardens so long it had worn itself thin as wire. He knelt in the wet grass at the base of the western stone and pressed his bare palm flat against its surface. Cold seeped up into his hand, then deeper, into the wrist, the forearm, the old ache behind his sternum where the guild's oath lived like a second heartbeat.

"Easy," he murmured. "I know. I know."

The stone hummed under his palm — not sound exactly, but something adjacent to sound, a vibration that lived in the teeth rather than the ear. It was hungry. They were always hungry this time of year, when autumn came down off the mountains and the nights grew long enough for things on the other side of the wards to press their faces against the invisible wall and wonder.

He uncorked the flask at his belt and poured a thin ribbon of tallow-and-ash oil around the stone's base. The smell rose up sharp and resinous, cutting through the damp rot of fallen leaves. The humming deepened, steadied, settled into something almost like contentment. Almost like a purr.

"There." He sat back on his heels and flexed his fingers, working warmth back into them. "That'll hold you till the frost."

The valley spread out below him in the last copper light of evening: forty miles of sheltered green folded between two ridgelines of the Graywall Mountains, stitched with fields going gold and grey, threaded by the river Vell which ran slow and silver and never once, in all recorded history, flooded. A hundred farmhouses with their windows just beginning to glow. The town of Emberdown in the middle distance, its chimneys standing up against the darkening sky like the strings of some vast, patient instrument.

And above it all, everywhere and nowhere, the wards. You couldn't see them. That was rather the point. But Sebastian could feel them the way a man feels his own skin — the great slow breathing dome of them, anchored at twelve points by twelve stones older than any kingdom anyone could name, holding back everything the world outside had become.

Holding back magic. All of it. Every spark and whisper and hungry, reaching thing.

* * *

He walked the last mile back toward town with his tools rattling in their leather roll and his collar turned up against the wind. The path along the river was rutted and familiar, and his boots knew it without his help, which left his mind free to do what it always did on these long walks: count.

Three wardens left. Down from three hundred at the guild's height, when the Ashgrove sigil — a tree with roots above ground and branches below, because nothing in their work ever grew the way it ought — had meant something in every court and crossroads from the coast to the capital. His grandfather used to say the guild didn't die; it just got quieter, year by year, like a bell rung softer and softer until the ringing passed out of hearing entirely.

Now there was Elder Maren, eighty-one years old and sharp as a chipped flint, who spent her days copying maintenance ledgers in a hand that shook only when she thought no one was watching. There was Tomas Reed, who had come to the valley as a refugee twenty years ago and stayed because, as he put it, someone had to mind the stones and everyone else had the good sense to die. And there was Sebastian. Twenty-six years old. Last apprentice the guild would likely ever take. Keeper of the western circuit, four stones, eleven miles, every third day, rain or snow or the particular gray boredom that settles over a place when nothing has happened for a very long time.

Nothing had happened for a very long time. That was the whole achievement. That was the entire point of the Ashgrove wardens, and Sebastian tried, most days, to be proud of it: the valley was safe precisely because nothing here was remarkable. Magic could not enter. Could not cross the wards, could not take root, could not so much as flicker in a candle flame or curl in a teacup. A hedge-witch from outside would walk into Emberdown and find her charms dead weight in her pockets. A curse would arrive at the boundary stones and unravel like cheap rope. For a hundred years — since the fall of Sable, since the night the kingdom two valleys over had been erased so thoroughly that maps showed only forest where its cities had been — the valley had been the one clean room in a dirty world.

His grandfather had explained it to him a hundred times, sitting by the fire with his hands open toward the flames as if warming them on the memory itself. *Magic is a hunger, boy. Everything else in creation eats and is eaten, but magic only eats. It takes memory, name, love, luck — whatever a thing holds dearest — and gives back nothing but more hunger. The wards don't keep danger out. They keep appetite out.*

Sebastian had been seven years old and had nodded solemnly and asked if the wards kept out dragons too, and his grandfather had laughed until he coughed, and that was the whole of Sebastian's magical education: a warning wrapped in laughter, delivered by a man who died believing the walls would hold forever.

Elder Maren believed it still. She said the wards had held for a century and would hold for ten more, and that the guild's dying was not a tragedy but a victory — you built a wall so good that eventually nobody remembered why it was needed, and then you could rest. Sebastian wanted to believe her. Most days he managed it.

Tonight, walking home through the blue hour with the first stars pricking through over the eastern ridge, he found it harder than usual. Not because of anything wrong. Because of something right, for once. Something terrifyingly right.

At moonrise, Mara Venn was coming to dinner.

* * *

The house on Lantern Row had been in the Ashgrove family for six generations, and it wore its age the way an old soldier wears his scars — without apology and without much effort at concealment. Floorboards that announced every footstep to the whole street. A kitchen hearth big enough to roast an ox, though Sebastian had never roasted anything larger than a chicken. And upstairs, behind a door he kept locked out of habit rather than secrecy, the study: the last archive of the Ashgrove guild, shelves of ledgers and ward-diagrams and grimoires that hadn't worked properly in a hundred years, gathered dust in the dry air of a valley where none of it could function anyway.

He'd cleaned the kitchen twice. He caught himself sweeping a floor that was already clean, and made himself stop, and stood in the middle of the room holding the broom like a man who has forgotten what brooms are for.

It had been four years since he'd asked anyone to dinner. Four years since the girl with the ink-stained fingers — a cartographer's daughter, passing through — had smiled at him across the market square and then, three weeks later, smiled at someone else in a town further down the road, because that was what happened to people in Emberdown. They passed through. The valley kept its residents safe and its visitors brief, and Sebastian had long ago stopped expecting anything else.

But Mara Venn had come to stay. She'd taken the little cottage on Tannery Lane eight months ago, set herself up as a bookbinder and restorer, and proceeded to become, with alarming efficiency, the person Sebastian looked for first whenever he entered any room. She had a laugh that arrived a half-second before she decided to let it. She argued with him about the proper way to shelve books and won. Last week, at the harvest market, she had held up a lopsided clay mug and said, "This one's yours, I've decided," and something in Sebastian's chest had quietly come loose after years of being fastened down tight.

So he'd asked her. Stammering, over a crate of apples, in a sentence that had taken two attempts and still ended badly. And she'd said yes before he finished, which was either kindness or eagerness, and he'd spent the intervening five days unable to decide which possibility frightened him more.

He lit the fire. He set the table — two plates, the ones without chips, then reconsidered and swapped one for a chipped plate because the unchipped pair felt like a statement he wasn't ready to make. He put bread on the board and the stew on to warm and went upstairs to change his shirt, and caught himself in the small mirror above the washstand looking like exactly what he was: a warden with dirt under his fingernails and his grandfather's jaw and an expression of barely contained panic.

"You tend walls for a living," he told his reflection. "Steady hands. Steady hands."

The mirror declined to comment.

* * *

She knocked at moonrise, exactly as arranged, and the sound went through the house like a struck bell.

For a moment he simply stood in the hallway with a dish towel over his shoulder, listening to the knock repeat — three raps, polite, slightly impatient — and feeling the strangest sensation: that the house had been waiting for this sound for six generations, and had finally heard it.

He opened the door. Mara stood on the step with wind-flushed cheeks and a bottle of plum wine cradled in both arms like a rescued animal, and her hair was coming loose from its braid in the way it always did, and she was smiling at him with the specific half-smile she saved for moments she suspected might matter.

"You look terrified," she said warmly.

"I'm not terrified. I'm — attentive."

"Mm." She handed him the wine and stepped past him into the hall, pausing to look up at the ceiling beams as if taking inventory. "Your house is bigger than mine. I'm deciding whether to hold that against you."

"Please don't. I already feel guilty about the floorboards."

They ate in the kitchen because the parlor felt too formal and the study was locked, and the stew was good and the wine was better, and somewhere between the bread and the second cup Sebastian stopped rehearsing sentences in his head and simply talked. About the stones, about the circuits, about the absurd bureaucracy of maintaining wards nobody could see. Mara listened the way she did everything — fully, leaning in, asking questions that proved she'd actually heard the answer to the previous question.

"So the stones are alive?" she said.

"Not alive. Aware, maybe. Grandfather called them *drowsy*. They sleep better fed."

"And your job is to feed them."

"My job is to make sure nothing ever happens." He turned his cup slowly on the table. "Fourteen years of training, and the sum of my life's work will be that nothing happens. Forever."

"That sounds like the loneliest job in the world."

Sebastian looked up. She wasn't teasing. Her eyes were dark and steady in the firelight, and there was something in her face — a kind of recognition, as if she'd read the sentence he'd never written down anywhere and was reading it back to him.

"It's not lonely," he said, which was a lie, and her expression said she knew it was a lie, and neither of them minded.

Later — much later, the fire burned low, the plum wine gone, the conversation having wandered through half the map of their lives — she asked to see the study. Just to see it. "A whole guild archive," she said, "and you keep it locked like a dragon. I restore books, Sebastian. It's practically sacrilege."

And he, warmed through and foolish with the ease of the evening, took the iron key from the mantel and unlocked the door.

* * *

The study smelled of paper and cold ash and time. Mara moved through it the way she moved through everything, fingertips hovering an inch above the spines, reading titles in whispers. Sebastian leaned in the doorway and watched her and felt the evening balanced on some fine point, some hinge, and did not yet know which way it would swing.

"This one's swollen," she said, drawing out a heavy volume bound in cracked black leather. No title on the spine. "Water damage, years ago. Poor thing." She carried it to the desk lamp. "May I?"

"It's just ledgers," he said. "Old maintenance records. Ward schedules. Honestly, it's the least interesting—"

Mara opened the book.

The lamplight caught the pages, and Sebastian saw her face change — saw the smile falter, saw her go very still, the way the stones went still when something pressed against the wards from outside.

"Sebastian," she said quietly. "What is this?"

He crossed the room and looked over her shoulder. The page was dense with old script, faded brown ink, the cramped hand of some long-dead archivist. And down the margin, beside a passage near the bottom, ran a line of writing that did not belong — newer, darker, in ink that seemed to sit *on top of* the page rather than in it, gleaming faintly the way wet ink gleams, though it was dry to any touch.

Two words. A name.

*Mara Venn.*

His own voice sounded far away when he spoke. "That's not possible."

"It's my name." Her finger hovered above it without touching. In the lamplight the letters had a faint luster, like embers under ash — and as they watched, the glow brightened, dimmed, brightened again. Rhythmic. Patient. Like breathing. Like something drowsy waking up.

"The wards," Sebastian said. His mouth had gone dry. "There's no magic in the valley. Nothing crosses. Nothing *can*—" He stopped. Looked at the glowing name. Looked at her face, pale and lit from below by a book that should have been dead paper in a dead room in a valley where nothing like this could happen.

Outside, far off in the hills, one of the boundary stones began — very softly, very faintly, beneath the threshold of hearing — to hum.

* * *

Neither of them moved for a long moment. The lamp hissed. The name glowed and dimmed and glowed, keeping its slow patient time, and Sebastian became aware that his heart had picked up the same rhythm, beating in time with the ink, and he hated it, and could not stop it.

"We should show Maren," he said finally. His voice came out steadier than he felt. "Tomorrow. First light. She'll know what—"

"Sebastian." Mara still hadn't touched the page. "Why does a hundred-year-old ledger know my name?"

"I don't know."

"Why does it *glow*, Sebastian? Nothing glows here. You told me yourself. Nothing crosses the wards."

"I know what I told you."

"Then either you were wrong," she said, very quietly, "or the wards are."

He reached past her — slowly, the way you reach toward a sleeping animal — and closed the book. The glow cut off. The room was suddenly, enormously ordinary: dust motes in lamplight, cold ash in the grate, the smell of old paper. Mara exhaled, a small shaky sound, and laughed — that laugh that always arrived before she decided to let it, except this time it arrived ragged at the edges.

"Well," she said. "That's one way to end a first date."

"Mara—"

"No, it's fine. It's fine. I restore old books for a living, I know what foxing looks like, I know what mold looks like, and I know that was neither of those things." She hugged her elbows, suddenly cold in the warm room. "Tell me again how nothing happens in this valley."

"Nothing happens in this valley," Sebastian said. "That's the whole point of it. That's the oath. We keep the walls, and the walls keep everything out, and in exchange—" He stopped. In exchange for what? His grandfather had never gotten that far. Or had gotten that far, once, late at night, in a voice Sebastian had been too young to remember properly. *In exchange, boy, the walls ask nothing of us. Pray they never learn to ask.*

"Sebastian?"

"In exchange for nothing," he finished. "It's a good bargain. It's always been a good bargain."

She looked at him for a long moment — looked at him the way she'd looked at the swollen book, assessing damage, estimating what could be saved — and then she nodded, briskly, and pulled her coat straight, and became practical the way she became practical when she was most afraid.

"First light, then. Your Elder Maren. And tonight—" she glanced at the closed book on the desk, sitting there innocently, radiating nothing, "—tonight I'm going to walk home very fast and lock my door and tell myself I imagined it. Agreed?"

"Agreed," he said. "I'll walk you."

"Don't be silly, it's four streets."

"Mara." He was already reaching for his coat. "I tend walls for a living. Humor me."

* * *

They walked through Emberdown under a high thin moon, and the town was asleep and utterly ordinary, chimneys smoking, a dog barking two lanes over, the river muttering to itself in the dark. Sebastian matched her pace and said nothing and listened — really listened — to the valley around them, the way he'd been trained to listen, and underneath the ordinary sounds he heard it: the faintest tremor in the night, like a note held too long on an instrument, like something vast turning over in its sleep.

The stones were restless. All of them, he'd have wagered. All twelve, from the western circuit to the far pass, humming their low drowsy hum, and for the first time in his life Sebastian Ashgrove wondered whether the hum was contentment — or appetite.

At her door on Tannery Lane, Mara paused with her key in the lock.

"For what it's worth," she said, not quite looking at him, "this was the best evening I've had in years. Before the book. The book doesn't count. Books are never allowed to count."

"Agreed. Books are banned from counting."

"Good." She smiled — the real one, the one that arrived early — and then it softened into something more careful. "Get some sleep, warden. Tomorrow we find out what your walls have been keeping out."

She went inside. The latch clicked. Sebastian stood alone in the empty lane for a while, breath steaming, looking up at the stars over the Graywalls, and felt the valley breathe around him — slow, enormous, patient — and told himself, the way his grandfather had taught him, the way every Ashgrove had told themselves for a hundred years:

The walls hold. The walls have always held. The walls will hold tomorrow.

Above the eastern ridge, unseen, the moon was climbing toward midnight — and somewhere out beyond the wards, in the forest where the maps showed nothing, something old had just remembered a name.

*Mara Venn.*

The ink was still warm.
