## 2. A Girl Named in Ink

Mara Venn had rehearsed the walk to Sebastian Ashgrove's door three times that afternoon, and all three rehearsals had failed.

The first time she'd imagined herself arriving casual, one hand in her coat pocket, a smile that said *oh, this? I just happened to be passing by your house at the exact hour you invited me.* The second time she'd imagined herself punctual to the point of rudeness, standing on his step at six o'clock sharp like a woman delivering a summons. The third time she'd given up on imagination entirely and stood in front of her closet for forty minutes, holding the green dress against herself and then the blue one and then, absurdly, the wool sweater she wore to mend fences, before settling on the green because it made her eyes look darker and dark eyes felt right for an evening she couldn't quite predict.

She was twenty-six years old. She had faced down a bull elk in rut, a flooded ford with her truck's axles underwater, and a town council meeting about the mill tax. She could not remember the last time her hands had shaken over a *date*.

The valley didn't help. Emberhollow in the early evening was so beautiful it felt staged — the last light coming down the Graywalls in long amber ropes, woodsmoke rising straight up from the chimneys because there was never any wind worth mentioning, the lane stones still holding the day's warmth under her boots. Somewhere a fiddle was being badly played in the taproom of the Drowned Lantern, and someone was laughing at it. It was the kind of place where nothing had happened for a hundred years, and everyone agreed this was its finest quality.

Mara had lived here eleven years and had never once managed to believe it was boring. There were nights — she'd never told anyone this — when she woke at two or three in the morning with the absolute certainty that the valley was *holding its breath*. Not peaceful. Patient. Like a woman listening through a wall.

She shook it off. Tonight was not a night for listening through walls.

Sebastian opened the door before she knocked, which meant he'd been watching for her through the window, which meant he was nervous too, and the discovery of this settled something in her chest like a warm stone dropping into cold water.

"You're early," he said.

"You're staring," she said.

"I'm — yes." He rubbed the back of his neck, a gesture she'd catalogued months ago the way a birdwatcher catalogs a rare warbler: *Ashgrove, Sebastian; distinguishing marks include chronic sincerity and an inability to lie with his face.* "Come in. Mind the — there's books. Everywhere. I did try to tidy."

He had not tidied. He had, at best, negotiated a truce with the clutter: towers of volumes along the study walls, field journals stacked like cordwood, brass instruments she couldn't name hanging from hooks, a long table under the window buried beneath maps drawn in ink gone brown as old tea. The whole room smelled of paper and lamp oil and, faintly, of the boundary stones — that cold mineral smell she'd noticed on him the first time they'd spoken, down by the millrace, when he'd been chalking sigils onto a waystone and she'd asked him what would happen if she touched it and he'd looked genuinely alarmed and said, *please don't,* and she'd thought: *well. There's a man who means what he says.*

"Can I get you wine?" he asked. "I have wine. I also have cider, if wine is too — I don't actually know what kind of evening this is yet."

"It's a wine evening," Mara said. "Definitely a wine evening."

He poured with the concentration of a man defusing something, and they ended up on the bench by the hearth because the chairs around the table were all hosting books, and the fire caught properly, and somewhere between the first glass and the second the awkwardness did not vanish exactly but changed shape — became something softer, almost companionable, the awkwardness of two people discovering they were nervous about the same thing.

"So," she said, tucking her feet up under her. "Tell me the thing you can't tell anyone else."

He blinked. "What thing?"

"Whatever it is. Everyone in this valley has one. Old Hollis at the forge has his dead wife's ring he talks to. The Widow Crane has her son who left and never wrote. You—" she gestured at the room, the whole silent archive of him, "—you have whatever all this is. The guild nobody remembers having members. Go on. I'm a good keeper of secrets. Ask anyone. Ask no one, actually, because then they'd know I keep them."

Sebastian laughed — a real laugh, startled out of him — and she counted it as the evening's first victory.

"It's not much of a secret," he said, turning his glass. "It's more of a... chore. A holy chore." He nodded toward the maps. "My family has kept the valley's wards for six generations. The boundary stones. There are ninety-one of them, and every one has to be walked, checked, re-chalked, and sung to — actual singing, don't make that face — on a schedule my grandfather set before I was born."

"Sung to," Mara repeated. "What do you sing?"

"Old words. Half of them don't translate anymore." His voice had shifted into a register she hadn't heard from him before, lower, careful, the voice of a man walking on ice he knew personally. "The point of it all is simple, though. Nothing crosses the boundary. Nothing comes in, nothing goes out. No magic. The valley is..." He searched for the word. "*Quiet.* That's the whole art of it. Keeping things quiet."

"And outside the valley isn't quiet?"

Something crossed his face then — quick, like a fish under water. "Outside the valley," he said, "there used to be a kingdom called Sable."

"There's no kingdom called Sable. I've read every map in the schoolhouse."

"No," he agreed quietly. "There isn't."

The fire popped. Mara found she had stopped breathing in the ordinary way and started doing it deliberately, one breath at a time.

"What happened to it?" she asked.

"That's the strange part. Nobody knows, because—" he turned his hand palm-up, an open question, "—there's nothing to know *with*. No ruins people can find twice. No histories. People who go looking come back with their notes full of blank pages and a headache and a strong desire to talk about something else. My grandfather said the kingdom wasn't destroyed. It was *unwritten*." He smiled, apologetic, aware of how it sounded. "Which is why we keep the walls. Whatever unwrote it might still be hungry."

Most people, Mara thought, would have laughed at this point. Made a joke about old families and their ghosts. But she had woken at three in the morning her whole life feeling the valley hold its breath, and instead of laughing she heard herself say:

"Show me."

"Show you what?"

"The book. The one with the old words. The one you're not supposed to show anyone." She held out her hand, palm up, the same open question he'd made. "You've told me the secret. Now show me the proof. Fair's fair."

For a long moment he just looked at her, and the firelight moved in his eyes, and she understood suddenly that he was deciding something enormous — that behind the mild, ink-stained, chronically sincere face of him, some sixth-generation warden was weighing a scale only he could see. Then he exhaled, set down his glass, and stood.

"All right," he said. "But you have to understand — most of these books aren't dangerous. They're just old. One of them is different."

* * *

The prophecy book lived in a cedar chest beneath the floorboards behind his desk, wrapped in oilcloth, wrapped again in linen, and bound — when the wrappings came away — in leather so dark it was nearly black, gone soft as deerhide with age. There was no title on the cover. There was a single mark pressed into the leather like a brand: a circle, broken by a vertical line.

"Ashgrove seal," Sebastian said. "Every warden's oath book carries it. This one's the oldest. Grandfather called it the Root Book. He said it came across the mountains with the first wardens, the night Sable went out like a candle." He hesitated, hands resting on the cover without opening it. "I've read it maybe four times in my life. It doesn't like being read. The pages fight you."

"Books don't fight people, Sebastian."

"This one does."

But he opened it, and Mara leaned in over his shoulder, close enough to smell the cedar and the dust and something underneath both — a smell like rain about to fall, like the air going green before a storm — and saw pages of dense, spidery script in an alphabet half-familiar, letters that kept almost resolving into words she knew and then sliding away, like names remembered on the tip of the tongue.

And then the lamp flame bent, though there was no draft.

And then the ink began to glow.

It started low, a warmth in the paper like embers breathing, and spread outward from the center of the page in slow veins of gold, and the sliding, unresolvable letters began — line by line, as if the book were remembering how — to make sense. Mara felt Sebastian go rigid beside her. Felt his breath stop entirely.

"Mara," he said. Very quietly. The way you say a word when you're afraid of waking something.

"I see it," she whispered.

Because she did see it. In the middle of the page, in a script older than the others, larger, written with a heavier hand — as if whoever had written it had pressed hard, had wanted it *found* — stood a passage that glowed like a window lit at night in a dark country. And the language of it, impossibly, was plain. Was common. Was the tongue of Emberhollow market-day and mill-tax meetings and everything ordinary, and it said:

*When the walls grow old and the last warden walks alone,*

*a girl shall cross the threshold wearing a borrowed name,*

*and the land that was unwritten shall taste her and remember hunger.*

*Mara Venn.*

Her own name. Her own name, in a book older than the valley, older than her grandmother's grandmother, glowing gold as harvest light — and beneath it, smaller, in a different ink, a second line that seemed almost an afterthought, almost a mercy, except that afterthoughts in old books were never mercies:

*and the boy who forgets shall pay the door in everything he loves.*

The lamp guttered. The gold light pulsed once, twice — patient, rhythmic, like a heartbeat, like something asleep turning over — and Mara became aware that she was gripping Sebastian's arm hard enough to leave marks, and that he was gripping hers, and that neither of them had moved or spoken or breathed for what felt like a full minute.

"That's not possible," she said. Her voice came out steady, which surprised her; inside, everything was tilting, the whole level world of the valley listing sideways like a deck in weather. "That's — Sebastian, that's my *name.*"

"I know."

"Books don't know my name. Books written a hundred years ago don't know my name. I wasn't — I wasn't even a *thought* a hundred years ago, my family came to Emberhollow when I was fifteen, we came for the mill work, there's no reason, there's no *reason*—"

"Mara." He closed the book. The gold light died between the covers like a snuffed candle, and the sudden darkness of the room felt physical, pressing. "Look at me."

She looked at him. His face was pale in the firelight, but his eyes were steady, and it occurred to her with a strange, distant clarity that this was what he looked like when he was frightened and refusing to run — that she was seeing, on their first date, over wine, the true face of the last apprentice of the Ashgrove wardens.

"Nothing crosses the boundary," he said slowly. "That's the oath. That's the whole art. Nothing comes in." He swallowed. "But the walls are old, Mara. Older than my grandfather admitted. And a prophecy isn't a wall. A prophecy is a *door*."

Outside the window, far off in the eastern hills, the first bell of the watchtower rang the hour. Nine o'clock. Three hours until midnight. The sound rolled down the valley and faded, and in the silence after it Mara Venn sat very still on a bench beside a man she liked more than she'd planned to, in a room full of books that kept other kinds of silence, and listened — really listened, for the first time in eleven years — to the valley holding its breath.

Somewhere beneath the floorboards, wrapped in oilcloth and linen, the Root Book lay shut and warm as a sleeping animal.

And in the eastern forest, past the ninety-one stones, past the edge of every map, something old began, patiently, hungrily, to count the hours until midnight.
