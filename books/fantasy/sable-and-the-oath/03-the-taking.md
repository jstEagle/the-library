## 3. The Taking

The wards hummed all evening.

Sebastian had lived beside that sound his whole life — a low, patient thrum beneath the valley floor, like a hive sleeping under stone. Most people in Hollowmere stopped hearing it by the time they were ten. He never had. His father said it was because the Ashgroves had been listening to the boundary stones for six generations, and the stones had learned to listen back.

Tonight, on the eve of his first date in four years, the sound was wrong.

He noticed it while dressing, standing before the cracked mirror in his study with one arm through his coat sleeve. The hum had always been steady as a heartbeat. Now it stuttered. A hitch every few minutes, small as a skipped breath, so faint he might have imagined it — except wardens didn't imagine things about wards. Imagining was how wardens died.

He finished buttoning the coat anyway.

"You're being ridiculous," he told his reflection. The reflection, gray-eyed and hollow-cheeked in the lamplight, did not look convinced. "It's dinner. People have dinner. It's not a summoning."

The mirror offered no opinion. Behind him, on the desk, the prophecy book sat where it had sat for three days now — since Mara had seen her own name burning off the page like a brand. *Mara Venn.* Two words in ink older than the kingdom that had written them, glowing with a light that had no business existing inside a valley where magic could not cross.

He had tried everything to make it stop glowing. Salt. Iron. Prayers in the old warden's tongue that his father had taught him only half of, because the other half had died with the guild. The name had dimmed, eventually, but it hadn't gone out. It sat there under the closed cover like an ember under ash, waiting.

*Nothing crosses the boundary,* he reminded himself. *Not magic. Not prophecy. Not kings.*

That was the whole point of the valley. That was the oath.

He checked the clock over the mantel — seven-forty — swore softly, and left the book where it lay.

* * *

Mara was waiting at the foot of the mill bridge when he arrived, and the sight of her knocked something loose in his chest that he'd spent four years nailing down.

She'd worn the green dress. She was fidgeting with the strap of her bag the way she did when she was nervous, which meant she was nervous, which meant this was real and not some elaborate joke the universe was playing on the last apprentice of a dead guild. When she saw him she smiled — quick, crooked, relieved — and Sebastian forgot, briefly, about stuttering wards and glowing names and every other weight he carried.

"You're late," she said.

"I'm punctual. You're early."

"Both true." She fell into step beside him as they crossed the bridge, and their hands found each other without negotiation, the way they had on the walk home from his study three nights ago. Her fingers were cold. He warmed them without thinking about it, and she let him, and neither of them made it strange.

The Gilded Perch was the finest restaurant in Hollowmere, which was to say it had tablecloths and a wine list of exactly two wines. They took the corner table by the window, where you could see the lanterns strung along the millrace and, beyond them, the dark shoulder of the western ridge where the boundary stones stood their silent watch.

"Okay," Mara said, once they'd ordered. She leaned forward, elbows on the tablecloth, chin in her hands. "I've been thinking about the book."

"Mara—"

"No, hear me out." Her eyes were bright in the candlelight, and he recognized the look. It was the same look she'd had the first day he met her, at the archive, when she'd argued with him for an hour about the dating of a cartographer's seal and been completely, insufferably right. "You said the prophecy predates Sable. You said it was written before the kingdom was erased. Which means whoever wrote it couldn't have known me. Couldn't have known my name. So either it's coincidence — and names like Venn aren't common, Sebastian, I've looked — or..."

"Or," he said quietly, "the book is still writing itself."

The words came out of him before he could stop them, and he watched them land. Mara's smile flickered. For a moment the brightness in her eyes went somewhere colder, deeper, and he saw what lived underneath the scholar: fear, held carefully, the way you hold a bird so it can't feel your grip.

"That's the part that scares me," she admitted. "Because if it's still writing, then it's not remembering something. It's *planning* something."

"The wards held for a hundred years," Sebastian said. "They'll hold tomorrow too. Whatever that book wants, it can't reach you here. Nothing reaches anyone here. That's the entire reason this valley exists."

"And if the book is already *in* the valley?" she asked softly. "It's sitting on your desk right now. Glowing."

He opened his mouth to answer — to say something reassuring, something warden-like, something true — and the lamps went out.

All of them. Every lantern along the millrace, every candle on every table, the great iron lamp over the bar. Not blown out. *Snuffed*, all at once, like a hand closing over the valley's eyes.

Then the hum stopped.

For the first time in his life, Sebastian heard the silence underneath the world. It was enormous. It was the silence of a held breath, of a heart between beats, and every hair on his arms rose as thirty years of instinct screamed at him that the thing holding the valley together had just let go.

Around them, diners laughed nervously in the dark. Someone struck a match. Somewhere a child started to cry.

"Sebastian?" Mara's hand found his across the table. Her grip was tight. "What's happening?"

"I don't know." His voice came out flat and calm, the guild-trained voice, and he hated how steady it sounded when nothing was. "Stay close to me."

The match flared. In its light he saw the window — and the window was wrong.

Beyond the glass, where the night sky should have been, there was a seam. A line of pale gold light hanging in the air above the rooftops, thin as a paper cut, vertical, impossibly tall, running from the chimney-tops up past the ridge into darkness. It wasn't shining so much as *opening*. And around its edges, the night bent inward, the way water bends toward a drain.

Every person in the Gilded Perch saw it. He heard the screams start.

But Sebastian was looking at Mara, because Mara had gone rigid, and her eyes — reflecting the seam's gold light even indoors, even in the dark — were fixed on something he couldn't see. Her lips moved. No sound came out. And under the skin of her forearm, faint as veins of ore in stone, lines of light began to rise.

Ink. Glowing ink, tracing itself along her arms like script being written by an invisible hand.

"*No,*" Sebastian breathed. "*No, no — not here, not inside the wards—*"

The clock tower struck midnight.

On the first stroke, the seam widened. On the second, every glass in the restaurant shattered in unison, a bright rain of fragments that froze mid-fall — actually *froze*, hanging in the air like stars — and on the third stroke, the pulling began.

* * *

Later, Sebastian would try to describe it and fail. The closest he ever came was this: imagine the world is a page, and someone has begun to erase you. Not violently. Carefully. The way you'd lift a pressed flower out of a book.

The pull took hold of Mara first.

She cried out — not a scream, something smaller and more terrible, a sound of *recognition*, as if some deep part of her had always known this was coming — and her chair went over backward as the seam hauled her toward the window. Toward the sky. Through the wall, he realized distantly; the wall parted around her like smoke, brick and plaster going soft and translucent where she passed, because the ordinary rules of the valley were already coming apart.

Sebastian caught her wrist.

Her hand was cold — colder than it had been at the bridge, cold as the far side of winter — and the pull wrenched his shoulder nearly out of its socket as he dug his heels in. Around them the frozen room hung suspended: shattered glass like a constellation, spilled wine a red ribbon in the air, faces turned toward them in masks of horror, none of them able to move. Only the two of them moved. Only the two of them were named.

"*Hold on!*" he shouted, though whether to her or himself he couldn't say.

"I'm trying—" Her voice tore apart in the wind that wasn't wind. The golden script had climbed her throat now, her jaw, the edge of her cheekbone, and where it touched her skin her outline blurred, as if she were becoming less solid, less *herself*, more a character than a person. "Sebastian — the book — it's *reading* me—"

"Look at me." He hauled her closer, arm screaming, boots skidding on flagstones that had gone smooth as ice. "Mara. Look at *me*. Not the light. Me."

Her eyes found his. They were terrified and furious and achingly alive, and in them he saw the exact moment she understood what he was doing — the last apprentice of the Ashgrove wardens, alone, holding the line the way six generations of his family had held it, except the line was a girl's wrist and the enemy was the sky.

"It won't let go," she said. "I can feel it. It's not angry, Sebastian, that's the worst part — it's *hungry*. Like it's been waiting a hundred years and I'm the first meal—"

"Then it waits longer!" He got his other hand around hers, both fists locked, and threw his weight backward. For one impossible second she slid toward him — an inch, two inches, her body dragging free of the pull — and hope flared in him so hot it hurt. "Wardens don't let go! Do you hear me? We don't—"

The fourth stroke of midnight fell.

And the valley's wards — the great, patient, hundred-year hum that had kept Sable's ghost-country out and its people safe — broke.

He felt it go. Everyone in the valley must have felt it, asleep or awake: a soundless detonation rolling outward from the boundary stones, a century of woven protection unraveling in a single instant like a sweater pulled by its thread. The silence underneath the world rushed in to fill the space the hum had left, and in that silence Sebastian heard — felt — *understood* something vast and old turning its attention toward Hollowmere. Toward the book on his desk. Toward the girl named in its pages.

Toward him.

The pull redoubled.

Mara's hand ripped halfway out of his grip — he caught two fingers, then lost one, then caught the heel of her palm with his thumb and held, nails biting deep enough to leave marks he would later find on his own skin, crescent-shaped, the only proof any of it happened. She was sliding again, out through the broken window frame, up off the ground, her feet leaving the earth, her hair streaming toward the seam like water finding a fall.

"*Don't follow me!*"

The words ripped out of her, raw, and her free hand stretched toward his face as if she could press the plea into him physically, make it take root where arguments couldn't.

"Sebastian — listen to me — whatever it costs to come after me, it's worse than you think — the toll, remember, the ferryman, the *king* — don't—"

"I'm not letting you go!"

"You already are!" And she was crying now, gold-lit tears cutting bright tracks down her face, and she smiled at him through it — that crooked, brave, infuriating smile — and said, softer, almost gently: "Let go. Please. I don't want the last thing you lose to be your hand."

His fingers were slipping. He could feel each one going, joint by joint, the pull peeling her away from him with the patience of tide taking sand. He thought — wildly, desperately — of the oath scroll in the guildhall, of his father's voice reciting the words, *we keep the boundary, we do not cross it, we do not trade what we are for what we want.* He thought of the empty chairs in the warden's hall. Of being the last. Of how the word *last* had always sounded like an ending and tonight, suddenly, sounded like a choice.

He chose.

He threw himself forward off the ground, both hands locking around her wrist, and for a heartbeat — one golden, frozen heartbeat, with the whole suspended world watching — he was airborne with her, rising, the rooftops dropping away beneath his boots, the seam's light washing over them both like the opening of an eye—

Something struck him.

Not a hand. Not a wall. Something older and more impersonal — the valley itself, perhaps, the last reflex of a dying ward, or the weight of a hundred-year oath finally enforcing itself — and it hit him like the whole ridge falling on his chest. His grip tore open. His back slammed into the millrace wall, stone cracking behind him, and the world tilted, spun, righted.

He was on the ground. Alone.

Above him, Mara hung in the mouth of the seam, silhouetted against the gold, growing smaller. Her hand was still reaching for his. Even now. Even as the light took her.

"*SEBASTIAN!*"

The seam snapped shut.

The sound it made was quiet — a soft, papery whisper, like a book closing — and somehow that was the cruelest part. All that light, all that tearing, all that screaming, ending in a whisper. The frozen glass rained down. The spilled wine hit the floorboards. The lamps relit themselves one by one, flame by flame, until the Gilded Perch glowed warm and ordinary again, and thirty stunned faces turned from the window to the man lying broken-backed against the wall with his hands still curled around the shape of a girl's wrist.

Sebastian didn't see any of them.

He was staring at the sky, at the place where the seam had been, at the clean and empty dark where Mara Venn had stopped existing. His palms were bleeding where her nails had cut him. His shoulder was dislocated and he barely felt it. And through the roaring in his ears, faint but unmistakable, the valley's hum resumed — steady, patient, indifferent — as if nothing had happened at all.

As if the wards had done exactly what they were built to do.

He laughed. It came out of him broken, one harsh note, and then he was on his feet and running — through the shattered restaurant, out into the street, up the hill through the dark with blood dripping from his hands — to the guildhall, to the study, to the book.

The prophecy book lay open on his desk. He had left it closed. He knew he had left it closed.

Two new lines burned on the page beneath Mara's name, the ink still wet, still glistening:

*The girl who enters shall be taken at midnight's stroke.*
*The boy who follows shall forget all that he loves.*

Below them, in smaller letters, letters that seemed to be forming even as he read, letter by letter, like a hand writing in haste:

*The Door opens in nine days.*

Sebastian stood in the ruins of his quiet life and read the words twice, three times, until they stopped swimming. Then he wiped the blood from his palms onto his ruined coat, straightened his dislocated shoulder with a sound like a branch snapping, and reached for the lamp.

Nine days.

Outside, far to the west, on the dark shoulder of the ridge, one of the ancient boundary stones began — very softly — to sing.
