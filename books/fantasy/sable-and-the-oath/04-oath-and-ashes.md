## 4. Oath and Ashes

The elder's house smelled of tallow smoke and old paper, the way it had smelled for as long as Sebastian could remember — which was now a wound in itself, because he kept thinking of how Mara had wrinkled her nose at it the first time he'd brought her past the boundary stones, three weeks ago, laughing that he lived like a monk who'd lost his faith.

He stood in the doorway of Warden Hollis's study with rain still dripping off his coat, and he said it again, because the first time hadn't worked.

"She was taken at midnight. The wards failed. I watched her go through."

Hollis did not look up from the ledger he was annotating. He was a small man made smaller by age, his spine curved like a drawn bow, his hands spotted and precise. Ninety-one years old, and the last of the elders who had been alive when the wards were raised. The last of anyone, really, who remembered Sable as more than a story.

"The wards did not fail," Hollis said. "They were opened. There is a difference, boy, and the difference will matter to you later, though I pray it won't."

"Then open them again. Let me follow."

"No."

The word fell into the room like a stone into a well. Sebastian heard it drop all the way down.

"Hollis—"

"You will not say that name to me again tonight." The old man set down his pen with terrible care, aligning it parallel to the ledger's edge, and only then lifted his eyes. They were pale, almost colorless, the eyes of a man who had spent a century looking at things that eroded whatever looked back. "Sit down, Sebastian."

"I'll stand."

"Sit *down*." It was not anger. It was something older and sadder than anger, and it put Sebastian in the chair before he understood he'd moved. "Now. Tell me exactly what you saw. All of it. Leave nothing out because you think it sounds mad. Madness is the native language of this guild; we are fluent."

So Sebastian told him. The date — their first real one, dinner at the tavern on the green, her stealing half his bread and calling it a tax. The walk home under a sky gone strangely clear after days of rain. The moment at his door when she'd stopped, tilted her head like a bird, and said, *Do you hear that?* And then the sound — a low seam-splitting hum, like the world being unzipped — and the light opening above the lane, vertical, thin as a paper cut, wide enough for a person.

And Mara's name rising off her skin like heat shimmer. He hadn't imagined it. He would swear it on anything: for one full second her name had been visible, written in light across the air where she stood, as though something very far away had read it aloud and the world had obeyed.

"And you held her hand," Hollis said quietly, when Sebastian finished.

"I held her hand until—" His voice cracked and he hated it, hated the wetness in his eyes, hated the way grief made him feel like a child in this room full of dead men's furniture. "Until there wasn't a hand. Until the light closed and my hand was holding nothing but night air, and the wards came back up behind it like a door slamming, and I was standing in the lane like an idiot shouting her name at the sky."

Hollis nodded slowly. Then he rose, joints crackling like green firewood, and went to the tall cabinet against the eastern wall — the one Sebastian had never once seen opened in nineteen years of apprenticeship. The key came out from around the old man's neck on a cord black with age.

Inside lay a scroll.

Sebastian had grown up on stories of the Oath. Every warden child had. *The Oath of the Ashgrove*, sworn by the twelve founders the night they sealed the valley: that no warden would pursue what Sable took, that no warden would cross into the ghost-country even to save a life, because the price of entry was not paid in blood or gold but in *self* — memory by memory, toll by toll, until nothing remained of the traveler but a body walking without a reason. That was why the kingdom had been erased rather than fought. You could not war with a country that ate the soldiers' reasons for fighting.

Twelve founders. Eleven oaths broken by death over the centuries, each warden's scroll burned at their funeral, the ashes mixed into the mortar of the boundary stones. One oath left. One scroll. Hollis was its last keeper, and Sebastian had always assumed — vaguely, the way children assume the sun will rise — that it would be his someday, a duty, a piece of paper in a box.

Hollis laid it on the desk between them.

It was smaller than Sebastian expected. A cylinder of vellum maybe eight inches long, bound in a band of lead, and even unopened it seemed to have weight beyond its size, the way a grave does. The seal was unbroken wax the color of dried blood.

"This is the last copy of the founding oath," Hollis said. "Everything else — the histories, the manuals, the maps of the old roads into Sable — those were burned generations ago, deliberately, so no one would be tempted. This survived because someone has to remember what we promised. Not what we promised *to do*. What we promised *not* to do." He turned the scroll slowly under the lamplight. "You want to know what happens to those who enter Sable. So listen, and I will tell you what my grandmother told me, and she had it from a woman who watched it happen."

Sebastian's hands had gone cold. "Tell me."

"The border takes a toll. Always. It is not negotiable and it is not kind. The first thing it takes is a face — someone you love, plucked out of your memory whole, so that you walk into that country already grieving something you can no longer name. And it does not stop at the border. Every mile inward costs another memory. Another face, another year, another love. The king sits at the heart of it like a spider, and everything the country eats, he eats." Hollis's voice never rose. That was the worst part. It stayed level and dry as dust, reciting arithmetic. "The few who ever crossed and came back — two, in all our records, and both within living legend — came back hollowed. One sat by our well for forty years trying to remember why she wept whenever she heard a fiddle. The other lasted a winter and walked into the river."

"Mara didn't cross the border," Sebastian said. "She was *taken*. Through the wards. That's different."

"It is different," Hollis agreed. "Which means she is not paying toll. Which means the king is keeping her *whole*. Do you understand what that means, boy? Nothing in that country keeps a thing whole unless it intends to use it. She was named. She was named in ink, in prophecy, a hundred years before she was born, and the king has been starving in his ruined court for a century, and now something has arrived that he believes he needs intact." The old man's pale eyes fixed on him. "Every hour she is in there, she is in the hands of a creature that eats souls the way you and I eat bread. And every hour *you* spend chasing her, you are feeding yourself to it one memory at a time. If you go in after her, you will find her — perhaps. And if you find her, and some miracle lets you bring her out, you will not know why you bothered. You will look at her face and feel nothing, because the country will have eaten every reason you had."

The lamp hissed. Rain ticked against the window. Somewhere outside, the valley lay quiet under its wards, safe and green and utterly indifferent, and Sebastian thought: *this is the cage too.* It had always been the cage. Safety and loneliness built of the same stones.

"There has to be another way," he said.

"There is not."

"There's always—"

"*There is not.*" For the first time Hollis's voice bent, and beneath the bend Sebastian heard something that stopped his breath: not authority. Grief. Ancient, worn-smooth grief, the kind that has been handled so long it fits the palm perfectly. "I told you the wards were opened, not failed. I know because I felt them open, boy. I am the last living anchor of that working. When the seam cut the night, I woke with my own heart hammering and the taste of ash in my mouth, and I knew — the way you know a bone is broken — that the oath had been invoked. Something on the other side called in a debt written a hundred years ago, and the wards, which are themselves an oath, had no choice but to honor it." He pressed both spotted hands flat on the desk. "Which means the prophecy is moving. Which means it cannot be argued with, only endured. My grandmother endured it. Her mother endured it. We buried eleven wardens who spent their lives waiting for this night, praying it would come after they were dead, and now it has come while I am alive, and I am telling you with everything left in me: *do not cross.* Grieve her. Mourn her properly, here, in the world that remembers her. That is the oath. That is all the oath has ever asked."

Sebastian looked at the scroll.

He looked at it for a long time.

*Grieve her properly.* As if grief were a room you could sit in. As if it had chairs. He tried to imagine it — the funeral-that-wasn't, the empty chair at the tavern, the years stretching ahead of him in this valley where nothing ever happened, tending stones, teaching no apprentice, growing old around a hole shaped exactly like a woman who stole his bread and called it a tax. He tried to imagine being ninety-one like Hollis, hollowed not by magic but by simple endurance, and knowing that somewhere on the other side of the sky a king was wearing her memories like jewelry.

He couldn't do it. The imagining refused. Some part of him simply would not pick up the pen and sign that future.

"Hollis," he said. His voice came out strange and calm, and he recognized the calm the way you recognize a fever — from the inside, too late. "Thank you for telling me the truth. Most people would have lied to stop me."

The old man went very still. Ninety-one years of reading people will make you fast, and Sebastian saw him arrive at understanding in the space between one breath and the next — saw his eyes flick to the window, calculating whether he could reach it, whether shouting would bring anyone, whether there was anyone left to bring.

"Sebastian," Hollis said softly. "Boy. Don't."

"I'm sorry."

"Don't make me watch this. I have watched enough."

"I know." And he meant it — God, he meant it, and it changed nothing. "But you told me yourself. The wards honor oaths. So I'm going to give them a different one."

He picked up the scroll.

It was warm. That was the detail that would stay with him afterward, the absurd human detail: the vellum was warm, like a sleeping animal, like a hand. Lead band and blood-colored wax and a hundred years of waiting, and underneath it all it was just warm.

Hollis did not lunge for it. Sebastian would think about that later too — how the old man simply closed his eyes, and bowed his head, and looked, in that moment, less like a guardian and more like a man standing at a graveside he had dug himself.

"The hearth is lit," Hollis said quietly. "Of course it is. It's always lit. Go on, then."

Sebastian carried the scroll to the fireplace. The flames were low and orange, patient. He broke the lead band — it snapped easier than he expected, a hundred years of tension letting go all at once — and cracked the wax, and unrolled the vellum just enough to see the first line of the founding script, the words every warden child learned before they learned their letters:

*We swear the valley shall keep what Sable cannot reach, and we shall keep the valley, and we shall not follow.*

"We shall not follow," Sebastian read aloud. His voice shook. "I'm breaking it. I'm sorry. I'm sorry to all twelve of you. But you wrote this oath to protect people, and there's a girl in that country right now, and she is the person, and the oath can go to hell."

He fed it to the fire.

It caught slowly, the way old things burn — reluctant, then ravenous. The script blackened and curled and the words he'd known his whole life peeled upward into flame, and the heat of it hit his face, and he stood there watching a hundred years of law become light and ash, and he did not look away, because it deserved to be watched. Because someone should witness it.

Behind him, Hollis spoke one last time, and his voice was steady again, and somehow that was the kindest thing he could have done.

"When you pay the toll," the old man said, "and you lose the first face — you will feel the shape of the loss without the content. Like a tooth gone from your jaw. Don't stop to mourn it. Mourning is how it takes the ones beside it. Keep moving. Keep her name in your mouth like a stone under your tongue. Names are the last thing to go." A pause. "And boy — if you find her — tell her the valley is sorry. The valley has been sorry for a hundred years."

Sebastian turned. "You're not going to stop me?"

"I stopped you as long as I could." Hollis smiled, and it was a terrible, beautiful thing, like frost on a window. "Besides. Someone has to tend the stones while you're gone. And someone has to leave the lamp lit for whoever comes home." He looked into the fire, at the last flakes of ash settling. "Go through the north ford. The wards are thinnest where the river crosses. And Sebastian — the toll gate doesn't announce itself. You'll know it when you're standing in it. Whatever it asks for first, don't hesitate. Hesitation is how it chooses *for* you."

* * *

The north ford lay a mile upriver, where the water ran shallow over white stones and the pines crowded close to the bank. Sebastian walked it in the dark with no lantern, because he knew the path the way he knew his own heartbeat, and because some animal part of him wanted to memorize everything — the smell of wet pine, the cold coming off the water, the particular silence of the valley at three in the morning — hoarding it against what was coming.

At the ford he stopped.

The wards were invisible in daylight, a fact of the world like gravity. Here, at midnight, at the place where the river slipped under them, he could almost see the seam of their making: a faint shiver in the air, a wrongness in the way the starlight bent, the sense of standing at the edge of a held breath. Beyond it, the hills rolled away dark and ordinary toward the rest of the world. Somewhere out there, past mountains and borders and a hundred years of deliberate forgetting, the erased kingdom waited with his whole heart in its fist.

He thought about turning back. Honestly, he gave it its due: he stood in the freezing shallows and let the argument run its course. *You'll forget her anyway. You'll forget your mother's face at the border, per Hollis. You'll forget the guild, the valley, maybe your own name. You'll trade everything you are for a chance at a girl you've known a season.* And the argument had teeth, real teeth, and it gnawed at him —

— and then he thought of her laugh, the specific one, the surprised bark of it when he'd tripped over the tavern step, and the argument died, because you cannot argue with a fact.

He stepped across the line.

Nothing happened. That was the horror of it — no thunderclap, no barrier, no angel at the gate. Just cold water on his boots and the same stars overhead, and the valley behind him suddenly feeling less like home and more like a painting of home, viewed from outside the frame.

Then the air ahead of him folded.

It opened the way it had opened above the lane — vertical, thin, humming — but wider here, wide enough to walk through, and instead of light it showed darkness: a darkness with texture, with depth, a darkness that was not absence but *place*. Cold breathed out of it, smelling of stone and salt and something sweetly rotten, like fruit left too long in a golden room.

And at the threshold, where his boot hovered over the seam, something stirred — a pressure, a presence, patient and vast and politely attentive, like a customs officer in the dark.

A voice that was not a voice, that arrived fully formed inside his skull, said:

*A toll is owed.*

Sebastian stood at the border of the country with no name, water rising around his ankles, and answered it the only way he had left.

"Take whatever you want," he said. "Just let me keep her name."

The darkness considered him.

And then it reached into his chest, gentle as a thief, and took his mother's face.
