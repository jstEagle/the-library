## 5. The Country With No Name

Mara woke to the smell of rain that had not yet fallen.

She was lying on a hillside of grass so green it looked painted, and above her the sky was the color of a bruise healing — violet at the edges, gold at the center, with no sun she could find anywhere in it. The light came from everywhere and nowhere. It fell soft as dust through her fingers when she lifted her hand.

She remembered the date. She remembered Sebastian's study, the smell of old paper and cedar, his laugh when he knocked over his own teacup because he'd been watching her instead of it. She remembered the book — *the* book — and her name glowing on the page like a coal someone had breathed on. *Mara Venn.* And then midnight, and the wards screaming down like struck bells, and the seam of light opening in the air of a quiet valley where magic was never supposed to touch anything.

She remembered his hand around hers. That was the worst part. Not falling — being *pulled* — but the moment his grip tore loose, one finger at a time, and the last thing she saw wasn't Sable or prophecy or light.

It was his face, coming apart from hers.

"Okay," she said aloud, because silence felt dangerous here. Her voice sounded flat. The hillside drank it without echo. "Okay. You're alive. You're somewhere. Figure out the somewhere."

She stood. Her knees held. Her coat was still on — Sebastian's spare coat, actually, too big in the shoulders, smelling faintly of woodsmoke and the particular dust that collects in rooms where people read instead of live. She'd stolen it off his hook when they left for dinner because the night had turned cold and he'd insisted, flustered, that she take it. She'd teased him about it. He'd gone red to the ears.

The thought steadied her more than any deep breath could have. *He'll come after me. That stubborn, oath-keeping idiot will come after me, even if he has to burn something to do it.*

Especially if he had to burn something.

From the crest of the hill, she saw the kingdom.

It spread below her like a painting left out in weather: towers of white stone crowned with roofs of slate and copper gone green, bridges arcing over canals the color of hammered silver, terraced gardens spilling down hillsides in ordered waves of lavender and rose. A cathedral with a hundred spires. A palace at the far heart of it all, vast and pale as a knuckle of bone. It was beautiful the way a music box is beautiful — intricate, deliberate, complete.

And utterly silent. No smoke rose from any chimney. No bell rang, though bell-towers stood ready at every quarter of the city, ropes hanging slack. The canals were full and no boat moved upon them. Windows caught that sourceless golden light and threw it back, and behind every window there was either nothing at all or something standing very still.

"It's abandoned," she whispered, and then, correcting herself, because abandoned things look abandoned — broken, looted, collapsed — "No. It's *waiting*."

The grass beneath her feet hummed. Not audibly. She felt it through her soles, a vibration like a word held just under pronunciation. When she took a step, the hum changed pitch. When she stopped, it stopped.

She walked downhill toward the city, because there was nothing else to do, and because the alternative was standing alone on a hill until fear ate her from the inside out. The road she found was paved in white stone, each flag carved with a name — small names, worn names, *Tomas Merrow* and *Ilse of the Ninth Bell* and hundreds more, a whole street of gravestones laid flat and walked upon. Except the stones weren't worn by feet. They were worn by *weather*, decades of it, while the buildings around them stood pristine.

Someone had wanted these names remembered badly enough to carve them into the ground. Someone else — or something else, or time itself turned strange — had been erasing them anyway.

*Welcome to Sable,* said a voice in her head that was entirely her own, dry with terror. *Population: unknown. Attractions include: everything, forever, unattended.*

*

She met the first citizen at the mouth of a bridge.

He was an old man sweeping the bridge. That was the whole of it — an old man in a gray coat, sweeping white stones with a broom whose bristles were worn to threads, moving in long patient strokes from one end of the bridge toward the other. As she watched, he reached the far end, paused, turned around, and began sweeping back the way he'd come, wearing the same fresh attention he'd worn the first time, as though the work were new.

"Excuse me," Mara called, and hated how young her voice came out.

The man looked up. His face was kind and seamed and utterly unstartled, which frightened her more than alarm would have. People who aren't surprised by strangers have usually forgotten what strangers are.

"Good morning," he said warmly. "Or evening. One of those."

"Which is it?"

He considered the sky with real interest, the way one considers a painting of fruit. "I couldn't say. I don't remember starting this bridge." He gestured with the broom at the clean stones behind him. "But look how well I've done."

"You've swept it twice since I've been standing here."

"Have I?" He beamed, delighted, as if she'd told him he'd won a prize. "Well. I must love sweeping."

"Do you?"

The question landed on him visibly. She watched it travel through him like a stone dropped down a well — a long fall, no bottom reached. His smile flickered, held, reassembled itself. "I must," he said again, gentler now, the way you repeat a thing you've decided not to examine. "Otherwise why would I be doing it?"

"What's your name?" she asked.

"Tomas Merrow," he said promptly, and pointed at the nearest flagstone with his broom. "It says so. Isn't that convenient? Otherwise I'd have to make one up, and I'm told I'm bad at making things up."

Mara crouched. The carving was deep and old and softened at its edges, the letters going round with age. *Tomas Merrow.* Decades old. But the man before her couldn't be more than seventy, and his hands were wrong for a stonemason's memorial — they were living hands, ink-stained at the fingertips, a writer's or a clerk's.

"How long have you been here?" she asked carefully. "On the bridge. In this city."

"Oh," said Tomas Merrow pleasantly, "I've always been here. Or nearly. Yesterday is a bit foggy." He leaned on the broom and studied her then, really studied her, and for a moment something moved behind his eyes — a fish turning under ice. "You're new."

"Yes."

"You came from outside." He said it the way other men might say *you've seen the sea*. "Is it true there are places where tomorrow comes? Where people wake up and last week is still there, solid, like furniture?"

"There are," Mara said. Her throat hurt. "That's where I'm from."

He nodded slowly, absorbing this, and then his face did something terrible: it went smooth. Not blank — smooth, like water closing over a thrown coin. All the longing folded away into courtesy. "Well," he said. "Mind the ferryman. He charges."

"I heard there's a king here too."

Something happened to the light. Nothing she could point to — the sourceless gold didn't dim, didn't shift — but every shadow on the bridge deepened by one degree, and Tomas Merrow's broom stopped mid-stroke.

"Don't say that word," he said quietly. All the warmth was still in his voice, which made it worse. "Not out loud, in the open, where the air can carry it. Say *the gentleman in the palace*, if you must say anything. He hears his own name. It makes him hungry."

"Hungry for what?"

Tomas Merrow smiled at her, and behind the smile the fish under the ice thrashed once and went still.

"That's the trouble, dear," he said. "Nobody remembers what he eats. That's how we know it's happening."

*

She found the river because all roads in Sable bent toward it eventually — a great slow ribbon of black water running through the city's heart, so still it reflected the impossible sky perfectly, doubling the world. Boats were moored along its banks, slender black-hulled ferries with lanterns at their prows, and every lantern burned although no flame flickered in them. Light without heat, without motion, without consumption.

Like everything else here, they were waiting.

The ferryman sat in the first boat, mending a net he would never finish. He was younger than Tomas Merrow but older than her — hard to pin down, the way a figure in fog is hard to pin down — with a lean, weather-cut face, dark hair tied back with cord, and eyes the gray of the canal water. He saw her coming from fifty yards off and did not stop working.

"No passage," he said, when she reached the bank.

"I haven't asked for any."

"Everyone asks. Eventually." He bit a thread free. "No coin does anything here. No passage."

"I'm not trying to go anywhere. I'm trying to get *out*."

That stopped the needle. He looked up, and now she got the full weight of his attention, and it was considerable — the attention of a man who spends his life watching water for what floats in it. "Out," he repeated.

"There was a door. A seam. It opened and I fell through it and I want to go home. There has to be another seam. Another door. Something."

"There's the Door of Unmaking." He said it flatly, testing her reaction.

"That sounds promising."

"It isn't. It unmakes." He set the net down across his knees. "Everything that goes through it stops being here. Most folk think that means it goes elsewhere." A thin shrug. "Most folk also think yesterday exists. This is not a country that rewards optimism."

"Then tell me what it does reward," Mara said, "because I'm listening, and I'm not leaving, and your lanterns are lit for passengers that never come, which tells me some part of you is waiting for exactly this conversation."

For the first time, the ferryman's mouth moved in a way that was almost a smile. "You argue."

"When I'm scared."

"Hm." He studied her a while longer, then hooked the net on a peg and nodded at the seat opposite him in the boat. She climbed down; the black hull barely swayed, as if the water beneath it were thicker than water. Up close the lantern's glow was warm and did not warm.

"Ferryman's name is Corvo," he said. "I know that because it's stitched into my coat, not because I recall anyone giving it to me. Same trick as your friend on the bridge, I expect. We keep our names written down because keeping them anywhere else doesn't work." He tapped his temple. "In here, it's all low tide."

"What do you mean?"

"I mean yesterday is a rumor in Sable. Everyone woke up this morning — or everyone woke up *a* morning — knowing their name, their trade, the shape of their hands, and nothing else. Ask them their mother's face. Ask them why they sweep bridges or mend nets or light lamps. They'll smile at you and say they must love it." His voice stayed level, but something underneath it did not. "We're not haunted, girl. We're *gutted*. Everything heavy's been scooped out and the walls repainted."

"The king," she said slowly. "Tomas wouldn't say the word. The gentleman in the palace."

Corvo's gaze went past her shoulder, downriver, toward the distant bone-white bulk of the palace, and stayed there a beat too long.

"He eats memories," Corvo said. "To stay alive. To stay *himself* — whatever's left of himself. A kingdom's worth of yesterdays, poured down a dying throat, year after century after year. Every time he feeds, we lose a little more. First the faces of our dead. Then our dead themselves. Then why we loved them. Then that we loved them." He picked the needle back up, threaded it with the ease of ten thousand repetitions, and did not begin sewing. "Some nights I sit in this boat and try to remember my mother, and what I get is the *fact* of a mother, the way you get the fact of a room you've never entered. Door, window, a chair. No one in it. Ever."

Mara's arms prickled. "And if someone new arrives? Someone from outside, with a whole lifetime intact?"

The needle paused.

"Then she's a walking feast," Corvo said, "and she should be very quiet about it, and very quick, and she should pray to whatever gods survived outside that she finds her door before the palace finds her scent." He looked at her directly then, and his gray eyes were not unkind, which somehow made the next words worse. "Because he'll taste you on the wind already. New memory comes off a person like heat off a stove. And a starving man walks toward heat."

*

She made him take her across the river anyway.

It wasn't negotiation so much as erosion — she simply kept asking, in different words, with different reasons, until Corvo sighed the sigh of a man surrendering to weather and cast off the mooring line. The ferry slid onto the black water without sound. No oars, no current; the boat went where Corvo tilted his chin, as though the river were a well-trained animal.

"Why help me?" she asked, halfway across. "If I'm a feast on legs, why warn me, why ferry me?"

"Because you're going toward the Door," he said, "and everyone who goes toward the Door passes my stretch of water twice. Once coming, once—" he tilted a hand, ambiguous, "—however it ends. I've ferried six of you in forty years. Prophecy-people. Marked ones. The ones the seams spit out."

Her stomach dropped. "Six?"

"All heading for the same door. None of them came back across my river going the other way." He let that settle between them like ballast. "So no, I don't help you. I observe you. It's the only entertainment a gutted man gets."

"What were their names?"

"Didn't ask. Asking names is how you start missing people." He adjusted the tiller-that-wasn't. "Though the last one, a boy about your age, kept saying a word over and over the whole crossing, like a prayer. Took me years to work out what it meant, because I had to learn the language of the living again to do it." The lantern-light caught the side of his face. "He was saying *sorry*. To somebody who wasn't there."

The far bank came up out of the black water, and beyond it the ruined-and-perfect city opened like a hand, and somewhere inside it, past the cathedral and the silent bells and the streets of buried names, the palace sat waiting with its hunger.

Corvo brought the boat alongside and held it steady with one hand on a piling.

"One piece of advice, free of charge, because you argued with me and I respect that." His voice dropped, and the river seemed to lean in to listen. "Whatever you're carrying — whoever you're carrying — hold on to it. Not in your head. In here." He touched his own chest, flat-palmed, hard. "He eats from the head. The head is where memories live, tidy, labeled, easy to take. But some things sink lower than the head. Some things live in the body. In the hands. In the way you stand when you hear a certain laugh." His gray eyes held hers. "The ones he can't reach are the ones you don't remember so much as *are*. Get yourself a few of those, girl. They may be the only food he can't chew."

Mara thought of Sebastian's coat on her shoulders. Of his laugh, knocked loose by a falling teacup. Of his fingers tearing, one by one, out of hers.

"He'll come after me," she said. "And when he does, he'll pay whatever toll this country charges, because he's the sort of man who keeps oaths and the sort of fool who breaks them for the right person. So I need to find that door, Corvo. Fast. Before he pays it."

Corvo was quiet for a long moment. On the water behind them, the six empty lanterns of six empty boats burned on, lighting the way for no one.

"Then you'd better start walking," he said, and nodded upriver, toward the palace and past it, toward a horizon where the perfect city frayed into salt flats and a darkness with a doorway in it. "And you'd better walk fast. Because the sun never sets here, Mara Venn —"

She hadn't told him her name.

"— but the gentleman in the palace eats three times a day," Corvo finished softly, "and it's nearly supper."
