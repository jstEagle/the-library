## 7. The Ferryman's Debt

The river had no banks.

Mara noticed it first as a wrongness in her peripheral vision — the water simply *ended*, not at a shore but at a softening, as though the world beyond it had been sketched in pencil and then half-erased. She stood on a dock of blackened wood that creaked beneath her weight and looked out across a current the color of tarnished silver, and she understood, with the calm that had been settling over her since she woke in this place, that the river did not flow through Sable. Sable flowed through the river. Everything here was carried. Everything here was going somewhere else.

The ferry came when she stopped looking for it.

That was the trick of it, she was learning. The ruined city behind her had taught her the grammar of this country: doors opened for people who weren't waiting by them; bells rang only when unlistened-for; the dead walked openly because no one remembered to be afraid of them. So she turned her back on the water, studied the crooked spire of a cathedral whose roof had folded inward like a closing hand, and let her mind go quiet and empty —

— which was dangerous, she realized a heartbeat too late. Empty minds were what the king fed on.

*Don't think about him,* she told herself, which was, of course, thinking about him.

She thought about Sebastian instead. That was safer. Sebastian with flour on his jaw from the bread he'd burned on their first date, laughing at himself before she could. Sebastian's hand hovering an inch from hers across the table all evening, too polite to close the distance, until she'd closed it for him. She held those images up in front of her mind like lanterns, the way you'd hold a lamp against something circling in the dark.

The boat bumped the dock.

It was long and low, built of the same black wood, and it sat so deep in the water that its gunwale nearly kissed the surface. The man poling it stood amidships, and he was younger than his voice would later prove to be — thirty perhaps, with a face like a coin rubbed smooth, all features present but worn down to suggestion. He wore a coat that might once have been fine; now its buttons didn't match, three of them carved from bone, and he watched her with eyes the gray of old ice.

"You're the one from outside," he said. Not a question. News traveled strangely here, she'd gathered — or perhaps there simply wasn't much news.

"I'm looking for the Door of Unmaking."

Something moved behind his face. Not quite a flinch. More like a door opening onto a cold room and being shut again quickly.

"Everyone who comes here is," he said. "Mostly they're lying to themselves about why." He poled the boat closer, and the water made no sound around the hull, which unsettled her more than any noise could have. "You want passage."

"I want to get to the Door."

"That's what I said." He extended a hand, and after a moment's hesitation she took it and stepped down into the boat. It rocked once and went still, unnaturally still, like a held breath. "Corvo," he said. "I ferry. It's what I do. It may be the only thing I do."

"Mara Venn."

He repeated the name slowly, tasting it. "Venn. That's a real name. Outside names are real names." He said it with something that sounded almost like hunger, and she understood suddenly why the citizens of Sable had stared at her in the streets of the ruined capital — not because she was strange, but because she was *whole*. "Sit down, Venn. The river doesn't like standing passengers. It thinks they're trying to leave."

She sat. The bench was cold through her clothes. Corvo pushed off from the dock, and the black city began to slide away behind them, its broken towers reflected upside-down in water that never rippled.

"Which way is the Door?" she asked.

"There isn't a way. There's a direction, and then there's how long it takes to walk it, and those two things have very little to do with each other." He poled with slow, practiced strokes, though she could see no current carrying them and no bottom beneath them. "Seven days, if the country's feeling honest. Longer if it isn't. Sometimes shorter, which is worse."

"Why would shorter be worse?"

He glanced back at her. "Because when Sable gives you something cheap, it means it wants something expensive later."

* * *

They talked, in the way of two people trapped in a small space with nowhere else to put their attention. Or rather, she talked, and Corvo answered in fragments, each one pried loose like a stone from a wall.

He had been born in Sable. That much he offered freely. He'd been a ferryman since — he paused here, genuinely searching — "since the river needed one," he finished, which was no answer at all. He could not remember his mother. He could remember a song, four notes of it, and hummed them once without seeming to notice he'd done it. He could remember that he had once been afraid of the dark, because he was no longer, and the absence had a shape.

"Do you know what the king does?" she asked, when the light — such as it was; the sky above Sable was a perpetual bruised dusk — began to deepen toward whatever passed for night.

Corvo's pole stopped moving. For a moment the only sound was the silence of the water, which was somehow louder than any sound.

"He eats," Corvo said finally. "Memories. Yours, mine, anyone's he can catch. A king has to eat, Venn. Even a dying one. *Especially* a dying one." He resumed poling. "Sable wasn't always like this. There was a kingdom here once, a real one, with harvests and weddings and taxes nobody liked paying. Then the erasure came — your guild's doing, I'd guess, though don't ask me how I know that word, *guild*. Something reached into the world and pulled Sable out of it like a splinter. And the king refused to be pulled. He anchored himself here with everything he could swallow. Every memory he takes makes him a little more solid and Sable a little less."

"And the Door of Unmaking?"

"Is the way out. The only way. It unmakes what stands before it — that's the price, that's the whole price, and it's ruinous." His voice flattened. "People have tried to cheat it. To give it something small and slip past. The Door isn't stupid, Venn. It weighs what you offer against what you're carrying, and it knows the difference between a coin and a heart."

"How do you know all this if no one remembers anything?"

For the first time, Corvo smiled, and it was a terrible thing to see, because it was the smile of someone remembering that they used to smile. "Because I've ferried every soul who ever tried to reach it. And I watch their faces on the way back. Or I watch the empty boat come back alone." He looked at her over his shoulder. "You should sleep. The river dreams, and it's rude to interrupt."

* * *

She did not sleep. She lay in the bottom of the boat under a tarp Corvo threw her, watching the bruised sky, and she took inventory.

This was a habit she'd developed in the first days, after the ferryman's warning and the starving villages and the woman in the market who had asked her, kindly, desperately, *what happened yesterday?* — because the woman could feel the hole where yesterday should have been, the way you feel a missing tooth. Mara kept her memories written now, in a small notebook she'd found in the ruins of a schoolhouse, in ink she made from berry-stain. Each morning she wrote the date — guessing at dates, here — and each night she wrote what had happened, and she read it back until her eyes ached. Because the country ate memory the way water wore stone: slowly, invisibly, and always from underneath.

*Day one in Sable:* woke in a field of white flowers that were not flowers but teeth, small and milk-colored, growing from the earth. Ran. Found the city.

*Day two:* the market woman. Learned the rule: write everything down.

*Day three:* met the ferryman.

She touched the notebook through the fabric of her coat and felt steadier. Whatever this place took, it couldn't take ink. Ink was outside magic. Ink was hers.

But even as she thought it, a colder thought followed, the kind of thought that arrives already knowing it's true: *the notebook can't make you remember. It can only prove you forgot.*

* * *

On the second day, the river narrowed and the banks returned — sudden, abrupt, as though someone had remembered to draw them — and they passed through a drowned village. Only the rooftops showed, tilted at angles roofs shouldn't hold, and beneath the surface she could see pale shapes moving. Not fish. People-shaped. They drifted among the chimneys with their faces turned upward, and although the water distorted them, she could swear some of them were smiling.

"The forgotten dead," Corvo said quietly. "The ones nobody left to remember them. They sink here. The river keeps what the country won't." He poled wide around a church steeple that leaned out of the water like a drowning man's arm. "Don't look at their faces too long. Some of them are still hoping to be recognized, and hope, down there, has teeth."

One of the shapes rose as they passed — a child, or what had been a child, its hair drifting like weed. It pressed both palms flat against the underside of the surface, and Mara felt its attention on her like a physical pressure, a wanting so pure and enormous it made her chest ache.

"I'm sorry," she whispered, not knowing what else to say.

The shape sank. And in the silence afterward, Corvo said, without turning: "You'll want to make a bargain with me soon. Everyone does, by the second day. So let me tell you my terms before you dress them up pretty, because I've heard every version and the pretty ones bore me."

She waited.

"I'll take you to the Door of Unmaking. All the way. No detours, no selling you to the king's collectors, no vanishing in the night — you have my word, and my word is the only thing I own that hasn't been eaten." The pole dipped, rose, dripped soundlessly. "In exchange: one happy memory per day of travel. From you. Freely given. I take nothing you don't hand me, and I take it clean — the whole memory, root and branch, none of this fading-at-the-edges rot the country does. You'll lose it entire, and I'll have it, and we'll both know exactly what was traded."

Mara's mouth had gone dry. "Why happy ones?"

"Because sad ones taste like ash and happy ones taste like bread." He said it simply, without shame, and somehow that was worse than shame would have been. "I haven't tasted bread in longer than I can measure, Venn. That's not poetry. I mean it literally. I can't remember what I'm missing. I only know there's a hole shaped like it."

"And if I say no?"

"Then I put you off at the next bank and wish you luck, and the luck will be bad, because everyone who walks to the Door on foot meets the king's collectors inside three days." He finally turned to face her fully, and his worn-smooth face was unreadable as the water. "So. Seven days. Seven happy memories. That's the toll."

She thought of Sebastian. Of course she thought of Sebastian — he was the fixed point she navigated by, the north star of all this madness. Their date. The way he'd walked her home and stood on the step not-quite-leaving for eleven minutes, both of them pretending the conversation hadn't ended twice already. The way he'd said, *I've been dreading this evening going badly for a week, and instead I'm dreading it ending.* She had maybe a hundred memories of him, newly minted, precious as eggs.

Seven days. Seven of them gone. And the road ahead — the Door, the king, whatever the prophecy wanted from her — would take more. This country was a ledger, and it always balanced.

But the alternative was walking, and the collectors, and becoming one of the pale shapes drifting under a silver river, palms pressed to the surface, waiting for someone to recognize her.

"One condition," she said.

Corvo raised an eyebrow.

"When you take one — you tell me which one you took. Afterward. You describe it back to me, so I can write it down. So I know what's missing." Her voice shook slightly and she hated it. "I can bear losing them. I can't bear not knowing what I lost."

Something flickered across his face — that door opening onto the cold room again. "That's a cruel term," he said softly. "For you or for me, I'm not sure."

"It's the only one I have."

He looked at her a long moment. Then he spat in his palm and held it out, and she took it, because apparently bargains in Sable were sealed the old way, with salt and skin.

"Done," he said. "First payment due at sundown."

* * *

Sundown came the color of a healing bruise, purple shading to yellow at the horizon's seam. Corvo moored the boat against a stone jetty that jutted from a hillside covered in grass the gray-green of old bronze, and built a fire from wood that burned without heat — she held her hands near it anyway, out of stubbornness.

"Now," he said, sitting across from her. "Choose."

And that was the cruelty of it, she realized. He wasn't taking. She had to *give*. He was making her complicit in her own erosion, and he was doing it deliberately, because — she saw it now — he wanted her to understand what she was asking him to help her survive. Bargaining with erosion meant picking up the spoon yourself.

She closed her eyes and laid her memories out like cards.

There was the day she'd gotten her acceptance letter to the conservatory, and her father had lifted her off the ground and spun her, and they'd laughed like idiots in the driveway. There was the summer she and her cousin had caught fireflies in mason jars and then, weeping, released every single one. There was the first snow of her eleventh year. There was Sebastian — no. Not yet. Not him. Not on day one. She wasn't strong enough to spend him yet, and she suspected she'd need to hoard him like the last coins of a dying currency.

Her father, spinning her in the driveway. The letter shaking in her hand. The smell of cut grass and gasoline from the mower. That one. It was far enough back that losing it wouldn't gut her daily life — that was the lie she told herself — and bright enough to be worth eating.

"This one," she said aloud, because the bargain required speech. "When I was seventeen. My father. An acceptance letter."

Corvo nodded. He reached across the fire — she flinched, couldn't help it — and touched two fingers to her temple, feather-light.

It didn't hurt. That was the horror of it. It felt like a page turning. Like a breath let out. One moment the driveway was there, gold-lit, her father's hands at her waist, the letter crinkling in her grip — and the next moment there was a smooth blank space where it had been, and she knew *that* it had existed the way you know a room exists behind a closed door, but the door wouldn't open, and it never would again.

Tears slid down her face before she'd decided to cry. She wiped them angrily.

"Well?" she demanded. "Describe it. Those are the terms."

Corvo sat back. His eyes were wet too, which startled her more than the loss had. When he spoke, his voice was rough, wondering, like a man reading a letter from someone he'd thought dead.

"A driveway in summer," he said. "Cut grass. A man lifting a girl off her feet, spinning her, and she's holding a letter so hard it's creasing, and neither of them cares, because the letter says yes. It says *yes*, and the whole street smells like lawnmowers, and the girl thinks: *this is the happiest my body has ever been.*" He swallowed. "She was right. It was."

Mara fumbled out her notebook and wrote it down with shaking hands, transcribing his words, preserving the ghost of the thing inside the grave of the thing itself. When she finished, Corvo was looking at the fire.

"Bread," he murmured. "It tastes like bread."

* * *

They traveled six more days, and she paid six more times, and she learned the arithmetic of herself.

She gave the fireflies. She gave the first snow. She gave her mother teaching her to waltz in the kitchen, counting *one-two-three* with flour on her hands. She gave the night she and her college friends had sung on a rooftop until strangers joined them from three floors below. Each time, Corvo described it back to her, and each time his description was exact and tender and unbearable, and each time she wrote it in the book, and the book grew heavier, and she grew lighter, like a candle burning down in a room where someone else was warm.

By the sixth night she had learned something else, something she hadn't expected: the memories didn't vanish from the world when Corvo took them. They lived in him now. When he described the rooftop, he sang a bar of the song they'd been singing, note-perfect. When he described the waltz, his feet shifted, unconsciously, in the pattern her mother had taught her. He was becoming a museum of her, a walking archive of everything she was paying out, and she could not decide whether that made the bargain holy or monstrous.

On the seventh morning, the river ended.

Not gradually — it simply stopped, the silver water pooling into a wide, motionless lake at the base of cliffs so tall their summits were lost in the bruised sky. And set into the cliff face, half-buried in rockfall, was a structure: a doorway, vast and ancient, framed in stone carved with script that hurt to look at directly. It was sealed. Around its threshold, the air shimmered faintly, like heat over a road.

Beyond it, faint as a heartbeat, she felt something pull at her — the same pull that had dragged her through the seam in Sebastian's valley. The way home. The way to him.

"We're here," Corvo said. He sounded strange. Hollowed. Seven of her happiest moments sat in him like swallowed stones. "The Door of Unmaking. What's left of it."

"What do you mean, what's left of it?"

He pointed with his pole, and she followed the line of it to the base of the great sealed door — and her stomach dropped.

There were tracks in the dust below it. Many tracks. And among them, unmistakable even to her untrained eye, were the prints of boots that had come and gone recently, and beside them, pressed deep into the earth as though by something immensely heavy, a seal stamped again and again into the ground:

A crown, cracked down the middle.

"The king knows you're coming," Corvo said quietly. "He's known since the day you arrived. And Mara Venn — someone got here first."

She was still staring at the bootprints, trying to read them, trying to hope, when Corvo stiffened beside her and said, in a voice she'd never heard him use:

"Oh no. Oh, you poor fool. *Him?*"
