## 8. Reunion in Ruin

Sebastian had been walking through Sable for three days, and the country had already taken more from him than he could count.

He knew this because he kept a list. It was a trick one of the elder wardens had taught him — *the mind forgets, but ink remembers* — and so each night he sat against whatever ruin offered shelter and wrote down what was missing. Not what he remembered. What he didn't. The gaps were strange things: not black holes but smooth places, like river stones where memories used to be. He could feel their shape. He just couldn't feel what filled them.

The list read:

*My mother's face.*
*The smell of the tannery on Bridge Street.*
*What rain sounded like before I learned to fear it.*

Three lines. Three days. The tollgates of Sable were small and unremarkable — archways of white bone-lattice that stood in the middle of roads for no reason, humming faintly, waiting. You walked under them because there was no other road. And as you passed beneath, something reached into you with fingers made of cold, and it took, and you kept walking, and only later did you notice the smooth place where something used to live.

He had crossed four gates. The list should have had four entries. He suspected it had more, and that some of the missing things were too smooth now even to mourn.

Tonight he sat in the shell of a chapel whose god had a name no one in Sable could recall. Stained glass still arched overhead, depicting saints whose faces had been scraped away by centuries of forgetting — or perhaps by the country itself, which seemed to resent the idea that anything should be held fixed. Outside, Sable's capital sprawled down a hill toward a dead harbor: a city of pale spires and leaning towers, beautiful the way a drowning person is beautiful, all grace and desperation. Lanterns burned in windows though no one Sebastian had met could say what day it was. The citizens smiled at him in the streets. They smiled at everyone. They had nothing else to offer strangers, having spent most of themselves staying alive.

He turned to a fresh page and wrote at the top, in careful warden's script:

*MARA.*

Below it: *Find her before the king does. Before the gates take the reason I came.*

Because that was the terror he hadn't written yet, the one that sat in his chest like swallowed ice: that he would walk through gate after gate after her, paying and paying, until one morning he found her standing in front of him and felt nothing at all. That the country wouldn't need to kill his love. It would simply invoice it, gate by gate, until the account ran empty.

A sound below — footsteps on the chapel's broken stair, quick and uneven, the gait of someone exhausted and trying to hide it.

Sebastian was on his feet before thought caught up, hand going to the warden's knife at his belt. Then the footsteps rounded the pillar, and the knife fell from his hand and rang off the stone floor, and he didn't hear it land.

Mara stood in the ruined doorway with a stolen lantern held high, its light catching the dust motes around her like a swarm of tiny stars. Her hair was tangled. There was a tear in her sleeve and a bruise darkening along her jaw, and she was thinner than she'd been five days ago at the table in his study, laughing at his terrible attempt at conversation over wine he'd bought specifically because the label looked expensive.

She was the most real thing he had seen since midnight took her.

"Sebastian," she said, and her voice cracked clean down the middle.

He crossed the chapel in six strides and then stopped, suddenly afraid, hands hovering an inch from her shoulders like a man afraid the vision would scatter. "You're—"

"If you say *real*, I'm going to hit you," she said, and then she hit him anyway, a soft blow against his chest that turned into both fists gripping his shirt, and then she was crying into his collar and he was holding her so tightly he could feel her heartbeat hammering against his ribs, answering his own.

"You're here." She pulled back just far enough to look at him, searching his face with the lantern-light between them. "How are you *here*? The wards — the valley — the oath—"

"I burned it."

"You *what*?"

"The oath scroll. Elder Halloway told me what the tolls were. Told me not to come. So I burned the last copy of the Ashgrove oath and walked out through the boundary stones while they were still screaming at me." He laughed, and it came out ragged. "There's no guild anymore, Mara. There's just me. I'm the whole order now, and my first act as its entire membership was heresy."

Something moved across her face — wonder, and underneath it, fear. "The tolls. How many gates did you cross?"

"Four."

"And you paid?"

"It's not optional. Believe me, I asked."

Her hands were still fisted in his shirt. He watched her go very still, the way she went still when she was working something out — he'd seen it across the dinner table, when she'd caught him lying about liking her favorite band. "What did they take?" she asked quietly.

"Things I won't miss until I do." He tried to make it light. He failed. "My mother's face. I know I had one. I know I loved her. There's just—" he touched his sternum, "—a smooth place. Like a stone in a river. I can feel the shape of her absence."

Mara stepped back.

It wasn't much. Half a step. But he felt it like a door closing, and when he looked at her face the joy there had curdled into something older and colder.

"Your mother," she said. "Lila Ashgrove. Who taught you to bind your first ward when you were nine. Who made honey bread every solstice and burned it every solstice and we ate it anyway." Her voice climbed. "Who cried at your graduation and pretended she hadn't. Sebastian, I met her *once* and I remember her better than you do right now."

"Mara—"

"No. No, look at me." She grabbed his face in both hands, hard, forcing him to meet her eyes. "Describe her to me. Right now. Describe your mother."

And the horrible thing, the thing that made his stomach drop through the chapel floor and into the dead country beneath it, was that he tried. He genuinely tried. He reached for Lila Ashgrove and found warmth without features, love without a face, a voice like wind through a keyhole. "Brown hair," he said slowly. "I think? She was — kind. She was kind, I know that the way I know north. But her face is—" His breath shuddered. "It's gone. It's just gone."

Mara let go of him like his skin had burned her.

"That's what it does," she whispered. "That's what Corvo told me. The king eats memory to stay alive, and the whole country is his mouth. Every gate is a tooth." She wrapped her arms around herself. "I've been paying too. One happy memory per day of travel — that was Corvo's price for guiding me. I gave him the lake behind my grandmother's house. I gave him my sixth birthday. I gave him—" her voice broke, "—I gave him the night my father taught me to skip stones, and I can't even remember giving it, which is the cruelest part. The memory of losing the memory is all that's left."

"So we're even," Sebastian said. "We're both—"

"We are not *even*." She whirled on him, and the lantern threw her shadow huge and shaking up the chapel wall. "Don't you dare make this symmetrical. I've been paying as I go, one piece at a time, trying to hold onto enough of myself to reach the Door. You—" she jabbed a finger at his chest, "—you crossed four gates in three days. Four. Do you understand what you're doing? You're spending yourself like coins. Like the tolls don't matter because the prize is me, and I am telling you, Sebastian, I am *begging* you, I am not worth becoming a hollow man for."

"Don't." The word came out flat and dangerous.

"Don't what? Don't tell you the truth?"

"Don't tell me what I'm worth." He heard his own voice rise to meet hers, echoing off the saintless glass. "You don't get to decide that. You don't get to stand there with your bruises and your missing birthdays and tell me the price is too high, because I already paid it, Mara. It's paid. My mother's face is *paid*. And if you tell me it wasn't worth it, then you're telling me she's gone for nothing, and I will not carry that on top of everything else."

The silence after was enormous. Dust drifted through the lantern light between them.

Mara sat down abruptly on a fallen pew, as if her legs had simply resigned. She put her face in her hands. When she spoke again, her voice was small and terribly steady. "When I saw my name in that book, glowing, I thought — this is what I'm for. Some grand purpose. Prophecy. Destiny." A wet, bitter laugh. "Nobody writes in the prophecy that destiny means watching the man you love forget his own mother. Nobody writes that the chosen girl gets to choose whether the boy who follows her comes back as a person or a shell wearing his coat."

"Then we change the ending," Sebastian said. He crouched in front of her, taking her hands, prying them gently from her face. "That's what wardens do. We don't accept the terms of a haunting; we renegotiate. The Door of Unmaking — Corvo told you it opens for a price. Fine. We find out exactly what price, and we pay it *together*, split down the middle, and neither of us arrives hollow alone."

"You can't split a soul down the middle."

"You can't burn an unbreakable oath either, and yet." He squeezed her hands. "Mara. Look at me. I have maybe half of myself left, and every single remaining piece agrees on one thing: I am not leaving this country without you. That's not bravery. That's not sacrifice. That's just — arithmetic. Everything I am minus you equals nothing worth carrying home."

She looked at him for a long time. In the lantern light her eyes were red-rimmed and furious and frightened and, beneath all of it, still — impossibly — warm. "You're terrible at romance," she said finally, thickly. "You know that? 'Arithmetic.' On our first date you knocked over the wine."

"I improved. I only knocked over one candle tonight."

"You haven't knocked anything over."

"There's still time."

And she laughed — cracked, exhausted, furious at herself for doing it — and pulled him up onto the pew beside her, and for a while neither of them spoke. The dead city glittered below the chapel window, ten thousand lanterns burning for people who couldn't say why. Somewhere out there, a starving king was hunting the girl who had escaped his court, and somewhere behind them, a ferryman named Corvo was spending Mara's childhood one golden coin at a time.

"Tell me about her," Mara said softly, after a long time. "Your mother. What's left."

Sebastian closed his eyes and reached into the smooth place, feeling the shape of the stone. "Warmth," he said. "Honey bread, maybe — I don't know why that word. Someone who was kind the way gravity is down. Just... constant." He opened his eyes. "Keep it for me. Until we're home. Keep her, so that when this is over, you can give her back."

Mara's jaw tightened. He watched her decide not to argue — watched her file the argument away for another night, another gate, another cost. "I'll keep her," she said. "But Sebastian — promise me something. When we get to the Door. Whatever it asks. You tell me first. You don't lie to me and you don't pay alone. Promise."

He promised.

He was lying when he promised, and some part of him — the warden part, the part trained to guard secrets inside wards within walls — knew that she saw it. But she was too tired to press, and the lantern was guttering, and outside, far across the salt flats beyond the dead harbor, a light was moving that was not a lantern and not a star.

It was coming toward the city. Slowly. Deliberately. Eating as it came.

Mara saw it too. She went rigid against his side. "That's how he tracks," she breathed. "Corvo told me. He devours villages' pasts and reads what's missing. Sebastian — he'll find the ferryman. He'll find what Corvo took from me. He'll find *us*."

Down in the streets of Sable, the first screams began — not screams of pain, Sebastian realized, but something worse: the confused, grieving cries of people who had just lost something they could no longer name.

He snuffed the lantern with his bare hand and drew Mara into the dark.

* * *

They ran.

Not immediately — first there were thirty seconds of frozen listening, the two of them pressed together in the chapel's black throat while the distant light brightened and the cries spread through the city like fire through dry grass. Then Mara grabbed his wrist and said, "The aqueduct. There's an old aqueduct along the north cliff — Corvo showed me on the map. It runs above the roofs. If he reads the city's memories, we want to be somewhere with no memories to read."

The aqueduct was a skeleton of pale stone stitched along the cliff face, its channel dry for a century, its arches open to the wind. They climbed to it through a gap in the chapel's crypt wall — Mara knew the way, God knew how, and Sebastian filed the question away — and then they ran along the spine of it, forty feet above the streets, while below them the King of Sable fed.

Sebastian forced himself to look once. Only once.

The light resolved into a figure on the harbor road, tall and thin and wrong, robed in something that moved like smoke remembering being silk. Around him the lanterns of the lower quarter were going out one by one — not blown out, but *drained*, and as each window died, the people inside stumbled into the streets looking bewildered and bereft, patting their pockets, calling names they'd forgotten belonged to anyone. The king moved among them without hurrying. Where he passed, dogs forgot their masters and followed him instead. A woman stood in her doorway holding a child and weeping, and Sebastian understood with sick clarity that she was weeping because she could no longer remember why the child mattered to her — only that he had mattered, enormously, and the enormousness remained with nothing left to attach to.

"He's not eating them," Mara gasped as they ran. "He's tasting. He's looking for us in what they know. A stranger came through the gates — a man who burned an oath — a girl with a lantern—"

"Then we get out of the city tonight."

"The Door is six days east. Across the salt plains. On foot, wounded, with him reading every village between here and there like pages." She skidded around a collapsed arch, caught his hand, kept running. "Sebastian, we need help. And the only person in this country who knows the way is a ferryman who sold me out once already and is currently sitting on five of my happiest days like a dragon on gold."

"Corvo."

"Corvo."

They stopped at the aqueduct's end, where the stone spine dissolved into cliffside stairs descending toward the north gate. Below them the city wept and forgot. Behind them the king's light paused at the foot of the chapel hill — paused, and tilted, the way a head tilts when it hears something interesting.

And in Sebastian's pocket, against his hip, the list he'd written — *MARA. Find her.* — suddenly felt less like ink and more like bait.

"He knows I came for you," Sebastian said quietly. "Which means he knows what I love. Which means he doesn't need to chase us at all." He looked at Mara, and the moonlight showed him her face going pale as she arrived at the same arithmetic he had. "He just needs to follow me, and wait for the gates to finish their work."

Far below, unhurried as tide, the light began to move north.
