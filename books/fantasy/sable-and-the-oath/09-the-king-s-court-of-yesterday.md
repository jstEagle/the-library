## 9. The King's Court of Yesterday

They did not bind her hands. That was the first thing Mara noticed, and the thing that frightened her most.

The soldiers — if that was the word for figures whose armor seemed stitched from old letters, tarnished silver script crawling over blackened plate — simply walked on either side of her through the ruined streets of the capital, and she understood that binding was unnecessary here. In Sable, you did not run from the king. You ran from yourself, and there was nowhere to go.

The city had been beautiful once. She could feel it in the bones of it, the way you could feel a melody in a broken instrument. Bridges of white stone arched over canals gone dry and silvered with salt. Bell towers leaned against the sky like tired old men. Every window was dark, but every window had flowers in its box — dead ones, preserved perfectly, petals like paper, as if someone had loved this place enough to keep watering it long after everything else stopped.

The people watched her pass. That was the horror of it: they were *there*, dozens of them, standing in doorways and leaning from balconies, dressed in fine clothes gone gray at the edges, and every single one of them looked at her with the same expression — a bright, vacant courtesy, the smile of someone who has just been introduced to you for the first time.

Because they had. They were being introduced to her for the first time. Every day.

"Good morning," said an old woman from a balcony, and her voice was warm and cracked and utterly sincere. "Lovely weather."

It was not morning. The sky above Sable was a permanent bruised violet, neither day nor night, and no weather moved in it at all. But Mara lifted a hand anyway. "Lovely," she agreed, and the woman beamed as though Mara had given her a gift, and by tomorrow she would not remember receiving one.

*This is what he eats,* she thought. *Not their lives. Their yesterdays.* He left them the shell of today, polished and empty, and took everything that made the shell worth living in.

The palace rose at the city's heart, and it was wrong in a way that took her breath. It was not ruined. It was *newer* than the city around it, its walls pale and uncracked, its banners unfaded — because the king poured his stolen years into keeping himself magnificent while his kingdom crumbled around him like a spent candle. The palace drank; the city dried. She could see the arithmetic of it in the stones.

The gates opened without being touched.

Inside, the court of Sable was in session, and Mara stopped walking before the guards gently, impersonally, pressed her forward.

The throne room was vast and lit by chandeliers of frozen light — actual light, caught and hung like glass, dripping slow luminous drops that vanished before they struck the floor. Courtiers lined the walls in ranks, hundreds of them, all facing the throne, all wearing the same pleasant blankness. And on the throne —

On the throne sat a man who might once have been handsome the way a winter sky is handsome: severe, cold, and full of a light that gives nothing back. He was not old. That was the obscenity of him. He should have been dust ten centuries deep, but he looked fifty, maybe sixty, with a silver beard trimmed close and eyes the color of the salt plains at dusk. He wore robes of deep blue worked with threads that *moved* — tiny scenes embroidered into the fabric, playing out over and over: a wedding, a harvest, a child learning to walk. A thousand small lives, sewn into cloth.

And upon his head, instead of a crown, he wore memories.

That was the only way Mara could describe it. Rings of light circled his brow like halos stacked out of true, and each ring held something — a face laughing, a hand gripping another hand, a doorway opening onto summer. They turned slowly around his skull, and when one dimmed, he would reach up with two fingers, pluck it away, and let it dissolve into his mouth like sugar. Then his eyes would close for a moment, and he would smile, and somewhere in the ranks of courtiers a person would sway slightly, frown, and forget why they had come to court today.

"Majesty," said a herald whose voice sounded like pages turning, "the outlander is brought before you. The girl named in ink."

The king opened his eyes.

Mara had braced herself for hunger. For the ravening emptiness Corvo had described, the great maw wearing a crown. What she found was worse: curiosity. He looked at her the way a scholar looks at a page he has waited a lifetime to read.

"So," he said. His voice was quiet, almost kind, and it filled the room anyway. "You are real. A hundred years I have kept my court fed and my kingdom breathing, and the prophecy sends me a girl from beyond the veil." He rose, and the rings of memory around his brow shivered. "Do you know what I am, child?"

"A thief," Mara said.

A ripple went through the courtiers — not shock, exactly. Something more like envy. When had any of them last dared say a true thing aloud?

The king laughed, low and unbothered. "A steward," he corrected. "When Sable was dying — when the magic turned on us and began to eat our own names — someone had to hold the door. Someone had to take the country's pain into himself so the people could live." He descended the dais steps slowly, robes whispering. "I have been holding it for three hundred years. Do you know what that costs? A king must be *sustained*. And there is only one food in all the world that sustains a thing that should have ended." He smiled, and it was almost gentle. "Yesterday."

"You're eating them," Mara said. Her hands had curled into fists without her permission. "Your own people. They don't remember their children. They greet each other like strangers every morning and think that's normal, because they can't remember that it isn't—"

"They are alive," the king said, and now the kindness fell away and something vast moved behind his face, something with teeth. "Which is more than they would be if I stopped. You judge me from a valley that walled itself off from the world and called it mercy. At least I kept *mine*."

She had no answer to that. That was the trap of him — every cruelty wrapped in a truth, every truth bent into a justification, until arguing felt like drowning.

"I am not going to hurt you, Mara Venn," he said, and hearing her name in his mouth made her skin crawl, because he pronounced it like an ingredient. "You are the most valuable thing to enter my kingdom in a century. The prophecy names you, and prophecy is the one magic even I cannot digest." He gestured, and the courtiers parted down the middle, opening a corridor toward a pair of tall doors. "You will be my guest. You will want for nothing. And in time, perhaps, you will tell me what the ink intends for you — or I will find it myself, in the reading of you. We are patient here. Patience is all we have."

Guards flanked her. There was no decision to make; there never had been. She walked.

* * *

Her chambers were beautiful, which she understood now was the local dialect of threat.

High windows overlooking the dry canals. A bed with linens soft as breath. A table laid with food that smelled astonishingly real — bread, honey, roasted pears — and when she ate, tentatively, it tasted real too, and she hated how much comfort it gave her. On the desk lay fresh paper, ink, a quill. An invitation. *Write your days here, girl. Give them to me willingly.*

She did not touch the quill.

She explored instead. The chamber had one other door, and it was not locked — of course it wasn't — and it opened onto a gallery, a long hall lined with framed glass cases, each one lit from within by that same caught light.

Museum pieces, she thought at first. Relics of the kingdom.

Then she saw that the relics were moving.

Each case held a memory, suspended like a moth in amber. A first kiss, two young faces tilting together in endless repetition. A soldier coming home, dropping his pack, lifting a child. A woman dancing alone in a kitchen, laughing at nothing, laughing at everything. Hundreds of cases. Thousands. The whole plundered yesterday of a nation, catalogued and shelved, and Mara stood in the middle of it with her hand over her mouth.

He didn't wear them all. He couldn't. These were the reserves. The pantry.

She walked the gallery slowly, and the memories noticed her — she could feel it, faint tugs of attention, like dogs lifting their heads. Some of them pressed against the glass of their cases, and she got the mad, unbearable sense that they *recognized* her, that they wanted out, that a memory locked in a case was a kind of ghost and ghosts knew things.

She was halfway down the hall when she saw the boy.

The case was smaller than most, set at a child's eye height, and inside it: a kitchen with copper pots. A woman with flour on her hands and dark hair escaping her braid, turning, laughing, saying something Mara could not hear but could feel — the specific warmth of a mother teasing her son. And the boy — eight, maybe nine, all elbows and solemn brown eyes, trying very hard not to smile and failing —

Mara's whole body went cold.

She knew that face. She had studied it across a candlelit table two nights and a lifetime ago, tracing it with her eyes while pretending to look at the menu. The same serious brow. The same way his mouth betrayed him when he was happy.

"No," she whispered.

The memory played on. The boy — Sebastian, it was Sebastian, younger than she'd ever seen him, gap-toothed and gangly — chased the woman around the kitchen table and she caught him and swung him up and the light in the case was golden and warm and it was *his*, it came from *him*, some piece of him had been taken—

But that was impossible. Sebastian wasn't in Sable. Sebastian was behind wards that magic could not cross, safe in the valley, furious and grieving and — and *coming*, maybe, a small treacherous voice added, because of course he would come, of course he had burned whatever oath stood in his way, because he was Sebastian and he loved stubbornly and completely—

But then how? How was his childhood hanging in a case in a dead king's gallery?

Unless the tollgates took from everyone who crossed. Unless the border stripped travelers on the way in, and the king collected what they paid. Unless Sebastian had already crossed — already begun paying — and this was the receipt.

Mara pressed her palm flat against the glass. The memory-Sebastian laughed silently under her hand, and the mother swung him in a circle, and Mara realized she was crying.

"This one," she said to the empty gallery, to the king she knew could hear, to anyone. "This one isn't yours. He isn't yours. None of them are."

The lights in the cases flickered — all of them, at once, like a held breath.

"He came looking for me," she said, and her grief hardened as she said it, annealed into something with an edge. "And you've been feeding on him at the door like a tick. So hear me, whoever's listening: I'm going to find the Door of Unmaking, and I'm going to open it, and I'm going to take every one of these back. His first. His *first*."

Silence. Then, softly, from everywhere and nowhere — from the walls themselves, from the embroidery of a thousand small lives — the king's voice, amused and unhurried:

"The Door does not give back, little Venn. Nothing in Sable gives back." A pause, and she could hear the smile in it. "But do keep talking. Anger seasons the memory. Yours will taste extraordinary."

The lights steadied. The gallery went quiet. And in the smallest case, the boy with the solemn eyes reached up in his endless golden loop toward a mother's face, and Mara Venn put her forehead against the glass and made him a promise she had no idea yet how to keep.

Behind her, unnoticed, the fresh ink on the desk in her chamber had begun, very faintly, to glow — and far below the palace, in a cell of salt and silence, a blind archivist lifted her head and spoke to the dark:

"She's awake. Bring the boy's keeper to me before the king reads her name."
