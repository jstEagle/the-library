## 11. Escape Through the Bone Orchard

**Sebastian**

The cell smelled of old paper and someone else's childhood.

Sebastian had learned that about Sable's prisons in the three days since the king's guards had dragged them from the archivist's tower: every cell held a residue of what had been taken. The walls wept it. He could feel it against his palms when he pressed them to the stone — not warmth exactly, but *weight*, like pressing his hand against a book that had been read so many times its spine had gone soft. Somewhere in these stones was a fisherman's memory of his daughter learning to walk. A baker's last morning in a village that no longer existed. The king ate what he wanted and stored the rest, the way a miser stores coins he'll never spend.

He sat with his back against the wall, watching Mara sleep sitting up, her chin on her chest, her wrists still raw from the manacles they'd been forced to wear through the court. They'd taken the manacles off only after the door closed. The king wanted them comfortable, the guard had said with something like pity. His Majesty prefers his guests rested.

*Guests.* As if they were staying for dinner.

"Sebastian." Mara's voice, low, without opening her eyes. "You're doing the thing again."

"What thing?"

"The thing where you stare at me like you're trying to carve me somewhere safe."

He almost smiled. "Is it working?"

"No." She opened her eyes. In the grey light that leaked through the slot window — Sable's light, which never quite decided whether it was dawn or dusk — her face looked drawn and older than it had in the valley. "Nothing works here. That's rather the point of the place."

From the corner of the cell, where he'd been pretending to sleep the way he pretended everything, Corvo spoke. "There is a way out of this room. Since you're both awake and miserable, I may as well tell you before I lose my nerve."

Sebastian turned. The ferryman sat with his long legs folded under him like a heron's, his coat pooled around him, his face — that face Sebastian still couldn't fully fix in his memory, as if the man were slightly out of focus at all times — tilted toward the ceiling.

"You've had three days to mention a way out," Sebastian said.

"I've had three days to decide whether getting out is better than staying in." Corvo lowered his gaze to them. "The answer changes hourly. But tonight the king feeds. When he feeds, the prison forgets to be a prison."

"Forgets?"

"This whole country runs on forgetting, boy. The locks are made of attention. The walls are made of certainty. When the king devours a village's worth of yesterdays, every ward in Sable loosens for an hour or two, drunk on the surplus. It's the only time anything in this kingdom can be stolen, escaped, or survived." He rose, unfolding joint by joint. "And tonight he's hungry. He lost his meal when your girl slipped out of his court, and he's been starving since. Starving kings eat large."

Mara stood too. "You said *your girl* like it costs you something."

"Everything costs something here. That's the first lesson of Sable and you two still haven't learned it." Corvo pressed his palm flat against the cell door, and Sebastian saw, for the first time, the faint shine of old scar tissue across the ferryman's knuckles — the mark of a man who had gripped things until they burned him. "The corridor turns left twice. There's a grate behind the third cell, rusted through because the jailer forgot it existed eleven years ago and the prison took him at his word. Beyond the grate is the orchard."

"The bone orchard," Mara said quietly. Sebastian had heard her speak of it — the archivist had named it once, in her dry blind voice, and even the naming had made the room colder.

"The petrified ones," Corvo said. "The memory-eaters that came before the king. Before there was a Sable to feed them, maybe. They stand in those woods like trees, and if you're quiet, and if you carry nothing they want, they'll let you pass. Mostly." He smiled, and it was not a comforting sight. "Mostly is the best word in this country."

"And past the orchard?" Sebastian asked.

Past the orchard, Corvo said, lay the salt plains, and past the plains, the mountains where the Door of Unmaking slept in its valley of nothing.

Sebastian looked at Mara. She was already looking at him, and between them passed the whole silent argument they'd been having since the court: *we shouldn't have come here, we should never have split up, I should go alone, you should go alone, don't you dare leave me.* He reached over and took her hand, and her fingers closed around his with a grip that hurt a little, and that was answer enough.

"How do we get the door open?" she asked Corvo. "The real one. Not the cell."

Corvo's face did something complicated and closed, like a shopkeeper pulling down a shutter. "When we're out," he said. "One bargain at a time."

* * *

They went at the hour when the light died completely — Sable had no true night, but it had this: a darkness like held breath, when even the ruins seemed to listen.

The corridor was exactly as Corvo had promised, which should have reassured Sebastian and instead set a small cold bell ringing somewhere beneath his ribs. Everything in Sable that came easily was a trap; the archivist had told him that too, in her voice like dry leaves. Left twice. The third cell's back wall gaped where iron had rusted into lace, and beyond it the air changed — cooler, wetter, smelling of stone and something faintly sweet, like fruit rotting in a far room.

The orchard began without announcement. One moment they were climbing through broken masonry; the next, the ground rose into low hills and the hills were full of *them*.

Sebastian stopped breathing.

They stood thirty feet tall, forty, some taller — shapes like people stretched and thickened into standing stone, their limbs grown together into trunks, their faces still faces. That was the worst part. Not statues. Not trees. Faces, each one different, each one caught mid-expression: terror, ecstasy, grief, a rapture so complete it looked like pain. Moss grew in the hollows of their cheeks. Lichen silvered their shoulders. Their arms had fused upward into branches that bore no leaves, only knots — hundreds of small, hard swellings that in the dying light looked almost like fruit.

"They were eaters," Corvo murmured, walking close along the narrow path between them, his voice flattened to almost nothing. "Before the king. They fed on memory the way he does, but greedily, all at once, whole villages stripped to husks. So the first wardens of Sable did what wardens do." He gestured up at the towering forms. "They gave the eaters exactly what they craved, all of it at once, until there was nothing left inside them but themselves. Petrified from the inside out. Full forever."

"Your guild did this?" Mara whispered.

"My guild." Something moved across Corvo's face and was gone. "Walk. Don't touch them. Don't look into their faces longer than you must — some of them aren't as empty as they look, and a hungry eye is still an eye."

So they walked.

It was slow going, the path winding between the colossi like a footpath through a forest of drowned gods, and the silence was total in a way Sebastian had never known — not the silence of emptiness but the silence of *held* things, a thousand tons of swallowed memory standing motionless on either side. His skin crawled. Once, passing so close to one that its stone knee brushed his shoulder, he heard — or thought he heard — a sound from deep inside the body of it, like a word spoken underwater. Like a name.

He kept his eyes forward. He held Mara's hand and kept his eyes forward.

They were perhaps halfway through when the king's hunger arrived.

Sebastian felt it before he understood it: a shudder running through the ground, and then through the petrified giants themselves, a tremor like wind through wheat. Far away — miles, maybe more — something vast was feeding, and the whole country leaned toward it the way flowers lean toward sun. Above them, in the knotted branches of the memory-eaters, the hard swellings began to *glow*. Faintly. Warmly. Amber light seeping out of the stone fruit like honey through a cracked comb.

"Oh no," Corvo breathed. "No, no — walk faster, don't run, whatever you do—"

"Why are they lighting up?" Mara's voice cracked.

"Because he's eating, and they remember what eating was." Corvo had stopped walking. He was staring up at the glowing branches with an expression Sebastian couldn't read — hunger of his own, or horror, or both wearing the same face. "A thousand years asleep and they can still smell it. Move. *Move.*"

They moved. The light grew around them, amber and pulsing, and now Sebastian could hear it clearly — not one voice under the water but many, a low moaning chord rising out of the petrified bodies, the sound of things dreaming of food. One of the giants nearest the path shifted. Not much. An inch. Stone grinding against stone, moss falling from its cheek in grey ribbons.

Its eyes opened. Just cracks. Just enough.

"*Don't look at it,*" Corvo snapped, and grabbed Mara's arm and hauled her forward, and Sebastian ran at their heels with his heart slamming, as around them the orchard woke by degrees — cracks of light opening along a dozen faces, a groaning like a fleet of ships settling, the sweet rot-smell thickening until it coated his tongue.

Then the path bent, and the trees thinned, and ahead — impossibly, blessedly — lay open ground: dark rolling hills under a starless sky, and beyond them the pale shimmer that could only be salt.

They broke from the orchard at a dead run and didn't stop until the last of the stone faces was a smudge of amber glow behind them. Then Mara doubled over, hands on her knees, laughing the ragged laugh of the nearly-dead, and Sebastian pulled her upright and held her, and for one moment — one — it was simple. She was alive. He was alive. They were out.

"We need to keep moving," he said into her hair. "How far to the plains?"

"A night's walk, if the weather holds, and the weather here never—" Corvo stopped.

Sebastian turned.

The ferryman was standing apart from them, facing back toward the orchard, and in his hands he held something that glowed softly amber: one of the knot-fruits, torn from a branch, its light leaking between his fingers.

"I don't understand," Mara said slowly. "You said don't touch them."

"I said *you* shouldn't touch them." Corvo's voice had changed. All the wry distance had drained out of it; what remained was raw and old and terribly tired. "I'm not you. I'm not — I haven't been anyone for a long time. Do you know what the king takes from the people who serve him? Not memories. Names. He keeps our names in his vault like coins in a purse, because a servant with a name might one day want something for himself." He turned the fruit over in his hands, and its light washed his out-of-focus face into sudden, terrible clarity — and Sebastian saw him, really saw him, for the first time: young, younger than his voice, with a scar across his knuckles shaped like a river. "I ferried you because I told myself it was kindness. It wasn't. It was a bet. And tonight, in that orchard, I realized the bet pays out."

"You're taking it to the king," Sebastian said. The words came out flat and cold. "The girl named in the prophecy. You're selling us."

"He's starving," Corvo said simply. "A starving king will trade anything. Even names." He looked at Mara, and for one unguarded moment his eyes were wet. "I'm sorry. I liked you. Both of you. That's the joke of this country — you can mean it and still do it."

Behind him, at the treeline, the amber lights of the waking orchard flickered — and among them, new lights answered. Smaller. Lower. Moving. Torches, coming down the hillside through the petrified forest, a dozen of them, two dozen, closing fast along the very path they'd just run.

Mara's hand found Sebastian's. Her grip was shaking.

"Run," she whispered.

They ran — out onto the open hills, into the dark, with the torches spreading behind them like a burning fan and Corvo's silhouette standing motionless against the glow of the orchard, holding his name in his hands like a man holding his own heart, just bought back at the price of everyone who'd ever trusted him.

The hills sloped down, and down, and somewhere below, pale and wide and merciless, the salt plains waited — miles of open white with nowhere to hide.

Behind them, faint on the wind, came the sound of hounds. Or voices. Or both.

Mara stumbled, caught herself, and gasped: "The lake — the archivist said — north of the plains, there's a lake —"

Sebastian didn't know what lake. He didn't ask. He took her hand tighter and ran.
