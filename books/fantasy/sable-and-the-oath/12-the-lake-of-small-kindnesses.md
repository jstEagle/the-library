## 12. The Lake of Small Kindnesses

Mara woke to the taste of copper and the sound of water.

For a moment she didn't know where she was — which was, she had learned, an ordinary condition in Sable. The kingdom did its best to unmake you gently, one morning at a time, so that you woke each day a slightly smaller person and never noticed the difference. But this morning the wrongness had a shape: her left side burned where the king's hunters had caught her at the edge of the salt plains, and Sebastian's face hovered above hers, gray with exhaustion, saying her name over and over as if it were a rope he was throwing.

"Mara. Stay with me. Mara."

"I'm here." Her voice came out cracked. "I'm not going anywhere. Mostly because I can't walk."

Something that was almost a laugh moved through him and died. He had her arm across his shoulders, and when he helped her up she saw where they were — and forgot, for one full breath, how to breathe.

They stood on the shore of a lake that glowed.

Not with light exactly. With *remembering*. The water was pale silver-green, and beneath its surface moved shapes like slow fish made of lamplight, drifting in long lazy spirals, rising and sinking. The shore was black sand that gave off warmth like a hearthstone. Around the lake, in a wide ring, grew trees unlike any she'd seen in Sable — their bark smooth and pale as candle wax, their leaves translucent, glowing faintly from within, so that the whole grove looked lit by ten thousand small green lanterns.

"What is this place?" she whispered.

Sebastian shook his head slowly. "I don't know. But look—" He pointed with his free hand. "The wards. There aren't any. I can't feel the country pulling at us here. Can you feel that? It's quiet. It's actually *quiet*."

He was right. Since crossing into Sable, Mara had felt the land like a hand pressed against the back of her neck — patient, curious, always tugging loose threads of her. Here the hand had lifted. The silence was so complete she could hear her own heartbeat, and under it, faintly, something else. A sound like humming. Like many voices humming very far away, all in tune.

"The wound first," Sebastian said, easing her down onto the warm sand. He knelt beside her and pulled back the blood-soaked bandage with hands that shook only a little. The gash along her ribs was ugly but shallow — the hunter's blade had grazed rather than bitten. He cleaned it with water from a skin, packed it with the grey moss Corvo had shown them to use before Corvo had sold them, and bound it tight.

"You're getting good at this," she said.

"I'm getting good at watching you bleed." His jaw was tight. "I'd trade the skill."

She touched his face. He leaned into her palm the way he always did, eyes closing for half a second, as if her hand were the only true thing in the country. Then he opened them and looked out at the glowing water, and she watched the warden in him come back up like a drawbridge.

"We should keep moving," he said. "The king lost our trail at the plains, but lost isn't gone. We rest tonight. One night. Then—"

"Sebastian." She pulled him gently down to sit beside her. "Look around you. When did we last have one night? Just one?"

He didn't answer. Out on the lake, one of the light-shapes rose near the surface, and in its glow Mara saw — or thought she saw — a face. Small. Round-cheeked. A child's face, looking up through the water with an expression of enormous, unhurried curiosity.

"Sebastian," she breathed. "There's someone in the lake."

* * *

They came up out of the water as the sun went down — if Sable even had a sun; the sky here did its dying-light routine without one — dozens of them, then hundreds, rising from the lake like bubbles of silver and taking shape on the shore.

They were the dead. Mara understood it without being told, the way you understand cold. They were thin and translucent and they flickered at the edges, but their faces were calm in a way no living face in Sable was calm. An old man sat down on the sand near the treeline and simply watched the water, content. A woman in a baker's apron wandered the shallows, trailing her fingers, smiling at nothing. A soldier still wearing half his armor lay back against a wax-pale trunk and closed his eyes.

"They're not suffering," Sebastian said quietly. He had positioned himself between Mara and the ghosts, old habit, though nothing here seemed to want harming. "Everywhere else in this country, the dead are hungry or angry or empty. These ones are just... resting."

"Because they've already been forgotten," Mara said. The words came out of her before she understood them, the way prophecy sometimes worked in her mouth these days. She pressed a hand to her side and went on, slowly, feeling toward the truth like feeling toward a wall in the dark. "That's what this place is. Everything the king eats doesn't vanish. It comes here. Every memory he swallows out of someone — the dead carry the leftovers. This is where forgotten things wash up."

The lake hummed. The leaves overhead glowed a little brighter, as if pleased to be understood.

And then the child stepped out of the water directly in front of her.

She was maybe seven years old, barefoot, in a nightdress that had been white once and was now the color of moonlight. Her hair floated around her head as if she were still underwater. She looked at Mara with the frank, bottomless stare of children and cats, and then she said, in a voice like a bell rung softly:

"You're bleeding remembering."

Mara blinked. "...I'm sorry?"

"You're leaking." The child pointed at Mara's side, at the bandage, and then — horribly, fascinatingly — Mara could see it too. Faint threads of light, silver-green like the lake, seeping out of the wound with every beat of her heart. Drifting off her. Dissolving. "When you hurt, you leak faster. The country drinks what leaks. That's why it hurts you on purpose sometimes. It gets thirsty."

Sebastian had gone very still. "Mara. Do you feel different? Anything missing?"

"No. Not yet." But she thought of the tollgates, of Sebastian coming through the border hollowed, of the day he'd turned to her on the ferry and asked, politely, kindly, who his mother had been. Her stomach clenched. "It's small things. Little shreds. It takes the edges first."

The child nodded solemnly, as if Mara had recited a lesson well. Then she did something extraordinary.

She reached out and cupped her small hands beneath one of the leaking threads of light — caught it, the way you'd catch a firefly — and held it up between them. And inside the little glow, Mara saw a picture: herself, younger, sitting on her grandmother's kitchen counter while flour drifted down like snow, both of them laughing at nothing, at everything, at being alive in a warm room in a safe world.

Her grandmother's kitchen. A memory she hadn't thought of in twenty years.

"That's mine," Mara whispered. "How do you have that?"

"It fell off you just now. When your side hurt." The child turned the memory in her hands like a marble. "But it's not broken. Look." And she pressed it gently back against Mara's temple.

Warmth bloomed behind Mara's eyes. The kitchen came back whole — the flour, the laugh, the smell of cardamom — not dimmed, not eaten, *whole*, as if it had never leaked at all.

She stared at the child. "You gave it back."

"Giving is easy." The child shrugged, a small, ancient gesture. "Taking is easy too. Everybody here does one or the other. The king only knows taking, so he's always starving." She said it the way a child reports that grass is green. Then she cocked her head. "You're the girl from the ink, aren't you? The one who's going to open the Door."

The word *Door* dropped through the evening like a stone through still water. Even the humming of the lake seemed to bend around it. Sebastian's hand found Mara's.

"How do you know about the Door?" he asked.

The child smiled, and for a moment her edges flickered hard, and Mara glimpsed something underneath — a hospital smell, a hand letting go, a name being spoken over and over by someone crying until the name wore smooth and slipped away. The dead of the lake, carrying the leftovers.

"Everything forgotten ends up here," the child said simply. "Even the parts of prophecies nobody wanted to remember." She took Mara's hand. Her grip was cool and certain. "Come. The others want to meet you. They've been waiting a long time for somebody who might be able to give something back."

* * *

They came to her all through the night, the dead of the lake, one and two and three at a time, and each of them brought a gift.

An old woman pressed a memory against Mara's brow: a wedding, decades gone, rain on canvas, a groom laughing so hard he couldn't say his vows. "I couldn't hold onto it anymore," the old woman said. "It got too heavy, being the only one who remembered. Carry it a while. It's lighter shared."

A fisherman gave her the smell of his daughter's hair. A stonemason gave her the sound of his chisel finding the perfect rhythm, the hour when the work stops being labor and becomes music. A girl barely older than Mara gave her a first kiss behind a granary, nervous and sweet and over too fast, and giggled — actually giggled, a dead girl giggling on a black-sand shore — at the look on Mara's face afterward.

None of them asked anything back. That was the part that undid her. In Sable everything cost — every bridge, every answer, every mile. Corvo had taught her that memories were currency, and currency meant accounting, and accounting meant that love itself became a ledger. But the dead of the lake gave the way rain falls. Without invoice. Without keeping count.

"Why?" she finally asked the child, who had stayed at her side all night like a small pale shadow. "Why give them to me? You don't know me."

"We know what you're for." The child traced a shape in the sand — a door, tall and narrow, with a keyhole like an open eye. "The Door eats what you love most. Everyone knows that much, even the forgotten. But everyone forgets the other half." She looked up, and her floating hair settled around her face like a curtain drawn back. "A door is just a mouth. What goes in depends on whose hand feeds it. The king would feed it stolen things, and it would eat forever and stay hungry. But if someone fed it *given* things—" she spread her small hands, "—given things fill a mouth up. Given things end hunger. That's why we waited. Somebody has to teach the Door a different meal."

Mara's heart was pounding. "The Door can be fed memories that were given freely? Not taken? Not sacrificed?"

"Given." The child nodded. "But there's a catch, because there's always a catch." Her voice gentled. "To give a memory truly — not lose it, not trade it, *give* it — you have to do it while you still love it completely. You can't wait until you're willing to spare it. You have to give the best thing you have, while it's still the best thing you have, and mean it."

Across the fire — they'd built one, though it wasn't for warmth, more for the comfort of the shape of it — Sebastian slept at last, really slept, his face slack and young. Mara watched him breathe and felt the trap close gently around her heart.

Because she knew what the best thing she had was. She was looking at him.

*Give him,* said a voice in her that sounded like the child. *While you still can. Give him freely, before the Door takes him by force, before the king tears him out of you piece by piece. Give the whole of him, given, and the Door will open full instead of hungry.*

And the other voice, the small human one, the one that had carried her across a haunted kingdom on a wounded side: *No. Not him. Anything but him.*

The child watched her struggle with soft, terrible patience.

"There's another way," Mara said at last. "There has to be. There's always something the prophecy left out."

"There's always something," the child agreed. "That's what makes them prophecies instead of sentences." She stood, brushing sand from her nightdress, and looked east, where the sky had begun its bruised pre-dawn color. Far away, across the dark, something howled — long, low, and hungry, a sound with a king inside it. The lake's humming faltered. The dead nearest the shore turned their heads like deer scenting wolves.

"He's crossed the salt," the child said quietly. "He ate a village in the night to find your scent again. He'll be here by tomorrow's dusk." She turned back to Mara, and took both her hands, and her grip was stronger than a seven-year-old's should be. "So you have until dusk, girl-from-the-ink. Learn giving fast. Because the Door is three days' walk, and the king is one."

Behind her, Sebastian stirred in his sleep and murmured her name — the only word, Mara thought, that he'd say without paying for it.

She squeezed the child's cold small hands and made herself ask:

"Teach me. Before dusk — teach me how to give."
