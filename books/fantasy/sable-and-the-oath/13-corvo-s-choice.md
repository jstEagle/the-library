## 13. Corvo's Choice

The name came back to him on the third night after the betrayal, in the worst possible way: unasked for, unwelcome, arriving like a stone dropped into still water.

*Corvo.*

He was sitting on the deck of his ferry, moored at the rotted pilings of a village that had forgotten it was a port, when the word simply surfaced. Not spoken. Not written. Just *there*, the way a bruise surfaces — rising through the skin of him until he could not look away from it.

Corvo. His name. The one he'd sold to the memory-eaters in the Bone Orchard to buy his own escape from the king's court, back when he'd still told himself that survival was a kind of virtue. He had traded it for passage out of the orchard while Sebastian and Mara ran the other way, pursued by things with too many mouths. He had watched them go and had not looked back, because looking back was how you died in Sable, everyone knew that, everyone said that, and Corvo had been saying *everyone knows* to himself for so long that he'd mistaken it for wisdom.

Now the name sat in his chest like a swallowed hook.

He tried to remember what it had felt like before — what it had felt like to be called something, by someone, with warmth behind it. There had been a woman once. He was almost sure there had been a woman once. He could feel the shape of her absence the way you feel the shape of a missing tooth: a hollow that the tongue returns to again and again, worrying at nothing.

Had she called him Corvo? Had anyone?

The river slid past, black and patient. In Sable, rivers did not reflect the sky; they reflected whatever you were trying not to think about. Tonight his river showed him a girl with storm-grey eyes standing on a dock, asking him to take her to a Door at the edge of the world, offering him memories like coins from a purse that could never be refilled.

*One happy memory per day of travel,* she had said. That had been the bargain. And he had taken them — taken her first snowfall, taken the taste of her grandmother's bread, taken a summer afternoon with a boy named Sebastian under an apple tree — and he had spent them all anyway, feeding them to the tollgates, buying shortcuts, buying silence, buying his own name back from the things in the orchard while the two of them ran for their lives without him.

He put his face in his hands.

The ferry creaked beneath him. It always creaked. It had been creaking since before the kingdom forgot itself, probably, and it would creak long after Corvo was gone, ferrying whoever came next across water that remembered better than its passengers did.

"You're a coward," he told the river.

The river showed him Mara's face when she'd realized he was gone — not anger, which he could have borne, but the quick, terrible flicker of *of course*, the look of someone who had expected betrayal and been right, and hated being right more than she would ever hate him.

That was worse. That was so much worse.

Corvo stood up so fast the boat rocked. He paced the length of the deck, three strides each way, the way he'd paced it for a hundred years or ten days — time moved strangely on the water — and argued with himself in the voices of every man he had ever ferried.

*They're dead already,* said the pragmatist. *No one reaches the Door. The king eats whole villages now; you've seen the salt plains where towns used to be. Going after them is just dying in a more complicated shape.*

*You took her memories,* said another voice, older, quieter, one he didn't recognize until he realized it might be his own, from before. *Not bought. Took. She gave them freely, yes, but you knew what they were worth and she didn't, because she was young and hopeful and hopeful people are terrible at arithmetic.*

*And the boy,* said the oldest voice of all. *The boy who forgets. You heard the archivist say it. You heard what he's going to have to give. And you left them alone with that.*

Corvo stopped pacing.

The truth was this, and he made himself hold it up to the light like a coin he was testing for false metal: he had not betrayed them because he feared death. He had betrayed them because he feared *irrelevance*. A man with no name and no past is a man with no story, and a man with no story will do anything — anything — to steal a page from someone else's book. The memory-eaters had offered him back his name, and he had seized it like a drowning man seizing driftwood, and only now, floating safe in the shallows of his own cowardice, did he understand that the name they'd returned to him was not a gift.

It was a summons.

Because now that he was Corvo again, he could be ashamed. Now that he was Corvo again, he could lie awake hearing Mara laugh at his grim jokes on the ferry deck, hearing Sebastian ask careful questions about the Door with the stubborn patience of a man who intended to move mountains one stone at a time. Now that he was Corvo again, he had something to lose — and everything he stood to lose, he had stolen from them.

A man with no name can't make amends. Amends require a signature.

"Damn you," he said softly, to the river, to the kingdom, to himself. "Damn you for giving it back."

The river, naturally, said nothing. But far downstream, where the water bent toward the salt plains, the horizon glowed faintly red — the color of the king's hunger, the color of a country being eaten alive from the inside. Somewhere out there, two small figures were running toward the largest appetite in the world, carrying between them the only thing it truly wanted.

Corvo went below decks and began to prepare.

* * *

There was not much to prepare. A ferryman's kit is a lantern, a pole, a knife, and a lifetime of knowing the water. But there was one thing more, and he took it down from the beam above his bunk where it had hung since the night he'd fled the Bone Orchard: a small glass vial, stoppered with wax, no bigger than his thumb.

Inside it swam a single memory, golden as lamp-oil.

It was the apple tree. Sebastian's apple tree — the summer afternoon Mara had paid him on the second day of travel, the one he'd sworn he'd spend only if he must, and then hadn't spent even when he must, even when the tollgates ground their teeth at him, even when the orchard things whispered *your name, your name, we'll give you your name*. He had starved instead. He had walked hungry past a dozen gates with that little vial warm against his heart, telling himself it was thrift.

It had not been thrift. It had been the last honest thing in him, hoarded without knowing why.

He held it up to the lantern light. Inside the glass, the afternoon turned slowly: green leaves, dappled sun, a girl's laughter caught mid-air like a bird in flight. He could not feel it anymore — that was the cruelty of stored memories; they were letters written in a language you'd forgotten — but he could see it, and seeing was enough to know it was precious beyond any price Sable understood.

"This is yours," he said to the empty cabin. "Both of yours. I'm only the courier."

He tucked it into his coat, over his heart, and went up to cast off.

* * *

Finding them was easier than it should have been, and that frightened him.

The king's hunger had grown so vast that it left wake-marks across the country — whole stretches of land scoured down to white bone-dust, villages reduced to perfect blank circles where even the grass had forgotten how to grow. And at the center of each circle, faint but unmistakable to a man who had spent a century reading water, ran a thread of scent: two travelers, one bright with borrowed hope, one dimming steadily, guttering like a candle in a draft.

They were heading for the Door along the old pilgrim road, and the king was herding them.

Corvo saw it from the ridge above the salt plains and felt his blood turn to cold brine. This was not pursuit. Pursuit was simple; pursuit you could outrun. This was craft. The king was not chasing them — he was *funneling* them, devouring the countryside in a great arc ahead and to either side, leaving exactly one path open: the road to the Door. He wanted them to reach it. He wanted them desperate and exhausted and willing to pay anything, because the Door did not open for the unwilling, and the king could not force it himself — but he could stand behind a broken pair of lovers and lap up whatever spilled.

And what would spill, Corvo knew, was everything.

He cut cross-country through the dead zones, moving fast and low, using tricks of the ferryman's trade that had nothing to do with water — walking backward through his own tracks, sleeping in the hollows of things too ruined to remember danger. Twice he heard the king's hunger pass overhead like weather: a pressure in the ears, a taste of copper, a moment when his own heartbeat seemed to pause and listen. Both times he pressed the glass vial tighter to his chest and thought, absurdly, *not yet, not this, I haven't delivered it.*

On the third day he found their camp.

Or rather, he found the ashes of it — and then found them, huddled in the lee of a fallen waystone, and stopped dead in the dark with his heart hammering, because he had imagined this meeting a hundred times on the road and in none of those imaginings had he managed to look them in the eye.

Mara saw him first. Of course she did. She came up off the ground with a shard of waystone gripped in her fist like a knife, and her eyes in the dying firelight were exactly what he deserved: flat, cold, unsurprised.

"Stop there," she said.

Sebastian rose more slowly. He was thinner than Corvo remembered — they'd all grown thinner; Sable ate from both ends, taking the past from some and the future from others — and there was something wrong with his gaze, something slightly unfocused, a man looking at the world through frosted glass. When his eyes found Corvo, they narrowed with effort, like someone squinting at a familiar face across a crowded room.

"Do I know you?" Sebastian asked.

The question landed harder than any blade could have. Corvo had known it was coming — the archivist had warned them all, in his blind, terrible way — but knowing a wound is coming does not blunt it.

"You did," Corvo said. His voice came out rougher than he intended. "Ferryman. Corvo. I carried you upriver. I—" He stopped. Every sentence he started ended in the same place, in the orchard, in the running, in the space where he should have been and wasn't. "I owe you both a debt I can't repay. I've come to try anyway."

"We don't need your help," Mara said.

"Yes," Corvo said quietly. "You do."

He knelt — deliberately, slowly, hands open and empty, the posture of a man surrendering to a court that had already sentenced him — and laid the glass vial on the ground between them. The firelight caught it, and inside, the golden afternoon turned and turned: green leaves, dappled sun, laughter mid-flight.

Mara stared at it. Something moved through her face — recognition, grief, fury, all at once, all fighting for the same ground.

"That's ours," she breathed. "That's the apple tree. You kept it. All this time, you kept it, and you still *left*—"

"I kept it because it was the only thing left in me that wasn't rotten," Corvo said. "I want you to understand what I am before you decide whether to hear me. I sold your travels gate by gate. I sold my name to the things in the orchard while you ran. I told myself a man has to survive, and then I got the name back, and do you know what the first thing was that I felt? Shame. Real shame, whole shame, shame with my name attached to it." He laughed, and it was an ugly sound. "That's what they gave me back. Not myself. My guilt. Turns out they're the same thing."

Sebastian had crouched near the vial without touching it. Now he looked up, and behind the frost in his eyes there was still something sharp, something that assessed. "Why come back? What do you get?"

"Nothing," Corvo said. "That's rather the point. Or it should be. I'd like to claim it's redemption, but I'm too old a liar to trust my own motives, so let me tell you what I actually know, and you can decide what I am afterward."

He settled back on his heels. Around them the salt plains hissed softly in the wind, and far off, the red glow of the king's hunger pulsed against the horizon like a slow heartbeat.

"The Door of Unmaking," Corvo said. "I've ferried pilgrims toward it for longer than I can count — literally longer than I can count; most of them are gone from me now. And every one of them believed the same thing: that the Door takes a price, a toll, memories, whatever you can spare. That's what the stories say. That's what I told you." He shook his head. "The stories are wrong. Or rather — they're the version for people who aren't meant to open it. I ferried one pilgrim who actually reached it, a hundred years ago or a thousand; she came back through my crossing going the other way, and she was empty as a winter reed, and before the last of her went, she told me the truth of it."

Mara had gone very still. "What truth?"

"The Door doesn't take memories," Corvo said. "Memories are just currency. What the Door takes is *what you love most*. Whole. Entire. Not the recollection of it — the love itself, torn out at the root. You can walk through having forgotten nothing, and still be unable to remember why anything matters." He looked at Sebastian, and made himself hold the boy's frosted gaze. "For most people, that's their own life. Their own name. Their own self-love, the deep animal thing that keeps a body walking. That's why the Door is called Unmaking. Most who open it, unmake themselves."

"But not everyone," Sebastian said. His voice was very quiet.

"No," Corvo said. "Not everyone. If what you love most is outside yourself — if it's another person — then the Door takes *that*. Every feeling. Every memory tangled in it, because the feelings live in the memories and can't be cut apart cleanly. You walk through loving nothing, missing nothing, grieving nothing — except you'll grieve, you'll feel the grief, with no object, no reason, no name. An ache with no address." He swallowed. "The pilgrim told me she watched her husband do it. He opened the Door for her and walked through smiling, and he looked at her on the other side like she was a stranger, and he was smiling because strangers can't break your heart."

Silence. The wind moved the ash of their fire.

"He'll do it," Mara said at last, and her voice cracked down the middle. "You've seen him. He's already paying. He'll stand in front of that Door and he'll lie to me and he'll do it, and I can't—"

"I know," Corvo said.

"And you came all this way to tell us this *now*?" She rounded on him, and the fury finally arrived, and he welcomed it, knelt into it, took it like rain after drought. "We're three days out. Maybe less. You could have—"

"Could have what?" Corvo asked gently. "Sent word? To whom? I burned every bridge I ever built, girl, and in Sable there's no paper and no post and no one remembers yesterday anyway. There was only ever coming back in person, on foot, through the dead zones, hoping I arrived before the king did." He spread his hands. "I'm late. I know I'm late. Redemption purchased too late isn't redemption — it's just an apology with mud on its boots. But it's what I have."

Sebastian picked up the vial. He held it to the light for a long moment, watching the golden afternoon turn, and Corvo watched the frost in his eyes deepen and crack all at once, because the boy couldn't remember the tree, couldn't remember the day, couldn't remember *her* laughing in it — but he could see that it mattered, could see it the way you see the shape of a missing tooth.

"What do we do?" Sebastian asked. "If the Door takes what you love most — how do we beat it? How does anyone?"

And here Corvo hesitated, because the last thing the pilgrim had told him was not a strategy. It was not a weapon or a trick or a loophole. It was smaller than that and larger, and he had carried it a century without understanding it, the way you carry a seed without knowing what tree it belongs to.

"She said one more thing," Corvo said. "At the end, when she was nearly gone. She said: *the Door takes what you love most — but it cannot take what you give away first.*"

Mara frowned. "What does that mean?"

"I don't know," Corvo admitted. "I've had a hundred years to work it out and I don't know. Give your love away first — to whom? To what? Maybe it's nonsense. Maybe it was just a dying woman's riddle." He rose stiffly to his feet, knees cracking, an old man's body though his face stayed young the way everyone's did in Sable, frozen at the age they'd been when the kingdom forgot itself. "But it's all I brought you besides the truth and the apology. Take the memory back. It was never mine. And then decide what you want from me, because I'm not leaving again. Whatever you're walking into, I walk into it too. Call it penance. Call it stupidity. Call it Corvo's choice — I finally have a name to hang a choice on, and I mean to use it."

Mara looked at him for a long time. Then she looked at Sebastian, and something passed between them that Corvo could not read and did not try to — some silent arithmetic of damage and necessity, two people deciding together how much broken help they could afford to accept.

"Get some sleep," Sebastian said finally. "We move at first light. The king's funnel is narrowing." He nodded east, toward the red pulse on the horizon, which had crept measurably closer since nightfall. "And Corvo — thank you. For coming back. I don't remember enough to know if I should trust you." A ghost of a smile, tired and real. "But she does, a little. And that's enough."

Corvo lay down on the cold salt earth with the wind talking overhead, and sleep, when it finally took him, was full of green leaves and dappled sun and a riddle he could not solve:

*What you give away first, the Door cannot take.*

Three days east, the king stopped hunting and began to wait.
