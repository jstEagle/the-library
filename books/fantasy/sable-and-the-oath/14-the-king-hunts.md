## 14. The King Hunts

The salt plains began where the last green thing in Sable had given up.

Sebastian felt the change under his boots before he saw it — a crunch like walking on old porcelain, a whiteness that stretched to every horizon and gave nothing back. The ground here was crusted over with mineral bloom, cracked into vast hexagonal plates, and when the wind moved across it, it made a sound he had never heard before and hoped never to hear again: a thin, keening whistle, as though the whole plain were breathing through broken teeth.

Behind them, three days' ride south, the king was eating.

They had seen the first village from a ridge at dusk. It had been a real place once — you could tell by the bones of its architecture, the graceful arches of its bridges, the orchard trees gone white and petrified like coral. But now the people stood motionless in their doorways and fields, dozens of them, faces smooth as eggs, eyes open and empty as weather. Not dead. Sable did not let its people die so easily. They simply had nothing left inside, and without memory a body was just a coat on a hook.

The king's hunger had passed through them like a tide.

"He's tracking us," Mara had said, her voice flat with horror. She'd learned to read the leavings of his feeding faster than Sebastian had — she said the air above an eaten place still shimmered faintly, the way heat shimmers off a road in summer. "He eats everything they remember about strangers passing through. Every face. Every rumor. He's using whole towns as nets."

"And if we go around the towns—"

"Then he eats the roads themselves." She'd pointed to the pale scar of the old highway below them, where the stones had lost their carvings, where the milestone markers stood blank as teeth pulled from a jaw. "He's not picky anymore. He hasn't fed properly since I got away from him. Look at him from far enough away and you can see it — he's starving, Sebastian. A starving thing doesn't set careful traps. It burns down the forest."

That had been two days ago. Since then they had walked north by night and hidden by day, folding themselves into the lee of salt formations and shattered wayshrines, sleeping in shifts, speaking in whispers even when there was no one for miles. The plains offered no cover worth the name. The horizon was a lie told in every direction.

* * *

On the fourth day, Sebastian counted the birds.

There shouldn't have been birds. Nothing lived out here — no insects, no grass, no water that wasn't brine. But high overhead, riding a thermal that shouldn't have existed, circled a single dark shape, patient as punctuation.

"Corvo?" Mara asked, following his gaze.

"No." Sebastian watched it bank, catch the light. It wasn't a bird at all, he realized. It was too regular, too deliberate in its circles. It was a shape the king wore because the king had once, in some stolen century, been a man who kept hawks. "It's him looking through something. Or it's bait."

"Everything is bait with him."

"Yes." Sebastian shouldered the pack and turned them east, off the line of the old highway, toward a jagged ridge of uplifted salt that might give them shadow. "So we stop being interesting."

Mara laughed — a short, exhausted sound with no joy in it. "I'm the girl named in a prophecy who escaped his court. You're the boy who forgets. We are the most interesting things this country has produced in a hundred years."

"I know," Sebastian said quietly. "But we can be uninteresting *tired* instead of interesting *dead*."

They walked. The sun here did not warm; it interrogated. By midday the salt glare came up off the ground so hard it felt physical, pressing against his eyeballs, and the world reduced itself to white below and blue above and Mara's dark head bobbing ahead of him, counting her steps under her breath the way she did when she was frightened and didn't want him to know it.

He knew all her tells now. That was the terrible arithmetic of the last weeks: every mile cost them something, but it also taught him something, and he hoarded what he learned the way a miser hoards coin. The way she pressed her tongue against her back teeth when she was calculating. The way she hummed three notes of a song she couldn't finish, some melody from before Sable took her, and always stopped at the same place as if the rest of it had been amputated. The way she looked at him when she thought he was asleep — not soft, exactly. *Inventorying.* As if she were memorizing him against a day when memorizing would be all there was.

He was doing the same thing. Neither of them said so. Saying it would make it true, and it was already true, and some part of them both understood that whatever was coming at the end of this country would demand payment, and they were spending these days the way you spend the last of the good wine — slowly, deliberately, tasting everything.

* * *

The second village found them before they could avoid it.

It clung to the edge of a dry lakebed — a scatter of low houses built from compressed salt brick, gleaming dully in the afternoon like lumps of sugar. Smoke rose from chimneys. Figures moved in the fields, harvesting something pale and rootlike from the crust.

"People," Mara breathed, and there was such naked relief in her voice that Sebastian's chest hurt. Days of empty country did that to you. Made you forget that most of Sable's emptiness was manufactured by the thing chasing you.

"They might be hollowed already," he warned. "We check the eyes first. If they're clear—"

But a woman had already seen them. She dropped her harvest basket and ran toward them across the field, shouting words that dissolved into the wind, and behind her came others — a dozen, twenty, faces brown and lined and *full*, eyes dark and human and terrified.

"They're alive," Mara whispered. "Really alive."

The woman reached them first, seizing Mara's hands, then Sebastian's, talking fast in the rural dialect of the plains, and it took Sebastian a moment to untangle it because the woman was crying while she spoke.

"You're running from the court. From the king." Not a question. "Word came up the salt-road yesterday — the king himself is moving, not sending riders, *him*, in his palanquin of glass, and everything behind him is gray. Everything behind him doesn't remember being anything." Her grip tightened. "You have to hide. You have to hide and we have to pretend we never saw you, because if he comes here—"

"If he comes here, he'll eat you whether or not you saw us," Sebastian said gently. "Hiding us won't save you."

"It might!" An old man had come up, leaning on a tool with a blade of polished salt. His voice cracked like the ground. "He takes what knows you. If we don't know you—" He stopped. Everyone stopped. And Sebastian watched, with a sickening lurch, as the entire village arrived at the same arithmetic at the same moment, and understood why the woman's hands had begun to shake.

If they didn't know the fugitives, the king couldn't take the knowledge of them from their minds. But the king was starving, and starving kings do not audit their meals.

"We won't stay," Mara said quickly. "Water. Just water, and we're gone before dark. We were never here."

The villagers exchanged looks over their heads — quick, desperate, guilty looks — and Sebastian recognized the expression because he had worn it once himself, standing in front of Elder Maren with an oath scroll in his hands. It was the look of decent people discovering the exact weight of their decency, and finding it lighter than they'd hoped.

They gave the water anyway. They always did. That was the thing about Sable that the histories back home had gotten wrong, Sebastian thought, drinking from a chipped clay cup in a salt-brick kitchen while children peered at him from doorways: the kingdom hadn't fallen because its people were wicked. It was falling the way a body falls to fever — the king was the sickness, and everyone else was just trying to stay a person for one more day.

They left before sunset, as promised, taking three skins of water and a sack of the pale roots, which tasted like nothing and kept you walking anyway. Behind them the village stood in its fields watching them go, and not one of them waved, and Sebastian loved them for it.

Two miles on, Mara finally spoke. "They'll tell him anyway. When he comes. They'll beg, and they'll tell him which way we went, because the alternative is watching him hollow their children."

"I know."

"And you still drank their water."

"I know," Sebastian said again. "That's how he wins, Mara. Not by making people evil. By making kindness expensive."

She was quiet for a long time. The plain whitened around them as the light failed, until they seemed to walk on the surface of a frozen sea under a sky going the color of a bruise.

"Then we make it cheap," she said at last. "We get to the Door. We end him. And nobody ever has to choose again."

It was a good sentence. It was almost believable. They walked on it like a bridge.

* * *

The king caught up to the horizon on the sixth night.

Sebastian saw it first — he'd taken the middle watch, wrapped in both their cloaks against a cold that bit through salt-crusted wool — and for a long moment he thought the southern sky was on fire. Then he understood that it was the opposite of fire. Fire gives light. This was a region of the darkness that *gave less than darkness*, a hole in the night shaped like progress, like appetite, moving.

And it was singing.

Not the king himself. His escort — the hollowed ones, the courtiers whose memories had been spent long ago and whose bodies the king kept the way a miser keeps empty purses. Hundreds of them, maybe thousands, marching in step across the plain with lanterns they no longer knew how to hold, singing a coronation hymn in perfect unison with voices that contained no one at all. The sound carried for miles over the salt, beautiful and obscene, a choir with no congregation.

Mara woke at the first note. She didn't ask what it was. She'd heard it before, from the walls of the court, and Sebastian watched her whole body change — watched the exhaustion burn off her like fog off a morning field, replaced by something harder.

"How fast is he moving?"

"Faster than us. Always faster than us." Sebastian was already kicking dirt over their cold fire-pit, already cinching the pack straps. "But he stops to feed. Every town, every ruin, every lonely shrine with a single pilgrim's prayer left in it — he stops and he eats. That's our margin. That's all it is. He's a fire and we're the smoke, and smoke moves faster than fire until it doesn't."

"Poetic," Mara muttered, shouldering her pack. "Where?"

"Northwest. There's supposed to be a range of stone hills past the flats — the ferryman's map showed caves." Corvo's map. Corvo's name sat between them these days like a knife left on a table; neither of them touched it, neither of them put it away. The ferryman had sold them once already, trading their route to the king's agents for a purse of his own returned memories, and only his own guilt — and a debt Mara refused to explain fully — had brought him back to warn them the night the court nearly had them at the Bone Orchard's edge. Trust was a currency in Sable, and theirs was nearly spent.

They ran. There was no art to it anymore, no cleverness. The clever phase of their flight had died somewhere on the salt, and what remained was animal: run, hide, sleep in fragments, run. The hymn behind them swelled and faded and swelled again as the king's column paused to feed and moved on, paused and fed and moved on, and each pause bought them an hour, and each hour cost some pocket of Sable its entire self.

At dawn they crested a rise of crusted salt and looked back, just once, because Mara needed to see it and Sebastian needed to see her see it.

The column stretched across the plain like a wound. At its center rode a palanquin of black glass on the shoulders of forty hollow men, and above the palanquin hung a light that was not a light — a slow, pulsing dimness, like a heart made of eclipse. Even at four miles' distance Sebastian felt it touch him, feel along the edges of his mind with fingers greasy with other people's lives, cataloguing, appraising, *hungry*. He had paid tolls at the border and at three gates since; he knew the sensation of memory leaving. This was different. This was memory being *shopped for*.

"He felt me," Mara whispered. Her hand had found his without either of them deciding it. "He does that. He tastes the air and I'm — I'm the meal that got away. He wants me specifically. The prophecy named me, and in Sable a name is a flavor."

"Then we make sure he starves." Sebastian turned her gently by the shoulders, away from the column, toward the northwest, where — God willing, though he had stopped believing God had any jurisdiction here — the stone hills waited. "One more day. Maybe two. We stay ahead of him until the hills, and then we lose him in rock, and then we run for the Door while he tears the mountains apart looking."

"And if the caves are a story the ferryman told us to keep us moving? If he sold us the map *and* the route?"

"Then we improvise." Sebastian heard his own voice come out level and calm and wondered distantly whose voice it had been before Sable started collecting pieces of him. His mother's face was gone. The guildhall's smell — beeswax and ink and cold stone — was gone. He had traded them at tollgates to keep them both breathing, and he had done it without hesitation, and some nights he lay awake trying to mourn them and finding only clean, smooth absence, like the socket of a tooth pulled so long ago the gum had healed over.

That was the worst of it. Not the losing. The *not minding*.

"Sebastian." Mara was looking at him with that inventorying gaze again, and he realized his hand was pressed flat against his own sternum, the way he did now when he checked whether anything else was missing. "What did you just lose?"

"Nothing." He smiled. It worked, mostly. "Nothing yet. Come on. Daylight's wasting."

* * *

They gained the foothills at dusk on the seventh day, and the hills were real.

Stone rose out of the salt in broken ribs — grey limestone riddled with caves and collapsed galleries, streaked with the mineral stains of some ancient wet age. Real cover. Real shadow. Sebastian could have wept at the sight of a horizon with texture to it.

They climbed until full dark, following a goat-track that became a scramble that became a crawl, and finally wedged themselves into a cave mouth thirty feet up the cliff face, deep enough that the wind couldn't find them, high enough that the plain below was a silver sheet stitched with the distant glow of the king's column, still hours south and west, still feeding.

Mara slept first this time. She'd been fighting it for two days, rationing her unconsciousness like the water, and finally her body simply collected its debt. She curled against the cave wall with her pack for a pillow, and within minutes her breathing went long and deep, and the tension she carried everywhere, in her jaw, in her shoulders, in the constant flicking of her eyes to the horizon — all of it let go at once, and she looked about sixteen years old, and impossibly far from home, and brave in the way that only unconscious people can be, having stopped performing courage and simply become it.

Sebastian sat with his back to the rock and kept the watch and did not sleep.

Below, the king's glow pulsed. Nearer tonight. A day, maybe less, if the column stopped for nothing. And somewhere ahead, past these hills, past the glass wastes the ferryman had described with his ruined half-voice, the Door of Unmaking waited in its valley of silence — the Door that opened for sacrifice, that ate love, that had been the whole point of every mile since the Bone Orchard.

He found himself doing the inventory again. It was a habit now, a rosary: *my mother's face, gone; my guild, gone; the valley's name, gone; the oath I burned, gone; the color of the study where I first kissed her —* he tested it, carefully, the way you probe a sore tooth — *still there. Still mine.*

For now.

In her sleep, Mara hummed her three notes and stopped where the song was cut, and in the silence after, the wind through the rocks filled in with a sound almost like the rest of the melody, and Sebastian listened to it and thought: *whatever it costs at that Door, I will pay it, and I will lie to her about it, and she will hate me, and she will live.*

The thought settled over him with a terrible peace.

Then, below, on the silver sheet of the plain, the king's glow stopped pulsing.

It flared — one vast, silent exhalation of hungry dark — and swung, unmistakably, deliberately, toward the hills.

Toward them.

And in the cave behind him, still asleep, Mara began to sing the rest of her song — every note of it, clear and complete, in a voice that was not entirely hers.
