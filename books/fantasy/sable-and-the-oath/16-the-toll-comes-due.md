## 16. The Toll Comes Due

The salt plains ended where the world began to bleed.

Sebastian felt it before he saw it — a wrongness in the air, the way the light bent toward them instead of past them, as though Sable itself had turned its face to watch. Behind them, on the horizon, the sky had gone the color of an old bruise, and in that bruise something moved. Not fast. Not slow. With the terrible patience of hunger.

"He's found the trail," Corvo said, and his voice had lost all its ferryman's ease. The man who had once bargained for memories like a merchant haggling over fish now gripped the rail of nothing at all, staring back the way they'd come. "He ate the village at Duskwater. All of it. Every name, every grave, every grudge three generations deep. He didn't leave enough of it to cast a shadow." Corvo swallowed. "That's how he tracks you now. By what's missing."

Mara stood beside Sebastian, her hand wrapped around his, her knuckles white. She had not slept in two days. None of them had. Sleep was a country none of them could afford.

"How long?" Sebastian asked.

"Until nightfall," Corvo said. "Maybe less. The king doesn't tire. He only gets *hungrier*."

They ran.

* * *

The land ahead of them had once been farmland — Sebastian could tell by the fossil shadows of hedgerows pressed into the salt crust, by the ghost-rows of an orchard whose trees had been eaten down to their last remembered apple. Now it was just emptiness, cracked and pale, stretching toward a line of hills that looked drawn rather than grown. Somewhere beyond those hills, Corvo said, lay the Door of Unmaking. A day's walk. Perhaps less, if the hills didn't hate them.

Behind them, the bruise spread.

It wasn't a storm. Storms moved with wind. This moved with *appetite*. Where it passed, the salt plains did not darken — they *simplified*. Complexity drained out of the world like color from a wet painting. Rocks became smooth. Ruins became mounds. And once, far off to their left, a herd of wild goats became, for one horrible moment, simply *animals*, grazing without names or fear or history, blank-eyed and content, before the hunger passed and they were goats again, screaming.

Mara stumbled. Sebastian caught her arm.

"We can't outrun it," she gasped. "You know we can't."

"I know we can't," he said. "So we don't try."

He stopped at the foot of the hills, where a shrine stood — or what remained of one. A stone alcove, roofless, its saint long since devoured; only the hollow where her statue had been remained, a saint-shaped absence in the rock. Sebastian set Mara inside it, against the wall, and turned to face the plain.

"What are you doing?" Mara's voice cracked. "Sebastian—"

"The toll," he said. "The border taught me how it works. Memory is currency here. It always has been. The king eats it — but so does everything else, if you know how to pay." He flexed his fingers, feeling for the old warden gestures, the ones carved into his hands deeper than any oath. "I'm going to pay."

"Pay *what?*" 

"Enough to buy us the hills." He smiled at her, and it was the smile from their first date, warm and crooked and lying through its teeth. "Stay behind me. Whatever I forget, don't remind me. If I forget *you*—" His voice caught. "Don't let me stop walking."

"*Sebastian.*"

But he was already turning away from her, raising his hands, and speaking the words every Ashgrove warden learned before they learned to write: *what is given freely cannot be stolen.*

The hunger came down the plain like a tide.

And Sebastian opened himself to it, and paid.

* * *

The first memory went like a tooth pulled.

His guildhall — gone. The great oak table scarred by a century of apprentice knives. Elder Maren's voice cracking as she recited the oath. The smell of ink and beeswax and cold stone. He felt it lift out of him whole, a room with all its furniture, and the hunger took it and *slowed*, sated for a moment, and in that moment they ran for the hills.

The second memory bought them the foothills.

His mother's lullaby — he'd kept her face until the border, but the song had survived, and now it went too, melody and all, leaving a silence in him shaped exactly like a voice he could no longer hear. He wept as he ran and did not know why, which was somehow worse than knowing.

The third bought them the switchback trail.

His apprenticeship. Ten years of mornings. The weight of the boundary stones in his palms. The pride of a ward holding true. It left him and he staggered, because without it he no longer knew why his hands knew things. They simply did. Skills without stories. A tool with no smith.

"Stop," Mara begged, dragging at his sleeve. "Sebastian, stop, there has to be another way—"

"There isn't." He heard his own voice and barely recognized it. Flat. Lighter. Each payment made him lighter, and lighter was not the same as free. "There's never been another way. There was only ever the door, and the door has a price, and I am paying it *early* so we live long enough to reach it."

Corvo watched from a boulder, and his face was terrible to see. "Boy," the ferryman said quietly, "you're spending yourself like a drunkard spends coin."

"I'm spending myself like a man who knows the bar closes at dawn."

* * *

They gained the ridge at dusk, and the king came to the foot of the hills, and stopped.

He was smaller than Sebastian had imagined. That was the horror of it — not a colossus but a *man*, gaunt and crowned, standing in the ruined fields below with his head tilted like a bird's, listening. Listening to them. To the memories still ticking inside Sebastian like coins in a purse.

"You smell of the valley," the king called up, and his voice was gentle, which was the worst thing about it. "Warden's boy. You carry a whole country in you. Green things. Rain. A hundred years of safety." He smiled, and his teeth were ordinary teeth. "Come down. I'll be kind. I'll take it slowly. You'll feel every year of it go, like snow melting, and at the end you'll be *light*."

Sebastian stepped in front of Mara.

"You've eaten a kingdom," he said. "And you're still hungry. Do you know what that makes you?"

"Alive," said the king.

"It makes you a hole," Sebastian said. "And holes don't get filled. They just get *bigger*."

The king sighed, and the sigh rippled up the hillside, and the salt beneath their feet began to remember being ocean — vast, drowning, pulling them down — and Sebastian understood that the king would not chase them anymore. He would simply make the ground itself hungry.

So Sebastian paid again.

His home — the cottage, the garden, the specific creak of the third stair. Gone. His teachers' faces — gone, all of them at once, a whole classroom emptied. The valley itself, the shape of it seen from the ridge at morning, mist in the low fields — gone, and with it went the word *home*, leaving only the concept, a house-shaped hole in a heart-shaped dark.

He paid until the world narrowed to a single point: the girl behind him, the hills ahead, the door somewhere past them both.

He paid until even *Sebastian* began to thin.

* * *

It happened on the high shoulder of the hills, under the first stars.

The king climbed. Of course he climbed. Hunger learns stairs. And they ran along the ridgeline with the ground softening beneath them, memory-hungry, and Corvo — Corvo stopped running.

Sebastian turned. "What are you doing?"

Corvo stood on the ridge with the wind pulling at his coat, and for the first time since the bone orchard, since the betrayal, since the guilt that had dragged him back to them like a fish on a line — for the first time, he looked *unafraid*.

"A ferryman's debt," Corvo said. "I sold your road once, girl. I sold this boy's name to the court for a chance at my own. And I've been carrying that like ballast ever since." He grinned at them, gap-toothed, ancient, alive. "Time to put it down."

"Corvo—" Mara started forward.

The king crested the slope below them, and Corvo walked *down* to meet him.

"No!" Mara screamed.

But the ferryman spread his arms wide, and Sebastian saw what he meant to do, and there was no time, there had never been any time—

"Here's my fare," Corvo shouted to the king, laughing. "Every crossing I ever made! Every soul I ferried! Every name I ever learned and sold and spent! Take it *all*, you great starving *hole* — take a life's worth and *choke* on it!"

And he gave it. Freely. All at once. A lifetime poured out in a single breath — decades of river-fog and lantern-light and passengers' secrets, the whole cargo of him, dumped into the king's open mouth like gold into a miser's throat.

The king reeled. Staggered. For one shining instant, the crown slipped sideways on a brow suddenly too full, too heavy, a vessel overfilled — and the hunger *stalled*, gorged, blind with surplus—

"RUN," Corvo roared, still laughing, already unraveling, his edges going soft as river-mist. "Run, you beautiful idiots! And when you reach the door — tell it — tell it Corvo finally paid his—"

The wind took the rest.

Where the ferryman had stood there was nothing but a lantern, falling, unbroken, on the salt.

* * *

They ran.

Down the far side of the hills, through a valley of black grass that whispered in voices almost familiar, hand in hand, while behind them the king convulsed and roared and burned through a dead man's lifetime — and Sebastian felt the last of himself fraying with every step.

At the crest of the final rise, Mara pulled him to a stop and grabbed his face in both hands.

"Say your name," she demanded. Tears cut clean lines through the salt dust on her cheeks. "Right now. Say it."

He opened his mouth.

And found nothing there.

Not forgotten — *spent*. He had paid it somewhere on the ridgeline without noticing, one more coin among coins, and now there was only a smooth blank place where a boy used to keep himself. He searched, and searched, and the panic rose in him like floodwater — and then Mara's eyes caught the starlight, gray-green and furious and alive, and some part of him that had no name reached for her anyway.

"I don't have one," he whispered. "I paid it. I think I paid it a mile ago. I'm sorry — I don't—"

"Then listen to me." She pressed her forehead to his. Her breath was ragged. "Your name is Sebastian. Sebastian Ashgrove. Last apprentice of the Ashgrove wardens. You broke an oath to save me. You burned the scroll. You crossed the border. You gave up your mother's face and your guild and your valley and your *name*, and you did it all so I could walk, and I will not let a door or a king or a god take the rest of you. Do you understand me? *I'll carry it.* Until you can hold it again, I'll carry all of it."

Below them, past the black grass, past the whispering dark, something glimmered.

A door, standing alone in a field of salt. Ancient. Patient. Waiting.

And behind them, gathering itself out of a dead ferryman's feast, the king began to scream.

Sebastian took her hand — the hand that held his name now, along with everything else — and together they ran down toward the light.
