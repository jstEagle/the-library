## 17. The Door of Unmaking

### Sebastian

The salt plains ended at the lip of a canyon so deep that the sky seemed to have forgotten it. Sebastian stood at its edge, wind tearing at his coat, and looked down at a staircase carved from black stone — a thousand steps spiraling into a dark that was not empty but *watching*.

Beside him, Mara pulled her cloak tighter. Her hands had not stopped shaking since Corvo fell. She hadn't spoken his name aloud. Neither had he. Some names were too heavy to carry in the mouth; they belonged to the ground now, along with everything else they'd paid on the road here.

"That's it?" she asked. "That's where it lives?"

"The archivist said the Door doesn't live anywhere." Sebastian's voice came out rough as gravel. He'd spent his own name days ago, and without it some part of him felt like a house with the furniture removed — still standing, still sheltering, but echoing in all the wrong places. "It just is. Like weather."

"Cheerful."

He almost smiled. That was her gift, even now — the small dry joke held up like a lantern against the dark. He wanted to tell her how much light she'd carried for him. He couldn't. Not because he lacked the words, but because the words would lead somewhere he could not let them go.

Below them, at the bottom of the stair, something pulsed. Once. Slowly. Like a heartbeat heard through a wall.

Mara felt it too — he saw it in the way she went still, the way her breath caught and held. "It knows we're here," she whispered.

"It's known since the border," Sebastian said. "Maybe since before either of us was born."

They began to descend.

The steps were worn smooth in the centers, hollowed by feet that no longer existed, belonging to people Sable had eaten whole. Sebastian counted them out of habit — wardens counted, always, because counting was a way of insisting the world had edges — but somewhere past four hundred the numbers began slipping through his fingers like water. He stopped counting. There was no one left inside him who needed to know.

Halfway down, Mara stumbled, and he caught her arm, and for a moment they stood pressed together on the narrow stair while the wind moaned up out of the deep like a choir rehearsing grief.

"I keep thinking about the inn," she said quietly. "The dancing. You stepped on my foot twice and blamed the floor."

"The floor was uneven."

"It was perfectly flat and you know it."

"I don't know anything anymore," he said, and meant it as a joke, and watched it land wrong — watched her face do the thing it did when she thought he wasn't looking, that crumpling behind the composure, that grief she kept folding smaller and smaller so it would fit inside the journey.

He turned away first. It was easier to lie to her if he wasn't looking at her.

The last hundred steps opened into a cavern vast enough to hold a cathedral, or a sky, or a lie large enough to build a life on. And there, at its center, floating a hand's breadth above the black floor, hung the Door of Unmaking.

It was not grand. That was the horror of it. It was the size of an ordinary cottage door, plain oak gone silver with age, iron hinges rusted to lace. No frame held it. No wall surrounded it. It simply stood upright in the emptiness, and around it the air bent inward like light approaching a star, and every surface of the cavern — every stone, every shadow — leaned toward it the way flowers lean toward sun.

Carved into the wood, in letters older than the kingdom that had died around it, were words that rearranged themselves as Sebastian watched:

**WHAT DO YOU LOVE MOST?**

His heart knew the answer before his mind could flinch away from it. His heart had always known. Every mile of the salt plains, every memory burned as toll, every piece of himself he'd fed to this hungry country — through all of it, one thing had remained constant, anchored, *true*, and the Door had smelled it on him from the moment he crossed into Sable.

Mara.

Every memory of Mara. The date at the noodle shop where she'd laughed so hard she snorted wine. The study, the glowing name in the prophecy book, her face going white as paper. Midnight, the seam of light, her fingers sliding out of his. The ferry crossing. The argument in the ruins. The inn, the dance, the way she'd said *memorize me* like it was a prayer. All of it. Everything. The Door wanted the entire architecture of her built inside him, torn out root and branch, and it wanted him to hand it over willingly.

Because that was the price of doors, wasn't it? They only opened from the inside.

"Sebastian." Mara's voice was very small in the enormous dark. "What is it asking?"

He realized she couldn't read the letters. They were written in whatever language a person's own heart spoke, and hers was speaking something different — or perhaps the Door was saving her question for later, the way a careful merchant saves his best goods.

"It's asking what I love most," he said.

Her breath stopped. He heard it stop — heard the silence where her breathing should have been — and he knew, in that instant, that she understood. She was quick. She had always been quicker than him at seeing the shape of a trap.

"No," she said.

"Mara—"

"*No.*" She grabbed his sleeve, spun him to face her, and her eyes were wet and furious and terrified all at once. "No, I know what you're doing. I can see it in your face. You're already saying goodbye. You've been saying goodbye since the plains, you — every time I asked what the tolls were taking, you changed the subject, you—" Her voice cracked. "Tell me I'm wrong. Look me in the eyes and tell me I'm wrong."

The Door pulsed. Patient. Hungry. Certain.

Sebastian took both her hands in his. They were cold. When had her hands gotten so cold?

"There's another way," he said.

And he lied.

He lied the way he had never been able to lie to her before — smoothly, calmly, with the steady voice of a warden explaining the rules of wards to a frightened child. *The Door takes a toll from whoever opens it, but it doesn't have to be the whole toll. The archivist said the Door can be bargained with. We'll split it. Half each. That's all. Half each, and we walk through together.*

Every word was a stone laid carefully over a grave.

She searched his face. God, how she searched it — he felt her gaze move across him like a lamp, looking for the crack in the wall, the seam in the lie. And because she loved him, because love makes us generous readers of the people we cherish, she wanted to believe him. He watched the wanting war with the knowing behind her eyes, and he hated himself with a completeness he hadn't managed even when the king was devouring his childhood.

"Half each," she repeated slowly.

"Half each."

"You swear it?"

"I swear it."

Something in her shoulders loosened — not belief, exactly, but the decision to stand beside belief until evidence arrived. "Okay," she whispered. "Okay. But Sebastian — if you're lying to me—"

"I'm not lying to you."

The second lie cost more than the first. The first had been craft. This one was a knife he turned in his own chest, and he smiled through it, calm as still water, because that was the last gift he had left to give her: a face she could trust, right up until the moment she couldn't.

Behind him, the Door waited. It was very good at waiting. It had been waiting since before kingdoms had names.

### Mara

There was a hairline fracture running through everything he said, and Mara stood on one side of it trying to pretend the ground was solid.

She let him lead her toward the Door. She let him talk — low, steady, reasonable — about halves and bargains and the archivist's words, and she nodded in the right places, and all the while some cold clear part of her mind, the part that had survived a kingdom that ate memory, sat apart and watched and *knew*.

She knew because she had seen him lie only once before, years ago, badly, about whether he'd read her unfinished manuscript. He'd touched his collar then. He touched it now.

She knew because the Door's carved letters had finally resolved for her in the last few steps — her heart's language arriving late, like thunder after lightning — and the question beneath the question was no mystery: **WHAT DO YOU LOVE MOST?** And she had looked at Sebastian and thought, *you*, without hesitation, without shame, the answer rising in her like water finding its level. If the Door asked her, she would pay in him. Every memory. Gladly. And she understood, with sickening clarity, that the Door was not asking her.

It was asking him. And his answer was already written on his face.

But she walked with him anyway. Because what else was there? Because maybe she was wrong. Because love, at the end of the world, looks a great deal like walking toward a door you dread, holding the hand of someone who has already decided to burn.

Ten feet away, the Door's presence pressed against her like deep water. She could feel it reading her — flipping through her the way she'd flip through the prophecy book, page after page, tasting. It lingered, she sensed, on certain pages. The noodle shop. The inn. The sound of his terrible laugh when he was truly happy, which happened perhaps six times total in all the time she'd known him, and which she would have recognized blindfolded, underwater, at the end of any lifetime.

"Wait." She stopped. "Before we — before anyone pays anything. I want to say something."

He turned. In the Door's silver light his face was gaunt and calm, and the calm was the most frightening thing about him.

"If there's a bargain to be made," she said, "I make it. Me. Not you. Promise me that."

"Mara—"

"*Promise me.*"

A pause, one heartbeat long, and in that heartbeat she saw him weigh the lie, measure it, find it still load-bearing. "I promise," he said gently. "Now come here."

She came. Of course she came. She put her arms around him and pressed her face into his coat, into the smell of salt and ash and him, and she memorized him — deliberately, ferociously, the way they'd memorized each other in the inn — the exact pressure of his hand between her shoulder blades, the uneven rhythm of his heartbeat, the way he tucked his chin against her hair like he was afraid the wind would take her.

"I love you," she said into his chest. "Whatever happens at this door. Whatever it asks. I need that sentence to exist somewhere outside both of us, so it survives even if we don't. Do you understand? Say it back."

His arms tightened. For a moment — one suspended, endless moment — she felt the truth of him break through the calm like a fish breaking water: the whole silent drowning weight of what he intended, surfacing, visible, gone.

"I love you," he said. "That's the truest thing I have left."

*Left.* The word went through her like ice water. What was left. What remained after the tolls. How little of him the road here had spared, and how much less there was about to be.

She pulled back. Smiled at him. Let him see the smile, because if these were the last minutes of being known by him, she refused to spend them crying.

"Open your half of the bargain, then," she said softly. "Warden."

He nodded. Turned to the Door. Rolled his shoulders back the way he did before difficult things, the way he'd done at midnight in the valley when the wards failed and the light took her — and oh, she should have known then, she should have *known*, that a man who reaches for you like that once will reach for you like that again, that some people are built to stand between you and the dark and pay whatever the dark demands.

Sebastian raised his hand to the Door of Unmaking.

And the carved letters blazed — **WHAT DO YOU LOVE MOST?** — and the cavern filled with a sound like a held breath, and Mara lunged forward, already knowing, already screaming *no* inside where her voice couldn't reach him—

"I love her," Sebastian said clearly, calmly, in the voice of a man laying down a heavy burden at last. "Every memory of her. Take them all."

The Door swung open onto a light that was not light.

And Mara Venn, named in ink, made her choice in the space between one heartbeat and the next — she threw herself forward, hand outstretched, reaching for him as the forgetting came down like a curtain —

—and found the Door's edge passing *through* her, cold as winter glass, closing behind him like a mouth.

On the far side of the threshold, in a country that was no longer Sable, a man stood blinking in ordinary sunlight, holding a woman's hand and not knowing whose it was.

On this side, Mara fell to her knees before a door that was already becoming a door no longer, and screamed his name into the closing light — and heard, faintly, impossibly, from beyond the seam, a stranger's voice ask her, gentle and lost:

*"Do I... know you?"*
