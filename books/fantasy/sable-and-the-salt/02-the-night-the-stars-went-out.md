## 2. The Night the Stars Went Out

The dead had been restless all evening, and by moonrise they would not be quiet at all.

Juniper Ashgrove sat in the ledger room of the Salt Guild with a rib bone cradled in her palms, pretending—she was always pretending—that she could not hear it. The bone belonged to a woman named Maren Colle, dead forty years, and it remembered a lullaby. It hummed against Juniper's skin like a struck tuning fork, the melody leaking through her fingers and up her wrists and settling somewhere behind her sternum where she kept everything she wasn't supposed to feel.

*Hush now, little sparrow. The salt wind sings you home.*

Juniper's stylus hovered over the ledger's ruled lines. *Item 447: carved rib-bone flute, provenance Colle estate, no anomalous properties.* The lie sat on the page like a stone on a grave.

Around her, the guild hall settled into its nighttime sounds—the tick of cooling braziers, the groan of salt-crusted beams, the distant shuffle of night clerks in the scriptorium above. Ordinary sounds. Safe sounds. She had spent thirty years learning to live inside them, folding herself small enough to fit into the space between one ordinary thing and the next.

But tonight the dead were pressing close.

They did that sometimes, when the wards along the border thinned with the season. They gathered at the edges of her hearing like moths at a lamp, murmuring their unfinished business: *the debt is owed, the debt is owed, tell the carver, tell the carver.* They'd been saying that for three nights now. *Tell the carver.* Juniper had decided it meant someone else. There were other carvers. There had to be other carvers.

She set Maren Colle's rib down gently and pressed her palms flat against the desk until the humming faded to a whisper, then to nothing—or to what passed for nothing, which was never truly silence for her, only a quieter kind of noise.

That was when the first star went out.

She didn't see it. She felt it—a sensation like a stitch pulled from the seam of the world, a small cold tug behind her eyes. Then another. Then another, faster, a cascade of tiny extinguishings, and Juniper stood up so quickly her chair skidded back and cracked against the floor.

Through the ledger room's high window, the sky was going out.

Not clouding. Not dimming. Going *out*, constellation by constellation, as if some vast hand were snuffing candles down a corridor. The Salt Road's guiding stars—the Cartwright, the Weeping Sisters, old Thorne's Lantern that every guild child learned before they learned their letters—vanished one after another, and the darkness that came rushing in behind them was not the soft dark of night but something thicker, something with texture, like ink poured across glass.

The last star held for a heartbeat longer than the rest. It hung above the eastern hills, stubborn, flickering—and then it, too, guttered and died, and the world had no stars in it at all.

Juniper's breath came out of her in a thin white stream, though the room was not cold yet. It would be. The dead went everywhere first, and the dead brought winter with them.

Then the guild hall began to fill.

They came up through the floors the way damp comes through plaster—not walking, exactly, but arriving, seeping, condensing out of the air itself. Juniper had seen ghosts her whole life; she had built her entire existence around the careful pretense that she hadn't. But she had never seen anything like this. They came in dozens, then hundreds, crowding the corridors and stairwells, standing shoulder to shoulder in the great hall below, and every one of them turned toward the ledger room as if pulled by a single string.

Salt-farmers in rotted oilskins. Guild clerks with ledgers fused to their skeletal hands. Children. Soldiers in armor green with age. A woman whose face Juniper knew from the portrait in the entry hall—First Warden Elsbeth Ashgrove, founder of the guild, dead two hundred years, her painted eyes now burning in a face made of gray light.

*The debt is owed,* they said, in a hundred voices that were somehow one voice. *The covenant fails. Tell the carver. Wake the carver.*

"I'm not—" Juniper's voice cracked. She gripped the edge of her desk. "I don't know what you want."

*Liar,* said the dead, gently, the way family says it. *Bone-carver. Name-keeper. You have heard us since you were small enough to hold in two hands. The wards are falling. The toll must be paid. Tell the carver.*

Her hands were shaking. She looked down at them—at the fine white scars across her fingertips from thirty years of carving, at the calluses shaped by gravers and files—and understood, with a lurch like a trapdoor opening, that they had always known. Every ghost she'd ever pretended not to hear. Every instrument she'd catalogued as *inert*. They had known, and they had waited, patient as bones in the ground, for the night the waiting ended.

Below, the great doors of the guild hall boomed.

Three knocks, spaced slow and deliberate, each one rolling through the building like a drumbeat in a chest. The crowd of dead parted, rippling backward, opening a lane from the doors to the stairs, and Juniper felt the pull of it even from above—an invitation written in emptied space.

She should have run. She thought about running. Her feet carried her down instead, past the dead who bowed their heads as she passed, whispering *carver, carver* like a tide, down the main staircase into the great hall where the braziers burned low and blue.

The doors opened on the third knock without anyone touching them.

Kestrel Vane stood in the doorway with snow in his hair and blood on his sleeve, and seven years fell away between one heartbeat and the next.

He was thinner than she remembered. Harder. The boy who had left Saltmere with his enforcer's badge stripped off and thrown in the mud at his feet had been all sharp angles and barely leashed fury; the man in the doorway had weathered into something quieter and more dangerous, like driftwood that had been at sea too long to float lightly anymore. His coat was Sable-cut, black wool gone gray at the seams, and in his gloved hands he carried something wrapped in oilcloth that glowed faintly through the fabric—a pale, sickly light, the color of the last star dying.

"Hello, June," he said.

Of all the terrors of the night—the vanished sky, the army of the dead, the voice of a woman two centuries in her grave—it was this, absurdly, that nearly broke her. *June.* No one had called her that since. No one had dared.

"You can't be here," she said. "You're exiled. If Aldous sees you—"

"Aldous will have larger concerns by morning." He stepped inside, and the doors swung shut behind him with a sound like a held breath released. Up close she could see the wardstone shard through the gap in the oilcloth: a fragment of pale crystal shot through with veins of silver, cracked down the middle, still humming with a frequency she felt in her teeth. "It fell near Harrow's Ford. Out of the sky. Like it was tired of holding." His eyes moved over her face, hungry and guilty at once, cataloguing changes the way she catalogued bones. "Seven years, June."

"Don't." The word came out sharper than she intended. "Don't stand in my guild hall with your fallen stones and your seven years. The sky just went dark, Kestrel. The dead are—" She gestured helplessly at the hundreds of ghosts watching them in silence, and hated herself for admitting it, finally, after a lifetime of denial. "The dead are *here*, and I need you to make sense, because I cannot—"

"It's the covenant." He said it quietly, and the hall went quieter still, every ghost leaning in. "The old binding between our realm and the Sable Court. It's failing. The wards aren't breaking, June—they're being *called home*. And there's a name on the original seal. An Ashgrove name."

The cold that went through her then had nothing to do with the dead.

"That's not possible," she whispered. "My family has kept salt ledgers for six generations. We're nobody. We're *supposed* to be nobody."

"I know." Something moved behind his eyes—guilt, old and deep as a well. "I've known for a long time. Longer than I should have. And I swear to you I didn't come here tonight to tell you this way, with the sky falling, but the shard—" He unwrapped the oilcloth fully, and the wardstone fragment lay bare in his palms, lightless now, inert. Dead crystal. "It won't answer me. It won't answer anyone. But when I carried it past the Ashgrove plot on the road, it started singing."

He held it out to her.

Every instinct she possessed screamed against it. Seven years of carefully constructed distance, of rehearsed indifference, of telling herself the wound had scarred over clean—all of it told her to step back, to call the night watch, to let him carry his broken crystal back into the snow and out of her life forever.

Instead, her hand rose. Her fingers, scarred and steady, closed over the shard.

It woke like a heart waking.

Light flooded up her arm, silver and warm, and the shard began to hum against her palm—a note she recognized, because she had been hearing variations of it her entire life in the whispers of the dead, in the songs of carved bone, in the space behind her sternum where she kept everything unsaid. The ghosts in the hall sighed as one, a sound like wind through a field of dry grass, and somewhere far away, beneath the starless sky, Juniper felt something ancient turn its attention toward her like an eye opening.

*Kestrel Vane,* said First Warden Elsbeth Ashgrove, her gray face solemn among the crowd of dead. *You were banished. Why do you return?*

Kestrel didn't flinch. He kept his eyes on Juniper, on the light climbing her wrist, and when he answered, his voice was low enough that only she could hear it.

"Because I ran once already," he said. "And it cost the world its stars."

The shard pulsed in Juniper's grip—once, twice—like a second heartbeat answering her own. Above them, through the high windows, the starless dark pressed against the glass, and in that dark, faint but unmistakable, something began to move.

The dead spoke together, one final time, their voices layered like sediment:

*Tell the carver. The toll is due at moonset. She must cross, or both worlds go dark.*

Juniper looked at the shard singing in her hand, at the man she had spent seven years learning to hate, at the ghosts of everyone who had ever trusted her family with their names.

"What name?" she asked him. "Whose name is on the seal?"

Kestrel Vane closed his eyes, and said hers.
