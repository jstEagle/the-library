## 5. The Drowned Market

*Juniper*

The town announced itself by smell before sight: brine and beeswax and something sweeter underneath, like fruit left too long in the sun. Juniper had expected Sable country to be dark—the stories all agreed on that—but the border town of Vesper's Landing sat beneath a sky the color of pewter, lit from below by lanterns that burned without flame, their light the pale green of foxfire caught in glass.

"It's called drowned because of the tide," Kestrel said, reading her expression. "The market floods twice a day. Everything sold here is sold wet."

"Charming."

"You wanted a guide into the interior. This is where guides are found." He adjusted the strap of his pack and did not look at her. He had barely looked at her since the tollgate, since she'd paid the first ward with her mother's face and come out the other side unable to remember whether her mother's eyes had been brown or gray. She kept reaching for it the way a tongue worries a missing tooth.

They descended the switchback road together, and the market opened below them like a wound in the hillside: hundreds of stalls built on stilts over black water, connected by plank walkways slick with spray. The crowd moved slowly, deliberately, as if everyone were wading. And perhaps they were—wading through time. Because here, Juniper saw within minutes, no coin changed hands.

A fishmonger held up a silver-bellied catch, and the buyer pressed two fingers to the fish's cold mouth and then to his own lips, and Juniper felt the *pull* from thirty feet away—a faint dragging sensation, like a draft under a door. The buyer swayed. The fish was handed over. Somewhere in that man's chest, two hours of his life were now swimming in a stranger's basket.

"They trade hours," she said quietly.

"Hours, days, sometimes years. The desperate sell decades." Kestrel's voice was flat, but his knuckles were white on the strap. "Don't touch anything without asking the price first. Don't let anyone take your hand. And whatever you do—"

"I know. Don't give my name."

"Your name is worth more than most people's whole lives, Juniper. Here especially."

She heard what he didn't say: *especially since we learned why.* An Ashgrove name, signed into a covenant generations before her birth. A guild that had buried her origins like a body under floorboards. Every step into Sable country made the shape of that burial clearer, and she hated how much she wanted to understand it, even knowing understanding would cost her.

They took a walkway toward the center of the market, where a bell tower rose from the water itself, its bell green with age and silent. Stalls crowded close: a woman selling bottled laughter; a man whose cart was stacked with keys that opened nothing in this world; a child, no older than eight, offering spools of thread dyed every color of forgetting. Juniper's bone-sense woke as they passed each one, whispering the way it always whispered—*that rib was a singer, that femur was a liar, that little finger belonged to someone who died forgiving*—and for the first time in her life she didn't clamp down on it. There was no guild master here to hide from. Only Kestrel, who already knew the worst of her.

"Guide," she said to a stall-keeper whose wares were maps drawn on skin—not cruelly, she sensed; the skins had been given gladly, and they murmured contentedly under her fingertips when she brushed them. "We need someone who knows the road to the Court of Unlit Candles."

The map-seller's eyes went wide and white-rimmed. "No one goes to the Court. People are *summoned* to the Court." But her gaze slid past Juniper's shoulder, to the water, and her face changed. "Though perhaps," she said slowly, "the road is coming to meet you."

Juniper turned.

Out past the last row of stalls, where the water deepened to true black, a light was rising. Not lantern-light. It was the color of candleflame seen through milk, and it came up through the water itself, climbing, until a figure stood upon the surface as though it were cobbles—an old woman in a salt-stiffened coat, her hair bound in a net of knotted cord, her hands folded like a woman waiting at a church door.

The market went quiet around them. Not silent—the sea still slapped the pilings, the foxfire lamps still hummed—but the *people* stopped, every face turning toward the water with an expression Juniper couldn't read. Reverence, maybe. Or the particular stillness of animals scenting something old.

"Do you know her?" Kestrel breathed.

"No." But her bones knew. Her bones were singing so loudly she could barely stand upright, a chord of recognition that started in her carved ribs and radiated outward, and she understood before the woman spoke, the way you understand a lullaby before you remember learning it.

"Juniper Ashgrove," the ghost said. Her voice carried across the water without effort, the way sound carries over ice. "You have your grandfather's stubbornness and your mother's habit of standing too near edges. Come down off those planks, child. I've been waiting in this harbor forty years for the tide to bring you."

* * *

*Kestrel*

He knew the name before she said it aloud, because he'd spent seven years memorizing every record the guild hadn't burned. Maren Ashgrove. Signatory of the second covenant. Dead sixty-one years—and yet here she stood on the harbor water, more solid than any ghost Kestrel had ever seen, her boots leaving ripples that spread in perfect rings.

"She shouldn't be able to manifest like this," he muttered to Juniper as they picked their way down the flooded lane. "Ghosts in Sable country don't hold form unless something is anchoring them. Something expensive."

"Then let's ask her what she paid."

Maren Ashgrove watched them approach with eyes the same gray-green as Juniper's, and Kestrel felt the old guilt rise in him like floodwater. He had read this woman's signature a hundred times in the sealed archives, traced the ink of her consent, and never once imagined the girl he loved was her blood. The guild had made sure he couldn't imagine it. They'd made sure *Juniper* couldn't imagine it.

"You're the enforcer," Maren said when they reached the water's edge. Up close, she smelled of salt and dried lavender, of a kitchen Kestrel had never stood in but Juniper suddenly gripped his arm hard enough to bruise. "Kestrel Vane. The one who wouldn't carry her."

"You know that."

"I've had forty years to watch, boy. Watching is mostly what the dead do." Her gaze softened as it moved to Juniper. "And you. Look at your hands. Still carving. I used to watch you in the cradle, moving your fingers like you were shaping something invisible. I told your mother: she'll work bone, that one. Your mother laughed. She wanted you to be a baker."

Juniper made a small wounded sound. "I don't remember her wanting anything."

"No," Maren said gently. "You don't. That was the first payment, child, and it wasn't yours to make—it was made *for* you, before you could refuse. Which is why I'm here. Walk with me. The market will part for us; they know a signatory when they see one."

And the market did part. Stall-keepers drew back their goods, buyers stepped into doorways, and the crowd bowed its head as Maren walked across the shallows and Juniper walked beside her on the submerged cobbles, water to their ankles, while Kestrel followed half a step behind like the shadow he'd been trained to be.

"The covenant," Maren began, "was not what the guild told you. They keep the ledgers, so they think they own the truth. Listen. Two hundred years ago, the stars were going out for the first time. The Sable Queen came to the border with her court starving for light, and our ancestors had a choice: war, which neither side could survive, or terms. The terms were these." She stopped walking. Around them the market held its breath. "One mortal line would bind itself to the wards. Not their labor. Not their deaths. Their *name*. The name would live in the wardstone, and as long as an Ashgrove lived freely—freely, mind you, that word is written three times in the original—the wards would hold and the stars would burn."

"Freely," Juniper repeated.

"Freely. The covenant runs on consent the way a lamp runs on oil. It cannot be forced. So the guild did something cleverer than force." Maren's voice hardened, and the water around her ankles frosted. "They raised you ignorant. A name given freely by someone who doesn't know she's giving it—that was their loophole. They thought ignorance was consent. They took your mother's memories of her own lineage so she couldn't tell you. They took your grandmother's—" her hand gestured at herself, at the translucent shimmer of her— "well. You can see what they took from me. And they've been spending you against the wards for years, child, in small installments. Every time you sat alone at night cataloguing bones and felt tired in a way sleep didn't fix? That was a withdrawal. You have been pre-spent since the day you were born."

Juniper stood very still. Kestrel watched her the way he'd once watched a wardstone crack—knowing the explosion was coming, unable to look away, unwilling to run.

"How much," Juniper said. Her voice was terrifyingly level. "How much of me is already gone?"

Maren reached out and took her granddaughter's hand—the first time, Kestrel realized, that anyone in Juniper's family had touched her knowingly in her entire life. "Give me your wrist, child. Let me count."

What passed between them Kestrel couldn't see, but he saw the effect. Juniper swayed. Her free hand flew to her mouth. And when she lifted her face again, the wonder that had lit it since the ghost appeared had curdled into something colder and older than dread.

"Thirty percent," she whispered. "Nearly a third of my life has already been spent. Birthdays. Winters. My mother teaching me to carve my first whistle—I *remembered* that, I thought I remembered—" Her breath hitched. "It's not a memory. It's a ledger entry. Someone wrote it back into me so I wouldn't notice the hole."

"Juniper." Kestrel stepped forward.

"Don't." She didn't turn. "Don't you *dare* comfort me right now, Kestrel Vane. Did you know this? In the archives—did you read the withdrawal schedule?"

He had. He said so. He owed her that much truth, at least. "I read it seven years ago. It's why I refused to deliver you. It's why they exiled me."

"And you let me walk into that tollgate at the first ward and pay with my mother's *face* without telling me there might be another way—"

"There is no other way. That's what I couldn't tell you. The withdrawals happen either way, Juniper. The only choice is whether they buy wards or line the guild's pockets." His voice cracked on the last word. "I chose exile rather than be the hand that spent you. Don't make me wish I'd chosen differently."

The silence between them stretched thin as wire.

Maren released her granddaughter's hand and stepped back, and the water around her began to darken, the tide pulling at her form like fingers tugging a hem. "The tide turns," she said. "I can't hold past moonrise. Listen, both of you, because this is the last thing I can give: the Queen will offer you restoration. Everything they took, returned whole. It will be the sweetest bargain you ever hear. But restoration through her hands means the covenant stays whole in *one* vessel—one life holding two hundred years of starlight—and no vessel survives that. Not even one as stubborn as you, my love."

"Then what do I do?"

"What our family should have done from the beginning." The ghost was nearly transparent now, her voice coming from everywhere, from the water and the fog and the bones in Juniper's pack. "Refuse the arithmetic. One plus one does not have to equal one. Find the third—"

The bell in the drowned tower rang, once, though no rope had moved, and Maren Ashgrove was gone.

In the ringing silence, Juniper turned to Kestrel, and her face was wet, though the spray had stopped.

"Find the third what?" she asked.

Behind them, at the mouth of the market lane, three figures in ash-gray cloaks stood perfectly still among the hurrying crowd—still as posts, still as gravestones—and every one of their cowled faces was turned toward Juniper.

"They heard her too," Kestrel said quietly, and put his hand on his knife.
