## 6. Kestrel's Debt

The drowned market had spat them out at dusk, and Kestrel had been counting the wrong things ever since.

He counted the way he always did when fear sat in his chest like swallowed riverwater: the number of lanterns still burning along the fen road (eleven, then nine, then seven), the interval between the frogsong and its sudden silences (shortening, always shortening, as if the marsh itself were holding its breath), the distance between Juniper's shoulder blades and the edge of the treeline where the Sable country proper began. Too far. It had been too far for an hour.

She walked ahead of him with her hood up and her hands buried in her sleeves, and every few strides her right hand moved—unconscious, habitual—tracing the shape of something against her forearm. He knew what she was tracing. She'd been doing it since the grandmother's ghost had finished speaking in that reeking market square: a name, over and over, carved into the air as if she could rehearse it into being true. *Juniper Ashgrove.* Except it wasn't quite her name, was it? That was the wound the ghost had opened, and Kestrel had stood there in the fish-smell and the candlelight trading hours of other people's lives and said nothing, because some confessions you cannot make in front of witnesses, even dead ones.

*You knew,* her eyes had said to him across the market's noise. Not yet spoken. But coming. Coming like weather.

The seventh lantern went out.

Kestrel stopped walking. "Juniper."

"I know." She didn't turn around. Her voice was flat and careful, the voice of someone standing very still on ice. "I've known since the frogs went quiet."

They came out of the reeds without sound, which was the worst part—huntsmen were supposed to announce themselves, that was the old courtesy, the old law, antlers raised and voices like winter wind giving you the formal choice: run or kneel. These gave no such courtesy. Six shapes unfolded from the marsh grass as if they had been growing there all along, tall and wrong-jointed, their bodies wrapped in grey hide that drank the last light instead of reflecting it. Their faces were smooth as river stones. Where eyes should have been there were only shallow dents, and somehow that was worse than eyes.

One of them carried a net woven from what looked like black hair. Another carried a hook of pale bone, and Kestrel's stomach turned, because he recognized the carving style. Guild work. Salt-country bone, salt-country craft, taken south across the border who knew how long ago and put to use by the Court's hounds.

"Behind me," he said.

"No," Juniper said, but she did come behind him, which he chose to count as a mercy.

The lead huntsman tilted its stone face toward Juniper, and though it had no eyes, Kestrel felt the weight of its attention settle on her like a hand on the back of the neck. When it spoke, its voice was the sound a frozen lake makes at night, cracking and groaning under its own weight.

"*The Ashgrove debt walks unclaimed. The Court calls it home.*"

"She's not a debt," Kestrel said. "She's a person."

The huntsman's head rotated toward him with the slow patience of something that had never once been hurried in its long grey life. "*The Vane. Returned. The Court remembers your refusal, little knife. It has been saving a place for you.*"

"I know," Kestrel said. "I'm going to make it an offer."

"Don't—" Juniper started, and then the net came down.

It happened fast after that, the way violence always does, too fast for dignity. The hair-net whistled; Kestrel got Juniper down and rolled with her, and the net caught the reeds behind them instead, and where it touched the living stalks they blackened and crumbled to ash-grey dust. The second huntsman swung the bone hook low, honest and brutal, and Kestrel took it on his forearm because there was nowhere else to take it, and felt the crack more than the pain, white fire up to the elbow. He heard Juniper scream his name. He heard himself say, absurdly, calmly, through his teeth:

"The toll. I invoke the toll."

Everything stopped.

Even the marsh seemed to stop—the frogs, the wind, the slow breathing of the reeds. The huntsmen froze mid-motion, six grey statues in the failing light, because whatever else the Sable Court was, it was old law down to its stones, and the toll was older than all of them. Older than the covenant. Older, perhaps, than the Court itself.

Kestrel got his feet under him, cradling his ruined arm, and faced the lead huntsman. His heart was slamming. His mouth tasted of copper and marsh rot. Behind him he could hear Juniper breathing fast and hard, and he made himself not look at her, because if he looked at her now he would lose the thread of what he had to do.

"A life for a passage," he said. "That's the toll. Any life freely offered. I offer mine."

"*The Vane's years are few,*" the huntsman said. Its tone did not change—it had no tones—but the words came slower, the way a merchant slows when he smells desperation and wants to savor it. "*Spent much already. Spent badly. What remains is thin broth.*"

"Then take the thin broth," Kestrel said. "All of it. Every year I have left, poured out here, tonight, and in exchange she walks free to the border wards. All of them. Free passage, no pursuit, no claim. Sworn on the Court's own root."

Silence. The kind of silence that has weight and texture, that presses on the ears.

Then Juniper hit him.

Not the huntsmen. Him. She came around from behind with her small hard carver's fist and struck him across the jaw, and when he turned, stunned, she struck him again, and her eyes were blazing and wet all at once.

"You don't get to do that," she said. "You don't—you absolute *bastard*, you don't get to buy my freedom with your corpse. Do you know what I've paid to get this far? Do you know what I gave at the first ward?" Her voice cracked. "My mother's face, Kestrel. I don't remember my mother's face anymore, and you were going to stand there and make me watch you die too? Make that the thing I carry instead?"

"It was the only coin I had left."

"That's not true!" She shoved him, both hands flat against his chest, and he staggered. "Nothing about you has ever been true! Seven years, Kestrel. Seven years I thought—I built whole cathedrals out of what you did, I built *cathedrals*, and now you're going to die nobly in front of me and I'll never even know why you—"

"Why I what?"

"Why you *left!* Why you really left!"

And there it was. The marsh, the huntsmen, the broken arm, all of it receding to the edges of the world, leaving just the two of them and the question she had been carrying since the night the stars went out, since before that, since a girl of nineteen had watched a boy walk out of the guild hall and never come back.

Kestrel looked at the lead huntsman. "*Sworn on the root,*" he said. "Give me until moonrise to speak my piece. Then take your answer."

The huntsman considered. Or performed consideration. "*Until moonrise,*" it said, and the six of them sank back into the reeds like stones into water, and were gone.

* * *

They made a fire of dead reeds, because the dark was total now—no stars, no moon yet, nothing above but a smooth black ceiling—and because Kestrel's arm needed splinting and his hands were shaking too badly to do it alone. Juniper set the break with a carver's economy that made him laugh once, breathlessly, until she glared at him and he stopped.

"My exile," he said, while she bound the splint. "You want to know. So."

She said nothing. She tied off the bandage with a sharp tug.

"I was nineteen. Newly sworn as enforcer. Aldous called me into the ledger room—you know the one, with the window that doesn't open—and there was a writ on the table. A delivery order. Sealed with the guild-master's mark and the Court's mark together, which I'd never seen before, and I've never seen since." He flexed his splinted fingers, watching the firelight move on them. "A child was to be delivered to the Sable Court. A tithe. The covenant required a keeper—a soul bound to hold the wards from the mortal side—and the Court had chosen its candidate before she was born. Named in the old signing. The writ had her name on it."

Juniper had gone very still. In the firelight her face was half bronze, half shadow.

"They told me the child was four years old," Kestrel said. "Living with a wet-nurse in the fen villages. They told me to collect her and carry her across, quiet, no fuss, and come back promoted." He laughed, and it came out ugly. "I rode out. I told myself it was duty. I told myself the covenant was bigger than one life. And then I got there, and she was asleep in a cot with her thumb in her mouth, and she had her mother's ears, and I sat outside that door for most of a night and could not make my legs stand up."

"Kestrel."

"I came back and told Aldous I'd lost the child. Told him the village had burned—there had been fever, it was plausible enough. And he looked at me for a long time, and then he smiled, and he said, *'Of course you did, boy. That was the test.'*" Kestrel's voice went rough. "The Court wanted an enforcer who would refuse. Someone with a conscience, someone who could be trusted near the keeper when she grew. So they let me stay close. Seven years I stayed close, and I told myself I was guarding you, and maybe I was, but mostly—" He stopped. Started again. "Mostly I loved you, and I let myself believe that was the same thing. And then the wards began to fail, and the Court sent the summons for real, and I ran. Because running was the only refusal I had left that they hadn't planned for."

The fire popped. Somewhere out in the black, a frog sang once and fell silent.

"So the exile," Juniper said slowly, "the disgrace, the seven years—"

"Punishment for the first refusal. Reward for the second, in a way. They wanted me broken and far away so I couldn't warn you." He finally looked at her straight on. "I have been a coward twice, Juniper. Once at your door when you were four, and once seven years ago. I am trying very hard not to be one a third time."

She was crying. She didn't wipe her face, didn't seem to know she was doing it. "You watched me carve bones," she whispered. "All those years. You watched me flinch when the dead spoke, and you knew what I was, and you never—"

"I was ordered not to. And then I was afraid to. And then it was too late." He shook his head. "None of those are excuses. They're just the shape of the cowardice."

For a long time she said nothing. The fire burned down. Above the reeds, the false sky stayed blank and starless, and Kestrel found himself thinking that if these were his last hours, at least the sky had the decency to match.

Then Juniper reached out and took his broken hand, carefully, splint and all, and held it in both of hers the way she held a fresh-carved bone—firmly, precisely, as if deciding what it could become.

"I'm furious with you," she said. "I think I will be furious with you for years. But fury needs you alive, Kestrel. So we're going to stand up, and we're going to tell that grey horror out there that the toll is refused, and you are going to keep every year you have left, because I need them. Do you understand? I *need* them."

Before he could answer, the reeds parted.

The lead huntsman rose among them, stone-faced, patient, and behind it the marsh had gone utterly silent. But it was not looking at Kestrel. Its blind attention had settled past them both, eastward, toward the distant line of the border hills—and when it spoke, its lake-crack voice carried something new in it. Something almost like reverence.

"*Moonrise comes early tonight, little knife. The Queen of Ash has lit her court. She bids the Ashgrove come—not as toll. As guest.*"

It extended one grey hand toward the darkness, toward the road they had been dreading since the market.

"And the Court," it said, "is *waiting*."
