## 7. The Court of Unlit Candles

The road to the Sable Court did not end so much as *admit* them.

One moment they were walking through a forest of grey-barked trees whose leaves chimed like struck glass; the next, the trees stood aside in ranks, and there it was—rising out of a valley of black grass that rippled without wind, pale and vast and wrong in the way that beautiful things are wrong when they have been beautiful too long.

Juniper had imagined a court of darkness. She had expected shadow, rot, the architecture of ruin. What she saw instead was light without source: towers of white stone veined with silver, bridges arcing between them like frozen waterfalls, windows glowing with a soft amber warmth that made her chest ache with homesickness for a home she'd never had.

And above it all—the sky. The wound where the stars should have been. It was not black here so much as *absent*, a ceiling of nothing that pressed down on the whole city like a held breath. She could feel it against her skin, the weight of all that missing light.

"It's starving," she said quietly.

Kestrel walked beside her, his wardstone shard wrapped in cloth at his belt, his hand never far from the knife he'd taken from the huntsman who'd nearly killed him. He looked worse than he had three days ago—the bargain at the tollgate had cost him years she was afraid to count—but his eyes were steady when they found hers.

"The whole realm," he said. "Every field, every river. They've been living on borrowed time since the wards broke. Literally." He nodded toward the road ahead, where figures moved through the black grass toward the gates: tall folk with ash-grey skin and eyes like banked coals, carrying offerings, carrying each other. Some of them were dying as they walked, gently, mid-stride, their hours simply running out, and those behind them stepped around the bodies with the courtesy of long practice.

Juniper's hand went to her satchel, to the tools within it. Carving knives of salt-steel. Bone blanks. And beneath them, wrapped in linen against her heart, the shard of fallen wardstone that hummed when she touched it—a hum she now knew was her own name, sung by stone.

"Stay close to me in there," Kestrel said. "Whatever they offer you—and they will offer you something—the Court does not give gifts. It gives trades dressed as gifts."

"You would know," she said, and immediately regretted it, because his face did the thing it always did: closed like a ledger, every figure hidden.

"I would know," he agreed, and said nothing else.

They joined the road of mourners and offerings, and the gates of the Court opened before them like a mouth deciding to be kind.

* * *

Inside, the Court of Unlit Candles earned its name.

The great hall was a cathedral of pale stone, and along its walls, on shelves carved from floor to vaulted ceiling, stood ten thousand candles. None of them burned. Each one was grey-white, cold, its wick dark—and yet each one *hummed*, faintly, the way the wardstone hummed, and Juniper understood with a lurch what they were. Not wax. Bone, rendered smooth and pale. A candle for every soul the Court held in trust—or in debt. She could hear them if she let herself listen: whispers layered on whispers, a congregation of the unspent, murmuring fragments of lives paused mid-sentence.

She had spent her whole life pretending not to hear the dead. Here, pretending was impossible. The hall was an ocean of voices, and she was drowning in it, and no one had ever told her that drowning could feel like coming home.

"Bone-carver."

The voice came from everywhere and nowhere, from the candles themselves, and then—from the throne.

The Queen of Ash sat at the far end of the hall on a seat of fused glass, and she was not what Juniper expected either. She looked like a woman who had been beautiful in the way rivers are beautiful, worn into her shape by centuries of patient motion. Her gown was the grey of old snow. Her eyes were the amber of the window-light, warm and utterly without mercy. When she rose, the ten thousand unlit candles leaned toward her like wheat toward sun.

"You are smaller than your grandmother," the Queen said. "But you carve the same way. I watched her hands once, across the border, through a keyhole of a ward. She held the knife like it was listening to her." She descended the dais steps, unhurried. "Do you know why your family's name is written in our oldest covenant? Not because we chose you. Because your blood remembers how to hold two worlds in one body without breaking. That is rare. That is *expensive*. We paid dearly for it, once."

"And then the Guild stole me back," Juniper said. Her voice came out steadier than she felt. "Erased my name. Hid me in a ledger room for thirty years."

"They hid you *poorly*," the Queen said, and something almost like amusement touched her mouth. "We have known where you were the entire time, Juniper Ashgrove. We waited because taking you would have broken the covenant faster than neglecting it. But now the covenant is broken regardless. Now the question is only what gets built from the wreckage." She stopped three steps away, close enough that Juniper could smell her—not ash, but rain on hot stone, petrichor and ozone. "And I have come to make you an honest offer. The first honest offer anyone in your life has made you, I suspect."

Behind her, Juniper heard Kestrel shift his weight. She didn't turn around.

"Name it," she said.

* * *

The Queen led them—not to the throne but to a side chapel, small and round, where a single candle stood on an altar of black glass. This one was lit. Its flame was white, and it cast no shadows, and when Juniper looked at it she felt her own heartbeat stutter in recognition.

"This is yours," the Queen said. "Or rather—it is everything you have paid. Your mother's face. Your seventh summer. The smell of bread in the Ashgrove kitchen. The sound of your own name spoken by someone who loved you. Every toll you surrendered on the road here, gathered and rendered." She touched the flame with one finger; it bent around her skin like a curious animal. "I can give it all back. Whole. Now. You would walk out of this hall remembering your mother's smile, your childhood, your name as it was meant to be spoken. You would be *restored*, bone-carver. Complete."

Juniper's breath left her slowly. She hadn't realized, until this moment, how hollow she had become—how many rooms inside herself stood empty, doors open onto nothing. She thought of her mother's face and found, where the memory should have been, a smooth blank wall, and behind that wall a grief so large it had no edges because it had no shape. To have it back. To look in a mirror and see her mother looking back at her—

"What does it cost?" Kestrel asked flatly.

The Queen did not look at him. "Nothing more than has already been spent. The covenant must be reforged, and it must be sealed in Ashgrove blood—that term cannot be altered; it was written before either of your nations had their present names. Take it alone. All of it. Become the living covenant, as your grandmother was meant to become before the Guild interfered. You would stand between the realms forever, sustained, honored, *whole*. The wards would rebuild themselves along your spine. The stars would return." Her amber eyes finally moved, sliding past Juniper like water around a stone, to fix on Kestrel. "There is one condition. The covenant cannot bear divided attention. Not at first. Not while it sets. Whoever binds to it must bind alone, and must forget whatever might pull their heart sideways."

"Forget," Juniper repeated.

"Him," the Queen said simply. "Kestrel Vane. His face, his voice, his betrayal, his sacrifice at the tollgate, the seven years you spent hating him and the seven before that you spent loving him. All of it. It would be taken cleanly, painlessly, the moment the covenant seals. You would not miss it. You cannot miss what you do not remember existing." She smiled, and it was a gentle smile, and it was the cruelest thing Juniper had ever seen. "Think of it, bone-carver. Everything returned. Your mother's face. Your name. Your wholeness. In exchange for one man—one man who already sold years of his life for you, who already took everything from you once, who is already half-spent. Is he worth more than your mother's smile?"

The silence rang like the leaf-glass trees outside.

Juniper turned and looked at Kestrel.

He stood very still, the way he'd stood still facing the huntsmen, the way he'd stood still at the first tollgate when she'd wept over the memory leaving her. He wasn't going to argue. She could see that. He had already decided, somewhere on the road, in some hour when she slept, that if it came to this—if it came to her wholeness or his presence—he would step out of the picture without complaint. His jaw was set. His eyes were wet and furious about being wet. Seven years of exile had taught him how to lose things quietly.

And God help her, she wanted it. That was the terrible truth rising in her like floodwater. She wanted her mother's face the way the drowning want air. She wanted the kitchen, the bread, the seventh summer, the sound of *Juniper* spoken soft. Thirty years of deliberate invisibility, of carving bones and pretending deafness, of being nobody on purpose—and here was the offer of being *somebody*, fully, completely, at last.

All she had to do was forget the only person who had ever seen her clearly anyway.

"Your Majesty," Juniper said, and her voice shook, and she let it shake, because she was done performing steadiness for institutions. "You said this was the first honest offer anyone ever made me. You're wrong. It's a lie wearing honesty's coat. Because you're not offering me my memories back. You're offering me a *curated* life—a life with the hard parts cut out. My mother's face without the grief of losing her. My name without the family that named me. A whole woman built out of half a history." She stepped closer to the altar, to the white flame that beat like a captive heart. "And you're right that he betrayed me. You're right that he cost me everything. But he also carried a shard of broken sky across two hundred miles of dying country to bring it back to me. He bargained away his years twice. He stood in front of huntsmen for me. Whatever Kestrel Vane owes me, he has been paying it down with interest, and *you* don't get to erase the debt just because it's inconvenient to your covenant."

The Queen's expression did not change, but the ten thousand candles in the great hall beyond shivered in their shelves, a rustle like wind through a bone orchard.

"You would refuse restoration," the Queen said. "For a disgraced enforcer."

"I'm not refusing restoration." Juniper reached into her satchel and drew out the wardstone shard, and its hum rose to meet the candle-flame, two notes finding harmony. "I'm refusing your terms. There's a difference. My grandmother taught me—I mean, the ghost of her taught me, in a drowned market, that covenants can be rewritten by whoever holds the knife. And I am the best bone-carver the Salt Guild ever produced, though they buried the record." She set the shard on the altar beside the white flame, and the flame leaned toward it like a child toward its mother. "So here is my counter-offer, Majesty. I'll seal your covenant. I'll even pay in memory. But I will decide which memories, and I will decide *who binds with me*—and I will need a working surface, a forge, and enough of your patience not to interrupt me while I carve."

For the first time in what might have been centuries, the Queen of Ash looked surprised.

Then she looked past Juniper—at the shard, at the flame, at the girl standing between them with salt-steel knives and a name that had been stolen twice—and her surprise cooled into something older and hungrier.

"A new carving," the Queen said softly. "In my court. With your own bone, if I know your grandmother's methods." Her amber eyes gleamed. "Very well, Juniper Ashgrove. You shall have your forge. But understand what you are promising: no one who has attempted a working of that kind has survived it. And if you fail, I take everything—all of it, both of you, down to the last unspent hour."

"Then I'd better carve well," Juniper said, and picked up the shard, and followed the Queen into the dark.
