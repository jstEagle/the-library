## 2. The Night the Stars Went Out

The page rewrote itself again at the ninth bell, and Isandro Iversen pretended not to notice.

He had gotten good at pretending. Three years in the Ledger Guild of Vharn taught a man to keep his face as blank as an unlined page — to dip his pen, make his entry, and let nothing flicker behind his eyes no matter what the ledgers did. Because the ledgers *did* things. That was the first secret of the guildhouse, and like all its secrets, it was paid for with years of your life.

He sat at his desk in the Long Scriptorium, one of forty scribes bent over desks beneath windows that hadn't shown stars in living memory — only the wards' pale shimmer, high above Vharn, holding back whatever the border held back. His candle burned steady. His ink pot was full. His left hand rested on the oldest ledger in the guild's keeping, the one they called the Root Book, and beneath his palm he felt it do the thing it was not supposed to do.

A tremor. Like a heartbeat under skin.

Isandro lifted his hand a fraction, casual as stretching, and looked down at the open page.

The entry he'd been copying — a debt of two hundred years owed by the city of Vharn to *something*, the counterparty's name written in characters that hurt to read too long — had changed. The sum was larger now. The due date had moved closer. And at the bottom of the page, where the covenant-seal should have sat fat and red and satisfied, there was instead a thin black line, struck through the sigil like a scar through an eye.

Someone had defaulted. Someone very old, very large, and very far away.

Isandro dipped his pen. Made his entry in the daily log: *Root Book reviewed, entries consistent.* A lie in good guildhand, neat as arithmetic. Around him, thirty-nine other scribes wrote their own small lies, or their truths, or their tedium, and none of them felt the tremor because none of them had spent three years learning to feel it — the way the ledgers breathed, the way debts had weight, the way certain pages grew warm when they were frightened.

That was Isandro's gift, though gift was a generous word for something that had gotten him beaten twice and reassigned four times. He could *read* covenants. Not just the words — the structure underneath, the grammar of obligation, the way a debt curved through time like a river through stone. The guild called him a parser and used him like a tool: here, scribe, untangle this impossible clause; here, scribe, tell us which village owes what to whom. And Isandro untangled, and told them, and each answer made some factor richer and some debtor poorer, and each night he went back to his cell in the indenture wing and lay awake doing sums he hated.

His life-bond was recorded in the front matter of the Root Book itself. Seventeen more years of service before his name came free. He had checked so often he knew the entry by heart, knew the exact slant of the clerk's pen on the final letter of *Iversen*. Seventeen years. He would be forty-one. He tried not to imagine being forty-one.

The tremor came again, stronger. The Root Book shivered under his fingers like a cold animal.

And then the ninth bell finished ringing, and the world tore.

It began as a sound — high, thin, wrong, like silk ripping somewhere behind the sky. Every scribe in the Long Scriptorium looked up at once, forty pens frozen mid-stroke, forty faces turned toward the windows. The sound stretched. It did not stop the way sounds stop. It kept tearing, wider and wider, and Isandro felt it in his teeth, in the roots of his hair, in the ledger under his hand which was now shaking so hard the pages fluttered.

"Master Quillon—" someone started.

The candle flames answered first.

Every flame in the scriptorium guttered at once, forty candles bowing in a wind that wasn't there, and their light turned blue. Not the blue of flame at its heart — the blue of deep water, of bruises, of the hour before dawn in winter. The whole room drowned in it. Isandro saw his own hands go corpse-pale, saw the ink in his pot ripple without being touched, saw old Master Quillon rise from his chair at the head of the room with an expression Isandro had never seen on him before.

Quillon looked afraid.

Then every flame died, and the dark came down like a lid.

For three heartbeats, nobody breathed. In the dark Isandro heard the sounds a building makes when it is trying to be calm: the shuffle of feet, a chair knocked over, someone whispering a warding verse they half-remembered from childhood. He heard the Root Book against his chest — he realized he had pulled it off the desk and into his arms without deciding to, the way you catch a falling child — and he heard it making a sound he had never heard a ledger make.

It was whimpering.

"The wards," Quillon said into the dark, and his voice cracked on the second word. "The wards are—"

Light bloomed outside the windows. Red-orange, flickering, growing. Not dawn — dawn was hours away, and dawn didn't come from below. Isandro leaned toward the glass and saw fire in the streets of Vharn, real fire, torch-fire, dozens of torches converging on the guildhouse square, and among them figures moving with the terrible efficiency of men who have done a thing many times: enforcers, guild livery dark in the torchlight, carrying not buckets but armloads of paper.

They were burning records. Their own records. Ledgers going into the flames by the stack, debt-pages curling and lifting on the heat like dying moths, and Isandro watched a hundred years of somebody's obligation turn to ash in the space of a breath and felt something in his chest crack open.

"They're destroying the evidence," whispered the scribe next to him — Pellam, young, earnest, useless. "Why would they burn the—"

Doors slammed open at the end of the scriptorium. Torchlight spilled in, blue-edged now too, everything blue-edged, and enforcers filled the doorway with the Root Book's cousins clutched in their fists. "Out," said their sergeant, a woman Isandro knew by reputation — Verity Hask, who collected debts in the lowlands and never smiled. "All of you. Single file. The guildhouse is closing."

"Closing?" Quillon's voice. "Closing *how*? The wards are down, man, the wards are—"

"The wards are down," Hask said, flat as a ledger line, "which is why the Master has ordered the house sealed and the archives purged. What the realms don't remember, the Hollow can't collect. Move."

*What the realms don't remember, the Hollow can't collect.* Isandro turned the sentence over as the scribes shuffled into line, and it made a horrible kind of sense, the way all cruel arithmetic makes sense. If the covenant was broken — if the payment had stopped, if the oldest debt of all had come due and gone unpaid — then every record of every debt was suddenly dangerous. Evidence. Leverage. A map of everything owed, lying open for whatever waited beyond the fallen wards to read.

So burn it. Burn the map. Blind both sides and call it mercy.

But the Root Book was still in his arms.

He understood, in the slow cold way understanding arrives, that the Root Book was the map. The first map. The original. And that somewhere downstairs, enforcers were feeding ledgers into fires, and that when they reached the vault where the Root Book lived — except it didn't live in the vault tonight, it lived pressed against a third-year scribe's chest, whimpering — they would find it missing, and they would find *him*.

The line moved. Isandro walked with it, hunched, the great ledger hidden inside his coat where his satchel usually hung, its edges digging into his ribs, its weight absurd and enormous, a thousand years of covenant riding against his heartbeat. His satchel hung from his shoulder instead, lighter than usual, and he felt the shape inside it that shouldn't be there — the sigil-stamp he'd taken two nights ago in a moment of madness he still couldn't explain, brass and bone, warm as a sleeping bird.

Two thefts now. Two nights running. He was either the worst scribe in the history of the guild or the beginning of something, and in the dark, with the wards torn and the city burning its own memory, he genuinely could not tell which.

They went down the great stair in torchlight that guttered blue and steadied, guttered and steadied, the flames sick and fighting. Below, the entrance hall was chaos — clerks hauling archive-chests, factors shouting numbers at each other as if numbers still meant anything, and through the great doors, the square: fire, ash rising, and enforcers feeding the pyre with methodical calm while somewhere a woman screamed a name over and over, a debtor's name, a name going up in smoke.

Hask counted them out. "Scribes to the east wing. You'll be sealed in with the rest until the Master says otherwise."

Sealed. The word landed in Isandro's stomach like a stone. Sealed in the guildhouse with the burning, with the Master's orders, with whatever Corvane intended when the last page turned to ash — and the Root Book heavy against his ribs, and the stamp warm in his satchel, and seventeen years of his life-bond waiting in the front matter like a leash.

The line funneled toward the east corridor. Behind it, the great doors stood open to the square, propped wide for the record-bearers coming and going, and beyond the doors the night was starless and enormous and absolutely, catastrophically free.

Isandro had never once, in twenty-four years, been outside after curfew. Had never walked where he wasn't sent, slept where he wasn't assigned, eaten anything he hadn't earned by the hour. His whole life was a ledger with entries already written, and every instinct he owned — three years of guild training, twenty-four years of servitude — told him to get in line, keep his eyes down, survive.

The Root Book whimpered against his chest.

Or maybe that was him.

The line reached the corridor mouth. Hask stood beside it, checking names against a list by torchlight, and Isandro watched her lips move over the scribe ahead of him — *Pellam, third-year, east wing* — and knew that in ten seconds she would look up and say *Iversen* and he would walk into the sealing and that would be his whole life, forever, until the wards stayed dead or the Master's plan finished cooking, whichever came first.

Ten seconds.

Five.

He turned left instead of right.

Nobody saw. Or nobody cared — the hall was too full of panic, too full of shouting factors and burning paper-smell, and Isandro walked fast, then faster, coat bulging, satchel bouncing, past the archive doors where enforcers were stacking ledgers like cordwood, past a clerk weeping over a strongbox of name-deeds, toward the propped-open doors and the square and the fire and the dark.

A hand closed on his arm.

He almost screamed. Hask. She'd seen him. Her grip was iron and her torch threw her shadow huge across the floor, and she looked at him — at his coat, at the impossible rectangular bulge of it — and Isandro watched her understand, watched the recognition move across her face like weather.

She let go.

"East wing," she said loudly, for anyone listening. Then, barely a breath, barely words: "Third door past the counting-room. It sticks. Go." And she turned back to her list and called the next name as if Isandro Iversen had never existed.

He never learned why. Maybe she had a debt of her own. Maybe she'd read the same tremor in the books and hated the Master for it. Maybe, in the tearing dark, everyone in that hall was choosing a side without knowing it yet.

He ran.

Through the third door past the counting-room — it stuck, she was right, he hit it with his shoulder and it gave with a crack — down a servants' stair he'd cleaned as an apprentice, out through a coal-yard where the sky overhead was a smooth black nothing, no wards, no stars, no moon, just emptiness pressing down like a held breath. The gate at the yard's wall was locked. Of course it was locked. Everything was always locked, everything was always owed—

The sigil-stamp was warm in his satchel. On impulse, on faith, on sheer stupid hope, he pulled it out and pressed it to the lock's plate.

The lock opened like a flower.

Isandro stood in the coal-yard with his heart slamming, looking at the hanging padlock, and understood two things at once. First: the stamp was exactly what the oldest ledger implied it was — a key, older than the guild, older than the locks it opened. Second: he was now, definitively, irrecoverably, a thief.

He laughed. It came out ragged and half-hysterical and it was the freest sound he had ever made.

Then he put his shoulder to the gate, slipped out into the starless streets of Vharn, and heard, behind him, the guildhouse begin to seal — stone grinding against stone, wards flaring weak blue along its walls, the whole vast building folding itself shut like a fist around everything and everyone still inside.

The Root Book pressed against his ribs, warm now instead of whimpering, as if it approved.

Behind him, his whole life locked its doors. Ahead, the city burned its memory, and something in the dark between the dead wards was already turning its attention toward the small, warm, guilty thing moving fast through the alleys — a scribe, a stolen book, a stolen key, and no name on any page that could protect him now.

Isandro ran, and did not look back, and for the first time in his life the terror and the joy were the same feeling, and he could not have told you where one ended and the other began.

Three streets later, he realized he was being followed.

* * *

It happened the way he'd learn fear worked in a starless city: not by sight but by absence. Footsteps that stopped when his stopped, a shade too long. An alley-mouth that swallowed a silhouette a half-second before he turned. The Root Book had gone quiet against his chest — not calm, he understood now, but *listening*, the way a dog goes quiet when it smells wolf.

He cut off the lantern-road into the warren of Tanner's Row, where the vats hung cold and dead and the smell of old lye masked everything, and the footsteps followed, patient, unhurried. Not an enforcer's tread — enforcers marched. This was softer. This was someone who knew these streets better than he ever could.

"You're leaking," said a voice from the darkness above him.

Isandro froze. Looked up. Perched on a vat-beam, wrapped in patchwork leather and ward-charms that glimmered faintly dead-blue, was a figure with sharp eyes and sharper cheekbones, watching him the way one watches a mouse that has wandered indoors carrying something interesting.

"Leaking," Isandro repeated.

"Light." The figure nodded at his coat. "Whatever you've got under there, it shines. To anyone who knows how to look. And tonight, friend, everything that knows how to look is awake." A pause, and then, with the weariness of someone reading a sign long expected: "You'd better come with me. Before something else finds you first."

The Root Book, pressed against Isandro's hammering heart, gave one small, unmistakable pulse — like a yes.

He looked back the way he'd come, at the empty street and the starless sky and the distant glow of the guild's bonfire eating a century of names. Then up at the stranger, whose hand was already extended, whose eyes said *quickly, quickly.*

Everything he had was stolen. Everyone who might help him was a stranger. The wards were down, the guild was sealed, and the oldest debt in the world had just come due.

Isandro took the hand.
