## 4. What the Guild Trades In

Sable kept her back to the door and her hands busy, because hands that looked busy didn't get dragged into the street by enforcers, and neither did their owners. She fed a strip of copper wire through the ward-knot she'd pulled off her own window frame three years ago — a knot she'd tied herself, back when the guild still paid in coin instead of favors — and pretended the two strangers crouched in her cellar workshop were furniture.

The scribe was the problem. He sat on an upturned crate with the ledger open across his knees, and he wasn't reading it like a man reads a book. He read it like a man reads a wound.

"You keep looking at me," Sable said without turning around.

"You said the stamp was a key." His voice had steadied since the alley, since she'd hauled him inside by his collar while the enforcers' lanterns swept past. Steadier than she liked. Frightened men ran; steady men asked questions, and questions got people hung. "A key to what? Doors lock from one side. This—" he touched the sigil-stamp where it lay wrapped in its rag among his things, "—this has teeth on all of them."

She let the wire sit half-threaded and turned around. Best to look at him when she lied or when she told the truth, so she'd know later which one she'd done.

"Sit properly," she said. "This takes longer than you have patience for."

"I have no patience at all."

"Then we'll be quick." She wiped her hands on her apron out of habit, though they weren't dirty. Ward-work never left dirt, only that faint smell of ozone and hot metal, like the air before lightning. "You're guild-trained. Indentured?"

"Since I was nine."

"Then answer me this, and think before you do, because I'll know if you're lying: what does the Ledger Guild actually *trade*?"

Isandro blinked. "Debt. Contracts. Sureties. The guild records obligations between houses, between merchants, between—"

"Between *realms*," Sable said.

The lamp guttered — not blue, just ordinary flame doing ordinary flame-things, but both of them flinched at it anyway. Outside, Vharn went on being dark. Three nights now. The sky over the city was a smooth black lid with no seam anywhere, and the markets had stopped selling candles because there was nothing left to light them with.

"That's a fable," Isandro said. "Children's stories. The dragon-mounts, the fire that holds the dark. My grandmother told—"

"Your grandmother was closer to the truth than your guild-masters ever let you get." Sable pulled the second stool across the floor with her boot and sat down opposite him, close enough that she could grab the ledger if she needed to. She probably would need to. "You've been keeping accounts for how long? Twelve years? And you've never once asked what the numbers were *for*."

"They're for debts. I just said."

"Whose debts?" She leaned forward. "You've copied ten thousand pages of obligation. Names given, names taken. Years sold against future labor. Memories pawned and never redeemed. You wrote them all down neat as scripture and never asked yourself why anyone would *want* to buy a man's memory of his mother's face, or twenty years of a woman's life, when they could just buy the woman's labor outright. What's the use of a thing like that? Who spends a memory?"

Isandro's jaw worked. She watched him reach for the guild answer — *the guild provides liquidity, the guild serves the flow of obligation* — and watched him fail to say it, because somewhere between the burning records and the sealed guildhouse door, the guild answer had died in him.

"I don't know," he said.

"*They* don't know either. That's the beauty of it." Sable stood, crossed to the shelf above her workbench, and moved aside jars of solder and coils of silvered wire until she found what she wanted: a scrap of parchment, old, soft as cloth, covered in writing that shimmered faintly even in lamplight. She'd stolen it eight years ago from a factor who'd been too drunk to count his inventory. She had owed the guild three favors ever since — the fine for theft, compounded annually, a debt she could work off for a lifetime and never clear. She set it in front of the scribe.

He touched it the way other men touched relics. "This is covenant-language. Pre-guild. Nobody can parse this anymore. It's not in any of the training codices—"

"It's in yours," Sable said. "Look again. Look at it the way you look at everything else."

And here was the thing about Isandro Iversen, the thing she'd guessed at in the alley and confirmed now as she watched: he didn't read like the rest of them. Most people saw words. He saw *structure*. She'd heard stories about guild scribes like this — one in a generation, the ones the masters kept close and used hard and never promoted, because a scribe who could parse the old covenants could also *audit* them.

His finger traced the shimmering lines, slow, then faster. "It's... it's a promissory instrument. A loan." His voice dropped like the words cost something. "Collateral clause — the whole realm held in surety. Interest schedule. Renewal terms. This is a loan agreement, Sable. It's the largest one I've ever seen. The principal is..." He stopped. Started again. "Fire. The principal is fire."

"Now you've got it."

* * *

She told it plainly, because plainly was the only way to tell a thing this size without it swallowing you whole.

"Long ago — and I mean long, longer than Vharn, longer than any kingdom standing — there was a hole in the world. Not a metaphor. An actual absence, north and east, past the old borders. We call it the Hollow. Things live there, or un-live there, whatever you want to call the opposite of existing. And they press. Always pressing. Like water against a dam."

"The border wards," Isandro said slowly.

"Wards are just shapes. Shapes need power to hold them. Mortal power burns out — ask any hedge-witch who's fried her own hands trying to hold a circle past dawn. But dragon-fire doesn't burn out. Dragon-fire is a *constant*. So our ancestors went up into the mounts and they begged. And the dragons, being old and proud and apparently sentimental, agreed to lend it. Fire enough to hold the wards forever, on one condition."

"Payment," Isandro said. He was holding the ledger against his chest now like a shield. "In what?"

"In the only currencies dragons value. They're creatures of memory, those beasts. They hoard more than gold — they hoard *knowing*. So the covenant paid in names, in years, in memories. Every generation, a tithe. A child here whose name goes unwritten. An old man there who forgets his wedding day and sits by the window puzzled at the stranger calling him 'love.' Whole villages that gave everything and became footnotes. That's what the impossible things in your ledgers are, scribe. That's what the guild trades in. Not debt between merchants. Debt between *worlds*."

"And the guild," Isandro said, and his voice had gone flat and careful, the voice of a man walking toward a cliff edge on purpose, "is the broker."

"The guild is the *middleman*. Somebody had to collect the tithes, match them to the interest schedule, carry the payments up-mountain. Honest work, once. Necessary work. And then some clever ancestor realized that if you control the ledger, you control the terms. You can skim. Round the interest up. Take a name here, a year there, record only half of it as delivered and pocket the difference." She tapped the covenant-scrap. "Dragons can't audit us. They live centuries up in the cold. They trust the paperwork. And the paperwork says exactly what the guild wants it to say."

"So the guild grew rich."

"The guild grew *fat*. Fat on skimmed years, skimmed names, skimmed remembering. Every impossible thing in your ledgers — every bottled year, every purchased face, every debt that can't be repaid in coin — it all flows uphill eventually. Into the Master-Ledger. Into the vaults under the guildhouse." She laughed, short and bitter. "I spent six months as a girl repairing the ward-arrays on the collection wagons. Do you know what I saw in those crates? Jars. Hundreds of jars. Each one glowing faintly, each one labeled with a name and a date. I thought they were preserves. I was twelve."

Isandro had gone very pale. Good. Pale meant he understood. Men who stayed pink while you explained this had already decided not to believe it.

"But that's theft," he said. "That's — the interest schedules alone, if the dragons ever audited—"

"If the dragons ever audited," Sable said, "there'd be no guild left to burn. Which brings us to the part you've been dancing around since you walked in here wearing a stolen stamp and a stolen ledger." She pointed at the ceiling, past it, at the black lid of sky. "Three nights ago, the stars went out. The wards fell. And I want you to tell me, scribe, with your gift for reading structure, what happens to a loan when the payments stop?"

Isandro didn't answer right away. He looked down at the ledger on his knees, at the page that had rewritten itself mid-entry — she'd seen it too, in the alley, lines crawling like ants — and she watched him do the arithmetic he'd been trained for, the only kind he knew.

"Default," he said finally. "Someone defaulted. Someone stopped paying, and the covenant—" He swallowed. "The covenant is calling in the collateral. The wards *were* the collateral. The realm itself."

"Or someone made it *look* like a default," Sable said quietly. "Either way. Either way, scribe, here's where we stand: the Hollow is pressing, the fire is withdrawn, and every soul in Vharn is living on borrowed time that nobody's paying interest on anymore."

* * *

For a long moment he just sat there, and Sable let him. There was a particular sickness that came with this knowledge — she remembered her own dose of it, eight years ago, hunched over a stolen scrap of parchment with her heart hammering. It was vertigo, was what it was. The feeling that the floor of the world was itself a floorboard laid over a deeper drop, and someone had just told you the nails were rusted through.

She watched it hit him in stages. First the denial, flickering and dying behind his eyes. Then the recalculation — she could nearly see him re-reading his own twelve years of entries, every neat line of figures suddenly revealing itself as a tithe-schedule, every "impossible asset" a stolen jar of somebody's life. Then, last, the guilt. That was always the worst stage. She saw his hand drift to the ledger, touch the cover, flinch away.

"I recorded them," he said. "Some of these entries are in my hand. Years. Names. I wrote a widow out of her own house in the spring quarter. I thought I was recording a sale. I thought—"

"You thought you were a clerk. You were. That's not the same as guilty."

"It feels the same."

"It always does." Sable took the stool again, nearer this time. "Listen to me, scribe. I owe the guild three favors I can never repay. You know what that means? It means I'm property with opinions. I know exactly how it feels to find out the floor is a loan. But here's what I also know: the first step under vertigo is to pick one fixed point and hold onto it. So pick one. What's yours?"

Isandro was quiet a long time. When he spoke, he didn't answer her directly — which, she was learning, was how he answered most things.

"This page," he said, opening the ledger to the self-writing entry. The ink had settled now, dried into lines that hadn't been there three nights ago. New text beneath the old. "It started rewriting the night the stars went out. I thought it was corruption. Now I think—" He turned it toward her, and even she, who couldn't parse covenant-language, could see the shape of it: a schedule. Dates. The next one soon. Very soon. "Now I think the ledger is still trying to make the payment. Or record it. It's a covenant-instrument, and instruments don't stop working just because the parties default. It's *pointing*, Sable. Page after page, each one newer than the last, each one further along this heading—" he tapped the margin, where a notation repeated like a refrain, "—northeast. Toward the old tollhouses."

"Tollhouses." She frowned. "On the mountain roads."

"On the *covenant* roads. The routes the payment-tithes traveled, before the guild rerouted everything through Vharn." He closed the ledger gently, the way you close a door on a sleeping child. "If someone stopped the payments, the record of *why* will be along that chain. Signatures. The original signatures, maybe. If the covenant can be defaulted, maybe it can be—" He stopped himself, wary of hoping out loud in front of a stranger.

"Reaffirmed," Sable supplied.

"I was going to say renegotiated."

"You were going to say reaffirmed, and you were afraid to." She studied him — this narrow, ink-stained man who'd committed one small act of theft and accidentally become the most dangerous person in a starless city. Enforcers were sweeping Vharn for him tonight, tomorrow, and every night after; she'd heard the bells change pattern at dusk. Hiding him was spending favor-currency she didn't have. Keeping him was worse.

Keeping him was also, she noticed with irritation, exactly what she intended to do.

"The tollhouse chain starts past the Eastgate," she said. "Which is sealed. Which means we go out through the underways, which means I spend a favor getting us past the toll-wardens, which means—" she spread her hands, "—you should understand what you're asking, even if you haven't asked yet."

"I'm not asking anything."

"No. You're sitting in my cellar holding a stolen covenant-ledger and waiting for me to volunteer." She stood, and to her own surprise, she smiled — small and crooked and entirely against her better judgment. "Fine. Consider it volunteered. But we leave before the fourth bell, and you carry the ledger, and if we meet enforcers, you follow my lead and you don't read anything aloud. Understood?"

"Understood." He stood too, awkwardly, clutching ledger and satchel. At the foot of the stairs he paused. "Sable. Why help me? You owe the guild. Helping me is the opposite of paying them."

She looked at him for a moment — at the ink stains, the frightened steady eyes, the ledger hugged like a shield.

"Because somebody finally read the contract," she said. "And I've waited my whole life to watch what happens next."

Above them, through the boarded window, the starless sky pressed down like a held breath. And in the ledger under Isandro's arm, unseen by either of them, a fresh line of ink surfaced on the oldest page, patient as a tide:

*Tollhouse One. The First Signature. Come and see.*
