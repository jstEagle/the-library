## 5. The First Signature

The ledger woke before dawn.

I had slept — if you could call it that — with my back against a dry-stone wall and the book open on my chest like a sleeping animal. Sable had warned me not to do it. *It's not a book anymore, Isandro. It stopped being a book the night the stars went out.* But I was a scribe to my marrow, and you don't sleep apart from your work. You just don't.

So when the pages began to turn against my ribs, soft as moth wings, I felt it in my body before my mind caught up.

"Isandro." Sable's voice came out of the dark, low and already moving. "Tell me that's you."

"It's not me."

The ledger lay open across my knees, and by the guttering light of our one permitted candle — Sable rationed fire like a miser now, and after what we'd seen flames do in Vharn, I didn't argue — I watched fresh ink crawl across the page. Not written. *Grown*, the way frost grows on glass. The letters assembled themselves stroke by stroke, and I read them as they formed, because reading was the one thing I had ever been good at, and even now, with the world unmoored, that hadn't changed.

*West road. The tollhouse at Marrow Ford. Where the first hand signed.*

Below it, smaller, almost an afterthought:

*Come alone. Come armed. Come anyway.*

"Well," Sable said, reading over my shoulder. Her breath stirred my hair. "That's friendly."

"It's a summons."

"It's a trap with directions." She crouched beside me, and in the candlelight her face was all sharp angles and shadow, a face built for squinting at ward-threads in bad light. Three days on the road together and she still looked at me like I might be a guild plant, some elaborate test she hadn't been briefed on. I couldn't blame her. Trust, I was learning, was a currency nobody traded honestly anymore.

"The tollhouse at Marrow Ford," I said. "Do you know it?"

"I know of it." Something shifted in her voice — the careful flatness she used when a subject mattered too much. "Everyone from the border country knows of it. It's where the covenant was sworn. The first time. Before the guild, before the wards, before any of it. My grandmother used to say the stones there still remembered the dragon-fire." She paused. "I always thought that was a story for children."

"And now?"

"Now I think my grandmother owed somebody something too." She stood, brushing grit from her knees. "We should reach it by midday if the roads hold."

"If the roads hold," I repeated. The Hollow had been seeping through the fallen wards for three days now, and the country between us and the mountains was going strange at the edges. We'd passed a village yesterday where every door stood open and every table was set for a meal no one had eaten. We'd passed a field where the wheat grew in perfect rows spelling out a word none of us could read, though I'd felt it trying to be read, pressing against my eyes like a thumb against a bruise.

Sable kicked dirt over the candle. In the dark, her voice again: "And Isandro? If this is a trap—"

"Then we're already caught. We've been caught since the night I stole this thing." I closed the ledger gently, the way you'd close a door on a sleeping child. "Let's go see who's collecting."

* * *

Marrow Ford earned its name honestly. The river ran shallow and quick over pale stones, and the ford itself was a spine of rock crossing the water like something buried that had decided, after centuries, to surface. On the far bank, the tollhouse crouched against the sky.

Ruined wasn't the word. Ruined implied collapse, decay, the slow indifference of time. This place had been *ended*. The roof was gone but the walls stood square and true, blackened at their tops as if a flame had passed over them once, precisely, and refused to touch anything below a certain height. The door hung open on leather hinges gone stiff as bark.

The ledger had gone quiet since morning. That worried me more than its whispering had. A book that stops talking is either satisfied or listening.

Sable stopped at the ford's edge and put a hand on my arm. "Look."

I looked. The tollhouse windows — empty sockets of them — were dark. But above the doorway, carved deep into the lintel stone, letters caught the grey starless light. Old script, older than the guild's tidy alphabet, the kind of writing I'd only ever seen in the sealed annexes of the Ledger Hall, behind doors that required two keys and a blood-oath to open.

I crossed the river without deciding to. Some part of me was already translating, the way it always did, the shapes resolving into meaning before I gave them permission:

*HERE THE FIRST DEBT WAS SWORN AND THE FIRST DEBT WAS HONORED. LET NO HAND ENTER UNPAID.*

"Charming hospitality," Sable muttered behind me. Then, sharper: "Isandro. There's someone inside."

I'd seen it too — or felt it. A weight behind the doorway, the particular pressure of a person standing very still. I had spent six years in the Guild of Vharn learning to sense enforcers the way sailors sense weather: a stillness in the air, a held breath in the architecture. Someone was waiting for us, and they were very good at waiting.

"We could go around," Sable said, without conviction. There was no around. The ledger had led us here the way a hook leads a fish, and we both knew it.

"No," I said. "We couldn't."

I stepped through the doorway.

* * *

He moved faster than anything that size should move.

One moment the interior was shadow and rubble; the next, a man filled it — huge, hooded, a blade already drawn and leveled at my throat with the economy of someone who had done this ten thousand times. Guild-issue steel. Guild-issue stance. Guild-issue everything except the desertion badge torn from his collar, the ragged hole where it had been ripped away still visible in the fabric.

"Drop the satchel," he said. His voice was gravel poured slow.

Sable froze in the doorway behind me. I raised my hands, slowly, keeping them away from my body the way the guild trained debtors to — palms out, fingers spread, nothing hidden. Six years of muscle memory, and for once it served me.

"I'm not guild," I said.

"You're carrying guild steel." The blade tipped toward my belt, where the sigil-stamp rode in its wrapping. He couldn't have seen it. He *knew* it was there anyway. "That stamp hasn't left a guildhouse in two hundred years. So either you stole it, which makes you a dead man, or they gave it to you, which makes you worse."

"I stole it."

Something flickered behind the hood. Not approval. Recalculation. "Thief, then. Better. Thieves I can kill with a clean conscience." The blade didn't waver. "Turn around. Walk back across the ford. Don't stop walking until the river's out of sight."

"This is the tollhouse," I said. "Where the first signature was sworn."

"I know what it is."

"Then you know why the ledger brought us here." I kept my voice level, though my heart was hammering against my ribs hard enough to hurt. "You've been waiting. Not for us specifically — for *someone*. For whoever the book chose. Because you can't guard this place forever, and you know it, and you're tired, and you want to be relieved of duty so badly you can taste it."

The blade pressed forward until the point dimpled the skin of my throat. Behind me, Sable made a small sound — warning, or protest, or both.

"One more word," the man said, "and I'll finish the sentence for you."

Every instinct I owned screamed at me to shut up. But I was a scribe, and scribes are trained from their first day in one iron law: *the record outranks the fear.* And I had spent the last mile studying him the way I studied everything — the torn badge, the guild stance worn like an old scar, the way his boots were planted not facing the door but facing the hearthstone at the room's center, where a slab of granite sat flush with the floor. He wasn't guarding the building. He was guarding the stone.

And I had seen his name.

It had crawled up the ledger's margin two nights ago, one entry among dozens, and I hadn't understood it then. I understood it now. The ink had written itself in the old script, the lintel script, and my mind had translated it without asking permission, the way it translated everything:

*Brannoc Hale, enforcer third rank. Debt outstanding: forty-one lives, debtor villages of Ellswater and Cindermoor, order of burn countermanded by conscience, penalty assessed. Status: payment deferred. Collector: pending.*

Forty-one lives. Villages. *Burn.* And a penalty deferred, hanging over him like a blade of his own — the guild's arithmetic never forgave, it only postponed.

"Brannoc Hale," I said.

The blade went still. Utterly still, the stillness of a man whose whole body has become the weapon.

"That name is dead," he said quietly. "I buried it in the Ellswater ash. Nobody living knows it."

"The ledger knows it." I lowered my hands, slowly, and reached for the satchel — and he let me, because curiosity is stronger than caution in men who have been alone too long, and I gambled everything on that. I drew out the book and opened it to the page and turned it toward him. "Read your own entry. Deferred, not forgiven. The guild doesn't forget debts, Brannoc. It just waits until the debtor has something worth taking."

He didn't look at the page. He looked at me, and for the first time the hood tilted enough that I saw his jaw, hard as the lintel stone, and one eye — grey, exhausted, human.

"They sent you," he said. "They wrote your little book and they sent you to collect."

"The guild wants to burn this book. They burned records in Vharn the night the wards fell. They're burning villages right now — I've seen the smoke." I turned the page toward him again, insistent. "Whatever wrote these entries, it isn't the guild. The guild writes ledgers to own people. This one—" I searched for the words and found them the way I found all words, in the grammar of debt. "This one writes to *remember*. There's a difference. Owning and remembering. The whole world runs on the difference, and nobody remembers it anymore."

Silence. The river spoke outside, patient, indifferent. Somewhere in the walls, wind found a gap and sighed through it like a voice practicing an old language.

Then Brannoc Hale did something I will remember for the rest of my life, however long or short the ledger decides to make it.

He sheathed the blade.

Not lowered. Sheathed — the full gesture, deliberate, ceremonial, the way a soldier ends a watch. And he pulled back his hood, and he was younger than his voice, thirty maybe, with a burn scar crawling up the left side of his neck like ivy, and eyes that had seen exactly the things his debt-entry said he'd seen.

"Deferred," he repeated. "Not forgiven." He laughed, and it was a terrible sound, rusted from disuse. "You know how long I've stood in this doorway waiting for the collector? Fourteen months. Every footstep on that ford, I thought: *now. Now they've come to make me pay for Ellswater.* And instead the book sends me—" he gestured at me with one enormous hand, "—a starving scribe and a tinkerer who walks like she's counting exits."

"She is counting exits," Sable said from the doorway. "Four. All of them past you."

"Good instincts." He looked at me again, and the exhaustion in his face rearranged itself into something harder to name. "You said the guild burns villages. Present tense."

"Cindermoor way. Smoke for two days. Fire-scribes, I think — erasing the old debt-records along with everything else."

His jaw worked. Whatever Cindermoor meant to him, it lived close to whatever Ellswater meant, and neither of those things lived anywhere gentle. "Then the deferral ends soon either way," he said. "If they erase the records, the debt goes nowhere. Unrecorded debts don't die, scribe. They just stop being *owed* and start being *owed by everyone*." He spat the last words like a seed. "That's the oldest lesson this house teaches. Look at the hearthstone."

I turned. The granite slab sat flush with the floor, and I had taken it for a foundation stone, but now, kneeling, I saw the carving. A signature — vast, looping, burned into the stone rather than cut, the way lightning burns a tree. Around it, smaller marks: dozens of names, hundreds, each one scored through with a single clean line.

"What are the struck-through names?"

"Guarantors." Brannoc's voice had gone quiet, reverent, the voice of a man in a shrine he hated needing. "Every few generations, someone signs the covenant anew. Pays with their name — their place in memory, in record, in the world. The old names get struck when the new one signs. My grandmother's grandmother was alive when the last one signed. She said the whole border country forgot the man within a year. Not died. *Forgot.* Like he'd been paid out of existence." He crouched beside me, and up close I could smell woodsmoke and old leather and beneath it something stranger, mineral, like the air before lightning. "The wards ran on that name for two hundred years. Now the wards are down. Do your arithmetic, scribe."

I did it. It was simple arithmetic, the cruelest kind.

The last guarantor's payment had run out. The covenant needed a new signature. And somewhere in the mountains, the thing whose fire had sealed the first covenant was either dead or dying, because the ledger's pages had started writing themselves in a hand that trembled.

"There's a dragon," I said. "Isn't there. Out there. Still holding the seal."

Brannoc and Sable exchanged a look over my head — the look of two people discovering they might be on the same side after all.

"The western flight kept the mountain-shrines," Brannoc said slowly. "When I deserted, I came east because west was where the dragons were, and I couldn't stand to watch another thing I loved burn. But if the wards fell..." He shook his head. "There'll be one left. Maybe. The old stories say the last dragon holds the last signature. And the last signature needs a mortal hand to guide it."

"A guarantor," I said.

"A volunteer," he corrected. "That's the difference. Same as owning and remembering." He stood, and offered me his hand — huge, scarred, hesitating halfway as if the offer surprised even him. "You read my name off that page. You could have used it to hang me. Instead you showed me my own bill and asked nothing." His grey eyes narrowed. "So I'll ask you straight, scribe, because I'm done with people who ask crooked. What is it you actually want?"

I took his hand. It swallowed mine whole.

"I want to follow the ledger," I said, "all the way to the last page. And I want to strike your debt clean on the way — not deferred. Clean. Forty-one lives is a debt I can't pay for you, but it's a debt I can *carry*, and carried debts weigh less than hidden ones. That's the first thing they teach you in the Guild of Vharn, and it's the only thing they teach that's true."

For a long moment he said nothing. Then, "You'd write yourself into my debt?"

"I'd write myself into everyone's debt. It's what I'm apparently for." I glanced down at the ledger, silent and warm against my chest, waiting. "The book seems to agree."

Brannoc Hale laughed again — and this time it wasn't rusted. It was startled out of him, brief and real, the laugh of a man who'd forgotten the mechanism existed. "A scribe who collects debts by taking them on himself. The guild would flay you."

"The guild is trying."

He turned to the hearthstone, to the great burned signature and all its struck-through names, and stood before it the way soldiers stand before graves. When he spoke again, his voice had changed — lower, formal, the cadence of an oath.

"Then hear the terms of this house, scribe, and I'll travel under them. No hand enters unpaid. No debt leaves uncarried. And no one walks the hollow country alone." He looked back at me, and then at Sable in the doorway, and something in his face — the first loosening of fourteen months of solitary penance — made the ruined tollhouse feel, for one breath, less like a tomb. "Those were the terms the first signers swore. I've kept them alone this long. I'd rather keep them badly, in company."

Outside, the starless light was failing toward evening. And as we gathered our things — Sable checking the ward-threads on her tools, Brannoc shouldering a pack that had clearly been packed for a departure he'd never believed would come — the ledger stirred against my ribs one final time.

New ink, growing letter by letter, in that same trembling hand:

*West. Up. She is nearly out of paper.*

I read it twice before I understood, and when I understood, my mouth went dry.

Dragons didn't keep ledgers. But fire, I was beginning to learn, was a kind of writing — and whatever remained of the western flight was running out of it.

"Brannoc," I said. "How fast can you walk?"
