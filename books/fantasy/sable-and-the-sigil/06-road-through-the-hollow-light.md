## 6. Road Through the Hollow-Light

Brannoc had walked this road before.

Not this road exactly — no one had walked this exact road in three days, because roads were one of the things the Hollow was eating — but this country. The low hills east of Vharn, where he'd marched with thirty enforcers behind him and a writ of erasure in his satchel. He knew the shape of the land the way you know the face of someone you've wronged: too well, and never again without flinching.

Now there were only three of them, and the satchel held a stolen ledger instead of a death warrant, and the sky above was a flat black ceiling where stars used to be.

He walked at the front. It wasn't a decision anyone made; it was just where his boots put him. Twenty years of enforcement did that. You learned to be the point of the spear or you learned to be dead.

Behind him, the scribe kept talking.

"—and that's the thing I can't stop turning over," Isandro said, breath ragged from keeping pace on the uphill stretch. "The ledger isn't recording what happened. It's recording what's *owed*. There's a difference. A record says *this is true*. A debt-page says *this must become true*. Do you see? It's not history. It's—"

"Isandro." Sable, at the rear, didn't raise her voice. She never raised her voice; she just aimed it. "If you narrate every thought, the Hollow will hear you coming before we do."

"The Hollow eats memories, not words."

"It eats whatever it can catch." Sable adjusted the strap of her tool-roll and said nothing else, which from her was a full paragraph of *shut up*.

Brannoc let them bicker behind him and watched the country.

They'd come down out of Vharn's outskirts at dawn-that-wasn't, because dawn required a sun and the sun still rose but gave no light worth the name — a pale coin behind smoke-colored glass, casting shadows so faint they might have been apologies. Under the fallen wards, the world ran on borrowed illumination. Lanterns burned yellow and then blue and then a color Brannoc had no word for, a color like remembering something that hadn't happened yet. Sable called it hollow-light. She said it the way sailors say *storm*.

The first bad place came an hour past the tollhouse ruins.

Brannoc smelled it before he saw it: a smell like a room after a funeral, like clothes kept in a drawer for years. Sweet and stale and final. He threw up his fist — old habit, and to his mild surprise both of them actually stopped — and crouched at the edge of the road.

Ahead, the fields lay under fog. Not weather-fog. This sat in a basin between two hills like milk poured into a bowl, perfectly still, faintly luminous, and full of things.

"Memory-pool," Sable murmured, coming up beside him. "Don't drink it. Don't touch it. Don't look at your reflection in it if you can help it."

"What happens if you do?"

"You remember something that was taken from someone else. And they don't get it back." Her jaw tightened. "There's a village two days south of here that lost its whole harvest-memory that way. Farmers standing in their own fields holding hoes, no idea what the hoes were for."

"And the things walking in it?"

Sable squinted. Brannoc counted four shapes moving through the glow, slow as wading men. They were roughly person-shaped and precisely nothing-shaped, edges that refused to commit to existing. Forgotten things, the old enforcer manuals had called them — though the manuals' actual advice had been *report sightings to the guild and withdraw*, which was another way of saying *run*.

"They're not dangerous," Isandro said quietly. He'd come up on Brannoc's left, ledger already open across his forearm, pen moving. "Look — they're circling the pool. Not entering it. They want in. They're forgotten things trying to climb back into memory." His pen scratched. "That's all the Hollow is, isn't it? Everything the covenant ever erased, waiting outside the door now that the lock is broken."

"Don't romanticize it," Brannoc said. "I've seen what forgotten things do when they find someone carrying something worth taking."

"I've seen what *enforcers* do," Isandro said, not looking up, "when they find a village worth burning."

The words landed clean, no anger in them, which somehow made it worse. Brannoc said nothing. He led them wide around the pool, through wet grass that glowed faintly where their boots broke it, and didn't speak again until the fog was a mile behind.

* * *

They camped that night in the shell of a waystation, a stone circle with a roof like a broken egg. Sable built a ward-fire — her craft, real craft, copper wire and chalk and a flame that burned steady blue-white and pushed back the hollow-light in a neat hemisphere. Within its radius, Brannoc noticed, colors came back. The world remembered how to be red and brown and green.

He took first watch because he always took first watch, and because sleep had been a negotiation he kept losing since the day he'd walked away from the guild.

He was sitting with his back to the wall, whetstone moving along his blade more for rhythm than need, when Isandro sat down across from him.

"Sable's asleep?"

"She sleeps like a cat. Light and offended about it. Why?"

Isandro opened the ledger on his knees. In the ward-light the pages looked ordinary, which Brannoc had learned was a lie; those pages were never ordinary. "I wanted to show you something."

"If it's my name again—"

"It's your entry." Isandro turned the book around.

Brannoc read it. He was slow at reading — enforcers weren't educated so much as *processed* — but he'd gotten better these last days out of necessity. The entry was long. It listed the villages. Ashford-in-the-Vale. The mill towns. The river settlements whose names the guild had struck even before the fire touched them, so that officially, no one had ever lived there at all. Thirty-one lines. Each line ended the same way: *burned by order, by hand of B. Dunmarrow, enforcer third rank.*

His name. Over and over. Thirty-one times, in ink that would never fade, carried in a book that could apparently rewrite itself.

"That's why I deserted," Brannoc said. Flat. Watching the fire instead of the page. "In case you were wondering. Everyone wonders. They think it was conscience, some big moment, kneeling in the ash. It wasn't. It was arithmetic. Thirty-one villages. I did the sum on my fingers one night and the number wouldn't fit inside me anymore. So I left."

"I'm not wondering anything," Isandro said. "I'm telling you what I did."

He turned the ledger back around and showed Brannoc the page again.

The entry had changed.

It still listed the villages. All thirty-one. Every name restored, every date, every horror intact — Isandro hadn't softened a syllable of it, and Brannoc understood, with a strange lurch, that this was deliberate and that it was mercy. But beneath each line, in the scribe's small precise hand, new words had been added:

*B. Dunmarrow carried four children out of Ashford before the fire reached the east row.*

*B. Dunmarrow refused the order at Millbrook and was flogged for it, then obeyed, which is worse and also human.*

*B. Dunmarrow has walked toward the dragon-mounts for nine days on half rations to try to unmake this.*

"Burned by order, by hand of B. Dunmarrow" remained. But now it was followed by the rest of the truth, the parts the guild's records had never bothered to hold because the guild had no use for a man's whole weight — only the part that could hang him.

"You can't do that," Brannoc said. His voice came out rough. "You can't just — amend a man."

"I'm a ledger-keeper. Amending is the entire job." Isandro closed the book gently. "A record that only holds your worst line isn't a record. It's a sentence. The guild wrote sentences for everyone. I'm trying to write entries."

Brannoc looked at the fire for a long time. The whetstone had stopped moving in his hand somewhere during all of it.

"My mother kept accounts," he said finally. "For the tanner in our village. Little book, everything written twice, once in ink and once in her head. When she died the tanner forgave what we owed and told everyone she'd been honest to the end." He turned the blade over, watching the light run along the edge. "Honest. That was the whole eulogy. Two decades later I burn villages for a guild that calls itself honest, and I finally understand she was something better than honest. She was *accurate*."

Isandro smiled — a small, tired, real smile. "Accurate. Yes. That's the word."

From the far side of the fire, without opening her eyes, Sable said: "If you two are done having feelings, one of you should take second watch, because I am not doing it."

Brannoc laughed. It startled him — the sound coming out of his own chest, rusty as an unused gate. He couldn't remember the last time. Possibly years. Possibly since before the villages.

"I'll take it," he said. "Sleep."

And for the first time since the wards fell, sleep came easy to all three of them, inside the little blue hemisphere of a fire that remembered what color was.

* * *

The second bad place found them, rather than the reverse.

It was midday-of-sorts, the pale coin sun directly overhead and giving less light than the lanterns, when the road ahead simply stopped being a road. Not washed out, not blocked — *stopped*, as if the idea of road had been subtracted from that stretch of country. Beyond it lay fifty yards of gray nothing, ground without texture, air without smell, and standing in the middle of it, a figure.

Person-shaped. Still. Waiting.

"Hollow-seep," Sable breathed. She'd gone very still, one hand flat against her tool-roll. "That's deep seepage. The wards fell hardest here — see how the land's given up?" She pointed at the fence posts marching toward the gray zone, each one fading from wood to suggestion. "We go around. North, through the rocks. It'll cost us half a day."

"We don't have half a day," Isandro said. He had the ledger open again, and his face had gone the color of old paper. "Look at the pages."

Brannoc leaned over his shoulder. The self-writing script was racing across the page, faster than he'd ever seen it, lines appearing and correcting themselves like something desperate:

*the covenant-chain runs through*
*the chain runs through*
*through*

"It wants us to cross," Isandro said.

"It's a book," Brannoc said. "It doesn't want."

"Everything here wants. That's the problem." Sable was already uncoiling copper wire. "Fine. We cross, but we do it my way. Rope, single file, hands on the shoulder of the person ahead. Whatever you see in there, whatever calls you, whatever shows you your dead mother asking why you never wrote — you keep walking. You hear me? The Hollow doesn't attack. It *invites*. That's what makes it worse than anything the guild ever built."

She tied the rope herself, checked each knot twice, and went first, because of course she did.

The gray took them one step at a time. Brannoc went second, Isandro's hand gripping his shoulder hard enough to bruise, and the world thinned around them like breath fading off glass. Sound went first. Then the feeling of his own weight. What remained was the invitation.

It came gentle. That was the horror of it.

*A field at dusk,* said the Hollow, in a voice assembled from every kind voice Brannoc had ever heard. *Your mother's voice, mostly. The tanner's field, behind the house. You are eleven. Your father is alive. Nobody has ever asked you to carry anything heavier than water. Come sit down. Come stay. Here you never burned anything. Here you are still the boy who was going to be a cooper.*

Brannoc kept walking. Hands on rope. One foot, then the other. The field shimmered at the edge of his vision, warm and gold and utterly convincing, and somewhere in it a woman with his mother's hands waved him toward a bench, and he loved her so much his chest cracked open, and he kept walking.

Beside him — though there was no beside in the gray, only the rope — he heard Isandro make a sound. Small. Wounded. And then, barely audible, the scratch of a pen.

*He's writing in there.* Brannoc almost laughed, and the laugh almost broke the rope-grip, and he squeezed Isandro's shoulder hard enough to say *stay with me* without words. The scratch continued. Of course it did. Give that man the end of the world and he'd take minutes.

Then Sable's silhouette firmed up ahead, and color bled back into the world like a wound healing in fast-forward, and they stumbled out onto honest dirt with the gray zone hissing faintly behind them like a tide going out.

Isandro sat down where he stood, shaking. Brannoc crouched next to him.

"What did it show you?"

Isandro was quiet a moment. "A room," he said. "The guild archive, night shift, just me and the lamps. Except in the version it offered, nobody had ever indentured me. I was there because I loved the work." He looked up, and his eyes were wet and furious. "It didn't even change the cage. It just told me I'd chosen it. That's what it thought would break me."

"Did it?"

"No." He wiped his face with the heel of his hand, angry at the tears more than the Hollow. "Because I've got a ledger that says otherwise. Apparently I've been writing evidence against despair for days without noticing."

Brannoc held out a hand and pulled him up.

They made camp early, in the lee of a rock spine Sable pronounced defensible, and while she worked on the ward-fire Isandro sat apart with the ledger open, reading the pages he'd written inside the gray. After a while he went very still.

"Brannoc."

"What."

"Come read this."

Brannoc read. The entry was in Isandro's hand but not Isandro's phrasing — clipped, blunt, a soldier's cadence:

*In the gray place, B. Dunmarrow was shown everything he ever wanted, and he kept walking. Write that down. Write it in the permanent ink. Whatever else the ledger says about the man, it must also say: he keeps walking.*

Below it, smaller, in Isandro's normal voice: *I witnessed this myself. I was holding his shoulder at the time.*

Brannoc read it twice. Something in his chest that had been calcifying for twenty years shifted, painfully, like ice going out on a river in spring.

"You understand," he said, "that if you keep writing me like that, I'm going to have to actually be that man."

"I know," Isandro said. "That's rather the point. Ledgers don't describe debts, Brannoc. They create them." He tapped the page. "Congratulations. You owe me."

"To whom?" Sable called over, without looking up from her wiring. "What's he owe?"

"A debt of accuracy," Isandro said solemnly. "Payable in walking."

Sable snorted. It was, Brannoc suspected, the closest thing to laughter she permitted herself, and he filed it away as proof that the day had been a good one despite everything.

* * *

Two days later, the mountains came up out of the dark like a decision being made.

They'd been visible as absence before that — a blacker black against the starless sky, a place where even the hollow-light dimmed, as if the land itself were holding its breath. Now, in the flat gray noon, they resolved into ridgelines and snowfields and passes, and at their feet the foothills began, forested and silent in a way forests shouldn't be.

No birds. Brannoc noticed it the same moment Sable did, because they both stopped walking at the same time.

"There should be birds," she said.

"There should be a lot of things." Isandro had the ledger out. The self-writing script had changed character again — slower now, deliberate, each line appearing with the gravity of a signature being set down:

*up*
*higher*
*where the last fire waits*
*she has been waiting a long time*
*hurry*

"*She*," Sable repeated. She looked at the mountains, then at Isandro, and Brannoc watched the color leave her face as the arithmetic completed. "The dragons. The western flight kept the high shrines. If any of them are still—" She didn't finish. None of them finished sentences much anymore; the endings were too often bad ones.

Brannoc looked up at the passes, and his gut did the thing it did before bad orders, the cold drop, the veteran's warning bell. Because he could see the pass they'd need to take — the only pass, this time of year, the notch in the ridge called the Shepherd's Throat — and he could see, even at this distance, movement in it.

Figures. A dozen or more. Gray cloaks. Moving with the drilled economy of men who had been trained by the same people who trained him.

Enforcers. Ahead of them, not behind. Corvane hadn't sent hunters trailing their path.

He'd sent them to guard the door.

"Well," Brannoc said, rolling his shoulders, feeling twenty years of muscle settle into old grooves like a key into a lock it had been cut for. "I know those men. I drank with half of them." He checked his blade, and found, to his complete lack of surprise, that his hands were steady. "Question is whether we go through them, around them, or whether one of us goes through them alone while the rest of you run for the shrine."

"Brannoc—" Isandro started.

But Brannoc was already looking at the pass, doing the arithmetic he'd sworn off doing since the villages, and finding that some sums you can't help working.
