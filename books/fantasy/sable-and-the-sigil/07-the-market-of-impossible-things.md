## 7. The Market of Impossible Things

The market found them before they found it.

Sable smelled it first — woodsmoke and hot metal and something sweeter underneath, like sugar burned in a pan, and she stopped so hard that Brannoc walked into her back and swore.

"What," he said.

"You'll want to put your hood up," she said. "Both of you. And Isandro — whatever you see tonight, don't touch anything with your hands. Trade with your eyes until I tell you otherwise."

"Is this another ward-tinker thing?" Wren asked. She'd taken to walking at Sable's hip like a burr caught in cloth, and Sable had long since stopped trying to shake her loose. Not that she'd tried very hard.

"It's a *market* thing." Sable pulled her own hood forward and checked the knife at her belt for the third time, though she'd checked it a minute ago. "Everything here is real. That's the problem."

They came down out of the rocks into a bowl of land where the Hollow-light pooled like spilled milk, and there it was: the Market of Impossible Things, strung across the dead valley on ropes and poles and prayer, hundreds of lanterns burning colors fire had no business burning. Tents of patched silk. Stalls built from wagon-beds and coffin lids. And everywhere, people — more people than Sable had seen since the stars went out, haggling, laughing, weeping quietly over small purchases, moving through the lantern-glow with the particular carefulness of those who all carried something they couldn't afford to lose.

A crier stood at the mouth of the main row, ringing a cracked bell.

"WELCOME TO THE FAIR OF FRAGMENTS!" he bellowed. "COVENANT PIECES BOUGHT AND SOLD! HONEST WEIGHTS! NO REFUNDS ON MEMORY!"

"Oh no," Isandro said softly.

"You've heard of it?"

"I've *filed* it." His voice had gone strange. "There's a ledger. Category nine, subsection four. Confiscated covenant fragments, disposition pending. I thought it was a rounding error. I thought it was maybe forty items." He looked out at the sea of stalls, the thousands of small glimmering things for sale, and Sable watched him do the arithmetic he couldn't help doing. "It's not forty items."

"No," Sable said. "It never was."

* * *

They moved into the crowd because standing still was worse, and because Sable needed things she could only find here: a coil of true-copper wire, a jar of lye, news of which passes were watched. The market gave it up grudgingly, stall by stall.

She bargained for wire while Isandro drifted along the shelves beside her, reading labels the way other men read scripture. Bottled years, stoppered in glass the color of old honey — *SUMMER, AGE 12, LIGHTLY USED; SOME RAIN.* Secondhand courage, sold by the swallow in clay cups, the vendor swearing each cup was tested on himself first. A woman selling handfuls of sleep from a sack like grain. A man with a tray of last words, laid out on black velvet, priced according to how much they'd meant.

And in the center of the fair, on a platform under its own canopy, a cage.

Not iron. The bars were made of ribbon — grey ribbon, the color of fog before rain, wound tight around nothing — and inside it hovered a dozen shapes like heat-shimmer, like breath on a cold window. Vows. Unspoken vows, harvested somehow, kept somewhere, waiting for someone to speak them or sell them. One of them pressed against the ribbon-bars as Sable passed, and she felt it against her skin like a hand she remembered.

She didn't look at it twice. Looking twice cost money here, one way or another.

"Sable." Isandro's voice, low and urgent. He'd stopped at a stall hung with keys — hundreds of them, brass and bone and black iron — and the vendor, a squat woman with ink-stained fingers, was staring at him with her head tilted like a bird's.

"That's guild script," the woman said. "That's guild *hand*. You're no debtor, boy. You're a pen."

"I'm nobody," Isandro said.

"You're the nobody they're paying for." The woman's voice didn't rise. That was the horror of it — she said it the way you'd comment on weather, and already her eyes were flicking past his shoulder, already her hand was drifting toward a little brass bell on her counter, the kind factors used, the kind that rang only for the people paid to hear it. "Full pardon and fifty crowns, the notice said. Alive and unspoiled."

Sable's knife was out before she'd decided anything. "Step back from the bell."

The woman smiled with all the warmth of an invoice. "Girl, I've been robbed by professionals. You're an amateur with feelings."

The bell rang anyway — the woman flicked it with one knuckle even as Sable lunged — and the sound went out over the fair not as noise but as *instruction*, a single clear note that Sable felt in her teeth, and then the whole market changed key. Conversations didn't stop, exactly. They just developed edges. Lanterns guttered. Somewhere behind them, a whistle answered the bell, then another.

"Run," Brannoc said, and put Wren up on his shoulders like she weighed nothing, which she didn't, which was its own quiet grief.

They ran.

* * *

The market turned against them the way markets do — not with violence but with geometry. Awnings swung down to block the lane ahead. Stall-keepers folded their tables into walls. The crowd itself thickened politely, helpfully, a hundred bodies suddenly very interested in being exactly where Sable needed to pass. Guild coin bought that. Guild coin bought everything except, apparently, a working covenant.

"They're funneling us," Brannoc panted. "Toward the east gate. There'll be enforcers—"

"I know where they're funneling us." Sable skidded around a corner and stopped so fast Wren yelped. Ahead, the lane ended at a wide square where three whistlers were already walking, unhurried, closing the last side-streets like fingers folding shut. Beyond them, past the fair's ragged edge, the ground dropped away into a gorge spanned by a single rope bridge, and on the far side of the bridge stood a flatbed wagon hitched to two mules, and beside the wagon stood a ferryman — the only person in the whole valley who ran the crossing, because the gorge below wasn't empty. It was one of the places where forgotten things pooled, and nothing with a name crossed it cheaply.

The whistlers were between them and the bridge.

Sable did the sums. She was good at sums; tinker-work was mostly sums. Three whistlers, two knives, one scribe who could rewrite reality but froze whenever anyone asked him to, one child, one deserter with a bad shoulder. The sums came out wrong every way she ran them.

Except one way.

At the edge of the square stood a booth she'd noticed coming in and hated herself for noticing: a narrow stall hung with scales, run by a factor — a real one, guild robes gone shabby, face like a closed ledger. Above the booth, in letters of silver thread, the market's oldest sign:

**FAVORS REDEEMED HERE.**

Every debt in the world flowed through booths like that one eventually. Including hers.

Three favors. That was what Sable owed the Ledger Guild, sworn when she was nineteen and desperate and the alternative was worse, and she'd spent six years carrying them like stones sewn into her coat. She'd told herself she'd pay them off slow, on her terms, in ways that hurt least. Everyone told themselves that.

She walked up to the factor's booth while the whistlers were still thirty yards out, and she put both hands flat on the counter, and she said the words she'd rehearsed in her head for six years without ever wanting to say aloud:

"I redeem a favor. Now. In person. Before witnesses." The market had witnesses everywhere; that was the point of markets. "I call it due."

The factor's eyebrows rose a fraction. "Name and debt-number."

"Sable Vess. Ward-tinker, third class. Debt of three favors, sworn under seal, year of the Long Frost." Her voice held. She was almost proud of it. "I spend the first favor now. Payment: safe passage out of this market for myself and my companions, four souls, unmolested, by whatever means the guild can compel."

The factor opened a book. Ran a finger down a column. And then he did something Sable had never seen a factor do.

He hesitated.

"That's—" He cleared his throat. "That favor is encumbered."

"*Encumbered.*"

"Pledged onward. Your first favor was collateralized, Mistress Vess, six years ago, against a bond of guild paper. It belongs now to a third party." He turned the book around, and there it was in cold columns, her own signature at the top and, beneath it, a transfer record in someone else's hand. "Your first favor is owed to a woman called Maren Ostwick. Of Harrow Fen."

Sable stared at the page. The name meant nothing. The name meant *everything*, because it meant that some clerk, six years ago, had taken her promise — the most personal thing she owned, the thing she'd signed with her actual self — and sold it, traded it, moved it around like a sack of grain, and she'd never known. She'd been carrying a stone that wasn't even hers anymore. Someone else had been holding her leash the whole time.

"That's not legal," she said, and heard how young she sounded.

"It's *accounting*," the factor said, with genuine sympathy, which was worse. "Everything is accounting."

Behind her, the whistles were closer. Brannoc had set Wren down and put himself between her and the lane, knife low. Isandro had gone pale in the lantern-light, and he was looking at Sable with an expression she'd learn later to hate — the look of a man watching someone pay his bill.

"The second favor," Sable said. "I spend the second favor. Same terms."

The factor winced. "Also encumbered."

"*What?*"

"Both of them. Transferred in the same instrument. I'm sorry. Whoever brokered your debt was thorough." He glanced past her at the approaching whistlers, and lowered his voice. "You have one left, Mistress Vess. Spend it well. Or—" he nodded at the bridge, the wagon, the mules, "—or don't spend it at all. Walk away owing what you owe. Some debts are lighter than their payment."

The whistlers rounded the corner.

And Sable Vess, who had spent six years protecting her last favor the way a miser protects a single coin, looked at the rope bridge, and at Wren, and at Isandro with his stolen ledger pressed against his ribs, and made the sum come out right the only way it could.

"The third favor," she said. "Safe passage. Four souls. Now."

* * *

The factor's redemption was efficient, because guild efficiency was the one honest thing about the guild. He blew a whistle of his own — a different note, higher, *command* rather than alarm — and the three pursuers stopped mid-stride like puppets whose strings had been re-tied. Orders conflicted; orders always conflicted; that was the flaw running through every machine the guild ever built, including the covenant itself.

Then they were on the bridge, all four of them, the planks swaying over a gorge full of soft grey light and murmuring shapes, and the ferryman took them across without ever asking their names, which Sable understood was the most expensive courtesy in the world.

On the far bank, by the wagon, the adrenaline drained out of her all at once, the way it does, leaving the bill behind.

Wren was asking if the glowing things in the gorge were ghosts. Brannoc was checking his shoulder and lying about it. Isandro came and stood next to Sable at the rail of the river landing, and for a while neither of them said anything, because the water below made a sound like pages turning.

"You spent it," he finally said. "Your last one. For us."

"For you, mostly." She meant it as a joke. It came out as a fact.

"Sable—"

"Don't." She held up a hand. "Don't do the thing where you add it to your ledger. Don't write me down as a debt you're going to repay, because that's not what this was." She looked out at the dark water. "Do you know what the worst part is? It's not spending it. I'd spend it again. The worst part is finding out I never owned it. Six years I guarded that favor like it was mine, and it had already been sold out from under me before I ever finished signing. That's what the guild *does*, Isandro. It takes the truest thing you have and puts it in a column."

He was quiet a moment. Then, carefully, in the voice he used for dangerous pages: "Whoever holds your first two favors. Maren Ostwick. We could find her. Debts can be renegotiated. I've read enough covenants to know the words matter more than the seals — if she agrees, the transfer could be unwound—"

"You'd hunt down a stranger in Harrow Fen to un-sell my promise?"

"I'd hunt down anyone," Isandro said, "who owns a piece of my family."

And there it was. Sable opened her mouth to argue — to tell him family was a strong word for five weeks of bad roads — and found she couldn't, because the word had already gotten inside her and set up shop.

Brannoc called from the wagon: mules were rested, road was climbing, dragon-country by tomorrow night if the weather held. Wren had already fallen asleep against a feed sack, one hand curled around a scrap of ribbon she'd picked up off the market floor — grey ribbon, fog-colored, torn from a cage of unspoken vows. Sable made a note to take it from her before morning. Some things shouldn't be kept by children.

She climbed onto the wagon bench. Isandro handed her the reins, then paused, and reached into his coat, and brought out the new ledger — the small one, the one he'd started without explaining — and wrote something in it by lantern-light before snapping it shut.

"What did you write?" she asked.

"Nothing," he said, in the tone of a man who has absolutely written something.

The wagon rolled north, into country where the stars should have been and weren't, and behind them the market's lanterns shrank to a scatter of dishonest jewels. Sable told herself the ache under her ribs was just the cost of doing business.

Two days later, at a crossroads shrine, they would find a notice nailed up in fresh guild ink: a reward notice, updated, the price raised. And beneath the description of the fugitive scribe, a new line added in a different hand — neat, patient, and addressed to no one in particular:

*Item: one (1) favor, unredeemed, belonging to Sable Vess, ward-tinker. Holders of information regarding the above are invited to inquire.*

Someone was already shopping for what was left of her.
