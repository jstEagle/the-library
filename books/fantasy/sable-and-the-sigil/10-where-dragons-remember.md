## 10. Where Dragons Remember

The mountain did not want us.

I'd climbed passes before — in the guild's service, escorting factors to remote tollhouses, hauling chains and writs up switchbacks carved by men who hated the work as much as I had. Mountains were indifferent. That was their nature. You climbed them the way you paid a debt: one grudging step at a time, resenting every one.

This was different.

From the moment we crossed the tree line, the mountain *noticed* us. The wind changed direction when we did, as if it were turning its head. The scree under our boots rang with a note too low to hear properly, more felt than heard, like a struck bell heard through water. And everywhere — everywhere — there was the light.

Hollow-light, Sable called it. It pooled in the high hollows like fog with intentions, silver-gray and slow, and where it gathered, things grew that had no business growing: grass the color of old pewter, flowers that opened only when you looked away. Twice I saw shapes standing in it, far off, patient. Once I saw a shape that wore my own coat, my own shoulders, standing very still until I blinked, and then it wasn't there anymore.

"Memories pool up here," Sable said, when she caught me staring at the place where I'd almost been. She walked with Wren strapped to her back in a sling of oiled canvas, the child's arms around her neck, her small face pressed into Sable's hair. "Old ones. Things the wards used to hold off. Don't talk to anything that looks like someone you've lost."

"What if it looks like someone I owe?" I asked.

"Then you're already talking to it," she said. "That's how it gets its hooks in."

Brannoc said nothing. He hadn't said much since the lowlands, since the ash, since Wren. He walked point with his axe across his back and his eyes on the ridgelines, and he counted things under his breath — steps, breaths, birds, though there were no birds. I'd started keeping count of his counting. It was how I knew something was wrong with him beyond the usual wrongness of a man carrying what he carried.

He'd stopped counting on the second morning. That frightened me more than the Hollow-shapes.

* * *

We found the first dragon on the third day.

Or what had been a dragon. It lay along a ridge like a felled mast, its body half-sunk into the stone, and the stone had grown around it the way bark grows around an old fence post — accepting it, incorporating it, making it part of the mountain whether the mountain liked it or not. Its scales had gone the gray of dead coals. Its wings were folded with terrible neatness, the way a soldier folds his kit before a march he doesn't expect to return from.

Wren slid down from Sable's back and stood before it, very solemn, and touched one claw with two fingers, the way you'd touch a sleeping person's shoulder.

"Is it sleeping?" she asked.

"No," Brannoc said. His voice came out rough, like a door opening after years shut. He crouched beside the great skull and did something I didn't expect: he took off his glove and laid his bare palm flat against the scales. Held it there. "It's spent."

"Spent doing what?" I asked, though I already knew. I could feel the ledger in my satchel warming against my ribs, the way it always warmed near covenant-work, and when I pulled it out, the pages had written themselves in that same urgent hand:

*Paid in full: fire, given against the Hollow, western flight, year uncounted. Balance remaining: none.*

"They kept the wards lit," Sable said quietly. She'd come up beside me, and she was reading over my arm, which should have made me flinch and didn't anymore. "Every ward-stone in Vharn, every border-ward, every little charm your guild sells to merchants for three silver — they all draw from dragon-fire. From *them*. The covenant isn't paper, Isandro. It's this. It's been this all along."

I looked at the ridge, and then up the mountain, following the line of it toward the peaks, and I understood what the ledger was telling me. One dragon. One ridge. How many ridges between here and the summit?

"How many were there?" I asked.

"The western flight?" Sable's jaw tightened. "My grandmother sang about them. Forty wings strong, she said, enough that their passing shadowed the valleys like weather."

"And now?"

Sable didn't answer. She didn't have to. We walked on, and we counted.

* * *

There were nine more before the crater.

Nine dragons in various states of the same ending. Some lay whole, like the first. Others had come apart — not torn, but *unraveled*, their bodies loosening into long ribbons of scale and sinew that the wind had arranged, gently, terribly, into shapes like calligraphy. One had crawled into a cave to die and the cave had collapsed around it, and we could see only the tip of a wing-bone jutting from the rockface like a white flag.

Wren stopped asking if they were sleeping after the fourth one. After that she just touched each one, two fingers, shoulder-pat, and whispered something I couldn't hear. I asked her once what she was saying.

"Telling them thank you," she said, like it was obvious. "They worked hard."

She was six, maybe seven. She couldn't remember her mother's face because the guild had burned the page it lived on. And she understood, without any of us explaining, that these creatures had died working for her. For all of us. For the guild-factors skimming interest in Vharn and the farmers who never knew why their fields stayed clean and the enforcers who burned debtor villages so the arithmetic would stay tidy.

I wrote it down that night, in the new ledger, the small one. *Debt incurred: nine lives, freely given, unpaid.* Under *debtor*, after a long moment, I wrote: *everyone.*

Brannoc watched me write it. He didn't say anything. But that night he started counting again, and I thought: good. Counting means you still believe there's something left to count.

* * *

The crater opened before us on the fifth day like a bowl the gods had set down and forgotten.

It was vast — a mile across, ringed with peaks, and the floor of it was smooth black glass where some ancient fire had melted the stone and let it cool. At its center stood a shrine: not built, exactly, but *arranged*, thousands of offerings laid out in concentric rings. Old swords. Farming tools. Children's toys worn smooth by handling. Letters, sealed and yellowed, stacked in careful towers. A wall of small clay tablets, each one scratched with marks I recognized with a lurch — tally-marks, the oldest script, the script of debt.

"People came here," Sable breathed. "Before the guild. Before any of it. They came to *thank* them."

"And to ask," Brannoc said.

He was staring at the far side of the crater, and I followed his gaze, and my heart forgot several beats in a row.

She was alive.

She was curled around the shrine itself like a hand around a candle-flame, and she was dying — there was no mistaking it, no pretending otherwise. Her scales, which must once have been the deep red-black of cooling lava, had gone dull and ash-colored, cracked in places, and through the cracks showed not flesh but a faint, guttering glow, like a banked fire seen through a broken grate. She was enormous — her head alone was the size of our cottage back in Vharn, if I'd ever had a cottage — and yet she seemed *smaller* than the dead ones on the ridges, diminished somehow, drawn inward, a flame conserving itself.

Her eye opened.

It was the size of a cartwheel and the color of molten gold, and it found us across a mile of black glass as easily as you'd find a fly on your own hand. I felt that gaze go through me, past me, into the ledger at my ribs, into the sigil-stamp, into the pages that had been writing themselves since Vharn, and I felt it *read* me.

"Well," said a voice like a landslide deciding to be gentle. "The guild finally sent someone honest."

* * *

We crossed the crater floor slowly. There was no other way to cross it. Every step felt witnessed.

Up close, Ysolde — though we didn't know her name yet — smelled of woodsmoke and hot iron and, beneath it, something older, something like the air before a storm that has been waiting two hundred years to break. Her breathing moved the loose scales along her flank with a sound like a ship's rigging in wind. Where her fire showed through the cracks, the light pulsed. Slowly. Irregularly.

Like a heart learning to stop.

"You know what I am," she said. Her eye tracked Brannoc first, and Brannoc — to his credit, or perhaps his ruin — did not look away. "An enforcer. Guild-made. I can smell the burning on you, little man. Villages. Ledgers." A plume of smoke curled from her nostrils, thin and gray. "Give me one reason not to finish what your masters started."

Brannoc went down on one knee. Not groveling — I'd seen men grovel before enforcers, and this wasn't it. It was the kneel of a soldier reporting to a superior he has failed.

"I burned Debtor's Row in Kelvingard," he said. "Eleven households. I told myself it was arithmetic. Then I was ordered to do it again at Marsh Hollow, and I couldn't tell myself anything anymore, so I ran." His voice didn't shake. That was the worst part. It was steady as a man reading charges. "I don't have a reason for you. I have a debt, and I'm walking toward paying it. If my death is the payment, take it. But these three are owed better than what the guild gave them, and I mean to see they get it."

The silence went on so long I could hear Wren's small hand find Sable's.

Then Ysolde laughed. It was a terrible sound, a rockslide with humor in it, and it shook loose dust from the shrine-rings.

"A debtor who knows he's a debtor," she rumbled. "Two hundred years, and the guild sends me a deserter with honest books. The old covenant must be truly desperate." Her eye swung to me. "You. Scribe. Open what you're carrying. I've been reading it since the treeline. It's been reading *me* longer."

I knelt and unwrapped the ledger. The pages turned themselves, or seemed to — the wind, maybe, or something else — and stopped at a page dense with that urgent self-written hand. I read it aloud because I couldn't not:

*Covenant of Fire and Name, sworn at the First Tollhouse, reaffirmed at the Crater-Shrine of the Western Flight. Guarantor: [name struck]. Seal: living flame. Term: while the flame endures.*

And beneath it, fresher, the ink still wet-looking:

*Flame failing. Guarantorship vacant. Successor required.*

Ysolde watched me read it with her great gold eye, and when I looked up, everything in me went cold and clear at once, the way it does when you finally see the shape of a thing you've been circling for days.

"How long?" I asked. "How long does the flame endure?"

The pulse behind her cracked scales flickered. Once. Twice. Slowed.

"Little scribe," she said, and her voice had gone quiet, the gentleness of it worse than the landslide, "when I close my eye, the last seal closes with it. The Hollow comes down these mountains like water finding a crack. Your city, your farms, your guild with all its fat arithmetic — gone inside a season." She drew a breath that stirred our hair and cost her visibly, the glow dimming with the effort. "I have held this since your grandmothers' grandmothers were girls. I am *tired*. And the covenant cannot pass to me again, or to another dragon — the terms forbid it. It needs a mortal guarantor. It has needed one for two hundred years."

"No one came," Sable said softly. It wasn't a question.

"No one came," Ysolde agreed. "The guild buried the terms. Easier to skim the interest than to pay the principal. Why do you think they burn the old records? Not to hide the debts, tinkerer. To hide the *vacancy*."

The wind moved through the crater, through the rings of offerings, and somewhere in the letter-towers a sealed envelope shifted and settled, and I thought: two hundred years of people climbing here to say thank you, to say please, and every one of them turned away or turned back, because the truth was a price no one would name aloud.

Wren stepped forward. Out of Sable's grip, out from behind Brannoc's leg, straight out onto the black glass toward a creature that could have ended her with one careless breath, and she held up both hands, cupped, the way children hold things they don't want dropped.

"You're the light," she said. "Aren't you? You're what's left of it."

Ysolde's eye widened — a movement like a drawbridge changing its mind. For a long moment the ancient dragon regarded the nameless child, and the guttering glow behind her scales steadied, just slightly, just briefly, the way a fire flares when you open the door to feed it.

"Oh," Ysolde said, very quietly, to herself or to the mountain or to two hundred years of silence. "*Oh.*"

And then her gaze lifted, past all of us, to the dark starless sky above the crater's rim, and when she spoke again her voice had hardened into something urgent, something with edges on it.

"We have less time than I said. Less than I hoped. Can you feel it, scribe? The pressure behind the wards is building — your guild-master's erasures have thinned the covenant to thread. When I gutter out, it will not be a season. It will be days." The gold eye fixed on me, and I felt the full weight of two centuries settle onto my shoulders like a yoke. "So I will ask you the question no one has been asked in two hundred years, and you will not answer tonight. You will answer when you understand it fully. But hear it now, and carry it:

"*Will you be bound?*"

Behind me, Sable drew a sharp breath. Brannoc's hand found my shoulder — not pushing, not pulling. Just there.

And in my satchel, unbidden, the ledger turned its own final page, and wrote, in ink that gleamed like embers:

*Awaiting signature.*
