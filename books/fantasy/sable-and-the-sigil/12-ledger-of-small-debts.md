## 12. Ledger of Small Debts

The crater was still warm.

Wren felt it through her boots first — a deep, patient heat rising out of the black glass ground, like standing on the back of something enormous and asleep. Which, she supposed, she was. Ysolde's crater-shrine curled around them in a ring of fused stone, and at its center the old dragon lay coiled beneath a skin of ash, her breathing slow as weather, each exhale stirring the cinders into little ghosts that rose and fell and rose again.

Wren had never been anywhere warm before that she could remember. The lowlands had burned, but that fire had been wrong — hungry, greedy, eating houses and mothers and names. This heat was different. This heat was like being held.

"You're staring," Sable said, without looking up from the tangle of copper wire in her lap.

"I'm allowed to stare," Wren said. "I've never seen a dragon."

"You've seen one for two days now."

"Two days isn't forever." Wren sat down cross-legged beside her on a shelf of smooth obsidian. "Forever is how long I'm going to look at her."

Sable snorted, but Wren caught the corner of her mouth doing the thing it did when it didn't want to be a smile and lost anyway. Sable's hands never stopped moving. Even resting, they worked — wire twisting into loops, loops into knots, knots into something that looked like nothing until suddenly it looked like everything. Ward-craft, she called it. The art of telling the dark where the edges of the world were.

"Here," Sable said finally, holding up the wire. "You wanted to learn. Lesson one."

Wren leaned in so fast she nearly fell off the shelf.

* * *

Lesson one, it turned out, was failure.

The wire was supposed to hold a shape. Sable bent hers into a small ring, whispered something under her breath — not words exactly, more like the memory of words — and the ring hummed faintly and glowed blue at the joints, a tiny fence against nothing. When Wren tried it, the wire stayed wire. She tried again. Wire. Again, harder, whispering louder, and the wire went cold in her fingers and dropped onto the glass like a dead thing.

"It hates me," Wren said.

"It doesn't hate you. It doesn't know you yet." Sable picked the wire up and pressed it back into Wren's hands, folding the girl's fingers closed around it. "That's all a ward is. Introducing yourself to the edge of something. You can't order the world to keep you safe. You ask it. Politely. Like you'd ask someone to share their fire."

"What if it says no?"

Sable was quiet for a moment. Out across the crater, Ysolde breathed, and the ash-ghosts rose and fell.

"Then you ask again tomorrow," Sable said. "And the day after. That's most of what craft is, Wren. Not talent. Just refusing to take no for an answer for long enough that the world gets embarrassed and gives in."

Wren looked down at the wire in her fist. It was warm now — or maybe her hand was. She tried once more, softer this time, not commanding but asking, the way she used to ask her mother for one more story before sleep, except she couldn't remember her mother's face, only the asking, only the warmth of the asking —

The wire hummed.

It was barely anything. A shiver of light along one bend of it, there and gone, like a fish surfacing in a dark pond. But Sable went very still, and then she grabbed Wren by both shoulders and said, "Again," in a voice that cracked in the middle, and Wren did it again, and the light came back and stayed three whole heartbeats before fading.

"That's not normal," Sable said. "That's — children don't do that. That takes years. That took me years."

"Maybe I'm not a child," Wren said. "Maybe I'm just short."

Sable laughed — a real laugh, startled out of her, loud enough that Brannoc looked over from his whittling with his eyebrows up, and Isandro lifted his head from his ledger with ink on his cheek. Wren decided right then that making Sable laugh was better than ward-craft. Better than almost anything. She would be collecting those laughs. She would keep them somewhere safe.

She didn't have anywhere safe to keep things. Not since the burning. But she was starting to think maybe people could be places. Maybe a person could be a somewhere.

* * *

Brannoc finished the toy at dusk.

Wren pretended not to watch him work, which meant she watched him constantly, peeking over the top of her knees whenever he glanced away. He sat apart from the rest of them, the way he always did, big shoulders hunched, a knife moving through a piece of pale mountain-wood in strokes so careful it seemed impossible they belonged to the same hands that had held a pass alone against thirty enforcers. His hands were wrecked — scarred, burn-mottled, two fingers on the left stiff and crooked — and they moved like they were apologizing to the wood.

He wouldn't let her see what he was making. Every time she drifted close, he turned his body, gruff as a locked door. So she learned his tells instead. The little grunt when a cut went right. The way his thumb tested every edge against itself, hunting splinters, because — she realized, watching — because it was for her, and things for children mustn't have splinters, and nobody had made anything for Wren in longer than she could remember, which wasn't very long, which was the whole problem.

When he finally held it out, he didn't look at her. He looked at the middle distance, jaw tight, like a man handing over a confession.

It was a dragon.

A small wooden dragon, no bigger than her hand, wings folded, head raised, carved with a patience that must have cost him hours. Its scales were suggested rather than shown — rows of tiny crescents — and its eyes were two dots of char rubbed in from the fire, so that it looked like it had seen something vast and survived it.

"It's Ysolde," Brannoc said. "Sort of. If Ysolde were — smaller. And less likely to incinerate you."

Wren took it with both hands, the way you take something holy.

"She's beautiful," Wren said.

"She's lopsided. Left wing's thicker than the right."

"She's perfect."

"She's — " Brannoc stopped. Cleared his throat. Wren watched, fascinated, as the biggest man she had ever seen fought visibly with his own face and lost. "Well. Good. Fine. It's nothing. It passes the time."

Wren threw her arms around his neck.

He froze solid, the whole mountain of him, arms hovering a foot away from her back like he'd forgotten what arms were for. For one terrible heartbeat Wren thought she'd done something wrong, broken some rule she didn't know, and she started to pull back — and then his hands came up, gentle as falling snow, and settled between her shoulder blades, and Brannoc held on like she was the last solid thing in a sliding world.

"Thank you," Wren said into his collarbone.

"Don't mention it," he rumbled. "Seriously. Don't. Ever. To anyone."

"Too late. I'm going to tell everyone."

"That's worse."

But he didn't let go for a while, and neither did she, and above them the starless sky wheeled, black and empty, and Wren found she didn't mind it so much tonight. The sky could be empty. Her arms were full.

* * *

Later, when the others slept, she found Isandro writing.

He sat near Ysolde's flank, where the dragon's residual warmth kept the cold off, with a fresh book open on his knees — not a guild ledger, nothing official, just a cheap bound thing Sable had bought at the Market of Impossible Things, its cover stamped with a symbol that might have been a moon or might have been an eye. His pen moved steadily. The ember-light from the shrine-pit made a small gold island around him.

"You're supposed to be sleeping," Wren said.

"So are you."

"I'm a dragon-tamer now. Dragon-tamers keep different hours."

"Do they." He shifted over without being asked, making room, and she wedged herself against his side and peered at the page. "What are you writing?"

Isandro hesitated. Then, instead of covering the book the way adults usually did, he tilted it toward her.

Entries. Dozens of them, in his quick scribe's hand, each one dated, each one small:

*Sable taught Wren the first principle of warding. Cost: one hour, one length of copper wire. Owed: everything that grows from it.*

*Brannoc carved a dragon for Wren. Cost: four hours, one knife-blade worn dull. Owed: a childhood, in installments.*

*Wren made Sable laugh. Cost: unknown. Owed: unknown. Interest compounding.*

"Wren made Sable laugh," Wren read aloud. "That's me."

"That's you. First entry in the book, actually. Before we even reached the mountains." He tapped the earliest page. "I started this the night after the tollhouse. After Brannoc. I kept thinking about how the guild counts things. Everything owed, nothing given. Every debt a chain." He turned the page. Fresh lines marched down it. "So I thought — what if someone counted the other direction? What if kindness got written down too? Not to collect on it. Just so it existed. Just so it counted."

Wren read the entries slowly, mouthing the words. *Ysolde shielded us from the wind. Cost: breath she cannot spare. Owed: remembrance.* *Isandro carried Wren when she slept walking. Cost: a sore shoulder. Owed: a song, someday, when she learns one.*

"There's a page about me carrying you?" she said.

"You talk in your sleep. You were singing something. You didn't know any songs, so you made one up. It was terrible." His voice was dry, but his eyes weren't. "I owe you a song now. Per the ledger."

"What does the ledger say happens if nobody pays?"

Isandro was quiet. Down in the pit, Ysolde exhaled, and the ash-ghosts rose, hung, fell.

"In the guild," he said, "unpaid debts become chains. That's all a chain is, Wren. A debt somebody refused to forgive, or refused to pay, until it hardened." He touched the cheap cover of the little book. "This is different. These debts aren't chains. They're — threads. You pay one, and it doesn't end the thread. It ties it tighter. To more people. More kindnesses. Until—" He stopped, as if he'd run further ahead in the sentence than he meant to.

"Until what?"

"Until cutting any one of them would unravel the whole thing." He smiled, small and crooked. "Until nobody could ever be taken out of it without everyone feeling the tear."

Wren thought about that. She thought about Sable's cracked-open laugh, and Brannoc's snow-gentle hands, and Isandro's shoulder that was sore because of her, and Ysolde's breath spent on keeping the wind off children who weren't hers. None of these people were her family. She couldn't remember her family; the page that held them had burned with everything else.

But her chest did something complicated and warm, looking at the little book, and she decided that whatever family was, it probably felt like this. Probably felt exactly like this.

"Read me another one," she said, and burrowed in, and Isandro — the last honest pen, the man whose name was written in ledgers across a dying realm — read her the accounts of their own small goodness until her eyes closed.

* * *

She woke once, deep in the night, to voices.

For one flaring instant she was back in the burning village, and the voices were fire-scribes, and she bolted upright with the wooden dragon clutched to her chest — but it was only Sable and Isandro, sitting close together at the crater's rim, talking low. Brannoc stood watch a little way off, a silhouette against the lesser darkness of the sky.

"—can't put it off much longer," Sable was saying. "Her flame's guttering. You saw the shrine-pit tonight. Two days ago it burned high enough to read by. Tonight it's an ember. Whatever you're going to decide, decide it soon."

"I know."

"And I know what it costs. I've read the same pages you have. A guarantor pays with his name, Isandro. With his place in every record, every memory. Everything he was." A pause. "Everything he *is*."

"I know what it costs."

"Do you? Because you sit here writing a ledger of belonging, and I don't think you've noticed what you've built." Sable's voice roughened. "You wrote yourself into it. Page after page. Carried her, taught her, bled for Brannoc, argued for Ysolde. You tied the threads, you stubborn, blind, wonderful fool. There isn't a version of losing you that doesn't tear all of us."

Silence. The ash-ghosts rose and fell.

"Then I'll have to make sure the tearing holds," Isandro said quietly.

Wren lay back down very slowly, the wooden dragon against her heart, and stared at the starless dark, and did not sleep again for a long time.

Because she had heard something underneath the words. Something Sable hadn't said and Isandro hadn't answered. And beneath it, farther off, threading through the mountain silence like wire through flesh — a sound she knew. Knew from the worst night of her life.

Boots. Many boots. Climbing.

She sat up. Far below the crater's rim, down in the black throat of the pass, torchlight flickered — dozens of flames, guttering blue at the edges, the way every flame burned when the Hollow was near.

"Isandro," Wren whispered. "They found us."
