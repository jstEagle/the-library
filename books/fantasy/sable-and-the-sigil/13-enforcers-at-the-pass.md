## 13. Enforcers at the Pass

Brannoc had spent eleven years learning to read the language of pursuit, and everything he knew told him they had half a day, maybe less.

He stood at the mouth of the pass while the others broke camp behind him — Sable kicking dirt over their fire with the brisk efficiency of a woman who'd done it a hundred times, Wren clutching her bundle of ward-craft scraps, Isandro hunched over the small ledger like it might whisper warnings if he held it right. Above them all, Ysolde circled once on wings that caught no wind, then settled onto a ledge to wait. Even diminished as she was, she moved like something that remembered being enormous.

The pass itself was a throat of gray stone, barely wide enough for two carts, climbing between walls of shattered granite toward the high country where the dragon-shrines waited. Snow in the shadows. Wind funneled through it with a low moan, like the mountain clearing its throat.

"They're coming up the valley road," Brannoc said. "Fourteen riders. Maybe sixteen."

Sable's hands went still over the dead fire. "You can tell that from here?"

"I can tell it from the birds." He nodded at the treeline below, where crows had lifted off in a ragged sheet an hour ago and hadn't come back. Enforcers rode quiet, but nothing made the land go silent like guild steel moving through it. "They'll reach the pass mouth by midday."

"Then we go now." Isandro was already packing the ledger away, movements quick but not panicked. He'd learned that much, at least. "There has to be another way around—"

"There isn't. Not for Ysolde." Brannoc looked up at the ledge where the old dragon lay with her chin on her forepaws, watching him with eyes like banked coals. "She can't fly the high passes anymore. Her fire's nearly spent. The only way through is this one, and it's narrow enough that fourteen men can't ride abreast."

Understanding came to Isandro slowly, the way understanding always did with him — you could watch him arrive at a thing, watch him hate arriving at it.

"You want to stay."

"I want to hold the pass." Brannoc rolled his shoulders, felt the old scar tissue pull across his back where an enforcer's whip had laid him open for asking why, once, just once, before they burned a debtor village. "It's what I'm for, scribe. I was made for it. Eleven years of guild training, and the only part I ever kept was knowing how guild soldiers fight."

"We're not leaving you," Sable said flatly.

"Yes, you are." He said it gently, which surprised him. Gentleness had never come naturally; the guild had beaten it out of him early, along with most other soft things. But somewhere between the burned villages and the tollhouse and the long gray road through Hollow-light country, gentleness had started growing back, like grass through ash. "Look at them, Sable. Fourteen enforcers, fresh-mounted, well-fed, carrying guild-forged blades. Look at us. A tinkerer, a child, a scribe, and a deserter with a bad knee. If we run together, we all die together on this mountain. If I stand here, some of us live."

Wren tugged his sleeve. She didn't say anything — she rarely did when it mattered — she just looked up at him with those too-old eyes and held out her hand. In her palm sat a ward-token, one of Sable's, a little knot of copper wire and river-stone that hummed faintly when you held it.

"It's not a weapon," Sable said quietly. "It's a marker. It'll show anyone carrying its twin where you fell."

Brannoc took it. It was warm from the girl's hand. "Then let's hope nobody needs to use its twin."

Isandro hadn't spoken. He stood there with his jaw tight, doing arithmetic Brannoc could almost see — the same arithmetic the guild taught every debtor: what is owed, what can be paid, what must be surrendered. Finally the scribe pulled the small ledger from his coat.

"Give me your hand," Isandro said.

"Why?"

"Because I'm going to write something down, and I need it to be true." Isandro opened the ledger to a page near the middle — Brannoc glimpsed his own name there, in the scribe's careful hand, surrounded by entries he couldn't read at this distance but recognized anyway. Debts. Kindnesses. Small things. *Carried Wren when her feet blistered. Shared his cloak. Taught her to whistle through a blade of grass.* Things so small they shouldn't have been worth ink. "I'm writing that you owe me nothing. That no one owes you anything. That whatever happens on this mountain today, you walk out of it clean."

"That's not how debts work," Brannoc said. "Guild or otherwise."

"I know." Isandro's pen scratched. "That's rather the point."

* * *

They climbed.

Brannoc watched them go from the floor of the pass — Sable first, hauling Wren up the switchbacks with the grim competence of someone who'd been escaping things her whole life; then Isandro, pausing twice to look back, the second time long enough that Brannoc raised a hand and made himself smile. Above them, Ysolde launched herself with a sound like a door groaning open after a hundred years, and her shadow crossed the snow, and then it was just him and the wind and the waiting.

He chose his ground with a soldier's care. A pinch-point where the pass narrowed to six feet, walled on the left by stone too smooth to climb and on the right by a drop into a ravine choked with old avalanche rock. Behind him, forty yards of switchback, each turn a place to make them pay again. He had no shield. His sword was guild-issue, taken the night he deserted, and it had killed enough men that it had opinions about it — or so he'd thought, once, before he learned that swords were just steel and men were just men.

He set Wren's token in a crack in the wall where he could see it. Copper wire and river-stone, humming its small warm hum.

*For luck,* she'd said, or hadn't said. With Wren it was hard to tell where words ended and meaning began.

They came at midday.

He heard them before he saw them — the particular rhythm of guild cavalry, hooves timed to a drumbeat cadence drilled into every recruit until it lived in the bones. Then the vanguard rounded the last bend below, and there they were: black-lacquered armor, visors down, the sigil of the Ledger Guild stamped on every breastplate. His own old sigil. He'd worn it for eleven years and it had never once fit right.

At their head rode a man Brannoc knew. Captain Vess. They'd shared watches, rations, a tent through two winters. Vess had taught him the drumbeat cadence. Vess had held him back from burning a debtor's barn once, saying *not this one, the order's wrong*, and Brannoc had believed, for a while, that there were lines even enforcers wouldn't cross.

Then Vess had crossed them, one by one, and stopped noticing.

"Deserter." Vess's voice carried up the pass, flattened by the visor into something without a face. "Master Corvane offers terms. Surrender the scribe and the ledger, and your death-debt will be commuted to service. You'll die old, in a guild uniform, with a pension."

"The last time I wore a uniform," Brannoc called back, "you ordered me to burn a village full of people whose only crime was owing money."

"Their debt was law."

"Their *names* were law, Vess. Their names, and their years, and their memories. And we took them because a page said we could." He shifted his grip on the sword. "I've read the pages since. You should try it sometime. There's arithmetic in them that doesn't balance."

Vess raised a gauntleted hand, and the column halted, and Brannoc felt the moment tip over — that suspended breath before violence, the one he'd stood in a hundred times, always on the other side.

"For what it's worth," Vess said, and there was something under the visor-flat voice, something almost human, almost sorry, "I'm glad it's you holding the pass. Anyone else would have broken already."

"For what it's worth," Brannoc answered, "so am I."

Then the arrows came.

* * *

He fought the way the guild had trained him, which was to say he fought without art and without mercy and without wasting anything. The pass was too narrow for more than three abreast, and he made those three pay for every yard. First man went down with a boot-slip on scree and Brannoc's pommel across the visor. Second took a thrust through the gap in his gorget that Brannoc had known about since he was nineteen years old, because he'd helped design the armor's replacement and the guild had never recalled the old pattern. Third, fourth — they learned fast, guild soldiers always did, and started coming with shields locked and blades low, and the fight became a grinding, ugly, honest thing.

An arrow had found his left shoulder in the first volley. He'd snapped the shaft and forgotten it. Now the arm was going numb, and the sword grew heavier with every exchange, and the switchbacks behind him bought time in minutes and he needed hours.

*Hours,* he thought, parrying, giving ground, taking a gash across the thigh that turned his leg to fire. *Buy them hours.*

He gave ground. Took the first switchback. Made them climb it over their own wounded. Took the second. Somewhere in the third hour — or the third minute; time had gone strange, the way it did when the body started spending itself faster than the mind could count — Vess himself came up the line, and they fought the way old friends fight, which is to say they fought like men who knew exactly where the other one hurt.

"You could still stop," Vess grunted, blades locked. "Corvane doesn't want you dead, Bren. He wants you *bought*. Says a man who deserted for conscience is worth ten who never had any."

"He's right," Brannoc said, and headbutted him.

Vess went down the switchback in a clatter of lacquered plate, and Brannoc laughed — actually laughed, blood in his teeth, Wren's token humming in its crack beside him like a small warm heart — and then the spear took him from the side.

He hadn't seen the climber. Of course there was a climber; there was always a climber; he'd have posted one himself. The point went in under his ribs, angled up, the way you kill a man so he stays killed, and the world tilted sideways and the sky swung down and the stone came up hard against his cheek.

He lay there. The fighting sounds receded, oddly polite about it. Someone was shouting orders — *leave him, the scribe's the prize* — and boots went past him, up the pass, and he thought: *good*, and thought: *hours*, and thought: *Wren, I'm sorry about the whistling lessons, you were getting good at it.*

His hand crawled toward the token. Couldn't find it. Found cold stone instead.

Above him, faintly, he heard Ysolde roar — a thread of a roar, a guttering lamp of a roar, but a roar — and heard men scream, and understood dimly that the old dragon had spent something precious to slow them, and hated her for it, and loved her, and the thoughts tangled and went dark.

* * *

Darkness, but not empty darkness. The kind with weight. The kind that pressed on the chest like a hand.

In the darkness, Brannoc felt the thing the guild had written into him at induction, the thing every enforcer carried and most never noticed: the death-debt. A clause, signed in his own blood at seventeen, that said his life belonged to the guild and would be collected whenever collection was convenient. He'd felt it all his life as a kind of background hum, a leash he'd stopped feeling because he'd never once reached its end.

Now he'd reached its end. He could feel the clause activating, feel the great cold machinery of the covenant turning over somewhere beneath the world, ready to write *collected* next to his name.

And then—

Then he felt something else. Something impossible. Something that felt like a pen scratching.

Far above — impossibly far above, at the top of the pass, at the edge of hearing and then past it — Isandro stood on a ledge with the small ledger open against the wind, and Brannoc knew this the way you know things in dreams, without seeing. Knew the scribe's pen was moving. Knew what it was writing, because Isandro had shown him the page that morning: *owes nothing, owes nothing, owes nothing* — line after line, kindness after small kindness, struck through and paid and struck through and paid, until the ledger arrived at the last entry, the oldest one, the one written in blood by the guild when he was seventeen.

And Isandro struck it out.

Not crossed out. *Struck out* — the way you strike a debt, the way you forgive one, the way the new ledger worked, the way it had worked on Wren's nameless grief and Sable's chained favors and every small freely-given thing they'd recorded since the crater. One line of ink, drawn through a clause written in blood, and the covenant — the real one, the old one, the one underneath all the guild's paper — paused, considered, and found the account balanced.

The hand on Brannoc's chest lifted.

Warmth came back into him like water into a dry channel. Not much. Enough. He felt the spear-wound pull closed — not healed, nothing so generous, but *closed*, knit rough and angry, the way a debt forgiven leaves a scar instead of a corpse. His lungs filled. His fingers found the copper wire and river-stone in its crack, humming, humming.

Boots above him. Sable, sliding down the switchback with Wren's twin token outstretched and her face white as the snow, and behind her Isandro, ledger clutched to his chest, looking down at Brannoc with the expression of a man who has just discovered what his pen can do and is terrified of it.

"Don't," Brannoc rasped, as Sable hauled his arm over her shoulders. "Don't make it a habit. Debts—"

"Are freely given and freely owed," Sable snarled, dragging him upright. "Shut up and walk, deserter."

He walked. Half-carried, half-dragged, up switchbacks slick with his own blood, past enforcers scattered and groaning where Ysolde's borrowed fire had thrown them — and past Vess, sitting against the wall with his helmet off and a look on his face that Brannoc would spend a long time not understanding. Vess didn't reach for his sword. He just watched them go, and at the last turn, quietly, he said:

"The clause didn't work. I felt it fail. We all felt it fail." A pause. "What did he do?"

Brannoc looked back at his old captain, at the man who'd taught him the drumbeat and crossed the lines one by one, and found he didn't hate him anymore, which was somehow worse.

"He forgave me," Brannoc said. "You should try it sometime. There's arithmetic in it that balances."

Then the mountain folded around them, and the pass was behind them, and ahead — deeper in, where the air thinned and the starless dark pressed close — Isandro stopped walking. Stopped, and went pale, and opened the ledger with shaking hands.

Because a new page had begun writing itself.

And the handwriting was his own.
