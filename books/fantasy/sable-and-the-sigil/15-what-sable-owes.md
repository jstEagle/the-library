## 15. What Sable Owes

The creditor came at dawn, the way creditors always do — when you're too tired to fight and too honest to run.

Sable felt it before she saw it: a tightening in her chest like a wire drawing taut, the old guild-mark on her wrist warming under her sleeve. She'd been awake most of the night anyway, re-lacing Ysolde's ward-harness with copper thread while the dragon slept her guttering sleep, each breath a little thinner than the last. The mountain air up here tasted of snow and old fire, and somewhere below them, past the tree line, the world was still coming apart at its seams.

She stepped out of the crater-shrine into grey light and found him waiting.

He stood at the edge of the shrine's broken ring of stones, where the wards Sable had spent three days weaving met the raw starless dark. A tall man in a coat the color of wet slate, with a ledger chained to his belt and a face so unremarkable that her eyes kept sliding off it. That was how she knew what he was. Guild factors trained themselves out of being remembered. It made collections easier.

"Sable Vess," he said. "Ward-tinkerer. Debtor, third class. Two favors outstanding."

"I know what I owe."

"Then you know why I've come." He smiled without warmth, the way a door opens without welcome. "The guild is calling in all paper, mistress Vess. Every debt, every favor, every promise written in our books. Master Corvane's orders." He consulted his ledger with a finger gloved in grey kid leather. "Favor one: your service. You will surrender yourself to guild custody and labor as directed until such time as—"

"No."

"—as directed," he continued, as if she hadn't spoken, "or until your account is otherwise settled. Favor two: your craft. All ward-work you produce henceforth belongs to the Ledger Guild of Vharn, in perpetuity, including any work performed for persons currently in violation of guild law." His eyes lifted, and for just a moment they were not forgettable at all. They were very old, and very patient, and full of arithmetic. "Including, I should say, the dragon."

* * *

They'd come down from the high pass two nights ago, after Brannoc's stand at the pass — after Isandro had done whatever impossible thing he'd done with the ledger that closed a wound that should have killed a man. Ysolde could no longer fly. Her fire was spent shielding the Master-Ledger's ember, and what remained of her was a vast, dull-scaled ruin of a dragon who moved like a ship taking water, who had insisted on descending because, she said, *a seal must be present when the covenant is signed, or it is only ink.*

So they'd made camp in the ruins of an old waystation below the snow line, half-collapsed, its hearth-stones still warm with something older than heat. Wren slept curled against Ysolde's foreleg. Brannoc sat propped against the wall, whittling, his wound bound but his color wrong. And Isandro —

Isandro was going to lose his mind.

She heard him coming before she saw him, boots scraping on scree, and by the time she turned he was already talking.

"What did he offer you?" His voice was low and shaking. "What did he *say*?"

"He said what he was always going to say. My debts are due."

"And?" She watched him do the math the way he did everything — reading the page, parsing the covenant no one else could see. She watched him arrive at the answer. "You're going to pay it. You're going to give yourself to them."

"The first favor buys the others time. If I go with him quietly, he leaves the rest of you alone today. Maybe longer."

"That's not — Sable, that's not how they work and you know it. You go into that guildhouse and you don't come out. I've seen their debtor rolls. I *wrote* some of them. People go in and the pages just... stop. No death recorded. No release. Just blank space where a life used to be."

"Then it's good I'm not people," she said. "I'm a ward-tinkerer. I'm useful. Useful gets you kept alive."

"Useful gets you *used*." He grabbed her wrist, pushed back her sleeve, and stared at the guild-mark burned into her skin — three interlocked rings, the sigil of perpetual obligation. "You told me once you owed them three favors you could never repay. You never told me what they were *for*."

"They were for my mother's funeral," Sable said quietly. "And for bread, the winter after. And for not asking questions when I started selling ward-work on the side." She pulled her arm free, gently. "That's what debt looks like when you're poor, Isandro. It doesn't look like a contract. It looks like staying alive."

Behind them, from the shadow of the waystation, the factor cleared his throat.

"If the lady has finished her farewells."

"She hasn't," Isandro said, and turned on him with something terrible in his face — the scribe's fury, cold and total. "I hold the ledger. I hold the *sigil*. Whatever paper you think binds her, I can strike it."

"You can amend entries," the factor said pleasantly. "You cannot erase obligations. Only payment does that. Or default." His smile returned. "And we both know what default costs now, with the wards down. Ask the child what happened to her village."

Isandro went white. Sable stepped between them before he did something magnificent and fatal.

"Here's what happens," she said to the factor. "You want my service. Fine. But there's a dragon in this ruin who needs safe passage down-mountain, through country crawling with your enforcers and worse things besides. Passage requires ward-craft. Ward-craft is my second favor — my craft, yours, in perpetuity." She held out her marked wrist. "So spend it. Spend favor two right now, factor. Order me to ward her passage. Because if you take me to Vharn and let her die on this mountain, the guild loses its living seal, and Master Corvane's beautiful blank slate stays blank forever. I don't think he wants that. Do you?"

The factor looked at her for a long moment. Then he opened his ledger, licked his thumb, and turned a page.

"Clever," he said. "Corvane hates clever debtors." He wrote. "Favor two: expended. The debtor will render ward-craft sufficient to secure the dragon's passage to the lowlands. Upon completion, the debtor surrenders herself at the tollhouse at Grayford within three days." He snapped the book shut. "One favor remains, mistress Vess. Spend it wisely. Or don't. We'll be collecting either way."

He walked into the starless dark between the stones and was gone, and Sable found she couldn't remember his face at all.

* * *

Isandro didn't speak to her for an hour. When he finally did, it was over the ward-lines she was already laying — copper thread and chalk and small knots of woven intent, anchored to Ysolde's fading warmth, a moving cloak of concealment that would read as empty road to anything hunting them.

"You bought her safety with yourself," he said.

"I bought *all of us* time. There's a difference."

"There isn't and you know it." He crouched beside her, took the spool from her hands, forced her to look at him. His eyes were red-rimmed and furious and frightened, and she realized with a jolt that the fear wasn't for himself. "Three days, Sable. You walk into Grayford alone and hand yourself to the people who burned the lowlands. Who burned *records*. Who erased a whole village down to a child's memory of her mother's face. And you did it without asking any of us. Without asking *me*."

"Would you have said yes?"

"No!"

"Exactly." She took the spool back. "Family means paying each other's debts without being asked. That's the whole point. If I have to ask permission, it isn't a gift, it's a negotiation. And I'm done negotiating with a world that keeps the books."

"That's not what family means. Family means you *let people help you*. It means Brannoc holds a pass for you and I write myself into a covenant and Wren—" his voice cracked, "—Wren carries an ember she shouldn't have to carry. We're all paying each other's debts, Sable. That's the covenant I'm trying to build. Not this. Not one person quietly bleeding out on the altar while everyone else sleeps."

For a moment she almost wavered. Almost.

But then she looked past him, into the waystation, where Wren was showing Brannoc how to fold a paper bird with clumsy, careful hands, and Ysolde's dim eye watched them both with something like tenderness, and Sable thought: *this. This is what I'm buying.*

"You want to know why I really did it?" she said. "When I was nine, my mother sold a year of her life to the guild to keep me fed. One year. She died eleven months later, and I swore I'd never be on either end of that trade again. Never the debtor. Never the payer." She tied off a knot; the ward-line hummed faintly to life. "And then I met four people I'd burn the world down for, and I understood my mother completely. It's not sacrifice, Isandro. It's just love, with a price tag attached. The trick is making sure the price lands on you and not on them."

"That's the stupidest definition of love I've ever heard."

"It's the only one that's ever worked for me."

He stared at her. Then he laughed — a short, broken sound, half fury, half grief — and sat down heavily in the frost-dead grass beside her.

"Three days," he said. "Then I'm coming to Grayford."

"Don't you dare."

"I'm a scribe, Sable. I don't leave debts unpaid. And you—" he bumped his shoulder against hers, "—you're the biggest entry in my book."

She wanted to tell him not to. She wanted to tell him that some ledgers should stay closed. Instead she leaned her head against his shoulder and let the silence hold them both, and above them the starless sky pressed down like an unpaid bill, and behind them a dying dragon breathed slow smoke, and a nameless child laughed at a crooked paper bird.

* * *

They moved at dusk.

Ysolde walked — there was no other word for it — a mile at a time, resting an hour, her vast body wrapped in Sable's concealment-wards until the mountains themselves seemed to forget she was there. Wren rode in the crook of her foreleg, tucked against scales gone dull as old pewter, chattering about nothing. Brannoc limped at the rear with an axe across his shoulders, watching the dark.

At the crossroads below the tree line, where the pilgrim road bent toward Grayford, Sable stopped.

This was where she left them. The factor would be watching. Three days meant three days.

Ysolde lowered her great head until her breath stirred Sable's hair. Up close, the dragon smelled of hot iron and old libraries.

"Little maker of wards," Ysolde rumbled. "You have spent yourself twice over for creatures you've known less than a season. Why?"

Sable shrugged. "Someone has to keep the lights on."

"Hm." One ancient eye, banked coals. "Two hundred years I kept the covenant's flame, and never once did a mortal pay a debt *unasked*. They paid when cornered. When bargained down. When it profited them." The eye narrowed. "You are either the bravest woman I have met or the greatest fool, and I have known both, and they look identical from inside a crater."

"Which am I?"

"Yes," said Ysolde, and touched her muzzle briefly to Sable's brow, and where the scales brushed skin Sable felt something settle — warm, heavy, unmistakably real. "A ward. The last I have strength to make. It will turn one blow. Choose carefully whose."

Then the dragon straightened, and the company moved on down the darkening road toward the lowlands, and Sable stood alone at the crossroads with one favor left in her wrist and three days to reach a tollhouse she had no intention of entering alive.

Behind her, far up the mountain, the ember in Wren's cupped hands glowed like a fallen star refusing to go out.

Ahead, down the valley, torchlight moved in formation. Enforcers. A dozen at least, marching not toward the dragon's route but toward the tollhouse at Grayford — toward the place where Sable was supposed to surrender.

Unless they weren't going there for her at all.

She squinted against the wind, counting torches, reading formations the way she read ward-lines — and her stomach dropped. The column wasn't heading for the tollhouse. It was fanning out across the valley floor. Sweeping. Hunting.

They knew the dragon was coming down.

They'd known all along.
