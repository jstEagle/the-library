## 16. The Guildhouse of Vharn

The guildhouse had sealed itself the night the stars went out, and it had not opened since.

We saw it from the ridge above Vharn at dusk — or what passed for dusk now, a bruised gray dimness where the sun should have been. The building crouched in the heart of the city like a fist closed around something precious. Its windows were dark. Its great bronze doors, which I had walked through every morning of my indentured life, were fused shut with wardwork so dense it hummed. Even from this distance I could feel it against my teeth, that low grinding note of magic being spent like coin by the fistful.

"They're burning through their reserves," Sable said beside me. She had her spyglass out, one eye shut, her face grim in the half-light. "Look at the roofline. See how the wards ripple? That's not defense. That's consumption. Corvane is feeding everything he has into the erasure."

"Then we go in tonight," Brannoc said.

Wren made a small sound and pressed closer to my side. She'd been quiet for two days — since the pass, since Brannoc's blood on the stones, since Isandro struck a death-debt off a ledger with a stolen stamp and watched fate flinch. Children are not comforted by explanations. They are comforted by proximity, and she had appointed herself to mine.

I knelt to her level. "You could stay with Ysolde."

Ysolde, cat-sized and dull-scaled and draped across my pack like a scolding, cracked one eye open. *The child stays with me,* she said, in that voice like a bell rung underwater. *And you stay alive long enough to be worth the trouble.*

"I need her," I said. It surprised me to hear it said aloud. "If the Master-Ledger is what I think it is, I'll need hands that aren't mine. And if things go wrong—" I looked at Wren. "You're nameless. The ledger can't find purchase on you. You can walk through fire that would take the rest of us."

"That's horrible," Sable said quietly.

"It's true," Wren said. Her voice was small but steady. "I'm nobody. Nobody's pages burned." She took my hand. "I'm coming."

* * *

We went down through the aqueducts.

Sable knew them — of course she did; she'd spent three favors' worth of years crawling under the city repairing ward-nodes no one thanked her for — and she led us through channels that stank of old water and older neglect, our boots splashing in inches of runoff, Ysolde riding my shoulders with her claws pricked lightly into my collarbone like a warning.

The guildhouse's foundations rose out of the dark ahead of us: black stone, seamless, older than the guild itself. The guild hadn't built this place. It had moved into something already here, the way a parasite moves into a body. I had worked eleven years beneath these foundations and never once wondered what they rested on.

Sable stopped at a wall that looked like every other wall and pressed her palm flat against it. "Node junction," she murmured. "They never re-warded the maintenance ways after the sealing. Why would they? No one knows they exist except tinkerers and rats." A pause. "And scribes who read covenants."

She drew a thin stylus from her kit and began to work, scratching lines into the stone that glowed faintly gold and then faded. Brannoc stood watch behind us, hammer loose in his grip, his back a wall of its own. The wound he'd taken at the pass had closed to an angry pink seam — my amendment holding, though he favored his left arm still, and I caught him flexing the hand as if checking that it belonged to him.

"There," Sable breathed. A seam of light split the wall, widening into a gap just wide enough for a body. Warm air rolled out, smelling of ink and smoke and something else — something sweetish and wrong, like flowers left too long in a closed room.

Burning names, I thought. That's what that smell is.

We went in single file: Sable first, then Wren, then me, Brannoc last. The wall sealed behind us with a sound like a held breath released.

* * *

Inside, the guildhouse was dying.

I had expected guards, traps, corridors full of enforcers. Instead we found silence and ruin. The great reading hall where I had spent ten thousand hours hunched over debtor-pages was dark, its lamps cold, its desks shoved aside as if in evacuation or panic. Papers lay scattered like snowdrifts. Somewhere far above, machinery ground — the sound of presses running without rest.

And everywhere, faintly, screaming.

Not people. Pages.

It came from the archive levels below, a sound I felt more than heard, a thin continuous keening that set my molars aching. I had heard it once before, years ago, when a junior scribe misfiled a covenant-page near a heat-lamp and the ink began to blister. A name in pain sounds like nothing else in the world. Now there were thousands of names in pain, tens of thousands, and the whole building resonated with them like a struck bell that would not stop ringing.

"The shelves," Sable whispered. She had gone pale. "Isandro, the shelves are—"

"I hear them."

We descended. There was no choice in it; the keening pulled us down like grief pulls mourners toward a grave. The stairwells were lit by emergency-glyphs guttering blue — the same blue the flames had burned the night the stars went out — and with each landing the smell of that sweet wrongness thickened until Wren pressed her sleeve over her nose and Ysolde dug her claws in hard enough to draw blood through my shirt.

The archive doors stood open.

Beyond them stretched the vaulted caverns where the guild kept its ledgers — every debt ever brokered, every name ever taken as payment, every year sold and memory pawned and favor owed across three generations of brokers grown fat on the interest. Row upon row of shelving climbed into darkness overhead, and along those shelves, fires burned.

Not ordinary fire. Guild-fire, white and hungry, crawling along the spines of the ledgers without consuming the shelves themselves — only the names. As we watched, a page curled and blackened, and the keening spiked, and somewhere in the world a person forgot something important. A mother's face. A childhood home. Their own middle name. Each burning page was a small amputation performed on reality, and the screams were the sound of the cutting.

"They're not destroying the records," I said. My voice came out flat and strange. "They're harvesting them. Look — the fire doesn't spread. It eats selectively. Names, signatures, bonds. Everything else is left intact." I understood then, with a lurch, what the presses upstairs were doing. "Corvane isn't erasing the debts. He's transferring them. Every name that burns here is being rewritten onto blank pages somewhere above us — onto *his* pages, under *his* seal. He's not abolishing ownership. He's consolidating it."

"Then we stop the presses," Brannoc said, and started forward.

"No." I caught his arm. "First the Master-Ledger. If we destroy the presses and the Master-Ledger survives, Corvane rebuilds by morning. The Master-Ledger is the root. Everything else is branches."

"And where is the root?"

I closed my eyes and let myself remember the floor plan I had memorized over eleven years of servitude, the way a prisoner memorizes bars. "Below the archive. The Undercroft. Where they keep the oldest covenants — the ones even factors aren't cleared to read." I opened my eyes. "Where they keep *me*, in a manner of speaking."

* * *

We went down through the burning shelves, and it was worse than any battle.

Because the names screamed, and some of them I knew.

There — third shelf, second row — the ledger containing the debts of Millbrook, the village where I'd been apprenticed out at nine years old because my mother's medical debt exceeded her life expectancy. The pages were burning. I watched the entry that recorded my own mother's final payment — three years of memory, traded for a bed in the charity ward — curl at its edges, and I heard her name begin to scream.

I couldn't stop. There was no time. That was the arithmetic of it, Corvane's arithmetic, and I hated that it worked on me: every second we lingered, more names died; every second we delayed reaching the root, the harvest continued. So I ran past my mother's burning name and did not look back, and I have never forgiven myself for the speed of my feet.

Wren, though — Wren stopped.

She planted herself in front of a shelf where a single small ledger sat untouched amid the flames, its cover scorched but its contents somehow spared, and she reached into the fire — into fire that should have taken her hand to the bone — and pulled it out. The flames parted around her like water around a stone. Nameless, the fire had nothing of hers to burn.

She carried it out cradled against her chest. "It was crying the loudest," she said simply.

Later I would learn it contained the covenant-records of her village. Later still, it would give her back her mother's face. But in that moment she only held it, and I only looked at her — this child I had acquired without deciding to, standing unburned in a holocaust of names — and understood that whatever Corvane had built, whatever machine of erasure and consolidation he had constructed, it had never accounted for her. The perfect victim of his system was also its perfect flaw.

"Come on," I said, and my voice broke on it. "Almost there."

* * *

The Undercroft door was bronze, and it knew me.

That sounds mad, but it's the truth of covenant-work: objects bound by oath develop something like recognition. I had signed my indenture in front of that door at nine years old, my small hand guided by a factor's dry grip, my name pressed into wax that the door itself had drunk. Eleven years later, it tasted my approach and swung inward before Sable could finish picking the lock.

"Convenient," Brannoc muttered.

"It's not convenient," I said. "It's *invited*. And I don't like anything that invites me."

Beyond the door lay a round chamber, ceiling lost in shadow, and in its center, on a plinth of black glass, the Covenant Master-Ledger.

It was smaller than I expected. That's always the way with terrible things. A book perhaps the size of a paving-stone, bound in scales — dragon-scales, I realized with a sick lurch, scales the color of old embers, Ysolde's color — and its pages turned slowly by themselves, as if stirred by a wind from nowhere, showing glimpses of script in a language older than any alphabet I knew.

Around it, arranged in a careful semicircle, stood six empty lecterns. Six blank books lay open upon them, pristine, waiting. The presses upstairs fed these, I understood. These were the vessels of the new covenant, the one Corvane was writing himself into as sole signatory.

And on the nearest lectern, placed with deliberate care, lay a single sheet of fresh paper bearing a single line of text:

*Scribe Isandro Iversen. Indenture record. Presented for review.*

My name. My record. Summoned.

"He knows we're here," Sable said.

"He's known since the aqueduct," I said. "Maybe since the pass. Maybe since the beginning." I approached the paper the way you approach a snake. "This is the invitation. This is why he wants me alive."

I picked it up. And as my fingers touched the page, the rest of the record surfaced — the way debt surfaces, the way interest accrues, all the hidden terms rising at once. The full indenture contract bloomed in my mind, every clause I'd signed as a child rendered suddenly legible by everything I'd learned on the road. Clause after clause after clause, and there, buried in the subtext like a hook in bait:

*A debt-bound scribe may be named guarantor without consent, his obligation transferable to any covenant to which his creditor assigns him.*

I read it twice. Three times. The words didn't change.

"A scapegoat," I said aloud, and the word tasted like ash. "He doesn't need a volunteer. He needs a *debtor*. Someone already bound, whose existing obligation can be assigned — transferred — into the new covenant as guarantor. The covenant takes the guarantor's name as payment. His name. Not Corvane's. Mine." I laughed, and it came out wrong. "All of it. The burning shelves, the harvested names, the new blank slate — it all funnels into one signature, and the signature is mine. He erases every debt in existence and the price lands on the one person who was already owned. The realms survive — barely, bound to him — and I pay for everything. The last honest pen, signing away the last free man's freedom. His own."

"That's why the pardon offer," Sable said quietly. "At the factor's camp. He wasn't testing whether you'd surrender the ledger. He was testing whether you'd *come*."

"And I did," I said. "Walked straight into the vault. Brought the sigil-stamp. Brought the ledger of small debts." I looked at my companions — Sable with her spent favors and her fierce eyes, Brannoc with his mended wound and his mended soul, Wren with her rescued book clutched to her chest, Ysolde coiled at my feet with her ember-heart guttering. "He thinks I'm the instrument. The pen in someone else's hand."

I set the indenture record down on the black glass plinth, beside the Master-Ledger, and took out my own ledger instead — the small one, the one Wren had started, the one full of freely given kindnesses.

"But a pen," I said, "is only ever borrowed."

The Master-Ledger's pages stopped turning.

Every page, all at once, went still — the way a room goes still when someone says something true. In the silence I heard, very clearly, from somewhere above us, the grinding of the presses cease.

And then footsteps. Unhurried. Descending.

Corvane had come down to collect his pen.
