## 18. The Last Dragon-Fire

I have burned for four hundred years, and I know the arithmetic of fire the way a miser knows coin.

A breath to warm a hearth: nothing. A breath to light the wards along a border: a season of my life. A breath to hold back the Hollow at a broken gate: a decade. I have spent myself in small denominations for two centuries, ever since my flight went up the mountain one autumn and did not come down, and I have kept careful count, because dragons are creatures of covenant and covenant is nothing but counting.

Tonight the count comes due.

I smell the guild-scribes before I see them. Guild-fire has a particular stink — not woodsmoke, not dragonfire, but something scorched out of language itself, a burning that leaves no ash because it consumes the *name* of what it burns. Corvane's pen-men climbed the mountain road an hour past midnight, forty of them in grey robes with iron styluses at their belts, carrying the Master-Ledger of Vharn on a bier between them like a coffin they intended to empty.

They think I am dying already. They are not wrong. But there is a wide country between *dying* and *dead*, and I mean to spend every mile of it.

* * *

The vault beneath the crater-shrine is old work, older than Vharn, older than the guild that squats in its shadow like a tick grown fat on a great beast's blood. My flight cut it from living stone when the world was young enough to still be negotiable. The Covenant Master-Ledger rests there — not paper, though paper is how mortals read it, but a lattice of binding laid into the rock itself, every promise ever sworn between realms written in fire that never learned how to go out.

It is guttering now. I can feel it through the soles of my claws, through the plates of my chest, through the hollow where my heart once roared. Each page Corvane's scribes burn above us takes a thread from the weave. The wards over the lowlands did not fall all at once; they fell the way a rope falls, strand by strand, and each strand was a debt someone decided no longer needed paying.

Corvane believes he is merciful. I have lived long enough to know that mercy is the mask cruelty wears when it wants to be loved. He will erase the debts and call it freedom, and he does not understand — or worse, he understands perfectly — that a covenant is not a chain. It is a handhold. Take away every handhold in the world and you have not freed anyone. You have only opened the abyss wider, so that more of them can fit.

The scribes reach the shrine mouth at moonrise, which is to say at nothing, because there has been no moon since the stars went out. Their lanterns make a sickly blue, the color of flame remembering it used to be something else.

I am too large to fit in the vault. This was always the design — the guarantor guards the door, not the book. So I coil myself around the crater's rim, around the shrine-stones worn smooth by two hundred years of my waiting, and I watch the grey robes come down the switchbacks with their coffin-bier, and I do the last arithmetic of my life.

* * *

There is a moment, before any battle, when the air goes very still and very clear, and everything ordinary becomes unbearably precious. Dragons call it the *last look*. We are taught it as hatchlings: before you spend yourself, look at everything. The shape of the stones. The cold coming off the ice-field. The small warm weight of a child asleep against your flank.

Wren is asleep against my flank.

She came to me three nights ago without fear, the way children come to things that frighten adults, and she asked me if my scales were dull because I was sad. I told her I was old. She said those were different things, and patted me twice, the way you pat a horse you like, and went back to whatever game Sable had invented to keep her hands busy. Since then she has slept against me every night, a small furnace of a girl, nameless and unbothered by it, humming songs half-remembered from a village whose page burned.

The others are at the pass below — Isandro, Sable, Brannoc, holding the narrow way against enforcers with ledgers full of Brannoc's death already inked and waiting. I can hear the clash of it faintly, iron on ward-iron, a sound like bells arguing. They bought me this hour. They are buying it still, with their bodies, with their courage, with debts freely taken on.

And Corvane's scribes are coming up the other road, unhurried, because they believe the pass is the only door.

They are wrong. There is another door. It is me.

* * *

Here is what the guild never understood about dragon-fire, though they brokered it for a thousand years: it is not fuel. It is *testimony*. Every flame I have ever breathed carries in it the record of why it was breathed. When I lit the western wards, that fire remembered the treaty. When I burned the Hollow back from the tollhouse at Grey Ford, that fire remembered the village behind it, every roof, every well, every name. Fire that has witnessed a covenant cannot be commanded to forget it. It can only be spent.

Corvane means to burn the Master-Ledger with guild-fire, which burns names out of the world. Against that, there is exactly one defense, and it is the oldest clause in the oldest contract, the one no mortal has ever had cause to invoke because invoking it ends the invoker:

*A party to the covenant may seal it with their whole reserve.*

Not a breath. Not a season. Not a decade. Everything. The fire in the marrow, the fire in the memory, the fire that is, at bottom, what a dragon *is* — all of it, poured out at once, given entirely to holding the weave together while the mortal world decides what it wants to be.

Two hundred years ago, when my flight flew up the mountain, they were going to do this together. Eleven dragons, sharing the cost. The Hollow broke through early, at the northern passes, and they went to hold it, and none of them came back, and the sealing was never done, and I have been the last reserve ever since — a treasury with one coin in it, guarding a vault, growing old.

One coin is enough. That is the secret nobody tells you about covenants. They were never designed to be paid by armies. They were designed to be paid by someone who means it.

* * *

The scribes reach the crater floor. Their leader is a thin man with ink to his elbows and the flat eyes of someone who stopped reading what he writes decades ago. He looks up at me coiled on the rim, and I watch him do his own arithmetic — old dragon, dull scales, guttering — and arrive at the answer he was sent to find.

"She's finished," he says. "Begin."

They open the bier. The Master-Ledger lies there in its traveling case, and even from here I can hear it — a sound like a choir heard through a wall, thousands of voices holding one long note. Names. Promises. The whole architecture of *we owe each other*, sung so continuously for so long that no one alive remembers it is a song.

The first scribe sets his stylus to the first page, and guild-fire blooms blue along the margin, and somewhere in the weave beneath my feet a thread snaps, and I feel it like a tooth pulled.

No more counting. The count is done.

"Wren," I say.

Her eyes open at once. Children who have lost everything sleep lightly; they surface fast, ready to run. She looks up at me, and then follows my gaze down to the blue flames blooming in the crater, and I feel her understand, in the way children understand enormous things — instantly, completely, without any of the bargaining adults use to avoid the truth.

"You're going to do the thing," she says. "The big fire."

"The last fire," I agree.

"Will it hurt?"

Dragons do not lie to children. "Yes. But not for long. And then never again, either way."

She considers this with the terrible seriousness of the young. Then she climbs up my foreleg, puts both small hands flat against my jaw, right over the old scar the Hollow left there, and says, "Then I'll hold your face while you do it. So you're not alone."

I have waited two hundred years for a guarantor. I did not know I was also waiting for this.

* * *

Isandro would want me to explain the mechanics. He is that kind of scribe — he believes that anything truly understood can be survived. Very well, old friend, wherever you are on that pass, bleeding for this hour: here is the mechanics.

The covenant is a weave. The weave is held taut by tension between realms — what one owes another, what another forgives. Corvane cuts threads. Cutting is easy; any fool with fire can destroy. What holds a weave is not the absence of cutting but the presence of *pull* — living force distributed across the whole, so that no single failure unravels the rest. My whole reserve, poured into the lattice, becomes new pull. Not forever. Long enough. Long enough for a scribe with a stolen stamp and a ledger of small kindnesses to do what armies and guilds and eleven dead dragons could not.

Long enough. That is all sacrifice ever buys. It has always been enough.

I draw the breath.

It is not like other breaths. Other breaths come from the chest. This one comes from everywhere — from the hatchling-fire my mother breathed over me, from the treaty-oaths, from the wards at Grey Ford, from every small warmth I ever gave the world. I feel it gather the way a river gathers at a falls, and I feel, with perfect clarity, the exact size of what I am about to lose, and I find — this surprises me — that I am not afraid. Two hundred years of hoarding, and it turns out a treasure is only real when you give it away.

Below me, Wren has not let go of my jaw.

I breathe.

* * *

Fire like the world has not seen since the making of it pours down into the crater and does not burn. That is the miracle of covenant-flame: it destroys nothing except forgetting. It pours into the lattice and the lattice *sings*, every severed thread seizing and knitting, every guttering line flaring white, and the guild-fire on the Master-Ledger's margins meets it and recoils like a hand from a stove.

The scribes scream. Not from heat — from witness. You cannot stand inside testimony that strong and remain unchanged. I see men drop their styluses. I see the thin leader clutching his own ledger of orders and weeping at what is written there, seeing it for the first time, really seeing it.

But there are forty of them and Corvane's will is in them like a hook, and after the first shock they regroup, and the blue fire comes again, harder, fed now from the case itself — Corvane anticipated even this. Of course he did. He has been anticipating me for two hundred years without knowing it.

So I give more. I give the shape of my wings. I give the memory of my flight, eleven shadows crossing a young sun. I give the name my mother gave me, which no mortal tongue can pronounce and which I have kept folded in my heart like a letter never sent. The fire does not diminish. It clarifies. And the weave holds, and holds, and holds —

— and I begin, at last, to end.

It is not falling. It is more like being *edited*. The vastness goes first, the great architecture of me, the wingspan and the centuries, folding down and down like a map being closed. Strange, how little I mind. I have been so large for so long, and largeness is lonely. The mountains stop fitting under my shadow and become merely mountains. Four hundred years compress into something I can hold in a thought instead of a life.

The last thing to go is the fire itself, my final coin, and I spend it deliberately, precisely, pressing it into the single most important thread in the whole weave — the one that runs from the Master-Ledger out into the world, out toward a pass where a scribe is bleeding, out toward whatever he is about to write.

The covenant-flame gathers off the lattice, off my body, off the air itself, and condenses.

Into an ember.

Small. Red-gold. No bigger than a coal from a hearth, glowing with everything it remembers: the treaty, the wards, the villages, the names, the whole long song of *we owe each other*. It drifts down through the smoke like a seed deciding where to land.

It lands in cupped hands.

Wren. She never let go of my face until the last moment, and then she turned and caught it — of course she caught it, the nameless child, the girl whose page burned, who knows better than anyone alive what it costs to lose your name and what it means to be given one. She holds the last light between realms in her two small hands, and it does not burn her, because covenant-fire only burns forgetting, and there is nothing forgotten in her, only lost, and lost things can be found.

"Ysolde?" she says. Her voice wavers. "Ysolde, it's so small."

"Yes," I say, and my voice is smaller now too, thinner, coming from a throat that is shrinking around the words. "That's the point. Big fires frighten people. Small ones, they carry."

Down on the crater floor, the scribes are fleeing — the weave blazes too bright now, sealed too tight, guild-fire sliding off it like rain off oiled cloth. The thin leader looks back once at the ember in the child's hands, and I watch him understand, in the last second before he runs, that his master's arithmetic has just failed, because you cannot erase a debt that has already been paid.

I settle onto the crater floor. I am smaller than I have ever been. The world is very bright and very simple: stone, starless sky, a girl holding a light, the distant sound of iron on iron at the pass where my friends are still buying time.

"Hold it safe," I tell Wren. "Carry it down. There is a scribe below who needs to see it — he needs to see what it costs, and what it's worth, and then he needs to write."

"I'll take care of it," she says fiercely, and folds her hands closer, and the ember glows up at her like something content.

"And Wren." The dark is coming for me now, gently, the way sleep comes for the old. "When he writes it — the new covenant — it will need a signature. Tell him not to sign with his name."

"Why not?"

"Because names can be stolen." My eyes are closing. The ember-light through her fingers is the last thing I see, and it is enough; it has always, always been enough. "Tell him to sign with something no one can take. Tell him to sign with what he *owes*."

The dark takes me, and it is warm, and somewhere far below a child begins to climb down a mountain carrying the last light between realms in her bare hands.
