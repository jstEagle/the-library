## 20. After the Ember

The first star came back while Sable was still on her knees in the ash.

She didn't see it happen. She felt it — a small, cold prick at the base of her skull, like a needle of winter sliding into a place that had been numb for weeks. She looked up out of habit, because for a month looking up had meant nothing but the flat black ceiling of a sky with no lights in it, and there it was: one point of silver, low over the western peaks, burning steady as a nailhead.

"Isandro," she said. Her voice came out cracked and strange, like she'd been breathing smoke all night, which she had.

He didn't answer. He was lying on his side near the crater's rim where the fire had thrown him, the small ledger still clutched against his chest, and for one horrible stretch of heartbeats Sable thought the covenant had taken more than his name. Then he coughed, rolled onto his back, and stared up at the same star she was staring at, and said, in the flat tone of a man checking arithmetic he doesn't trust:

"That wasn't there before."

"No," Sable said.

"It should be there."

"I know."

A second star kindled. Then a third, east of the first, then a scatter of them like sparks off a struck flint, each arrival marked by that same cold needle-prick against her skin. The wards were rising. She could feel them now that she knew what to feel for — a faint hum in the ground, in her teeth, in the ward-tinker's sense she'd been born with and spent her whole life using to patch other people's protections while her own debts rotted underneath her. The wards weren't the great iron lattice they'd been before. They were thinner than that. Patched, not forged. A quilt sewn from scraps.

But holding.

"They're going to hold," she said, and heard her own voice shake, and didn't care. "Isandro. They're actually going to hold."

He sat up slowly, the way old men sit up, though he couldn't have been more than five years older than her. He looked down at the ledger in his hands — the small one, the one Wren had started, the one full of kindnesses recorded in four different hands — and then at the ember-light still fading from the scorched circle where Ysolde had made her stand, and then at nothing at all.

"I can't remember my mother's name," he said.

Sable went still.

"I know I had one," he went on, conversationally, terribly calm. "I know there was a woman who taught me my letters. I remember her hands. I remember the ink stain on her right forefinger. But the name is—" He made a small gesture, opening his fingers like a bird being released. "Gone. And my own name too. I keep reaching for it and finding a shape where it used to be. Like a tooth-socket." He looked at her, and his eyes were wet but his voice never wavered. "Was it worth it?"

And Sable, who had spent three favors and most of her future to get these people up this mountain, who had watched a dragon burn herself down to a coal to shield a book, who had held a child while the sky went dark and held her again while it relit — Sable laughed. It burst out of her ugly and loud and half-sob, echoing off the crater walls.

"You absolute ledger-brained idiot," she said. "Yes. Yes, it was worth it. Look *up*."

He looked up. The stars kept coming back, dozens of them now, and somewhere below them in the dark country the Hollow was retreating from places it had seeped into for a month, forgotten things folding themselves back into forgetting, memory-fog thinning off the valleys like morning mist. Isandro watched it with the expression of a man reading a page that had rewritten itself — wonder and grief in the same face, neither winning.

Then something bit Sable's boot.

* * *

She yelped and jumped back, hand going to her knife, and found herself glaring down at a dragon.

Or what had been a dragon. It was the size of an overweight cat, dull-scaled, the color of old pewter gone greenish at the joints, with wings folded tight against its flanks like a bad-tempered hen. Its eyes were the only thing unchanged — molten gold, ancient, and currently fixed on her ankle with an expression of profound offense.

"You stepped on my tail," Ysolde said. Her voice had been thunder once; now it was the crackle of a hearth-fire, small and dry and unmistakably smug. "Two hundred years I waited on this mountain. Two hundred years I kept the flame. And when the moment comes, the tinker steps on my tail."

"You were *lying in the ash*," Sable said. "You were the size of a cathedral an hour ago!"

"And now I am not. Grief does that. So does spending every last reserve of fire you have shielding a book from men with torches." The little dragon heaved herself upright with visible effort, staggered, caught her balance, and pretended she hadn't. "I would appreciate it if no one commented on the change."

"You're the size of a loaf of bread," Brannoc said.

He came limping across the crater with Wren riding his shoulders — the big man moving carefully, favoring the side where an enforcer's blade had opened him at the pass, though the wound beneath the bandages was a pink seam now instead of a death. Isandro had struck the debt clean. Sable had dragged him clear. Neither of them talked about it much. Some ledgers you don't read aloud.

Ysolde turned her golden gaze on him with glacial dignity. "I am *conserving*. There is a difference. When my strength returns—"

"How long?" Isandro asked quietly.

The dragon was silent for a moment. Ash drifted through the returning starlight between them.

"A century," she said at last. "Perhaps two. Perhaps never. The fire is not a well that refills, scribe. It is a life, and I spent mine." She lifted her chin, and for just a heartbeat Sable saw the mountain-shape of her flicker behind the small body, vast and winged and burning, and then it was gone and there was only a grumpy pewter lizard standing in a dead dragon's footprint. "It was worth it. I would appreciate it if no one commented on that either."

Wren slid down from Brannoc's shoulders and crouched in front of the dragon, eye to eye, the way only children and fools and children-who-have-been-fools do. She studied Ysolde with grave attention.

"You're going to need someone to carry you," Wren said. "Down the mountain. When we go."

"I will *walk*."

"You'll get tired."

"*Dragons do not get tired.*"

"You're the size of a loaf of bread," Wren agreed solemnly, and held out both arms, and after a long, furious pause, Ysolde allowed herself to be picked up, cradled against the girl's chest like the world's most indignant kitten, her tail lashing once — narrowly missing Sable again — before settling.

"There will be terms," the dragon muttered.

"Name them," said Wren, and walked off toward the crater rim with her cargo, and Sable caught Isandro watching them go with an expression she decided privately never to tease him about, because she suspected her own face wore it too.

* * *

They buried the guild's ledgers at dawn.

Not burned. That had been the argument, and it had gone on most of the night while the stars multiplied overhead and the company sat around a fire Brannoc built from the wreckage of the enforcers' camp. Burn them, Brannoc had said. Every record of every debt ever extracted, every indenture, every life-bond, every name the guild ever took — feed it all to the flames and let the world start clean.

But Isandro had shaken his head, and when Isandro shook his head about a book, the whole company had learned to listen.

"If we burn them," he'd said, turning a charred ledger-spine in his fingers, "we erase the debtors along with the debts. Somewhere down in those pages there's a woman whose village was freed because she paid three years of her life into the wards. There's a man who sold a memory to buy his daughter medicine. If we burn the records, their payments never happened. Their sacrifices become nothing." He'd looked around the fire. "Corvane wanted a blank slate. I'm not going to finish his work for him because blank feels cleaner."

So they buried them.

There was a cave high on the crater's inner wall, dry and deep, its mouth narrow enough to wall up with stone. All that day and the next they carried the salvaged ledgers up to it — hundreds of them, hauled in armloads and satchel-loads from the wreck of the guildhouse's vault-train, the books Corvane's scribes hadn't managed to burn before Ysolde's fire and Isandro's covenant had ended everything. Some of the pages screamed softly as they were carried. Sable got used to it the way you get used to any grief: imperfectly, and forever.

Brannoc stacked them with a mason's care, spine to spine, oldest deepest. Wren laid wildflowers on the top layer, which did nothing whatsoever and mattered enormously. Isandro stood at the mouth of the cave for a long time before the last stone went in, one hand resting on the stack, lips moving. Sable didn't ask what he was saying. She suspected he was reading names. There were so many names, and almost none of them his anymore.

When the wall was done, Brannoc carved a mark above it — not a sigil, not a guild-stamp, just a simple open hand, palm out. *Nothing here is taken.*

"The small one stays with us," Isandro said. He held up the ledger Wren had started, the one with the debts freely given: *Brannoc whittled Wren a fox. Owed: one story. Sable taught Wren the first ward-knot. Owed: one song. Wren shared her bread with Ysolde. Owed: one warm place to sleep.* "This is the only ledger anyone keeps now. Anyone who wants to."

"And if someone owes something they can't pay?" Sable asked.

"Then we write it down anyway," he said. "And we carry it together until they can. That's the whole covenant, Sable. That's all it ever was, under the interest and the brokers and the fear. Somebody gives. Somebody remembers. Somebody pays it forward." He closed the small ledger and tucked it inside his coat, against his heart, where the stolen page had ridden out of Vharn a lifetime ago. "The guild just forgot the giving part."

* * *

They came down the mountain slow, because Wren was seven and Brannoc was still healing and Ysolde refused on principle to admit how tired she was, which meant they stopped often, which meant — without anyone deciding it — that they talked.

Sable walked beside Isandro on the second day, on a switchback trail with the whole star-hung sky opening below them, and told him about the favors.

All of them. What they'd been, whom she'd owed, what each one had cost. The first, spent at the Market of Impossible Things, buying passage that had been promised to someone else — she still didn't know what had happened to the woman it was meant for, and the not-knowing sat in her chest like a swallowed stone. The second, spent buying Ysolde safe passage down-mountain, leaving herself bound to a creditor she'd have to face someday. The third—

"The third is still owed," she said. "Somewhere. To somebody. I don't even know who holds it anymore, now that the guild's gone. Maybe nobody. Maybe it just... dissolved, with everything else."

"Maybe," Isandro said. "Or maybe it transferred. Debts don't like dying, Sable. You taught me that."

"Don't remind me. I created a monster. I showed a scribe how covenants work and now he corrects *me*."

"I would never."

"You corrected me about the tollhouse signature. In front of Brannoc."

"That was different. You were wrong."

She shoved him, gently, and he stumbled theatrically on the trail, and Wren ahead of them shouted that if they were going to fight she was going to let Ysolde bite them, and the little dragon from her perch in the crook of Wren's arm said "*gladly*" with such relish that everyone laughed, even Brannoc, whose laugh was still rusty, like a gate that hadn't swung in years.

But that evening, at the fire, Isandro came and sat beside her and said, quietly, without preamble: "Your favors. If one of them is still out there, floating loose — I want to find it. Before it finds you. A free debt is just a hook waiting for a fish."

"You don't owe me that."

"I know." He looked into the fire. "That's why I want to. Everything I owed before, I paid because I had to. This is the first debt I've ever chosen." He glanced at her, and in the firelight his face was the face of a stranger she knew better than anyone — unnamed, unrecorded, belonging entirely to the people around this fire. "Besides. You're family. Family means paying each other's debts without being asked. Your words."

"My words are going to haunt me," Sable said, "for the rest of my life."

"Paid in full, daily," said Wren sleepily from her bedroll, half-dreaming, and Ysolde snorted a single thread of smoke that smelled, faintly and impossibly, of home.

* * *

On the fourth day they reached the edge of the lowlands, and Sable saw the first living green she'd seen in a month — a hillside of grass coming up through the ash, stubborn and bright, and beyond it the far smoke of towns lighting their lamps against a night that finally had stars in it. Word had run ahead of them somehow, the way word does. People stood at the edges of fields as they passed and stared at the ragged company: the big scarred man, the tinker with the tool-bandolier, the child carrying a cat-sized dragon, and the scribe with no name whom everyone somehow recognized anyway.

An old woman pressed bread into Wren's hands and wouldn't take payment. A farmer offered them a cart and waved off the offer of work in return. Small debts, freely given, springing up in the covenant's wake like the grass through the ash.

"They're doing it already," Isandro murmured, watching. "Without a guild. Without wards. Just—" He shook his head, at a loss, and Sable finished it for him.

"Just people. Yeah. Terrifying, isn't it? Corvane built an empire on the idea that we'd tear each other apart without a ledger to keep us in line." She watched the farmer's daughter chase Wren in a circle around the cart, both of them shrieking, Ysolde clinging to Wren's collar and pretending to disapprove. "Turns out we mostly just feed each other."

That night they camped within sight of the road that led back toward Vharn, and Sable lay awake a long time, listening to the sounds of her strange, impossible family breathing around the banked fire, and felt the wards humming faint and steady beneath the earth like a heartbeat under a floorboard.

Faint. Holding. Like them.

She was nearly asleep when Isandro spoke from the darkness on the other side of the fire, quiet enough that she almost missed it.

"Sable. The small ledger. Page nine."

She frowned at the embers. "What about it?"

"I've been reading it over. Checking the entries." A pause, and when he continued, his voice had that careful flatness she'd learned meant trouble — the voice of a man reading a clause he wished weren't there. "The entry from the crater. Where I wrote myself in as guarantor. It's changed."

Sable sat up. Across the fire, Brannoc's eyes opened. Even Ysolde stirred, lifting her blunt snout from Wren's shoulder.

"Changed how?" Sable asked.

Isandro held the little book up to the ember-light, open to page nine, and even from across the fire she could see it — a line of fresh ink gleaming wet and dark beneath his guarantor's mark, in a hand none of them recognized, elegant and old and patient as deep water:

*A guarantor's bond may be honored — or collected.*

Below it, in smaller script, as if the page itself were leaning close to whisper:

*The collector is coming.*
