## 21. The Unnamed Scribe

The price came due the way all the gentlest debts do: quietly, while no one was watching.

I noticed it first in the scriptorium's morning ledger. I had written my own name on the day-sheet — habit, muscle memory, a scribe signs his work — and when I looked down at it, the ink was still wet and the space where the name should have been was blank.

Not smudged. Not scraped. Blank, the way a page is blank before anything is ever written on it. The pen had moved. The ink had flowed. Something had simply declined to remain.

I wrote it again. *Isandro Iversen.* Watched the letters sit there for three heartbeats, four, five — and then fade like breath off cold glass, until the parchment showed only the ghost of an indentation, as if the words had been pressed into it from somewhere far away and were now being pulled back through.

"Well," I said, to no one, because that was becoming a theme.

Sable found me an hour later sitting very still at the workbench with the day-sheet in front of me like a dead bird I didn't want to touch.

"Say something," she said.

"Something."

"No — your name. Say your name."

"Isandro Iversen." It came out fine. My mouth remembered it even if the paper wouldn't. "Why?"

She sat down across from me slowly, the way you sit down when you've already decided the news is bad and you're just choosing the shape of the chair. "Brannoc tried to introduce you to the miller's boy yesterday. He got as far as 'this is' and then stopped. Stood there for a full ten seconds with his mouth open. Thought he'd been hexed."

"He wasn't?"

"Wren says he wasn't. Wren's been keeping track." Sable turned the day-sheet toward herself, watched my name finish dissolving under her eyes. Her jaw tightened. She had freckles I had never bothered to count and a scar along her thumb from the first ward-sigil she ever cut wrong, and I realized, with a lurch, that I was memorizing her — cataloguing her the way I used to catalogue debts — because some part of me already knew I was next on the list of things that could be taken.

"The covenant took its payment," she said. "You're paying it."

"I know what I agreed to."

"Knowing it and watching it are different animals." She pushed the sheet back. "How does it feel? In your head. Honest answer."

I considered lying, decided against it — she could read my face better than I could read covenants, which was saying something. "Like a room being emptied," I said. "Furniture first. Then the walls."

* * *

It went in stages, and the stages had their own terrible orderliness, because of course they did. I was a scribe. Even my erasure kept books.

First the records. Guild archives, parish rolls, the indenture-ledger that had owned eleven years of my life — every mention of me faded or resolved into someone else's handwriting, someone else's name, until the documents read as though a clerk named Isandro Iversen had never held a pen in Vharn. Brannoc rode down to the lowland market and came back gray-faced. "The chandler remembers a boy apprentice," he said. "Remembers everything about him except what he looked like. Says he had a way with figures. Can't say his name. Can't say why he's crying about it, either."

Then the memories. That was worse, because memories don't fade clean. They rot. People who had known me started remembering *a* person where I had been — a shape, a function, a gap wearing my coat. The innkeeper at the Tollhouse Rest swore she'd always had two rooms free, not three. A woman in the valley wept at my funeral without knowing whose it was; Brannoc had joked about holding one, and then hadn't laughed, and neither had anyone else.

And then — last, the way the covenant keeps its cruelest clause for the end — my own reflection.

I want to be precise about this, because precision is the last craft I have left. It wasn't that the mirror showed nothing. It showed a man. Correct height, correct hands ink-stained at the knuckles, correct tired eyes. But looking at him was like reading a contract written in a language I almost spoke. Every feature was *familiar* the way a word is familiar when it's on the tip of your tongue. I would stand at the basin in the mornings and think: *that is a face. Someone lives behind it. I appear to be him.*

Once, I caught myself introducing myself to my own reflection. Politely. As a stranger.

That was the night I stopped sleeping well.

* * *

"You're moping," Wren announced, dropping onto the bench beside me with the total lack of ceremony that was her signature move, her opening gambit, her entire diplomatic strategy.

"I'm contemplating."

"You're moping *contemplatively.* There's a difference and it's boring." She swung her legs. She was nine now by our best guess, ten by hers, and she had grown half a hand since the mountain and lost none of her talent for stating the obvious like a verdict. "Ysolde says you keep checking whether we remember you."

The cat-sized dragon in question, draped over the warm stone of the hearth, opened one copper eye. "I said no such thing," Ysolde said. "I said he keeps *asking*, in the transparent manner of his kind, whether we remember him, which is worse, because it forces us to answer."

"Do you?" I asked. And hated how small it came out.

Wren looked at me for a long moment. Nine years old, and she looked at me the way I imagined an auditor looks at a ledger that has been lying to itself.

"I remember you," she said. "But not the way I did."

"What way, then?"

"You used to be the man who found me in the ash. Now you're—" she frowned, hunting for it, and I braced myself for the wound— "the man who stayed. The finding is gone. The staying isn't." She shrugged. "I think I got the better trade."

I laughed, and it cracked in the middle, and she pretended not to notice, which was its own kindness.

Because here is the thing nobody tells you about selling your name: you expect to lose the past. You don't expect the present to keep arriving without you. Every morning Sable made tea exactly how I liked it — too strong, honey stirred counterclockwise, an old guild habit I'd never explained and she'd never asked about — and every morning she made it without knowing why she made it that way, only that she did, only that it felt right. Brannoc saved me the end piece of the loaf because "someone around here likes the crust," and handed it to me with a frown, unable to say who that someone was. The world kept loving me on momentum. Memory had fallen out of the machine, but the machine kept turning.

It was the loneliest thing I have ever experienced, and I once spent three years indentured to a guild that owned my heartbeat.

* * *

It was Wren who broke it open. Of course it was.

Her memories had been coming back all winter — slow seepings, fragments surfacing like drowned things. Her mother's hands kneading bread. A song about a river with no name. And words. Odd little words, the private vocabulary of a burned village, a language of hearth and field that existed nowhere else now except inside one child's skull.

She came bursting into the scriptorium on a grey morning in late thaw, snowmelt dripping off her boots, waving her arms like a windmill having opinions.

"I've got it," she said.

"Got what?"

"A word." She planted herself in front of me. Behind her, Sable looked up from a ward-diagram and Brannoc set down his whittling knife, both of them going still in the particular way of people who have learned that when Wren announces something, it matters.

"A word for what?"

"For *you*. Obviously." She said it the way you'd say *for Tuesday*. "You gave up your name so everyone else could keep theirs. So everyone gets to be themselves, but you don't get to be anybody. That's not fair. Fairness is a debt and I'm collecting." She crossed her arms. "So I'm paying you back. With a name. Mine to give — it's from my village, from before, so it's mine, and I can spend it however I want, and I'm spending it on you."

The room was very quiet. Ysolde had lifted her head off the hearthstone. Even the fire seemed to lean in.

"Say it," Sable said softly.

"*Vaelin*," Wren said.

And I felt it land.

I don't know how to describe what a name does when it's given rather than assigned. My old name had been a chain — it had come with a debt attached, a guild stamp, a price per year of my life. This word arrived the opposite way. It came with warmth in it. Later Wren told me what it meant in her mother's tongue: *the one who writes things down so they aren't lost.* A keeper-word. A hearth-word. A name you'd call across a dark field to bring someone home.

"Vaelin," I repeated.

My reflection, that morning, had felt like a stranger. But standing in the scriptorium with a nine-year-old's whole inheritance pressed into my hands, I felt — for the first time since the ember — *located*. Not restored. Chosen. There is a difference, and the difference is everything. A given name can be erased. A chosen one has to be un-given, and no ledger in any realm holds that power.

"It fits," Ysolde observed, settling her chin back onto her forepaws with the air of a critic delivering a verdict she'd prepared in advance. "It's smaller than your old name. Good names usually are. Big names are for people trying to fill them. Small names are for people who've already grown."

* * *

Here is what happened next, and I tell it plainly because it deserves plain telling.

Sable took the name to town. Walked into the chandler's shop — the same chandler who had wept for an apprentice he couldn't remember — and said, "There's a man at the scriptorium on the hill. His name is Vaelin. If anyone asks after the scribe, that's what you call him." And the chandler, who owed the scriptorium a debt of gratitude for the wards we'd cut over his door, nodded and passed it on.

Brannoc took it to the valley. He was bad at subtlety and good at stubbornness, so he simply introduced himself to every farmer, ferryman, and fence-mender within twenty miles as "Brannoc, companion of Vaelin," repeating the name until it stuck in the region's mouth like a burr in wool. Within a month, children were shouting it at me from rooftops. *Vaelin! Vaelin, the wards are humming again!* They shouted it like it had always been mine. Maybe, by then, it had.

One person at a time. One introduction, one letter, one shouted greeting. A name rebuilt the way a wall is rebuilt — stone by carried stone, by hands that chose to carry.

And I will confess the thing I am least proud of and most certain of: some nights, I still grieve. I grieve the boy in the Ledger Guild who signed his first indenture with a shaking hand. I grieve the man who stole a page and fled a burning guildhouse with terror and freedom fighting in his chest. I miss being someone history remembers. Some evenings I stand at the basin and the stranger in the glass looks back, and I practice, silently, the new name, fitting it over the absence like a bandage over a wound that has finally, finally stopped bleeding.

But then Wren yells it from the yard. Or Sable says it over tea, counterclockwise honey and all. Or Brannoc bellows it across a field with the subtlety of a landslide. And the name comes home to me the way I once came home to them: not because it was owed.

Because it was given.

* * *

On the first true day of spring, Wren climbed onto the scriptorium table — she was forbidden to climb on the table, which is why she did it — and opened the small ledger to a fresh page.

"We owe each other everything," she wrote, in her enormous, lopsided hand. "Paid in full, daily."

Below it, in my hand now steadier than it had ever been in the guild, I added the day's entries. Sable owed Wren a lesson in sigil-cutting. Brannoc owed Sable a repaired shutter. Wren owed Ysolde three sardines and an apology for the incident with the yarn. Ysolde owed no one anything, a fact she insisted be recorded verbatim.

And at the bottom, where a scribe signs his work:

*Vaelin.*

The ink held. It held the way spring holds — not because frost can't return, but because enough things have decided to grow.

Outside, the first star of the evening pricked through the lightening sky, steady and bright above the wards. Inside, my family argued cheerfully about sardines.

Somewhere far off, I thought I heard — faint, at the edge of hearing, the way you hear a word spoken in a language you almost remember — a sound like a great ledger turning its final page.

And beneath it, softer still, something beginning to write.
