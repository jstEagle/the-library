## 22. A New Ledger Opens

The scriptorium had once been a tollhouse.

That was Sable's doing, mostly. She'd found the ruin a year ago on the road down from the mountains — the same ruined tollhouse where Brannoc had nearly killed them all, though she liked to tell it with more knives and fewer tears than the truth allowed — and she'd stood in its broken doorway with her hands on her hips and declared that any building where a man had once chosen not to murder his friends was a building worth saving.

Brannoc had rebuilt the walls. He was good at rebuilding things; he said it was penance he could do with his hands instead of his head. Isandro had rebuilt the shelves, because Isandro could not look at an empty shelf without feeling personally responsible for it. And Wren had painted the sign.

**THE LEDGER OF SMALL DEBTS**, it read, in letters of varying sizes, because Wren had been learning her letters for exactly one year and some of them were still taller than others. Beneath it, in smaller, more careful script — Isandro's hand, though no one in Vharn would ever be able to prove it: *Debts freely given. Debts freely forgiven.*

They opened at dawn, three days out of every seven, and anyone could come.

* * *

On the first morning of autumn's second year, the first visitor through the door was a farmer named Odell Hask, who owed his neighbor a debt so old and so tangled that neither of them could remember how it started.

"It was a pig," Odell said, sitting across the scarred oak table with his hat wrung in both fists like a chicken he wasn't sure whether to keep or kill. "Or maybe it was a fence. Or maybe the fence was because of the pig. My wife says I should just let it go. My wife is dead now, gods rest her, and I find I still can't let it go."

Isandro slid a fresh page across the table. The paper was good paper — Sable traded ward-work to a papermaker two valleys over, and the papermaker traded back generously, because Isandro had once forgiven her a debt of eleven years' guilt over a sister she hadn't written to. Everything in the new economy worked like that. It made the old guild factors furious when they heard about it, which was a bonus none of them would admit to enjoying.

"Write it down," Isandro said. "Whatever you remember. It doesn't have to be true. It only has to be honest."

Odell wrote: *I owe Tam Weaver for a pig or a fence or forty years of being too proud to ask which.* Then he sat looking at it, and his eyes went wet, and Isandro waited the way he had learned to wait — patiently, without watching, the way you wait for bread to rise.

"Can I forgive it?" Odell asked finally. "Just like that? Don't someone have to approve it?"

"No one has to approve anything," Isandro said. "That was the whole problem with the last system."

So Odell drew a line through his own entry, and then — this was the part Isandro loved, the part that never stopped surprising him — he asked for a second page, and wrote: *Tam owes me an apology. Forgiven before it's given. Let him take his time.*

He walked out lighter. Isandro watched him go up the road with his hat actually on his head instead of strangled in his hands, and felt the small, quiet click inside his chest that he had come to think of as the sound of a ledger balancing.

Behind him, Ysolde sneezed, and set the inkwell on fire.

* * *

Ysolde was the size of a large cat now, and she had opinions about everything, and she expressed most of them by sitting on things.

Today she was sitting on the ledger itself, which was her way of saying that Isandro had been working too long. Her scales had gone from the white-fire silver of the western flight to a dull, dented bronze, and one wing folded wrong and always would, and she was — by her own frequent account — magnificent.

"You're staring," she said. Dragons, even small ones, even diminished ones, could speak into your mind, and Ysolde used the ability primarily for commentary.

"I'm working."

"You're brooding with a pen in your hand. There's a difference. Brooding with a pen is how we got the guild." She extended one claw and tapped the page. "You've written the same word four times."

Isandro looked down. She was right. *Forgiven forgiven forgiven forgiven.* He crossed three of them out.

"Two hundred years I waited in that crater," Ysolde said, and her mind-voice went softer, the way it did when she talked about the long dark. "Two hundred years, and do you know what I imagined? Fire. Sacrifice. Some hero bleeding out over the covenant-page while I sang the old songs. I did not imagine a scribe who keeps a jar of honey on the shelf for children who cry. I did not imagine *this*." She gestured with her whole small body at the room — the shelves, the sunlight, the smell of bread Sable was baking in the back because the scriptorium had somehow become also a bakery, no one remembered deciding this. "It's better. I hate that it's better. I had a very good speech prepared."

"You can still give the speech."

"I will. At your funeral. In eighty years. Start eating more honey."

She settled back onto the ledger, purring like a landslide, and Isandro worked around her, because that was simply how the ledger was kept now: under a dragon.

* * *

At midday, Brannoc came in from the road with sawdust on his shoulders and a child on each arm.

This was the other thing the scriptorium had become, though no one had planned it. Word had spread — word traveled strangely fast through a land that had spent a year relearning its own stars — that there was a place where debts could be put down. And people came carrying debts they couldn't write: the ones burned in the lowlands, the ones erased by guild fire-scribes, the ones that lived in the body instead of the page. Villages that had forgotten their own founding. Mothers who had lost their children's names. Children who had lost everything, including the word for what they'd lost.

Wren met them at the door. She was nine now, or possibly ten; they had decided her birthday was the day the wards fell, because that was the day her life had started counting again, and she defended this position with the ferocity of a much larger person. She took the smaller child by the hand with an authority that made Brannoc's arms suddenly unnecessary, and led her to the corner where the memory-books lived — the ones Sable bound with ward-thread, the ones that held what people brought them, safe, until the owners were ready.

"Her village burned?" Wren asked Brannoc, quietly, the way adults asked questions in front of children.

"Lowlands. Two years gone." Brannoc set the older boy down. His voice did something complicated around the word *burned*. It always did. It probably always would. "There's a grandmother in Thornhollow who might remember the mother's face. I told them I'd walk them there next week."

"You'll stay for supper first," Wren said. It was not a question either. Nobody gave Wren questions anymore; they gave her answers.

Brannoc looked at Isandro over the children's heads, and his expression was the one Isandro had learned to read in the first hard weeks on the road: the ex-enforcer's permanent astonishment that this was his life now. That he built cradles and toy dragons and walked orphaned children to their grandmothers, and that no one had struck him down for it, and that the ledger — Isandro had shown him, that night in the pass, when the wound closed — the ledger said his death-debt was paid, struck clean, and whatever he did with the rest of his days was nobody's sentence but his own choice.

He still didn't talk about the villages. But he had started planting trees along the road south. One for each name he could remember. There were a hundred and six trees now, and every spring he added more, and last spring Wren had planted one too, and neither of them had discussed it, and it was the most important conversation either of them had ever had.

* * *

Sable closed the shop-side at dusk and found Isandro on the step, watching the road.

The road was empty. The road was usually empty at dusk. But he watched it anyway, and she knew why, and she sat down beside him without making him say it.

A year, and Corvane had never come. No enforcers in the night, no blank-faced factors offering bargains. The guildhouse in Vharn stood sealed and silent; travelers said the shelves inside had gone to dust, that the screaming had stopped, that the great vault door hung open on nothing at all. Whatever Corvane had been building toward, the erasure had failed, and the man himself had become a story people told badly — *the guild-master who wanted to forgive everyone by force*, mothers said to children, *and learned you can't forgive a debt that isn't yours.*

But Isandro watched the road anyway. Some nights, Sable knew, he dreamed of the empty page where his name used to be, and woke reaching for a pen.

"He's not coming," she said.

"I know."

"You don't, though. You *know* it and you don't believe it." She bumped her shoulder against his. "Want me to say the thing?"

"No."

"The thing where you already won?"

"*No.*"

"The thing where the scariest man in Vharn got beaten by a book club—"

"Sable."

"—run by a nine-year-old and a cat—"

"She's a dragon."

"She's the size of a loaf of bread and she naps in the flour bin." Sable grinned, and after a moment, helplessly, Isandro grinned too, because a year of practice had taught him that resistance was futile. "Come eat. Brannoc made stew. It's terrible. We owe it to ourselves to suffer together."

Inside, the scriptorium smelled of woodsmoke and rosemary and the particular warm-paper scent that Isandro had followed his whole life and finally, finally caught. Ysolde had abandoned the flour bin for the hearth and lay with her chin on Wren's knee while the girl read aloud — slowly, sounding out the tall letters — from a book of stories about a fox who kept accounts. The fox was fictional. The fox's habit of writing down every kindness done to him was not, and everyone at the table pretended not to notice whose idea the fox had been.

Four bowls. Four spoons. One dragon who ate from the fifth bowl and complained about portion size.

Isandro sat down among them, and the strange old grief moved through him and past him, as it did most evenings now — the grief for the name he'd paid, for the mother and father he could no longer quite picture, for the indentured boy in Vharn who had hidden a page in his coat and run into the dark. He let it move through. Grief, he had learned, was just love with nowhere to go, and his had somewhere to go now. It had a table to sit at.

"Wren," he said. "Pass the bread. You owe me."

"I do *not*," she said with dignity, passing the bread. "I bought you a rock yesterday."

"A very good rock," he conceded. "We'll call it even."

* * *

Later, when the others had gone to bed and the fire was down to its patient orange heart, Isandro lit the lamp and opened the ledger to the final page.

It was the last blank page. A year of entries filled the rest: Odell's pig-or-fence. The papermaker's eleven years. A fisherman who forgave the sea. A woman who recorded that she owed her dead friend a dance, and marked it *paid, in my kitchen, alone, and it counted*. Page after page of small debts, freely entered, freely struck, in a hundred different hands, because Isandro had stopped keeping the ledger alone sometime in the spring and no one had thought to make it official. It belonged to whoever held the pen.

He turned to the final page and found it wasn't blank.

Wren's handwriting — taller letters and shorter ones, careful, tongue-between-teeth careful — covered it in pencil, which meant she'd written it knowing it could be erased, which meant she'd written it knowing *he* would read it and decide.

*We owe each other everything,* it said. *Paid in full, daily.*

Below it, a line had been left open, waiting. Not for a signature. For tomorrow's entry.

Isandro sat for a long time with his hand flat on the page, feeling the truth of it settle into him the way the wards had settled over the land — faint, imperfect, holding. They did owe each other everything. That was the whole secret Corvane had never understood: the debt was not the chain. The debt was the *thread*. Cut every thread and you get a world of loose ends. Keep them, tend them, pay them daily in bread and rocks and terrible stew and trees planted along the road — and the threads weave themselves into something no covenant ever managed to be.

Something you belong to. Something that belongs to you.

He picked up the pen. Under Wren's words, in a hand no record anywhere would recognize, he wrote:

*Entry the first of the second year: today we were owed nothing we didn't want to owe, and paid everything we could afford, and it was enough. It will be enough tomorrow. Come see.*

Then he blew out the lamp, banked the fire, and went to bed among his debts.

Outside, the stars were all present and accounted for. Inside, a small bronze dragon opened one eye, checked that her people were home, and slept.
