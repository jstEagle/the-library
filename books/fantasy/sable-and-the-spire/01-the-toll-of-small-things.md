## 1. The Toll of Small Things

The trouble with paying for magic in memories is that you never get a receipt.

I've been keeper of the Thornfell gate for nine years now, and in that time I have forgotten my mother's voice three times. The first two didn't take — the ward spat them back like a coin too worn to spend, and I got to keep the sound of her humming over the washbasin a little longer, hoarding it the way other people hoard silver. Tonight is the third attempt. Third time's the toll, as the old instruction manual doesn't say, because there is no instruction manual, because whoever built this gate assumed the next keeper would be someone who'd been properly trained instead of a nineteen-year-old smuggler's kid who took the job because it came with a roof and nobody asking questions.

I sat down on the cold stone step inside the gatehouse, pressed my palm to the ward-stone, and went looking.

Here's how it works, if you're lucky enough never to have to know: you reach into yourself the way you'd reach into a pocket, and you find something small and warm and yours, and you hold it up to the stone, and the stone takes it, and the wards hum a little brighter, and you go on with your evening minus one thing you'll never be able to name. The trick — the only trick — is choosing something small enough. A whole memory is too rich; the ward gorges and then demands again within the week. But a fragment, a sliver, a single thread pulled from the weave — that buys you a month, sometimes six weeks if the weather's kind.

I found her voice the way I always do: not by remembering it, exactly, but by remembering that I had it. There's a difference, and the difference is grief. I keep an inventory in a little leather book — not what the memories are, I couldn't write that even if I wanted to, but their shapes. *Mother's voice, third attempt. Mother's face, second attempt, partial.* You learn to catalog your own erosion. It's very tidy work. I recommend it to no one.

*Take it,* I told the stone. *Just the voice. Leave me the rest.*

The ward drank. Somewhere behind my ribs, a door closed on a room I would not remember standing outside of tomorrow. And then, as always, the horrible little silence where she used to be — not an absence I could feel, worse, an absence I couldn't, a smooth patch of wall where I'd later find, from my own ledger, that a window used to be.

"Right," I said aloud, to no one, because saying things aloud is the last luxury of people who live alone with a monument. "That's the rent paid."

I lit the lamp, opened the logbook, and made my entry.

---

The Thornfell gate does not exist. That's not poetry; that's the job description. Nine years ago, when I swore the keeper's oath with my hand on the ward-stone and my whole future still intact, the border-warden who administered it explained it to me with the brisk cheer of a man describing a tax code. *The wards don't just guard the pass, Okafor. They unguard it. Nobody sees the gate. Nobody remembers the road through it. Smugglers cross it without knowing they've crossed anything, and by morning they've forgotten they were ever here.* He said it like it was clever. It is clever. It's also the loneliest piece of engineering on the continent, because a gate nobody notices needs a keeper nobody thanks, feeding it pieces of himself so that everyone else can go on blissfully unaware.

So: invisible service. My mother — whoever she was before I started selling her off in installments — would have had opinions about that. Probably loud ones. I've kept the shape of her stubbornness even where the voice went; it's the one inheritance the ward can't seem to digest.

Tonight's entry, in my neat keeper's hand:

*Night of the 14th. Two riders crossed southbound at roughly the eleventh hour, leading a pack mule. Branded the mule myself while they slept off the crossing — the forgetting fog makes them drowsy, always has. Cargo smelled of raw spirits and, beneath it, something mineral I didn't care for. Logged the brand number. No incident.*

Routine. Gloriously, mind-numbingly routine. In nine years I have logged four hundred and eleven crossings, foiled precisely none of them (that's not the job), and spoken more words to a ward-stone than to any living soul. The nearest village is half a day's walk down the valley, and the villagers, thanks to the wards, experience me as a sort of low fog of disinterest — they'll nod at me on market days and forget my face by the time they've bought their onions. I used to fight it, the first year. I'd introduce myself twice, three times, leave tokens, carve my initials into the tavern table like a lovesick boy. Then I read the ledger of the keeper before me — Maren Solenne, her name inked on the flyleaf in a hand much finer than mine — and found forty years of entries in the same tidy script, and not one letter from anyone, not one visitor noted, not one line that wasn't cargo or weather or tolls paid.

Forty years. She did it for forty years, and then the ledger simply stops, mid-sentence, in the middle of an ordinary week, and there's no record of what happened to her. I try not to think about that part. I think about it constantly.

I banked the fire, checked the ward-lamps along the pass — seven of them, burning that particular colorless flame that means the boundary is holding — and settled in for the watch. This is the part of the night I actually like, if I'm honest, which I generally am only to this logbook. The pass at midnight is beautiful in a way that feels private, like it's been arranged for an audience of one. Black pines shouldering the snowline. The wind coming down off the Sable peaks with that clean mineral smell. And above it all, the stars, thick as spilled salt, wheeling slow over the mountains.

I make a point of looking at them every night. It's not sentiment. Well — it's mostly sentiment. But it's also bookkeeping: the stars are the one thing up here that never changes, and a keeper who's forgetting herself by inches needs at least one fixed point to navigate by. Count the stars, Coral. They'll still be there when you're not sure who's counting.

That's when I saw it.

At first I thought it was cloud — high, thin cirrus will do strange things to starlight, and I've been fooled before. But there was no cloud. The air was glass-clear, cold enough to ache in the teeth. And over the saddle of the pass, dead center in my field of view, a star flickered. Not twinkled — stars twinkle, that's just the atmosphere breathing. This was different. This was a guttering. A candle-flame kind of stutter, bright-dark-bright, like something drinking the light in sips.

Then a second star did it. Then a third, off toward the Spire's jagged silhouette, where no star should be doing anything but burning.

I stood very still in the gatehouse doorway with my ledger under my arm and watched, and I felt the wards respond before I understood why — a faint wrongness in the hum under the flagstones, the way a dog goes stiff before the earthquake. Seven ward-lamps along the pass, all burning steady for nine years. The nearest one flickered. Just once. Just slightly.

"Huh," I said, which is the traditional battle cry of the deeply alone.

I told myself it was atmospheric. I told myself it was fatigue — I'd paid a toll tonight, and the hours after a payment are always strange, the world feeling slightly secondhand, like a room after the furniture's been moved. I wrote it in the log anyway, because that's the discipline, because Maren Solenne wrote everything down for forty years and maybe that's why it took her so long to disappear:

*Night of the 14th, addendum. Stars over the central pass observed flickering irregularly between the eleventh and twelfth hours. Three confirmed, possibly more near the Spire. Ward-lamp four guttered once. Cause unknown. Watching.*

Cause unknown. Watching. Two phrases I would give a great deal, later, to have written differently. But that's the thing about the end of the world — I know that now, though I didn't know it then — it doesn't announce itself with trumpets. It starts as a flicker. A wrong detail in an otherwise ordinary night. A star that stutters, a lamp that hesitates, a keeper standing alone in a doorway with her ledger, telling herself everything is fine because everything has always been fine, because fine is the only story the gate has ever let her keep.

I stayed up until dawn anyway. Old habit, or new instinct — hard to say which. And just before first light, when the sky was going grey at the edges and the eastern peaks had turned the color of old pewter, the flickering stopped.

All the stars went out at once.

Not dimmed. Not clouded. *Out* — the whole salt-spill of them, snuffed in a single breath, like a hand closing over a candle. The sky over Thornfell pass became a flat, absolute black I had no name for, a black with nothing behind it.

I stood in the doorway until my hands stopped shaking enough to write, and then I wrote the only honest entry of my nine years of service:

*Night of the 15th. The stars are gone. Request instructions.*

There was no one to send it to. There never had been.

And far below the pass, down the dark southern road, something was coming through the broken edge of the sky — falling, burning, small and bright as a struck match — straight toward my gate.
