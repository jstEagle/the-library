## 2. When the Sky Went Dark

I want it on the record that I did not panic immediately.

There was a full three seconds between the moment the stars went out and the moment the wards shattered, and in those three seconds I did something genuinely useful: I put down my tea. This is the kind of competence nine years of gate-keeping builds in a person. Not heroism. Not wisdom. Just the reflexive instinct to protect the teacup when the universe starts coming apart at the seams.

The stars did not fade. That's what I keep returning to, later, when I try to make sense of it. They didn't dim or gutter the way they'd been flickering for weeks—those little dying-candle stutters over the pass that I had logged, dutifully, in my reports, under *atmospheric anomalies*, because that is what you write when the alternative is *the sky is being eaten* and you would very much like to keep your posting.

No. At the stroke of midnight they simply stopped. All of them. At once. Like a hand snuffing a thousand wicks in a single motion.

The dark that followed wasn't ordinary night-dark. Ordinary dark still has texture—the grey suggestion of mountains, the silver ghost of a river, your own hands if you hold them close enough to your face. This was dark with weight. Dark like wet wool pressed against the eyes. I stood on the gatehouse steps holding my cooling tea and felt the whole sky lean down toward me, patient as a debt collector.

Then the wards broke.

I have fed those wards every single night for nine years. I know their sound the way you know a lover's breathing: the low harmonic hum of the threshold stones, the tick of the boundary-lanterns cycling, the almost-subliminal thrum you feel in your back teeth when something on the far side presses against the veil and finds it solid. The wards were the most constant relationship of my adult life, which says something sad about my adult life that I've chosen not to examine.

They shattered like glass. Not metaphorically—I heard glass. A crack ran through the air itself, high and clean, and then a thousand smaller cracks chasing after it, and the lanterns along the Thornfell road burst one by one down the valley, pop, pop, pop, like someone walking away and snapping their fingers.

The hum stopped. My back teeth rang with the absence.

"Well," I said, to no one, because there was never anyone. "That's not good."

* * *

Here is what I knew, in the aftermath, standing in the ruins of my one job: the gate was open. Not damaged—open. The difference matters. A broken ward leaks. An opened gate invites. And the air coming through the Thornfell pass had changed temperature, gone cold in a way that had nothing to do with weather, a cold with intention behind it.

I should tell you what I thought first, because I'm trying to be honest in this account even where it flatters me poorly. My first thought was not *the world is ending*. My first thought was: *Command is going to blame me for this.* Nine years of invisible service, and my mind went straight to paperwork. There is a version of me who spent that night writing an incident report in careful official script while the darkness poured through the pass unimpeded, and honestly, some nights I think that version had the right idea.

But then she fell out of the sky.

I don't mean that poetically. I mean there was a sound like a struck bell, and a streak of light—actual light, the first light I'd seen since midnight, gold-white and desperate—and it came down out of the dead sky at an angle no falling thing survives, and hit the hillside above the gate hard enough to shake frost from the pines.

I climbed. Don't ask me why. Duty, probably, or its ugly cousin, curiosity. I remember the frost crunching under my boots and the cold pressing on my shoulders like hands, and I remember thinking, distantly, that whatever was up there had come from the place where the stars used to be, and that meant it had fallen a very long way indeed.

She was lying in a crater of scorched bracken, and she was young—younger than me, maybe nineteen, maybe younger, though her eyes were old in a way I recognized. Gatekeeper's eyes. Watcher's eyes. Eyes that have been alone with a duty too long.

And her chest was glowing.

Not metaphorically. Under the torn fabric of her shirt, beneath the skin, something burned—a shard, a splinter, a sliver of starlight lodged in her sternum like a knife made of morning. With every heartbeat it pulsed, and with every pulse the darkness around us recoiled a half-step, hissing, like water off hot iron.

"Hello," I said, because I am, apparently, the kind of person who greets a miracle.

Her eyes found mine. "Run," she said. "They're coming. They felt me fall."

"Who's—"

The bell-sound again, further off, wrong. And down in the valley, along the Thornfell road, torches. Six of them. Seven. Moving fast, moving together, moving with the particular terrible coordination of people who are not people anymore.

I knew them. That's the part I still can't shake. I knew the smugglers who worked this pass—knew them by gait, by silhouette, by the way they whistled when they thought the border was clear. These moved like none of them. These moved like puppets whose strings had been cut and replaced with something worse. And when the lead figure raised a torch, the light caught its face, and the face was smooth. Not scarred. Smooth. Features present but somehow *unfinished*, like a sketch of a man done by someone who had only ever had a man described to them.

Hollowed. I didn't have the word yet—that came later, from a ghost, as these things do—but I understood it in my body before my mind caught up. Whatever had put out the stars had gotten to these people first, and it had left the shape of them and taken everything else.

"They're hunting the light," the girl said. She was trying to stand. She failed, elegantly, and swore in a language that sounded like wind through a canyon. "It's in me. They can smell it. If they take it—" She looked up at me, and the shard in her chest lit her face from below, and I saw that she was terrified, and that she was trying very hard not to show it, and that she was failing at both. "Please."

Now. Let us pause here, because here is where I broke the rules, and I want to be precise about the breaking.

Nine years. Nine years of neutrality. That was the whole architecture of my service: the keeper sees, the keeper logs, the keeper does not interfere. Smugglers cross, and I note it in the ledger and feed the ward another memory and let the border stay invisible. It was a good system. It was a coward's system dressed in a good system's clothes, and I wore it like a uniform, and I told myself for nine years that watching was the same as guarding.

Those seven hollowed things were coming up the hill toward a wounded girl, and every rule I had ever kept said: *log it, report it, do not engage.*

I set down my lantern. I picked up the iron fire-poker I'd carried for nine years against wolves and never once used. And I heard myself say, in a voice I barely recognized—steady, almost bored:

"Gentlemen. The pass is closed."

* * *

I won't give you the fight blow by blow, partly because fights are chaos and partly because my contribution was less *heroic last stand* and more *extremely motivated nuisance*. But I'll tell you the parts that matter.

The first one swung at me and I learned two things in the same instant: they were strong, and they did not defend. Obedience doesn't duck. I got my poker into the lead one's knee and it went down without a sound—not a cry, not a grunt, nothing, and that silence frightened me more than any scream could have.

The second one caught me across the shoulder and threw me into a pine, and I tasted blood and bark, and the girl screamed—not fear, fury—and the light in her chest flared so bright the whole hillside went white. The hollowed ones staggered, hands to their faces, and I understood: the light hurt them. Of course it did. You cannot look at a thing that reminds you of everything you've lost.

"Take my hand," I shouted, and she did, and we went down the hillside together in a graceless controlled fall that I will generously call tactical, crashing through bracken while the white flare died behind us and the dark came rushing back in to fill the space.

We made the gatehouse. I barred the door, which was theater—we both knew the door would stop nothing—but some rituals hold you together, and barring a door is one of mine.

Then I turned around, and there she was, sitting on my floor, bleeding starlight onto my flagstones, looking around at nine years of my small invisible life: the ledger, the tea things, the wall where I'd scratched a mark for every year served. She looked at all of it, and then at me, and said:

"You're the keeper."

"I was," I said. "I believe I've just been made redundant."

"What's your name?"

And here is the thing I need you to understand, the thing that has kept me up every night since. I reached for my name and it was *there*, thank the darkened stars, it was there—Coral, Coral Okafor, keeper of the Thornfell gate—but reaching for it hurt. Like a bruise touched. Like something had already been chewing at the edges of me, quietly, for years, and I had been calling the missing pieces *duty*.

"Coral," I said. "Coral Okafor. And you?"

"Wren." She smiled, and it was a crooked, exhausted, gallant little smile, and the shard in her chest pulsed with it. "I fell. From up there." She pointed at the dead sky, casually, as if pointing out a cloud formation. "I don't know how many of us fell. I don't know if I'm the last. But the thing that put the lights out—it's coming for this one next." She pressed her hand flat over the glow in her chest. "It eats remembering. And I am *made* of remembering."

Outside, down in the valley, the torches regrouped. More of them now. Ten. Twelve. Patient. They knew where we were. They had always known where we were.

Wren looked at me. "You should run. You don't owe me anything. We've never met."

I looked at my ledger. Nine years of sightings, tolls, and small surrenders, all of it neat, all of it pointless, all of it feeding something I hadn't known existed. I looked at the door, and beyond it the pass, and beyond the pass the cold pouring down from a sky with no stars in it.

"No," I said. "But I owe somebody. I'm just now working out how much."

I banked the fire, gathered what was worth carrying—which was less than you'd think; exile teaches you to travel light—and took down the old keeper's cloak from its peg, the one Maren Solenne wore before me, the one I'd never had reason to wear because no one ever came and no one ever left.

"We go east," I said. "Off the roads. There's a man in the lowlands who owes the crown a great debt and hates it. If anyone knows what broke tonight, it's him."

Wren frowned. "Who?"

"A cartographer," I said, and pushed open the door onto the starless dark. "He drew maps of places that aren't supposed to exist. I confiscated one, once. I shouldn't have filed it. I should have read it."

Behind us, the first torch crested the hill.
