## 3. The Cartographer's Confession

*Coral*

The cartographer's house was the ugliest building I had ever loved on sight.

It sat crooked at the edge of a ravine, three days' hard walk from the broken gate, its walls papered inside with maps—hundreds of them, layered like feathers, some so old the ink had gone the color of dried blood. Every window was shuttered. Every surface that wasn't map was book, and every book was about places.

"Nobody's home," I said, because nine years of guarding a border nobody noticed teaches you to say things like that to wounded star-children shivering in your arms. "We're trespassing."

Wren, half-carried against my shoulder, looked up at the crooked eaves with her too-bright eyes. "There's light under the door."

"There's always light under doors. That's what doors are for." I set her down on the step, which groaned like an old man asked to kneel, and knocked twice before I could talk myself out of it.

The door opened on a chain, then all the way, revealing a man who looked like he'd been assembled from spare parts by someone in a hurry. Grey stubble. Ink-stained fingers. Eyes that went straight past me to Wren—and stopped there, the way a man stops when he sees something he drew once, long ago, in a margin he should have left blank.

"You're bleeding starlight," he said to her.

"You're standing in a doorway," she said. "Everyone's disappointing today."

He let us in.

* * *

*Wren*

His name was Idris Vane, and his house smelled of tea gone cold and ambition gone colder. He wrapped me in a blanket that smelled of cedar and old mistakes, put a cup in my hands, and then stood across the room from us like we were a fire he hadn't decided whether to warm himself at or put out.

Coral sat by the hearth cleaning their knife with the patience of someone who had done nothing else for nine years. They'd barely spoken since the gate. Since they'd broken their own law—their one law—for me. I kept waiting for them to regret it. They kept not doing it out loud, which I was learning was how Coral did most things.

"You fell," Idris said to me. Not a question.

"The sky went out," I said. "Everything falls when the sky goes out. I just fell further than most."

"And the thing in your chest?"

I put my hand over it. The shard pulsed against my palm, faint but stubborn, like a heart that refused to take hints. "A piece of the dark's opposite. It was mine before it was anything else. Now everything hungry wants it."

"Hungry," Coral repeated, without looking up from the knife. "You keep using that word."

"Because it keeps being true."

Idris crossed to a shelf, ran his inked fingers along the spines, and pulled down a roll of vellum tied with black cord. He didn't untie it. He just held it, the way you hold a letter you've read so many times you know it by the weight.

"I was royal cartographer," he said. "Twenty-two years. I mapped coastlines no ship survived to confirm and made them beautiful anyway. And eleven years ago, the crown gave me a commission unlike any other. A survey of the Thornfell pass." His mouth did something that wasn't quite a smile. "Except it wasn't a survey. It was a blueprint."

"For the wards," Coral said quietly. The knife stopped moving.

"For the cage." He untied the cord at last and spread the vellum on the table between us, weighting the corners with teacups. What bloomed across it wasn't a map of the pass as any traveler would know it. It was the pass seen from below—from underneath—as if the mountains were the lid of something. Lines of ward-work radiated inward from every direction, converging not on the border but on a single point far beyond it, drawn in ink so fine it might have been spider silk.

At the center, in the royal cartographer's own careful hand, was a tower.

"The Spire," Idris said.

* * *

*Coral*

I knew the shape before he named it. Of course I did. I'd walked beneath it every night for nine years, a smudge on the horizon I'd told myself was a watchtower, a ruin, a trick of weather. You can un-know a thing very thoroughly if your job depends on it.

"That's not on any map," I said. My voice came out flat. "I've read the surveys. There's nothing past Thornfell but wilderness."

"There's nothing on any map because I removed it." Idris's hands were steady; his voice wasn't, entirely. "Every copy. Every archive. I redrew the passes, erased the roads, wrote *impassable* across country I had personally measured to the fathom. Do you know what it does to a man, to spend his life making things true by declaring them false?" He laughed, one dry note with nothing behind it. "It makes him very good at his job. That's the horror of it. I was promoted."

Wren leaned over the vellum, and where her shadow fell across the Spire, the ink seemed to flinch. "What's in it?"

"I don't know," Idris said. "That was the other condition of the commission. We drew the cage; we weren't told the animal." He tapped the ward-lines radiating from the borders. "But I can do arithmetic. These lines don't run outward. They run in. Whatever the crown built at Thornfell, it wasn't keeping smugglers out of Sable. It was keeping something in."

The fire popped. Outside, the shutters rattled in wind that had been wrong for three days—wind with no weather behind it, blowing out of a sky with no stars in it.

"Nine years," I said. I hadn't meant to say it aloud. "I fed those wards for nine years. Small things. A voice. A face. A name. The tolls were small so no one would notice—" I stopped, because the sentence had a bottom to it and I could see the bottom now. "So no one would notice. Or so *I* wouldn't notice. If the tolls had been big, I'd have asked what they were for."

"Yes," Idris said simply. "That was the design. I helped draw that part too. The cruelty of small payments." He looked at his own inked fingers as if they belonged to someone he'd been trying not to be. "I told myself I was mapping. I told myself knowledge was neutral. Then I finished the last plate, sealed it in the royal vault, took my pay, and when the dreams started—when I began dreaming of a tower with a door in it that breathed—I resigned, moved to the edge of nowhere, and spent eleven years drinking tea and pretending the shaking in my hands was age."

"It's not age," Wren said gently.

"No," he agreed. "It isn't."

* * *

*Wren*

Here is what I understood about Idris Vane within an hour of knowing him: he had rehearsed this. Some part of him had been rehearsing it for eleven years, alone in a crooked house full of maps, waiting for the knock at the door that would finally make his guilt useful instead of merely heavy. When Coral laid the broken ward-shard on his table—a sliver of Thornfell's lattice, still humming with dead light—he picked it up like a man picking up a pen he'd sworn off.

"The lattice failed from the center outward," Coral said. "Whatever broke it, broke it from inside."

"Then it's awake," Idris said. "Or it was never asleep." He set the shard down and looked at the two of us—at the guard who'd fed the cage and the star who'd fallen out of the darkened sky—and I watched him arrive at a decision he'd clearly been circling for a decade. "There is a route to the Spire. I drew it myself, back when I still believed drawing a thing made it survivable. Through the Ashlands, along the smugglers' roads, up the mountain's blind side. No one has ever come back along it."

"Cheerful," Coral said.

"I'm being accurate. Cheerfulness is for people with better material." He was already pulling maps from the walls, rolling them, stacking them with quick ink-stained hands. "I'll guide you. Not because I'm brave—I want that on the record. I am a coward who has run out of places to hide from himself. The prison was drawn in my hand. It seems fitting the way out should be too."

Coral studied him for a long moment, the way they studied everything: like checking a lock. "Why help? Truly."

Idris paused, a rolled map in each hand, and for the first time since we'd arrived he looked directly at Coral instead of slightly to the side of them, where guilty men usually look.

"Because I erased the road," he said, "and you guarded the door, and neither of us asked why. I'd like, before I die, to ask one question properly and stay for the answer."

Outside, the wrong wind rose to a howl, and every map on every wall stirred at once, like a room full of birds deciding which way to fly. And in the silence after, from far off down the ravine, came a sound that made all three of us go still:

Footsteps. Many of them. Walking in perfect, terrible unison.
