## 5. Smuggler's Road

Here is what nobody tells you about falling out of the sky: the ground is a terrible conversationalist, and after that it's all downhill.

I'm joking. I joke when I'm frightened. It's a star thing — we burn brightest right before we go out, and I have decided to be very bright about everything until further notice.

Three days on the smuggler's roads now, and I've learned this much: Coral Okafor does not know their own name half the time, but they know every stone between Thornfell and the horizon like the stones are relatives they're fond of. We'll be walking along a ridge that looks like every other ridge in this grey country, and Coral will stop, tilt their head, and say, "There's a cache under the split rock, twenty paces east. Dried plums, probably gone to leather by now." And there will be. Dried plums, gone to leather. Idris eats them anyway and says they build character.

"How do you know that?" I asked the first time.

Coral had frowned at the question like I'd asked them how they knew how to breathe. "I don't," they said finally. "My head doesn't. But my feet do." They looked down at their own boots with something like suspicion, as if the boots were keeping secrets from them. Which, to be fair, everything was keeping secrets from Coral. The tolls had eaten nine years of their life and left the body behind like an empty coat that still remembered how to walk.

So we traveled the way Coral's body wanted: by night mostly, along roads that weren't on any map — Idris checked, twice, and got that wounded look cartographers get when geography has personally betrayed them — through gullies and goat-tracks and one memorable stretch where we walked inside a river because, according to Coral's knees, the water confused scent-hounds.

"Whose hounds?" I asked.

"Nobody's yet," Coral said. "Give it time."

It took less time than anyone wanted.

* * *

The hollowed ones came at dusk on the fourth day, and I will tell you exactly what they looked like, because someone should write it down and I appear to be the only one of us who remembers things properly anymore.

They came up the road in a line, six of them, walking in step like a single creature wearing six coats. And here is the thing that made my light-shard ache in my chest: they weren't monsters. That would have been easier. They were people. A woman with grey in her braid. A young man whose boots were too big for him, someone else's boots. An old soldier with campaign ribbons still stitched to his chest, medals for wars he could no longer name, for a country he could no longer love. Their faces were smooth the way a bed is smooth after the sleeper has gotten up and gone somewhere else entirely.

Their eyes were the worst part. Not empty — I want to be precise about this, because empty would almost be a mercy. Full. Full of nothing, which is different. Full of obedience the way a bell is full of sound just before it rings.

"They were on the road ahead of us an hour ago," Idris whispered. We'd flattened ourselves into a fold of hillside above the track, and his hands were shaking around his spyglass, which never shook, because Idris Vane believed his hands were instruments and instruments did not tremble. "Ahead of us. Walking north. How are they behind us?"

"Because the Hollow King isn't chasing us," Coral said quietly. "He's herding us." They said it the way you'd report weather. I was learning that Coral saved all their feeling for small things — the way they straightened Idris's collar without being asked, the way they always gave me the first watch because they'd noticed, somehow, that I slept better knowing dawn was mine to greet.

"Can they be reasoned with?" I asked. I know. Naive. But one of the hollowed ones had paused at a wild rosebush growing out of the roadside wall, and for a moment — one moment — her hand had risen toward the flowers, some ghost of a gesture, picking blossoms for someone, braiding them into someone's hair. Then the hand fell, and she walked on, and I understood that whatever the Hollow King ate, he left the shape of the person standing, moving, obeying. He left the house but took everyone who ever lived in it.

"No," Coral said. "That's the point of them."

We went around, wide, through the rocks, quiet as regret. And it worked, right up until it didn't.

* * *

The ambush came at the ford, and afterward Idris swore — actually swore, three words I didn't know a cartographer knew — that he'd marked that crossing as defensible on a map he drew at nineteen. The hollowed ones hadn't been following our trail. They'd been sitting on the one place we had to pass, patient as stones, because the Hollow King knows roads too, and grief taught me this: the enemy of memory is not forgetting. It's patience.

They came out of the reeds on both banks, and there was no cleverness available, no hidden path, no body-knowledge to save us. There was only the water, the packs, and the fight.

I don't remember most of it clearly. Light does things to memory, and my light was doing a great deal. When the first of them grabbed Idris, I put my hand flat against his shoulder and let the shard show itself — one pulse, white and furious, enough to drop the man like a cut marionette. It cost me. Everything costs me now. I felt myself go thinner, the way a candle goes thinner, and I sat down hard in the shallows and watched the world swim.

What I remember instead is Coral.

Coral, who had spent nine years being no one so that everyone else could keep being someone, wading into six hollowed soldiers with a quarterstaff and absolutely no plan, shouting — actually shouting, voice cracked from disuse — "Idris, the packs! Wren, don't you dare spend yourself on me!" And when one of them, the old soldier with the ribbons, caught Coral across the face and drove them into the water, Coral came up swinging, and then — this is the part I keep returning to, the part I turn over like a stone to see what lives under it — Coral hesitated. Half a heartbeat. Looking at the old man's ribboned chest. Because Coral understood, better than any of us, what he was. What he'd been emptied of. What it cost to be full of nothing.

That half-heartbeat nearly killed them. The woman with the grey braid got an arm around Coral's throat, and I burned — I spent a whole hour of my own fading just to tear her grip loose with light — and then we were running, all three of us, upstream through the shallows while the hollowed ones stood hip-deep in the water and did not pursue. They didn't need to. They'd taken what they'd come for.

They took the packs. Food, the oilskin, Idris's spare ink, the dried plums, the flint. Everything except the master map, which Idris had strapped against his ribs like a second heart, and except me, which I gathered from the way Coral kept looking back at me — counting me, the way you count coins you can't afford to lose.

* * *

We made camp that night in a hollow beneath a dead oak, cold camp, no fire, sharing a strip of smoked fish Idris produced from his boot with the sheepish air of a man revealing a minor crime. My hands wouldn't stop shaking, and it wasn't fear. I pressed my palm to my sternum and felt it: the shard, dimmer. Noticeably dimmer. Like a window at dusk when you keep meaning to light the lamp.

"Show me," Coral said.

"It's fine."

"Wren."

"It's *fine*, Coral, I'm a star, we're famously durable—" But Coral had already taken my wrist, gently, the way you handle something you've decided matters, and pulled my hand away from my chest, and in the dark I saw their face change. They'd seen it. The light under my skin, guttering.

"How long?" Coral asked.

"I don't know. Stars aren't built with hourglasses." I tried for a grin. "We're built with more of a... general sense of eventual darkness."

Neither of them laughed. Idris looked away, at the map, at anything. Coral held my wrist a moment longer, thumb resting just over my pulse, as if checking whether the rest of me was keeping pace with the light.

Then Coral did something I didn't expect. They reached into their shirt and drew out a cord, and on the cord a small wooden bead, worn smooth, and they pressed it into my hand and closed my fingers over it.

"What's this?"

"I don't know," Coral said. "I found it in my pocket the night the wards broke, and I don't know why I kept it. Some version of me thought it was important." Their voice went rough. "Some version of me had good taste in talismans. So. Hold onto it. If your light gets low, hold something that mattered to somebody. That's what my mother used to—"

Coral stopped. Stared at the middle distance, at nothing, at the hole where the rest of that sentence used to live.

"That's what my mother used to do," they finished, slowly, like a man crossing ice. "I didn't know I remembered that."

"You did," I said. "Your feet did."

And there in the dark under the dead tree, with no food and no fire and the Hollow King's patience stretched out all around us, Coral laughed — short, startled, real — and Idris muttered something about insubordinate celestial objects, and for about ten seconds we were not three fugitives on a dying road. We were something else. Something smaller and harder to kill.

I held the bead all night. In the morning, my shard was no brighter — I won't lie to you, it wasn't — but I was. Slightly. Enough.

We broke camp at grey dawn, and Coral stood looking south toward a smudge on the horizon that Idris identified, reluctantly, as Greyhollow. A city, he said, that had been on his maps. Past tense doing heavy work.

"The road through it is the only road," Coral said. "My feet say so." They flexed their hands, once, like a door testing its hinges. "And my feet also say we're being followed again. Two days behind. Maybe less."

"Then we walk faster," Idris said.

"We walk *through*," Coral corrected, and started down the slope, and we followed, because that was becoming the shape of us: Coral deciding, Idris navigating, me burning at the center of it like a small stubborn candle refusing, on principle, to notice the wind.

Behind us, far off, something began to hum — a low, tuneless note, like a bell full of nothing, ringing.

The hollowed ones were singing. Or whatever passes for song, when everything human has been eaten but the walking.
