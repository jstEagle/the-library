## 6. The City That Forgot Itself

Greyhollow announced itself the way a wound announces infection: with a smell. Woodsmoke and wet ash and something sweeter underneath, like bread left too long in a warm room, like forgetting itself going stale.

"Charming," Wren said from my shoulder, where she'd been riding for two days because her legs still shook whenever she tried to walk on ground that didn't fall. The light-shard in her chest pulsed faintly through her coat, dimmer than it had been at the river crossing. She pretended not to notice. I pretended to believe her. We were getting very good at pretending together, which is either love or denial, and I've never learned to tell them apart.

Idris stopped at the crest of the hill and unrolled his master map across his forearms even though he knew every line of it by heart. He did this when he was nervous—consulted paper for reassurance paper could no longer give him.

"Greyhollow," he said, "was a market city. Forty thousand people, give or take. Wool, tin, pilgrims passing through to the shrines in the eastern hills." His voice had the flat carefulness of a man reading an obituary he wrote himself. "The Hollow King reached it eleven years ago. Not all at once. In sips."

"Sips," I repeated.

"He's patient when he feeds. Takes a name here, a wedding day there. Enough to hollow a person without killing them. A body can keep walking long after the self has been eaten out of it." He rolled up the map. "We go in quiet, we buy supplies if anyone remembers how selling works, and we leave before dawn."

"And if someone stops us?"

"No one will stop us," Idris said. "That's rather the horror of it."

* * *

He was right, which I'm told is his most irritating quality.

The gates of Greyhollow stood open. Not broken—open, tended, oiled hinges and all, as if openness were simply what gates did and no one had ever needed a reason. A watchman sat inside the gatehouse with a spear propped against the wall like a broom. As we passed, he raised his hand in a greeting so automatic it looked rehearsed, and said:

"Welcome home."

I stopped. "Do you know me?"

He considered me with mild, empty eyes—the eyes of a man looking at weather. "Welcome home," he said again, with the exact same cadence, the exact same warmth. It wasn't a greeting. It was a groove worn into him, a ritual with its meaning scooped out like the flesh of a gourd. Somewhere under those words there had once been a sentence: *welcome back, traveler, state your business.* The business had been eaten. Only the welcome remained, going through its motions forever.

"Thank you," I said, because what else do you say to a man who has forgotten he is capable of meaning anything?

Inside, Greyhollow went about its day, or something shaped like one.

Market stalls stood open with goods arranged in neat rows. No one sold anything. Buyers drifted past, picked up loaves of bread, turned them over, set them down. A woman stood at a fountain drawing water in slow, perfect arcs—filling, pouring out, filling again—and I watched her for a full minute before I understood she wasn't doing anything. She was performing the memory of work. Her hands remembered; nothing else did.

Children played a game in a square involving chalk circles and counting rhymes, except the rhyme had holes in it. They'd skip the missing verses without stumbling, humming through the gaps, and the gaps had shapes. You could almost hear what used to live there.

"They're not unhappy," Wren whispered. She'd slid off my shoulder onto her own feet, drawn forward by some gravity of pity. "That's what's wrong with it. Look at them. They're *content*."

"They don't know enough to be otherwise," Idris said.

A man swept the same doorway over and over, each stroke identical to the last, a loop with no exit. An old woman fed pigeons that had long since stopped being hungry, scattering grain with ceremonial precision. Nobody asked who we were. Nobody looked twice. Curiosity, I realized, requires a self to be curious *from*, and Greyhollow had spent eleven years being sipped down to the dregs.

"We shouldn't linger," Idris said, but I had already seen the chapel.

* * *

It stood at the end of the market street, small and gray and stubborn, with candles burning in the windows that nobody lit and nobody would ever put out. A shrine to some saint whose name had been eaten off the plaque above the door—a smooth blank rectangle of stone where letters used to be. Inside, a dozen people knelt in the pews, praying with their lips moving and no sound coming out, or sound coming out that was only breath, the words long since devoured.

She was at the front.

I knew her before I knew why I knew her, which is the cruelest trick a memory can play—arriving in the body first, in the spine, in the throat, while the mind scrambles behind it holding an empty bag. She was maybe sixty. Gray hair pinned back with the kind of plain wooden comb that only ever belongs to women who have spent their lives making do. Broad hands, scarred knuckles. She was folding a cloth altar covering into precise thirds, unfolding it, folding it again.

My hands folded laundry exactly like that. I have known this about myself for nine years without knowing where I learned it, because I paid where I learned it to the wards, coin by coin, night by night.

"Coral," Idris said quietly behind me. "Coral, we don't know that—"

I walked down the aisle.

She looked up when my shadow crossed her. Her eyes found my face and held there, and something moved behind them—not recognition, nothing so kind as recognition. More like a fish turning under ice. A shape passing beneath a surface too thick to break.

"You," she said. Her brow creased. The folding stopped.

"Yes," I said. My voice came out wrong, high and thin. "Do you—"

"You fold corners," she said. "You fold corners first."

"I—" My throat closed. Nine years of tolls, and here was the arithmetic made flesh: I had fed my mother's voice to the gate in my first month, then her face, then her name, then the fact that she had existed at all, and the ward-tolls had gone *somewhere*, everything goes somewhere, and somewhere was here, was her, was a woman kneeling in a stolen chapel folding cloth into thirds because thirds were all that was left of her.

"What's your name?" I asked, kneeling beside her. Gently. The way you approach a bird you're afraid will startle. "Can you tell me your name?"

She smiled at me, warm and vacant, and my heart broke along a fault line I hadn't known was already there.

"I had one," she said, pleased, as if this were good news. "Everyone does. Mine's—" Her mouth worked. Her hands pressed flat against the folded cloth. "It starts soft. Like the beginning of a prayer. It starts soft and it ends like rain on a roof."

*Amara.*

I didn't say it. I couldn't. If I said it aloud she would just smile that smile, and the word would sit between us with nowhere to land, and I couldn't bear to hear my mother's name make that journey and come back homeless.

"It ends like rain," I managed.

"Yes!" Delight. Genuine delight, bright as a struck match in a dark room. "You remember it?"

"No," I said. And then, because lying to her felt like the last theft I had any right to commit: "Yes. A little."

She took my hand. Her grip was strong, working hands, and she patted it the way mothers pat, an inheritance of gesture that had survived the famine of everything else. "You should visit more," she said. "I think—I think I waited for someone. I don't know who. There's a shape in me where waiting used to be, and it fits you."

Behind me, Wren was crying silently, the light in her chest flickering with her breath.

"The tolls," I said, to no one, to Idris, to the blank saint above the altar. "Every payment went to him. Every memory I gave to keep the border hidden—he ate them. She's half-hollow because of me. I fed him pieces of her one night at a time and called it duty."

Idris put a hand on my shoulder. He had the grace not to offer comfort in words. Words, in Greyhollow, had proven unreliable.

"She kept the corners," the woman said suddenly, looking at her folded cloth. "Whoever she was, whoever I was. She always folded the corners first. I don't know why I know that."

"Because it's true," I whispered.

* * *

We left the chapel at dusk. I bought bread from a stall whose owner counted my coins three times with genuine concentration and then handed them back to me, having lost the thread of the transaction entirely, and I left the coins on the counter anyway and took the bread, and that was Greyhollow's whole economy now: gestures continuing after their reasons had died.

Wren walked beside me in silence for a long time. Then: "She's still in there."

"A shape in her where waiting used to be."

"But there. The corners are still hers." Wren touched her own chest, the shard pulsing its dim stubborn pulse. "Things that are eaten aren't gone, Coral. They're just... somewhere else. Held."

"Held by the thing wearing a dead king's crown," I said. "Along with nine years of me. Along with my mother's voice, and her face, and her name, and—" I stopped walking. The street was empty around us, candlelit windows, silent prayers. "Wren. When we get to the Spire. If there's any way to take it back—all of it, everything he's swallowed—we're not just saving the world. Do you understand? There's a woman in a chapel who ends like rain on a roof, and she's been waiting for someone, and the shape of the waiting fits me, and I am going to fill it or die on that mountain."

Wren looked at me with those starlight eyes that had fallen out of a darkened sky, and she nodded slowly.

"That's the plan, then," she said. "Not mercy. Not justice. Grocery retrieval."

Despite everything—a laugh cracked out of me, ugly and wet. "The most important errand in history."

Idris appeared at the corner ahead, moving fast, map forgotten, face pale in a way that had nothing to do with Greyhollow's ghosts. He grabbed my arm hard enough to bruise.

"Trouble," he said. "On the east road. Soldiers—but not the hollowed kind, not shambling. These ones are marching in formation, and they're carrying banners, and the banners are Sable's colors." He swallowed. "And at their head is a rider in a crown of black iron, Coral. He's not sending hunger ahead anymore. He's coming himself. And the road he's marching down leads straight through the Ashlands—to Veska."

Behind us, in the chapel, a woman folded corners into thirds, waiting for someone whose name started soft and ended like rain.
