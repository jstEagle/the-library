## 7. Bargain at the Ash Chapel

I have drawn maps of places that do not exist, and I have drawn maps of places that should not. The Ashlands are both.

From the ridge above the salt flats, the land looks like a page someone burned and then regretted—grey to every horizon, stitched with the black ribs of old roads, the wind moving across it in long slow strokes like a hand smoothing paper. Somewhere past all that grey, past the salt and the slag and the bones of a forest that burned before either of us was born, stands the Spire. My life's work. My life's shame. Drawn a hundred times in my own hand, each line a small betrayal I told myself was scholarship.

"You're doing the face," Wren said beside me.

"What face?"

"The one where you look like a man reading his own death warrant and finding the handwriting familiar."

I adjusted my satchel strap and did not dignify that with an answer, which of course was an answer. Nine days on smuggler's roads will teach you things about your companions you'd rather not know: Coral goes silent before danger the way other people go quiet before prayer, and Wren fills silence the way a crack fills with light—which is to say she cannot help it, and it is the only reason any of us are still sane.

Coral crouched at the ridge's edge, studying the flats through a cracked spyglass they'd traded two days of rations for. "Chapel's still there." They passed me the glass. "Smoke from the chimney. She's home."

*She.* Veska Marrow, queen of nothing but ash and the roads through it, keeper of every hidden way between Thornfell and the Spire's shadow. And the person Coral Okafor had betrayed so thoroughly that her name made them flinch like a struck nerve.

I'd heard the story in pieces over nine days of walking, offered up reluctantly, like coins counted out of a nearly empty purse. A job gone wrong. A promise broken. Veska's brother taken by the crown's soldiers because Coral gave up his name to buy time for something—or someone—they no longer remembered giving anything for. That was the cruelty of the tolls, I'd come to understand. Coral hadn't even been able to choose which memories to lose. The wards took what they took, and what remained was a person shaped entirely by duty, holding a guilt with no story attached to it.

"I should go alone," Coral said.

"No," said Wren.

"They have every right to—"

"No," Wren said again, with the particular stubbornness of someone who had fallen out of the sky and therefore considered herself an authority on landing badly. "You don't get to do penance alone anymore. That's not how this works now."

Coral looked at her—at the faint glow beneath her collarbone, dimmer than it had been a week ago, a candle burning down toward its wick—and something in their jaw set. "Fine. But if she tries to kill me, you two stay out of it."

"She won't try," I said. "She'll succeed. There's a difference, and it matters mostly to you."

The Ash Chapel had been a place of worship once, back when the Ashlands had a name that wasn't a warning. Now its bell tower leaned like a drunk against its neighbor, and its windows held glass in every color except holy, and its doors stood open to the grey wind. Veska's people moved among the gravestones outside—smugglers, deserters, the lost—all of them armed, none of them surprised to see us. Word traveled faster than we did. It always does.

Veska Marrow received us in the chapel's nave, seated on the altar itself, which told you everything about her theology. She was smaller than I'd imagined and older, silver threading her braids, a scar running from her jaw to her collarbone like a road on one of my maps. Her eyes found Coral first and stayed there, and the temperature of the room dropped ten degrees.

"Nine years," she said. "Nine years I wondered what the crown was paying you, and it turns out it wasn't paying you anything. You were paying *it*. Is that true, gatekeeper? All those years, feeding yourself to that border, piece by piece?"

"It's true," Coral said quietly.

"And you don't even remember what you sold me out for." It wasn't a question. Veska laughed, and there was no humor in it, only an old wound flexing. "That's the funniest thing I've heard in a decade. The crown didn't even bother to bribe you. You just *gave* yourself away."

"Veska." Coral's voice cracked on the name. "I can't give you back what I don't have. But I'm asking anyway. Passage across the Ashlands. To the Spire."

"To unbind the true king." Veska's gaze slid to Wren, and I watched her see it—the light under the girl's skin, guttering now, a star's last ember wearing a borrowed body. Something moved behind the queen's eyes. Hunger, maybe. Or arithmetic. "And you need my roads to get there. Because the flat ways are crawling with hollowed soldiers, and the high passes are watched, and every soul who's tried the open salt has come back as a puppet or not come back at all."

"We need your roads," I said. "Yes."

"I know you." Her attention swung to me, and I felt like a specimen pinned under glass. "Idris Vane. The cartographer who drew the cage and called it a kingdom. They stripped your credentials in the capital, did you know? Your name's a curse word in certain drawing rooms." She smiled without warmth. "I like that about you. Disgraced men are honest men. We've got nothing left to protect."

"I'm aware of my reputation."

"Good. Then you'll appreciate a straightforward offer." Veska rose from the altar, boots ringing on stone, and walked down the nave toward Wren. My hand found Coral's arm before I knew I'd reached for it. "Passage for three. My best guides, my quietest routes, safe to the Spire's foot. In exchange—" she stopped before Wren, close enough that the girl's fading glow lit the scar on her jaw, "—the shard."

The chapel went very quiet.

"The shard stays where it is," Coral said. Flat. Final. A door closing.

"Gatekeeper." Veska didn't look away from Wren. "That light is killing her. You've seen it. It burns a little more of her every day—I can see it from here, the way she holds herself, the way her shadow's gone thin at the edges. I have collectors who can take it clean. She lives. Maybe not long, maybe not whole, but she lives, and my people cross the Ashlands untouched, and the Spire gets its king back. Everyone profits."

"And then the stars stay dark forever," Coral said. "That light is the last piece of the sky that fell. It's not currency."

"Everything's currency. That's the first lesson of the roads." Veska finally turned to face Coral fully, and the two of them stood there like mirror images—one who had given everything away and one who had never given anything for free. "Here's what I think, gatekeeper. I think you've spent nine years learning that sacrifice is just a slower kind of spending. So spend wisely. One girl's ember, bought at the price of a kingdom's dawn. You do the sums."

"I've done them," Coral said. "No."

Wren spoke for the first time, small and steady. "What if I said yes?"

"*No,*" Coral and I said together, and then looked at each other, surprised to find ourselves allied.

"You're both deciding for me again," Wren said, and there was steel under the softness. "It's my chest it's sitting in."

"It's our sky it came from," I said. "And I have spent twenty years watching powerful people treat living things as line items. I will not stand in a chapel and watch it done to a child who fell out of heaven."

Something flickered across Veska's face—not anger. Recognition, maybe. Grief's dull cousin. "You sound like my brother," she said to Coral, unexpectedly. "Before your name cost him everything." Then the moment closed like water over a stone, and she stepped back, arms crossing. "Then we have no bargain. And you have no passage. And in four days, maybe five, that girl gutters out on my doorstep, and I'll bury whatever's left and keep the ember anyway, because it'll be mine by then. Think on it. You have until moonrise."

* * *

They gave us a cell—a storeroom behind the chapel, sacks of salt grain for beds, a single candle stub. Wren fell asleep almost immediately, exhausted the way the dying are exhausted, her breathing shallow, the glow at her throat pulsing slow as a tide going out. Coral sat against the wall, sharpening a knife they barely knew how to use, the whetstone whispering in the dark.

I could not sleep. I sat with my master map across my knees—the one Idris Vane had bled for, the one with the Spire at its heart—and I did sums of my own.

Because here is the truth I have not spoken aloud: I understood Veska's offer. Not the cruelty of it. The *arithmetic*. I have spent my whole life trading pieces of the world for lines on paper. I drew the cage around Sable and called it cartography. I watched the crown feed lies to a nation and rendered the borders neat and true. What is one ember against a kingdom? What is one girl against the dawn?

And what is Idris Vane against redemption he'll never earn honestly?

There is a route across the Ashlands that isn't on any map I've ever shown them. A dead channel under the salt flats, a smuggler's vein, passable without Veska's blessing—but only if someone opens the floodgates at the far end, alone, ahead of the group, and holds them open while the others cross. The mechanism is old crown engineering; I helped survey it, thirty years ago, when I still believed the crown surveyed things for good reasons. Whoever works the gates stands in the open, in sight of anything the Hollow King sends hunting. It is, by any honest accounting, a death. But it is also a passage that requires no one's shard, no one's daughter, no one's sky.

I could do it tonight. Slip out while they sleep, open the channel, leave the map marked, let them wake to a gift and a ghost. Coral would hate me. Wren would mourn me. And the world would be one disgraced cartographer lighter and one star-child richer, and no bargains would need striking with queens who sit on altars.

My hand was on the door latch when Coral spoke from the darkness.

"Don't."

I froze. "You're awake."

"I'm always awake. Haven't slept properly since the wards broke." The whetstone had stopped. In the candle stub's last light, Coral's eyes were open and fixed on me, and there was no anger in them, which was somehow worse. "You've been doing sums all night, Vane. I can hear them. You count when you're calculating. Little breaths, like beads."

"I don't know what you—"

"There's another way across, isn't there. One you haven't shown us. One that costs you." Coral rose, slow, joints stiff from the cold floor. "Maren told us what you were before they stripped you. The best mapmaker the crown ever had. The kind who solves problems by removing himself from the map." They crossed the room and stood over me, and I saw that their hands were shaking—not with fear. With fury, banked and old. "I fed nine years of myself to a ward because I thought it was duty. I know exactly what it looks like when someone decides they're the price. I am *not* letting you do it."

"And what's your alternative?" My voice came out harsher than I intended. "Wait for Veska's moonrise and refuse her again? Watch the girl fade on a chapel floor while you keep your hands clean? Someone pays, Coral. That's the whole architecture of this world. I've read its blueprints. I built half of them."

"Then we find a way where the payment isn't a person."

"That way doesn't exist!"

"It existed once." Coral's voice dropped, and the fury in it turned into something rawer. "The gate worked for two hundred years before the wards started eating keepers. Maren paid small things. Flowers. Songs. Names of streets. It was the crown that taught the wards to eat *people*. Your crown, Vane. Your maps. So don't tell me the world's architecture demands a body—it was *rebuilt* to demand one, by men like you, and men like you can rebuild it again."

The candle died. In the dark, neither of us moved.

"I watched you break nine years of neutrality to pull a stranger off the ground," I said finally, quieter. "I watched you stare down a queen for a girl you've known eleven days. You keep choosing people over arithmetic, and it keeps working, and I don't understand it, and I envy it, and if you die learning otherwise I will never forgive myself for following you."

"You won't be following me." Coral's hand found my shoulder in the blackness, brief and hard. "We're walking side by side or not at all. That's the deal. That's the only deal."

Outside, somewhere across the flats, a horn sounded—long, low, wrong. Not Veska's signal horns. Deeper. The kind of sound that comes out of hollow throats.

Coral went very still. "That's the second one tonight."

Through the storeroom's narrow window, out on the grey sea of salt, lights were moving. Dozens of them. In formation. Heading for the chapel.

And beneath the floor, faint but unmistakable, the old crown-machinery in the salt below began to turn—waking, as if something on the other side of the Ashlands had called it by name.
