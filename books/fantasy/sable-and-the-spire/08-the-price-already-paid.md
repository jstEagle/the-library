## 8. The Price Already Paid

The Ashlands did not deserve the name. Ash implied something had finished burning, that some fire had done its work and moved on. What lay around us was worse: a country mid-sentence, interrupted. Fields gone grey halfway to harvest. A village with its doors still open, its bread still in the ovens, and not one living soul inside—just hollowed soldiers standing in the streets like punctuation marks waiting for a sentence to resume.

"They're not chasing us anymore," Wren whispered. "They're herding."

She was right, and I hated her for being right, because I'd been telling myself for two days that our luck was holding. The light-shard under her skin had dimmed to an ember's worth of glow, and she'd stopped pretending it didn't hurt. She walked like someone carrying a bowl of water across ice.

Idris hadn't spoken since dawn. He'd been consulting his master map—the one he'd guarded through disgrace and exile and nine years of my gate-tolls going unnoticed—and every time he looked at it, his face did something complicated. I'd seen that look before, on my own face, in the moments after paying a toll. It was the look of a man doing arithmetic he already knew would come out badly.

"The pass narrows at Sorrowfen," he said finally. "One road in, one road out. If we can get through before they close the net—"

"And if we can't?" Wren asked.

He folded the map away. "Then we can't."

Maren drifted ahead of us, translucent as breath on glass, and where she passed the ash seemed to remember being soil. She'd been fading for days too—a ghost is only memory given somewhere to stand, and the Hollow King had been drinking from the same well she stood in. When she turned back to look at me, her expression was the one I'd come to think of as hers: patient, tired, and absolutely certain of what needed doing.

"Coral," she said. "When they come—and they will come—you take the map and you run. You don't stop. You don't turn around. Promise me."

"I'm not promising you anything," I said. "You sound like someone planning a funeral."

"I've been dead for forty years, dear. Everything I say sounds like that." Her smile flickered. "Promise me anyway."

I promised her nothing, which is how I learned that silence can be a promise too.

* * *

They came at dusk, when the sky over the Ashlands was the color of a bruise deciding whether to bloom. No horns, no war-cries. The hollowed soldiers simply appeared along the ridgelines, dozens of them, moving with the terrible economy of things that no longer waste anything—not fear, not hesitation, not thought. Their faces were smooth as river stones. Once, each of them had been somebody. Now they were the King's appetite wearing borrowed bodies.

"Left," Maren said sharply. "There's a cut in the rock behind the shrine. Go."

We went. The cut was barely wide enough for Wren, who was smallest, then me, then Idris—but the soldiers were faster than they should have been, and the first of them reached the mouth of the passage just as Idris turned to hold it.

What happened next took less time than it takes to tell.

A soldier lunged. Idris stepped into the blow instead of away from it—I saw him choose it, saw the arithmetic finish—and drove his knife up under the thing's jaw even as its blade opened him from hip to ribs. They went down together. Another soldier came, and Maren flared.

I have no other word for it. She *flared*, the way a dying star does, pouring out in one instant everything she'd hoarded across four decades of haunting. The passage filled with white. The soldiers screamed—not the scream of pain but the scream of things suddenly remembering, all at once, every name they'd ever lost. For a moment the cut in the rock was full of people again, weeping, reaching for faces they'd forgotten they loved.

Then the light guttered, and Maren Solenne was a smear of silver, and then she was nothing at all.

"Maren—" Wren's voice cracked.

"Don't stop," I said, and heard her voice in mine, and hated that too. "Don't turn around. That was the promise."

* * *

We made perhaps half a mile before Idris collapsed.

Wren caught him under the arms; together we dragged him into the lee of a fallen boundary stone, one of the old ward-markers, its runes long dead. His blood was everywhere, black in the failing light, and it kept coming with the calm insistence of something that had made up its mind.

"Well," he said. "That's that, then."

"Shut up," I said. "Press here. Wren, your scarf—we bind it, we get him to Greyhollow, there are healers—"

"Coral." He said my name gently, the way you speak to someone standing on a ledge. "Coral. There are no healers for this. There never were any healers for any of it. Sit down. Let an old liar do his last honest work."

I sat down. My hands wouldn't stop shaking, so I pressed them flat against the stone.

He fumbled the master map from inside his coat. Not the working copy—the true one, drawn on vellum the color of old teeth, covered in his own hand and, beneath it, in faded ink, the hand of whoever had taught him. He pushed it against my chest until I took it.

"You'll need this past the Ashlands. The Spire isn't where the crown says it is. It's where the *old* kings buried it, because they were ashamed." He coughed, wetly. "I helped draw the prison, Coral. Did I tell you that? Forty years ago, young and clever and royal, and they asked me to chart the wards, and I drew them so beautifully. Every line a lock. I told myself it was cartography. It was carpentry. I built a cage and called it a map."

"You didn't know—"

"I didn't *ask*. That's worse." He closed his eyes a moment. "Listen to me now, because I won't be able to say it twice. The door of the Spire doesn't open to force. It doesn't open to keys or blood or crowns. We built it that way, God help us, we built it so nothing could get *in* to free what's inside—or out. There's only one key. The door opens to someone willing to be wholly forgotten."

The words went through me like cold water through cloth.

"Willing to be—" I started.

"Every trace. Every name, every face, every memory anyone holds of you. You walk through that door and the world lets go of you entirely. That's the toll. Not small things. Not a mother's voice, not a summer, not a name. *Everything.*" He gripped my wrist with what strength remained, and his eyes were fierce and clear. "I spent forty years ashamed of the cage. This is the only penance I have left to give: the truth, delivered in time to matter. Don't thank me. Use it."

"There has to be another way," Wren said. Her hand glowed faintly at her sternum, ember-dim. "There's always—"

"No," Idris said. "There isn't. And listen—the King will offer you a bargain. He always offers. He's starving, not stupid. He'll offer you your memories back, all of them, every toll you paid, if you'll open the door from the outside and let him keep what he's taken. It will be the most beautiful lie you've ever heard. It will sound exactly like your mother's voice." His breath rattled. "Don't take it. Take the harder road. Take the door."

"How do you know about the bargain?" I asked.

"Because I took it," he said. "Forty years ago. A smaller version. They offered me my career back if I forgot what I'd mapped. And I said yes." He laughed, and it cost him dearly. "I've been trying to remember why I was ashamed ever since. Isn't that a joke? They let me keep the shame and took the reason. I had to draw it all again from guilt alone."

Wren was crying silently. I found I wasn't crying at all. Something in me had gone very quiet and very level, the way it used to go on long nights at the gate when the wind came down the pass and the wards hummed and I was the only person in the world who knew the border existed.

"Idris," I said. "Tell me what to do with the map."

"Follow the grey line. Not the red—the red is what I wanted to believe. The grey line goes through the drowned chapel and up the stair that isn't there unless you're already forgetting yourself." His grip loosened. "And Coral—one more thing. When you give yourself away—and you will, I know you, I've watched you feed those wards for nine years like it was prayer—when you give yourself away, someone has to keep the story. Memory doesn't die if it's loved out loud. Make them promise. Make the star-child promise."

"I promise," Wren said instantly, fiercely, tears cutting tracks through the ash on her face. "I swear it. Every dawn. Until—"

"Until she comes back down the mountain," Idris said. "Or until the story finds her. Whichever comes first." He smiled at me then, and for a moment he was not a disgraced cartographer bleeding out against a dead ward-stone; he was a young man with clean hands and a beautiful map, before the world taught him what maps could be for. "You were the best thing I ever found, Coral Okafor. And I found you by getting lost."

His hand slipped from my wrist.

The wind came down off the ridge and moved through the Ashlands, stirring the grey fields, and I sat very still while Wren bent over him, and I did not turn away, because turning away was a luxury the dead had earned and I had not.

* * *

We buried him at dawn beneath the boundary stone, marking the grave with his compass needle driven upright into the earth. Wren spoke the words she knew from wherever star-children learn such things, and I spoke the ones I knew from the gate, and between us we managed something like a funeral.

Then I unrolled the master map across my knees and found the grey line.

It began at our feet, more or less—Idris had drawn it in ink mixed, I suspected, with something of himself—and it ran west through the drowned chapel, north across the bone-flats, and ended at a small, careful symbol I recognized from no royal atlas: a circle with a single vertical stroke. A door. And beside it, in his handwriting, small enough that I had to bring the vellum close:

*It opens for the one who agrees to be no one. I am sorry. I am so sorry. Go anyway.*

Wren stood beside me, her ember-heart pulsing slow and faint against the grey morning. Behind us, far off along the ridgeline, the hollowed soldiers had resumed their patient advance—no longer hurrying, because they knew now that we had nowhere left to go except the one place their king wanted us to arrive.

"They're letting us run," Wren said quietly. "Why would he let us run?"

I looked at the little door-symbol, and at the words beneath it, and felt the answer settle over me like snowfall.

"Because he knows what the door costs," I said. "And he thinks that when we reach it, we'll choose him instead."

I rolled up the map, shouldered my pack, and turned west, toward the drowned chapel and the stair that wasn't there yet.

"Then let's go teach him what a keeper looks like," I said, "when she's done being afraid."

And we walked into the grey morning, toward the Spire, carrying everything we intended to lose.
