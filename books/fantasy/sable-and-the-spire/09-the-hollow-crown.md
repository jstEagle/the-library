## 9. The Hollow Crown

*Coral*

The Spire did not look like a prison from the outside.

I had expected something crueler — black stone, screaming gargoyles, the usual architecture of damnation. Instead it rose out of the mountain's crown like a needle of white bone, thin and patient, its summit lost in a sky that had no stars left to decorate it. It looked, if I'm honest, like a lighthouse with nothing left to warn anyone about.

"That's it?" Wren said beside me. Her voice had gone thin over the last week, the way paper goes thin when you fold it too many times. The shard in her chest was a coin of grey light now, barely enough to throw shadows. "It's smaller than I thought."

"Most terrible things are," I said. "They just get described loudly."

Idris would have laughed at that. Idris would have made some dry remark about cartographic exaggeration, about how every map he'd ever drawn made the Spire taller because fear adds a hundred feet at least. But Idris was two days behind us and a mile deep under cairn stones we'd piled with our own hands, and Maren was nowhere at all now, burned down to a last warm smell of cedar on the wind. So it was just Wren and me and the mountain, and the mountain didn't joke.

We climbed.

The stairs inside the Spire were worn hollow in their centers, which told me everything before I knew anything. Not the wear of soldiers marching. The wear of one pair of feet, going up and up and up, for years, for decades, for however long it takes to polish stone into a confession. Whoever lived here had walked this same path so many times they'd carved themselves a groove, the way water carves a riverbed, the way grief carves a face.

Halfway up, I found a tally scratched into the wall. Little marks, thousands of them, grouped in fives. I put my fingers against them and felt something I couldn't name yet — a kinship so sharp it was nearly nausea.

"Coral." Wren touched my sleeve. "The tolls."

"I know."

"You paid nine years of them. He paid—" She looked at the wall and couldn't finish.

"He paid longer," I said. "Come on."

* * *

*Wren*

The top of the Spire was a room with no roof, open to the dead sky, and in the middle of it stood a man.

That was the first wrongness. I had spent the whole climb imagining a monster — something vast and shadowed, all hunger and no edges, the way the smugglers' stories painted him. The Hollow King who ate memories. The thing in Sable's crown. I'd built him out of nightmare parts: teeth, darkness, a voice like a landslide.

He was not that. He was a man, tall and stooped, wearing a crown that had gone dull as old tin, and his robes were patched — actually *patched*, sewn by hand with clumsy stitches, the way you mend things when there is no one else to mend them for you. He was looking out at the starless sky the way you look at a grave.

When he turned around, his face was ordinary. Tired. Lined around the eyes. And his eyes —

His eyes were empty the way a house is empty after the family has gone. Not dark. Just vacant, swept clean, waiting for furniture that was never coming back.

"You climbed my stairs," he said. His voice was soft. Rusty, like a hinge unused for years. "No one has climbed my stairs in a very long time. They used to send me reports. Then they stopped sending reports. Then I stopped remembering what reports were for." He tilted his head at Coral. "You're the gatekeeper. Thornfell. You smell like wards. You smell like *toll*."

Coral went very still beside me. I could feel the heat coming off them, the fury they'd been carrying since Greyhollow, since the woman who almost remembered being their mother. I braced for the shouting to start.

Instead Coral said, quietly: "How long?"

"How long what?"

"How long have you been paying?"

The Hollow King smiled, and it was the saddest thing I had ever seen, sadder than falling, sadder than the dark where my sisters used to be. "I don't remember," he said. "That was rather the point."

* * *

*Coral*

Here is what I expected to feel, standing in front of the creature that had eaten my mother's voice and my father's hands and the name of my home village and the taste of the bread I grew up on: hatred. Clean, simple, righteous hatred, the kind you can hold in both hands like a weapon.

What I felt instead was recognition, and it was worse.

Because I knew those patched robes. I knew that stoop. I had seen it in my own reflection for nine years — the lean of someone carrying weight they've forgotten the shape of. I knew the tally marks on the stairs because I kept tallies too, little notches in the gatehouse doorframe, one for every night survived, until I stopped counting because the counting hurt. He had counted longer. He had counted until the numbers were all he was.

"You were a keeper," I said. "Before. Like me."

"I was—" His empty eyes flickered, hunting. "There was a door. There was always a door. Something on the other side of it that wanted out, that wanted *through*, and someone had to stand in front of it. Someone had to feed the wards." His hand rose to the dull crown, touched it like an old wound. "The tolls were small at first. A summer afternoon. The color of a friend's coat. Small things. Small things are easy to give away because you tell yourself they weren't worth much anyway." He laughed, a dry papery sound. "And then one day I turned around to remember why I was guarding the door, and there was nothing there. No reason. No door. Just me, and the hunger, and the crown, and the understanding — arriving far too late — that the wards had never been keeping the hunger *out*."

"They were keeping it in you," I said.

"In me." He said it gently, like a diagnosis. "Every toll feeds the ward and the ward feeds the cage and the cage is me. I have been eating myself for so long that there is almost nothing left, and what's left is only appetite wearing the shape of a duty it can't recall. Do you understand? I am not the jailer. I am not even the prisoner. I am the *wall*." He spread his arms, and the patched sleeves hung off them like flags of a surrendered country. "And walls are hungry, gatekeeper. Walls take. That's all they know how to do anymore."

Behind him, at the edge of the roofless chamber, I saw it: the door. Old iron, unmarked, humming faintly with the last threads of the wards — the true wards, the first ones, the ones he'd fed for centuries. Whatever was behind it had gone quiet long ago. Or patient.

"You could stop," I said. "Put the crown down. Walk away."

"Walk where?" And there it was, the question I had asked myself a thousand nights at Thornfell, alone with the wind and the wards and the slow leak of myself. "Everything I might have walked toward, I ate. There is no *away*, child. There is only the next toll, and the next, and the hope that if I grow full enough I'll finally remember something worth keeping." His gaze moved past me, to Wren, and the emptiness in his eyes sharpened into want, and I saw the monster after all — not in his shape but in his attention, the way a starving man looks at bread. "But she brought me something," he breathed. "A piece of the sky itself. Oh, that would buy me *years*. That would buy me—"

"No," Wren said.

* * *

*Wren*

Coral stepped between us before I finished the word.

I had watched Coral do brave things for weeks now — break nine years of neutrality for a stranger, walk into Greyhollow knowing what waited there, refuse Veska's bargain with a queen's calm. But this was different. This time Coral wasn't standing in front of danger.

They were standing in front of a starving old man the way you stand in front of a fire you intend to walk into.

"Take mine instead," Coral said.

My heart — the human-shaped one, the one I grew on the way down — stopped. "Coral, no. Your memories are *mine too* now. I carry them. The story—"

"The story keeps," Coral said, without turning around. Their voice was steady in the way that ice is steady, the way a bridge is steady right up until it isn't. "Wren. Listen to me. He doesn't need light. Light is just fuel. What he needs — what he's been starving for since before my grandmother was born — is a reason. A self. Something whole enough to remind him why anyone guards anything." They took a step toward the Hollow King, and I swear the air bent toward them, the way air bends toward an open window. "I have nine years of tolls sitting in these wards. My mother's voice. My father's hands. My village, my childhood, my name-day, my first snow. All of it, still in here, still *his*, hoarded like a miser's gold because he can't digest what he won't understand." Another step. "Give it back to him. All of it. And then give him the rest of me too — everything I have left. Let him remember being a keeper by remembering me."

"And what's left of you?" I demanded, and my voice cracked like a falling star.

Coral finally looked back at me, and they were smiling, and it was the worst kindness I have ever witnessed.

"A stranger," they said. "Coming down a mountain someday. With familiar eyes."

* * *

*Coral*

The Hollow King had gone very quiet.

"You would do that," he said. Not a question. An astonishment. "You would give yourself to the thing that robbed you."

"You're not a thing," I said. "You're what I'd be in another hundred years. I've seen the road, and it goes through you, and I am done pretending there's any version of this where I keep myself and everyone else loses." I held out my hand. "So here's the trade. The oldest trade. The one the wards were always asking for, except honest this time. Everything. Not a memory at a time. All of it, at once, freely given — and freely given means it *nourishes*, old man. Stolen bread fills the belly; shared bread fills the soul. Take all of me and maybe, maybe, you'll be full enough to stop being hungry. Full enough to remember the door. Full enough to open it, or seal it, or whatever a whole keeper decides a door deserves."

He stared at my hand. His own rose, trembling, patched sleeve slipping back off a wrist thin as kindling.

"This will unmake you," he warned. "Not death. Death leaves bones. This leaves nothing."

"It leaves a story," Wren said fiercely, at my shoulder. She pressed her grey little shard of light against my chest, and warmth bloomed there like dawn. "I'll make sure of it. Every morning. Until the world runs out of mornings or you come walking down that mountain, do you hear me? *Both.*"

"I hear you," I said.

I turned back to the Hollow King, and I gave him the last thing I owned, which was also the first thing I ever owned:

I remembered him. On purpose. Completely. Coral Okafor, keeper of Thornfell, daughter of a voice I could no longer hear and hands I could no longer see — I gathered every scrap of myself, the wry and the weary and the stubborn and the scared, and I offered it up like bread to a beggar king.

"Remember," I told him, as I came apart into light. "Someone has to. It might as well be you."

* * *

*Wren*

The light went through the Spire like sunrise through water.

I felt Coral go — not die, nothing so merciful as an ending, just *unfasten*, thread by thread, pouring out of the world and into him. The Hollow King fell to his knees in the flood of it, and I watched nine years of stolen tolls return to him and catch fire: a mother's voice singing in a language he'd never learned, a father's scarred hands braiding a rope, a village named Okafor-after-the-river, a first snow tasted on the tongue of a child who grew up to guard a gate. And then Coral themselves, all of it, the whole bright stubborn architecture of them, settling into that hollowed-out man like furniture into an empty house.

He wept. Kings aren't supposed to, but then, he had never really been a king.

When it was done, he knelt in the center of the roofless chamber wearing a crown that shone again — not gold, not silver, just clean, like something washed — and he looked at his own hands and said, in a voice that was half his and half one I'd only heard in stories:

"Maren Solenne kept the first watch. I keep the second. And I remember—" his breath caught, "—I remember *why*."

Behind him, the old iron door glowed once, softly, and went still. Sealed. Or satisfied. Even now I'm not sure there's a difference.

Then the sky caught.

It started above the Spire — one star, then ten, then a thousand, reigniting like a city lighting its lamps at dusk, and the light poured down the mountain and across the Ashlands and over Greyhollow, where I promise you people stopped in the streets and suddenly, wonderingly, said their own names out loud.

I should have risen. That was the plan. The shard in my chest blazed white and my sisters were calling, the whole lit sky calling, and I got as far as spreading my arms.

Then I looked down the mountain, at the long road, at the world where a stranger with familiar eyes might come walking any day now, any year, any century — and I folded my wings and sat down on the Spire's top step to wait.

"Story's not finished," I told the stars, and they, being newly generous, let me stay.

Below me, dawn was coming up over Thornfell, and somewhere on that road, someone was about to hear how it all began.
