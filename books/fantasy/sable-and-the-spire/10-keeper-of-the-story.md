## 10. Keeper of the Story

Here is what nobody tells you about watching a god remember himself: it is not loud.

I had expected thunder. I had expected the Spire to crack like an egg, for the mountain to shrug off its crown of shadow, for some great bell of light to toll across the Ashlands and announce that the long hunger was over. I am a star-child. We are dramatic people. We fall in fire and we rise in glory, and we assume everything else does too.

But when Coral Okafor gave away the last of themselves—every name they'd kept, every scar they'd earned, every small stubborn kindness they'd hoarded through nine years of invisible service—the Hollow King simply *stopped*. The way a fever breaks. The way a held breath finally lets go.

He sat down on the summit steps, this vast gaunt thing wearing a dead man's crown, and he put his face in his hands, and he began to cry.

And the stars came back.

Not all at once. First one, high over the eastern ridge—a green-white point of light that made my chest-shard sing so hard my teeth ached. Then three more. Then a handful scattered like spilled salt, then dozens, then the whole spilled treasury of the sky pouring back into its vaults, and every star that reignited was a memory returning to the world, because that is what stars are, up close: memories with the courage to burn.

I know because I'm one. Past tense. Mostly.

The Hollow King lifted his head, and under all that hollow there was a face. Old. Tired. A keeper's face—I recognized the shape of it, the same shape Coral's had worn at the gate, the same shape Maren Solenne's ghost had worn before she burned out holding the line in the Ashlands. Guardians, all of us. We keep things. Sometimes we forget we were supposed to be among them.

"Who paid?" he asked, and his voice was a man's voice, cracked and ordinary. "Who paid the price?"

"Coral," I said. "Coral Okafor."

He closed his eyes. "Okafor," he repeated, tasting it. "That was a good name." And then, softer, to himself or to the mountain or to whatever waits between stars: "I will carry it. I swear it on the crown. I will carry it until it can be given back."

Which is how I learned that even a king who ate nine years of a person cannot digest them entirely. Somewhere inside him, folded up small and safe as a seed, was everything Coral had ever been. Waiting.

* * *

I rose toward home that night. Of course I did. My chest was full of light again, my sisters were singing down their gravity-wells, and the whole sky had reopened like a road after flood. It would have been madness to stay. It would have been ungrateful.

I got as far as the tree line below the pass.

Here is the other thing nobody tells you about falling out of the sky: you land somewhere, and the somewhere gets into you. I had landed in snow on the worst night of the world's worst decade, bleeding starlight, hunted by men with hollow eyes—and a stranger had broken nine years of perfect neutrality to drag me out of the dark. Coral hadn't known me. Hadn't owed me anything. They'd just looked at a dying light and refused to let it go out.

You don't forget that. Apparently not even if you're built of forgetting-proof stuff.

So there I stood at the tree line, half-lifted off the ground by my own rising, looking back down at Thornfell pass where the gate stood healed and humming, and I thought about dawns. About how many dawns Coral had watched alone from that gate, paying pieces of themselves into the wards so no one would ever notice the border existed. Nine years of dawns, spent on us. On smugglers who never thanked them. On a kingdom that never knew.

Somebody should watch the dawns now. Somebody should tell the story. And somebody should be standing at the bottom of that mountain when—if—the stranger with familiar eyes comes walking down.

I set my feet back on the ground. It felt like landing twice.

"Stubborn," said Veska, behind me.

I nearly went straight up again, dignity abandoned. She was leaning against a pine with her arms crossed, wearing the expression she wore instead of apology—which is to say, none at all, just a flat assessing stare that had once made grown smugglers confess to crimes they hadn't committed.

"You're supposed to be gone," I said. "Everyone saw your company ride south."

"My company rode south." She pushed off the tree. "I didn't. I wanted to see the sky come back from underneath. Seemed only fair, since I helped put it out." She glanced up at the rekindled stars with something that wasn't quite softness but was adjacent to it, the way a knife is adjacent to a gift. "You're staying."

It wasn't a question. Veska doesn't ask questions; she states conclusions and dares you to file objections.

"I'm staying," I agreed.

"The gate needs a keeper. Coral's gone, Maren's gone, and the old king—" she nodded up the mountain, where a faint warm light marked the Spire's summit, "—has enough penance to do without adding sentry duty. The wards are healed but they're young. Young wards need feeding."

My stomach dropped. "Feeding how? Because if anyone thinks I'm paying memory-tolls—"

"Not memories." She smiled, and it was almost kind, which from Veska is terrifying. "That was the old design. Cruelty dressed up as necessity. The new gate runs on something else. Coral's last gift changed the architecture." She turned and started walking toward the pass. "Come see."

* * *

The gate had been rebuilt—not by hands, exactly, but by remembering. Where the old wards had been a wall of forgetting, the new ones were a lattice of story. I felt it the moment I stepped through: a warmth, a recognition, a sense of being *known*. The gate didn't erase travelers anymore. It welcomed them, and it remembered them, and somehow that was a far stronger lock than any forgetting had ever been. You can slip past a thing that doesn't notice you. You cannot slip past a thing that knows your name.

At the center of the archway, where the toll-stone used to sit, there was a lantern.

It wasn't burning—or rather, it was burning with something that wasn't fire. A soft gold glow, steady as a heartbeat, and when I leaned close I heard it. Faintly. Underneath the hum of the wards.

A voice. Telling a story.

"—and so the keeper broke nine years of silence," the lantern was saying, in a voice like warm stone, "because some lights you do not let go out—"

"That's—" My throat closed.

"Coral's voice," Veska said. "What was left of it. The gate caught it. Or maybe Coral left it on purpose—last coin in the purse, spent on the door." She shrugged, elaborately casual, which is how she handles feelings. "Every dawn, the wards drink a little of that telling. That's the toll now. A story, told aloud, at first light. Keeps the lattice fed. Keeps the border kind."

"And if the story ends?"

"Then someone starts it again." She looked at me sideways. "That's the job, star-child. Not sacrifice. Repetition. You think you're up to it?"

I thought about my sisters in the sky, singing their ancient slow songs. I thought about how they'd welcome me home, and how the welcome would eventually become routine, and how I would spend eternity being a beautiful point of light that no one below ever thanked.

Then I thought about dawn coming up over Thornfell pass, and me standing at the gate with Coral's voice in a lantern, saying the words so the wards could eat them, so the border could stay gentle, so that someday—

Someday.

"I'm up to it," I said.

* * *

Veska's company came back north in the spring. Reformed, she called it, though most of them still walked like smugglers and negotiated like pirates. But they took the gate-watch seriously, in their way. They fixed the keeper's cottage roof. They planted a garden that Kess swore would fail and then quietly tended every night so it wouldn't. They left offerings at Maren's cairn in the Ashlands and at the roadside grave where we buried Idris Vane with his master map rolled beneath his crossed hands—the map that had led us here, drawn by a guilty man, finished by a dying one, redeemed by everyone who walked it after him.

And every morning, before the sun cleared the eastern ridge, I climbed to the gate and lit the lantern and told the story.

I told it in Coral's cadence when I could manage it—dry, wry, a little tired, secretly tender. I told about the night the stars went out and the wards shattered like glass. About the wounded star-child who crashed through the broken gate trailing light and blood. About a cartographer's forbidden maps and a ghost's terrible truth and a smuggler-queen whose heart turned out to be buried deeper than anyone guessed. About Greyhollow, and the woman who almost remembered being Coral's mother, and how Coral had held her hand and said, *it's all right, you don't have to know me to be kind.*

About the summit. About the starving guardian and the choice that wasn't a battle. About a person who gave away their whole self so that someone else could have theirs back.

The wards drank it in. The lantern glowed. The border stayed kind.

Travelers began to stop, eventually. Word spread of the gate where the keeper tells stories at dawn, and pilgrims and peddlers and the occasional lost noble would gather in the grey light to listen. Some of them wept. Some argued about whether it was true. One old woman swore she'd known a Coral Okafor once, years ago, at a market in the low country—a person who'd haggled fiercely and overpaid anyway and remembered everyone's children's names.

"Tell me more," she said, gripping my wrist with surprising strength. "Tell me everything. Details matter. If a person is going to be found again, the details are how you'll know them."

So I added details. The way Coral logged sightings in a ledger with cramped, slanted handwriting. How they took their tea bitter enough to strip paint. How they talked to the gate itself, good-morning and good-night, nine years of politeness to a wall. Every detail a hook cast into the wide water of the world, waiting.

* * *

This morning was the four hundred and twelfth dawn. Frost on the wardstones, breath steaming, the lantern lit, the story begun. I was at the part about the ambush in the Ashlands—Wren's favorite part, Wren being me, and yes, I admit I tell myself generously—when I heard footsteps on the mountain path.

Not traveler's footsteps. Travelers come up the road, weary and shuffling. These came *down*, deliberate, unhurried, with the particular rhythm of someone who has walked a long way and knows exactly where their feet are.

I stopped mid-sentence. The lantern flared—just slightly, just enough—and the wards hummed a note I had never heard them hum before. Recognition. The deep kind. The kind that doesn't come from story.

The figure came around the last bend into the grey-gold light, and the frost caught fire with sunrise, and I could not breathe.

They stopped at the edge of the gate's shadow. A stranger's face. Familiar eyes.

"Sorry," they said, in a voice like warm stone, rough with disuse and distance. "I heard a story. I've been hearing it for a long time, I think, from very far away. I couldn't tell you my name." They frowned, touching their own chest as though checking for something. "But I appear to have arrived at the ending. Would you—would you start from the beginning?"

The lantern blazed like a small sun.

I wiped my face with my sleeve, laughed, and opened the ledger to the first page.

"Yes," I said. "Yes. From the beginning."
