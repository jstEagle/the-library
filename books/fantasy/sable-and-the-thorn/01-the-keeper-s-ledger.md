## 1. The Keeper's Ledger

The last light left the pass at the hour Ada Okafor called the turning — not dusk, exactly, and not night, but that thin gray seam where Thornfell stopped belonging to anyone. She had learned to read it the way other people read weather. When the seam closed, the Sable Gate belonged to her.

She stood at the high window of the gatehouse with a cup of tea gone cold in one hand and the ledger open on the sill beside her, and she watched the mountain take itself back. Shadow climbed the western crags first, then poured down the scree like water finding its level, then swallowed the road in one long breath. Somewhere below, past the third switchback, a mule train would be lighting no lamps, because the smugglers who used the low road knew better than to advertise themselves to her. The pilgrims on the high path would be lighting all of theirs, because they wanted to be seen by something, though not by her.

Ada drank the cold tea anyway. It was bitter, oversteeped, the way she made it every evening without ever once deciding to. Routine was a kind of architecture. You built it around yourself and eventually you could live inside it.

"Ledger," she said aloud, to the empty room, because saying it aloud made it official. Ten years of nights, and she still said it aloud. Her father had said it before her. His mother before him, presumably, though the records from that far back were more legend than ink.

The ledger itself was a heavy thing, bound in black leather gone soft as cloth at the corners, its spine cracked into a fan of pale ridges. Three generations of Okafors had filled its predecessors; this one was hers alone, begun the winter she took the gate at twenty-three, when the old keeper — her father, Aldric — had walked out onto the pass in a snowstorm and not walked back. The Crown had sent a man to ask questions about that. Ada had answered every one truthfully, which was the easiest way to make an inquisitor lose interest: give them nothing but facts, arranged so neatly they read like a wall.

She opened to tonight's page and dipped her pen.

*Fifth night of the waning moon. Wind north-northwest, light. Pass dry. Visibility good.*

Then she waited for the gate to tell her what it knew.

People thought the Sable Gate was a wall, because walls were easier to understand. It wasn't. It was a threshold — a span of black stone arching the narrowest point of the pass, older than any kingdom on either side of it, set with a keystone carved in shapes no living stonemason could copy. The covenant said the gate divided the human realm from the Emberlands, where the dragons kept their fire and their own affairs. In practice, the gate did something stranger. It *noticed*. Anything crossing the threshold passed through a field of attention older than language, and the gate remembered, and Ada — as Keeper, as the last of a line sworn to the keeping — could read what it remembered, if she sat very still and let her hands rest flat on the ledger's page.

It was not magic the way the ballads described magic. No glow, no thunder. Just a pressure behind her eyes, a warmth in the stone under her palms, and then knowledge arriving the way rain arrives — not all at once, but everywhere at once, until you realized you were wet.

Tonight the gate gave her three crossings.

The first came through at half past the turning: two pilgrims, upward-bound toward the Emberlands' edge shrines, a mother and her grown son by the weight of their footfalls and the small domestic rhythm of their breathing. They carried candle-offerings, tallow, cheap. They paused at the keystone and the son touched it, quickly, the way you touch a thing you've been told is holy but have never felt anything from. Ada noted them. *Two, high road, offerings tallow, devotional, no toll.* Pilgrims paid no toll. That was covenant law, and covenant law was the only law out here that everyone — smuggler, soldier, priest, dragon — still pretended to honor.

The second crossing was the interesting one, and it arrived, as it always did, on the low road, in the dark, unlit, quiet as intent.

Ryn Vasq.

She didn't need the gate to tell her it was him. She'd know his tread anywhere now — that particular rolling gait of a man who has spent his life walking loaded, weight settled low, ready to drop or run. Fourteen years he'd been coming through her pass, since he was a boy of seventeen running bolts of untaxed silk for his uncle, and in fourteen years he had never once tried to lie to her, which she had come to regard as the single most trustworthy thing about him.

He crossed at the third hour. Six mules. She counted them by sound alone before the gate confirmed it: six, one favoring its near hind shoe, the farrier's work overdue. He'd pay for that too, or rather, he wouldn't, and she'd note it, and next month he'd show up with the same lame mule and a grin and some story about how the farrier in Lowmark charged thieves' rates.

Toll goods, per the gate's reading: salt, iron nails, dried fish, and — wrapped in oilcloth under the fish, because Ryn always hid the interesting cargo beneath the boring cargo — forty pounds of raw amber resin, Emberlands-cut, which was contraband twice over. Once for the tax. Once because amber resin was dragon-touched, and dragon-touched goods moving into the human realm without writ were the kind of thing that got keepers replaced and borders redrawn.

Ada wrote it down. All of it. The mule, the resin, the hour, even the fact that he'd paused at the keystone for eleven seconds longer than usual, which was new, and which she filed away in the margin with a small mark she used for things that might mean nothing or might mean everything.

She had never seized his cargo. Not once in fourteen years. This was the arrangement nobody had ever written down: Ryn Vasq moved his goods through the Sable Gate, declared honestly through channels only the two of them could hear, paid a toll calibrated to what he could afford rather than what the law demanded, and in exchange he told Ada things. What moved on the roads south. Which villages were hungry. Which of the border lords was raising soldiers under the cover of a harvest festival. Whether the fires in the Emberlands looked right this season, seen from the low ridges where a smuggler's eyes went places a keeper's never could.

In ten years as Keeper, Ada had never requested soldiers from the Crown. She had never needed them. She had Ryn Vasq, and a ledger, and a gate that remembered everything, and the particular authority of a woman who knows exactly what crosses her line and has decided, with full deliberation, what to do about each item on the list.

Some nights she wondered if that made her the most honest criminal in Thornfell.

Most nights she didn't wonder at all. There wasn't time.

* * *

The third crossing came near dawn, and it was wrong.

Not dramatic-wrong. Nothing flared, nothing screamed. The gate didn't work that way, and in ten years Ada had built her whole understanding of the world on the assumption that it never would. But the reading came through muddy, blurred at the edges, like ink stirred with a finger. A single traveler, downward-bound, out of the Emberlands. An Emberfolk trader, by the build and the gait — one of the scaled-folk who lived in the fire-shadow of the great drakes, the ones who traded at the border markets in the old days before the markets thinned. And yet the gate's memory of him was somehow *incomplete*, as though the stone had seen him pass but couldn't quite hold the shape of him.

That had never happened. In three generations of ledgers, there was no entry like it. Ada checked. She pulled the older volumes off the shelf — her father's hand, then her grandmother's, both slanting and sure — and she sat with them until her tea went cold a second time, comparing crossings, looking for precedent. There was none.

She wrote it down anyway, exactly as received, and marked it with the double-ring symbol she reserved for anomalies, and beneath it, because she was her father's daughter and honesty was the family trade, she added a note in her own small tight script:

*Nature of anomaly unknown. Gate's attention imperfect. Possible causes: fatigue (mine), instrument drift (the gate's), or change in the thing itself. To be watched.*

To be watched. That was the whole job, distilled. Everything else — the tolls, the ledgers, the careful mercies, the tolerated smuggler — was just the shape the watching took.

She closed the ledger as the sun came up over the eastern shoulders of Thornfell and lit the pass gold, and for a moment the Sable Gate stood revealed in full morning light, black stone drinking the sun without giving any of it back, beautiful the way a blade is beautiful. Ada looked at it the way she looked at it every morning: with the specific, exhausted tenderness of a woman greeting the only companion she had chosen and would ever choose.

Then she banked the fire, hung the ledger on its peg, and went down to meet the day's honest traffic — the carter with apples, the letter-carrier, whatever the daylight world thought the pass was for.

* * *

Midmorning brought the peddler.

He came up the high road on foot, which was already unusual — the high road was pilgrim ground, and peddlers took the low road or no road at all. He was old, gray-skinned with altitude and age, driving a handcart with one wheel that sang on every revolution, a thin metallic complaint that carried half a mile. Ada heard him coming while she was still arguing with the carter about axle-grease prices, and she finished the argument first, because a keeper who abandons business mid-haggle teaches the whole pass that her attention can be bought with noise.

Then she turned to the peddler, and saw the state of him, and forgot the carter entirely.

He was dying. There was no polite word for it. The gray went deeper than skin — it was in the way he held his chest, in the wet rattle underneath his words, in the careful economy of a man spending his last strength on a hill he should never have climbed. Sweat stood on his forehead in the cold air. His eyes had the fixed brightness of fever.

"Keeper Okafor," he said, and his voice was a ruin. "They said at the shrine below — they said you'd be here."

"They said correctly." She crossed to him. "Sit. There's water."

"I'll stand." He gripped the cart's handle like a rail. "I don't sit, these days. Sitting's a habit I'm trying to break." A wet laugh escaped him, terrible and bright. "I have goods to sell, Keeper. Only one customer in mind."

"I don't buy on the road. House rules."

"I know your rules." He knew a great many things, she was beginning to suspect. "This isn't a sale you can refuse on the road. This is a sale you have to climb a mountain to refuse." He fumbled at the cart with shaking hands, and Ada — watching, wary, her hand nowhere near her knife but aware of it the way she was aware of weather — saw him unwrap a bundle wrapped in waxed linen wrapped in oilcloth, three layers deep, the wrapping of something someone had very much wanted to survive the journey.

A book.

Old. Scorched along one entire side, the leather bubbled and blackened, pages fused at the corner into a single charred wedge. The surviving cover was dark green, stamped with a device she didn't recognize — a circle broken by a vertical stroke, worn nearly smooth. The book was heavy for its size. Books that heavy were either full of metal fittings or full of importance.

"What is it?"

"That," the peddler said, "is the question I've been carrying up this mountain for nine days." He wiped his mouth with the back of his wrist. "I bought it in Lowmark, off a debt-claim, a dead scholar's effects. I buy dead men's libraries, it's honest work, mostly. This one came with a note in the lot. Written instructions. *Deliver to the Keeper of the Sable Gate. Tell her to look at the fifth month, the ninth day.*"

Ada went very still.

"Who wrote the note?"

"The dead scholar, presumably. Or whoever killed him. Or—" he coughed, long and wet, and steadied himself against the cart, "—or the book itself, if you believe that sort of thing, which after nine days on the road with it, Keeper, I am beginning to."

"Why me? Why this gate?"

"Because of what's in it." His fever-bright eyes fixed on her. "I looked. Of course I looked, I'm a peddler, looking is my whole profession. Most of it's prophecy — old verse, ember-language mixed with ours, the kind of thing the Crown burns men for owning. But there are pages in the middle, plain-handed, recent-ish, a hundred years old maybe. Notes. And those notes mention a name."

He let that hang in the thin cold air between them.

"They mention *Okafor*."

The wind moved through the pass. The cart wheel sang its one thin note. Ada became aware, distantly, that the carter had gone, that the letter-carrier had gone, that the high road stood empty in both directions, and that she and a dying man and a burned book were the only things on the mountain.

"Price," she said quietly.

"For a dying man?" He almost smiled. "Warmth. A bed for however many nights I've got. Someone to bury me facing east, when it comes to it. And one promise, Keeper, and it's the only term I'll insist on." He pushed the book across the cart's rail toward her with both trembling hands, an offering, a surrender, a warning. "You look at the fifth month, the ninth day. Tonight. Before you sleep. Because whatever's circled on that page, it's circled in ink a century old, and ink doesn't lie about its own age, and—"

His voice cracked apart into coughing. When he surfaced again, he was smaller, grayer, and utterly certain.

"—and the name written next to it, in that same century-old ink, is yours."

* * *

She carried the book inside. She made the peddler eat — soup, bread, the heel of the good cheese she saved for days she expected bad news — and installed him in the gatehouse's second room, the guest cell that hadn't held a guest in six years, and told him the healer from the valley village would come up tomorrow whether he liked it or not. He said he liked her already, for lying about tomorrow. Then he slept, rattling, alive, and Ada sat down alone at her table with the scorched book and a lamp and the ledger hanging on its peg behind her, and she did the thing she had done every night for ten years, and the thing she had never once done before.

She opened someone else's book.

The verse pages were beautiful and unreadable — Old Emberish, mostly, the flowing angular script of the dragon-courts, interleaved with human glosses in a dozen dead hands. She could pick out fragments. *Fire remembers. Stone forgets. The daughter counts the cost.* Prophecy-language, circular, self-satisfied, the kind of text that meant anything and therefore nothing, until it meant one specific thing at one specific time.

The notes began a third of the way in. Plain hand, plain ink, a scholar's tidy mind recording something urgent. Dates. Observations. And then, on a page near the middle, a calendar table, and in that table, a date.

The fifth month. The ninth day.

Circled. Circled twice, the second circle pressed hard enough to groove the paper, the hand that drew it steady and deliberate and afraid. Beside the circle, in the same brown century-old ink, a single word in the human alphabet, small and clear:

*Okafor.*

Ada sat without moving for a long time. The lamp burned. The gate stood silent outside, black stone in starlight, remembering everything as it always had, asking nothing, promising nothing, holding the pass the way it had held it for a thousand years and would hold it, she had always believed, for a thousand more.

Tomorrow was the fourth of the month.

She rose, took down the ledger, opened it to tonight's page, and added a final entry in her smallest, steadiest hand:

*Anomaly noted. Book received. Date circled: five days hence. Name given: mine. No action determined. To be watched.*

She hung the ledger on its peg. She banked the lamp. And she lay down in her bed in the gatehouse above the Sable Gate, in the room where three generations of Okafors had slept watchfully, and for the first time in ten years, Ada Okafor lay awake, listening to the wind move through a pass that had five days left to be ordinary.
