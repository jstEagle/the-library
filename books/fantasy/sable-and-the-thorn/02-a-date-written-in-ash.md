## 2. A Date Written in Ash

The fourth of the month came up gray and cold, the way most days did in Thornfell, and Ada Okafor rose before dawn because she had always risen before dawn and because sleep had abandoned her somewhere around the second bell anyway.

She lit the lamp. She took down the ledger. She read her own entry three times, as if the words might rearrange themselves overnight into something less impossible.

*Anomaly noted. Book received. Date circled: five days hence. Name given: mine.*

They hadn't rearranged themselves.

She put the ledger back on its peg and went down to the strongbox beneath the floorboards of her office, where she kept the things that mattered more than coin: the covenant's seal impression, her grandmother's key ring, and now a book that smelled like a house fire.

She had wrapped it in oilcloth the night before, the way you'd wrap a snake you weren't sure was dead. She unwrapped it now on her desk, in the lamplight, and looked at it again with the cold patience she brought to everything — smugglers' manifests, toll disputes, the slow arithmetic of a pass that never slept.

It was a small book, octavo, bound in leather gone stiff and dark. The scorching had taken its corners and half its spine; someone had carried it through fire, or out of one. The title page was gone. What remained began mid-sentence, in a hand that slanted hard to the right, the ink brown-black with age:

*—and in the hundredth year the keeping shall fail, for no leash holds forever, and the blood that forged the bond shall—*

Ada turned pages with a letter-opener, not her fingers. Old habit. Old caution. The book was a mess of prophecies, fragments, marginalia in at least four hands, some of it in Old Emberish, which she could read only slowly and badly, the way a border-keeper reads anything: well enough to catch trouble coming.

Most of it was the usual fever-dream stuff. Stars falling into valleys. A king with no shadow. Rivers running backward to their sources. A century of prophets all drunk on the same imagery, and none of it worth a copper piece until—

Until the page near the middle, where the calendar of the old realm ran down its columns in faded script, and one date stood out from all the others because someone had drawn a circle around it. Not a hasty circle. A careful one, compass-steady, made by someone who wanted it found.

Tonight's date. Five days from the night the peddler handed it to her. And beside the circle, in the same century-old ink, two words:

*Okafor keeps.*

She had stared at those words until midnight. She stared at them again now, and they were no better company the second time.

*Okafor keeps.* As if it were a duty roster. As if some dead hand had known, a hundred years ago, which name would be written in the Sable Gate's ledger on this night, in this year, in this generation.

Ada closed the book. She did not believe in prophecy. That was practically a professional requirement. In ten years of keeping the Gate she had watched a dozen self-declared seers come through the pass, and every one of them had been running from something or selling something, usually both. The future did not announce itself. The future arrived the way weather arrived, and you kept your roof mended and your stores counted and you did not waste candlelight on omens.

But ink aged in ways that could be tested. And a lie that had waited a hundred years to be told was still, in its way, an impressive kind of lie, and Ada respected craftsmanship even when it was aimed at her.

She spent the morning doing what she always did, because routine was a wall and she intended to stand behind it. She logged the night's traffic: two wool carts down from Highmere, a tinker with a permit three days stale whom she fined rather than turned away, a party of pilgrims walking the low road to the shrine at Hollowfell, singing. She collected tolls. She checked the lamp-oil stores against the delivery manifest and found the count short by two flasks and wrote a note to the chandler in terms that would make him wince. She walked the gate itself, top to bottom, palm flat against the keystone the way her grandmother had taught her, feeling for the faint warmth that lived in the stone like a sleeping animal's flank.

The warmth was there. Steady. Ordinary.

"Good," she said to the stone, and felt slightly foolish, and did it anyway, because her grandmother had done it, and her great-grandfather, and because a keeper who stopped talking to her gate was a keeper who had stopped listening.

* * *

The peddler died on the second day.

She learned of it from a carter named Doss who came up the switchbacks at midmorning waving his hat before he was close enough to be heard, which among mountain folk meant either celebration or catastrophe.

"Marta's boy found him," Doss said, breathless at the gate. "Down past the third cairn, off the pilgrim road. In the rocks."

Ada already knew who. "The old man. Gray coat. Sold me a book four nights ago."

Doss blinked. "You bought something off Tam Vetch?"

"You knew him?"

"Everybody knew Tam. Half-smuggler, half-holy man, wholly mad, my mother used to say." Doss shifted his weight. "He's been coming through Thornfell thirty years. Never sold anybody anything, far as I know. Gave things away. Odd little things. Charms. Bits of verse."

"And now he's dead in the rocks."

"Fell, likely. Or lay down and didn't get up. He was old, Keeper. Older than old." Doss hesitated, then lowered his voice, though there was no one within fifty yards. "There's a thing, though. Marta's boy says his hands were folded. On his chest. Like they'd been laid there."

"People fold the dead's hands."

"Sure," Doss said. "If there's somebody there to do the folding."

Ada looked down the pass, toward the third cairn, a gray smudge in the gray morning. Wind moved through the stones and made a sound she had heard ten thousand times and never once listened to.

"I'll want to see him," she said.

"You don't want to see him," Doss said, with the frankness of a man who had carted goods through her gate for fifteen years and earned the right. "Keeper, I've seen dead men on this road before. He wasn't just dead. He was *done*. Like a candle burned to the socket. Boy said it looked like Tam had been old for a hundred years, if you take my meaning, and I don't think you do yet, but you will."

She went anyway. Of course she went. She left the gate in the hands of her deputy, a solid, unimaginative woman named Brek who asked no questions, which was why Ada tolerated her, and walked the pilgrim road alone with her cloak buttoned to the chin.

Tam Vetch lay where the boy had found him, in a hollow of rock below the cairn, and Doss had been right, and Doss had also been wrong. He looked old, yes. But he looked *light*, was the thing. Like whatever had been inside him had finally been set down after a very long carry. His face was peaceful in a way that made her angry, though she couldn't have said why. His hands were folded on his chest. There was no one else around for miles.

She knelt beside him, there in the wind, and searched the body the way she'd search any traveler — thorough, respectful, quick. No purse. No papers. No second book. But tucked into his collar, pinned with a rusted brooch, she found a scrap of parchment gone soft as cloth, and on it, in the same hard-slanting hand as the prophecy book, four words in Old Emberish.

She copied them onto her cuff with a pencil stub, because she was not going to carry strange writing against her skin, and translated them that evening by lamplight, working through the grammar twice to be sure.

*Ask the stone who paid.*

Ada sat back in her chair and looked at the sentence for a long time.

Then she got up, went down to the gate, laid her palm against the keystone, and asked it nothing at all, because she was not a woman who talked to rocks about payment, and because she was suddenly, deeply afraid of what a rock might say back.

* * *

On the third day she tested the ink.

She sent to Highmere for it — a favor called in from an apothecary who owed her for looking the other way about a shipment of poppy resin two winters back — and the man came up himself, a nervous little ferret of a person named Quill, who examined the circled date under a lens for the better part of an hour while Ada stood at the window and pretended to review manifests.

"It's old," Quill said finally. "I mean old old. The iron-gall's oxidized all the way through. Brown, not black. You see how it's eaten into the paper a hair? That takes decades. Fifty years minimum. A century wouldn't surprise me." He glanced up. "Where'd this come from?"

"A peddler."

"Could someone have faked it? Made new ink look old?"

"They could try."

Quill shook his head. "Not like this. You can stain paper, you can bake a page, you can bury a book in ash and hope. But the way the ink sits *in* the fibers, under the sizing — that's time, Keeper. Nobody bottles time." He hesitated. "There's something else. The circle and the words — *Okafor keeps* — they're the same ink as the rest. Same batch, same age. Whoever wrote the prophecy wrote the circle. Same sitting, or near enough."

"So it wasn't added later by some fraud with a steady hand."

"No, ma'am. It was always there. Somebody wrote this a hundred years ago and then the book spent a century getting here, to you, for this week." Quill wrapped his lens away with shaking fingers. "I'd like to go home now, if it's all the same. I don't like the mountains when they're being meaningful."

Ada paid him double and let him run.

That night she pulled the Okafor family records — the real ones, not the ledger she showed inspectors — and sat with them until the small hours, tracing names backward. Her great-grandfather, keeper of the Gate, forty-one years. Her great-great-grandmother before him. Back and back, keepers all, Okafors in the gatehouse going further than the records reached, further than family memory, back to a first name on the oldest page written in a hand so formal it might as well have been carved:

*Sarel Okafor, First Keeper, who made the keeping.*

*Who made the keeping.* Not *who took up* the keeping. Not *who was granted* it. *Made*. As if the Gate were a thing an Okafor had built. As if the covenant were a thing an Okafor had authored.

The prophecy book lay on her desk, patient as a trap.

*The blood that forged the bond shall—*

Shall what? The page tore off mid-sentence, scorched away. She had read that broken line forty times hoping the missing word would simply appear out of stubbornness. It didn't. Somewhere in a fire a hundred years dead was the rest of the sentence, and the rest of the sentence was probably the only part that mattered.

* * *

On the fourth day, the gate flared.

No cause. No caravan, no storm, no tremor in the deep rock that she could feel. Just past dusk, with the lamps newly lit and Brek at the supper table, the keystone woke.

Ada was standing three feet from it when it happened. The warmth in the stone — the sleeping-animal warmth she had touched every day of her working life — drew suddenly tight, then blazed, a pulse of heat like a heart slamming once behind the granite. Light moved through the gate's arch, amber and brief, chasing along veins in the stone she had never seen before, patterns like script, like Emberish, there and gone in the space of a breath.

Brek came running with a poker. Ada waved her off.

The stone cooled. The veins faded. The arch stood dark and ordinary, and the wind came through the pass exactly as it always had, and if Brek hadn't seen it too, Ada might have spent the rest of the night persuading herself she'd imagined it.

"What was that?" Brek whispered.

"I don't know," Ada said. Which was true. What she didn't say was the other thing, the thing she could hardly admit to herself: that in the instant of the flare, in the half-second the light lived, she had *heard* something. Not with her ears. Deeper in, the way you hear your own name across a crowded room.

A voice. Dry as pages turning. Counting.

She hadn't caught the number. She wished, violently, that she hadn't caught anything at all.

"Brek," she said. "Double the watch tonight. Quietly. If anyone asks, it's a drill."

Brek looked at the keystone, then at her, and — bless her unimaginative soul — asked nothing, and went.

Ada stayed in the gatehouse alone until midnight, palm against cooling stone, listening to a pass that had one ordinary day left, and hating how well she now understood the phrase.

* * *

On the fifth day — the day — she did not open the prophecy book. She made herself do everything in order, everything by the ledger, everything the way ten years of discipline had built her, because if the world intended to end on schedule then Ada Okafor intended to meet it with her accounts current.

Tolls collected. Stores counted. Lamp trimmed. Keystone touched, warm and silent, saying nothing, counting nothing, or counting too quietly to hear.

At dusk she climbed to the gatehouse roof, as she did most evenings, to watch the light die along the Thornfell peaks — the one indulgence she allowed herself, the reason she'd never once regretted a life spent in cold stone. To the east, beyond the pass, the high shoulders of the Emberlands caught the last sun, and dragon-fire glowed in the deep vents, as it had glowed for as long as anyone remembered: a faint red gold under the snowfields, the hearth-light of another realm, steady as a banked coal.

Steady. She looked at it a long time. It was steady. It was fine.

Everything was fine for exactly as long as it took her to climb back down and find Ryn Vasq waiting in her office, seated on her stool with his boots off — *his boots off*, the absolute gall of the man — turning the prophecy book over in his scarred hands like a man greeting an old debt.

"How did you get in here?" Ada said, from the doorway, very calmly, because shouting would tell him he'd surprised her, and she would die before she gave Ryn Vasq the satisfaction.

"Door was latched, not locked," he said. "You're getting comfortable, Keeper." He set the book down on her desk, aligned it square with the edge, and looked up at her, and for the first time in eight years of tolerating each other across a toll table, Ryn Vasq looked frightened.

"We need to talk," he said, "about what I've been carrying through your pass."
