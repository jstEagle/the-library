## 3. The Smuggler Who Knew

Ryn Vasq had a rule about the Sable Gate: never be interesting.

Interesting got you searched. Interesting got you remembered. Interesting got you a name in Ada Okafor's ledger, and the ledger was the closest thing Thornfell had to a god — omniscient, unforgiving, and written in a hand so neat it looked printed. For six years Ryn had moved silk, salt, and the occasional unlicensed remedy through the pass by being precisely boring: correct tolls, correct papers, correct smile, never more than two words past what courtesy required.

Tonight he was going to break his rule so thoroughly that he might as well set fire to it on the way out.

He waited until the last pilgrim lanterns had guttered down toward the valley and the gatehouse windows went amber, then darker, then down to the single lamp Ada kept burning over her desk like a votive to arithmetic. Then he climbed the goat track behind the gatehouse — a path she knew about because she'd caught him on it in his first year and fined him for it, which was how they'd met, if you could call a fine a meeting — and knocked on the kitchen door with the rhythm of rain on slate. Three quick, two slow. A signal they'd never agreed on but that she'd stopped commenting on around year three.

The door opened before the last knock landed. Of course it did. She probably heard him coming from the treeline; the woman counted heartbeats for sport.

"You're late," Ada said.

"I'm punctual," Ryn said. "The mountain's late."

She stepped back to let him in, which was itself a small miracle, and he took stock of her the way he took stock of every room: exits first, then weapons, then the thing that didn't fit. Exits: kitchen door, cellar hatch, window over the sink. Weapons: the short blade she wore at her hip even indoors, and the poker by the hearth, positioned for a left-handed grab. The thing that didn't fit: a book on the table, scorched black along one edge, open to a page marked with a strip of red cloth.

Ryn's stomach performed a slow, professional drop.

"So," he said, keeping his voice light the way you keep your hands visible when a dog is deciding about you. "You've been shopping."

"Where did you get them?"

Not *what is this*. Not *how did you find my table*. Where did you get *them*. Plural. She already knew there were others.

Ryn pulled out the chair across from her, slowly, and sat down without being invited, which was another rule breaking tonight, and the night was young. "That depends," he said, "on whether we're having the conversation where you arrest me, or the one where you don't."

"That depends," she said, "on your answer."

"Then let me ask mine first." He nodded at the book. "How much did the peddler charge you?"

Her eyes narrowed a fraction. With anyone else it would have been nothing. With Ada Okafor, a fraction was a shout. "He didn't."

"No," Ryn agreed. "They never do. That's the trick of it. Old Tam sells you a dying man's book for free, and six months later somebody else comes asking who bought it, and suddenly free turns out to be the most expensive word in the pass." He leaned forward. "What did the page say? The one with the cloth."

"Why do you think there's a page with a cloth?"

"Because you're a keeper, not a reader. You mark things you intend to come back to, and you only mark things that frighten you, and I have watched you not-frightened for six years, Keeper, so whatever's under that cloth has teeth." He spread his hands. "And I know it has teeth because I've been carrying its brothers across the pass since spring."

The silence that followed had texture. It had weight and grain, like the cold coming off the stone walls. Ada didn't move. Somewhere in the gatehouse a log settled in the grate, and the sound of it seemed enormous.

"Say that again," she said quietly, "slower."

"I've been carrying prophecy pages across Thornfell since the thaw. Loose pages, torn out, wrapped in oilcloth, sewn into bales of salt wool. Maybe forty of them by now. Maybe more. I stopped counting after the third time I nearly died delivering them." He held up a hand before she could speak. "Before you ask — no, I don't know who wrote them. Yes, I know where they were going. And no, I am absolutely not going to tell you either of those things for free."

"Ryn." His name in her mouth sounded like a summons. "You've been running heresy through my gate. Through *my* pass. Under my nose. For months."

"Yes."

"And you're telling me this now because—"

"Because tonight I found out what's in them matters to *you*." He tapped the scorched cover once, lightly, like knocking on a coffin lid. "Because the pages I carried and the book on your table are the same work, same hand, same century-old ink, and because three days ago the people who hired me stopped paying me and started killing the couriers instead. Which tells me the job changed. Jobs change like that when the thing being moved stops being valuable and starts being dangerous." He met her eyes. "And because I'd rather be blackmailed by you than buried by them."

For a long moment she just looked at him, and Ryn had the uncomfortable sensation of being audited. He'd seen her do it to smugglers, to pilgrims with too many rings, to a Crown tax assessor once, and the assessor had left the pass two days early and pale as milk. Being on the receiving end was worse than he'd imagined. She wasn't looking for lies. She was looking for the shape of the truth underneath them, the way a butcher reads a carcass.

"The pages," she said finally. "Describe them."

"Vellum. Old. Burn marks on some, water damage on others, like they were pulled out of a fire or a flood. Emberish script on the margins — I can't read it, but I know it when I see it, everyone who works the high passes knows it. And diagrams." He hesitated. "Diagrams of gates. Not just ours. Gates all down the spine of the mountains, drawn like anatomical plates, like someone was dissecting them. And every diagram has a line running through it, and every line ends at a family name."

Ada went very still.

He watched her arrive at it — watched the thought land on her like weather. Her hand drifted toward the book on the table and stopped an inch short, as if the leather had gotten hot.

"What name," she said. It wasn't quite a question.

"I don't read Emberish," Ryn said. "But names are names. There was one that showed up on every page. Short. Two syllables." He let the pause stretch just long enough to be cruel, and hated himself a little for enjoying it. "Okafor."

She stood. Not fast — that was the frightening part. She rose the way water rises in a lock, smooth and total, and walked to the window, and stood with her back to him looking out at the dark mass of the gate, and for a while the only sound in the kitchen was the wind worrying the eaves.

"Who hired you," she said to the glass.

"A factor out of Lowmark. Called himself Master Ellery. Soft hands, soft voice, paid in old coin — real old, pre-Founding mint, which should have been my first warning, because nobody honest pays in coins older than their grandmother." He shrugged. "I asked questions eventually. Questions are expensive in my trade. The answer I got was a name I didn't recognize and a description of where my body would be found if I kept asking."

"And the couriers. The ones being killed."

"Two that I know of. Sefa, who ran the southern route — found in the Wrenshall river with her own pack straps. And a boy called Finch, nineteen, maybe. They say he burned. In wet grass, in the rain, with nothing near him to catch." Ryn turned his cup in his hands. He hadn't touched the tea she'd poured; habit said never drink on business, and besides, his hands wanted something to do that wasn't shaking. "I don't spook easy, Ada. You know that. But fire that picks its target—"

"—isn't fire," she finished. "It's covenant work."

Now it was his turn to go still. "You know what's in the book."

"I know what's on one page." She turned from the window. Her face was doing something complicated, grief and fury fighting for the same ground, and Ryn understood with a lurch that he was seeing something almost nobody alive had seen: Ada Okafor off-balance. "Tonight's date. Circled. In ink older than my grandmother. Next to my name."

"Next to—" He laughed, one sharp note with no humor in it. "Of course it is. Of course. Why would the prophecy name a stranger when it could name the keeper?" He rubbed his face. "All right. All right. Here's where we are, then. You have a book that says the world ends tonight at your front door. I have forty pages of the same work sewn into salt wool somewhere between here and Lowmark, and a dead man's employer who wants both of us quiet. And neither of us can go to the Crown, because the Crown sends inquisitors, and inquisitors burn books *and* the people who read them, and I'm guessing — tell me if I'm guessing wrong — that your book would not survive Halvard Brann's attention."

Something flickered across her face at the inquisitor's name. Filed away, Ryn thought. Something happened there too. This mountain was full of secrets tonight, and he was starting to feel like the only person in Thornfell who'd come to his secrets honestly.

"So," he said. "Mutual blackmail."

"That's not what this is."

"It's exactly what this is, and it's beautiful, and I want you to appreciate it." He counted on his fingers. "I know you've been harboring heretical texts in a Crown installation. You know I've been smuggling them. If you turn me in, I turn you in, and we both hang, and the gate fails, and everybody dies per the prophecy, which frankly feels like sloppy planning on someone's part. Whereas if we help each other—" He spread his hands. "Maybe nobody hangs. Maybe nobody burns. Maybe we even find out who's been playing puppeteer with a hundred years of history, and introduce them to some consequences."

"You're describing treason."

"I'm describing Tuesday," Ryn said. "Treason is just trade with worse accounting."

Almost — *almost* — her mouth twitched. It was gone instantly, folded away like contraband, but he'd seen it, and he banked it the way he banked everything: carefully, against a colder night.

"There's a condition," Ada said.

"There's always a condition."

"When this is done — whatever 'done' turns out to be — I take the pages. All of them. The book stays with me. Nobody else reads them. Nobody else *keeps* them."

Ryn considered the ceiling. Considered the deal. Considered the fact that the pages were, technically, no longer in his possession — that he'd handed the last bale to a relay runner out of Wrenshall four days ago, before the killings started, before he knew what he was carrying, and that the runner was supposed to surface again tomorrow.

"Most of them," he said. "One bale's already moving. I can get it back, but I need until tomorrow night, and I need you to look the other way while I do it — properly look the other way, ledger-blind, like I was never in the pass."

"You want me to falsify my own ledger."

"I want you to *omit*, which is different, and which you've done before." He held up a hand. "Don't glare. Year two. The widow Anselm's medicine shipment. You logged it as lamp oil. Very tidy work, actually. I've always admired it."

The glare arrived anyway, magnificent and cold, and Ryn grinned into it because grinning was armor and right now he needed all the armor he could get. Because here was the truth under the truth: he wasn't telling her everything. He hadn't told her about the second payment — the one that hadn't come in coin. He hadn't told her what Master Ellery had whispered to him at their last meeting, standing too close in a Lowmark alley, smelling of ash and honey: *When the gate opens, Vasq, be on the right side of it.* He hadn't told her that he still didn't know which side that was, or why a soft-handed factor spoke about the Sable Gate failing like a man speaking about a wedding he'd been invited to.

And he especially hadn't told her that the reason he'd come tonight, the real reason, the one beneath the blackmail and the alliance and the pretty speech about consequences — was that three nights ago, camped above the tree line, he had dreamed of a voice counting down in a language he didn't speak, and had woken with frost on his blanket in midsummer, and had known, with the dumb animal certainty that had kept him alive through twenty years of bad roads, that the count was nearly finished.

Tomorrow, he told himself. Tomorrow he'd tell her about the dream. Or the next day. When he knew whether she'd use it against him. Trust was a currency too, and he'd been poor before.

"Tomorrow night," Ada said at last. "The south relay. I'll be auditing the lower stores — alone, officially. My ledger will show nothing unusual." She picked up the scorched book and closed it, and the sound of the cover settling was final as a cell door. "And Ryn — if you're playing me, if any part of this is a setup, understand what you're setting up against. I have kept this gate for eleven years. I know every smuggler road, every cache, every grave. People who lie to me end up in my ledger, and my ledger lasts longer than they do."

"See, this is why our arrangement works," Ryn said, standing and pulling his coat on. "You threaten people with paperwork. It's very you."

"Go home, Vasq."

"Can't. Home's a word other people get." He paused at the kitchen door, hand on the latch, and looked back at her — at the keeper framed by lamplight and scorched prophecy, the loneliest powerful person he'd ever known — and heard himself say, before he could stop it:

"The voice in the book. Whatever it told you tonight. It's true, Ada. Or close enough to true that the difference won't matter." He opened the door onto the cold and the dark and the vast blind shoulder of the mountain. "I've heard it counting."

Behind him, the lamp flame bent, though there was no draft.

And far below, down in the roots of the pass where no lamp had ever reached, the Sable Gate began — softly, patiently, like something that had waited a hundred years and could wait a little longer — to hum.

* * *

Ryn made it as far as the switchback above the tree line before he let himself stop shaking.

He crouched in the shadow of a leaning pine, breath steaming, and did what he always did after a bad meeting: inventory. Blade, present. Coin, present. Nerve — he checked — mostly present. The gatehouse glowed faintly below him, one amber window, one keeper, one book full of his death warrant. Behind and above, the pass wound north into darkness, and somewhere along it, sewn into a bale of salt wool on a runner's back, rode forty pages of a dead century, heading for a hand he was no longer sure he wanted them to reach.

*When the gate opens, Vasq, be on the right side of it.*

He'd assumed Ellery meant the human side. Standing here now, with the mountain humming under his boots like a struck bell that hadn't finished ringing, Ryn found himself wondering, for the first time, whether there was going to be a human side at all.

He pulled his collar up and started down toward the valley, taking the smuggler roads, the hidden ones, the ones only he and the dead knew — and behind him, faint but unmistakable, the humming followed.
