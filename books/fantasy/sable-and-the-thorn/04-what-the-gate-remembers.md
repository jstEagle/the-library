## 4. What the Gate Remembers

The peddler's book sat under the floorboard where Ada had hidden it, and all evening it had felt like a coal buried in her chest.

She tried to work past it. There was always work. The Sable Gate did not keep itself; that was the first lesson her mother had taught her, standing on this same flagstone floor with a candle stub and a ledger, when Ada was seven and thought the gate was simply a very large door. *The gate keeps nothing by itself,* her mother had said. *It is stone and oath. You are the hinge.*

So she worked.

The nightly audit took two hours when she did it properly, and she always did it properly. She spread the day's tallies across the gatehouse table — three ledgers, one for traffic, one for tolls, one for the quiet things that never went in any book the Crown would ever see. Forty-one souls through the pass today. Twelve pilgrims bound for the Emberlands' edge shrines, carrying candles and grief and nothing else worth taxing. Nineteen traders with honest papers. Ten whose papers were honest enough, which in Thornfell was its own category, and which she recorded in the third ledger in her own shorthand: initials, goods, direction, and a small mark for whether they'd looked her in the eye while lying.

Ryn Vasq had gotten no mark tonight. That bothered her more than she wanted to admit.

He'd come through at dusk with a load of mountain wool he plainly hadn't sheared himself, paid his toll without haggling for the first time in six years of haggling, and paused at the threshold long enough to say, "Cold coming, Keeper. The kind that comes off fire, not off winter." Then he'd walked into the dark on the far side, toward the Emberlands, which was backwards from where his wool was going.

Ada had written him up in the third ledger and then, after a moment, added the words he'd said. She told herself it was habit. She told herself a lot of things were habit.

The gatehouse was warm. She'd banked the fire, set water for morning tea, checked the lamp oil twice. Outside, the wind came down the pass in long slow breaths, and the mountains stood against the stars like the shoulders of something sleeping. This was the hour she loved best and admitted to loving least — the hour after the audit, when the books balanced and the road was empty and the whole weight of Thornfell rested on her alone, quietly, the way she preferred everything to rest.

She should sleep. She knew she should sleep. Instead she found herself kneeling at the loose floorboard beneath the storage shelf, lifting it, taking out the scorched book.

Up close it smelled worse than it had in the peddler's cart — char and old fat and something underneath both, a smell like a snuffed candle in a closed room. The leather cover had been burned nearly through in one corner. The pages inside were vellum, expensive vellum, the kind the Crown reserved for its own histories, and someone had tried very hard to destroy them and failed.

*The Emberhold Codex*, the first page read, in a hand so formal it might have been printed. Below it, smaller: *Being a true account of the covenant between the realms, as witnessed.*

As witnessed. Not as written. Not as decreed. As *witnessed*.

Ada turned pages with the care she'd give a wounded bird. Most of the text was in Common, dense legal prose about passages and tolls and boundaries — duller than her own ledgers, if anything. But every few pages there were lines in Old Emberish, the dragon-tongue, angular script that looked like lightning frozen mid-strike. She couldn't read it. No keeper could; the Crown had made sure of that three generations ago, burning the translation grammars along with everything else heretical. Her mother had known four words of it, taught to her grandmother by a dragon who owed them a debt, and those four words had been passed down like contraband: *vess*, fire. *arath*, last. *okafor*, keeper. And *sable*, which meant not the color but the silence after a bell is struck — the held breath before sound returns.

Her own name was in the language of dragons. She'd known that since she was small and had never once let herself think about what it meant.

She reached the page with the circle.

There it was again, in ink gone brown as old blood: tomorrow's date. Not tonight's — she'd checked three times now, holding the page to the lamp, counting backward from the month's name. Tomorrow. And beside the circled date, in the same century-old hand, two words in Common:

*The gate fails.*

And below that, half-burned away, letters that might once have spelled a name. What remained was *—kafor*.

Ada closed the book. Her hands were steady. She noted this the way she noted everything, with the detached precision that had kept her alive and sane behind this gate for nineteen years, and she understood that the steadiness was the worst sign of all. A frightened woman would have burned the thing. A sensible woman would have ridden for the capital and put it in the Crown's hands. She did neither. She wrapped it in oilcloth, put it back beneath the floorboard, and pressed the board down until it seated with a soft knock, like a door closing on a room she wasn't ready to enter.

Then she climbed the stairs to the gate-top walk, because the alternative was sitting alone with the sound of her own thinking.

* * *

The Sable Gate was not a wall. People who came through the pass expecting a wall were always disappointed, then unsettled. It was an arch — a single span of black stone taller than ten men, standing free at the narrowest point of the gorge, with no fortification attached, no battlements, nothing to defend it except the small gatehouse crouched against the cliff face like a barnacle. The stone was older than the kingdom. Older than the Emberlands' borders, older than the roads, older, the oldest stories said, than the mountains around it, which had grown up like weeds around something planted.

In daylight it was just rock. But at night, in certain lights, the arch held a faint sheen, like the memory of heat in a hearthstone hours after the fire died. Every keeper learned not to mention it. Mentioning it invited questions, and questions invited the Crown, and the Crown's interest in the Sable Gate had historically been the kind that ended with people disappearing into records that no longer existed.

Ada walked the length of the arch with her lantern raised, doing the thing her mother had called *listening with your eyes*. Cracks: none new. Mortar: none, because the arch had no mortar, which was one of the many facts about it that Ada had spent a lifetime declining to think about too hard. The keystone at the crown of the span was smooth as river glass, carved with a spiral that predated every alphabet she knew.

She was halfway back down when the gate flared.

Not glowed. *Flared.* Light poured out of the black stone like breath through parted lips — a deep ember-red that lit the whole gorge at once, threw her shadow huge and wavering across the cliff wall, and turned the falling snow to drifting sparks. The cold vanished. For one heartbeat the air smelled of summer, of hot iron and green growing things, of a world that had no business existing at the top of a frozen pass.

Ada did not move. Later she would be proud of this. Her knees wanted to fold and her body wanted to run and instead she stood on the frozen walk with her lantern hanging forgotten from her hand and watched the light climb the arch from base to keystone like fire climbing a wick.

The keystone's spiral began to turn.

She heard it before she saw it — a grinding, subterranean groan, stone moving over stone though the spiral was carved, not cut through, though there was nothing behind it to move. The light gathered there, concentrated, brightened until she had to squint, and out of the brightness came a voice.

It was not loud. That was the horror of it, or the wonder — she could never afterward decide which. It spoke at the volume of a person standing beside her, calm and unhurried, in Old Emberish, and it was *counting*.

*Ninety-nine. Ninety-eight. Ninety-seven.*

Ada knew numbers. Of all the fragments her family had smuggled down through the generations, numbers had survived best — keepers needed them, needed to know when a dragon said *one* toll or *ten*, needed to know the difference between *come* and *go*, between *safe passage* and *no passage*. The voice was counting down. Backward, from ninety-nine, in a tongue that had not been spoken aloud in this pass since before her grandmother's grandmother.

She counted with it. She couldn't help it. *Ninety-six. Ninety-five.* Each number landed like a footstep on a stair going down into the dark, and with each one the light dimmed a little, drew inward, banked itself like a fire being carefully, deliberately smothered.

*Ninety-four.*

"What are you?" she said. Her voice came out flat and small in the vast ringing air. "What are you counting?"

The voice did not answer. It had not been speaking *to* her — she understood that with sudden, total clarity, the way you understand you've interrupted a conversation by walking through the middle of it. It was counting for itself. Or for something else. A prisoner marking days on a wall. A clock running down. A promise keeping its own time.

*Ninety-three. Ninety-two.*

"Stop," she said, and hated how thin it sounded. "I am the keeper of this gate. By the covenant, I command—" 

Command what? She didn't know the words. Three generations of Okafor keepers, and the Crown had burned the grammar books, and now the gate was speaking the only language it had ever truly spoken and its keeper could do nothing but stand in the snow and count along like a child.

*Ninety-one.*

The light sank into the keystone and went out. The spiral stopped turning. The cold rushed back in so fast her breath cracked in her throat, and the gorge went dark except for her lantern, which seemed suddenly absurd, a toy, a candle held up to a cathedral.

Snow drifted down through the space where the light had been. The arch stood silent, black, ordinary. If she hadn't seen it — and part of her was already, treacherously, suggesting she hadn't — she would have sworn the gate was exactly what the Crown's histories said it was: old stone, old oath, nothing more.

Ada stood on the walk for a long time. Her legs shook now that it was over, a fine continuous tremor, and she let them, because no one was watching and because shaking was honest in a way that steadiness had stopped being.

Ninety-nine. She turned the number over. Ninety-nine of what? Nights? Hours? Heartbeats? The prophecy book under her floorboard said the gate failed tomorrow. If the voice was counting nights, she had ninety-nine of them — a season, a reprieve, time to think. If it was counting hours, she had four days. If heartbeats—

Don't, she told herself. Don't borrow fear from a number you can't translate.

But her hands, when she finally made herself unclench them, were white at the knuckle, and the lantern glass had fogged with her own breathing, and somewhere below in the gatehouse the loose floorboard seemed to knock softly in the wind, over and over, like something asking to be let out.

* * *

She did not sleep. She sat at the table with all three ledgers open and wrote, in the third one, the only record of the night that would ever exist:

*Gate flared at the second hour. Duration, less than a minute. Voice in Old Emberish, counting downward from ninety-nine. Cause unknown. Damage, none visible. Response:* 

She stopped. Her pen hovered. Everything she wrote next would be a lie or a confession, and she wasn't ready for either.

Finally she wrote: *Response: observed. Logged. Under investigation.*

Under investigation. As if there were some office above hers, some chain of inquiry she could hand this to. There wasn't. There was only her, and a dead language, and a book that named her family beside a date that was almost here.

Near dawn, exhaustion finally pulled her under, and she dreamed — not of the gate, but of heat. Vast, dry, golden heat, and a valley full of green that hurt to look at, and somewhere above her something enormous breathing slowly, each breath a bellows-draft she felt against her skin. In the dream she was small. In the dream she was being *looked at*, considered, weighed, by an attention so large it made the sky feel like a roof.

A voice said, in Old Emberish, the word for *daughter*.

Ada woke with a start, hand already reaching for the knife under the table, heart slamming. Grey light stood in the windows. Snow fell straight down, windless, the kind of snowfall that muffled the world like a held blanket. On the pass below, a rider was coming up toward the gatehouse at speed — a Crown courier, by the sash, riding like the devil was paying by the mile.

She was still standing there, knife in hand, watching him come, when she understood what the dream had been. Not a dream. A summons. Something in the Emberlands had spoken her name in its sleep, and she had heard it across the border of the world, and whatever came next, the quiet life of the Sable Gate — audits and tolls and lies that looked her in the eye — was finished.

The courier hauled rein at the foot of the stairs and shouted up through the snow, breathless: "Keeper! Letter from the capital — the Inquisition's coming to Thornfell. They'll be here within the week."

Ada looked at the loose floorboard. She looked at the black arch standing silent in the falling snow, holding its secret, holding its count.

"Ninety-eight," she said, to no one, in a language she barely knew. And went down to meet the letter.
