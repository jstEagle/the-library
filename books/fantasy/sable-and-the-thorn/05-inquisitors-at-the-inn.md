## 5. Inquisitors at the Inn

Halvard Brann counted the bells of Thornfell village as his carriage climbed the last switchback, and found them wrong.

Not broken — wrong. The bell in the chapel tower rang seven strokes where the capital's almanac said six, and the difference was not sloppiness. He had spent eleven years listening for what towns tried to hide beneath their noise, and a village that kept its own time was a village that had decided, long ago and quietly, that the Crown's time did not apply to it. He wrote the observation in the small black book he carried everywhere, in the shorthand only three living people could read, and two of them were dead.

The carriage door opened onto cold that bit through wool and leather both. Thornfell sat in a bowl of the mountains like a coin at the bottom of a well: one street, one inn, one chapel, a market square crusted with old snow, and above it all — visible from every window in town, because the town had been built to face it whether it wished to or not — the black arch of the Sable Gate, standing on its shoulder of rock with snow smoking off its edges.

He had read everything the archive possessed on the Gate. Which is to say he had read almost nothing, because the archive possessed almost nothing, and what it had was forty years stale and written by a man who had clearly never climbed the pass himself. *The Keeper maintains order. The covenant holds.* Eleven words. For an institution that had burned nine thousand books in Halvard's lifetime, the Inquisition was remarkably incurious about the one structure in the realm that actually required guarding.

That incuriosity was why he had volunteered. His superiors believed heresy lived in cities, in printing houses, in the marginalia of university men. Halvard believed heresy lived wherever someone had worked very hard to make sure no records existed. A silence that large was not emptiness. It was architecture.

"Your Reverence." The innkeeper met him in the yard, bowing at the correct depth, smiling the correct amount. "The Grey Hearth is honored. We've your rooms made up, and supper—"

"The keeper of the Gate," Halvard said. "Where?"

The smile flickered — barely, barely — before it steadied. "Keeper Okafor? She keeps to the gatehouse mostly, Reverence. Comes down for supplies, first market-day of the month."

"When was the last market-day?"

"Four days past."

"And she didn't come."

The innkeeper's hands found each other. "Snow's been heavy."

"Mm," said Halvard, who had crossed three mountain passes in worse snow to get here, and whose mules had managed it without complaint. He let the silence do its work. Innkeepers were not trained liars; they were trained hosts, and hosts filled silences the way water fills a crack. This one held out for eight seconds, which was better than most, and then: "She came down after, though. Tuesday evening. Bought candles and lamp oil, more than she'd usually need, and—" He stopped himself, visibly appalled at his own mouth.

"And?" Halvard asked pleasantly.

"Bread. Salt fish. Nothing else, Reverence, I swear it on the Hearth."

*Tuesday evening.* The courier's letter had left the capital nine days ago. Nine days' ride to Thornfell, if the roads were clear, which they were not. Which meant either the warning had traveled faster than any Crown rider — or it had never needed to travel at all, because someone in the capital had sent word ahead by private means, and Ada Okafor had known he was coming before his own superiors knew he'd been assigned.

He thanked the innkeeper, declined the supper, and asked — as though it were an afterthought — whether the inn had a common room where travelers might sit.

"It's early in the season, Reverence. Just the locals now."

"Locals are the best company," Halvard said, and went to find them.

* * *

The common room of the Grey Hearth smelled of tallow, wet wool, and juniper smoke, and it went quiet in the particular way rooms go quiet when they are deciding, collectively, how much to fear him. Halvard chose the corner table with his back to the wall — not for safety but for sightlines — ordered nothing but hot water, and listened.

Inquisitors had a reputation for interrogation. It was undeserved, mostly. Interrogation was crude; it told you what a man feared you knew. Listening told you what a man feared regardless of you. And so Halvard sat with his back straight and his hands folded and drank his hot water while the room slowly forgot him, and learned more in an hour than his superiors would have learned in a month of warrants.

He learned that the smugglers' road through Thornfell had gone quiet in the last month — too quiet, the way a forest goes quiet when something large moves through it. He learned that the chapel priest had preached twice on obedience in the last fortnight, which was once more than his custom, and that the second sermon had mentioned, obliquely, the sin of *unauthorized reading*. He learned that a peddler had died in the pass some weeks ago, and that the manner of his death was discussed in the lowered voices people reserve for deaths that don't add up.

And he learned, from a drover's wife speaking to her sister across the room, that Keeper Okafor had bought candles on Tuesday — *a whole case of them, Sela, a case* — and had paid in old coin, the kind nobody minted anymore, and had looked, in the drover's wife's phrase, "like a woman counting something in her head that wasn't money."

Halvard turned his mug slowly on the table. Candles. Lamp oil. Old coin. A keeper preparing for a siege, or a vigil, or a burning. Or all three.

"You'll be wanting to see the Gate, Reverence."

The speaker had appeared beside his table without his noticing, which alone made Halvard reassess the evening. A young man — mid-twenties, dark-haired, with the sun-browned face and easy slouch of someone who walked the high roads for a living, and eyes that were doing none of the slouching. He wore a carter's coat over a sword-hilt he hadn't bothered to disguise well.

"I'll want many things," Halvard said. "You have me at a disadvantage."

"Ryn Vasq. I run freight over the pass." He said it lightly, the way a man says a thing he has said a hundred times until it sounds like nothing. "Folk say you're here about books."

"Folk say correctly."

"Well." Ryn Vasq pulled out the opposite chair without asking and sat, which no one in this village had yet dared. "Then I'll save you some climbing, Reverence. There's nothing up there but the Keeper, her ledgers, and a lot of weather. She counts wagons. That's all the Gate is — a tollbooth with delusions."

"A tollbooth," Halvard repeated.

"Between us and the Emberlands. Dragons beyond, us behind, and a hundred years of nobody crossing except folk with honest cargo and honest reasons." He smiled. It was a good smile. It was also, Halvard noted, deployed exactly one beat too late. "So whatever text you're hunting, it won't be at the Gate. Try the chapel. Priests hoard worse than anyone."

It was, Halvard reflected, a masterful performance. Every sentence true, every truth arranged into a lie. *She counts wagons* — certainly. *Nothing up there but ledgers* — possibly. *Try the chapel* — deflection dressed as helpfulness, offered by a stranger who had sought him out within an hour of his arrival, which meant either the man was an informer, a protector, or a principal. In Halvard's experience those three categories overlapped more often than the manuals admitted.

"Thank you, Master Vasq," he said warmly. "I'll be sure to take the tour. Tomorrow, perhaps. Tonight I'm tired, and tired men sign careless papers." He paused. "You know, you're the first person in this village to speak to me unprompted."

Something moved behind the young man's eyes — quick, quickly buried. "Someone had to. You'd have sat there till spring otherwise."

"Perhaps." Halvard rose, dropped a silver mark beside his untouched water — an extravagance that would be talked about in the common room for a week, which was rather the point — and added, mildly: "Give my regards to the Keeper. I understand she buys her candles on Tuesdays."

He felt the stillness behind him all the way up the stairs, and smiled at it, privately, in the dark.

* * *

He did not sleep. Sleep was for men whose work was finished. Instead he sat by the window of his rented room with the shutter cracked an inch, watching the Sable Gate stand against the stars, and worked through the case as he built it, brick by brick, in the black book.

Fact: the Inquisition's own founding charter, drafted a century ago, named the Sable Gate among the *Crown's Sacred Trusts* — and then, uniquely among such trusts, exempted it from inspection. No other clause in the charter contained the word *except*. Someone had wanted the Gate beyond reach, and had been powerful enough, a hundred years ago, to make it so.

Fact: the exemption's author was unrecorded. The signature line bore a seal he had never seen anywhere else — a thorn wreath around a closed eye. He had spent four months in the archives looking for that seal. He had found it exactly once more: pressed into the flyleaf of a book listed in the 60-year-old confiscation rolls as *burned*, title unknown, provenance Thornfell.

Books listed as burned were sometimes actually burned. Sometimes they were simply listed that way. Halvard had learned to distrust the word *burned* the way other men distrusted the word *voluntary*.

Fact: three days ago, a capital bookseller with Inquisition ties had reported a buyer asking, in a whisper, after *prophecy concerning the Sable Gate*. The buyer had paid in old coin.

Old coin. The same dead currency the keeper had spent on candles.

Which meant the book — if there was a book — was already here. It had come home. And whatever it said, Ada Okafor had thought it worth a case of candles and a lie about market-days, and Ryn Vasq had thought it worth charming an inquisitor within the hour of his arrival.

Halvard blew out his candle and watched the Gate instead. Snow smoked off its arch in the wind, and once — he told himself it was the wind, and wrote it down anyway — the darkness inside the arch seemed to pulse, slow as a sleeping heart.

Here was the thing his superiors would never understand, the thing that had made his career difficult and his conscience complicated: Halvard Brann did not love burning books. He loved *truth*, and he had learned the hard way that truth and the Inquisition were only occasionally allies. He hunted heretical texts because heretical texts were where institutions hid their crimes — wrapped in blasphemy, sealed with fear. Nine times in ten, when you pried open a forbidden book, you did not find a threat to the realm.

You found a receipt.

Somewhere under that arch, behind a keeper's meticulous ledgers, was a receipt a century old. And someone had gone to great lengths — an exemption clause, a false burning, a village that kept the wrong time — to make sure no one ever came to collect.

He slept at last in the grey hour before dawn, and dreamed of a thorn wreath around a closed eye, opening.

* * *

Morning came white and silent. Halvard dressed, loaded his satchel with the tools of his trade — wax seals, writ papers, the small black book, and a vial of lampblack for taking impressions — and set out on foot for the gatehouse, declining the mule the innkeeper offered. He wanted to arrive the way weather arrives: gradually, unavoidably, giving everyone on the mountain time to decide what they would do about him.

The pass road wound up through pine and shadow. Halfway, he passed a shrine — small, ancient, half-swallowed by lichen — and stopped, because habit demanded it. Three names were carved above the alcove, worn nearly smooth:

*For the keeping, and the kept.*

No dates. No titles. Just that, and beneath it, in smaller letters so eroded he had to brush away snow and trace the grooves with a fingertip: *let neither realm forget what was given.*

Let neither realm. Not *the realm*. Two realms, named as equals, in a shrine on imperial soil, carved — he judged by the letterforms — roughly a century ago. And then forgotten, or made to be forgotten, so thoroughly that the archive held nothing.

Halvard stood in the cold a long moment, snow settling on his shoulders, and felt the peculiar vertigo of a man realizing the ground under his feet has a floor beneath it, and the floor has a cellar, and the cellar goes down farther than anyone will admit.

At the top of the pass, the gatehouse resolved out of the morning mist: a low stone building crouched against the arch itself, smoke rising from its chimney in a thin, disciplined line. A woman stood in the doorway. She had been waiting — he saw that immediately, saw it in the way she stood, weight even, arms loose, a woman who had decided hours ago exactly how this meeting would begin and had merely been waiting for the world to catch up.

Dark-skinned, grey-templed, with the stillness of someone who had spent a lifetime being the only fixed point in a landscape of weather. Her eyes went to him, measured him, and dismissed him in the space of a breath — not from arrogance, he realized, but from arithmetic. He watched her do it. Watched her decide what he was worth.

"Keeper Okafor," he said, and bowed precisely as deep as protocol required and no deeper. "Halvard Brann, of the Holy Inquisition. I've come to inspect the trust."

"The trust is in order," she said.

"I'm sure it is." He smiled, and meant it, which was the most dangerous thing about him. "That's rarely the problem, Keeper. It's the *order* I've come about."

Behind her, through the open doorway, he glimpsed the interior of the gatehouse — the ledger desk, the lamp, the neat ranks of supplies. And beside the hearth, a floorboard sitting a hair's width proud of its neighbors, as though it had been lifted recently and replaced by someone whose competence extended to everything in the world except carpentry.

Ada Okafor did not follow his gaze. That was the tell. A guilty woman looks. An innocent woman looks. Only a woman performing innocence for a man she has already assessed as dangerous keeps her eyes perfectly, deliberately still.

Halvard Brann folded his hands, breathed the thin bright air, and prepared to spend several days being sincerely, unfailingly courteous to the most interesting liar he had ever met.

"I wonder," he said, "if I might trouble you for a cup of tea."
