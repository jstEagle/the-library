## 6. The Last Dragon's Summons

Ada did not mean to sleep.

She told herself she would sit up with the prophecy book until dawn, the way she had sat up with ledgers during hard winters, cataloguing every line until the lines made sense or stopped mattering. She lit two lamps instead of one. She set the book on the table at arm's length, open to the page with her name circled in century-old ink, and she told herself that a keeper who fell asleep on watch deserved whatever came to her.

The fire in the grate burned low. The gate hummed its nightly note through the stone — that deep, patient vibration she had known since childhood, the sound of the covenant holding, of the pass held shut against everything that wanted through it.

She meant only to rest her eyes.

* * *

The Emberlands smelled like her childhood.

That was the first wrong thing. Ada had never been past the Sable Gate; no Okafor had crossed it in living memory except in the pages of sealed reports. But the air that filled her lungs was warm and mineral-sweet, thick with the scent of hot stone after rain and something older underneath — a smell like iron left in the sun, like blood baked into rock over centuries. It was the smell of the gatehouse hearth when she was six years old and her mother still alive, before duty had worn her mother down to a schedule and a frown.

She stood on a hillside that should not exist.

Grass ran down the slope in waves, silver-green, each blade tipped with a faint ember-glow as if the land itself were banked coals pretending to be meadow. Beyond it, valleys opened in long green folds toward mountains that burned without smoke — veins of molten light running through their flanks like the glowing seams of a forge. The sky was dusk-colored but sourceless, a deep violet with no sun anywhere in it, and yet everything was lit, softly, from below.

Beautiful. That was the second wrong thing. Every report, every warning placard, every story the Crown paid bards to sing described the Emberlands as ash and hunger, a wasteland of fire and teeth. This looked like the world before winter had ever been invented.

"Reports lie," Ada said aloud, and her voice sounded strange here, too close, as if the air carried it further than air should.

"They do," said a voice like a landslide learning to speak gently. "It is the first thing any realm learns to do well."

She turned, and turning took too long, the way it does in dreams, her body lagging behind her intent. When she finally faced the voice, she had to tilt her head all the way back.

The dragon lay coiled across three hillsides.

Vessarath. She knew the name the way she knew her own — from the oldest ledger in the gatehouse archive, the one written in a hand so fine it looked printed: *Vessarath, last of the Emberborn, signatory and bound.* He was vast beyond sense, his scales the black of cooled lava shot through with fading seams of orange, like a fire dying inside a mountain. His wings lay folded along his flanks, and where they folded she could see the thinness of him, the architecture of ribs showing through hide that had once been armor. One eye was open. It was the size of a cart wheel and the color of a banked hearth, and it regarded her with an expression she had no training for, because no ledger had prepared her to read grief on something this old.

"You are dreaming," he said. "But you are also here. Both things are true. Do not waste time being frightened of that."

"I'm not frightened," Ada said, which was a lie, and by the way his nostril flared — a movement like a bellows shifting — she understood that lying was useless here. "I'm a keeper. I don't frighten easily."

"No," Vessarath agreed. "You endure. I have watched your family endure for four generations. It is not the same thing as courage, but it is adjacent to it, and I have learned to be grateful for adjacencies." His head lowered, slow as a drawbridge, until that burning eye was level with her. Heat rolled off him in waves, but it did not burn; it felt like standing near the gate itself, that familiar pressure of contained power. "You have the book."

Not a question. Ada's hand went to her belt out of reflex and found nothing — of course nothing, she was asleep in a chair in Thornfell with the book on her table a hundred miles away. But the dragon's eye flickered, satisfied, and she understood that he hadn't needed her answer.

"A peddler sold it to me. Dying. He said—" She stopped. In the dream, saying things felt dangerous, as if words spoken here became objects that could be picked up later by other hands.

"He said it was yours to read. He was right." The great head turned, and Ada followed his gaze down the valley, to where the green grass gave way to a stretch of gray — actual gray, colorless ground where the ember-tips had gone dark, frost glittering on stone in a land that had never known frost. "You have seen what the book says about tonight. I felt you reading it. Words written in my fire carry warmth; I know when they are read, the way you would know if someone walked across your grave."

"That's a grim way to put it."

"I am a grim creature, daughter of Thornfell. Grimness is what remains when everything else has been taken." He exhaled, and the breath moved the silver grass in waves, and somewhere far below, a river of light dimmed slightly as it passed. "Come. There are things you must see before you decide what tonight means. And you will decide. That is the cruelty of it. Your family made certain there would always be a choice, and then made certain the choices were all terrible."

He extended one foreleg — an invitation, absurdly courtly, like a man offering an arm. Ada hesitated for exactly as long as it took her pride to lose the argument with her curiosity. Then she stepped onto the scaled wrist and climbed, hand over hand, finding holds in the natural ridges of him, until she sat in the hollow behind his skull where the neck met the shoulder, and the whole burning country lay open beneath them.

"Hold on to the spine-ridge," Vessarath said. "I am old, but I remember how to be gentle."

* * *

He did not fly. He walked, and somehow that was worse — each footfall shivering through the hills, each stride covering a distance that should have taken her a day of climbing. From her perch she could see the Emberlands laid out like a map drawn by someone who loved it.

And like a map of a body dying.

The gray patches were everywhere once she knew to look. A valley where the rivers of light had thinned to threads. A forest of trees like black glass, half of them dark, their ember-hearts gone cold. Frost in the hollows. Frost, in the land of fire.

"The covenant feeds on dragon-fire," Vessarath said as he walked. "That is its engine. Your gate is not a wall, keeper. It is a tap. It draws from us — from me, now, since I am what remains — and it uses that fire to hold the pass, to bind the border, to keep the realms separate and quiet and safe." He said *safe* the way a starving man says *bread*. "Your side calls this peace. My side called it theft, for the first fifty years. Then we called it survival, because those who resisted were... reduced. Now we call it nothing. We have run out of words. We are running out of fire faster."

"How much longer?"

"Ask me again at midwinter." The joke landed like a stone in still water. "The book you hold speaks truly. Tonight the gate strains. Whether it fails depends on choices not yet made. But understand this, keeper: even if tonight passes quietly, the arithmetic does not change. The covenant takes more than I can give. It was designed to."

"Designed to?" Ada leaned forward against the spine-ridge. "By whom? The treaty records say—"

"The treaty records say the Emberlands attacked first. That dragons burned the human kingdoms until the brave signatories forged a binding to protect the innocent." Vessarath's voice stayed level, but the seams along his flanks brightened, orange flushing toward white, and the air around them grew hot enough that Ada's eyes watered. "I was there. I am the only one who was there. Would you like to hear what actually happened?"

The hillside crested, and below them, in a bowl of dark stone, stood a ruin.

It had been a shrine once — she recognized the architecture, the same severe lines as the old waymarkers along the Thornfell pilgrim roads, but built at dragon scale, pillars like felled towers. Most of it had fallen. What remained standing was a single carved face, thirty feet tall, weathered nearly smooth: a woman with her hands raised, palms out, in a gesture that could have been blessing or could have been pushing something away.

At the statue's base, half-buried in frost-killed grass, lay a keystone.

Ada knew it instantly, the way you know your own handwriting. The Sable Gate's keystone sat above the pass, forty feet up, and she had climbed to it every year on the night of renewal to check its seals. This was its twin — or rather, this was the original, and the one in her gate was the copy. Carved from the same black stone, marked with the same interlocking sigils, the knot-pattern of the covenant that no living scholar could fully translate.

Except the sigils on this one were broken. Chiseled through. Deliberately.

"This was the first gate," Vessarath said. "Before there was a Sable Gate, there was this — a meeting place. Open. The Emberfolk and your people traded here. Married here. Argued here, at length, badly, wonderfully. For three hundred years, the border between our realms was a conversation." His tail curled around the ruin, almost tenderly, the way a man might put his arm around a grave. "Then came the Scarcity. A blight in the human lands — your histories call it the Gray Harvest. Crops failed for seven years. Your people starved at our border while our valleys burned green and full."

"And the dragons wouldn't help?"

"One dragon offered to help." The eye turned to her, and Ada felt very small and very seen. "Me. I proposed opening the pass. Sharing fire freely, teaching your smiths and healers what fire could do beyond burning. The council of that age refused — not from malice, but from fear. Fire given can become fire owed. They had seen what humans did with steel and feared what they would do with flame. So the offer died in council, and the Gray Harvest continued, and a woman came across the mountains alone to change that."

The wind moved through the ruin. Somewhere in it, Ada thought she heard voices — many voices, layered, arguing in a language she almost understood.

"Her name was Sefa Okafor."

The name went through Ada like cold water. Her family's founding name. The name on the oldest page of the oldest ledger. The name her father had made her memorize before he taught her to write her own.

"She was not a soldier or a queen. She was a scribe — a translator, one of the few humans who spoke Old Emberish fluently enough to argue with a dragon and win. And she came to me with a proposal wrapped in compassion, which is how the worst ideas always arrive. The humans were dying. She could not bear it. She asked me for fire, and I said yes, and together we began to build a channel for it — a controlled flow, warded, safe. The keystone you see below us was the heart of it. We carved it together, her hands and my fire. I trusted her." The seams along his throat pulsed, dimming. "I want you to understand that clearly, keeper, because everything that follows happened because I trusted her."

"What did she do?"

"She lied." The word came out flat, exhausted, a word he had carried so long it had lost all its edges. "The channel we built was meant to be a door. She made it a leash. While my fire was in the stone, shaping it, she wrote bindings into the sigils that I could not see — bindings keyed to her own bloodline. The covenant did not share dragon-fire with humanity. It *bound* dragon-fire to humanity's need. The gate drew from us automatically, endlessly, according to terms only an Okafor could alter. And the terms were written so that the more humans suffered, the more fire was taken. Our plenty became your relief. Our decline became your stability. She stole our choice and called it mercy, and she wrote it all in Old Emberish because she knew none of her own people would ever bother to learn to read it."

Ada's mouth was dry. In dreams, she supposed, you couldn't be thirsty, but her body disagreed. "The renewal rites. The keeper's vigil. The—"

"Blood," Vessarath said simply. "Every generation, the covenant demands a renewal, and every generation an Okafor provides it. You have performed the rite yourself. A cut palm, pressed to the keystone, a night of pain, a week of weakness. You believed you were paying a toll to keep the peace." He laughed, and it was a terrible sound, like rockfall in a deep place. "You were feeding it. Your blood is the signature on the contract, renewed. Your family did not guard the gate, child. Your family *is* the gate. The lock and the key, grown into the door."

The hillside seemed to tilt. Ada gripped the spine-ridge and stared down at the broken keystone, at the chiseled-through sigils, and thought of every palm-cut she had ever pressed to black stone. Eleven of them. Eleven renewals. She had a scar for each, a thin pale line across her left palm like a sentence written in a language she'd never been allowed to read.

"My father knew," she said. It wasn't a question either.

"Every keeper knows, by the end. Some are told. Some figure it out. Your father found your grandmother's private papers — I believe you inherited a locked box you have never managed to open." Her hand twitched; the dragon saw. "The box will open for you soon. Grief is often the key. But that is a matter for another night. Look at me, Ada Okafor."

She looked. It was like looking into a furnace that had learned to be sad.

"Why show me this? Why now? If your faction wants the gate broken — if that peddler and his pages and Ryn Vasq's smuggling runs are all part of some plan — why tell me the truth instead of just using me?"

"Because the plan requires you to choose," Vessarath said, "and a choice made by someone who does not know the truth is not a choice. It is a habit. Your ancestors' sin was committed in compassion and concealed in duty, and it has been renewed for a hundred years by keepers who never chose anything at all — they simply kept, the way a knife keeps its edge. I will not make you a knife, daughter of the forger. I would rather die unfree than live another century on stolen fire purchased with your ignorance."

*Daughter of the forger.* The words settled on her like snow. She waited for anger to rise and defend her ancestor, defend her blood, defend the eleven scars and the lonely decades and the whole architecture of her life. It didn't come. What came instead was memory: her father's face the last time he pressed her hand to the keystone, the way he had looked away, the way he had said *it's necessary, Ada* in a voice that meant *don't ask me what it's necessary for.*

He had known. He had fed her to the covenant anyway, one cut at a time, because that was what Okafors did.

"Dawn is coming on your side," Vessarath said. The violet sky was lightening at its edges, though there was still no sun. "I have kept you longer than I intended. Before you go, you must know the shape of what stands before you, so you cannot claim surprise. There are three roads from tonight. The gate renews, and the theft continues until I am ash, and then both realms learn what the gate was really holding back. The gate breaks, and the fire returns to us — and the border opens unwarded, and everything pent on both sides spills through at once. Or—" the great eye narrowed, and for a moment the dying fire in it flared bright, "—or someone writes new terms. Not an Okafor's secret binding. New terms, in the open, witnessed by both realms. It has never been done. It may not be possible. Your ancestor designed the covenant so that it could not be done."

"But the book—"

"The book is a map, not a verdict. Read the margins, keeper. Margins are where the truth hides when the text belongs to liars." He lowered his shoulder, an offer and a dismissal at once. "One more thing. The Crown's hounds are already on your road. A man named Brann. When he comes — and he will come — remember that institutions send sincere men to do dishonest work, and that sincerity is more dangerous than cruelty, because it cannot be bought."

"How do you know about Brann?"

"I am a dragon, Ada Okafor. Knowing things is the last power I have left." The heat of him wrapped around her like a blanket as the dream began to thin at its edges, the silver grass going gray, the burning mountains receding. "Go home. Open the box. Read the margins. And when you press your palm to the keystone next — if you press it — know exactly what you are signing."

The last thing she saw was the broken keystone in its bowl of frost, and the statue above it, the woman with her raised hands, and Ada understood at last that Sefa Okafor had not been blessing anyone.

She had been holding the door shut.

* * *

Ada woke in the gatehouse chair with both lamps burned out and dawn graying the windows, her left palm throbbing.

She turned it over. The eleven scars lay pale across it, familiar as her own heartbeat — and beside them, faint but unmistakable, a twelfth mark was forming: a thin red line, curved like the beginning of a sigil, like ink rising under skin.

Like something writing itself.

Outside, hoofbeats came up the pass road — too many, too disciplined for smugglers, too early for pilgrims. Ada closed her fist around the new scar, took the prophecy book off the table, and went to find somewhere to hide it before whoever was riding up the mountain arrived at her door.
