## 7. Blood Price

Ada woke with the taste of ash in her mouth and her own name ringing in her ears.

*Daughter of the forger.*

She lay in the narrow bed above the gatehouse, the grey light of not-quite-dawn seeping through the shutters, and waited for the dream to dissolve the way dreams did — into nonsense, into fog, into nothing she'd have to answer for. It didn't. It sat in her chest like a swallowed coal. Vessarath's voice had been old beyond reckoning, a voice like a landslide deciding whether to fall, and it had known her name before she'd given it.

She got up, dressed, and did what she always did when the world tilted: she went downstairs and opened the ledger.

The ledger didn't tilt. The ledger was four hundred pages of names and goods and tolls paid, in her own neat hand, going back through three keepers before her — her mother, her grandmother, her great-grandfather Edran Okafor, whose handwriting had been cramped and furious, as if he were writing against something. Ada ran her finger down his columns and felt the dream pressing against the back of her teeth.

*Your house built the gate,* the dragon had said. *Not to keep us out. To keep us bleeding.*

"Ridiculous," she said aloud, because the gatehouse was empty and no one would hear her talk to herself, which was its own small humiliation.

But she took down the family records anyway.

They lived in the locked cabinet behind the hearth — the one every keeper swore an oath over at their investiture, the one Ada had assumed held tax rolls and land grants and the tedious paperwork of stewardship. Her mother had opened it exactly once in Ada's memory, on the night she handed over the keys, and whatever she'd read there had made her hands shake so badly she'd poured tea on the floor instead of into the cups. *You'll understand when it's yours,* she'd said. Then she'd died two winters later without explaining anything, the way Okafor women apparently did things.

Ada unlocked the cabinet with the small iron key she wore on the same ring as the gate keys, and had never once used.

Inside: a bundle of letters tied with black cord. A leather case holding a quill that looked ordinary until you turned it and saw the nib was stained dark brown, permanently, no amount of cleaning ever touching it. And a book bound in grey hide — not the prophecy book; this one was older, fatter, its spine cracked like riverbed clay in drought.

The title page said only: *Terms of Keeping. Set Down by Edran Okafor, Third Keeper of the Sable Gate, in the Year of Ash.*

Ada sat down at her desk, the same desk where she'd audited ten thousand smuggler manifests, and began to read.

* * *

The first forty pages were what she expected — boundary descriptions, toll schedules, the formal language of treaty. She skimmed them with growing impatience, almost relieved. Old paperwork. Dust and ceremony. Whatever Vessarath had shown her in the dream, here was the actual record, and the actual record was boring, and boring meant safe.

Then she reached page forty-one.

*On the Matter of Sustenance.*

The handwriting changed here. Edran's cramped fury gave way to something slower, more deliberate, the script of a man choosing each word the way you'd choose footing on scree.

*I set down now what I have never spoken aloud, and what my heirs must know, though God and the Emberlands forgive me for making them know it.*

*The gate does not run on stone. Stone is only the vessel. The gate runs on fire, and fire does not burn for free.*

*When we made the covenant — when I made it, let the histories say what they like, it was my hand and my price — we did not ask the dragons to share their flame. We took it. Vessarath came to the pass under a flag of parley, and my grandfather fed him wine laced with ground keystone dust, and while he slept we drove the first binding through him. You have seen the wound. Every keeper has seen the wound, if they knew to look: the scar across the dragon's chest shaped as our keystone is shaped. That is not a mark of alliance. That is a lock. He is the well, and we built the pump.*

Ada's hand stopped moving.

She read the sentence again. Then again. The fire in the hearth popped, and she flinched hard enough to knock the inkwell, catching it mid-slide with a reflex she didn't know she had.

*The flame flows from him through the keystone and out along the veins we cut into the mountain — you have seen them glow on the cold nights and called it pretty. It warms our villages. It ripens our orchards a month early. It keeps the Thornfell passes open all winter, and the Crown calls this the miracle of the covenant. It is not a miracle. It is a tap left running from a living body.*

*And because fire spent must be renewed, the binding requires renewal. Not by masons. Not by priests. By blood.*

Here Edran's pen had pressed so hard it had torn the page slightly, a thin white scar running through the words.

*A keeper's blood, freely given at the keystone, once each generation. A cupful, no more — the binding is greedy but not wasteful. The keeper who gives it does not die. I want that written plainly, because I have heard the young romanticize it. You do not die. You give, and the gate takes, and something else goes with the blood that I have never found a word for. My grandfather gave the first cup and afterward he could not abide the smell of woodsmoke. He burned the kitchen wing rather than live near the hearth. My father gave his and forgot the face of his own brother — not the name, the face, looked at a portrait and asked who the stranger was. When I gave mine, I lost—*

The rest of the line was scratched out. Scratched out thoroughly, furiously, the paper nearly worn through.

*You will be told, at your investiture, that the rite is a blessing. You will be told the dragon consents. Look at the consent clause. Read it as I should have read it, all those years ago, before I signed. "Consent given in perpetuity by the bound party." We wrote his consent for him. We wrote it into his flesh. There has been no choice in the Emberlands for ninety years, and there will be none for ninety more, unless one of us finds a braver ending than I did.*

*I did not find one. I am telling you how to look for it instead.*

Below that, in different ink, different decades — a list. Ada's eyes went down it and her stomach went with them.

*Renewals of the Binding:*

*Maren Okafor, Second Keeper. Given at the keystone, spring. Loss reported: the sound of birdsong.*

*Edran Okafor, Third Keeper. Given at the keystone, autumn. Loss reported: [illegible].*

*Sela Okafor, Fourth Keeper. Given at the keystone, summer. Loss reported: color. Saw only grey thereafter.*

*Tomas Okafor, Fifth Keeper. Given at the keystone, winter. Loss reported: [entry torn away].*

*Ilse Okafor, Sixth Keeper. Given at the keystone, spring. Loss reported: memory of her son's voice.*

*Yara Okafor, Seventh Keeper. Given at the keystone, autumn. Loss reported: hope. Wrote in her final letter that she felt nothing whatsoever for eleven years and wished someone had warned her.*

*Merin Okafor, Eighth Keeper. Given at the keystone, winter. Loss reported: [blank].*

*Veyra Okafor, Ninth Keeper. Given at the keystone, spring. Loss reported: [blank].*

*Ada Okafor, Tenth Keeper. Entry pending.*

Her own name. In ink that had dried decades before she was born, in her great-great-grandmother's careful hand, waiting for her like a seat kept at a table.

Ada pushed back from the desk so fast the chair went over backward. She stood in the middle of the gatehouse floor breathing like she'd run the pass twice, and the ledger lay open where she'd left it, and the morning light came through the shutters in grey bars, and everything was exactly where she'd put it last night except the entire shape of her life.

Every keeper's oddness. She'd grown up on them, absorbed them as family texture the way you absorb a limp or a temper. Great-aunt Sela who described sunsets as "bright" and "dark" and nothing between. Grandmother Ilse, who'd gone silent whenever anyone sang, who had once asked Ada — Ada, who had been standing right there talking to her — to describe what her mother sounded like. Her own mother, Yara's successor Merin's successor Veyra's—

Her mother. Who had shaken over the tea. Who had said *you'll understand when it's yours.* Who had died before her renewal was due, and whose death, Ada now understood with a horror that arrived slowly and then all at once, might itself have been the entry in the column. *Loss reported:* everything.

"No," Ada said. "No, no."

Because here was the thing her mind kept sliding around, the thing she couldn't stop looking at directly any more than you can look at the sun: she had been giving blood to the gate for years. Small cuts, small pricks — the rite of maintenance, performed quarterly, taught to her at fifteen as simple as feeding a stove. Prick your thumb, press it to the keystone's base, speak the keeping-words. She'd done it a hundred times. She'd done it *last week.*

And what had she lost?

She tried to inventory herself the way she'd inventory a smuggler's cart, item by item, and found she couldn't do it. That was the trap, wasn't it? How do you miss what's gone when the gone leaves no hole? You can't count pockets you don't remember having. Somewhere in her there might be a whole country of feeling, of sense, of self, razed to make room for the gate's appetite, and she would walk through the ruins of it forever without knowing ruins from sky.

She thought of Ryn Vasq, and hated herself for it, and thought of him anyway. *You watch the road like a woman counting exits,* he'd told her once, half-drunk on his own terrible plum brandy, laughing. *I've watched you for six years, Keeper, and I couldn't tell you one true thing about what you love.* She'd thrown him out of the gatehouse for that. She'd told herself he was a smuggler and liars get thrown out of places.

He hadn't been lying.

* * *

She didn't remember lighting the lamp, but it was burning when she next looked up, and the light through the shutters had gone gold and low. Afternoon. She'd lost hours to the grey book, reading it twice, three times, taking notes in the margin of her own audit ledger because her hands needed to do something orderly or she was going to scream.

The terms were clear. That was the worst part — how clear they were, how administrative. Her ancestors hadn't just committed the crime; they'd documented it beautifully. Indexed it. Cross-referenced it. Somewhere in the Emberlands a dragon was dying by inches into a pump mechanism, and the Okafor family had filed it under *Sustenance, On the Matter of.*

Page sixty-three held the clause she'd been dreading and found anyway:

*Should the renewal lapse past the appointed season, the binding shall not gently fade. Fire withheld curdles. The veins will run hot and then cold, the valleys of the Emberlands will frost even as the pass burns, and the bound party — see Addendum on the Bound Party's Vitality — will suffer the withdrawal unto death. The gate itself will fail within a turning of the moon, and what comes through a failed gate is not covered by these terms.*

A turning of the moon. The peddler's scorched book had named tonight. The gate had flared and counted down in a dead language. And somewhere north of the pass, a dragon with a keystone-shaped wound was running out of time because a woman named Ada Okafor had been too busy auditing smugglers to bleed on schedule.

Except she hadn't missed it. She'd done the rite. Thumb, keystone, words. So why was the gate counting down?

She flipped to the addendum, hunting, and found the answer in a paragraph she'd already read twice without understanding:

*Note well: the binding draws freely only from a willing keeper. Blood taken under duress sours the vein and must be purged — hence the flare and the counting, which the gate performs of itself when it rejects tainted offering. If the keeper's heart turns against the covenant — if she gives her blood while wishing it unwelcome — the gate will know, and the gate will refuse, and the appointed season will pass unfilled.*

Ada stared at it.

Last quarter's rite. She remembered it with terrible clarity now: kneeling at the keystone in the dark, thumb already bleeding, speaking the keeping-words — and hesitating. Just for a breath. Just long enough for a thought she'd never let herself think to surface whole: *why should it be me, why should it be us, why should anyone pay for a bargain struck before her grandmother's grandmother drew breath.*

One breath of doubt. One unwilling heartbeat. And the gate had tasted it, and refused her, and begun to die.

She had broken the covenant without lifting a finger against it. All she'd done was mean something.

Ada laughed — one short, ugly sound with no humor anywhere in it — and pressed both hands flat against the desk to stop them shaking. Outside, far up the pass, the evening wind was coming down off the peaks, and beneath it, faint as a pulse, she could feel the gate humming. Restless. Hungry. Counting.

There was a solution. Of course there was; her ancestors had engineered one, tidy as a toll schedule. Go to the keystone tonight. Cut deeper than a thumb-prick. Give the blood *willingly* — truly willingly, heart aligned, doubt smothered — and the binding would renew, the countdown would stop, Vessarath would live another generation, and the villages would stay warm, and the Crown would stay calm, and everything would go on exactly as it had for nine generations of women who each walked away from the altar missing some piece of themselves they'd never get back.

All she had to do was want it.

All she had to do, knowing what she now knew — the leash, the theft, the wound, the list of losses in her great-grandmothers' hands — was kneel at that stone and give gladly.

Ada looked at the ledger. At her own name, *entry pending*, patient as a grave.

Then she looked at the window, north, toward the Emberlands, where the peaks were catching the last red light like iron pulled from a forge — and she thought she could see it, or imagined she could, the faint glow along the mountain veins dimming, guttering, a great body's warmth failing.

Somewhere out there, the thing her family had chained was dying of what her family had done. And the only way to save it by the rules was to become the next link in the chain — willingly, lovingly, complicit with her whole heart.

Footsteps on the gravel outside. Unhurried, familiar, insufferably light: Ryn Vasq, arriving at dusk as he always did, with his cart and his contraband and his eyes that saw too much.

Ada closed the grey book, slid it into her coat, and went to open the door — because whether she liked it or not, the smuggler who knew her secrets was about to learn the biggest one.

And because she had decided, somewhere between the torn page and the fading light, that she was not going to kneel at that stone tonight.

Whatever came of it. Whoever froze. Whoever burned.

The chain ended with her — one way, or another.
