## 8. The Road Through Thornfell

Ryn Vasq had been running from things his whole life — debt collectors in Saltmere, a husband in Corrow Bay, the Crown's excise men twice, and once, memorably, a bear that had opinions about his lunch. He knew how to run. What he didn't know how to do was run *with* Ada Okafor, because Ada Okafor did not run so much as relocate with intent, and she expected everyone around her to keep up.

"Left," she said, without slowing.

He went left. The trail she'd chosen wasn't a trail at all — it was a seam in the rock face, barely wide enough for his shoulders, hidden behind a curtain of dead bracken that she swept aside like she owned it. Which, he supposed, after twenty years of keeping this pass, she more or less did.

"You could have told me you were going to burn your own ledger," he said, catching up to her on the other side of the seam. "I had a whole speech prepared about how destroying evidence is a crime."

"So is smuggling."

"I've made my peace with my crimes. You're new to yours. I wanted to watch."

She shot him a look over her shoulder. In the grey pre-dawn light her face was all angles and exhaustion, and there was ash still smudged along her jaw from shoving the prophecy book into the gatehouse wall-cavity an hour before Brann's riders came up the switchbacks. "You got your speech out?"

"The important parts."

"Then save the rest." She turned back to the path. "We have maybe four hours before Brann widens the search. Less if he's smart."

"He's smart," Ryn said. "That's what worries me. Stupid inquisitors kick doors. Smart ones knock first and count who answers."

Ada said nothing to that, but her pace picked up, which was answer enough.

They climbed. Thornfell in the dark was a country of sound — water somewhere below them, wind combing the pines, the far-off complaint of a mountain goat that had no idea how good its life was. Ryn kept one ear tuned behind them for hoofbeats and the other on Ada's breathing, which was steady but too controlled, the breathing of someone managing pain or fear and refusing to name either.

The gatehouse was two miles gone when he finally asked it. "What did the book say? Exactly. Not the version you gave Halvard Brann over tea."

"I didn't give him anything over tea."

"You gave him something. You were smoother than you've ever been, which means you were lying, which means you gave him exactly enough truth to be believable and not one word more." He ducked under a leaning birch. "So. The real version."

Her boots crunched to a stop on a shelf of granite. Below them the pass unspooled in the last of the starlight, the Sable Gate itself just visible as a darker darkness in the mountainside, its arch faintly warm even now — he could see the heat-shimmer off it, like air above a summer road. It had been doing that since midnight. Since the date circled in century-old ink.

"It says tonight," Ada said quietly. "It says the gate fails tonight. And next to the date, in the same old ink, someone wrote my name."

Ryn felt the words land in his chest like dropped stones. He'd carried pages of that book across the pass for months — folded into bales of salt wool, sewn into coat linings, once inside a hollowed-out wheel of cheese that still haunted him — and he had never read them whole. Fragments only. A line here, a phrase there, enough to know they were worth money to the people paying and dangerous to everyone else. He had told himself he was a courier, not a believer.

"My name," Ada repeated, "in ink older than my grandmother."

"That's not possible."

"No," she agreed. "It isn't. And yet." She started walking again, faster now, angrier. "So either the book is a fraud, or the fraud is very old, or—" She stopped herself. Ryn watched her jaw work through whatever the third option was and decide not to say it aloud. Saying it would make it load-bearing.

*Or the book knows something about your family you don't,* he thought, and kept it behind his teeth, because he was a smuggler, not a fool, and there was a difference between carrying secrets and handing them to a woman with a knife and a grudge against the universe.

* * *

An hour past dawn they came down into the Hollow Way, and Ryn allowed himself the small vanity of watching her reaction.

Most people saw the Hollow Way and thought: landslide. It looked like one — a mile-long field of tumbled black stone where the mountain had shrugged sometime in the last century, choked with scree and deadfall, impassable, pointless, marked on no map any Crown surveyor had ever drawn. That was the point. There was a road through it, if you knew where to put your feet. His crew knew. Nobody else alive knew, except apparently the Keeper of the Sable Gate, who picked her way across the third fallen slab as confidently as if she'd built it.

"How," he said.

"Ledger-keeper," she said. "Every route through this pass crosses my desk eventually. Yours included. You think I let you run salt wool and Emberish relics under my nose for six years because I'm bad at my job?"

"I think you let me run them because I split the profits with the garrison cook and you liked the cheese."

"I *loved* the cheese," she admitted. "But I also mapped every road you ever used. The Hollow Way. The Widow's Stair. The culvert under the old shrine road. You weren't smuggling *past* me, Ryn. You were smuggling *through* me."

He stopped on a flat stone and stared at her. Somewhere in the last six years, the picture he'd built of himself — clever, invisible, untouchable — quietly rearranged itself into a picture of a man walking down a hallway with glass walls, waving.

"You knew the whole time."

"I knew most of the time. The cheese surprised me. That was genuinely good work." She glanced back at him, and there — buried under the dread and the fury and whatever the gate had done to her last night — was the ghost of a smile. "Don't look wounded. If I'd shut you down, some worse smuggler would have taken the road, one who didn't leave the pilgrim caravans alone in winter. You were the devil I preferred."

"The devil you preferred," he repeated. "I'm having that carved on my headstone."

"Keep moving and you might live long enough to need one."

They crossed the slide-field in silence after that, but it was a different silence than before — less like two strangers sharing a road and more like two people discovering they'd been sharing a room for years without turning on the lights. Ryn found he minded it less than he should have. He also found he trusted her more than he should have, and both of those facts were going to get him killed, probably today.

* * *

The dogs found them at midmorning.

Not literally — he never saw the dogs, only heard them, a thin bright crying threading down from the ridge above the shrine road, and then the answering horn from the valley floor, one note, then two, then three. Search pattern. Brann wasn't kicking doors. Brann was playing chess with a board the size of a mountain range.

"They're ahead of us," Ada breathed. They'd flattened themselves against a rock overhang above a streambed, cold water soaking through Ryn's knee within seconds. "How are they ahead of us?"

"Because you took the obvious escape," Ryn said. "No offense. The Hollow Way is the best road south and anyone who's spent a week in these mountains knows it. Brann will have a man who grew up here. Somebody's uncle ran wool through that slide-field in the bad years."

"Then we go north."

"North is the Emberlands."

"North is the Emberlands," she agreed, and the look in her eyes made his stomach drop through the wet stone beneath him.

"Absolutely not. I don't cross the Gate. Nobody crosses the Gate. That's not a rule, that's a — it's a law of nature, like gravity, like taxes—"

"Your employers paid you to carry their pages across the pass. Where did you think they were going?"

"To buyers! Collectors! Rich idiots in Saltmere who like owning forbidden things!" But even as he said it, he remembered the weight distribution of the packages he'd carried north-bound versus south-bound, the wax seals stamped with a sigil he'd never been able to place — a circle broken by a vertical line — and the way the Emberlands faction's agent, a soft-spoken woman with frost-pale eyes, had once watched him walk away with an expression he'd filed under *contempt* and was suddenly re-filing under *pity*. "Oh," he said. "Oh, no."

"Yes."

"I'm not a traitor. I want that on the record. I'm a businessman with terrible clients."

"Noted for the record." Ada was already moving, low along the streambed, and he scrambled after her. "The dogs will lose us at the water. Then we cut east through the culvert and come at the north road from below. Brann expects a fugitive to run *away* from the thing hunting her. We run toward it."

"That's insane."

"It's also the only direction where nobody's looking."

"And what happens when we get there?" He caught her sleeve, and she stopped, and for a moment they were face to face close enough that he could see the sleeplessness printed under her eyes. "Say we reach the Gate. Say we slip past your own garrison, who by now all have orders with your face on them. What's the plan, Ada? Knock on a mountain and ask the dragons for asylum?"

Something moved behind her expression — not fear exactly. Recognition. Like he'd guessed the shape of a thing she'd been refusing to look at directly.

"There's something I have to see," she said. "About the covenant. About my family. And the only place to see it is on the other side."

"Your family."

"The covenant didn't write itself, Ryn. Someone forged it. Someone human. And the book thinks that someone's blood is in me." She pulled her sleeve free, gently, which somehow stung worse than if she'd wrenched it away. "You can turn back at the culvert. Take the Widow's Stair down to Corrow Bay, disappear like you always do. I won't tell Brann where you went. As far as I'm concerned you've earned that much."

Ryn stood in the freezing stream and considered his options, which were: one, run south and spend the rest of his life knowing he'd left her; two, run south and spend the rest of his life not knowing what happened, which was somehow worse; three, follow a woman he'd been professionally deceiving for six years toward a border that ate people, pursued by the Inquisition, on the day a hundred-year-old book said the world would crack.

"You're a terrible client," he told her. "Worse than the frost-eyed woman. At least she paid in advance."

"Is that a yes?"

"That's a yes." He sighed and started upstream. "But when this goes wrong — and it will go wrong, Ada, I want history to show I predicted it would go wrong — I want it noted that I chose this. Nobody bought me. Nobody blackmailed me. I walked into the Emberlands of my own free stupid will."

"Noted," she said, and this time the smile made it all the way to her eyes, brief and tired and real, and Ryn Vasq, veteran of a hundred betrayals and exactly zero acts of loyalty, felt something in his chest turn over like a boat coming about.

* * *

The culvert was everything he remembered and less: a dripping throat of cut stone beneath the ruined shrine road, big enough to crouch-walk, home to bats who objected loudly to the intrusion. Halfway through, wedged in a crevice where the mortar had crumbled, Ada stopped so abruptly he walked into her back.

"What—"

"Look."

In the torchlight — she'd lit a stub of candle, reckless, necessary — he saw what she'd seen. Scratched into the old stone, low, half-hidden by moss: letters. Old Emberish, the angular script the dragon-clans used before the covenant froze their tongues to formal rite. Ryn couldn't read it. But he recognized the sigil beside it, the circle broken by a vertical line, the same seal that rode every package he'd ever carried north.

And below it, smaller, newer, in a human hand he did recognize — neat, upright, unmistakably a ledger-keeper's hand:

*Someone has been reading the walls.*

Ada's fingers hovered over the fresh carving without touching it. "This is my handwriting," she whispered. "I've never been here."

The candle guttered though there was no draft. Far off, muffled by a mile of mountain, the Sable Gate gave a sound like a struck bell heard underwater, and the bats poured past them toward the daylight, screaming.

"Change of plan," Ryn said, already moving. "We run."
