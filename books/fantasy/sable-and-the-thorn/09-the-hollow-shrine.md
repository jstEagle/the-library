## 9. The Hollow Shrine

The storm found them an hour past dark.

It came down the eastern face of Thornfell like something with intent — not the soft grey rain of the lowlands but a hard, sideways sleet that turned the smuggler road into a river of ice and made every handhold a gamble. Ada climbed it anyway, because the road was hers, because she had walked it a thousand times in better weather and twice in worse, and because stopping meant freezing, and freezing meant Brann's riders would find two bodies instead of a trail gone cold.

"Tell me again," Ryn shouted over the wind, "why we couldn't have taken the low road."

"The low road has a toll station on it."

"We could pay a toll."

"With what? Your charm?" She hauled herself over a lip of black rock and reached back for his wrist. His fingers were bloodless with cold. "The toll station reports to the garrison at Hallowmere. The garrison reports to the Crown. And the Crown, as of this morning, wants me in chains."

"And as of this morning I'm an accessory." He pulled himself up beside her, breath ragged, hair plastered to his skull. "You know, most people thank their accomplices."

"I'll thank you when we're alive."

She said it lightly, and she didn't mean it lightly at all. That was the thing about fear she had learned in thirty years behind the gate: you wrapped it in dry humor or it wrapped itself around your throat. But tonight the wrapping kept slipping. Tonight her hands shook on the rock, and not from cold alone.

*Daughter of the forger.*

Vessarath's voice was still in her ears, still under her skin, the way a bell keeps ringing after the rope goes still. She had woken from that dream with frost on the inside of the window and the word *forger* burning behind her eyes like a coal she couldn't spit out.

Ahead, half-swallowed by the sleet, a shape rose out of the mountainside — a squat stone shoulder jutting from the slope, crowned with a broken arch.

"There," she said. "The Hollow Shrine."

Ryn squinted through the white. "I've run this pass eleven years. There's no shrine on my maps."

"That's because your maps are wrong." She allowed herself the ghost of a smile. "That's rather the point of smuggler roads, Ryn. Nothing on them is where anyone says it is."

* * *

The shrine had been hollowed out of the mountain itself, which was why it held warmth the way a held breath holds sound. A single chamber, maybe forty feet deep, its walls dressed in stone so old the chisel-marks had weathered to silk. Someone had built it before the gate, before the covenant, before any history Ada had ever been taught — and then someone else had filled it with the covenant, because the covenant got everywhere, the way water gets everywhere, the way guilt gets everywhere.

A fire ring sat near the entrance, swept clean. Dry wood was stacked beneath a stone shelf, seasoned and covered, exactly where a keeper would leave it.

"You knew about this place," Ryn said. He was wringing out his coat by the fire she'd coaxed alight, watching her with those flat grey eyes that missed nothing. "You've been here before."

"Every keeper comes here once. It's part of the succession." She fed another branch to the flames. "When my mother was dying, she brought me up here and showed me the walls. Told me it was where the first signatories met. Where they swore the oath." She paused. "She called it the holiest place in Thornfell."

"And you?"

"I called it a cold room full of dead people's names." She looked at the fire. "I was nineteen and very sure of myself."

"What changed?"

"Everything," Ada said. "Everything changed."

The fire cracked and settled. Outside, the storm hammered the mountain like a fist demanding entry. Ryn spread his wet boots by the flames and said nothing more, which was one of the things she had always tolerated about him — tolerated, she corrected herself, and then wondered why she'd spent years choosing a smaller word than the true one.

She took out the prophecy book.

It rode against her ribs now in an oilskin wrap, tied with cord, like a second heart. The scorched leather was still warm from her body heat, which felt obscene. She unwrapped it with hands that were almost steady and set it open on her knee to the page she'd read a hundred times since the peddler died in her gatehouse with the book already in his arms.

*On the night the moon stands empty above Thornfell, the Sable Gate shall fail, and the daughter of the forger shall stand at the breaking of the world.*

Tonight the moon stood empty above Thornfell. She'd checked. Of course she'd checked; she checked everything, that was who she was, that was all she'd ever been — the woman who audited the night, who counted every smuggler and pilgrim, who believed that if she just kept the ledger perfectly the world would hold its shape.

Beside the prophecy, in ink gone brown as old blood, someone had written a date. Century-old ink. And beneath the date, in the same cramped hand:

*She will know what to do. Pray she is braver than her grandmother.*

Ada closed the book.

"She," she said quietly. "Not 'the keeper.' Not 'a daughter of Okafor.' *She.* Whoever wrote this knew there would be a specific woman, on a specific night, standing at a specific gate." She looked up at Ryn. "Prophecies don't work like that. Prophecies are fog. This is a schedule."

"Maybe your family wrote it."

"My family *kept* it. My family guarded the gate for six generations." She heard her own voice sharpen and didn't rein it in. "My mother died at that gate. Her mother lost two fingers to gate-frost. We gave that mountain everything, Ryn. Every Okafor for a hundred and sixty years has given that mountain everything, and now some dragon tells me we're *forgers*, and some book tells me I'm a line item on a calendar—"

Her voice cracked. She stopped.

The fire popped. Ryn watched her across the flames, and for once he didn't smirk, didn't deflect, didn't reach for some crooked joke to sand the edges off a moment. He just let her sit in it, which was somehow worse and somehow kinder.

"Your grandmother," he said at last. "The book says pray she's braver than her grandmother. You know who that means?"

"I assumed it meant whoever signed the covenant."

"It might." He leaned forward, elbows on knees, firelight moving in his eyes. "Or it might mean the last Okafor woman who stood where you're standing, knowing what you're going to find out, and chose wrong."

The words went into her like a splinter. She wanted to argue and couldn't find the angle.

Because here was the thing she hadn't told him, hadn't told anyone, barely admitted to herself: her grandmother, Maren Okafor, had left the shrine once, at midwinter, sixty years ago, walking alone into a whiteout with no lantern and no reason anyone ever learned. They'd found her three days later at the treeline, frozen upright against a pine, facing the Emberlands. Facing away from home.

Ada had been seven. She remembered being told it was grief. Grandfather had died that autumn; grief explained anything if you repeated it firmly enough.

Grief did not explain why a dying woman would walk toward the dragons.

* * *

Sleep came in shallow pulls, the way it does in strange places with too much truth in the air. Ada dreamed of counting — the voice from the gate, the one that spoke Old Emberish while the stones flared, numbering down from some vast number toward some small and terrible one — and woke with her heart slamming and the fire burned to embers and Ryn already awake, sitting cross-legged, feeding it sticks.

"Storm's easing," he said. "We should talk about where we're actually going."

"Away."

"That's a direction the way 'down' is a destination." He poked the embers. "Brann will have riders on every road out of Thornfell by tomorrow noon. Your smuggler routes buy us a day, maybe two. Then we need a plan that isn't a compass point."

"The Emberlands."

He looked up sharply. "No."

"They summoned me, Ryn. Vessarath named me. Whatever the covenant is — whatever my family *did* — the answer's on the other side of that gate, and you know it."

"I know the other side of that gate is dragons." He said it flatly, without theater, which from Ryn was the equivalent of shouting. "I've seen what's left of villages that argued with dragons. I've carried the survivors' goods to market and listened to them not talk about it. You want to walk into the Emberlands on the word of a dream—"

"On the word of a dragon who is dying." She heard how it sounded even as she said it — mad, traitorous, the exact accusation Brann would make — and pressed on anyway. "Something is wrong over there. The fire's dimming. You've seen it; everyone on the pass has seen it. The Emberfolk trade less every year, the border villages starve a little more every winter, and nobody asks why because asking why means admitting the treaty we've all been hiding behind might be rotting from the inside."

"And if it is? If your family really forged this thing, however that works — what do you imagine you can do about it? Rewrite a hundred-and-sixty-year-old covenant with good intentions?"

"Yes," Ada said.

Ryn stared at her.

"I don't know how yet," she said. "I don't know what it costs. But I know that I am done being the last person to learn the truth about my own life. Whatever's in these mountains — whatever my grandmother walked out to find — I'm going to look at it with my eyes open. That's the plan. That's the whole plan. Eyes open."

For a long moment the only sound was the wind worrying the entrance arch, a low moan like something grieving.

Then Ryn nodded, once, and started pulling on his boots. "Eyes open," he said. "Fine. But we do it in daylight, and we do it fed, and first—" he tipped his head toward the darkness at the rear of the chamber, where the firelight gave out, "—we see what your holiest place is hiding back there."

* * *

She had been in the shrine four times in her life and had never gone past the fire ring.

There had never seemed to be a reason. The chamber narrowed toward the back, the walls closing in, and her mother had always drawn her aside before she wandered — *come away, Ada, the names are for keepers at the end of their service, not girls at the start of theirs* — and Ada, obedient in all the ways that mattered and none of the ways that counted, had come away.

Now she lifted the torch Ryn lit from the embers and walked into the dark.

The names began ten feet in.

They were carved in columns, floor to ceiling, rank upon rank, in a script that started formal and grew hurried as the generations passed — the signatories of the covenant, human side. Names and dates and small carved marks beside each one that Ada understood with a lurch: gate-sigils. Each name bore the mark of the Sable Gate. Each name was an Okafor, or married to one, or bound to one.

*Edran Okafor, First Keeper, Year One of the Accord.*

*Ilset Okafor, Second Keeper.*

*Maren Okafor, Sixth Keeper.*

Her grandmother's name. Fresh-cut compared to the others, though sixty years old — the grooves still sharp-edged, as if the carver had been angry, or grieving, or both. And below Maren's name, unlike all the others, there was more than a sigil. There was text. Lines of it, small and dense, cut deep enough to catch the torchlight in shadow.

Ada raised the torch.

And read.

*Let it be written where only a keeper will read it, for I cannot say it aloud and cannot carry it silent either.*

*I did not sign the covenant. I renewed it, as my mothers renewed it before me, and I tell you now what the renewal is, what they never put in any ledger, what Edran Okafor swore the first keepers to secrecy over and I swear to you, daughter-of-my-line, whoever you are, reading this by torchlight in the dark of the house our family built on a lie.*

*The covenant is not a treaty. Treaties are made between equals who choose. The covenant is a wound that we taught to close around a knife.*

*Edran did not save the realms. Edran betrayed them. In the year of the Burning Pass, when dragon-fire failed and the humans of the borderlands starved, Edran went to Vessarath as a friend, as the dragons had always counted us friends, and asked to share the fire. And when Vessarath opened his chest to give it — for that is how dragons give, wholly, the heart itself offered — Edran drove the keystone into the offering and bound the giving fast. Made the gift a debt. Made the friend a well.*

*Every renewal since has been a keeper's life poured into the binding, to keep the knife seated. Not sacrifice. Maintenance. We are not martyrs, we are jailers, and the prisoner is the oldest friend our people ever had.*

*I learned this at my own renewal, standing where you stand. I could not do it. I fled into the high snows meaning to die rather than feed the binding, and the binding took my sister instead, and her death bought the gate another generation, and I came down from the mountain and kept the secret and hated myself with every breath until the snow took me at last on the treeline, facing east, toward the friend we chained.*

*Daughter of my line: the book is right. The gate fails on the appointed night. What happens then depends on what you carry in your hands — the knife, or the truth.*

*Pray you are braver than I was.*

*Maren Okafor, who was not brave.*

The torch guttered. Ada realized distantly that her arm had dropped, that the flame was sinking toward the stone, that Ryn was saying her name from somewhere far away, that her knees had found the floor without consulting her.

She should feel horror. She knew she should feel horror. That would come later, probably, the way pain arrives after a bad fall — but what flooded her now, hot and total, drowning everything else, was anger. Anger like she had never once in her disciplined, dutiful, ledger-keeping life permitted herself.

Six generations. Six generations of Okafors had stood at that gate in the cold, had bled on its threshold, had died — her mother, her uncles, the cousins whose names she'd grown up tracing with reverent fingers — and for what? Not duty. Not peace. Not the sacred balance between realms that she'd been raised on like scripture.

Maintenance. Jailers. A knife kept seated in the chest of the oldest friend their people ever had.

And the dragons had known. Had to have known. Vessarath had known for a hundred and sixty years what was done to him, and had waited, and had sent dreams, and had named her *daughter of the forger* with a patience so vast it made her sick — because what else could he do? Rage, and the knife twists. Fight, and the binding tightens. The perfect cage: one built from your own generosity, locked from the outside, tended by the grandchildren of the thief.

"Ada." Ryn's hand on her shoulder. "Ada, breathe. What is it? What does it say?"

She couldn't answer. Her throat wouldn't work around the fury. So she did the only thing her body would allow: she took the torch and traced the letters again, slowly, letting the light rest on each word, and watched Ryn read over her shoulder and go grey.

"That's—" He stopped. Started again. "That's your grandmother's handwriting. Carved. She carved a confession into a wall and hid it behind six generations of names."

"She couldn't say it aloud." Ada heard her own voice come out level and cold and strange, a stranger's voice. "So she put it where only a keeper would ever look. Where only someone of my blood, standing exactly where I'm standing, in exactly this much trouble, would ever find it." She laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, jagged. "Sixty years. She's been waiting sixty years for me to walk back here and read it. *Pray she is braver than her grandmother.* She wrote that in the book too, Ryn. She's been leaving me notes across a century."

"Ada—"

"Don't." She was on her feet now, and the torch was shaking in her grip, throwing her shadow huge and wavering across the columns of names. "Don't tell me to sit down. Don't tell me to rest. My whole life, Ryn. My whole life I guarded that gate like it was holy. I froze for it. I bled for it. I buried my mother for it. And it was never holy. It was a *leash*. We were never keepers. We were never guardians of the peace between realms. We were the lock on a cage, and the cage was made out of a gift, and every Okafor who ever died at that gate died *feeding it*—"

The torch flared — impossibly, wildly, flame leaping a foot high with no wind to feed it — and every carved sigil on every wall lit at once, gate-marks glowing ember-orange in the dark, and somewhere far below them, deep in the bones of Thornfell, something answered. A pulse. A slow, vast heartbeat rolling up through the stone floor, through Ada's boots, through her teeth.

The voice. Counting. Lower now. Closer to the end.

Ryn had his knife out, turning in a circle, finding nothing to fight. "What was that? Ada, what was *that*?"

She knew. God help her, she knew, the way you know a voice through a door.

"It knows I've read it," she said quietly. The fury in her had gone very still and very clear, the way a river goes clear before it freezes or floods. "The binding knows its jailer's daughter just learned what she's heir to."

She looked at the last lines of her grandmother's confession, at the words cut deepest of all, as if Maren had braced herself to carve them:

*The knife, or the truth. Choose before the night chooses for you.*

Outside, faint under the dying storm, from the direction of the lowlands — the direction of Hallowmere, of the garrison, of the Crown — came a sound that did not belong to wind or wolves.

Horns. Many horns, moving up the pass.

Ryn went to the entrance arch and looked out a long moment, and when he turned back his face was lit orange by the glowing walls and very grim.

"Riders," he said. "A lot of them. And they're not heading for the gatehouse, Ada. They're heading for us."
