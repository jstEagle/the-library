## 10. Brann's Bargain

Halvard Brann had been a soldier before he was an inquisitor, and he had learned one lesson in the mud of the Kessarin marches that no seminary ever taught him: never corner what you cannot afford to kill.

He thought about that lesson now as he stood alone in the mouth of the Hollow Shrine, his six soldiers waiting in the treeline behind him with orders to hold, and watched Ada Okafor's hand drift toward the knife at her belt with the unhurried certainty of a woman who had already decided how this would go if it went badly.

"Keeper," he said. "You look tired."

"You look lost, Inquisitor." Her voice was steady. Behind her, half in shadow among the carved pillars of the shrine, the smuggler Ryn Vasq had gone very still in the way of a man calculating exits. There were none. Halvard had made sure of that. He'd had two days to make sure of it, following their trail up through Thornfell while pretending to follow nothing at all.

"I'm not lost," Halvard said. "That's rather the point."

The shrine was older than anything else on the mountain. He'd read the reports — every inquisitor assigned to the Thornfell circuit memorized them — but reading was not standing inside it. The pillars rose around him like frozen smoke, each one carved with names in three scripts: the flowing human hand, the angular Emberish glyphs, and between them a third script that appeared in no Crown archive at all. His lantern threw shadows that moved wrong, or perhaps the light itself was strange here, filtered through some quality of the stone.

And on the far pillar, half-erased by centuries of weather, a confession. He had found it an hour ago, alone, while his soldiers searched the treeline. He had stood before it for a long time without moving.

*We did not sign a treaty. We forged a chain.*

He had copied the words into his field journal in his own careful hand, and then he had sat down on a fallen block of stone and looked at them until the lantern guttered, and he had felt something shift in him that he could not yet name — a hairline crack running through the foundation of everything he believed about his office, his Crown, his God-anointed duty.

Then he had heard Okafor and Vasq coming up the shrine trail, arguing in low voices, and he had put the crack away in a drawer inside himself and become what he was sent here to be.

"Surrender the book," he said now. "Both of you come down the mountain with me. And I give you my word — my written word, sealed — that neither of you sees a tribunal cell."

Ada laughed. It was not a kind sound. "Your word. You burned the Ferrow sisters' farm in March, Brann. Over two pamphlets."

"The Ferrow sisters were printing heresy that told farmers to withhold tithe-grain from the granaries during a famine year." He kept his voice level. "People starved because of ink. I don't expect you to mourn them, Keeper, but don't pretend they were martyrs."

"And the boy who carried their letters? Sixteen years old?"

Halvard did not answer immediately, and the silence cost him something. "He ran," he said finally. "I gave the order to fire warning shots. The sergeant misjudged." It was true, all of it, and none of it helped. That was the thing about the work — every sin had a clean ledger entry, and the ledgers never bled.

"So when you say your word," Ada said, "you mean the word of a man whose sergeants misjudge."

"I mean the word of a man who is offering you the only mercy the Crown has left to offer anyone on this mountain." He took a step forward, slowly, hands visible at his sides. "Listen to me. Both of you. Because I am going to say things tonight that I should not say, and I will only say them once."

Vasq shifted in the shadows. "This should be good."

"Shut up, smuggler." Halvard didn't look at him. He kept his eyes on Ada, because she was the one who mattered, she was the hinge this whole rotten door swung on. "Three weeks ago I was given a warrant for a book. A prophecy text, scorched, provenance unknown, surfacing out of Thornfell. Standard heresy work. I have done forty of these warrants. I know the shape of them." He paused. "Two weeks ago I was given a second set of orders. Not from the Office of Inquiry. From the General Staff. Do you understand what that means? Inquisitors do not take orders from generals. Church and Crown are separate swords. Except now they are not."

Ada's hand had stopped moving toward the knife. "Go on."

"The general's name is Corvane. You may not know him. The mountain folk won't, not yet. But he has been asking questions about the Sable Gate that no general should ask. Not *who guards it*. Not *is it secure*. He asks *what powers it*. He asks *can it be turned*. Three days ago a supply column came up the pass road under his seal — four hundred men, siege engineers, and enough alchemical reagent to crack a city wall." Halvard let that sit in the cold air of the shrine. "They are camped at Thornmouth. They are waiting for me."

"Waiting for you to do what?" Vasq asked, and his voice had lost its irony.

"To find the book. To find the keeper. To establish that the gate is compromised by heretical influence, so that its seizure reads as liberation rather than theft." Halvard spread his hands. "I am the paperwork, Keeper. I am how they make it legal. And I came up this mountain ahead of my own soldiers because I needed to know — before I wrote a single word of my report — whether the gate is what the Crown says it is."

"And is it?" Ada asked quietly.

"No," Halvard said. "It isn't. And neither, I think, am I."

* * *

He had rehearsed none of this. That was the strange part, looking back on it later. Forty years of discipline, of reports filed in perfect script, of doctrine recited like prayer — and when the moment came to betray it, the words simply fell out of him like water from a tipped jug.

They moved deeper into the shrine, away from the treeline, where the pillars would muffle their voices from his own soldiers. Vasq walked with a knife palmed — Halvard noticed and chose not to notice — and Ada walked with the scorched book wrapped in oilcloth against her chest, and Halvard understood suddenly that he was not negotiating with suspects. He was standing in the presence of the only two people on this mountain who knew the truth, and he was begging.

"Show me the page," he said. "The date. I've seen fragments — pages seized over months, scattered across half the smuggling routes in Thornfell. My office pieced together maybe a third of it. Tonight's date is circled. Beside it, a name."

"You know whose name," Ada said.

"I want to hear you say it."

She unwrapped the book. Even by lantern-light it looked wrong — charred along one edge as if it had been pulled from a fire that wanted it dead, the vellum scarred but the ink impossibly fresh, black as wet slate. She turned to the marked leaf and held it out.

Halvard read it. He had imagined this moment for two weeks, and in his imagination he had always felt triumph, or vindication, or at least the cold satisfaction of a case closing. Instead he felt the hairline crack widen.

*On the night of the ash-moon's return,* the old ink said, *the gate fails, and the daughter of the forger stands at the breaking.*

"Daughter of the forger," he repeated. "Not keeper. Forger."

"My family built the covenant," Ada said. "Or built the cage — I don't yet know which word is honest. The gate doesn't keep dragons out, Brann. It keeps dragon-fire *in*, and it runs on blood. Okafor blood. Every generation, a keeper pays, and every generation the Crown calls it duty." Her voice did not shake. That frightened him more than shaking would have. "I found my ancestor's confession carved into that pillar three nights ago. Would you like to hear what your anointed history is founded on?"

"Yes," Halvard said, and meant it, and hated how much he meant it.

She told him. All of it — the betrayal at the founding, the stolen choice, the leash disguised as a treaty, the wound in the last dragon's chest shaped like the gate's keystone. She spoke for a long time, and Vasq added pieces she didn't know, routes and rumors and the starving villages on the border where the failing fire had turned harvests to frost, and Halvard stood in the ancient shrine with his lantern burning low and let the whole architecture of his life come apart quietly, rafter by rafter.

When she finished, he was silent for a long moment.

"In the seminary," he said finally, "they teach that the covenant is the Crown's first miracle. The day humanity and dragonkind made peace. We sing about it at midwinter. I sang about it at midwinter." He looked at the confession-pillar, at the worn letters. "And it was a mugging. Sanctified, institutionalized, inherited down nine generations — a mugging."

"Now you understand why I can't hand you the book," Ada said.

"On the contrary." He turned back to her. "I understand exactly why you can't. Which is why I'm going to tell you what happens next, and then I'm going to offer you the only bargain I have left, and you are going to hate it, and I need you to listen anyway."

* * *

Here is what Halvard Brann offered, in the hollow shrine under the ash-moon, and here is why it was poison wrapped in silk:

"Clemency," he said. "Real clemency, not the tribunal's version. Surrender the book to me — to me, not to Corvane's staff officers. I will file my report. I will write that the heretical text was recovered and destroyed, that the keeper Okafor cooperated fully, that the gate shows no signs of compromise. And then—" he held up a hand before she could speak— "and then the Crown manages the transition. Corvane wants the gate's power harnessed; I cannot stop him, he has four hundred men and I have six. But a managed seizure is not a massacre. Engineers study the keystone instead of shattering it. The Emberlands are brought under administration gradually. Dragons die of old age instead of fire and steel. Villages like Emberfall get grain convoys instead of frost. No war. No apocalypse. No gate failing on schedule and drowning both realms in each other."

"That's not mercy," Ada said. "That's a slower leash."

"It's a leash that holds," Halvard said. "Yes. I'm offering you safety, Keeper. Not truth. Truth died as a governing principle somewhere around the founding of the covenant, if your ancestor's stone is honest, and I no longer have the authority to resurrect it. What I have is the ability to keep four hundred men from doing this the fast way." His voice dropped. "You've seen what the gate does when it flares. You've heard it counting down. If it breaks uncontrolled — if Corvane cracks it open for his engines — the fire goes everywhere, or the frost does, or both. I have read the same prophecies you have. The difference between us is that I believe we survive them only if someone holds the mechanism. Even a corrupt mechanism. Even a cruel one."

"And the book?" Vasq asked. "The truth? The confession on your own pillar? What happens to all of it?"

"Goes into my vault," Halvard said. "Where I can protect it. Where it waits for a day when the Crown has a different face." He almost smiled, and it was a terrible smile, full of everything he wasn't saying. "I am not naive, smuggler. I know what I'm proposing. I'm proposing we bury the truth alive and stand on the grave and call it peace. I've done it before. I'm professionally good at it." He met Ada's eyes. "But buried truths can be dug up. Burned realms cannot be unburned. That is the entire argument. That is all I have."

Ada was quiet for a long time. The lantern hissed. Somewhere above the shrine, wind moved through the pass with a sound almost like speech, almost like counting.

"You believe it," she said at last. "That's the worst part. You genuinely believe you're saving us."

"I know I'm offering you the only exit that doesn't end in fire," Halvard said. "Whether it saves us — that depends on whether you think people can be trusted with freedom, or only with survival. Your ancestor made that choice for everyone, once. Badly. I'm asking you to make it again. Better, I hope. But the same choice."

"And if I refuse?"

He did not flinch. "Then at dawn I report what I've found, Corvane's men come up the mountain, and I will spend the rest of my life knowing I handed the fastest killers the fastest path. I'll live with it. I've lived with worse." He paused. "But I am asking you not to make me live with it. I am asking, Keeper. An inquisitor of the Crown, begging in a heretic shrine. Mark the hour. It won't come again."

* * *

Later — much later, in the years when people would ask him about this night and he would give different answers depending on who asked and how much wine had been poured — Halvard would remember most clearly not Ada's refusal, which he had expected, nor Vasq's hand drifting toward his knife again, which he had also expected.

What he remembered was the moment before her answer, when she looked past him at the confession-pillar, at the worn words of her ancestor, and her face did something he had no doctrine for. Not fear. Not anger. Recognition — the look of a woman meeting, across nine generations, the exact shape of the trap she was standing in, and understanding that the trap had been built by someone who loved the world just as fiercely and just as wrongly as the man offering her silk-wrapped poison in the dark.

"No," she said.

"Keeper—"

"No." She wrapped the book back in its oilcloth, movements precise, unhurried. "You're offering me survival bought with the same lie my family told. I've read how that story ends, Brann. It ends with a dragon bleeding in a cave and a village freezing and a girl standing at a broken gate wondering why nobody told her the truth. I'm not renewing the lie. I'm not letting the Crown manage it. And I'm not surrendering the only true book on this mountain to be buried in your vault until some future coward decides the timing is convenient." She slung her pack. "You want to help? Then stop being paperwork. Corvane is coming regardless — you said so yourself. So pick a side that exists."

For a long moment Halvard Brann stood in the shrine of the first signatories, holding an offer no one had accepted, and felt the last load-bearing wall of his old life come down.

Behind him, faint but unmistakable, carried down the pass on the wind: horns. Not his horns. Deeper, brassier, military — the signal-call of a column on the march. Four hundred men, moving early.

Corvane hadn't waited for dawn after all.

"He's coming up the pass tonight," Halvard said, and heard his own voice go flat and cold, the soldier rising through the ruins of the inquisitor. "There's a fork below the shrine — the old drover's road splits toward Emberfall valley. It's the only route off this mountain his column won't reach before midnight." He snatched up his lantern. "Run. Both of you. And, Keeper—"

She paused at the shrine's edge, silhouetted against the moonless dark.

"When you find whatever third option you're clearly hunting for," Halvard Brann said, "I suggest you find it fast. Because as of tonight, I'm no longer able to protect anyone — including myself — and the man coming up this mountain doesn't bargain at all."

Far below, the horns sounded again, closer now, and the mountain began to burn with torchlight.
