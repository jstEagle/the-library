## 11. Emberfall Village

Ryn Vasq had crossed the Thornfell pass more times than he could count, and he had a smuggler's rule for every mile of it. Don't light fires above the treeline. Don't trust a road that smells of rain. Never, under any circumstances, take the low trail past Emberfall after dark.

He broke that last rule at midday, with Ada Okafor walking beside him and the Crown's warrant burning a hole in his memory like a coal in his pocket.

"We should go around," he said, for the third time.

"The low trail is faster," Ada said, for the third time, "and Brann's men will be watching the high passes. You told me that yourself."

"I told you they'd be watching the high passes. I didn't tell you to walk straight through the one village on this mountain where every soul knows my face."

"Then we'll be quick."

Ryn looked at her. She was still wearing the gatekeeper's grey, though she'd pulled the hood up and rubbed ash into the silver clasp so it wouldn't catch the light. Two days out from the gatehouse, two days since Brann's riders had come over the ridge with their paper and their seals, and she walked like a woman who hadn't decided yet whether she was fleeing or advancing. He recognized it. He'd seen it in deserters, in debtors, in men who'd burned a bridge and kept looking back at the smoke.

"You don't understand what this place is," he said. "Emberfall isn't a village. It's a wound."

"Then show me the wound," she said. "I've spent my whole life guarding a door. I've never once looked at what was on the other side of it. Or below it." She nodded down the slope, where the trail bent toward a scatter of rooftops in a fold of the valley. "You've been carrying prophecy pages across this pass for months, Ryn. Somebody paid you. Somebody fed you. You know these people."

That landed harder than she probably intended. He turned away and started down the switchback, because it was easier than answering.

* * *

The first thing you noticed about Emberfall, if you'd known it before, was what was missing.

Ryn remembered the village as it had been ten years ago, when he was young and greedy and running silk and saltwine over the pass. Emberfall had been rich then — not coin-rich, but rich the way border villages got rich when the covenant ran warm. The terraces had been green to the ridgeline. The forge had run day and night on fire drawn from vents that never cooled, dragon-fire seeping up through the mountain's bones, free for the taking. Travelers had come just to see the glass-smooth stones of the shrine path, fused by old heat into something like jewels. Children had swum in pools that steamed even in winter.

Now the terraces were grey-brown stubble. The forge stood cold, its chimney a dead finger against the sky. The vent-stones along the shrine path were cracked and dull, and frost furred them in patches though it was high summer. A thin crowd worked the fields with the slow, stumbling movements of people who were doing arithmetic with every step — how many hours of labor against how many mouthfuls of food.

A boy of maybe eight sat by the trailside, sorting stones. Not playing — sorting. Ryn knew the game. You sorted the warm stones from the cold ones, and the warm ones went home in a basket to heat the bed at night, and the cold ones went back on the pile. Ten years ago there had been no cold ones. Now the boy's basket held three.

Ada stopped walking.

Ryn watched her look at the boy, at the frost on the shrine stones, at the dead forge. Watched something move behind her face that he couldn't name and didn't like.

"This is the covenant failing," she said quietly. It wasn't a question.

"It's the covenant *dwindling*," Ryn said. "Failing comes later. Failing is when the vents go cold all the way through, and the Emberlands freeze from the inside out, and everything on this side of the mountain that depends on the overflow goes with it. This—" he gestured at the grey fields, the shivering boy "—this is just the interest coming due."

"And nobody does anything?"

"Who would? The Crown says the covenant is eternal and the keepers say the gate is sound and the priests say frost is a test of faith. Meanwhile the village sells its goats and then its roof-beams and then, eventually, its children south to workhouses." He said it flatly, because he'd learned long ago that pity was a luxury smugglers couldn't afford. "Welcome to the ledger nobody audits, Keeper."

She flinched at the word. He noticed, and filed it away, and hated himself a little for using it.

* * *

They found bread and beds at the only inn still standing, a squat stone place called the Vent & Vine that smelled of woodsmoke and boiled barley. The innkeeper was a broad woman named Sela Marrow who took one look at Ryn and said, "Vasq. I thought you were dead or honest."

"One of those is still possible."

"You're paying double. Everything's double. Firewood's worth more than wine now." She set down two bowls of barley soup that was mostly water and memory. "And your friend keeps her hood up or she eats outside. There are Crown men through here twice a week asking after strangers."

"They're not asking after me," Ada said.

"They're asking after *anyone*, girl. That's what a warrant is." But she took their coin, which was the truest language on the border, and left them alone in the corner by the window.

The common room was half full, which meant maybe fifteen people where thirty belonged. Ryn ate and listened, because listening was the trade he was actually good at. A drover complained that his mules wouldn't take the high road anymore — spooked, he said, by a smell of smoke with no fire behind it. An old man by the fire said the vents under his house had gone cold in spring, all four of them, after sixty years of warmth. A woman with chapped hands said her sister's family had gone south to Corvane's works, and hadn't sent word, and everyone at her table pretended not to know what that silence meant.

And near the hearth, a man in a faded red coat — the color of the Emberlands' old trading livery — was telling a story too quietly for most ears. Ryn's ears weren't most ears.

"...swear it on my mother's grave. Up past the shrine, where the ground's still warm. There's a crack in the rock wider than a door, and a light in it. Not firelight. *Moving* light, like something breathing. And a voice. Old words. My grandfather's prayers, the ones they stopped teaching. Counting."

"Counting what?" someone asked.

"That's the thing," the man said, and drained his cup. "It counts *down*."

Ryn felt Ada go very still beside him. He didn't have to look to know her face had gone the color of the frost outside. She'd heard that voice before. Four nights ago, through the Sable Gate itself, counting down in Old Emberish while the keystone flared without cause.

She pushed back her bowl. "We need to see that crack," she murmured.

"No," Ryn said. "No, we need to leave before dawn, take the mill road, and put forty miles between us and that warrant. That's the plan. The plan has been the plan since the gatehouse."

"The plan changed when I heard a dying mountain count."

"It hasn't changed, it's *paused*—"

"Ryn." She turned and looked at him full, hood falling back, and he saw what was behind her face now. It wasn't fear. It was worse. It was recognition. "I have spent eleven years keeping traffic off a road I thought mattered, and balancing a ledger I thought was the whole truth, and it turns out my family built a leash and called it peace. Every cold field down here, every child sold south — I signed off on that. Not with ink. With *silence*. So yes. We're going to look at the crack in the rock."

He wanted to argue. He was good at arguing; it was half his income. But there are arguments you win and arguments you survive, and he could tell which kind this was.

"Fine," he said. "But we do it smart. And if anyone asks, I'm selling you knives and you're a widow with poor taste."

* * *

The shrine above Emberfall had been a place of power once, and you could still see the shape of what it had been, the way you can see the shape of a burned house. Ring of fused-glass stones. Broken pillars. A basin carved with interlocking script — human glyphs woven through Old Emberish, the two languages holding each other up, and Ryn had never noticed until now how much that weaving looked like a cage.

The crack was behind the shrine, up a goat track through frost-killed bracken. Steam rose from it in a thin, steady ribbon, and the air around it smelled of hot iron and, underneath, something older — the way a struck bell smells, if bells had a smell.

Ada knelt at the edge and put her palm flat on the warm stone.

Ryn saw it hit her like a current. Her spine straightened, her breath caught, and her lips began to move — shaping sounds he didn't know, syllables with weight to them, Old Emberish rolling out of a gatekeeper's daughter as if she'd been born to it. Which, he was beginning to suspect, she had.

"What's it saying?" he asked, when she came back to herself, pale and shaking.

Her eyes were wet. That frightened him more than the voice had.

"It's the same voice from the gate. It's Vessarath." She said the name carefully, like setting down something fragile. "The last dragon. He's dying, Ryn. He's been dying for a hundred years, slowly, and the covenant is drinking what's left of him, and the countdown — the countdown is him telling us how long he has." She looked at her own hand as if it belonged to someone else. "And he knows my name. He called me *daughter of the forger*. He said—" her voice cracked and steadied "—he said my blood is the lock."

The wind moved through the dead bracken. Down in the village, someone was calling a child in to supper, and the sound was so ordinary it hurt.

"So that's the mission now?" Ryn said, and heard his own bitterness. "Walk into the Emberlands and give yourself to a dragon? Because I've read those pages you made me carry, Ada. Every ending in that book costs somebody everything. And I've watched you since the gatehouse. You're already pricing yourself."

"I'm pricing *truth*."

"Truth is what you tell yourself so the price feels fair." He crouched, picked up one of the warm stones, held it in his fist. It was faintly, stubbornly alive with heat — the last ember of a fire that had warmed this valley for a thousand years. "Look down there. Those people didn't forge anything. They didn't leash anybody. They just lived downstream of your family's great work, and now the river's drying up and they'll starve politely while scholars argue about prophecy. If you go charging into the Emberlands to fix it your way, you're doing exactly what your ancestor did. Deciding for everyone. Alone. From above."

She stared at him. For a long moment he thought she'd cut him open with whatever she'd learned to say in Old Emberish.

Then she laughed — short, broken, real. "Eleven years auditing smugglers," she said, "and the first honest audit I get is from one."

"I'm not honest. I'm expensive." He tossed her the warm stone. She caught it, cupped it in both hands like a bird with a heartbeat. "Keep it. Proof the fire's not dead yet. Proof there's something left to save besides your guilt."

They started back down toward the village in the blue hour, and that was when Ryn saw the horses.

Three of them, tied outside the Vent & Vine, mud to the hocks, big-boned — Crown remounts. And in the inn's doorway, talking to Sela Marrow with a paper in his hand, a man in a grey travel coat with the bearing of someone used to being obeyed. Even at this distance, even in failing light, Ryn knew the set of those shoulders.

Halvard Brann had reached Emberfall first.

Sela looked up, across the darkening fields, directly at the two figures on the shrine path — and did not point, did not shout, did not so much as nod. She simply turned back inside and closed the door.

Ryn exhaled. "Your ledger," he said quietly, "owes that woman."

Ada's hand closed around the warm stone until the light showed between her fingers.

"She gave us until morning," she said. "Maybe less. We need somewhere to go, Ryn. Somewhere the book can't be burned and the truth can't be arrested."

He looked north, toward the black line of the pass and the frozen shimmer of the Emberlands beyond, where a dying dragon counted down the dark.

"I know a road," he said. "Nobody's used it in a hundred years. For good reason."

"Tell me."

"Not till we're on it." He took her arm and drew her back into the shadows of the bracken, away from the inn, away from the warrant, down toward the last warm stones of a cooling world. "Some doors, Keeper, you only open once."

Behind them, faint as a heartbeat under the mountain, the voice in the crack went on counting.
