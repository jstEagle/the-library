## 12. The Second Prophecy

The page was not where the book said it should be.

Ada had read *The Ember Codex* four times since fleeing Thornfell — twice by candlelight in Ryn's safehouse above Emberfall, once by the grey light of dawn when sleep refused her, and once aloud, slowly, while Ryn watched her face for reactions he could catalogue and sell later if it came to that. She knew its rhythm now. The prophecies moved in threes: three signs, three keepers, three nights of counting. The binding cracked in two places. The ink on the final leaf had been smeared by water or tears or blood, a brown bloom like a pressed flower.

But tonight, running her thumb along the spine's broken gutter, she felt something the other readings had missed. A thickness. A seam.

"Ryn," she said. "Your knife."

He looked up from the crossbow bolt he'd been fletching by the fire — his own bolt, pulled from his own shoulder two days ago, the head bent like a question mark. He'd kept it. She hadn't asked why. "Which knife?"

"The thin one."

He passed it over without argument, which was itself remarkable. Two weeks ago he would have asked what she intended to cut open and whether there was profit in it. Now he just watched her slide the blade into the seam between the endpaper and the board, and held his breath with her when the paper gave.

A page slid out. Thin as onion skin, folded twice, hidden so long against the binding glue that it had taken on the board's shape, a ghost-leaf pressed flat as a moth in a collector's case.

Neither of them spoke. The fire popped. Somewhere below, Emberfall's last dozen dogs were barking at nothing, the way they had been barking at nothing all week.

Ada unfolded the page on her knee.

It was written in the same hand as the rest of the codex — the tight, slanted script of someone writing fast and believing every word — but the ink was different. Darker. Fresher, somehow, though it had to be a century old at least. And unlike the rest of the book, this page did not prophecy. It offered terms.

*Two roads from the failing gate,* it began. *I have seen both. I write them down so that whoever comes after will know the choice was never hidden — only never spoken.*

Ada's mouth went dry. She read on.

*The first road: the covenant reforged. A keeper of the forging blood gives herself willingly at the keystone, and the leash is remade — new links, same chain. Dragon-fire flows again to the valleys. The Emberlands warm. The villages eat. The dragons live out their long decline in comfort and captivity, and the humans of Thornfell keep their stewardship, their titles, their gate. This road costs one life and buys a hundred years of the lie.*

Her eyes moved to the second passage, and stopped.

*The second road: the covenant broken. The keystone shatters unbound, the blood-price ends, and no Okafor dies at the stone. But the fire does not flow free — it simply stops. The Emberlands freeze from the peaks down. The dragons gutter like candles in wind. The border villages starve first, then the lowlands, then everyone who ever ate bread grown under dragon-warmed skies. This road costs no single life. It costs, in time, nearly all of them.*

"There's more," Ryn said quietly. He had come around behind her without her noticing; she felt his breath stir her hair as he read over her shoulder. "Keep going."

*Between these roads,* the page ended, *there is no third. I have looked. I have prayed. I have begged the fire itself for another way, and the fire is honest: it offers only what it is. One realm lives warm and enslaved. Both realms die cold and free. Choose, daughter of the forger. The choice is the only inheritance your line ever left you worth having.*

No signature. No date beyond the century the ink implied. Just those words, in that hand, and beneath them — Ada turned the page toward the light — a small sketch in charcoal, smudged but unmistakable.

The Sable Gate. Drawn from the inside. And standing before it, small and alone, a figure with dark skin and braided hair bound up in a keeper's knot.

Herself. A hundred years before she was born.

* * *

She didn't remember setting the page down. She found it later, smoothed flat on the table where she'd left it, and herself standing at the window with the frost climbing the glass, watching the peaks north of Emberfall.

They were wrong. That was the thing she kept returning to. The mountains were wrong.

Thornfell's southern faces had always glowed faintly after dark — dragon-fire, banked deep in the high holds, bleeding warmth through stone the way a hearth bleeds through a wall. Every child of the pass grew up reading that glow the way lowland children read clouds. *Bright fire, good roads. Dim fire, hard winter.* It was the first lesson of her office and the last thing she checked each night.

Tonight the southern faces were dark. Not dim. Dark. And along the ridgelines, where the snow should have lain soft and blue in the starlight, she could see the frost had gone grey — hoarfrost thick as fur, creeping down the rock like something alive and hungry.

"The green valleys go first," she said. She hadn't meant to say it aloud. "Vessarath told me. Fire dwindles, frost takes the low meadows, and everything that grows there dies inside a season. Then the herds. Then—"

"Then us." Ryn set the bolt down. "Emberfall's already halfway there. You saw the granaries."

She had. She'd stood in one yesterday while a woman with a starving baby's cry memorized into her chest had shown Ada the ration tallies — autumn stores meant to last until thaw, already half-gone, and thaw months away even in a good year. In a good year, the dragon-warmed winds off the peaks pushed the growing season three weeks longer and the frost three weeks later. Nobody in Emberfall had known a good year in five.

"And if the covenant breaks," Ada said, "that's the whole world. Every valley. Every—" She stopped. Pressed her palm flat against the cold glass. "He wrote it down. Whoever he was, this forger's ghost, this scribe — he looked at both endings and wrote them down like a merchant listing prices."

"Maybe that's mercy." Ryn's voice was careful. He'd been careful with her since the shrine, since the confession carved in stone, since she'd come out of that hollow place with grief still wet on her face and anger underneath it like a blade. "Maybe he thought whoever found it deserved the truth instead of a comfortable lie."

"The truth?" She turned on him, and heard her own voice crack sharp as ice splitting. "The truth is there is no road through this. Reforge it, I die at the keystone and my family's leash gets another century. Break it, and Vessarath dies, and the Emberfolk die, and Emberfall dies, and probably Thornfell after — all so no one has to be complicit anymore. That's not a choice, Ryn. That's a trap with two doors, and both doors are painted on."

Ryn was quiet for a long moment. When he answered, he didn't flinch and he didn't soften, which was why, despite everything, she trusted him.

"You're assuming the page is honest."

"It's in the book."

"So was the covenant." He nodded at the codex. "Written by people who wanted something. Ask yourself what the man who hid that page wanted. Because a hidden page in a hidden book, telling the finder there's no third option — that's not just information, Ada. That's steering. Somebody a hundred years dead is trying to tell you which door to walk through, and he's doing it by convincing you there's only two."

Ada opened her mouth to argue. Closed it. Looked back at the page.

*I have begged the fire itself for another way, and the fire is honest: it offers only what it is.*

But Vessarath wasn't fire. Vessarath was a dying dragon with a wound shaped like a keystone and a century of grief, and he had called her *daughter of the forger* not as an accusation — she understood this now, finally — but as an introduction. Here is what your family took. Here is what remains. Here is you.

And the council of ash had demanded the gate destroyed outright, and Brann wanted it managed, and the Crown wanted it harnessed, and every hand reaching for the Sable Gate wanted it to be one thing or the other, one door or the other, because doors that simple were doors that could be controlled.

"I need to see it again," she said.

"See what?"

"The marginalia. All of it. Every note, every correction, every smear in that book." She was already moving, gathering the codex off the table, cradling it like something wounded. "If our ghost scribe spent a century begging the fire for a third way, he didn't stop at one page. People like him never stop. They write in the margins. They scratch things out. They leave threads."

Ryn rose too, wincing as his shoulder took the weight. "And if there's nothing? If the two roads are all there is?"

"Then I'll choose," Ada said. "With my eyes open, knowing the price, choosing it myself instead of inheriting it. That's the one thing my family never did." She met his eyes across the small firelit room, and outside, far up the black peaks, something flickered — dragon-fire, guttering, or maybe just starlight on frost, impossible to tell anymore. "But not tonight. Tonight I read."

She sat down with the book and the hidden page side by side, and began at the beginning, slower than she had ever read anything in her life.

By the third hour, Ryn was asleep in his chair. By the fifth, the dogs had gone quiet. And by the sixth hour past midnight, in the margin of a page she had read four times and thought she knew — squeezed sideways in faded ink, so small she'd mistaken it for a stain —

Ada found a single line she had never seen before:

*The fire is honest about what it is. It is not honest about what it could become.*

Below it, in the same cramped hand, a direction. Three words, underlined twice:

*Ask the wound.*
