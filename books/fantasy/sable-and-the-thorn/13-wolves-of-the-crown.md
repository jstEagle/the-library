## 13. Wolves of the Crown

Ryn smelled the smoke before he saw it.

That was the first wrong thing. The Hollow Shrine sat in a fold of mountain where no one cooked, no one camped, and no one burned anything except candle stubs and old prayers. Woodsmoke drifting down the switchbacks at midmorning meant only one thing: someone had decided the trail behind them should stop existing.

He caught Ada's sleeve before she took the next bend.

"Wait."

She froze the way she always did — completely, instantly, like a woman who had spent thirty years listening to a gate and trusted her ears more than her eyes. Her head tilted. Her nostrils flared.

"Smoke," she said.

"From below us. Behind." He crouched at the edge of the trail and looked back the way they'd come. The pass dropped away in a series of stone shelves stitched with pine, and threading through all of it was the thin gray ribbon of smoke rising from somewhere near the shrine. "They're burning the forest road."

Ada came down beside him on her heels, careful as a cat on a ledge. "The shrine?"

"Maybe just the trail. Maybe everything." Ryn chewed the inside of his cheek. He'd run this route eleven times in his life — twelve if you counted the night he'd carried his father's ledger out of Thornfell with two broken ribs and a price on his head. He knew every culvert, every scree chute, every hollow where a man could wait with a crossbow and patience. And he knew what burning a trail meant. It wasn't pursuit. Pursuit was fast and loud. This was slow and thorough. This was a man saying: *you have nowhere to go but where I want you.*

"Brann's soldiers?" Ada asked, though she already knew.

"Who else burns things for the Crown? Smugglers burn cargo we can't move. Inquisitors burn anything that might be true."

She didn't laugh. She hadn't laughed since the shrine — since she'd stood in front of that carved confession with her ancestor's name cut into the stone like a wound, reading words that had been waiting a hundred years for an Okafor to find them. Since then she'd been walking like a woman carrying something breakable inside her chest, and Ryn had the uncomfortable suspicion it wasn't the prophecy book in her satchel.

"We go up," he said. "Not down. Down is where they want us."

"The pass down is the only way to Emberfall village."

"And Emberfall is exactly where Brann will look, because it's the only place you'd go, because you're the kind of person who walks toward starving villages instead of away from them." He said it flatly, not as a compliment. "We go up, over the shoulder of Cindermark, and drop into the valley from the north. Adds a day. Keeps us alive."

Ada looked at him for a long moment. He'd learned to read her silences the way other men read weather, and this one said: *you're right, and I hate it, because a day is a day and the fire in the peaks is dying.*

"All right," she said. "Up."

* * *

They climbed for three hours, and the smoke followed them.

Not close — never close enough to see flames — but always there at the edge of vision, a gray smudge crawling along the lower slopes like something living. By noon Ryn could pick out individual columns. Four. Five. They weren't burning one trail. They were burning the whole web of them, every smuggler path he'd ever walked, every route he'd ever sold or guarded or kept secret, going up in orange and black.

"There goes my livelihood," he muttered.

"There goes your map," Ada corrected. "Livelihoods are harder to burn."

He almost smiled at that. Almost. But there was something sour sitting under his ribs that had nothing to do with smoke, and it had been there since the shrine, since the confession, since the moment he'd understood that the woman beside him had inherited a hundred years of theft dressed up as treaty. He'd known about the covenant's ugliness for months — he'd been carrying prophecy pages across the pass for months, pages paid for by people in the Emberlands who wanted the whole rotten arrangement torn open. He'd told himself he was doing it for coin. Then he'd told himself he was doing it because the pages were true. Now, watching Crown soldiers torch the mountains he'd grown up in, he admitted the truth he'd been dodging:

He was doing it for her.

And that was a problem, because Ryn Vasq had survived twenty-nine years in the trade by never making anyone indispensable. People who mattered got you killed. People you'd die for got you dead faster.

"You're quiet," Ada said.

"I'm thinking."

"That's new."

"It happens when I'm not counting exits." He scrambled up a shelf of granite and reached back for her hand without deciding to. She took it. Her palm was rough from the gatehouse ropes, from thirty years of hauling chains and checking seals, and he held on maybe half a second longer than the climb required. Neither of them mentioned it. That was their whole arrangement, really — a hundred small things neither of them mentioned.

From the top of the shelf they could see south across the valley, and Ryn stopped breathing for a moment.

Soldiers. Not a patrol — a line of them, moving along the ridgeline below like ants following a scent, maybe forty strong, with pack mules and a supply cart and banners he couldn't read at this distance but didn't need to. The Crown's wolf sigil, black on white. Brann's company, or part of it, sweeping the slopes the way you sweep a house when you know the rat is still inside.

"They're not chasing us," Ryn said quietly. "They're herding us."

"Herding us where?"

"Wherever's worst." He studied the terrain, mapping it against the map in his head. The soldiers held the southern ridges. Smoke held the low trails. That left north — high, cold, exposed country above the treeline where two travelers would stand out against the sky like ink on paper. Or east, into the Cindermark scree fields, which was faster but funnel-shaped, which meant—

Which meant a kill zone, if whoever commanded that column knew these mountains even half as well as Ryn did.

But they wouldn't. Nobody knew these mountains like him. That was the one card he'd always held, the thing that made him worth tolerating to keepers and worth paying to smugglers. His knowledge was the last unburned thing in Thornfell.

Except.

A thought slid into his head, cold and unwelcome: *someone sold the shrine.* The Hollow Shrine wasn't on any Crown map. It wasn't on any map at all. Brann's men had found it, and found the trail to it, and started burning — which meant someone had talked. A guide. A local. One of Ryn's own people, probably, because who else knew?

His crew. His lovely, greedy, frightened crew, who'd watched dragon-fire gutter on the peaks and decided the smart money was on the Crown.

"Ryn?" Ada's hand was on his arm. "You've gone pale, and not in the altitude way."

"My people sold us," he said. It came out steadier than he felt. "Or some of them did. Doesn't matter which. What matters is Brann knows our routes now, and he knows them because someone drew them for pay." He laughed, short and ugly. "I taught half those guides myself. You spend your life building a road and then somebody sells the road out from under you."

Ada said nothing for a while. When she spoke, her voice was gentle in a way that made it worse. "You could have sold me. Weeks ago. At the inn, with the book in your pack. You'd have been rich."

"Yeah, well." He shouldered his pack. "I'm a terrible businessman."

* * *

They crossed the Cindermark scree in late afternoon, moving fast and low through fields of broken slate that clinked underfoot like crockery. The sun was dropping behind the western peaks and dragging the temperature down with it, and Ryn's lungs burned with the altitude, and none of it worried him as much as the silence.

There should have been birds. There should have been marmots whistling from the rocks. There was nothing. The mountain had gone quiet the way a room goes quiet when someone's standing behind the door.

He raised a fist. Ada stopped.

"What?"

"Listen."

Wind. Slate ticking as it settled. And underneath both, faint but unmistakable, the creak of leather and the jingle of mail coming from the ridge above them.

Ryn closed his eyes and cursed every god he'd ever cheated.

"Ahead of us too," Ada breathed.

"Flankers. Brann split his force — burners below, blockers above. Classic pincer." He pulled her down behind a slab of granite the size of a hay wagon. Through the gap beneath it he could see the ridgeline fifty yards up, and figures moving along it — six, seven, eight, spread out in a skirmish line, crossbows unslung. "They're not hunting us specifically. They're sweeping everything between here and the valley. We're just vermin in the walls."

"Then we go around."

"With what daylight? Look at the sky. We've got an hour, maybe less, and that ridge runs clean to the cliffs. There's no around. There's through, or there's—" He stopped. Because there was another option, and he hated it, and it was the only one that worked.

Through the gap in the rocks he looked again at the skirmish line. Eight men. Spread wide. Crossbows loaded but lowered — they were searching, not expecting contact. Expecting their quarry to be huddled in some hole waiting for dark.

Behind him, Ada was already pulling the prophecy book from her satchel, wrapping it in oilcloth with hands that didn't shake. Of course she wasn't shaking. She'd kept a gate against dragons and smugglers and the slow rot of a century. A dozen soldiers with crossbows was a Tuesday.

"If they take me," she said, "you take the book north to Vessarath—"

"Stop."

"—and you tell him what the shrine said, word for word, and you don't let Corvane's people anywhere near the keystone—"

"*Stop.*" He grabbed her wrist. Harder than he meant to. He didn't let go. "You don't get to write your own death into the plan. Everyone keeps doing that — you, the dragon, the whole cursed prophecy. Everybody assumes the Okafor ends badly and plans around it. Well, I've planned around worse odds than this, and here's how it actually goes: nobody gets taken today. We go through that line together, or we don't go through at all."

"How?" she asked. Simply. As if he were a craftsman and she genuinely wanted to see the work.

Ryn looked at the ridge. Looked at the light. Looked at the slope below the skirmish line — steep, loose, a long fan of scree running down three hundred feet into shadow.

"Scree slide," he said. "Right through the middle of them. They'll hear us coming and by the time they do, we'll be past. It's fast, it's stupid, and it works about half the time."

"And the other half?"

"The other half we find out what the bottom of a rockslide looks like from the inside." He grinned at her, the grin that had talked toll-keepers out of their tolls and magistrates out of their suspicions. "Odds better than most things in my life. Coming?"

* * *

It went wrong in the first ten seconds.

Later, Ryn would reconstruct it a hundred times: the push off the granite slab, the first bounding strides down the slope, the world turning to thunder as thousands of slates broke loose and ran ahead of them like water. It had worked in his head. It had even worked for the first stretch — the soldiers above shouting, crossbow strings snapping uselessly at noise and dust, the whole ridgeline dissolving into chaos.

Then the slope steepened where it shouldn't have. A seam of ice under the scree, left over from a winter the valleys had already forgotten. His leading foot hit it and the mountain simply removed itself from under him, and Ryn Vasq went from running to falling in the space of one heartbeat.

He heard Ada scream his name. He saw her above him, sliding, reaching, her fingers grazing his — and he saw, beyond her shoulder, a soldier on the ridge who hadn't panicked. One man, kneeling, bracing his crossbow against the chaos, taking the time to aim properly while his comrades fired wild. Taking aim at the easier target. At her.

Everything after that happened very quickly and very slowly at once.

Ryn twisted. It was the only move available — throw his body sideways into her line, put himself between the bolt and the woman, and trust to speed and luck and whatever gods owed him favors. He'd spent his whole life calculating angles; this was just one more trade. His weight for hers. His blood for her road.

The bolt took him high in the chest, left of center, punching through the shoulder muscle with a crack he felt in his teeth rather than heard.

Then the scree finished its argument and deposited them both behind a spur of rock in a ringing, choking cloud of dust, and the world went quiet except for his own heartbeat, which sounded wrong. Wet. Labored.

"Ryn." Ada's hands were on him, tearing open his coat, and her face above him was the color of the slate. "Ryn, stay with me, look at me—"

"'M looking," he managed. "You should see your face. Like I died already."

"Don't." Her voice cracked straight down the middle. "Don't you dare joke."

He tried to laugh and it turned into a cough, and the cough brought copper into his mouth. Below his collarbone, the bolt shaft jutted out at an angle, snapped off in the fall, leaving four inches of ash-wood fletched in goose feather. Blood pulsed around it, slower than he'd expected. He'd seen enough wounds to know slow was good, mostly, until it wasn't.

"Head wound?" he rasped. "Check me for a head wound. I feel clever. That's not normal."

"Shut up." She was pressing cloth around the shaft, hands quick and sure — keeper's hands, gatehouse hands, hands that had bound smuggler cuts for years without asking names. "The barbs haven't set. If it hasn't hit the lung—"

"It kissed the lung. We're acquainted." He grabbed her forearm. The dust was settling. Above them, faint through the ringing in his ears, voices — soldiers regrouping on the ridge, arguing about whether to come down after them. "Listen. They'll sweep the slide by dusk. You can't carry me and outrun them."

"I'm not leaving you."

"You're not *leaving* me. There's a difference, and it's the kind of difference smugglers live on." He fumbled at his belt with his good arm and pressed his knife into her hand — the good one, Thornfell steel, the one he'd never once let anyone touch. "Half a mile north there's a chimney — a crack in the cliff face, hidden by a fallen pine. Climbers' cache inside. Rope, lamp, dried meat, and a route down into the valley that isn't on any map Brann bought. My map. The real one. The one I never sold."

"Ryn—"

"Take the book. Take the route. Get to Vessarath before Corvane gets to the gate." Each sentence cost him breath, and he could hear himself getting quieter, and he hated it, hated the weakness leaking out of him like the blood leaking onto the stones. "I'll be here. Bleeding convincingly. They'll find me, they'll patch me up because Brann wants me talking, and I'll talk — I'll talk all night, stories, lies, my entire childhood, anything to buy you time. I'm very good at talking. Ask anyone I've cheated."

Ada's eyes were bright and furious. "You absolute fool. You jumped in front of a crossbow for me."

"Yeah." He smiled, and it hurt, and he did it anyway. "Turns out I'm an even worse businessman than I thought."

For a moment she bowed her head over him, and he felt — absurdly, terrifyingly — like a gate himself, something old and load-bearing, holding while the weight passed over. Then she tore a strip from her cloak, knotted it tight above the wound, and did the thing he should have predicted and somehow hadn't: she refused.

"No," she said.

"Ada—"

"I'm not carrying you. You're right, I can't." She looked up, and the fury in her face had cooled into something much more dangerous: the flat calm of a woman who has audited a thousand ledgers and knows exactly what she owns. "So we walk. Slowly. Together. You lean on me, and we make that chimney before dark, and if the soldiers catch us, they catch two of us and get half the story from each. That's the plan. Amend it and I'll leave you here to argue with the rocks."

Above them, boots crunched on slate. Close. Two men, maybe three, descending carefully through the settling dust.

Ryn opened his mouth to argue, and the argument died, because there was no version of it he could win — not against her, not with a bolt in his chest and maybe ninety seconds of lead time. So instead he did the hardest thing he'd done in twenty-nine years of hard things.

He put his good arm over her shoulders and let her lift him.

"One condition," he whispered as she hauled him upright and the world went white at the edges. "When this is over — when the gate's whatever it's going to be — you tell me why. Why you came back for me. Properly. No jokes."

Ada shifted his weight, set her feet on the slope, and began to climb north through the dying light, toward the hidden chimney and the dragon and the war gathering behind them like weather.

"Because," she said, "somebody has to keep you alive long enough to finish being a terrible businessman."

Behind them, on the ridge, a soldier shouted — and then another, and the shout became a horn, one long note rolling down the valley, calling the wolves home.

Ryn gripped her shoulder, put his head down, and ran on legs that barely answered, while the horn sounded again, closer now, and the first snow of the season began to fall on Thornfell.
