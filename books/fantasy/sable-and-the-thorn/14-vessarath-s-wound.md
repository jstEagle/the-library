## 14. Vessarath's Wound

The last mile of the climb, Ada stopped counting her steps.

She had counted them for years — a keeper's habit, the gatehouse stairs were one hundred and forty, the pass road from the shrine to the high col was three thousand and change — but here the mountain refused arithmetic. The smuggler maps Ryn had pressed into her hands before he collapsed showed the way in fragments: *the cairn that isn't a cairn, the shelf where the snow never lies, follow the smell of hot stone.* She had climbed through the night with Ryn's blood drying stiff on her sleeve because she hadn't been able to bring herself to wash it off. It felt like a debt she was carrying to him, proof she owed him more than gratitude.

He was alive. That was the only prayer she had left, and she wasn't sure anyone was listening. Brann's field surgeon — a defector now, like his inquisitor — had dug the crossbow bolt out of Ryn's shoulder at dawn and pronounced him *too stubborn to die*, which Ada chose to take as a medical prognosis.

So she climbed alone, up past the tree line, up past where even the hardy Thornfell pines gave up and became wind-bent skeletons, up into air so thin it tasted of iron and old fire.

And then she smelled it. Hot stone. Sulfur. Something underneath both, sweeter — resin, almost, like sap from a tree that had never grown on any mountain she knew.

The lair opened out of the mountainside without warning. One moment she was picking her way across a scree field the color of old bone; the next, the slope simply fell away into a cavern mouth tall as a cathedral door, its edges melted smooth, glassed by heat from some age before memory. Warmth rolled out of it like breath.

Ada stood at the threshold with her hand on the knife at her belt — not to draw it, she realized. Just to feel its weight. To feel like she was still armed against anything.

"You may come in, daughter of the forger."

The voice did not echo. It arrived whole, the way thunder arrives, already everywhere inside the stone. It was not loud. That was the worst part. A voice that size should have shattered her; instead it settled over her like snowfall, patient and vast and tired beyond anything she had words for.

She went in.

* * *

The cavern opened into a hollowed mountain, and the mountain was lit from within.

Veins of ember-light ran through the walls like the glowing roots of an inverted tree, pulsing slow, dimming, pulsing again — and Ada's keeper's eye, the eye that had audited every cart and candle on the Sable road for fifteen years, read the rhythm before she understood it. It was a heartbeat. The mountain itself was beating, and each pulse was weaker than the one before.

Vessarath lay coiled in the center of the hollow, and she understood in one vertiginous instant why the Emberlands called him *the last*. He was enormous — his body filled the chamber the way a river fills a canyon — but he was wrong-sized for it, folded tight, wings drawn close like a mourner's cloak. His scales, which the old ledgers described as black shot through with living gold, had gone the gray of banked ash. Where the light touched him, faint gold answered, guttering.

His head rose when she entered. Eyes like deep furnaces opening found her across forty yards of stone.

"Keeper," he said. "You came without soldiers. Without your book, even." A sound moved through him that might have been a laugh, if laughter could be made of grief. "Brann carries the book now, I am told. How strange. The Crown guards my story better than I can."

"I came to see you," Ada said. Her voice sounded very small. "I came to understand."

"Understanding is a wound," Vessarath said. "But come. You have walked far enough on other people's lies. Walk the last few steps on the truth."

She crossed the cavern floor. Up close, the scale of him stopped being awe and became something closer to terror — not of him, but of her own smallness, of how easily a creature like this could have ended her line a hundred years ago and instead had chosen — or been forced into — patience. Heat came off him in waves. His breath stirred her hair.

That was when she saw the wound.

It sat high on his chest, above the great plates of his sternum, where a heart would beat if dragons kept their hearts where humans kept theirs. It was old — centuries old, scarred over, the scales around it fused and warped like metal around a forge accident. But it had never healed. And it was not ragged, not torn, not the mark of claw or bolt or lance.

It was shaped.

Shaped exactly, precisely, unmistakably, like the keystone of the Sable Gate.

Ada knew that shape better than she knew her own face. She had dusted it every morning for fifteen years. The wedge-cut shoulders, the tapering foot, the small notch cut into the upper-left shoulder where a mason's chisel had slipped in the year the gate was raised — she had run her thumb over that flaw ten thousand times without ever wondering why a flawless covenant-stone bore a craftsman's mistake.

The wound bore the same mistake. The same notch. The same slipped chisel, written in dragon-flesh.

The cavern tilted. Ada caught herself against a fallen scale the size of a barn door, and the scale was warm as a sleeping animal, and none of it mattered because her mind was doing what it always did when the world broke open — assembling, auditing, refusing to look away.

The keystone wasn't carved *in the image* of anything. The keystone was a cast. A mold taken from a living wound. Someone had pressed stone into the flesh of a dragon and built a gate around the shape of his hurt, and everything — the covenant, the border, the quiet roads, her family's honor, her own life's work — everything had been mortared into the geometry of that injury.

"The gate is your wound," she said. Her voice cracked on the second word.

"The gate is *made* of my wound," Vessarath said. "There is a difference, and the difference is the whole of your inheritance."

* * *

He told it plainly, which somehow made it worse. Dragons, she was learning, did not soften things.

"A hundred and twelve years ago, your ancestor Maera Okafor came to me under a flag of parley. There was war then — real war, fire on the lowlands, famine in the passes. Both realms were dying politely, which is how humans die, and loudly, which is how dragons die. She offered peace. I wanted peace. I was young, and young dragons believe treaties."

His furnace-eyes half-closed. The ember-veins in the walls pulsed, dimmer.

"She brought masons with her. Under the parley banner. While we spoke terms, they took the mold — wax and clay pressed to my chest while I slept after the feast she hosted, drugged with poppy and something older I still cannot name. They took the shape of my hurt — for I was already wounded, Keeper. Your realm's ballistae put that hurt in me in the third year of the war. And from the shape of my wound they cut a keystone, and into the keystone they bound the terms: that dragon-fire would feed the human lands, warm their villages, ripen their fields, hold back their frosts — and that in exchange, the Emberlands would be *contained*. Not protected. Contained. My fire would flow only along channels they approved. My people would pass only where they permitted. And the binding would be renewed—"

He stopped. The pause went on so long Ada thought he would not finish.

"—renewed by a keeper's hand, once each generation. Blood of the signer's line, freely given onto the stone. That is the price your ledger never recorded, though your grandmothers all paid it. Did you never wonder, daughter of Okafor, why every keeper in your family died young? Why the office passed down so fast? Why your mother went to 'tend the gate' on a winter night and did not come back?"

Ada's knees went. She sat down on the warm stone without deciding to, the way a felled tree sits down.

Her mother. Who had kissed her forehead at dusk and said *watch the ledger until morning, little auditor.* Who had gone up the gatehouse stairs and never come down, and whose death the Crown had recorded as *a fall on the stair*, and whose funeral Ada, aged nine, had attended holding her father's hand while everyone said *the gate demands much of its keepers* and she had thought it was a figure of speech.

It was never a figure of speech. It was a schedule.

"They didn't tell us," she whispered. "They didn't tell *us*. We thought we were guarding a border. We thought—"

"You thought you were keepers." Vessarath's voice gentled, which was somehow more terrible than his anger could have been. "You were. The best of them. That is the cruelest design of it. Your family built the leash, and then your family was made to hold it, generation after generation, believing it a sacred duty — so that the hands on the collar would always be sincere. No soldier guards a lie forever. But a believer will guard it unto death. Unto *young* death, arranged and scheduled and written in a ledger your ancestors kept hidden even from themselves."

Ada thought of the prophecy book, scorched and traded to her by a dying peddler. Tonight's date circled in century-old ink beside her name. She thought of the gate flaring, the voice counting down in Old Emberish. She thought of Halvard Brann's earnest, terrible sincerity, and of Ryn bleeding in a surgeon's tent because he'd stepped in front of a bolt meant for her.

Everything fit now. That was the sickness of it. Every strange thing she'd stumbled over in the past weeks slotted into place with a click she could feel in her teeth, like tumblers in a lock turning home.

"The date," she said. "The book says tonight the gate fails. Is that true? Is that you?"

"No." The word came heavy as a landslide. "I have held my wound closed for a hundred and twelve years, Keeper. Holding it closed *is* the covenant — my pain, shaped into your order. But I am dying now regardless. The fire dims because the fire's vessel dims. When my heart stops, the keystone loses its mold, and the gate comes apart on its own — no army required, no prophecy needed. The page your peddler carried was not a threat. It was a diagnosis. Tonight — or soon, for the counting is imprecise at the end — the Sable Gate fails because I fail."

"And the Emberlands freeze," Ada said. "And the lowlands burn, because the fire runs wild with no channel."

"Yes. Unless—" He paused again, and this time the pause had a different texture. Careful. Almost afraid. "Unless the covenant is renewed. A willing Okafor, blood given freely onto the keystone, binds the wound anew for another century. Another leash. Another generation of keepers dying on schedule. Another hundred years of my people penned behind your mountains."

Ada looked at the wound. At the perfect, horrible architecture of it. At the notch where a mason's chisel had slipped while carving a treaty out of a dragon's chest.

"And if it breaks?"

"Then both realms bleed together, and we find out what we are to each other with no wall between us. Perhaps we slaughter each other. Perhaps—" the furnace-eyes opened fully, and for a moment she saw something in them she had never expected to see in a dragon: hope, worn thin as last winter's coat. "Perhaps we do what Maera Okafor should have done at the parley feast. Speak. Freely. With no mold pressed to anyone's sleeping chest."

Ada sat on the warm stone of a dying mountain and understood, finally, completely, the mechanism of her whole life. The gate was a wound. The covenant was a scar dressed up as a treaty. Her family had been the sutures — sincere, dutiful, dying young — and the Crown had held the needle.

And tonight, one way or another, the stitching came out.

"There has to be another way," she said. "Not renewal. Not collapse. Something else."

Vessarath was silent for a long moment. The ember-veins pulsed — dimmer now, she was sure of it, dimmer than when she'd entered.

"Your ancestor's confession," he said at last. "The one carved in the hollow shrine. Read it again, Keeper. All of it. Even the parts that seem like rambling. Maera wrote more than guilt into that stone." His eyes fixed on hers, ancient and burning low. "She wrote a warning. And I think — I have thought for a century — that she also wrote a *door*."

* * *

Far below, in a surgeon's tent smelling of pine pitch and blood, Ryn Vasq woke with a gasp and Ada's name in his mouth — and outside the tent flap, on the eastern horizon, the peaks of the Emberlands flickered gold, then gray, then gold, like a lamp running out of oil.

And in the high lair, Vessarath's heartbeat skipped.

Ada felt it through the stone beneath her palms. Felt the mountain miss a step.

The countdown, whatever counted it, had heard.
