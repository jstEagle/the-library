## 15. The Forger's Daughter

I have lived nine hundred years, and I remember all of them.

That is the first thing you must understand about dragons, daughter of the forger — we do not forget. Men build their forgetting into stone and paper and the soft tissue of their children's minds. They call it mercy. We call it what it is: a way of surviving your own crimes.

You are dreaming now. Do not fight it. The fire between us carries you as easily as it carries light, and I have not the strength left to be gentle about it. My wound speaks louder than my courtesy these days. So listen. You came to me asking why, and a dying dragon does not ration his answers.

* * *

Before there was a Sable Gate, there was a pass. That is all Thornfell was — a pass, a throat of stone between two worlds that had learned, over long centuries, to breathe around each other without choking.

The Emberlands were green then. Say it aloud sometime, when no one is listening, and feel how the words sit wrong in your mouth. Green. Terraced vineyards on the warm slopes of Mount Vessarath's shoulder — my shoulder, little keeper, I am named for the mountain or the mountain for me; even I have stopped being sure which came first. Rivers that ran silver with snowmelt and gold with sun. And fire — fire everywhere, but fire as we keep it, which is not the fire men know. Ours was a hearth-fire scaled up to the size of a sky. It warmed. It ripened. It did not, unless provoked beyond all patience, destroy.

Your people came to us in winter. That is how the story always begins, in every version, because it is true in every version. A famine in the lowlands. Crops blackened by blight, granaries empty, rivers frozen in a cold so deep the old men said the world had forgotten how to turn. Refugees climbed the pass in their thousands, and at the top they found us.

We fed them. Understand this, whatever else you take from tonight: we fed them. Dragon-fire under their cookpots. Dragon-warmed caves for their children. We gave them seed-grain from our own terraces and taught them which slopes would take it. There was no treaty because none was needed. There was trust, which is older than treaties and worth more, and which is precisely why its betrayal cuts the way it does.

Among those refugees was a woman named Sela Okafor.

* * *

Ah. There it is. Your breath just changed. Good. Hold that feeling — the small cold drop behind the sternum. You will need it later, when I tell you what she did, because if you flinch away now you will flinch away from everything after.

Sela Okafor was not a soldier or a priestess. She was a ledger-keeper. A woman who counted things. In the famine years she had counted the dead of her village until the numbers outgrew her grief, and grief that cannot fit inside a person goes looking for somewhere else to live. Hers went looking for power.

She stayed after the famine broke. Most of your people went home to their mud and their recovery, but Sela remained, ostensibly as an envoy, keeping accounts of the debt her people owed us. She sat in our councils. She learned our tongue — Old Emberish, the language you heard counting down through the stones of your gate, the language in which oaths are not spoken but *woven*. She studied us the way you study weather, daughter. Not as a peer. As a force to be predicted, managed, and — when the moment came — harnessed.

And here is the part your Crown's histories got almost right, which is worse than getting it wrong: the humans *did* ask us for a covenant. Formally, solemnly, a generation after the famine. They asked for a binding of realms, mutual keeping, free passage. We debated it among ourselves for a year. Some of us wanted it. I wanted it. I argued for it, in the great ash-circle, with my name offered first.

We said yes.

Do you see? We were not tricked into the covenant. We chose it. That is the wound beneath the wound, the one that has kept me alive all these centuries when a clean death might have been kinder. We were not deceived. We were *outvoted by our own hope.*

* * *

Now the betrayal. I will tell it plainly, because plainly is the only way it can be told, and because you will need every word of it verbatim before this is done.

The covenant as agreed was a weaving of equals. Two signatures of intent — human and dragon — braided into a single pattern, neither thread holding the other. Free passage across the pass. Shared stewardship of the fire. Either realm could loose the weave by speaking the unbinding, and both would return to what they had been. Choice, preserved on both sides. That was the design. That was what we wove into the keystone with our own flame while Sela Okafor stood witness with her ledgers and her patient eyes.

What we did not know — could not know, because we do not think the way you think, because treachery of this fineness requires a mind that counts things — was that Sela had spent twenty years preparing a second keystone.

She cut it herself. Obsidian from the deep pass, cooled in blood — her own, freely given, which is the only currency strong enough to hold a god's attention. And into it she carved a counter-weave, a splinter of intent hidden inside the braid like a worm in fruit. When the two keystones met at the gate's crowning, hers drank ours. Not destroyed it. *Drank* it. Our half of the covenant became fuel for hers, and hers had terms written in it that no dragon ever agreed to.

Here are those terms. Learn them by heart, because they are still in force, and because you are going to rewrite them:

Dragon-fire bound to human survival. Not shared — *bound*. Our flame would feed the lowlands' warmth, the fertility of border soils, the health of every crop within a hundred miles of Thornfell, forever. And the price, the leash-end knotted around our necks: if the fire failed, the humans bound to it would sicken and die, and their deaths would fall upon the dragons as a debt of blood. We could not withhold our fire without killing innocents. We could not leave. We could not refuse. Every year of warmth was a year of consent we never gave.

And the second clause, the one that shames my whole kind: the gate required a keeper of Okafor blood. A living anchor, generation after generation, whose life-force fed the counter-weave. Sela wrote her descendants into the spell as its fuel. She made her own line into the lock.

Why? you ask. Why would a woman who owed us her children's lives do this?

Because she counted. Because in the ledgers of her grief, the arithmetic ran thus: humans are many and mortal and helpless against winter, and dragons are few and mighty and might someday choose otherwise. She did not hate us. That is the horror of it, daughter — she may even have loved some of us. But she looked at the shape of the future and decided that our choice was a risk her people could not afford, and so she took it from us the way you'd take a knife from a child. Quietly. For our own good. With love, perhaps, in the hand that held the blade.

She stole our tomorrow so that her people could have one. And it worked. Gods forgive us all, it worked. The lowlands bloomed. The famines ended. Your kingdoms grew fat and safe behind a mountain that could never again say no.

* * *

When we discovered it — a decade later, when the first keeper sickened and we felt our fire *pull*, felt it drain toward the lowlands like blood toward a opened vein — there was war. Of course there was war. Three years of it. You will find it in your histories as the Border Troubles, a squabble, a footnote. We burned the pass roads. Your knights drove us back into the high valleys with iron forged in our own donated heat. And in the end we stopped fighting for the simplest and most terrible reason: every season of war starved the lowlands, because our fire was already theirs, and every starving village was another weight of guilt laid on our backs.

Sela's masterpiece was not the theft. It was making our retaliation cost *us* more than it cost her.

So we withdrew into the Emberlands and called it sovereignty. Your kings called it peace. The green valleys began their long cooling — a dragon's fire, leashed, gutters — and the frost came down the terraces a little more each century, and we told ourselves it was temporary, that a way to unweave the counter-spell would be found, that pride was enough to live on.

It was not. Pride is not enough to live on. Ask anyone who has tried.

Nine hundred years. Nine generations of Okafors standing at my gate — my gate, which became their gate, which became the leash itself — each one dying a little earlier than the last, each one's life poured into the stone that holds me here. I watched them come. I watched them go. Some hated me. One or two loved me, in the way you love a mountain you've been chained to. None of them knew. Sela kept her secret well; the confession she eventually carved in the hollow shrine was written in cipher and shame, and her heirs were raised on the noble story, the treaty story, the lie with the good posture.

Until now. Until a peddler carried a burned book up a frozen road and put it in the hands of a keeper with her ancestor's eyes and, apparently, her ancestor's talent for finding the flaw in any system.

* * *

You are quiet. Dreamers usually argue with me at this point — *not my family, not my blood, we were told it was a treaty* — but you are not arguing. You are doing the arithmetic. I can feel it, the ledger turning behind your eyes. Counting the dead of the famine against the dead of the leash. Counting my nine hundred winters against your grandmother's short warm life. Counting what it costs to be free against what it costs to be safe.

Be careful with that arithmetic, daughter of the forger. It is exactly how Sela started.

But do not stop either. That is the trap set for you from the other side — the ones who will tell you the past is too heavy to lift, that the covenant is simply how things are, that a working leash is better than a broken world. They will offer you comfort the way Sela offered herself certainty. Refuse both comforts. Sit in the vertigo. The truth lives there, and only there.

Because here is what I have not yet said, the thing I brought you into the fire to say, the reason a dying dragon spends his last clear hours on a history lesson:

The counter-weave is failing. Sela built it to run on Okafor lives, and the line has thinned, and the gate is cracking, and when it cracks the binding does not dissolve — it *releases*. All nine hundred years of stored fire, unbound at once, into a world that has forgotten what raw dragon-flame is. Your lowlands will burn. My Emberlands will freeze, because the fire that leaves will not be replaced. Both realms end on the same night, and the date is written in a book you now carry, circled in ink older than your kingdom.

There were supposed to be two ways out. The prophecy-keepers found them both: reforge the covenant with a willing sacrifice — an Okafor giving herself wholly, renewing the leash with fresher blood — or break the gate entirely and let the realms divide forever, dragons freezing in our high country while humans keep the warmed lowlands. Sacrifice yourself, or sacrifice us. Those were the choices written into the world by a woman who counted things, and I have hated her for nine hundred years for leaving only those two.

But I am old, and old dragons know something about books that book-burners never learn.

The margins matter.

* * *

Wake now. Someone is coming up the mountain through the dark — limping, cursing, carrying more than he told you he carried. Your smuggler. He has been paid, daughter, though not by whom you think, and the coin in his pocket weighs more than his silence, and he has chosen, tonight, which weight to set down. Whether he tells you himself or you drag it out of him, hear it with the ledger open. Everyone serves something. The question that matters — the only question Sela never asked, and the question that can unmake her work — is whether a person can choose to stop.

He is about to show you the answer.

Go. The fire will carry you back. And Ada Okafor —

When you stand at my gate again, look at the keystone. Look at what she hid inside the gift. Then decide what *you* hide inside yours.
