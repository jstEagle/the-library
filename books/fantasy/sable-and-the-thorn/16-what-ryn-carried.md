## 16. What Ryn Carried

The bolt came out at dawn.

Ryn had insisted on watching the whole thing, mostly because he didn't trust anyone in Emberfall Village not to rob him while he was helpless, and partly because he wanted to see what his own body looked like from the outside. The answer was: worse than advertised. The healer — a broad-shouldered woman named Sefa who smelled of pine resin and old smoke — worked the barbed head free with a pair of tongs and a patience that seemed almost insulting given how much noise Ryn was making about it.

"Breathe," she said.

"I am breathing."

"You're breathing like a man being robbed. Breathe like a man paying a debt."

He laughed, which was a mistake. The laugh turned into a cough, the cough into a white flare behind his eyes, and when it cleared, Ada was there. She had been there all night, apparently, sitting on a stool by the shuttered window with the prophecy book open on her knees and her boots still muddy from the shrine road. She hadn't slept. He could tell because she did that thing where she read the same line four times and turned the page anyway, the way people do when they're reading to stay awake rather than to learn anything.

"How bad?" she asked.

"Sefa says I'll live."

"Sefa says you'll live if you stop moving," Sefa said without looking up. "There's a difference. Men like you die of the difference."

She packed the wound with moss and honey and something bitter that made the whole room smell like a distillery's regrets, bound it tight, and left with instructions delivered over her shoulder: no walking for two days, no lying about how much it hurt, and absolutely no arguing with the Okafor woman, because in Sefa's professional experience the Okafor women always won arguments and it was better for everyone if the losing happened quickly.

Then it was just the two of them, and the small room above the village's one tavern, and the sound of wind working at the shutters.

Ada closed the book. She set it on the table between them, deliberately, the way you'd set down a weapon you weren't sure you were done needing.

"You should sleep," Ryn said.

"You should talk." She pulled the stool closer. "I've had two days to think about why a crossbow bolt found your back on an empty trail that only three people knew existed. Two days to think about who else knew it."

"Brann's scouts—"

"Brann's scouts burned the shrine. They didn't know the cut-across past the falls. Nobody knew that route except my family's ledger and—" She stopped. Looked at him. "And whoever told them where we'd be."

The wind filled the silence. Somewhere below, the tavern keeper was arguing with a cart driver about the price of flour, their voices rising and falling like weather.

Ryn lay back against the pillows and studied the ceiling beams, because it was easier than studying her face. There were knots in the wood shaped like nothing at all, and he gave them his full attention.

"The crew sold the route," he said finally. "Doss and the others. After the shrine burned, they figured the pass was going to change hands no matter what, so they made sure it changed hands to whoever paid best. Brann's quartermaster. Three hundred crowns and a pardon apiece."

Ada nodded slowly. "That explains the soldiers. It doesn't explain the crossbowman. Brann wants me alive — he wants the book, but he wants *me* to hand it over. A dead keeper is a martyr. Martyrs are inconvenient."

"No," Ryn agreed. "A dead keeper is very inconvenient. That wasn't Brann's man."

"Whose, then?"

He took a breath, and it hurt, and he let the hurt buy him another second before the truth came out, because he'd been rehearsing this speech for two days and every version of it sounded worse than the last.

"Mine."

* * *

She didn't move. That was the first thing he noticed — she didn't flinch, didn't stand, didn't reach for the knife she'd taken to wearing at her hip since the shrine. She just went very still, the way the gate used to go still before a flare, and he understood that he was looking at the same quality in her that lived in the stone: something patient and load-bearing that you did not want to test.

"Say it plainly," she said.

"There's a faction in the Emberlands. Remnant loyalists — dragons' partisans, humans born under the covenant who think the whole arrangement is a cage. They've been smuggling pages out of Vessarath's lair for years. Old texts. Prophecy fragments. I carried them across the pass, page by page, sewn into bales of saffron and bolts of wool, and I never asked what they added up to because asking questions is how smugglers retire early." He gestured weakly at the book on the table. "Those pages are mine. Or they were. I'm the reason the book exists in one piece."

Ada's jaw tightened. "You're the reason I have it."

"And six months ago, my employers decided the book shouldn't just exist. It should be *used*. Delivered to the right hands at the right time. Tonight's date, Ada. They've known it was coming longer than you have. And when you started asking questions instead of burning the book like any sensible keeper would — when you started treating prophecy as a problem to solve rather than a message to obey — they decided you were a complication."

"So they sent someone to kill me."

"They sent someone to kill *you*, yes. And when I wouldn't tell them which road you'd take out of Thornfell—" He tapped his own shoulder, near the bandage. "They sent someone to make a point about me instead. Doss didn't sell them the route. Doss doesn't know the cut-across. I do. And the only person I told was a courier named Maren, who I thought was crew, and who was theirs from the start."

The argument downstairs had stopped. In its place there was just the wind, and the creak of the building settling, and Ada's breathing, slow and even, the breathing of a woman counting to some number Ryn couldn't guess.

"You let me believe it was Brann," she said.

"For two days. Yes."

"Why?"

"Because 'your ally sold you out to dragon cultists' is a harder sentence to survive than 'the Crown is hunting us.' Because I kept hoping I'd find a way to fix it before you had to know. Because I'm a coward in the specific ways smugglers are cowards — we lie beautifully and call it craft." He pushed himself up on one elbow, and the wound screamed, and he kept talking through it because stopping would be worse. "I chose you over the coin, Ada. When they gave me the choice — your location or your death — I gave them a lie instead, and I've been bleeding for the lie ever since. But I don't get to dress that up as heroism. I carried their pages for years. I built the road they tried to kill you on. Every mile of it, I chose the work first and told myself the choosing didn't count."

"And now?"

"Now it counts."

Ada stood. Walked to the window. Opened the shutter a hand's width, and the light that came through was wrong — thin and gray, the light of a morning that hadn't decided to be one. Beyond the rooftops of Emberfall, the peaks of the Emberlands stood against the sky, and Ryn saw her see it: the frost line, creeping down the southern faces where green terraces should have been. The covenant failing in real time, written in ice on mountainsides.

"My grandmother kept a saying," Ada said, without turning around. "*Trust the ledger, not the trader.* She meant it as advice about grain shipments. I've been applying it to people my whole life, and it's kept the gate quiet for eleven years, and it has also kept me alone in a stone house on a mountain, and I want you to know that I understand exactly what kind of fool it makes me that I trusted you anyway."

"Ada—"

"Not finished." Her voice stayed level. "You lied to me for months. You let me walk into an ambush. You let me believe the worst of the wrong enemy while the actual enemy sharpened knives in the dark. And then you took a bolt meant to buy me an hour's head start, and you nearly died, and you confessed the whole architecture of it while bleeding in a tavern bed because you couldn't stand letting me owe you gratitude on false pretenses." She turned around. "I don't know what to do with you, Ryn Vasq. Every instinct I have says close the account. Every other instinct — the ones I trust less, the ones that got me into this whole catastrophe — says you're the only person in either realm who's told me the truth at cost."

"That second instinct," Ryn said, "is the better one."

"It usually isn't."

"No. But it is now."

She looked at him for a long moment. Then she crossed the room, picked up the prophecy book, and held it against her chest like a shield that had learned to be a door.

"The faction that hired you," she said. "The remnant loyalists. What do they actually want?"

"The gate broken. All the way. Not reforged — *broken*. They think the covenant's been a leash so long the only freedom left is to cut the throat of the thing wearing it."

"And Vessarath? Where does the last dragon stand?"

Ryn hesitated. This was the part he'd hoarded, the piece he'd told no one, the payment he'd extracted from his employers in exchange for the lie that had gotten him shot. He'd told himself he was saving it for leverage. Lying in the bed, watching Ada hold the book, he understood it had never been leverage. It had been a message he was too afraid to deliver.

"They want him to die with the gate," he said quietly. "A dying dragon can't contest whatever comes after. The loyalists aren't fighting for Vessarath, Ada. They're fighting for an Emberlands with no dragon in it — theirs, human-shaped, finally free of fangs and fire and the old debts both." He met her eyes. "Your prophecy has two endings. Reforge or break. But the people pulling strings on both sides of the pass have already picked their endings, and neither of them includes the last dragon living to see the morning after."

Ada stood very still. Outside, the frost crept another inch down the mountain, patient as ink spreading through water.

"Then we're out of time," she said. "Can you ride?"

"Sefa says two days."

"Sefa," Ada said, reaching for her pack, "isn't the one the prophecy circled."

* * *

Sefa, predictably, won the argument — but only partially. She relented to a litter, two strong-backed villagers hired at rates Ryn would have called extortionate if he'd had any standing to complain, and a pouch of willow bark and bitterroot sufficient to sedate a mule. Which was, she observed, roughly what she was working with.

They left Emberfall at midmorning, heading north along the frozen terraces toward the high country where, according to everything Ryn had carried and everything Ada had read, a dying dragon was running out of dying to do.

Ryn drifted in and out of fever-sleep as the litter swayed. At the edge of one waking, he found Ada walking beside him, matching the bearers' pace, the book wrapped in oilcloth and strapped across her back like a second spine.

"Hey," he said. His voice came out sanded down. "For what it's worth. The pages I carried — I read them. Near the end, once I started suspecting what they added up to. There was a marginal note in one of them. Old Emberish. I had it translated by a fence in Lowmark who reads dead languages for fun."

Ada stopped walking. "What did it say?"

"*When the keystone cracks, look for the third hand.*" He watched her face do something complicated — hope and terror arriving at the same door. "I don't know what it means. But somebody wrote it in the margin of a prophecy a long time ago, and they bothered to hide it, and hidden things are usually hidden because they matter."

Ada looked north, toward the peaks, toward the wound in the world that was widening with every hour. Then she looked down at him, and something in her expression shifted — not forgiveness, not yet, but the ground where forgiveness might someday be planted.

"A third hand," she repeated. "Not the Crown's. Not the loyalists'. Not yours, and not mine."

"Somebody's," Ryn said. "Somebody who saw the trap before either ending sprang it."

She adjusted the strap on her shoulder and started walking again, faster now, and the litter-bearers hurried to keep up, and the frozen terraces fell away behind them one by one.

Above the tree line, far off, something vast exhaled a thread of fire at the gray sky — thinner than it should have been, guttering, a candle-flame trying to remember it had once been a sun.

"Hold on, old one," Ada murmured, too quiet for anyone but Ryn to hear. "We're coming. And we're bringing a third option."

The wind off the peaks carried her words away north, toward the lair, toward the listening dark — where, unknown to either of them, something that was not a dragon had heard the phrase *third hand* before, and had spent a century making sure no one reached it alive.
