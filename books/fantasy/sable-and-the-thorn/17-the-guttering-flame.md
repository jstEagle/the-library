## 17. The Guttering Flame

Ada woke to a silence she had never heard before.

She had spent thirty-one years listening to the Sable Gate. Even in the Emberlands' high passes, three days from the gatehouse now, she had carried its hum with her like a second heartbeat — that low, mineral thrum of bound dragon-fire, steady as tide, constant as grief. She had slept to it as a child. She had measured her days by it. When Ryn asked her once what home sounded like, she had said, without thinking, *like a held breath that never ends.*

This morning the breath was coming apart.

She lay still in the hollow beneath the shrine's fallen lintel, Ryn's bedroll a dark shape across the firepit, and listened to the sound she could only describe as a stutter. The hum was there — but it caught. Held. Caught again. Like a heart remembering, between beats, that it was mortal.

*The wound,* she thought. *The keystone-shaped wound in his chest.* Vessarath's fire bled through it day and night into the covenant's machinery, and the machinery was failing, and somewhere behind her ribs the arithmetic finished itself: every stutter in the hum was a stutter in the dragon.

She got up quietly, wrapped her cloak over the shift she'd slept in, and climbed out through the shrine's broken eastern wall to where she could see the peaks.

What she saw stopped her breath for the second time that morning.

The Emberlands had always burned. That was the first thing any child in Thornfell learned, before letters, before prayers: the far mountains wore crowns of light. Dragon-fire glowed along the high ridgelines like embers banked in a hearth the size of the sky — red-gold, patient, eternal. Pilgrims wept at the sight of it. Smugglers navigated by it. Ada had audited its constancy for three decades without once thinking of it as anything but permanent, the way you never think of your own pulse.

This morning, the northern ridge was dark.

Not dimmed. Not veiled by cloud. *Dark* — a long stretch of the Crownspine where the glow simply ended, a black gap in the burning crown like a tooth knocked from a jaw. And south of it, where the light still held, it flickered. She watched it for a full minute, unmoving, cold working down through her cloak into her bones, and the flicker resolved itself into rhythm.

It was keeping time with the stuttering hum.

*Oh,* she thought, with terrible clarity. *It's not a metaphor. It never was.*

"Ada." Ryn's voice, rough with sleep, behind her. "You're standing in frost."

She looked down. She was. White rime silvered the shrine stones around her boots, and beyond the wall, on ground that should have been warm enough to grow ember-moss year round — ground that had never, in the memory of any ledger she'd read, known ice — the frost ran downhill in fingers toward the valley.

"The fire's guttering," she said.

Ryn came to stand beside her. He didn't ask what she meant. He looked at the dark ridge, and she watched his face do something she'd rarely seen it do: go completely honest. No smuggler's charm, no calculated half-truths. Just a man looking at the end of a world he'd made his living skirting.

"That's the Emberlands," he said. "That's — people live down there, Ada. Valley folk. The green valleys."

"I know."

"You said the covenant was failing. I believed you. But believing a thing and seeing—" He gestured at the dead ridge, and his hand wasn't quite steady. "How fast?"

"I don't know." She hated saying it. Her whole life had been built on knowing — on ledgers and tallies and the exact weight of every cart through the gate. "The prophecy gave us tonight. Whatever happens at the gate happens tomorrow nightfall. But this—" she nodded at the frost, "—this isn't waiting for the prophecy. This is the wound bleeding out ahead of schedule. Or—" and the thought arrived cold and complete, "—or the gate is already pulling everything back through the keystone. Hoarding what's left."

"For what?"

"For whoever holds it when it breaks."

They stood in silence while the sun came up gray behind thin cloud, and the frost did not melt.

* * *

They walked down into the valley because there was no choice in it. The road to the gate ran through Emberfall country — the border villages that had traded dragon-warmth for human grain since before either realm kept records — and if the fire was dying, then the people who lived closest to it were dying first.

Ada had passed through Emberfall Village twice in her career, both times on inspection, both times with the brisk pity of a Crown official noting conditions for a report no one would read. She remembered tidy lanes and roof-vents open to catch the heat-shimmer off the peaks, gardens growing things that shouldn't have grown so far north, children barefoot in months that would have killed them in Thornfell. She remembered thinking, with the small complacency of the well-fed: *the covenant works. Look how well it works.*

The village was still there. That was almost the worst part. It hadn't burned or flooded or been swallowed; it had simply begun, stone by stone and breath by breath, to die of cold.

The vents were shut. Every one of them, boarded against a wind that carried no warmth anymore, and the boards were new-cut, pale wood bright against old stone. The gardens were black rows. A woman was hauling water from the well with the stiff, economical movements of someone conserving every calorie, and when she saw them coming down the road she didn't call out or run. She just set down her yoke and waited, arms crossed, the way you wait for weather.

"You're not soldiers," she said. It wasn't a question. Her eyes went to Ada's keeper's badge — Ada hadn't thought to hide it, and now it sat on her chest like an accusation — and something moved through her face too fast to name. "Keeper. We don't see keepers down here unless someone's died."

"No one's died," Ada said. Then, because the woman's face told her the word was wrong: "That isn't why we've come."

"No. Nothing dies quick anymore. That'd be a mercy." The woman picked up her yoke again, more to have something in her hands than because she meant to carry it anywhere. "Maren Solva. If you're here about the fire, you're late. It started going out eight days ago. Ridge above the north fields first. Then the hot springs went tepid. Now the wells are steaming less each morning, and my boy asks me why his hands hurt at night, and I tell him it's growing pains, and he's seven, Keeper, he knows what lies sound like."

Eight days. Ada did the arithmetic against the timeline in the prophecy book and felt the floor tilt. Eight days ago was when she'd first heard the voice counting down in Old Emberish. The gate's failure and the land's freezing weren't two events. They were one event, unfolding at both ends at once, and she was standing inside it.

"What do the people here know?" she asked. "About the cause?"

Maren Solva laughed, and it was the worst sound Ada had heard in weeks. "We know what our grandmothers knew. The fire is a gift and gifts can be withdrawn. We know the keepers keep it flowing and the dragons make it and nobody down here gets a vote on either." She shifted the yoke. "You want the truth? The truth is we were warm for four hundred years and we never once asked what it cost anyone else. Why would we? You don't ask the price of bread while you're eating it."

Ryn made a small sound beside her. Ada glanced at him; his jaw was tight, eyes fixed on the black garden rows. She understood. In the Hollow Shrine she had read her ancestor's confession carved in stone — *I bound their fire to our survival, and called it peace* — and had felt the vertigo of inherited sin. But Maren Solva was describing the other side of the same coin: not the forger's guilt, but the forged-for's comfort. Four hundred years of Thornfell's grain and Emberfall's warmth, and neither realm asking questions, because the questions were all answered in advance by a leash disguised as a treaty.

"We're going to the gate," Ada said. "Tomorrow night it fails — fully, finally, unless something changes. I'm going to change it. I can't promise what comes after. But I needed to see this." She gestured at the village, the boarded vents, the frost on the shrine stones miles above. "I needed to see what I was keeping."

Maren studied her for a long moment. Whatever she found in Ada's face, it softened her by perhaps a degree. "My grandmother said the first keeper was a thief," she said. "Everyone says it, quietly, the way you say things that would get you taken by inquisitors. We just never thought the bill would come due in our lifetime." She turned, then paused. "There's soup on if you want it. It's thin. But you'll walk better for it, and I'd rather feed the person fixing this than bury another neighbor."

* * *

The soup was barley and snow-water and a scrap of salt pork stretched past reason, and it was the best meal Ada had eaten in years, because hunger shared in a dying village tastes like communion. Sixteen people crowded the common house around the single stove they still dared to fuel — coal, hauled up from Thornfell at prices that would have made Ryn blush — and they ate in a quiet that wasn't peaceful. It was the quiet of people listening to their own houses cool.

A girl sat near Ada, maybe ten, mending a net with fingers gone chapped and red. She kept glancing at the keeper's badge.

"You're the one who decides about the fire?" the girl asked finally.

"I decide about the gate," Ada said. "Which is nearly the same thing."

"My da says the dragons are dying and it's our fault for taking their fire." The girl's needle didn't stop moving. Children here, Ada realized, learned to work while they talked, because stopping cost warmth. "Is that true?"

Ada could have given her the official answer. She had given it a hundred times, to pilgrims and inspectors and frightened travelers: *the covenant is a mutual bond, freely entered, sustaining both realms.* She heard the words queue themselves up, smooth and practiced, and found she could not say them. Not here. Not under this cooling roof, to this child with her ruined hands.

"It's true that the fire was taken," she said. "By my family, a long time ago. They bound it so it couldn't be refused. And now the binding is breaking, and the fire is going home, or going out, and I'm trying to find a way that it does neither." She hesitated. "I'm sorry. I know that's not comfort."

The girl considered this with the unnerving directness of children everywhere. "Better than the lie," she decided. "Ma says the lies are what got us here." She went back to her net.

Afterward, outside, Ryn lit a cigarette with hands that shook slightly and pretended they didn't. "You realize," he said, exhaling, "that if you fix this — whatever fixing looks like — none of it matters to them unless the heat comes back. You could rewrite the covenant in gold letters and free every dragon in the sky, and if Emberfall stays frozen, you've just changed which side starves."

"I know."

"And if you reforge it the old way — blood price, willing Okafor, all of that—" he wouldn't look at her as he said it, and she let him not look, "—then it works, and it's a leash again, and this village stays warm by keeping someone chained. By keeping *you*—"

"Don't," Ada said quietly.

"—chained," he finished anyway, because Ryn Vasq had never once in his life left a sentence unfinished out of politeness. He flicked the cigarette away. "I've been thinking about it since the shrine. There has to be a way that isn't those two. Renewal or ruin. There's always a third door. Smugglers live by third doors — the road everyone thinks is closed, the pass that's only open at certain light, the toll that's really a bribe. Prophecies are just roads, Ada. Somebody wrote this one, and somebody who writes a road can leave a track off the map."

She stared at him. "You think the prophecy has a hidden route."

"I think a book that careful — dates circled, names written, endings laid out like tollgates — was written by someone who wanted the reader to arrive at a specific place. And writers who want you somewhere always leave out the option that ruins their design." He shrugged, that loose shrug that had gotten him out of a hundred checkpoints. "Or I'm a smuggler rationalizing. Could be both."

But she was already pulling the book from her pack.

* * *

They read by the last of the daylight, in the lee of the common house wall, wrapped in blankets Maren's neighbors pressed on them with the fierce hospitality of people who refuse to let cold make them cruel. The prophecy book lay open across Ada's knees, its scorched edges crumbling a little more each time she turned a page, and she went through it the way she audited a ledger: line by line, entry by entry, hunting the discrepancy.

Two endings. She'd known them for days now, and they'd sat in her chest like swallowed stones. *The gate renewed in a keeper's blood: the realms endure, divided, forever.* And: *the gate broken: the fire goes free, and the human lands go dark.* One saved the dragons. One saved the humans. The book offered nothing else. It was a corridor with two doors and no windows, and she had been pacing it for days, hating the architect.

Now she read like a smuggler instead of a keeper. She read for what *wasn't* there.

Page after page of verse, dense and formal, Old Emberish rendered in a human hand — and the hand troubled her, the more she looked at it. The script was meticulous. Too meticulous. Real prophecies, real visions, came out ragged; she'd seen genuine seer-work in the gatehouse archives, and it scrawled and contradicted itself and dripped. This was composed. Edited. Someone had taken raw material and shaped it into a corridor with exactly two doors.

"There," Ryn said suddenly, tapping the margin of the page that described the breaking. "Look. The ink's different."

He was right. Down the outer edge of the margin ran a line of annotation in paler ink, faded almost to nothing, in a smaller, hurried hand — not the composer's hand at all. Ada tilted the page against the dying light, then struck a match and held it close, and the words surfaced like fish rising through dark water:

*She who reads this will be told there are two ways. There were three. The third was cut from the telling because it costs the tellers everything. Ask what the keystone was before it was a keystone. Ask what the gate was before it was a gate. The answer is not in this book. It is in the stone.*

Ada read it twice. Three times. The match burned down to her fingers and she didn't notice until Ryn closed her hand around it.

"'Ask what the keystone was before it was a keystone,'" she repeated. Her voice came out strange. "Vessarath's wound. The wound in his chest is shaped exactly like the Sable Gate's keystone. I saw it. I thought it meant the gate was killing him — that the binding drew its power through his body and marked him."

"Maybe it means more than that." Ryn's eyes had gone bright and sharp, the look he got when a route opened where maps said cliff. "If the keystone was something *before* it was a keystone — if the gate was built *out of* something—" He stopped, and she watched him get there a heartbeat before she did, watched the color leave his face. "Ada. What if the gate isn't a machine with a dragon plugged into it. What if the gate is made *of* a dragon."

The frost crackled in the silence. Somewhere up the valley, a child coughed in the cold.

"Not a dragon," Ada said slowly, the thought assembling itself out of terror and wonder in equal measure. "*The* dragon. The first one. The one the covenant was signed with — the confession said 'signed in fire,' and I read it as oath-fire, ceremony. But what if it was literal? What if my ancestor didn't bind dragon-fire in the abstract. She took a living creature and made it the cornerstone of a wall between worlds, and every renewal since has just been... maintenance. Patching. Feeding the wall."

"And Vessarath is what's left of its kin," Ryn said. "Wearing the shape of the wound."

Ada looked up at the peaks. The southern ridges still glowed, but faintly now, a hearth at dawn rather than midnight, and the dark gap in the north had widened since morning. She could measure it. Of course she could measure it; measuring was the only prayer she'd ever known. Half a mile, perhaps, since sunrise. At that rate—

"At that rate the Emberlands go dark two days before the prophecy date," she said aloud. "The land doesn't care about the book's schedule. The wound is opening faster than the story."

"Then we're out of time to be careful." Ryn was already rolling the blankets, already moving, because underneath the charm he had the same engine she did: momentum, motion, the refusal to stand still inside a burning room. "The stone. 'The answer is in the stone.' Which stone?"

Ada closed the book and held it against her chest, and thought of the Hollow Shrine, and the confession carved there, and of every block of the Sable Gate she had touched ten thousand times without ever once *asking* it anything.

"All of them," she said. "Every stone my family ever laid. Starting with the shrine — the first signatories built it, and if the first keeper hid a truth even from her own book, she'd hide it where her descendants would come looking when it mattered." She stood. The cold hit her like a wall and she welcomed it; cold was information, and information was the only weapon she had left. "We walk tonight. Through the frost. To the shrine."

Ryn shouldered the packs. "And if the army's watching the trail?"

"Then we'll know," Ada said, "exactly how much time we have left."

Above them, as they turned onto the frozen road, the last ridge in the south flickered — caught — held — and burned on, thinner than a candle flame in wind. Behind them, Maren Solva stood in her doorway with her arms crossed, watching them go, and did not say goodbye, because goodbye was a word for things that ended cleanly, and nothing in Thornfell had ended cleanly in four hundred years.

The frost followed them down the road like a debt collecting interest.
