## 19. The Council of Ash

The Emberlands did not want them.

Ada had understood that in the abstract since the moment she and Ryn crossed the old boundary stones — the ones no human was supposed to cross, the ones her family had spent four generations pretending were merely survey markers. But understanding a thing in the abstract and standing in front of it were different animals entirely.

The council convened in what had once been a temple to the flame, back when the Emberlands had flame enough to warrant temples. Now the great hearth at its center held a fire no bigger than a cookpot's, and nine figures sat around it in a crescent of cracked basalt thrones, and every one of them watched Ada Okafor walk down the aisle like she was something that had crawled out of a grave.

Which, she supposed, in their accounting, she was.

"Daughter of the forger," said the one at the center. She was old the way mountains are old — not bent by it, just worn smooth, her scales gone the grey of banked coals. Emberfolk, the last of a people who had once been as numerous as stars over Thornfell. Her name, when Ryn had whispered it to him on the road up, was Serevanne. "You come to the Council of Ash. Speak, and be brief. Our fire is short."

Ryn stood at Ada's shoulder, his bandaged shoulder, his whole left side stiff under the sling he'd refused to stop wearing even though Maera — the herb-wife who'd pulled the bolt out of him two days ago — said the wound was clean and would knit if he'd only stop being a stubborn mule about it. He'd insisted on coming. *You're not walking into a room full of grieving dragons without someone watching your back,* he'd said. *It's the only kind of watching I'm good at.*

Now he shifted his weight, and Ada felt him there, solid and hurting, and drew breath.

"I've come to ask for aid," she said. "Vessarath is dying. The gate is failing. And the Crown is—"

"We know what the Crown is." This from a younger one on the left, male, his voice like gravel sliding. "We have known for a hundred years while your realm grew fat behind our stolen fire."

"I'm not here to defend the Crown."

"No." Serevanne's eyes were the color of embers dying in rain. "You are here to defend yourself. There is a difference, though I suspect you cannot yet see it."

* * *

They had climbed for three days after the shrine. Three days through passes where the frost crept visibly downhill each morning, silvering grass that should have been green until midsummer, cracking stone with ice that had no business existing this close to dragon-fire country. Ada had watched a waterfall frozen mid-fall, a white tongue hanging off a cliff face, and thought: *this is what we did.* Not the Crown. Not some distant abstraction called history. Her blood. Her name. Okafor.

Vessarath had shown her the wound before they left — or rather, she had finally understood what she was looking at. A scar across the dragon's chest the exact shape of the Sable Gate's keystone, ancient and never-healing, the place where her ancestor had driven the binding home. Every year the covenant renewed itself, and every year the renewal drew a little more fire out of the Emberlands and poured it, measured and leashed, into the human realm below. Warm hearths in Corvale. Green fields in the river valleys. And up here, cold spreading like ink through water.

She had asked Vessarath why he didn't simply tear the gate down himself.

*Because the binding runs through me,* the dragon had said, his voice like a landslide heard from very far away. *I am the anchor as much as the prisoner. If I break it by force, everything the fire has fed dies with it. Both realms. All of it.* A pause, long enough for snow to fall between heartbeats. *There are those among my remnant who do not consider that a reason to wait.*

She hadn't understood then. She understood now, sitting in the ash-light of the council fire, because Serevanne had stopped listening to her petition somewhere around the word *aid*, and the crescent of faces around the hearth had begun to speak all at once.

"—the gate must come down—"

"—every season we wait, another valley goes grey—"

"—she comes asking help to *preserve* it, the audacity—"

"*Enough.*" Serevanne's voice cut through, and the fire guttered as if flinching. She turned back to Ada. "Tell me, daughter of the forger. What is it you propose?"

"That we work together. That we find a way to unmake the binding without unmaking everything it holds up. Vessarath believes—"

"Vessarath believes many things. Vessarath is old, and old things believe in patience because patience is all they have left." The young one again — Ada caught his name now, Korrath, spoken in a hiss by one of the others. He rose from his throne, and he was tall, taller than any man, his scales still holding a dull red heat along the edges like iron fresh from the forge. "Your ancestor bound us. Your family kept the leash taut for six generations. And now that the leash frays, you climb our mountain and ask us to help you mend it *gently*. So that nothing changes too fast. So that your realm keeps its warm hearths a little longer while ours freezes."

"I'm asking so that no one starves," Ada said. "Not your people. Not mine."

"Your people have been eating our future for a hundred years." Korrath came down the steps of his throne, slow, deliberate, and the heat rolled off him in waves that made the air shimmer. Ryn tensed beside her; Ada felt it more than saw it. "Every loaf baked in a Corvale oven. Every forge lit south of the pass. It was ours. It was taken. And you stand there—" his voice dropped, and it was worse than shouting, quieter and colder than shouting, "—and call it *stewardship*."

"I don't," Ada said. "I know what it was. I've read my ancestor's confession. I stood in the Hollow Shrine and read it carved in stone, and I will say it aloud in whatever square you ask me to, in front of whoever needs to hear it. The covenant was theft. My family were thieves. I am not here to preserve the theft."

"Then what are you here to preserve?"

"The world it's holding up."

Silence. Even the fire seemed to lean in.

"You think I don't understand what you want?" Ada went on, and her voice shook, and she let it shake, because hiding it would be one more lie and she had sworn off lies at the keystone of everything. "You want it broken. Tonight. You want the gate shattered and the fire returned and damn the consequences, because the consequences fall mostly on the people who built the cage. I get it. I've stood where you're standing. In the shrine, reading that confession, I wanted to take a hammer to the gate myself. But breaking it outright doesn't free you. It kills you slower and kills us faster. Vessarath told me — the fire isn't stored in the gate. It's *routed* through it. Shatter the route and the fire doesn't come home. It bleeds out. Both realms go dark. Is that your victory? Ash on both sides of the pass?"

"It is justice," Korrath said.

"Justice that starves the same villages you're trying to save."

"Our villages are already starving." It was Serevanne who spoke, and her voice was terribly quiet. "Emberfall. Cinderhollow. The low valleys where the folk who could not fly could not flee either. Have you seen them, keeper? Have you walked the rows of dead orchards?"

"I've seen Emberfall," Ryn said, from beside her. His voice was rough. "I trade through it. Traded," he corrected himself, grim. "Half the village is gone. The other half is deciding whether to follow."

"Then you understand," Serevanne said, "why patience is a luxury we no longer possess. Why a council that once debated for decades now votes in hours." She looked at Ada, and there was something in the old emberfolk's face that was almost — almost — grief. "You come asking for aid. Here is our answer. We will give you none toward mending, none toward reforging, none toward any scheme that leaves the gate standing in any form. What we will give you is time. When the moon turns full, nine days hence, the Council of Ash marches on the Sable Gate with every able hand and every spark we have left, and we break it ourselves. Join us, daughter of the forger, and perhaps your realm will remember you kindly. Stand against us, and you will be buried under the rubble of the thing you guarded."

* * *

They were given a chamber in the temple's undercroft — courtesy, or contempt, Ada couldn't decide. A brazier with barely enough coals to boil water. Two pallets. A door that didn't lock, which meant either trust or the certainty that locking it would accomplish nothing.

Ryn sat on his pallet with his back against the wall and worked at the strap of his sling with his teeth and his good hand, and Ada watched him and said, "Leave it. Maera said—"

"Maera said a lot of things." He got the buckle anyway, wincing, and flexed his arm slowly. The bolt had gone through the meat below his collarbone; she'd changed the dressing herself each morning, hands steadier than she'd expected them to be, and each morning the sight of it had landed somewhere under her ribs and stayed. "Point is, if your new friends decide to make an example of us, I'd like both arms available."

"They're not going to make an example of us."

"You sure? Because the big one looked at you like you were kindling."

"He looked at me like I was everything he's ever hated wearing a sympathetic face." Ada sat down across from him. The brazier light moved on the walls, weak and orange. "And the worst part is he's right to. Ryn — did you hear them? Nine days. They're going to march on the gate with everything they have left, and when they hit it, the covenant will treat it as an attack, and the binding will—"

"Bleed out. Yeah. I was listening." He leaned his head back against the stone. "So we've got allies who'll kill everyone faster than your enemies will. That's a new one for the collection."

"They're not wrong, though. That's what I can't stop chewing on. They're not wrong about any of it. The theft, the leash, the hundred years of our hearths burning their valleys cold. If I were sitting on that throne, I'd vote the same way. Break it. Break it tonight." She laughed, and it came out hollow. "My enemies want me dead and my allies want everything I ever protected destroyed, and honestly, Ryn, the allies might be the more reasonable of the two."

He was quiet a moment. Then: "You really believe it, though. That shattering it kills the fire instead of freeing it."

"Vessarath believes it. And I've seen the wound in his chest shaped like the keystone. I've seen how the covenant routes through him. He's not lying, Ryn. He can't afford to lie. He's the anchor." She rubbed her eyes. "But here's what keeps me up. If breaking it outright destroys both realms, and keeping it as-is drains theirs and leashes mine forever — then the prophecy wasn't offering two endings. It was offering two ways to lose. Somebody wrote that book a century ago knowing the choice was rigged. Knowing whichever way we jumped, somebody paid."

"Somebody always pays." Ryn said it flatly, without self-pity, the way men say things they've learned by losing. "Question's just who, and whether they get a say."

"And nobody gets a say. That's the trick of it. The covenant decides. The prophecy decides. The council decides. Everyone but the people bleeding." She stared into the brazier. "There has to be another way. There has to be. Not renew it, not break it. Something else. Something nobody's tried because nobody with the power to try it ever wanted to."

Ryn looked at her for a long moment. In the brazier light his face was all planes and shadows, the smuggler's mask half-off, and underneath it something she was beginning to suspect he'd been carrying longer than the prophecy pages.

"You know what you sound like?" he said.

"Tired."

"Hopeful. Which is worse, for a person in your position." He shifted, winced, settled. "Get some sleep, keeper. Nine days. We'll need all of them, and I need you thinking straight, which means sleeping, which means I'm confiscating the ledger look off your face."

"What ledger look?"

"The one you're doing right now. Like the world's an account that won't balance and it's personally insulting you." He closed his eyes. "For what it's worth — I don't think it's rigged. The choice. I think it's just badly written. And badly written things can be rewritten."

Ada lay back on the pallet and did not sleep. Above, faint through the stone, she could hear the council still arguing, voices rising and falling like wind through a pass, and beneath the argument, deeper, a sound she felt in her teeth more than heard: the mountain's own slow pulse, the routed fire, dimming.

Nine days.

* * *

She dreamed of the gate.

In the dream she stood before it as she had ten thousand nights before, ledger in hand, checking traffic, and the black stone was smooth and whole and utterly silent, and the silence was wrong, because the gate had always murmured to her, always, a low constant voice like a river under ice, and now it was quiet as a dead man's mouth.

Then, in the dream, the keystone split. Just one hairline crack, thin as a pen stroke. And through the crack came not fire but a voice, counting down in Old Emberish, the same voice she'd heard the night the gate first flared — except now she understood the words, the way you understand words in dreams, and what it was counting was not hours.

It was names.

Her name first. Then Ryn's. Then Halvard Brann's, though she didn't know how the gate knew him. Then a fourth name, deep and grinding, that made the dream-tremors start: *Corvane.* A name she didn't recognize, and somehow knew she would.

She woke with frost on the inside of the undercroft window — frost, in the Emberlands, creeping inward — and Ryn already awake, already dressed, standing at the sill with an expression she'd learned to dread.

"Riders coming up the pass," he said. "Human riders. Crown livery." He glanced back at her. "A lot of them."

Ada crossed to the window. Far below, winding up the smuggler roads she alone had mapped, torches moved in column — dozens, scores, a serpent of fire crawling toward the last free ground in the Emberlands. And at its head, a banner she knew too well, and a figure riding beneath it whose posture she recognized even at this distance: upright, patient, terrible.

Not Brann. Brann hunted books. This was something hunting territory.

"Nine days," Ada said quietly. "They gave us nine days."

"Yeah," Ryn said. "Looks like the Crown gave us two."
