## 20. Smuggler's Justice

The Salt Ladder was the last honest lie in Thornfell.

Ryn had named it himself, years ago, when he'd first climbed the switchback trail above Emberfall Village and seen how the terraced fields caught the last light like steps cut for a giant's boot. The village below it was dying by inches — he'd seen that too, two nights past, Ada's face in the firelight as she counted empty doorways — but the Ladder itself still worked. Still carried salt up and wool down and, if you knew which cairns to move, a hundred other things that never appeared on any ledger.

Which was why he'd come back. And why coming back was a mistake.

He smelled the crew before he saw them. Tallow smoke and wet wool and the particular sourness of men who'd been drinking since noon because there was nothing else worth doing. They had a fire going in the hollow beneath the third cairn, tucked out of the wind, and around it sat six faces he'd known for half his life.

Doss Marrow looked up first. He'd gone grey at the temples since Ryn last saw him, and there was a new scar through his eyebrow that Ryn didn't ask about, because Doss wouldn't answer, and because asking questions you already knew the answers to was a habit of keepers, not smugglers.

"Well," Doss said. "The ghost of the pass."

"The ghost brought salt." Ryn let the sack slide off his shoulder and hit the dirt between them. "And a question."

"Questions are free," said Petra Vane, who was mending a net even though the nearest water was three miles down. "Answers cost."

Ryn sat. Nobody made room for him, so he made room for himself, taking the flat stone nearest the fire. He kept his hands where they could see them. Old rule. You never sat among professionals with your hands hidden.

"I need the Ladder open," he said. "Three days, maybe four. A party moving south to north, quiet, no cargo worth mentioning. I need the cairns marked and the watch at the Widow's Tooth paid to look elsewhere."

"And who's the party?" That was young Ferro, who'd been a boy carrying bolts of cloth when Ryn left the crew, and was now a man with a knife he liked too much.

"People."

Ferro grinned. "People is cargo."

"Not these people."

Doss turned the spit. Fat dripped into the fire and flared. "You've been living behind the gate, Ryn. Playing house with the keeper. And now you come down here asking us to move *people* — not salt, not silk, people — through my pass, in the same season the Crown puts a price on heretical texts and doubles the garrison at Lowmark." He shook his head slowly. "You want to know what I heard? I heard an inquisitor came through the valley villages a week back. Polite man. Paid good coin. Asked about routes through Thornfell, and who ran them, and whether any of the old crews were still active."

The fire popped. Somewhere above them, a rock loosened and clicked down the scree, and every man at that fire went still for one heartbeat before pretending it hadn't happened.

"What did you tell him?" Ryn asked.

Doss met his eyes. "I told him the truth. The old crews are dead. Trade dried up when the dragons started guttering, because nobody buys silk when their children are hungry, and nobody runs contraband through a pass that might freeze solid by midwinter." He shrugged. "It was true when I said it."

"But?"

"But then he asked about you." Doss smiled, and it was the smile Ryn remembered from a dozen successful runs, the smile of a man about to say something clever. "Specifically. By name. Said you'd been seen with the keeper of the Sable Gate, and that she was wanted, and that anyone who delivered information leading to her capture would be compensated. Not arrested. *Compensated.* He used that word twice. Inquisitors don't use words twice unless they mean them."

Petra set down her net. "How much?" she asked, and Ryn could have kissed her for it, because at least she was honest enough to ask the real question out loud.

"A lot," Doss said. "Enough to feed Emberfall through winter. Enough to reopen the southern route. Enough that we stop being smugglers and start being merchants again, with papers and everything." He spread his hands. "You see the problem, Ryn. You're asking me to choose between an old friend and everyone whose belly I'm responsible for. And you already know what I'm going to pick, because you'd pick the same, because we learned it from the same hard years."

Ryn looked around the fire. Ferro wouldn't meet his eyes. Petra was watching him with something that might have been apology. The others studied the flames like scripture.

"You haven't picked yet," Ryn said quietly. "Or I'd already be dead."

"You haven't given me a reason yet." Doss leaned forward. "So give me one. Tell me what she's doing. Tell me why the gate matters. Because from down here, all I can see is: the dragons are dying, the fire's going out, the villages are starving, and the Crown wants the gate. Maybe the Crown's right. Maybe it's time somebody held the pass instead of a woman with a ledger and a family secret."

"She told you about that, did she?" Ryn said. "The Crown's version."

"She told us the Crown's version is a version."

"It is." Ryn pulled his knees up, wrapped his arms around them, and made a decision he'd been avoiding since the hollow shrine — the decision to spend truth like coin, knowing he couldn't take it back once it left his hand. "Here's mine. The covenant isn't a treaty. It's a leash. It runs on Okafor blood, renewed every generation, and it's been strangling the Emberlands for four hundred years while the humans on this side got fat on stolen fire. Her ancestor forged it. Betrayed a dragon to do it. And the reason your villages are starving isn't the dragons' dying — it's the gate itself, bleeding the Emberlands dry so the lowlands stay green."

Silence. Even the wind seemed to lean in.

"That's a story," Ferro said at last, but without conviction.

"It's the story the prophecy book tells, and the story the dragon tells, and the story carved into the confession stone at the Hollow Shrine, which any of you can walk up and read with your own eyes." Ryn stood. "Ada Okafor isn't running from the Crown because she's guilty. She's running because she's trying to fix something the Crown would rather own. And if Brann takes the gate for Corvane, the fire doesn't get shared — it gets *harvested*. Weaponized. You think Emberfall starves now? Wait until some general decides dragon-fire makes a better siege engine than a hearth."

Doss was quiet a long moment. Then: "You believe all this."

"With my whole chest."

"And her? The keeper. What does she believe?"

Ryn thought of Ada at the keystone, palm bleeding, writing terms in a language older than either realm. "She believes it enough to die for it. She just hasn't decided if she has to."

Something moved behind Doss's eyes — not softening exactly, but a recalculation, a merchant weighing goods. "Four hundred years," he said. "One keeper's bloodline. And now the book says tonight—" He stopped himself.

Ryn went cold. "What did you say?"

"Nothing."

"*Doss.* What did the inquisitor say about dates?"

But Doss had closed like a purse. He stood, brushed off his knees, and clapped Ryn on the shoulder with a grip that was almost friendly. "You should eat before you travel. Ferro, get our guest a bowl." Then, lower, only for Ryn: "I'm sorry, old friend. Truly. But everyone serves something. You serve her. I serve the crew. The inquisitor serves the Crown. The only sin is pretending otherwise."

* * *

The bowl was mutton and barley, and Ryn ate every bite, because a body needed fuel for what came next, whatever that was. He ate slowly and talked easily — about the road south, about a woman in Lowmark, about anything except the Ladder — and watched the light change and the stars come out over the Widow's Tooth, and counted.

Six men at the fire. Two had drifted toward the horses. One had taken the watch position at the cairn line, which happened to command the whole northern approach. Petra had vanished entirely, which meant she was either fetching something or warning someone, and Ryn had known Petra long enough to bet on both.

They were going to sell him. Not Ada — him, tonight, as a down payment, proof of good faith before they handed over the route. It was almost respectable. Almost fair. He'd have done the same at twenty-five, and hated himself less than Doss clearly hated himself now, because Doss couldn't look at him anymore, and that was its own kind of confession.

Ryn finished the bowl. Set it down. Stood, stretched, complained cheerfully about his back, and walked toward the horses.

Nobody stopped him. That was the tell. Six professionals and no one stopped him, which meant the stopping was scheduled for somewhere else — the narrows below the Ladder, probably, where the trail pinched between drop and cliff and one man with a crossbow owned the world. They'd let him ride into it. Cleaner that way. No blood at the fire, no bad feeling over the mutton.

So Ryn didn't ride into it.

He took the second horse — Doss's bay mare, forgive me, old friend, add it to the bill — and went down the goat track instead, the one that wasn't a trail so much as a suggestion the mountain occasionally indulged. It was ugly going in the dark. Twice the mare nearly went down, and twice Ryn whispered apologies to her and promises of apples he had no intention of being alive to deliver. Below him, faint on the wind, he heard voices at the narrows calling to each other, puzzled, then angry.

By midnight he was in the valley, and by the gray hour before dawn he was climbing again, on foot, toward the hollow shrine, because Ada would be waiting there and because he had maybe six hours before Doss's conscience finished losing to Doss's arithmetic.

He was wrong about the timeline. He was wrong by most of the night.

Because as he came over the shoulder of the ridge, dropping toward the shrine's broken arch, he saw the fires. Not campfires — signal fires, laid in a line along the eastern trail, burning green-white in a way that meant oil and pitch and military discipline. Soldiers. Dozens. Moving up the shrine road in column, torches bobbing like a procession of angry stars.

And at their head, riding a grey horse with the black-and-silver of the Inquisition at his shoulder, a tall figure Ryn recognized even at distance, because some men you only had to see once to remember forever.

Halvard Brann had bought more than a route.

Ryn dropped flat behind the ridge stones, heart hammering, mind doing the terrible fast arithmetic of a man counting exits and finding the sum short. The shrine was ahead. Ada was in the shrine. The army was between them, and behind him the mountain offered nothing but scree and dark and a fall that would kill him faster than any soldier.

Then he saw movement on the far side of the ruin — a slim figure slipping out through the shrine's rear wall, moving low and fast, heading upslope toward the high passes. Toward the Emberlands. Toward Vessarath.

She'd seen them. Of course she had; she'd probably heard them an hour before Ryn had, because the gate had taught her ears things no smuggler ever learned. She was running, and she was running alone, and the column was angling to cut her off at the switchbacks, and there was one thing on this mountain that moved faster than a column of soldiers.

Ryn stood up on the ridgeline, in full view of forty torches, filled his lungs, and did the stupidest, most necessary thing he had ever done.

"OI!" he bellowed, at the top of a voice built for shouting across market squares. "INQUISITOR! YOU WANT THE KEEPER? SHE'S NOT UP THERE — BUT I KNOW WHERE SHE'S GOING, AND MY PRICE JUST WENT UP!"

Every torch on the trail swung toward him. Somewhere below, a horn sounded, sharp and startled.

Everyone serves something, Doss had said. Fine. Ryn Vasq served a woman with a ledger and a dragon's debt and a date written in ash, and he intended to go on serving her past the point of sense, past the point of survival, all the way to whatever ending the margins of that cursed book had hidden from them.

He raised both hands, smiling his best market-square smile at the crossbows leveling below, and began, casually, to back toward the high passes — buying seconds with bravado, spending his life's luck one coin at a time.

Behind him, far up in the dark, something vast exhaled, and the clouds over the peaks glowed briefly gold.
