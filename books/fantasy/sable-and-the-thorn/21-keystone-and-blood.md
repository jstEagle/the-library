## 21. Keystone and Blood

The keystone had been above her head her whole life.

That was the thought that kept snagging as Ada stood in the ruined gatehouse with a lantern raised and her neck craned back, looking at the great black arch that had framed every dawn she'd ever known. She had walked beneath it ten thousand times. She had touched it only twice — once as a girl, standing on her father's shoulders to trace the sigils with cold fingers, and once the night the gate flared without cause and the voice began counting down in Old Emberish.

Now it was cracked. Not broken — not yet — but fissured down the left side like river ice in late spring, and through those fissures came a light that was not firelight and not lantern-light and not anything the human realm was supposed to contain. Dragon-fire. The real thing, raw and old, bleeding through the seam between worlds because someone's ancestor had decided, a hundred years ago, that a leash was safer than a handshake.

Ada set the lantern on the fallen lintel and pulled off her gloves.

"You're going to touch it again," Ryn said from the doorway. He wasn't asking. He'd stopped asking questions about her decisions somewhere around the burning pass; now he just narrated them like a man calling odds at a cockfight. "You're going to put your bare hands on a cracked piece of the oldest magic on the continent."

"I'm going to read it," Ada said. "There's a difference."

"There really isn't."

She ignored him. That was their arrangement now — he ignored the parts of her that frightened him, and she ignored the part of him that wanted to carry her over the mountains to some coast where prophecies couldn't find them. Both fictions held. Mostly.

The prophecy book lay open on the lintel beside the lantern, weighted down with a horseshoe Ryn had pulled off a dead soldier's horse. The scorched pages were soft as cloth now, handled so often they'd gone velvety at the edges. The marginalia — the third path, the covenant rewritten rather than renewed or broken — stared up at her in faded brown ink. *Not the blood-price paid again. Not the chain cut clean. The terms themselves remade.*

Remade by whom? That was the question the margin didn't answer. Remade with what?

She climbed onto the lintel, boots finding the old handholds worn into the stone by generations of keepers who had maintained this thing without ever once being told what it actually did. Her fingers found the sigils. They were warm. Not sun-warm. Body-warm, skin-warm, the warmth of something alive that had been holding very still for a very long time.

"Vessarath said the wound is shaped like you," she murmured, mostly to herself. The dragon's voice still lived behind her sternum sometimes, that vast slow resonance: *daughter of the forger.* "So let's see what else we're shaped like."

She pressed her palm flat against the keystone.

The world went quiet. Not silent — the wind still moved through the shattered pass, and Ryn was still saying something sharp from below — but *quiet*, the way the inside of a bell is quiet between strikes. And then, through her hand, through the bones of her wrist and arm and the cage of her ribs, she felt the covenant itself.

It was a rhythm. She had expected — she didn't know what. Machinery. Chains. Something architectural. Instead there was a pulse, deep and patient, like a heart beating under floorboards. And wrapped around the pulse, threaded through every beat of it, was something familiar.

Blood.

Her blood. Or near enough that the difference was a technicality. The covenant recognized her the way a lock recognizes its key — instantly, completely, without ceremony. It knew her name before she offered it. It knew her mother's name, and her grandmother's, and all the way back to Sera Okafor, who had stood on this exact stone a century ago with a knife and a lie.

Ada snatched her hand back and nearly fell off the lintel.

Ryn caught her ankle before she went down entirely, hauling her back to solid ground with more force than grace. "What did I say," he said. Not angry. Worse than angry — resigned. "I said don't touch it."

"It spoke to me." Her own voice sounded far away. "Not in words. In — it knows me, Ryn. It's been waiting for me."

"Everything in this gods-forsaken pass has been waiting for you since the day you were born. I could have told you that."

They made camp in what remained of the gatehouse's eastern room, where half a roof still stood and the wind had to work harder to get at them. Ryn built a small fire out of broken door-frames and the ledger shelves she'd spent twenty years keeping in perfect order. Watching the shelf-wood burn felt like watching a funeral she hadn't been invited to plan.

Ada sat cross-legged with the prophecy book in her lap and did not open it. She didn't need to anymore. She needed to think, and thinking required her hands to be still, which was a discipline she'd never mastered.

"The marginalia says the covenant can be remade," she said. "Rewritten, not renewed. But everything written in that book is in code or cipher or Old Emberish, and the one thing it's never ambiguous about is cost. Every working has a price. So the question is what remaking costs."

"Food," Ryn suggested, turning a burning plank with a stick. "Sleep. Distance from this pass measured in hundreds of miles."

"The renewal costs blood." She said it flatly, because flat was the only way she could hold it. "A keeper's sacrifice, each generation. That's what my family was. Not wardens. Not diplomats. Livestock. We fed it on schedule and called it duty." She looked at her palms, at the small white scar on the right one where she'd cut herself on a smuggler's knife at nineteen and never told anyone. "And Vessarath's wound is shaped like the keystone. The gate doesn't just channel dragon-fire. It's driven *through* him. He's been impaled on my family's bargain for a hundred years, alive enough to feel it."

Ryn had gone still. He was good at stillness when he chose it — a smuggler's skill, the ability to become furniture when soldiers passed. But his eyes stayed bright and fixed on her, and she knew he was already ahead of her, already seeing where the road bent.

"So if renewing takes an Okafor death," he said slowly, "and rewriting is bigger than renewing—"

"Bigger working. Bigger price." She turned the thought over the way she'd turn a coin suspected of clipping, examining both faces. "But here's what the keystone showed me. Here's what I keep circling. The covenant runs on Okafor blood, yes — but it ran on Sera Okafor's *betrayal*. On a death taken, not given. Taken blood is theft. Stolen goods sour. That's why the whole system rots, why the fire gutters, why both realms are starving off the same poisoned well. The magic isn't evil. It's been running on bad faith for a century, and bad interest compounds."

"And?"

"And a freely given death is a different currency." Her voice didn't shake. She noted that with distant professional approval, the way she'd note that a ledger balanced. "A willing sacrifice. An Okafor who walks to the keystone with open eyes and pays the debt honestly. That wouldn't just renew the covenant. That would *cleanse* it. Wipe a century of compounding rot in a single honest payment. From there, rewriting the terms would be possible. Maybe even easy. No blood-price after. No leash. Free passage, mutual keeping — the third path, built on a foundation that can actually bear it."

The fire popped. Somewhere out in the dark, the mountain groaned as frost split rock, the Emberlands dying a little further north.

Ryn set down his stick. "Say it plainly."

"You know what I'm going to say."

"Say it anyway. I want to hear you choose the words."

Ada looked at him across the small fire — at the smuggler who had taken a crossbow bolt meant for her, who had carried prophecy pages across a pass for months for reasons even he hadn't fully understood until it was too late, who was looking at her now like a man watching a ledger burn and knowing exactly which page was next.

"A willing Okafor sacrifice reforges the covenant," she said. "I'm the last Okafor. The price is my death, freely given, on that stone. Tonight, or soon. Before the countdown reaches zero."

There. Named plainly. It hung in the air between them, and she felt something strange loosen in her chest — not relief exactly, but the particular steadiness of a decision finally spoken aloud. She'd been carrying it since the keystone touched her back. Naming it made it weigh less. Naming it also made it real, and those two things arrived together and she had to accept the bundle as priced.

"No," Ryn said.

"It's not a request."

"It's a terrible idea, and I'm vetoing it."

"You don't get a veto. You get a vote, and I'm afraid the council consists of me." She closed the book. "Ryn. Listen to the arithmetic. If I die willingly, the covenant cleanses. The Emberlands stop freezing. Vessarath stops bleeding. The border villages eat again. Brann gets his truth, Corvane loses his justification, and whatever comes next gets built on honest stone instead of a grave nobody marked. One death against two realms."

"One death," Ryn repeated softly. "Listen to yourself. You've been reading ledgers so long you've started keeping them for the universe."

"Somebody has to. Nobody else reads the fine print."

He stood. He crossed the space between them in three strides and crouched so they were level, eye to eye, close enough that she could see the fire reflected twice in his eyes. "Then hear my fine print," he said. "One: you don't know it works. You felt a heartbeat in a rock and built a theology on it. Two: the marginalia offers a third path that might not need your death at all, and you haven't finished reading it because you've already picked your ending and reading might change your mind. Three—" his voice caught, just slightly, and he hated it, she could see him hating it, "—three, I did not take a bolt through the shoulder so you could donate yourself to a rock."

"That bolt was your choice. This one is mine. There's a difference, and it's the whole point."

"Don't quote my own argument back at me."

"Then stop making arguments that are true."

For a long moment neither of them moved. The wind found a gap in the roof and moaned through it, low and almost melodic, like the counting voice in Old Emberish that lived at the edge of her sleep. *Nine days,* it had said, weeks ago. The count was shorter now. Some nights she could feel it ticking behind her teeth.

Ryn sat back on his heels. When he spoke again, his voice had changed — quieter, stripped of bluster, which from Ryn Vasq was more alarming than shouting.

"When I was sixteen," he said, "my sister died of lung-rot in Emberfall village. Bad coal, bad winter, no dragon-fire to heat the houses cheap because your beautiful covenant had already started strangling the supply. My mother sold everything to buy a healer from the lowlands. Everything. And the healer came, and looked at Nessa, and said the rot was too far gone, and took the fee anyway because my mother signed a contract she couldn't read." He poked the fire, hard. "I've spent my whole life since learning to read contracts for people who can't. And I'm telling you, Ada Okafor, as a professional: whatever bargain you just described, you haven't read the whole document. You've read the price column. You haven't read what happens *after* payment. Who holds the new covenant? Who enforces it? What does 'witness bound to both realms' mean, in the fine print, in Old Emberish, in the clauses nobody translates until it's too late?"

Ada opened her mouth. Closed it.

Because he was right, and she hated it. She had read the cost. She had not read the consequences. A keeper to the bone, she had audited her own death and skipped forward past the signature line.

"I'll read it," she said finally. "All of it. Every clause. Before anyone cuts anything."

"Good."

"But Ryn—" she reached out and took his wrist, the unbandaged one, feeling his pulse jump under her fingers, stubborn and alive, "—if the document does say what I think it says. If there's no other way. You have to let me choose this. Promise me. Swear it on your sister. Because if you try to drag me away, I'll go, and I'll hate you, and the Emberlands will freeze with my consent withheld, and we will both live long miserable lives knowing exactly whose fault that was."

His jaw worked. The firelight made shadows move across his face like weather.

"You're asking me to promise to let you die," he said.

"I'm asking you to respect a contract freely entered. Isn't that the smuggler's creed?"

"The smuggler's creed is that all contracts are negotiable."

"Not this one."

He stared at her for a long time. Outside, the wind rose, and beneath it — faint, unmistakable, threading up through the cracked keystone and the broken pass and the frozen dark — came a sound like a great bell struck once, far away, and the number *four* spoken in a language older than either of their names.

Ryn flinched. Ada didn't. She was beginning to find the counting almost companionable. That, she thought, was probably the most frightening thing that had happened all week.

"Four days," she said quietly.

"Three and a half. The count slips at night." He rubbed his face with both hands. Then he dropped them and looked at her, and whatever war had been raging behind his eyes declared a truce. "Fine. Read your document. All of it, tonight, while I watch the road. And Ada — if there's any clause in there, any line, any smudged marginal note that gives us another way—"

"There won't be."

"—then you take it. Sworn on Nessa. Deal?"

She hesitated one breath too long, and he saw it, and his face did something complicated and wounded.

"Deal," she said, because it was mostly true, and because lies told kindly were still lies, and she had sworn off those along with everything else her family had built.

Ryn nodded once, took his crossbow, and went to the doorway where the dark and the road waited.

Ada opened the prophecy book on her knees and began, clause by clause, to read the fine print of her own death.

By the third hour she had found what she was looking for. By the fourth, she wished with everything in her that she hadn't — because buried in the closing lines of the marginalia, in a hand smaller and shakier than the rest, was a condition she had never seen named anywhere in the prophecy at all:

*The sacrifice must be willing — and must be witnessed by both realms, or the payment bounces like a forged coin.*

Both realms. Dragons and humans, together, watching her die.

And the last living dragon was three days' crawl away through a freezing pass, with an army marching on Thornfell, and four days left on the clock.
