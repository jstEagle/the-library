## 22. Refusal

Ryn Vasq had spent his whole life learning what things cost. A bolt of Sable silk cost three days of hard riding and a bribe at the tollhouse. A man's silence cost a bottle of plum brandy, usually two if the man was honest. His own life, as far as he could reckon it, cost whatever someone was willing to pay for it — and for years he'd assumed that price would be paid in coin by some Emberlands faction with more gold than patience.

He'd been wrong about that too. It turned out his life's price was Ada Okafor's death, and he wasn't willing to pay it.

She sat across the fire from him in the hollow beneath the shrine ruins, the keystone rubbing she'd made spread out on a flat stone between them like a map to her own grave. Charcoal lines. Old Emberish glyphs. And in the corner, where she'd worked through the night translating, a single sentence in her own hand:

*A willing Okafor, freely given, renews the covenant for another hundred years.*

"You've been staring at that since midnight," she said, not looking up.

"Someone has to stare at it. You keep looking away."

"I'm not looking away." She looked up then, and the firelight did something cruel to her face — hollowed her cheeks, deepened the shadows under her eyes until she looked like a woman carved from the same black stone as the gate she'd guarded. "I'm reading it. There's a difference."

"There is," Ryn said. "One of them means you're planning to die."

The wind came down off the peaks carrying frost with it. Three nights now the cold had been creeping south, past the pass, into valleys that hadn't seen ice in living memory. He'd watched frost kill a peach orchard outside Emberfall Village in an hour — watched blossoms turn to glass and drop like shattered lanterns. That was the covenant failing. That was Vessarath's wound weeping dragon-fire into nothing.

And Ada thought the answer was to lie down on an altar.

* * *

He'd known her nine years. Nine years of ledgers and tolls and the particular way she said *no* — flat, final, a door closing. Nine years of smuggling under her nose while pretending he wasn't, and her letting him pretend because the goods he moved were mostly medicine and mostly for people who couldn't afford the Crown's prices. Nine years of tea in the gatehouse at odd hours, of arguments about tariffs that were never really about tariffs, of a friendship so carefully unnamed that neither of them had ever dared say its actual name aloud.

He loved her. He'd never once said it, because saying it would have required admitting there was something to lose, and a smuggler who admits what he can lose stops being any good at his trade.

But tonight the arithmetic had changed. Tonight she'd laid her death on the stone between them like a merchant laying out wares, and every hour he didn't speak was an hour closer to her deciding this was settled business.

"Say it," she said quietly. She always knew. Nine years and she still always knew. "Whatever you're chewing on, Ryn. Say it."

"You die," he said. "That's the plan. You cut your throat or your palm or whatever the keystone wants, and the covenant runs another hundred years, and the dragons stay warm, and Thornfell stays green, and everyone lives happily ever after except you."

"That's roughly the shape of it."

"And you've decided this without asking anyone."

Her jaw tightened. "I asked the prophecy. I asked the keystone. I asked my own blood, which apparently signed us all into servitude before my grandmother's grandmother was born. Who else is there to ask?"

"Me." The word came out harder than he meant it to. He let it sit there, hard, because softness would give her somewhere to hide. "You could ask me, Ada. You could ask the one person who's spent nine years watching you carry this gate like it was your spine. You could ask me whether I want to spend the rest of my life walking past a gatehouse knowing you're ash inside the walls."

The fire popped. Somewhere above them, in the ruined shrine, wind moved through the carved names of the first signatories — the forgers, the liars, the Okafor who'd bound a dragon's heart to human hunger.

"It isn't about what you want," she said.

"No," he agreed. "It's about what you want, which is why you haven't asked me. Because you know what I'll say."

"What will you say?"

Ryn stood. His knee ached where the crossbow bolt had gone through it ten days ago; Brann's soldiers' bolt, taken on the shrine trail so she could run. He'd have done it again tonight. He'd do it again every night for the rest of his life and consider it a fair wage.

"I'll say no," he told her. "I refuse. Flatly, finally, the way you say no to smugglers. I refuse to help you die, I refuse to carry you to that gate, and if you start walking toward Thornfell alone I will follow you and make a nuisance of myself until you either give up or have me dragged off the mountain."

"You can't stop me."

"I stopped better men than you from worse ideas than this. Ask anyone in the Emberlands faction — they hired me twice and regretted it both times."

Something flickered across her face at that — the old wound, the confession he'd made bleeding in the snow: *they paid me to break your gate, Ada, and I chose you over coin.* She hadn't forgiven him yet. He suspected she'd filed it somewhere behind her ribs next to everything else she refused to feel.

"The Emberlands freeze without the renewal," she said. Her voice had gone lecture-flat, keeper-voice, the voice she used when the truth hurt and she wanted it to hurt cleanly. "Vessarath dies. The dragon-fire goes out. Emberfall starves — you saw them, Ryn, you saw the children. And when the fire dies entirely, the wild magic in those mountains doesn't vanish politely. It comes south. Through the pass. Into villages that have no idea what's coming."

"I know."

"Then how—"

"Break it instead."

The words were out before he'd fully decided to say them, and once they were out they filled the hollow like smoke. Ada went very still. Even the wind seemed to pause.

"Break it," she repeated.

"Break the gate. End the covenant now, tonight, on our terms — not Corvane's, not the Crown's, not the prophecy's. You said it yourself: the covenant is a leash. Leashes can be cut."

"And everything you just listed happens anyway." She rose to face him, and for a moment they were both standing, squared off across the little fire like the last two signatories of something. "The Emberlands freeze. Vessarath dies. The wild magic comes south. Nothing changes except I'm alive to watch it."

"Everything changes," Ryn said. "Because right now the only ending on offer is *you die and maybe it works.* Maybe. Based on a book a dying peddler sold you and a rubbing you took off a rock. You want to bet your blood on century-old ink? Fine. But I've read that book too, Ada, and here's what I noticed: the prophecy doesn't say the sacrifice saves anyone. It says the sacrifice *renews* the covenant. Renewal. Another hundred years of the same leash, the same lies, the same Okafor bleeding out so both realms can pretend nothing was ever stolen. That's not salvation. That's a subscription."

"So breaking it is better? Breaking it damns the Emberlands *now*. Not in a hundred years. Now. Tonight. While we stand here arguing."

"Yes." He made himself hold her eyes. Made himself not flinch from what lived in them. "Yes, it does. And I hate that, and I will carry that, and I'll spend the rest of my life hauling grain north to Emberfall if that's what it takes. But hear me, Ada. Hear me the way you hear smugglers lie — listen for the true thing underneath."

"Which is?"

"I won't trade you for mountains." His voice cracked on it, and he let it crack, because hiding it would be one more lie and he was done lying to her. "I won't trade you for dragons. I won't trade you for prophecy or covenant or the green fields of Thornfell or the frozen children of Emberfall. Everyone else in this story gets to spend you — the Crown, the council, the dead ancestor who forged this thing, even Vessarath with his sad ancient eyes. I'm the only one whose job is to not spend you. So I won't."

For a long moment she said nothing. The fire burned low. Frost crept silver along the edges of the keystone rubbing, and Ryn thought absurdly that the cold was trying to read it too.

"You love me," she said at last. Not a question. Not tender, either — furious, almost, like he'd committed a crime.

"Took you nine years to notice. Very observant, Keeper."

"Don't." She pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes. "Don't do this now. Don't put that weight on me the same night you're asking me to let the world burn."

"I'm not asking you to let the world burn. I'm asking you to stop being the world's kindling." He crossed to her, slow, the way you approach a wounded animal or a lit fuse. "Ada. Look at me. Chapter twelve of your cursed book — the second prophecy, the two endings. Reforge or break. One saves the dragons, one saves the humans, not both. You've been choking on that choice for days. But you told me yourself there was marginalia. Notes in the margins. Someone wrote in that book besides the prophet."

"A hint. A scrap. 'The third hand writes what the two hands refuse.' It might mean nothing. It might be a recipe for soup."

"Then we find out. We go back through the book, page by page, margin by margin, and we find the third path before anyone bleeds. That's the plan. Not your altar. Not my demolition. Something neither of us has thought of yet, because we've both been too busy being noble."

She lowered her hands. Her eyes were wet and furious and so tired that it frightened him more than any army.

"And if there is no third path?" she whispered. "If the margins are empty and the choice stands exactly as written — dragons or humans, my blood or their frost? What then, Ryn?"

He should have lied. He was good at lying; it was his trade. Instead he reached out and took her hand — the right one, the one she'd already cut once at the hollow shrine, the one that carried the Okafor blood-price in its palm — and held it the way you hold something you're refusing to surrender.

"Then we keep looking," he said. "Until the frost takes us both. I'd rather freeze beside you than warm my hands at your funeral. That's my final offer, Keeper. Take it or arrest me."

A sound escaped her that was half laugh, half sob, and terrible, and the most honest thing he'd heard from her in nine years.

"You're impossible," she said.

"Noted. Frequently."

"You'd damn an entire realm. For me."

"I'd damn two," he said, "if it came to that. Fortunately for everyone, I'm also a coward, so let's find the third path first."

* * *

They worked through what remained of the night, shoulder to shoulder over the prophecy book, her fingers tracing glyphs, his holding the fire-lamp steady. Twice she found a marginal mark and her breath caught; twice it resolved into nothing — a smudge, a stain, a bookseller's tally.

The third time, near dawn, it didn't resolve into nothing.

"There," she breathed. "There — look. Under the second prophecy. Someone scraped the vellum and rewrote over it, but the ghost of the old ink is still—" Her finger trembled against the page. "It's a phrase. Old Emberish. *What was forged in betrayal cannot be renewed by blood, but only unmade by truth.*"

Ryn read it twice. Then a third time, because the first two readings felt too much like hope, and hope was a smuggler's cargo that spoiled fast.

"Truth," he said slowly. "Not blood."

"Not blood." She looked up at him, and dawn was coming through the shrine's broken roof, grey-gold, catching the frost on the stones until the whole ruin glittered like something newly made. "Ryn — the confession. My ancestor's confession, carved in the shrine. The truth of what was done. If the covenant was built on a lie, maybe it doesn't need a death to sustain it. Maybe it needs the lie to end."

"Then nobody has to die."

"Nobody has to die," she echoed — and then her face changed, the way weather changes on the peaks, and Ryn knew before she spoke that the world had already found the catch. It always did.

"But someone has to tell it," she said. "Publicly. Before both realms. The whole confession, the whole crime, spoken aloud where the Crown and the Emberfolk and the dragons themselves can hear it — and Halvard Brann's army is marching on Thornfell right now, and General Corvane is marching behind him, and the gate has maybe days left in it."

Ryn looked east, toward the pass, toward the thin line of woodsmoke rising from the low country where twenty thousand soldiers were walking toward the only door between two worlds.

"Well," he said, pulling his coat on and reaching for his boots. "Good thing you know a smuggler. We're going to need roads nobody's watching — and someone mad enough to walk a confession into the middle of an army."

Ada closed the book. When she looked at him again, the keeper was gone from her face, and something new was there instead — something that hadn't existed in a hundred years of Okafor keepers.

"Then we'd better move," she said. "Before the truth arrives too late to matter."
