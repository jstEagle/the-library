## 23. The Third Path

Ada did not sleep.

She sat in the hollow shrine with the prophecy book open on her knees, the fire Ryn had built burned down to a red whisper, and she read the thing through for the fourth time since they'd fled Thornfell. The pages were soft as cloth now where her thumbs had worried them. Outside, frost ticked against the shrine stones. Somewhere below in the dark, a wolf sang and was answered by another that sounded wrong — too many voices in one throat, or none at all. The Emberlands were bleeding cold into the world, and every hour the line between the realms grew thinner.

She had read the two endings so often she could recite them like prayers.

*The gate renewed by willing blood shall hold the line between.* That was the first. Reforge the covenant. A keeper's death, freely given, poured into the keystone like wine into a cup. The dragons kept their fire; the humans kept their wall; everything stayed exactly as it had been for three hundred years, except that Ada Okafor would not live to see morning after the rite. She had made her peace with it on the road from Emberfall Village. She had rehearsed it the way you rehearse a confession: *I will kneel at the keystone. I will open my throat or my wrist. It will be quick, and it will be mine.*

*The gate broken shall loose the fire to fade, and the cold shall have the high places.* That was the second. Break the covenant entirely. No more leash, no more blood-price — and no more dragon-fire either, because the binding went both ways. Vessarath would gutter out over some number of winters. The green valleys of the Emberlands would go grey. The border villages would starve slowly instead of quickly. And Ada would live, which felt less like mercy and more like a bill come due.

A rigged choice. She'd said as much to Ryn, and he'd shrugged his smuggler's shrug and said, * rigged games are the only kind there are. You just pick the table where you lose least.*

But she hadn't been able to stop reading.

Because there was something else in the book. There had been something else all along, and she had missed it, because she had been trained her whole life to read ledgers — columns, sums, totals — and this was not a ledger. This was a conversation. Someone had talked back to this book across centuries, in margins thin as spider silk, in ink gone brown, in a hand that changed and changed again as if generations of readers had passed it along like a relay baton.

Ada turned to the page near the end, the one headed *Of the Two Endings*, and held it close to the coals' light.

The main text was clear enough. Two endings, two prices. But beneath the second ending, crammed into the bottom margin in handwriting so small she'd needed to wet her fingertip and rub ash into the grooves of the letters to make them legible at all, someone had written:

*the book shows two because the asker asked twice. there is a third question. find who asked it and what they paid.*

Ada sat very still.

Then she flipped backward through the book, faster now, hunting. Marginalia everywhere — she'd catalogued it before, but only as noise, only as the scribblings of monks and madmen. Now she read it as a thread. Near the front, in a firm old script: *who bound the fire?* Answered, pages later, in a different hand: *the forger, with blood and lie.* Later still: *can a lie be unbound?* And beside that, pressed so hard the quill had torn the vellum: *not unbound. REWRITTEN.*

Rewritten.

Her heart was doing something stupid and loud in her chest. She forced herself to slow down, keeper's discipline, audit the column before you trust the total. She went page by page, tracing the marginal hands. There were at least four of them, maybe five, spanning what looked like centuries. And they were not random. They were a chain. Each reader had answered the last. Each reader had pushed the question one step further. And each reader, she noticed with a chill that had nothing to do with the frost, had signed their contribution with a small mark at the margin's edge —

— a thorn drawn around a circle.

The mark of the first keepers. Her mark. The mark stamped into the Sable Gate's lintel, worn nearly smooth by three hundred years of weather. The mark she pressed into wax on every ledger entry she had ever filed.

Every reader in the margins had been an Okafor. Or something like one.

"Oh," she said aloud, and her breath fogged the page. "Oh, you clever, cowardly bastards."

They had known. Some line of them — keepers, her own blood, generation after generation — had suspected the covenant was a forgery, had carried this book down the years like contraband, had asked their questions in margins too small and too coded for any inquisitor to hang them for. And then, apparently, they had stopped. The marginalia ran out about a hundred and forty years ago, mid-question, in a hand that shook:

*if rewritten, what is the price? I dare not —*

And nothing. The trail went cold. Whoever that keeper was, they had gotten to the edge of the answer and stepped back from it. Had closed the book. Had gone back to counting smugglers and pilgrims and keeping the quiet, and had died with the question unfinished in their throat.

Ada understood that keeper completely. She was that keeper, sitting in a freezing shrine with a knife's worth of hope in her hands.

* * *

Ryn came back an hour before dawn, snow in his black hair, a brace of coneys over his shoulder that he'd somehow snared in a landscape that was supposed to be dying. He dropped them by the fire and crouched, warming his hands, studying her face with the flat assessment he probably thought was subtle.

"You didn't sleep."

"I found something."

"Last time you said that, we ended up running from the Crown." He fed a stick to the coals. "So naturally I'm thrilled."

She turned the book toward him and put her finger under the torn-vellum word. "Not unbound. Rewritten."

He read it. She watched him read it — watched the smuggler's skepticism move across his face like weather, then stall on something underneath. "That's not in the text," he said. "The text gives two endings. I've carried this thing long enough to know the Crown wants the first and the Ember council wants the second, and both of them want it because both of them keep what they've got."

"The text gives two endings because the text was written by the forger's faction. By the people who built the leash and wanted everyone believing the choice was renew-or-ruin forever." Ada heard her own voice come out hard and certain and was surprised by it. "But the readers argued back. Keepers. My family. They left a trail, Ryn. A hundred and fifty years of it, hidden in plain sight, and it ends at a third option nobody ever put in the main text because nobody ever dared finish asking the question."

"What's the price?"

"That's the part that stops." She tapped the shaking hand's last line. *I dare not.* "Whoever wrote this got to the door and wouldn't open it. Either they died before they could, or—" she paused, choosing honesty over comfort, "—or they looked inside and liked the other doors better."

Ryn was quiet for a while. The fire popped. Outside, the wrong-sounding wolf sang again, closer, and he glanced at the shuttered window without moving his head. Frost had climbed the shrine walls from the outside in a hand's width overnight. The cold was coming faster now. They both knew what that meant without saying it.

"So it might be nothing," he said finally. "A dead woman's half-thought in a margin. We're betting Vessarath's life and every valley village on the Emberlands side on a scribble."

"We're betting it anyway. The other two endings are certain. One kills me and keeps the leash. One saves me and kills the dragons slow. This one might kill us all, or might save everyone, and it's the only door anybody in my bloodline ever flinched away from." She closed the book gently, the way you close a thing that has become precious. "People flinch from the truth, Ryn. They don't usually flinch from lies."

He looked at her a long moment. Something moved behind his eyes — the same thing that had moved when he'd taken the crossbow bolt meant for her, the thing he pretended wasn't there. "You sound like a zealot."

"I sound like a keeper who just found out her whole ledger was missing a column."

"And if the third path needs a price worse than your death?"

"Then I'll pay it knowing what I'm buying. That's the difference. That's the whole difference." She stood, joints cracking, and began rolling the book into its oilcloth. "Vessarath told me the covenant was forged with blood and a lie. Blood I understand. But nobody's ever named the lie to me plainly — not the dragon, not the confession in the shrine, not the book. If I'm going to rewrite anything, I need to know exactly what was written first."

Ryn rose too, slinging his pack. "The lair's two days hard walking in this cold. One and a half if the pass roads haven't iced shut."

"They'll be iced shut."

"Then we go the way I'd go if I were smuggling something the Crown wanted badly." His grin was thin and sharp. "Which, conveniently, we are."

Ada slung her own pack and paused at the shrine door, one hand on the carved stone — the first signatories' names worn shallow under her fingers, her ancestor's confession beneath them like a scar. Behind her, the little fire died. Ahead, the peaks glowed faintly wrong, ember-light and frost-light fighting along the ridgeline like two tides.

In the oilcloth against her ribs, the book felt heavier than paper had any right to feel. Somewhere in those margins, a keeper a hundred and forty years dead had stood exactly where she stood, at the edge of the third question, and had turned back.

Ada pushed the door open into the freezing dark.

She was not going to turn back. But as she stepped out onto the ice-glazed trail behind Ryn, one thought followed her down the mountain like a shadow with teeth: *every door her family had ever opened in that book had cost them something. And the one they refused to open might cost the most of all.*

Two days to the lair. Two days to learn what the third path demanded.

Behind them, far down the valley, she saw torchlight moving — a long, orderly snake of it, too disciplined for smugglers, too numerous for wolves.

Brann's soldiers had found the shrine trail after all.
