## 24. March of the Inquisition

Halvard Brann had marched behind many banners in his twenty years of service to the Crown, and he had learned to read an army the way other men read weather.

This one smelled wrong.

Not the men — soldiers smelled like soldiers everywhere, leather and woodsmoke and the sour sweetness of fear sweat that no drill ever fully burned out of a column on its third day of hard road. It was the *order* of it that sat wrong in his chest. The Inquisition's field companies were built for pursuit: light, fast, cruel at the edges. What moved behind him now up the frozen switchbacks of the Thornfell approach was something else entirely — siege train, sappers, wagons of chained stone and iron, a hundred mules loaded with nothing but rope and grapnel and the particular patience of an institution that intended to *stay*.

You did not bring siege engineers to arrest one keeper and burn one heretical book.

He had told himself, for two days, that he had misread the orders. That was becoming harder to do.

"Magister." Sergeant Ilse Vey fell into step beside him, breath steaming, her voice pitched under the crunch of four hundred boots. "The vanguard's found the shrine trail. Or what's left of it."

"Collapsed?"

"Collapsed deliberate. Whole span of it came down into the ravine. Fresh breaks." She glanced back at him, and in the grey morning light her face was carefully blank, which was how Ilse said things she wasn't supposed to say. "Somebody bought themselves a head start with a mountain."

Ada Okafor, then. Alive, moving, and heading somewhere fast enough to bury the road behind her. Halvard felt something that was not quite relief and refused to examine it too closely.

"And the shrine itself?"

"Gutted before the collapse. Someone took the confession stone out whole — pried it off its plinth with tools, not fire. Clean work." A pause. "The men are saying it's witchcraft."

"The men say everything is witchcraft. It saves them from thinking." He said it automatically, the old reflex, and heard how hollow it had gone. Because he had stood in that gutted shrine two nights ago with his lantern held over a wall of carved names, and he had read what was carved beneath them, and he had not slept since.

*We did not make a treaty. We made a leash, and we lied about the leash, and the lie has kept us fed for three hundred years.*

Signed, in a stonemason's careful hand, by Aldric Okafor. First Keeper of the Sable Gate. Founder of the covenant. The man whose portrait hung in every Crown hall from here to the capital, whose name schoolchildren inked onto festival banners alongside the words *PEACEMAKER*.

Halvard had spent his life hunting heresy, and he knew its shape intimately: the small cold thrill of a truth that unmade the world you were standing in. He had felt it now twice — once reading the confession, once reading the pages of the prophecy book Ada Okafor had carried out from under his nose while he stood in her gatehouse drinking her tea and telling himself he was being courteous.

He had let her go. Not lost her. Let her. There was a difference, and the difference was going to hang him.

"Magister?" Ilse again.

"Nothing. Have the sappers clear a path through the scree. We lose no more than half a day."

"And after the path?"

He looked up the valley. Thornfell's peaks stood against the sky like the teeth of something sleeping, and somewhere among them, high and black against the snowfield, he could just make out the scar of the Sable Gate — the pass that had been closed, guarded, *managed*, for as long as there had been a Crown to manage it.

"After the path," he said, "we march where we're ordered to march."

It was the most honest lie he'd told all week.

* * *

They made camp that night in the ruins of Emberfall village.

Halvard walked it alone, which drew looks he ignored. The place had been dying for years — everyone in the borderlands knew that much, the way everyone knew the weather — but knowing a thing and standing in it were different animals. Half the houses were roofless. The well had frozen black. In the shrine-house someone had stacked the village's dead for want of ground soft enough to dig, and laid them out with a care that hurt worse than neglect would have: each body wrapped, each face turned toward the peaks, toward the Emberlands, as if even in death they expected the dragons to come back and fix this.

Dragon-fire had warmed these valleys once. Everyone knew that too. The covenant's great gift, sung about at every founding-day feast — the flame that never failed, the hearth that never went cold, the pact between realms that kept the winters gentle on both sides of the mountains.

Except the flame had been failing for a generation, and the Crown's answer had been to stop singing about it.

A girl watched him from a doorway. Seven, maybe eight, wrapped in a coat that had belonged to someone bigger. She didn't run. Children in dying villages stopped running from uniforms eventually; it was one of the stages of the sickness.

"You're the god-catchers," she said.

"We're the King's inquisitors. We look for lies."

"There's a lie right there." She pointed past him, up-valley, toward the gate. "My gran says the gate's a cage. Says the fire used to come *through* it, free, before the keepers started charging tolls nobody wrote down." Her chin lifted, defiant, rehearsed — some adult's words in a child's mouth, but true for all that. "Is that a lie? Do you catch it?"

Halvard crouched to her level. His knees cracked; he was getting old for this work, whatever his superiors believed.

"What's your name?"

"Maret."

"Maret. If I told you I'd come to find out whether your gran is right — would you believe me?"

She studied him with the flat, total assessment only children and cats can manage. "No," she said. "But I'd watch."

"That," said Halvard Brann, rising, "is the correct answer, and I wish four hundred grown men behind me had your sense."

He walked back through the dead-stacked shrine-house and out into the cold, and the confession stone's words walked with him, and Maret's question walked with him, and by the time he reached his tent he had done the arithmetic he'd been refusing to do since the shrine.

An army does not march to arrest one woman. An army marches because the institution that sent it wants the thing she's guarding — or wants the thing she *knows* buried so deep that no Maret in any village will ever stand in a doorway and ask about it again.

He ducked into his tent, lit the lamp, and read the orders for the fourth time.

*By command of His Majesty's Inquisition, under seal of the Office of the Provost Marshal: Magister Halvard Brann is directed to proceed to the Sable Gate, secure the site and its keystone against all parties, take into custody the former Keeper Ada Okafor and any persons aiding her, and hold the site until relieved.*

*Until relieved.* By whom?

The seal at the bottom was the Provost Marshal's. But the countersignature — the authorization for the siege train, the sappers, the second company still a day behind — bore a mark Halvard had seen only twice in his career, and both times on documents that ended careers, or villages.

The mark of General Aurelian Corvane.

Halvard sat very still and listened to the wind worrying the tent ropes, and understood, finally and completely, that he was not the hammer of this expedition.

He was the handle.

* * *

Corvane's advance party caught them at dawn on the third day.

Halvard heard them before he saw them — not the drum-and-boot rhythm of regulars but the lighter, faster stride of picked riders, and then they were threading up the column on shaggy mountain-bred horses, six of them in slate-grey cloaks with no insignia at all, which was itself an insignia, and the oldest kind.

Their leader reined in beside Halvard without saluting. A lean woman, fifty-odd, frost in her cropped hair, eyes like coins left out in weather.

"Magister Brann."

"Captain."

"Not a captain." She smiled without warmth. "I'm Wren. I have the General's compliments and his instructions regarding the final approach to the gate."

"I have my instructions."

"Yes." She produced a sealed tube and held it out, unhurried. "These supersede them."

Around them, the column had slowed without any order being given, the way water slows around a stone. Four hundred men pretending not to listen. Halvard took the tube, broke the seal, and read.

It was short. That was the first bad sign. Institutions wrote long when they wanted deniability and short when they wanted obedience.

*Magister Brann will deliver the site intact. The keystone is not to be damaged, marked, or studied. The prisoner Okafor is to be taken alive if practicable. All texts recovered from the site or her person are to be surrendered unopened to the bearer. — A. Corvane, General, Commanding the Ember Marches.*

*Unopened.* His own orders had said *catalogued*. Cataloguing meant reading. Reading meant witnesses. Corvane wanted no witnesses between the gate and himself.

"The General," Halvard said carefully, "is not stationed within five hundred miles of this pass."

"The General goes where the Crown's interests go." Wren gathered her reins. "You've read the shrine stone, Magister. Don't bother denying it — my people read your report before you sent it. Interesting reading. A lifetime of burning heretics, and the first real heresy you find, you *file*." She leaned down slightly, voice dropping to something almost confidential. "The General found that very useful, actually. Proof the rot reaches deep. Proof the gate needs firm hands." She straightened. "We move ahead of your column at midday. You'll follow. Any deviation, any delay, any attempt to communicate with the prisoner or her associates —"

"I know how warrants work."

"I don't think you do anymore, Magister. I think you think you're still on the side of warrants." She turned her horse, then paused. "One more thing. The General asked me to tell you personally: he knows you let her go at the gatehouse. He considers it a *test you passed*. He says a man who can be moved by a woman's tears can be moved by a Crown's purpose, and the second is more reliable." The coin-eyed smile again. "Congratulations. You've been promoted into a leash."

She rode forward, and the six grey riders threaded away up the column, and Halvard stood in the trampled snow feeling the exact shape of the trap close around him with a click he could feel in his teeth.

Ilse appeared at his shoulder. "Orders, Magister?"

Behind her, the column waited. Ahead, the pass climbed toward the gate, toward Ada Okafor and Ryn Vasq and a dragon he had never seen and a truth carved in stone that the Crown had decided, sometime in the last month, was worth an army.

Every instinct he had honed in twenty years said: *turn aside, send word, find the Keeper, warn her.* Every oath he had sworn said: *obey.*

Both instincts, he realized, agreed on exactly one point. Whatever Corvane intended for that keystone, it was not the covenant reforged and it was not the covenant broken. It was the covenant *taken*. A leash, pulled out of the dragon's flesh and fastened to a new hand.

"We follow," Halvard said. "At marching pace. No faster."

Ilse frowned. "Wren's party rides ahead at midday. If we're slow —"

"If we're slow," Halvard said, "we arrive late to a crime instead of early to one, and late men see more than early ones." He met his sergeant's eyes. "And Ilse — from here on, the company keeps its own count of everything we see. Everything. In writing. Under seal."

Her frown deepened, then cleared into something else. Twenty years of service; she'd read the same orders he had. "A record, Magister?"

"A ledger," said Halvard Brann, who had spent his life hunting other people's lies and had finally, at fifty-one, begun keeping honest accounts of his own. "Every keeper keeps one. It seems it's catching."

* * *

They crossed the last ridge at dusk, and the whole valley opened below them, and the column stopped as one body, four hundred men forgetting discipline in a single held breath.

The Sable Gate stood black against a sky the color of a bruise — and it was *burning*. Not aflame; alight. Cold white fire ran along the arch of it in slow veins, pulsing like something breathing hard, and the snow around its base had melted back in a perfect ring, and above the pass the clouds turned in a slow spiral, as if the sky itself had begun to orbit the stone.

And beneath the fire, faint but unmistakable, carried down the frozen air to four hundred soldiers and one doubting magister:

A voice. Counting.

Halvard had studied Old Emberish for eleven years out of professional necessity, the way a locksmith studies locks. He knew the numbers the way he knew his own name.

*Four,* said the gate. *Three.*

Beside him, Ilse whispered, "What's it saying?"

Halvard didn't answer. He was looking past the gate, up the far slopes, where against the last red light of day something vast was moving — slowly, laboriously, leaving a furrow in the snowfield wide as a road.

Something with wings, dragging itself toward the pass.

*Two,* counted the Sable Gate, in the tongue of the realm the Crown had leashed for three hundred years. *Two. Two. Two.*

And down the trail behind them, hoofbeats — Wren's riders coming back fast, too fast, with word that could only mean the General himself was already on the road.

Halvard Brann stood in the falling dark between the army at his back and the burning gate ahead, and understood at last that there were no more sides to choose.

There was only what a man did in the space between the counting and the end of it.
