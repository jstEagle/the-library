## 25. The Burning Pass

The road had a name, and that was the worst of it.

Ada stood at the mouth of the Serpent's Stair with the wind pulling at her coat and the first grey light of dawn seeping through the pass below her, and she thought: *I gave this road its name.* Not officially — no clerk of the Crown ever recorded it — but she had been the one to say it first, years ago, to a young smuggler who'd asked whether the switchbacks above the Widow's Drop were passable in winter. *Only if you climb like a serpent,* she'd told him, and he'd laughed, and by the time he reached Emberfall Village on the other side the name had traveled ahead of him, and it stuck.

Thirty-one switchbacks. She knew every one. She knew which stones wept water in spring and which held frost until midsummer. She knew the exact width of each ledge — four feet here, barely two where the mountain pinched above the seventh turn — and she knew, better than anyone alive, how much weight each one could bear before it gave.

That was the knowledge she was about to use against them.

"They'll reach the third switchback by midmorning," Ryn said behind her. He was crouched over the map they'd scratched into the dirt, though neither of them needed it. His shoulder was still bound where the crossbow bolt had gone through, and he favored his left side when he moved, but his hands were steady as he weighted the corners with stones. "Corvane doesn't march. He *processes*. Banners out, drums, the whole theater of it. Which means he's slow, and he's loud, and he wants to be seen."

"Then let's give him something to see."

Ryn looked up at her. In the half-light his face was unreadable, the way it always was when he was deciding whether to say the thing he was actually thinking. "You're sure."

It wasn't really a question, and they both knew it, and he asked it anyway, because that was Ryn — he made you answer out loud so the choice belonged to you and not to circumstance.

"No," Ada said. "But I'm going to do it."

* * *

She had spent twenty years keeping these roads open.

That was the truth of her stewardship, the part no ledger recorded: not just the gate itself, not just the covenant traffic of pilgrims and sanctioned traders, but the whole living web of paths that threaded Thornfell like veins through a body. The smugglers' routes she had tolerated because tolerance was cheaper than war. The shepherd trails. The pilgrim stairs cut into the cliff face three centuries ago by hands that believed the dragons were gods. Every one of them maintained, in its way, under her eye. She'd paid for rope bridges out of her own purse. She'd bribed masons to shore up the Widow's Drop after the rockslide of her twelfth year as keeper. She'd sat up nights with maps and candle stubs, memorizing load-bearing stone the way other women memorized psalms.

Because that was what a keeper did. A keeper didn't just guard the gate. A keeper kept *everything* — the peace, the passage, the people who moved through the dark on both sides of the law.

And now she was going to bring down the finest road in the mountains.

"The charges go here," she said, kneeling at the third switchback, "and here." She pressed her palm flat against the rock face, feeling for the hollow note beneath. "This shelf is natural, mostly. But there's a seam running underneath — my grandfather's surveyors found it when they were considering widening the road. They never widened it. Too dangerous, they said." Her mouth twisted. "Too dangerous. As if the danger were the mountain's fault."

"You've done this before," Ryn said. It wasn't admiration in his voice. It was something quieter and sadder.

"I've *prevented* it before. Twenty years of preventing exactly this." She took out the fire-oil flasks they'd bought from a smuggler in Emberfall who hadn't asked questions, because smugglers never did, and set them in a row along the seam. "There are forty families who make their living off this road. Carriers, guides, the woman at the halfway shelter who sells stew and dry socks. When this comes down, the Serpent's Stair is closed for a generation. Maybe forever. Maybe someone finds another way around, but there isn't one — I would know. There has never been another way."

"Ada."

"And the Emberfolk on the far side lose their last reliable trade line into the human realm, which means the villages that are already starving starve worse, and the ones that aren't start to be." She screwed the cap off the first flask. Her hands were perfectly steady, which felt like a betrayal of everything she was feeling. "And Corvane's army — six hundred men, supply wagons, siege equipment for a gate that was never meant to be besieged — loses its only route through the pass and has to either turn back or spend a month cutting a new road while Vessarath's fire gutters out entirely."

"So we save the dragon and damn everyone who ever walked this road."

"We buy time," Ada said. "Time is the only coin we have left, and I am spending everything to get it."

Ryn was quiet for a long moment. Below them, far down the switchbacks, she could hear it now — faint on the wind, carried up through the cold morning air. Drums. The measured, patient beat of an army that believed nothing in the mountains could stop it.

"There's a version of you," Ryn said finally, "that would have died before touching this road."

"I know." She poured the first line of fire-oil into the seam. The stone drank it greedily, darkening. "She's still in here somewhere. I can hear her screaming. I've decided to let her scream and do it anyway."

* * *

They worked upward through the morning, switchback by switchback, and Ada catalogued what she was destroying the way a priest recites sins.

The third switchback: the widest ledge on the whole stair, broad enough for two loaded mules to pass. Old Marta's son had proposed marriage to his wife on that ledge, according to smuggler legend, because it was the only place on the road where you could stand side by side instead of single file.

The fifth: the overhang where travelers sheltered from storms, its ceiling black with decades of torch smoke, initials carved into the rock by a hundred years of hands. *K + W, 812.* Someone had loved someone up here, and the mountain had kept the record, and by noon the mountain would eat it.

The seventh: the pinch point, two feet wide, where Ada herself had once pulled a falling boy back from the drop by the collar of his coat. He'd been eleven. He'd cried and called her the angel of Thornfell, and his mother had pressed a copper into her hand that Ada had kept in her ledger drawer for fifteen years because refusing it would have shamed the woman.

She poured oil into every seam. She packed powder into every joint. And with each one she heard the drums a little louder, and she thought about Vessarath's wound shaped like the gate's keystone, and the frost creeping into green valleys, and the page in the prophecy book that showed the Emberlands burning and freezing at once, and she told herself: *this is arithmetic. This is arithmetic and I will not flinch.*

But arithmetic, she was learning, was just grief wearing a clean shirt.

* * *

At the top of the ninth switchback, they found people on the road.

A carrier train — six of them, mules laden with salt fish and iron nails, goods bound for Emberfall. They'd come up from the valley before dawn to beat the army's march, gambling that they could slip past the soldiers in the fog. Bad gamble. Now they were trapped between the drums below and the woman with fire-oil above, and their lead guide — a graybeard Ada had known since he was that same falling boy's age — stared at her across thirty feet of ledge with an expression she had never once seen him wear.

Fear. Of her.

"Keeper Okafor." He said her title like an accusation. Behind him, the younger carriers were already backing their mules toward the shelter of the overhang, reading the scene faster than he did — the flasks, the soaked seams, the woman standing over the road's throat with a lit lantern in her hand.

"Dann," Ada said. "Take your train back down. There's a side track at the second switchback, the old goat path — it's rough, but it reaches the valley without crossing what I'm about to do."

"You're going to blow the Stair." Not a question. The graybeard's voice cracked on the last word. "Twenty years you kept this road. Twenty years you fined us, taxed us, chased us off it when the Crown looked — but you *kept* it. Everyone knows that. Everyone knows the keeper keeps the roads." He spat, deliberately, onto the stone between them. "What are you now?"

Ada held his gaze. She owed him that much — owed all of them that much. Not excuses. Not the prophecy, not the dragon, not the army. Just the truth, plain.

"A traitor," she said. "To the Crown, to the covenant, and today, to this road. Take the goat path, Dann. Take your people and go, and if you live long enough, tell them why."

He stared at her a moment longer. Then he turned and shouted the order himself, voice raw, and the carriers moved — fast, practiced, mules protesting, boots finding the treacherous descent. Ada watched them go until the fog took them, and she noticed that her eyes were wet and did nothing about it, because there was no time and no one to see and it wouldn't change the charge she was about to light.

Ryn touched her elbow, gentle. "Last chance to be the keeper."

"The keeper's dead," Ada said. "She died somewhere between the shrine and the shrine trail. What's left is the woman who read the confession, and she knows what happens if that army reaches the gate with Corvane at its head." She lifted the lantern. "Stand clear."

* * *

The first charge went at the fifth switchback, and the mountain answered like a struck bell.

The sound rolled up the pass and came back off the far walls, doubled and redoubled, and then the ledge simply *left* — a hundred feet of ancient road folding inward into the seam Ada had fed, stone sliding into stone with a grinding roar that she felt in her teeth, in her chest, in the soles of her boots. Dust boiled up white and enormous. Somewhere inside it, she knew, the carved initials were falling into darkness. *K + W, 812.* The mountain kept its records no longer.

Below, the drums stopped.

For one full minute there was silence except for the settling of the mountain, and then the drums started again — faster now, no longer patient, and Ada allowed herself one breath of grim satisfaction. Corvane had understood. Whatever else the general was, he was not stupid, and stupid men did not wear a general's cloak.

"He'll split his force," Ryn said, squinting down through the thinning dust. "Send climbers up the goat paths to find another way. It'll cost him a day, maybe two."

"Two days is a lifetime." Ada was already moving up the stair, to the seventh switchback, to the next seam, because one collapse was an obstacle and three was a message. "Vessarath doesn't have two days. Have you seen the peaks this morning?"

Ryn hadn't, or wouldn't say. But Ada had, before dawn, from the high shoulder of the pass: the old fires along the Emberlands' ridgeline burning low and blue instead of gold, like coals dying in a grate, and above the southern valleys a haze that was not cloud but *frost-smoke*, the breath of a land forgetting warmth. The covenant was failing. The leash was slipping off a neck that had been collared so long the flesh had grown around it, and when it came off — suddenly, violently, unmanaged — both realms would bleed.

Unless she bought enough time to reach the keystone first. Unless the army arrived to find the gate already rewritten rather than ripe for theft.

The seventh switchback went with a sound like the world clearing its throat. Then the ninth. Each time, Ada stood and watched it fall, and each time she named aloud what she was burying — the wedding ledge, the storm shelter, the place where she had saved a boy — because she refused to let it be anonymous. If she was going to betray her stewardship, she would do it with her eyes open and a full accounting, the way she'd done everything else in her life. The ledger-keeper's final entry, written in falling stone.

By late afternoon the Serpent's Stair was a scar. Thirty-one switchbacks reduced to nineteen, the upper twelve severed from the world, the road that had carried a century of salt and nails and secrets and pilgrims now a tumble of scree and dust and silence. From the ridge above, it looked almost peaceful. Ruins often did.

Ryn stood beside her, arm wrapped around his bad shoulder, looking down at the wreckage of the only livelihood half the pass had ever known.

"Marta's going to kill you," he said. "The halfway shelter. No road, no customers."

"Marta will survive. She's meaner than the mountain." Ada's voice came out flat and strange. "It's the ones who aren't that keep me up at night. There's a family at the eleventh mile — the Verens. Three children. The father carries grain over the Stair twice a week. That's the whole of their living, Ryn. I just buried it under forty feet of rock, and I did it on purpose, and I'd do it again before sundown."

"That's not a confession," Ryn said quietly. "That's just what it costs."

"Yes." She turned away from the ruin. "Now you understand what the covenant was. Somebody, three hundred years ago, decided what everyone else would pay, and wrote it down, and called it peace. I'm doing exactly what my ancestor did. Choosing the price for people who never got a vote." She laughed, short and bitter, breath smoking in the cold. "The only difference is I'm honest about it. And I intend to write the terms differently, if I live long enough."

* * *

They camped that night on the high shoulder, above the smoke of the ruined road, and it was there that Ada saw the answer to her arithmetic coming down the far side of the pass.

Torches. Dozens of them, snaking along a route that should have been impassable — the northern goat tracks, the ones even smugglers avoided, the ones that clung to the cliff face above the freezing falls. An army should not have been able to move on those tracks. An army moving that fast, that silently, without drums, without banners, could only mean one thing.

Corvane had sent climbers ahead in the dark. Not to find another way — to flank her. While she sat watching her handiwork and counting her day gained, the general had been spending men's lives like fire-oil, feeding them onto cliffs that would kill one in three, to put soldiers above her position by morning.

Ryn saw it a heartbeat after she did. His hand found her wrist in the dark.

"How many?"

"Too many." Ada was already kicking dirt over their small fire, her mind racing through the map of her own mountains, hunting for a way out that didn't exist. "They'll reach the high shoulder before dawn. We can't outrun them downhill — not with your shoulder, not in the dark."

"Then we don't run." Ryn's grin flashed, sharp and reckless, the grin of a man who had spent his life turning losing positions into profitable ones. "We run *sideways*. There's a way off this shoulder nobody's used in twenty years. You told me about it once, drunk on festival wine. The keeper's private path."

Ada went cold. "That path hasn't been cleared since my predecessor's day. It may not exist anymore."

"Or," Ryn said, rising, pulling her up by the good hand, "it's the only road in Thornfell the army doesn't know, because the only person who knew it is the woman holding my hand. So, Keeper —" and there it was, the old title, but for the first time it sounded like a dare instead of a chain — "keep us."

Behind them, the torches multiplied along the cliff track. Ahead, the dark swallowed the mountain whole.

Ada ran.
