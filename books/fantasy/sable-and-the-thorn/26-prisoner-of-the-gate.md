## 26. Prisoner of the Gate

Halvard Brann had taken prisoners before. He knew the arithmetic of it — how a person shrank in chains, how fear did half the work of any rope, how by the third day of a cell most captives would trade anything, even truth, for warmth and bread.

Ada Okafor was not shrinking.

They brought her up the mountain road at dusk, wrists bound with iron-cored cord, flanked by six soldiers who had drawn lots for the privilege and now walked like men escorting a lit fuse. She stumbled once on the scree where the Burning Pass had come down, and Halvard watched her catch herself without help, jaw set, eyes forward, as if the collapse of an entire smuggler road were merely weather she disapproved of.

She had collapsed that road. He kept returning to it. A keeper whose whole life was stewardship had buried the only route through Thornfell rather than let his army use it — and she had done it knowing what it cost her, because he'd seen her face when the last of the rock settled. Not triumph. Grief. The grief of a woman burning her own house to slow an invader.

*That is not the face of a heretic,* he thought, and hated himself for thinking it, because he had signed the warrant himself.

The gatehouse rose against the last light, its black stone drinking the sun. Half of it was ruin now — Corvane's vanguard had breached the eastern wall two nights past, and the keystone above the great arch wore a crack like a lightning scar. Dragon-fire still seeped from that crack at intervals, a thin orange breath that made the soldiers flinch and mutter prayers to saints who had never stood this close to the Emberlands.

Halvard had ordered the camp pitched outside bowshot of the arch. His men thought it caution. It was not. It was the memory of what fire-drunk Corvane had looked like when they pulled him from the pass, skin glowing from within like a paper lantern, laughing at wounds that should have killed him. Corvane lived, sealed in a wagon of cold iron under guard of forty men, and the glow had not dimmed. Halvard had looked through the wagon's grate once. Once was enough.

"Put her in the old records room," he told the sergeant. "Not a cell."

The sergeant blinked. "Inquisitor?"

"You heard me. Chains off. Door barred from the outside, if you must, but no irons." He paused. "And bring her food from my own stores. Hot."

The sergeant's face performed a careful blankness, the kind soldiers perfected around officers who gave orders they didn't want examined. He bowed and went.

Halvard climbed the outer stair alone.

* * *

The records room had been Ada's study for twenty years, or so the burned ledger fragments suggested. Someone — Ryn Vasq's crew, probably, in the chaos of the breach — had ransacked it, but they had looted for coin and left the paper. Shelves of night-audit ledgers lined the walls, decades of them, each spine labeled in a small precise hand: *traffic, tolls, names.* Halvard had read three of them by candlelight over the past two nights, telling himself it was interrogation preparation.

It was not interrogation preparation. It was something closer to reading a stranger's diary and finding your own doubts written there in someone else's handwriting.

Every entry balanced. Every name logged — smuggler, pilgrim, deserter, widow carrying contraband salt — with notations not just of goods but of circumstance. *Marta Ell, flour, 3 sacks; husband dead at Redford; waived toll, winter remission granted.* *Vasq, R., silk, undeclared; warned, released; reason: feeds the Emberfall village children when the passes close.* Page after page after page of a woman holding a border together with arithmetic and mercy, in defiance of every regulation the Crown had ever issued about the Sable Gate.

Regulations Halvard had memorized at seventeen and never questioned until this month.

He heard the door unbar behind him and did not turn. Her footsteps crossed the room — steady, unhurried, cataloguing, he suspected, every change since she'd last stood here. The missing ledgers. The scorch mark on the ceiling where dragon-fire had licked through during the breach. Him.

"Inquisitor Brann." Her voice was rougher than he remembered from the inn, from the shrine trail, from all their cat-and-mouse across these mountains. Captivity had spent some of her voice. "You've redecorated."

"I've requisitioned," he said, and turned.

She stood by the table with her bound hands loose at her sides, and up close the cost showed: a cut across her brow crusted dark, a burn along one forearm where the pass had fought back, dirt ground into everything. But her eyes were the same. That was the trouble with her. The eyes never changed. They weighed him every time, and he always felt the weight land.

"Sit," he said. "Please."

"Chained women don't get *please* from the Inquisition."

"This one does." He drew out the chair opposite his own. "I'm going to have those cords cut. I'd take it as a courtesy if you didn't make me regret it."

Something flickered in her face — suspicion, mostly, and under it exhaustion, and under that, briefly, something that might have been curiosity. "You're not going to interrogate me?"

"I'm going to feed you first. Interrogation on an empty stomach produces lies. Both directions." He gestured, and the soldier at the door came in with a tray — bread still warm, stew, tea — and a knife. Halvard took the knife himself and sawed through the iron-cored cord, and felt her go very still as the blade passed her wrists, and understood exactly what he'd just done. You did not put a knife near a prisoner's skin unless you trusted her. Trust was a currency here, and he had just paid first.

She flexed her hands. Looked at the stew. Looked at him.

"Poisoned?" she asked, almost lightly.

"If I wanted you dead you'd have died on the road. There were four convenient moments. I counted them for you."

"That's either reassuring or the most sinister thing anyone's said to me." She sat. She ate like a soldier, fast and thorough, and Halvard sat across from her and drank his tea and let the silence do its work, because he had learned long ago that silence made honest people nervous and guilty people talkative, and he genuinely did not know which she was anymore.

When the bowl was empty she set down the spoon with deliberate care. "Say it, then. Whatever this is. You didn't drag me up a mountain for stew."

"No." He reached into his coat.

Her whole body changed — a coiling, a readiness, the keeper calculating distance to the door and odds against six soldiers. He moved slowly, deliberately, and laid the book on the table between them.

The prophecy book. Scorched cover, century-old ink, the page with tonight's date circled beside her name. The book he had hunted across three provinces. The book whose loss had been the stated purpose of his warrant, his commission, his entire march into Thornfell.

He watched her stare at it. Watched her not touch it. Watched her look up at him with the first genuine uncertainty he'd ever seen crack that composed face.

"You had it searched out of my pack at the shrine," she said. "I watched your man take it."

"I did." He turned the book and pushed it, gently, across the wood. Toward her. "Now I'm giving it back."

* * *

For a long moment neither of them moved. Outside, faint through the shuttered window, the keystone breathed its thin orange light against the clouds, and somewhere below a soldier began the evening psalm and stopped halfway through, as soldiers had been doing all week, as if the words had gotten too small for what they'd seen.

"Why," Ada said finally. Not *what trick is this.* Why. As if the question itself were a weapon she was deciding whether to draw.

"Because I've spent eleven days reading things I wasn't supposed to find." Halvard leaned back. "Your ledgers. The shrine confession — yes, I found the Hollow Shrine, and yes, I read what your ancestor carved into the stone, and no, I haven't reported it. And then there's General Corvane."

"What about him?"

"He rode into this pass to seize a gate he believes is a weapon. He has orders — real ones, sealed, from the Crown — authorizing him to 'stabilize' the covenant by placing it under direct military control. Do you understand what stabilize means, Keeper? It means leashing the leash. It means whatever your ancestor built to bind the dragons, the Crown intends to build again, tighter, pointed outward." He heard his own voice flatten, and let it. "Corvane drank raw dragon-fire to prove he could master it. He is currently glowing in an iron wagon. My general, whom I have served for nine years, is dying of power and calling it victory, and the official dispatch I am required to write will call it a miracle."

Ada hadn't touched the book. Her bound-cut hands rested flat on the table on either side of it, framing it, not claiming it. "So you've lost your faith."

"My faith is fine. It's my institution that's failing inspection." He almost smiled. It surprised him that he almost could. "I joined the Inquisition because I believed the Crown kept true records. That someone, somewhere, was writing down what actually happened so that power couldn't rewrite it later. That was the whole sacred trust of my office — not the burning, not the warrants. The record." He tapped the table. "And then I find that the founding document of the oldest covenant in the realm is a lie built on a betrayal, and the Crown's response is not correction but confiscation. They don't want the truth managed, Keeper. They want it owned."

"And you want it free."

"I want it *proven.*" He said it plainly, because there was no point dressing it. "There's the difference between us, maybe. I can't break covenants or rewrite prophecies or walk into dreams with dragons. I can establish a record. Testimony. Evidence entered before witnesses, such that no future Crown can quietly bury it. That's the only power I have ever actually held, and I have held it wrong for nine years, and I am attempting to hold it right for approximately the remainder of my life, which may be short."

She studied him for a long time. The fire-breath from the keystone pulsed once, twice, lighting the seam of the shutters orange, and in that light her face looked younger and more tired and more decided, all at once.

"You know what they'll call you," she said. "If they learn you gave me this back. Traitor. Worse. Corvane burns people for less than a returned book."

"Yes."

"And you're doing it anyway."

"I'm doing it anyway." He folded his hands. "Here is what I propose, and you may refuse, and if you refuse I will return you to custody and say nothing of this conversation, though I suspect we're both past the point where that holds. Tomorrow at midday, Corvane convenes a tribunal at the gate — his tribunal, his stage, both armies watching. He intends to try you for heresy and gate-treason before executing the sentence publicly, to demonstrate that the covenant now belongs to the Crown. He wants a spectacle."

"He'll get one."

"He'll get a trial," Halvard said. "Which is a different thing, if you use it correctly. A trial requires testimony. Sworn testimony, entered into the record, before officers of both realms — and Corvane, arrogant as he is, has invited Emberlands observers to legitimize his authority over the gate. He thinks they'll be cowed. He's wrong. They'll be listening." He pushed the book the last inch toward her. "Read your ancestor's confession aloud. Read the prophecy. Name the blood-price. Let the record show what the covenant truly is, in front of every rank and realm that matters. Make the truth so public, so witnessed, so *entered*, that no one can ever again claim the Crown's version was the only one."

Ada's breath had gone shallow. He could see her running it — the danger, the trap-possibility, the shape of the stage he was offering her — and he waited, because there was nothing left to add. He had given her the book and the plan and the risk, all three, and the fourth thing, the only thing that mattered, she had to bring herself.

"And you?" she asked quietly. "When I stand up in front of Corvane's tribunal and call his general's covenant a forged leash — what do you do?"

"I stand up," Halvard said, "and I certify the testimony. Under seal of my office. Which makes it law-record, untouchable, permanent — and makes me, incidentally, a traitor alongside you." He let that sit. "I've been drafting my own confession for three nights, Keeper. It seemed dishonest to ask you to burn your life down for the truth unless I was willing to hand over mine."

The silence stretched. Then Ada Okafor did something he had not seen in all their months of pursuit.

She laughed — short, cracked, real — and picked up the book at last, and held it against her chest like something returned from the dead.

"The smuggler told me once," she said, "that everyone serves something. He meant it as a warning." She looked at him across the ruined study, across the ledgers of a life spent balancing other people's survival, and her eyes — those weighing, unchanging eyes — rendered their verdict. "I think you serve the record, Inquisitor. Gods help us both, I think that might be enough."

"It will have to be," Halvard said. "Because tomorrow—"

The keystone screamed.

Not breathed — screamed, a sound like tearing metal and a voice underneath it, and both of them were on their feet before thought caught up, and through the window the crack above the arch blazed wide as a wound, and the counting they had all heard in nightmares rang down the pass in Old Emberish, clear and close:

*Three.*

Below, the camp dissolved into shouting. Somewhere in the iron wagon, muffled through layers of cold steel, General Corvane began to laugh.

Halvard met Ada's eyes across the table, and saw his own dread reflected there, and said what they both already knew.

"We're out of tomorrows. It starts tonight."
