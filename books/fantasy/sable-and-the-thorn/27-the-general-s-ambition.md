## 27. The General's Ambition

General Corvane had never once in his life been early to anything he did not intend to own.

He arrived at the Sable Gate two days after Halvard Brann's messenger reached the capital, which meant the message had taken four days and his decision had taken none. He came with three hundred soldiers of the Ninth Column, a siege train disguised as supply wagons, and a portable writing desk that had crossed more battlefields than most of his officers.

The gatehouse looked smaller than the maps promised. That was always the way with things people had learned to stop looking at.

"Brann," Corvane said, before the man had finished saluting. "You found a heretical text at the site of a national covenant, you let its keeper escape into the mountains, and then you sent me a letter asking for guidance." He dismounted himself, waving off the groom. "Which word in that sentence did you think would not reach me?"

"Inquisitor Brann's report was thorough," said the woman beside him — Inquisitor-Superior Vell, who had come down from the capital on the same road, uninvited by everyone including Corvane. "Thorough enough that I have questions about why it took four days to arrive."

"The pass is slow in thaw," Brann said. His voice was level. He had the particular stillness of a man standing in a room where the floor has already gone out from under him once and he is checking whether it will do it again.

Corvane ignored both of them and walked toward the gate itself.

It was, he admitted privately, an ugly thing. Black stone older than any masonry he'd commissioned, veined with something that caught light wrong — not reflecting it so much as holding it, the way coal holds fire. The keystone sat above the arch like a closed eye. The air near it tasted faintly of struck flint.

He had read every surviving document on the covenant. He had read them twice, once as a colonel ordered to summarize them and once as a general who no longer took anyone's summaries. And somewhere between those readings, in the margins of a treaty copy nobody had opened in sixty years, he had noticed what everyone else had spent a century declining to notice: the covenant described dragon-fire as *bound*. Not shared. Not gifted. Bound — the way one binds a beast, or a prisoner, or a weapon.

A bound thing can be unbound. A bound thing can be *taken*.

"General." Brann had followed him. Of course he had. The man had a talent for being exactly where he was least wanted. "I'd ask what the Crown intends here."

"You'd ask," Corvane agreed pleasantly. "That's your function."

"The keeper, Ada Okafor, believes the covenant is failing. The prophecy text supports her. If she's right—"

"If she's right, then within a season the Emberlands freeze and the border villages starve, and the Crown inherits a catastrophe instead of an asset." Corvane ran a gloved hand along the gate's cold stone. "If she's wrong, we've marched three hundred men into a mountain pass to admire architecture. Either way, I needed to see it myself. You understand. You wrote that you saw it yourself, in your letter. I'm told imitation is flattering."

Brann's jaw tightened. Good. Corvane preferred subordinates whose fear had somewhere to go.

"There is another possibility," Brann said carefully. "One I've been considering since the shrine. That the covenant was built on a crime. That the Okafor line bound the dragons without consent, and everything since — the peace, the trade, the quiet — has been interest paid on stolen principal."

"And?"

"And if that's true, the Crown's official history is a lie, and I've spent eleven years enforcing it."

Corvane turned and looked at him properly for the first time. Halvard Brann: honest eyes, careful hands, the kind of sincerity that made lesser men trust him and made Corvane want to check his pockets. There was something else there now, though. Something cracked open. Doubt was a wound, and wounds either healed or festered, and Corvane had commanded long enough to know which way this one was leaning.

"Inquisitor," he said, "do you know what I did before the war ended?"

"You broke the siege at Marrow Ford."

"I held Marrow Ford with four hundred men against two thousand, because I understood something my opposite number didn't." He tapped the gate's stone, once, like knocking on a door. "Power isn't a possession. It's a *relationship*. The rebels thought their numbers were power. They were wrong. The river was power, and I owned the ford. Here—" he gestured up at the black arch, the whole silent weight of it, "—here is the largest river in the world, and for a hundred years the Crown has employed one old woman per generation to stand next to it and count smugglers."

"That 'old woman' rewrote nothing and still the realms held," Brann said. "Maybe the relationship doesn't need improving."

"Everything needs improving. That's what improvement means." Corvane smiled, and knew exactly what the smile did to men like Brann, and used it anyway. "The keeper is missing. The book is missing. The prophecy says tonight — some night soon, dates are slippery things in texts that old — the gate fails. I am going to make certain that when it fails, it fails *for us*."

* * *

He worked through the afternoon in the gatehouse's map room, which the keepers had apparently used for exactly this purpose for six generations, and which now held his field desk, his instruments, and his silence.

Vell came in without knocking. She was the Crown's second-best inquisitor and the first-best survivor, and she stood at the edge of his lamplight the way she stood everywhere: slightly outside it.

"You're going to try to draw the fire," she said.

"I'm going to study the mechanism."

"You're going to try to draw the fire. I've read your requisitions, General. Cold-iron lattice, salt-glass, forty pounds of quicksilver, and a wagon labeled 'survey equipment' that my people say weighs like a bell." She folded her hands. "You mean to harness it as a weapon."

Corvane set down his pen. Outside, the wind moved through the pass with a sound almost like breathing.

"Do you know why the Emberlands war ended?" he asked.

"Because the covenant was signed."

"Because the dragons could not be beaten and we could not afford to lose. Eleven years of war, two hundred thousand dead, and the treaty was written by a mountain family with more ambition than army. Do you know what that means, Vell? It means the strongest force on this continent was neutralized not by valor but by *bookkeeping*. By one clever traitor and a stone." He leaned back. "Now imagine the next war. Kessia rearms. The coastal leagues build fleets. And the Crown arrives at the negotiation table with the fire of dragons under its command. Not allied with it. Not renting it. Commanding it."

"And the dragons?"

"What dragons? Vessarath is the last, and he's dying, and the rest are ash and story. There is no one left to object except a smuggler, a runaway clerk, and my own inquisitor, who appears to be developing a conscience at the worst possible moment of his career."

Vell didn't blink. "Halvard Brann is the best officer the Inquisition has produced in twenty years."

"Halvard Brann is a good man having a bad month. I've buried better." He picked the pen back up. "Watch him. Don't arrest him. Arrested men become martyrs; watched men become useful."

"And when Okafor is found?"

"Then we'll finally learn how the lock works." He said it lightly, but his hand had stopped moving on the page, and he was aware of it, and hated that he was aware of it. Because the truth — the truth he had confessed to no one, not even the mirror — was that he had dreamed about this gate for years. Since Marrow Ford. Since the night he'd stood in a burning ford with half his command dead and understood, with total clarity, that he would never again accept a world where the decisive power belonged to someone else.

Everyone serves something. He had chosen, long ago, to serve the thing itself.

* * *

At dusk he walked the wall alone, as he always did in a new position. A commander who didn't walk his own ground deserved to lose it.

From the western parapet he could see the pass winding down toward the lowlands, and beyond it, far off, the Emberlands' peaks — and there, unmistakable even at this distance, a wrongness in the light. The high snowfields glowed with the old dragon-fire, deep red-gold, steady as coals. But lower down, where green valleys should have caught the glow, the color was thinning. Fading at the edges like a garment wearing through.

It was dying. The keeper had been right. The prophecy had been right. The whole elegant machine was running down, and the Crown had slept through a century of maintenance calls.

Which meant, Corvane thought with something close to joy, that the timing was perfect.

Below him, torches moved in the courtyard — Brann's inquisitors, drilling, or pretending to drill. Somewhere in those mountains were a fugitive keeper, a wounded smuggler, and a book that could unravel a nation's founding myth. Let them run. Every hour they ran, the gate weakened, and every hour the gate weakened, the argument for seizing it grew stronger. They were, all unknowing, doing his lobbying for him.

"General." A courier, breathless, mud to the knees. "Rider from the south road. Says he carries word from the Thornfell crews."

Corvane took the sealed scrap. Smuggler hands, smuggler seal — a wax blob pressed with a coin rather than a signet. He read it twice.

They were selling the route. The mountain crews, frightened of losing their livelihood, had offered the hidden roads through Thornfell to the Crown. Prices, waypoints, the shrine trail, the lot. For amnesty and silver.

He almost laughed. A hundred years of the Okafor stewardship, of careful ledgers and tolerated crime and quiet complicity, and it came apart in a week because the criminals were the only honest men on the mountain. Everyone serves something. The smugglers served their purses, and their purses had just changed masters.

"Tell the rider the Crown accepts," he said. "Full amnesty for the crews. Silver on delivery. And tell them—" he paused, savoring it, "—tell them the General thanks them for their patriotism."

The courier went. Corvane stayed on the wall as full dark came down, and the peaks of the Emberlands burned their fading gold above the black line of the mountains, and he stood very still in the cold and felt the future arrive like weather.

Tomorrow his guides would lead three hundred men down roads no soldier had ever marched. Within days he would hold every approach to the gate. And the keeper — the last Okafor, the daughter of the forger, the woman who had somehow talked a career inquisitor into doubting his own Crown — would come back to the only home she had ever known, because people like her always did.

She would come back to save the gate.

And he would be waiting inside it.

Far below, so faint he might have imagined it, the black stone of the arch hummed — one low note, like a struck bell deciding whether to ring.
