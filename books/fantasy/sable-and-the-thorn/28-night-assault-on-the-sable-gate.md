## 28. Night Assault on the Sable Gate

Ryn Vasq had smuggled through the Sable Pass for eleven years, and he knew every way it could kill a man. The ice shelves above the switchbacks. The widow-makers — those leaning slabs of stone that waited for a footfall and a prayer. The cold that got into your lungs on the high stretch and stayed there like a debt.

He had never once thought to fear the gate itself.

Now he stood in the gatehouse yard with a crossbow bolt still stitched into his shoulder, watching General Corvane's army come down the pass by torchlight, and understood that everything he'd feared before had been small.

"They're not even trying to hide," Ada said beside him. Her voice was flat, which he'd learned meant she was more afraid than she would ever admit. "Four hundred men. Torches lit. He wants us to see them coming."

"He wants the whole mountain to see," Ryn said. "That's the point. You don't sneak up on a miracle."

Below them, the pass narrowed toward the gate — the Sable Gate itself, a wall of black stone older than any kingdom, set into the throat of Thornfell where the mountains pinched shut. From this distance it looked like nothing. A dark seam in the rock. For eleven years Ryn had walked past it, paid his tolls to Ada's ledger, never once wondered what it held back.

He wondered now. He could feel it wondering back.

"How long?" Ada asked.

Halvard Brann stood at her other side, out of uniform, his inquisitor's coat traded for plain wool that fooled no one. He'd counted the columns twice already. "They'll reach the outer barricade within the hour. Corvane won't wait for dawn. Men like him believe darkness is a weapon issued only to their side." He paused. "There's something else. The vanguard isn't carrying siege ladders."

Ryn saw it too now, squinting against the torch-glow. No ladders. No rams. Instead the front ranks hauled sledges, and on the sledges, wrapped in oilcloth and chained down like prisoners, were shapes that caught the firelight wrong — too smooth, too regular.

"Stonework," Brann said quietly. "Quarried keystone blanks. He means to replace it, not break it. Crack the old stone and seat his own before the fire escapes."

"That's insane," Ryn said. "You can't just swap out a piece of a covenant like changing a lock."

"You can if you have the original mason's notes," Brann said. "Which the Crown archives have held for two hundred years, catalogued under *infrastructure*." His mouth twisted. "I signed the requisition myself three months ago. I thought I was ordering building materials."

Ada turned from the parapet. In the torchlight from below her face was half gold, half shadow, and Ryn thought — not for the first time, but with new urgency — that he was tired of seeing her like that, split down the middle by other people's wars.

"The keystone cracks," she said, reciting it like scripture because it was, "the covenant fails, the realms bleed together, and whatever Corvane bottles in the meantime is his to sell. That's the prophecy date. Tonight. It doesn't say we lose tonight. It says *this* happens tonight." She looked at Ryn. "We can still choose what happens around it."

"I've got thirty fighters who used to be smugglers," Ryn said. "Half of them are drunk, a quarter are terrified, and one — Tamsin — is currently asleep in the woodshed. Against four hundred."

"Then we don't fight four hundred," Ada said. "We fight for time. Vessarath is coming."

"Vessarath can barely fly. He's crawling up the far valley, and I felt every mile of it in my chest like a hot wire." She pressed her palm flat against her sternum, where the dragon's wound had somehow become hers to carry. "But he's coming. And when he gets here—"

The gate sang.

It wasn't sound, exactly. It came up through the soles of their boots and out of the stone itself, a low harmonic note that made the torches gutter and made Ryn's teeth ache in their gums. Down in the yard, the horses screamed and pulled at their lines. Somewhere inside the gatehouse, crockery slid off a shelf and burst on the floor.

Everyone stopped talking. Everyone listened.

"It's counting again," Ada whispered. "Old Emberish. It's counting down."

* * *

They had six hours, by Ada's reckoning, and they spent them like a miser spends his last coin.

Ryn ran the defense the only way he knew how: like a smuggling run gone wrong, all improvisation and ugly pragmatism. They didn't man the walls — walls were for soldiers, and they weren't soldiers. Instead they salted the approach with everything the pass had ever taught him. Oil and tallow poured on the frozen switchback and left to glaze. The widow-maker slabs above the road, pried loose and balanced, waiting. Tamsin — awake now, furious about it — rigged the old smuggler signal-fires along the ridge so that lighting one lit three, a false army burning in the dark.

"You know what I keep thinking?" Tamsin said as she worked, tying knots with quick bitter fingers. "I spent ten years running goods past this gate. Silk, brandy, unlicensed iron. Never once asked what it was *for*. None of us did. We just assumed it was there, like weather."

"It was there to be walked past," Ryn said. "That was the design. Keep everyone moving, keep nobody looking."

"And now we're the ones standing on it."

"Now we're the ones standing on it," he agreed, and handed her another coil of fuse-cord.

Ada spent the hours underground, in the vault beneath the gatehouse where the original ledger sat chained to its stand. Ryn found her there near midnight, surrounded by paper, her sleeves rolled, ink to the elbow, copying — no, *composing*, he realized when he read over her shoulder. New words. New terms. A covenant written in a living hand instead of carved in dead stone.

"You think you can rewrite it mid-battle?" he asked.

"I think the battle is just noise," she said without looking up. "The real work happens at the keystone or it doesn't happen at all. Everything else is me buying minutes." She finally looked up, and there was something in her face he hadn't seen before — not fear, not resolve. Relief. Terrible relief. "Do you know what it's like, Ryn, to spend your whole life guarding a lie and then finally get to hold the truth in your hands? Even if it kills me. Even tonight. At least it's mine."

"Don't say *even if it kills you* like it's a rounding error."

She almost smiled. "You first. You took a bolt for me on the shrine road."

"That was tactics. You were the faster runner."

"Liar."

"Yeah," he said. "Liar."

Brann appeared in the vault doorway, and the temperature of the room changed the way it always did when an inquisitor entered, even a defected one. "Scouts report the column has reached the frozen switchback," he said. "And General Corvane has done something strange. He's stopped. They're making camp. Cooking fires."

Ada frowned. "He's early. Why would he wait?"

"Because he's not waiting for dawn," Brann said slowly, and Ryn watched the understanding arrive on the man's face like frost spreading across a window. "He's waiting for the gate. The countdown. If the keystone is going to crack on its own at the prophesied hour, why spend lives breaking it? Let the prophecy do the work. He only needs to be standing there with his replacement stone when it does."

"So we don't need to beat four hundred men," Ryn said. "We need to beat midnight."

* * *

The hour arrived the way disasters do — first slowly, then all at once.

At the turn of the watch, the singing in the stone changed key. Ryn felt it in the yard, felt it in the fillings of his teeth, felt it in the old bolt-wound in his shoulder like a second heartbeat. The torches along the walls burned steady but their flames leaned, all of them together, toward the gate, as if the fire itself wanted to look.

Then the keystone cracked.

Not broke. *Cracked.* A single line, thin as a hair, splitting the black stone from crown to base with a sound like the world's largest tree giving up in winter. And through that hairline crack came light — not firelight, not sunlight, something older and redder and hungrier — and with the light came a scream.

Dragon-fire didn't leak. It *screamed*. The pressure of a century of held breath escaping through a wound the width of a knife blade, and the sound it made was the sound Vessarath must have made when the Okafor betrayal drove the binding home, and every person in the yard — smugglers, deserters, a defected inquisitor — dropped to their knees or their faces or simply stood swaying with their hands over their ears while the pass filled with a voice that had been screaming since before their grandparents were born.

Ryn had heard dragons once, as a boy, from very far away. This was not that. This was grief with a temperature.

"IT'S EARLY!" Ada was shouting, hauling herself upright against the parapet, blood trickling from her nose. "THE COUNTDOWN MOVED — CORVANE'S STONE IS INTERFERING — THE QUARRY-BLANKS ARE PULLING AT IT—" She grabbed Ryn's collar. "The sledges! The blanks aren't inert, they're cut from the same vein, they're *drawing the fracture open*—"

Down the pass, Corvane's camp erupted into motion. Four hundred men rising at once, torches streaming, the sledges creaking forward toward a gate that was already dying on its own. The general had planned to catch the fire in a bottle. Now he was going to catch it in a landslide.

Ryn did the arithmetic he'd been avoiding all night. The barricade would buy twenty minutes. The oil-glaze on the switchback, maybe ten more. The widow-makers, if Tamsin's fuses held, five after that. Forty minutes against an army, and behind them a crack in the world widening one heartbeat at a time.

"We hold the yard," he said. "We hold it until Vessarath gets here, and then we get Ada to the keystone, and then everyone else runs."

"No one runs," Ada said.

"Everyone runs. That's the whole point of you rewriting this thing — someone has to survive to sign it." He caught her shoulders. "Listen to me. Eleven years I moved contraband past your ledger and you never once asked me to be honest, and I'm being honest now: I will put my body between this army and that stone, and I will enjoy it, because it's the first useful thing I've ever done. But you have to live. Not for the plan. For me. Selfishly. I'm allowed one selfish request."

Her eyes were wet and furious. "One."

"One."

Above the pass, on the ridgeline, Tamsin's false fires bloomed — one, then three, then nine, a burning constellation meant to make Corvane's scouts see an army that wasn't there. For one beautiful moment the column hesitated.

Then the sky over the far valley turned orange, and a sound rolled up the pass that was not the gate's scream but answered it — a deep, ragged, wounded bellow, the voice of the last dragon announcing himself to the mountains of his birth.

Vessarath had come. Crawling, bleeding, half-dead, but *here*.

Corvane's army broke formation entirely. Ryn saw it happen — the front ranks turning, torches swinging toward the ridge, officers shrieking orders that dissolved into the wind. And in that chaos, in that single stolen minute, the keystone gave its second crack, wider than the first, and the light pouring through it began to burn patterns into the snow of the yard. Old letters. Covenant-script, searing itself into the ground, the old terms dissolving letter by letter—

Ada was already running for the stairs.

Ryn followed, crossbow up, and the night came apart around them.

* * *

What happened next, Ryn would remember forever in fragments, because some things are too large for a mind to hold whole.

The vanguard reaching the barricade and the barricade going up in a sheet of blue flame — not their fire, the *gate's* fire, leaking through the stones of the yard itself, defending its keeper. Tamsin screaming the widow-makers loose and a hillside coming down on a hundred men who mostly survived it, God willing, mostly. Brann walking unarmed into a knot of Crown soldiers and shouting his name and rank until they faltered, because even now, even treasonous, Halvard Brann's voice carried the weight of the institution he was betraying, and men hesitated, and hesitation was all Ryn needed to put bolts into the right shoulders and thighs — he'd told Ada once he'd never killed a man, and tonight he kept that true by inches, by mercy shots, by luck he didn't trust.

And through all of it, the crack in the keystone widening, the scream becoming a roar becoming a voice, and Ada Okafor climbing the rubble toward the broken heart of the Sable Gate with a knife in her hand and her ancestor's confession tucked against her ribs, small and stubborn as a coal that refused to go out.

Ryn reached the base of the keystone dais just as the third crack came, and this one was final — he knew it in his bones, the way you know a bridge is going before your feet do. The black stone groaned, bulged, and split from crown to base, and the Sable Gate — the wall that had stood since before kingdoms, the leash forged in betrayal, the door he'd walked past ten thousand times without looking —

— shattered.

The explosion wasn't fire. It was *absence* — a moment where the pass had no sound, no light, no air — and then everything arrived at once. Dragon-fire in a pillar that punched through the cloud layer. Snow flash-boiling to steam. The mountains themselves ringing like struck bells. Ryn was thrown the length of the yard, slammed into the gatehouse wall, and lay there with the taste of copper and ozone in his mouth, watching the sky fill with light that had been imprisoned for a hundred years.

Through the ruin of the gate, the Emberlands showed themselves for the first time in living memory: green valleys and black peaks, frost and flame fighting across the same hillsides, a realm freezing and burning at once exactly as the scorched page had promised. And somewhere in that impossible country, a shape the size of a cathedral lifted its head and roared at the opening sky.

Ada. Where was Ada.

He found her at the edge of the blast-scar, on her knees in the steaming snow, alive, singed, her ledger-book clutched to her chest like a child. Behind her, where the keystone had been, there was nothing but a socket of raw stone and drifting embers — and kneeling in that socket, her palm pressed to the rock, her blood already welling black in the ember-light, beginning to write.

"Ryn," she said, without turning, her voice raw and clear and carrying across the ruined yard the way Vessarath's roar had carried across the pass. "Get everyone back. What happens next, nobody stands close enough to touch."

Down the pass, through the smoke, General Corvane rose from where the blast had thrown him. His sledge of quarry-blanks was splinters. His army was scattered. His face, when he turned toward the open gate and the free fire pouring through it, wore an expression Ryn had seen only once before — on a gambler watching the house burn with his stake still on the table.

Corvane started walking toward the fire.

Toward the pillar of raw dragon-fire, unprotected, arms spread, *smiling*.

"Oh no," Ryn breathed. "Oh, you absolute fool—"

Ada turned, followed his gaze, and went white. "He's going to drink it," she said. "He's going to try to take it into himself."

The last dragon's shadow swept over the pass like an eclipse, and the night was nowhere near over.
