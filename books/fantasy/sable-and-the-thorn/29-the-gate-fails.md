## 29. The Gate Fails

The keystone cracked at the hour the prophecy named, and it did not crack like stone.

It cracked like a knuckle. Like ice on a pond in late spring. Like something that had been holding its breath for three hundred years and finally, finally let go.

Ada felt it before she heard it — a shudder through the soles of her boots, up through her knees, into her teeth — and she was already running when the sound came, sprinting down the shattered corridor of the gatehouse with Ryn's blood still tacky on her sleeve and Vessarath's name burning in her throat. Behind her, Corvane's soldiers poured through the breach they'd made of the eastern wall, torches and steel and shouting, but none of it mattered now. None of it had ever mattered. They had all come to fight over a door that was about to stop being a door.

"Ada!" Ryn's voice, ragged, from somewhere behind. He shouldn't be upright. He'd taken a bolt two days ago and marched on it anyway because he was Ryn Vasq and stubbornness was the only inheritance he'd ever claimed. "Ada, the pass—"

"I see it!"

She did see it. That was the horror of it. She saw everything at once, the way you see everything in the last moment before a fall: the keystone high in the Sable Gate's arch splitting along lines no chisel had cut, hairline veins of light spreading through the black stone like frost across a window; the air above the pass beginning to shimmer, to *bend*, the way heat bends over summer roads except this was cold, a cold shimmer, a wrongness; and beyond the arch, where there should have been only mountain and night sky, something else was showing through. Something green. Something burning.

The Emberlands were bleeding into Thornfell, and Thornfell into them, and the seam between the worlds was coming apart like wet paper.

*The date is circled,* the dying peddler had said, pressing the scorched book into her hands a lifetime ago — twenty-nine days ago, an entire life ago. *Tonight's date, keeper. In ink older than my grandfather. And beside it, a name.*

Her name. It had always been her name.

She reached the base of the arch as the first true fracture opened, a seam of white fire running the height of the gate, and the sound it made was not thunder. It was a voice. The same voice she'd heard weeks ago counting down in Old Emberish, patient as water dripping in a cave, and now it wasn't counting anymore. Now it was saying a word over and over, one word, and though she had never studied Old Emberish, though no keeper had in generations because the Crown had decided dead languages were safer than living ones, she understood it the way you understand your own heartbeat.

*Unbound. Unbound. Unbound.*

"No," Ada said, to the gate, to the prophecy, to her ancestor Sefu Okafor whose confession she had read aloud to two armies only hours ago. "No, I rewrote it. You heard me. I *rewrote* you."

But the covenant had been dying since the moment she cut her palm at the keystone and wrote new terms in her own blood, and a dying thing does not distinguish between the hand that frees it and the hand that leashed it. The old magic was coming apart, and it was taking the architecture of both realms down with it.

The keystone fell.

Not all at once. A wedge of it, black stone the size of a coffin lid, dropping out of the arch like a tooth from a rotted jaw — and where it had been there was a hole, and through the hole came the Emberlands.

Not a view of them. Not a glimpse. *Them.* Air that smelled of sulfur and snowflowers. Light that was amber and moving, alive, dragon-light diffused through a sky the color of deep honey. And cold — gods, the cold, pouring through the gap like water through a breached hull, frost blooming instantly across the gatehouse stones, across the fallen keystone, across the arms of the soldiers who had been running toward her and now stood frozen mid-stride, staring, their war forgotten on their faces.

And beneath the cold, fire. Because that was the Emberlands' curse and its nature, the thing the covenant had held in balance for three centuries: a realm that burned and froze in the same breath, dragon-fire and glacier-winter locked in an embrace that should have destroyed them both and instead had made a world. Without the gate to mediate, without the Okafor leash to hold the two natures apart, they were collapsing into each other.

Ada could see it happening. Through the widening hole she could see the Emberlands' near valleys — the green ones, the ones Vessarath had shown her in dreams, terraced and orcharded, tended by the emberkin folk with their copper skin and furnace eyes — and she watched frost race down those valleys even as fire climbed the ridges above them. Burning and freezing at once. The orchards going white while the forests went black. A whole realm devouring itself.

"We did this." Ryn reached her, gray-faced, one hand pressed to his bandaged side, and followed her gaze through the gap. His voice came out flat with shock. "Breaking the gate — the second ending — I thought—"

"I know what you thought." So had she. The hidden page had been clear enough: *one saves dragons, one saves humans, not both.* Breaking the gate damned the Emberlands. Reforging it cost a keeper's life. She had found the third path, the marginalia path, the rewritten covenant — and she had written it, and it hadn't been enough, because the old covenant had been load-bearing for three hundred years and you cannot swap out a foundation while the house is standing on it.

Or maybe you can. Maybe the house just has to fall first.

"The new terms need time," she said, and was surprised how steady her voice came out. "To take root. To bind. The old magic has to finish dying before the new one can live. This—" she gestured at the apocalypse spilling through the arch, "—this is the interval."

"And how long is the interval?"

"I don't know."

"Ada."

"*I don't know, Ryn.*" The steadiness cracked and she let it. There was no more use for masks tonight, not with the sky bleeding. "Maybe hours. Maybe days. Maybe the new covenant never binds at all and both realms burn and freeze together until there's nothing left of either one. I wrote the terms. I didn't write the certainty. Certainty was what the old covenant sold, and look what it cost."

Above them, the arch groaned. Another wedge of keystone shifted, ground, and dropped, exploding against the frost-slick stones. The hole doubled. Beyond it, impossibly far and impossibly close, a shape moved against the honey-colored sky — vast, winged, trailing smoke from a wound in its chest shaped exactly like the arch it was crawling toward.

Vessarath. He was coming. Dragging himself across his own dying realm toward the failing gate, toward the daughter of the man who had forged his chains, because if the gate died unmediated then everything he had spent three centuries protecting burned with it.

"He won't make it," Ryn said quietly. "Look at him. He can barely fly."

"He doesn't have to fly. He has to *witness*." The new covenant's final clause, the one the magic had demanded at the last, the price she'd paid without fully understanding: *a witness bound to both realms.* She had thought it meant her. She had been wrong, or half wrong. It meant everyone. It meant every soul on both sides of the seam watching the old lie die together, and what they built from the watching.

Shouting behind them. She turned. Brann was coming up the ruined road at a dead run, his white-and-silver inquisitor's cloak torn and scorched, a dozen of his defected company behind him — the men who had turned when she read Sefu Okafor's confession aloud, who had chosen truth over the Crown's pretty history. And beyond them, at the mouth of the pass, Corvane's army: torchlight and pike-tips and the general's personal banner, the golden flame on black, advancing on the ruin of the gate like men marching to claim a treasure that was actively destroying itself.

And past *them*, further back, higher up — movement on the peaks. Winged shapes. The emberkin, the remnant council's people, descending from the Emberlands' side despite their council's vote, despite their demand that the gate be destroyed outright. Coming anyway. Coming to see.

Both realms, converging on the threshold. Just as the third clause required. Just as the prophecy's marginalia had hinted in that cramped, ancient hand: *when the door dies in front of everyone, the door can be reborn in front of everyone.*

If anyone survived to do the rebirthing.

The temperature was still falling. Frost crawled up Ada's boots, her cloak, the hilt of the knife at her belt — and then, a heartbeat later, heat rolled over it all, dry and enormous, and the frost flashed to steam. Freeze and burn, freeze and burn, the two natures lashing each other through the widening gap, and anything caught in the crossing-place would be destroyed by both. Anything standing at the threshold when the gate finished failing—

"Everyone back!" Brann's voice, carrying the parade-ground authority that had once hunted heretics and now, apparently, saved them. His soldiers scrambled back from the arch. Even Corvane's vanguard hesitated, their officers shouting, the golden banner snapping in wind that blew from two directions at once.

Ada did not step back.

Because she could hear it again. Under the groaning of the stone, under the screaming of the colliding airs, under the word *unbound* repeating like a bell that wouldn't stop ringing — she could hear the new covenant. Faint. Newborn. The words she'd written in her own blood, echoing back to her from inside the gate's death-throes, waiting.

*No blood-price. No leash. Free passage and mutual keeping.*

Waiting for the old structure to finish falling so it could stand in the ruins.

"It's not ending," she breathed. Ryn looked at her. Brann looked at her. Even the frost and fire seemed to pause, a held breath between agonies. "It's *changing*. The gate isn't failing — the *lie* is failing. The gate is what we make of what's left."

"That's very inspiring," Ryn said, "and we're all about to die."

"Then we die at the front of it." She pulled the scorched prophecy book from inside her coat — Brann had given it back to her in chains, and she had carried it through the battle like a heart outside her chest — and opened it to the page with the circled date. The century-old ink had changed. She had noticed it an hour ago and told no one, because some things you have to be sure of before you say them. Where the prophecy had named the gate's failure, the letters had blurred, run, reformed — and beneath the old doom, in handwriting that matched her own blood-written terms exactly, in handwriting she had never written with any mortal pen, new words had appeared:

*The gate fails as it was. The gate stands as it will be. Between the last breath of the lie and the first breath of the truth, the threshold belongs to whoever holds it.*

Holds it. Not owns it. Not commands it. Holds — the way you hold a stranger's hand. The way you hold a door open.

Ada Okafor had spent her whole life holding a gate shut. It had never once occurred to her, until this exact moment, standing in the ruin of everything she'd guarded, that holding a gate open was also keeping.

The keystone gave its final groan. Above the arch, the last great wedge of black stone tilted, cracked through, and began to fall — and through the widening wound the Emberlands poured in earnest, frost-fire and honey-light and the distant, terrible beauty of a realm eating itself alive, and somewhere in that light a wounded dragon lifted his head and saw her standing at the threshold and roared a warning she chose, with her eyes open, to ignore.

Ada stepped forward into the gap, raised the book over her head so both armies could see it, and shouted the new covenant's terms into the collapse — her voice, her blood's words, raw and human and utterly insufficient and the only thing in either world that might be enough.

Behind her, the keystone hit the ground, and the Sable Gate, as it had been, ceased to exist.

* * *

The silence afterward lasted three heartbeats. Then both realms screamed through the opening at once.

Cold took her first — a cold with texture, with intent, the glacier-heart of the Emberlands reaching through the dead arch to claim the warm world — and Ada's breath crystallized in the air and her eyelashes frosted and she thought, distantly, *so this is the second ending after all, the rigged one, the one where nobody gets saved* — and then the fire came, rolling over the cold like a tide over sand, and the two forces met directly above her head and tore at each other, and she stood in the seam of their war and refused to move.

*Free passage,* she shouted, into wind that was two winds blowing opposite ways. *Mutual keeping! No blood-price! No leash!*

The words left her mouth and did something words shouldn't do: they stayed. Hung in the air. Glowed faintly, gold-white, the color of the blood she'd written them in. Each phrase hung like a plank laid across a chasm, and the chasm — the failing seam between worlds — seemed to lean on them, to test them, the way a new bridge is tested by its first weight.

Frost raced up her arm. Fire licked along her other side. Her body was becoming the border, the way the gate had been the border, the way her ancestor's blood had been the border for three hundred years — and she understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, why the new covenant had demanded a witness bound to both realms.

It hadn't been asking for a witness.

It had been asking for a *keystone*.

"No," Ryn said, right beside her — when had he crossed the threshold, fool, brave fool, wounded fool — his hand clamping her shoulder, his warmth suddenly the most real thing in the world. "No, whatever you're realizing, un-realize it. We find another way. We always find another way."

"There is no other way. There's only the way, and whether someone walks it." She looked at him, and the frost on her lashes was melting in the fire-heat and refreezing in the cold, and she was crying and the tears froze on her cheeks and she laughed, actually laughed, at the absurdity of a body trying to grieve in two climates at once. "Ryn. Listen to me. Listen. The old covenant ran on stolen blood and a stolen choice. Mine runs on given blood and a given choice. That's the whole difference. That's the entire rewrite. Three hundred years of theft, answered by one gift, freely offered—"

"Don't." His voice broke on it. "Don't you dare finish that sentence."

Above them, through the chaos, wings. Vessarath, close now, close enough that she could see the keystone-shaped wound in his chest pulsing in time with the gate's death-throes, close enough to see that his ancient eyes were fixed not on the collapsing arch but on *her* — and what was in those eyes was not warning anymore.

It was grief. And beneath the grief, acknowledgment. The look of a father watching a daughter walk a road he had walked, in the other direction, three hundred years ago.

The dragon opened his jaws, and his voice came out not as roar but as speech, in the common tongue, in a register that shook loose stones from the broken arch:

*"Daughter of the forger. The terms are heard. The terms are weighed."*

The frost and fire paused. Both armies, both realms, every living soul at the threshold, held its breath.

*"One term remains unwritten,"* Vessarath said. *"The covenant asks its price. Name what you will give — or turn away now, and let both worlds fall."*

Ada looked at Ryn. At Brann, standing at the edge of the threshold with his sword drawn and useless and his face full of a faith he'd found too late to be comfortable. At the emberkin descending the far slopes, and Corvane's army faltering at the pass's mouth, and the burning-freezing splendor of the Emberlands bleeding through the ruin of everything her family had built.

She opened her mouth to answer —

— and the ground split beneath the keystone's corpse, and out of the crack rose a figure wrapped in golden flame, radiant and monstrous and burning from within, and General Corvane smiled at her with eyes like banked coals and said:

"You'll give nothing, Keeper. *I've* already taken what the gate needs."
