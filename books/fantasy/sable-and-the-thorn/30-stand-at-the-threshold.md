## 30. Stand at the Threshold

Ryn Vasq had spent twenty years learning every way a body could hide in Thornfell. He knew the scree fields that swallowed footsteps whole. He knew which ledges held shadow past noon and which caves breathed warm enough to sleep in through winter. He knew how to be nothing — no sound, no shape, no name.

There was nowhere to hide on the broken gate.

The Sable Gate had been a wall once, and before that an arch, and before that — if you believed the shrine stones — a wound in the world that two peoples had agreed to dress. Now it was a ruin with a cracked keystone bleeding light into the pass, and Ryn stood on its threshold with a stolen soldier's shield and a crossbow he'd reloaded so many times his fingers had gone numb, and there was no cover at all. Just him, Ada, and Halvard Brann, three figures on a broken causeway against four hundred men and the thing wearing Corvane's face at their back.

"Left side," Brann said. His voice was calm in the way of a man reading scripture aloud during a fire. "They'll come up the left. The rubble's climbable there."

"You'd know," Ryn said. "You trained them."

"I did." Brann didn't take offense. He never took offense; that was the most unsettling thing about him. He adjusted his grip on his mace — he'd abandoned the inquisitor's staff somewhere below, along with his white cloak, and now he looked like what he'd been before the Church found him: a border soldier gone gray at the temples. "Sergeant Odo leads the vanguard. He flanks left out of habit. Good man. Slow to commit. If we kill him early the rest will hesitate."

"We're not killing Odo," Ada said.

Ryn didn't look at her. He kept his eyes on the slope where torchlight was pooling like spilled oil, dozens of flames converging in the dark below the gatehouse ruins. But he heard it in her voice — that flat, exhausted certainty she'd carried since the keystone cracked, since the fire screamed through the pass, since Vessarath fell from the burning sky and hit the mountainside like a landslide given wings.

"He'll kill us," Ryn said.

"Maybe." Ada crouched behind a slab of fallen lintel, her palms still crusted black with dried blood and keystone dust. She hadn't stopped moving since the breach. Ryn suspected she wouldn't stop until something made her. "But we hold this threshold until Vessarath reaches it. After that, none of this matters."

"That's not comforting."

"It wasn't meant to be."

Below them, the army began to climb.

* * *

Ryn had run from better odds than these. That was the honest arithmetic of his life. Twenty years of smuggling taught a man exactly when a fight was lost, and the trick — the whole art of survival — was leaving *before* the losing started, while your legs still worked and your name was still worth something on the far side of the mountains. He'd left lovers, partners, a brother, a whole crew once in the Saltmarsh years. Each time he'd told himself the same thing: *alive is the only position you can bargain from.*

He thought about it now, absurdly, as the first soldiers crested the rubble line forty yards down the causeway. He could see a path. There was always a path — down the eastern stair, through the old drainage cut, into the maze of smuggler roads that only he knew. He could have Ada there in ten minutes. Brann would die here, probably, but Brann had chosen this, and men who chose things got to own the cost.

The problem was the dragon.

Vessarath lay half a mile down the slope, a mountain of scorched bronze and torn wing, dragging himself upward inch by inch through the snow. Ryn could hear him breathing — a wet, bellows-deep sound that shook loose stones. Every few minutes the dragon moved, and every movement was a small earthquake, and every pause lasted too long. The wound in his chest glowed like a forge door left open, shaped exactly like the keystone above them, and through that wound Ryn could see something that wasn't flesh: a darkness going cold at the edges, frost creeping inward across scales that should have shimmered with heat.

The Emberlands were dying through him. And Corvane's men were climbing toward the dragon with fire-pots and harpoon-throwers, because General Corvane had decided a dying god was easier to harvest than a living one.

"Ryn." Ada's hand closed on his wrist. "Wherever you just went — come back."

"I'm calculating," he said.

"You're composing your exit speech."

He almost laughed. "You know me too well for someone who's known me three weeks."

"I've known you three years. You just didn't tell me the truth for two and a half of them." She squeezed his wrist once, hard, and let go. "If you want to run, run. I mean it. I won't think less—"

"Don't." The word came out sharper than he intended. He took a breath. Snow was starting again, fine and dry, hissing on the hot stone where dragon-fire had passed. "I sold my route to your Crown for coin once. Sold out my own crew. I'm done being the man who leaves. Let me have this one thing, Ada. Let me be the man who stands."

She looked at him for a long moment. In the light bleeding from the cracked keystone her face was all planes and shadows, and there was blood on her jaw that wasn't hers, and she was the most frightening person he had ever loved.

"Together, then," she said.

"Together," said Brann, without turning around. "Less talking. They're at the lip."

* * *

They came up the left, as Brann had promised.

Odo's vanguard came over the rubble in a wedge, shields locked, torches guttering in the wind off the high pass. Behind them the rest of the army spread across the slope like a dark tide finding its level, hundreds strong, and above them all — impossibly, horribly — burned Corvane.

The general stood on a shelf of rock thirty feet up, and he was on fire. Not burning — *burning* implied consumption, damage, an end. Corvane blazed. Fire poured off his shoulders like a mantle, rose from his fists in slow banners, lit his face from below so that his smile looked carved by candlelight. He had drunk raw dragon-fire at the breach, Ryn had seen it happen, seen the man kneel at the screaming crack in the keystone and *drink*, and whatever lived in that fire had gotten into him and was slowly eating him alive from the inside. He glowed at the seams. When he raised his arm, Ryn could see the veins in his wrist shining orange through the skin.

"Hold the threshold!" Brann bellowed, and then the vanguard hit them and there was no more room for words.

It was ugly, close work. Ryn fought the way he did everything — cheaply, efficiently, with no honor whatsoever. He used the shield edge, the pommel, his boots, the rubble itself, hooking a man's ankle and letting gravity do the killing work. A spearman lunged and Ryn caught the shaft under his arm, twisted, shoved the man backward off the causeway into the dark. Somewhere to his right Brann swung his mace in short, brutal arcs, calling names as he struck — *"Odo! Stand down, Odo, stand DOWN"* — and to his left Ada fought with a smuggler's short blade and a keeper's fury, giving ground not an inch, holding the exact center of the broken gate as though the geometry of the world depended on it.

Maybe it did. Ryn genuinely no longer knew.

"Second rank!" Brann shouted. "They're feeding the second rank!"

Ryn reloaded. His hands knew the motion the way they knew knots and lockpicks: bolt, seat, crank, done. He put a bolt through the thigh of the second rank's sergeant and the man went down screaming and the rank wavered — because soldiers were still soldiers, even marching for a burning madman, and a screaming sergeant sounded like a bad omen in any army.

Then the harpoons came.

Ryn saw them arcing up from the slope below — three of them, trailing chain, launched from the big frame-throwers Corvane's engineers had hauled up the pass. They weren't aimed at the gate. They were aimed past it, down-slope, at the crawling mountain of bronze and blood.

"No," Ada breathed.

The first harpoon missed, burying itself in scree. The second struck Vessarath's flank near the hind leg, and the sound the dragon made was not a roar. It was lower than that, older — a note that Ryn felt in his teeth and his sternum and the fillings of his teeth, a grief so vast it registered as weather. The third struck home in the shoulder, and chains snapped taut, and fifty men below leaned back and dug in their heels and *pulled*.

Vessarath stopped crawling.

His head came around, slow, majestic, ruined — one eye clouded dead, the other burning like the last coal of a dying fire — and he looked at the men on his chains, and he looked up at the broken gate, at the three small figures standing on it, and Ryn would swear until his dying day that the dragon was checking. Checking whether the daughter of the forger still held the threshold. Whether his crawl had been worth it.

"HOLD IT!" Ada screamed, to Brann, to Ryn, to herself, to the stone. "HOLD THE THRESHOLD!"

The third wave came, and it was not a probing attack anymore. Corvane had stopped playing. Soldiers swarmed the rubble from both sides at once, and the causeway became a single continuous collision, shield-bosses cracking against ribs, blades ringing off stone, breath steaming and blood steaming and everywhere the strange amber glow of the keystone's leaking fire turning every face the color of candle wax.

Ryn took a sword-cut along the forearm and barely felt it. He shot a man point-blank and felt it plenty. He lost track of Brann, then found him again surrounded, swinging, roaring scripture now — actual scripture, verses Ryn half-recognized from funeral rites — and taking wounds and refusing to fall, because Halvard Brann had spent his whole life believing in a covenant story that turned out to be a leash, and this causeway was where he'd decided to make his apology.

A soldier got inside Ryn's guard. Big kid, maybe nineteen, eyes wide with terror and duty in equal measure. Ryn had a clear thrust. Took it away instead — slammed the pommel into the boy's temple, dropped him sleeping, stepped over him.

"Sentimental," grunted Brann, appearing at his shoulder, bleeding from the scalp.

"Economical," Ryn said. "Dead men get replaced. Sleeping ones remember."

Something like a laugh crossed the inquisitor's ruined face, and then a crossbow bolt from the slope took Brann high in the chest, and he sat down hard against the fallen lintel, and the line bent.

* * *

The line bent, and did not break, because Ada Okafor stood in the gap and became the line.

Ryn saw it happen and understood, finally, what she had been training for her entire life without knowing it. All those years auditing traffic, counting heads, keeping the ledger of the pass — it had been discipline, yes, but underneath the discipline had been *standing*. The keeper's whole art was to be the fixed point everything else arranged itself around. Smugglers routed around her. Pilgrims queued for her. The covenant itself had bent its long dark history around the Okafor name. And now she planted herself in the breach with her ancestor's blood on her hands and refused, absolutely, to move, and the assault broke against her like water against a pier.

"Fall back!" someone shouted below. "Fall back and burn them!"

Fire-pots next. Of course. Ryn grabbed Ada's collar and hauled her down behind the lintel as the first clay sphere burst against the causeway, liquid flame spreading in a hungry yellow sheet. Heat washed over them. The old stone, soaked in a century of keeper's vigilance, did not catch — but the wooden barricades they'd rigged did, and the smoke rolled thick and black, and through it Ryn heard the army regrouping, hundreds of boots finding rhythm, the harpoon crews reeling their lines, and above it all Corvane's voice rolling down the slope like a landslide with words:

"THE GATE IS CROWN PROPERTY! THE FIRE IS CROWN PROPERTY! KILL THE TRAITORS AND BRING ME THE DRAGON'S HEART!"

Ada coughed, wiped her eyes, looked at Ryn. Her face was streaked with soot and blood and she was smiling — a thin, terrible, luminous smile.

"He's afraid," she said.

"He's *on fire*, Ada."

"He's afraid," she repeated. "You don't scream like that unless you're afraid. The fire's eating him and he knows it, and he thinks if he takes the heart fast enough he can outrun it." She gripped Ryn's arm. "How much farther?"

Ryn looked down the slope. Through the smoke and the snow-glow he found the dragon.

Vessarath had moved.

The chains were still in him, the harpoons still buried deep, but he had torn one anchor free — Ryn could see the ripped-out iron and the chunk of mountainside still attached to its cable — and he had dragged himself another hundred yards upslope. His good eye was fixed on the gate. On Ada. His breath came in shudders now, and frost rimed his nostrils, and where his belly dragged the snow did not melt anymore.

"Close," Ryn said. "Half the distance. Maybe less."

"Then we hold." Ada stood, drew her blade, and stepped back into the smoke. "One more wave, Ryn. Whatever it costs. One more wave."

* * *

The last wave came with the dawn still hours away and no moon to help them, and it nearly worked.

Corvane sent his reserves up both flanks at once and drove the survivors of the vanguard straight up the throat of the causeway, and for a few endless minutes Ryn Vasq understood, with perfect smuggler's clarity, that he was going to die on this broken gate in the dark, and found, to his genuine surprise, that he was at peace with it. Not resigned — peace was different from resignation. Peace was looking at the boy in front of him, the one whose sword he'd turned aside twice already, and choosing again not to kill him. Peace was hearing Brann, half-dead against the lintel, still calling out warnings in that infuriatingly calm voice — *"left, Ryn, LEFT"* — and knowing the man would spend his last breaths keeping a smuggler alive because the smuggler mattered to the woman holding the center. Peace was Ada's back against his, her breath ragged, her blade notched, her voice never once stopping: *"Hold. Hold. Hold."*

Three people. Four hundred. A threshold between worlds.

And then the mountain spoke.

It was not a word. It was the absence of one — a silence so total it fell over the battle like a struck bell falls over a market square. Every soldier froze. Every torch guttered upright. Even the fire on the barricades stood still, flames frozen mid-lick like painted things.

Vessarath had reached the base of the gate.

The dragon pulled himself onto the causeway approach, and the stone sang under his weight, and the harpoon chains snapped one after another like thread — not cut, *unmade*, the covenant's old bindings recognizing, at the last, whose blood had written them and whose will now unmade them. He raised his head. The wound in his chest blazed, keystone-shaped, pouring cold light and dying heat, and he looked upon the army of the Crown with his one burning eye, and he did not roar.

He simply looked. And four hundred men remembered, all at once, in their marrow, what dragons were.

"NOW," Ada shouted, voice cracking across the silence. "BRANN — THE STANDARD! RYN — WITH ME!"

Brann, gray-lipped, bolt still in his chest, hauled himself upright and tore the white-and-gold standard from a fallen sergeant's grasp and planted it upside down in the rubble — the old sign of surrender, of parley, of *witness*. And Ryn ran to Ada's side at the center of the broken gate, and together they stood before the dying dragon and the shattering keystone, three small figures and one vast failing heart, facing down an army that had suddenly, collectively, forgotten why it had climbed.

Corvane descended from his rock shelf in a sheet of flame, landing on the causeway's far end, blazing brighter than ever, brighter than any man should, his skin splitting along the arms with seams of light.

"The heart," Corvane said. "Mine. Step aside."

Ada did not step aside. She lifted her blood-crusted palm, and Ryn saw the keystone light answer it — flicker, lean toward her like a plant toward sun.

"You're too late," she said. "It was never yours to take. And it was never ours to give."

The keystone, above them, cracked a final inch — and the light that came through was not fire, and was not cold, but something else entirely, something neither realm had a word for yet.

Ada turned to Ryn, and her eyes were wet and steady.

"Get ready," she said. "I'm going to need a witness."
