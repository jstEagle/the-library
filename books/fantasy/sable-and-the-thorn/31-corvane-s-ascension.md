## 31. Corvane's Ascension

General Corvane had never once in his life been cold.

He had marched the frozen reaches of the Vhalen coast, had wintered at Kestrelmoor where men lost fingers to frost and their minds soon after, and he had not shivered. Cold was a condition of lesser men. He had decided this long ago, in the way he decided most things — early, absolutely, and without appeal to evidence. A man who believed himself warm would be warm. A man who believed himself destined would be destined.

Tonight, standing on the shattered threshold of the Sable Gate with dragon-fire screaming through the pass like a living thing, Corvane was cold for the first time he could remember.

It was not the mountain air. It was the fire itself. He could feel it from thirty paces — a heat that did not warm but *took*, pulling warmth out of him the way a debt collector pulls coin, and leaving behind something else. Something bright and hollow and hungry.

*Good,* he thought. *Something that takes is something that can be taken.*

The gatehouse was ruins now. The keystone — the great black wedge of stone that had held the covenant for three hundred years — hung cracked down its center, weeping light. Through the breach poured the raw stuff of the Emberlands: not flame exactly, though it burned; not wind, though it moved; a current of gold and white that ran along the broken road like water finding its old riverbed. Where it touched stone, the stone glowed and remembered being magma. Where it touched snow, the snow screamed into steam.

His soldiers had stopped at the tree line. Sensible of them. Cowardly, too, but sensibility and cowardice were often the same animal wearing different coats.

Corvane walked forward alone.

Behind him, the army he had marched up Thornfell — four thousand men, siege engines, alchemists' wagons, the whole grinding apparatus of Crown ambition — waited in the dark like a dog told to stay. He had promised them glory. He had promised them fire they could hold in their hands and point at enemies. He had promised them a new age in which the realm would never again beg dragons for warmth or cower behind a keeper's gate.

He had not promised them truth. Truth was a currency he spent carefully, and only on himself.

The heat thickened as he climbed the ruined approach. His boots smoked at the soles. The hair on his forearms curled and blackened, and he watched it happen with the detached interest of a man reading someone else's letter. Pain came with it — real pain, a bright wire drawn through his skin — and he catalogued it, filed it, set it aside. Pain was information. Information was power. Therefore pain was power, improperly understood by everyone who had ever flinched from it.

At the edge of the breach he stopped.

The fire ran past him in a broad river, close enough to touch. Up close it had structure — threads within threads, a braid of gold around a core of white, and beneath both something darker, an ember-red pulse like a heartbeat slowed to the pace of mountains. This was what the Okafors had leashed. This was what three hundred years of keepers had died rationing out to a grateful, ignorant realm: warmth for hearths, fire for forges, a trickle when the covenant allowed a trickle.

And they had called it *keeping*. As if power were a thing to be kept rather than used.

Corvane knelt at the river's edge the way other men knelt at altars.

He had read the confiscated texts. He knew what the old alchemists claimed — that dragon-fire was not fuel but will, that it carried the dragon inside it the way blood carries a self, and that to drink it raw was to invite another soul into your house and hope yours was the stronger tenant. The alchemists had all died. Every one. Their notes ended mid-sentence, their pages scorched from within.

Corvane had read those notes twice and found them encouraging. Men who feared the fire were already half-consumed by fear. Fear made room. He had no room. He had spent forty years filling every chamber of himself with purpose until there was nothing left loose enough to burn.

He cupped his hands and dipped them into the river of fire.

It should have taken his hands off at the wrist. He understood that even as he did it — understood it the way you understand the height of a cliff only when your toes are over the edge. The fire closed around his fingers and instead of destroying them it *entered*, pouring up through his palms, into his wrists, his arms, his chest, and Corvane threw back his head and drank it as it came, mouth open, throat working, swallowing light.

For one perfect moment there was nothing in the world but him and the fire, and the fire was saying yes.

* * *

Later — minutes later, hours, he would never be able to say — he stood at the center of the breach with his arms spread and the fire running under his skin like veins of ore in dark stone.

He could see everything.

That was the first gift, and it nearly undid him. He could see the pass below, every soldier, every wagon, every man's heartbeat flickering in his chest like a candle seen through cloth. He could see up the mountain to the lair where the last dragon lay wounded, a vast dimming furnace wrapped around a wound shaped like a keystone. He could see through the broken gate into the Emberlands themselves — valleys going grey with frost at their edges while their centers still smoldered, green things dying between two extremes, a realm caught between its own extinguishing and its own excess.

He could see the girl.

Ada Okafor was running along the ridge above the pass, small and fast and lit from within by a purpose he recognized because it had once been his own reflection. Beside her, limping, bleeding through a shoulder dressing, went the smuggler Ryn Vasq — a name Corvane had bought and sold a dozen times through intermediaries, a thread he'd pulled expecting a garment and found instead a knot. And behind them, walking with the unhurried gait of a man who had stopped pretending to be their enemy, came Inquisitor Halvard Brann, carrying a book under his arm like a relic.

Corvane watched them and felt — distantly, from very far away, as if reported by a subordinate — anger.

They thought the story was theirs. The keeper, the smuggler, the doubting priest. They thought the prophecy was about them, that the gate was theirs to break or reforge, that history would remember their names in its margins. He almost pitied them. They had spent these weeks learning the truth of the covenant, and truths, however large, were still only furniture. What mattered was who sat in the chair.

He sat in the chair now. He could feel it: the fire answering him, pooling in his chest when he called it, rising to his eyes when he willed it. He raised one hand and a lance of gold leapt from his palm and struck a dead pine two hundred yards away, which detonated into embers with a sound like applause.

Yes. Like applause.

*You see?* he said — aloud, to the fire, to the world, to whatever tenanted the fire besides himself. *This is what keeping is for.*

The fire said nothing. But it moved in him, and its movement had a quality he chose not to examine — a slow turning, like something settling deeper into a bed it intended to keep.

* * *

By the time his officers found him, Corvane had remade the breach into a throne room.

Not deliberately — or not only deliberately. The fire leaked from him now whenever his attention slipped, and where it leaked, stone flowed. The rubble of the gatehouse had risen and smoothed into columns, into steps, into a dais of black glass at the exact center of the broken keystone's shadow. He stood on the dais and let them look at him.

Colonel Merrick stopped at the base of the steps. The man had served him eleven years and had a face built for reporting bad weather. Tonight the face was doing something Corvane had never seen it do. It was afraid of *him*.

"General." Merrick's voice was steady. His knees were not; Corvane could see the tremor, could see the pulse hammering in the man's throat, could see — this was new, this was wonderful and terrible — the faint gold shimmer of terror-heat rising off human skin. "The men are asking for orders. The breach is... the breach is unstable. Alchemists are saying the whole pass could—"

"The pass will do what I tell it to do."

Merrick blinked. "Sir?"

Corvane descended one step. Light came off him. He could feel it coming off him, a radiance that had nothing to do with warmth — the soldiers nearest the ruin had begun to call it that among themselves, he learned later: *the Radiant General*, whispered the way you whisper about saints or plagues. "Colonel. Look at me."

Merrick looked. It cost him.

"What did the Crown send us here to take?" Corvane asked.

"The gate, sir. Control of the gate."

"And what is the gate?" He spread his arms, and the fire in his veins answered, and the air around him bent with heat-shimmer. "A leash. A leash on *this*. Three centuries of Okafors holding the leash and metering it out — a hearth-fire here, a forge-flame there, the realm kept warm the way you keep a beggar warm. Enough to survive. Never enough to *rule*." He came down another step. Stone softened under his boots and hardened again, printed with his tread. "I have drunk the source, Colonel. Not the trickle. The river. And I am still standing."

Merrick's throat moved. "The alchemists said the others who tried—"

"The others wanted power." Corvane smiled, and was aware even as he did it that the smile showed too much light behind his teeth. "Wanting is a hunger, and hunger can be fed on. I don't want power, Colonel. I *am* the power now. There is a difference, and the difference is why I am alive and they are ash."

He believed it completely. That was the marvel of it — belief arrived whole, unasked, the way it always had. Somewhere beneath the radiance a small cold clerk of a mind noted that his hands had begun to glow even at rest, that the skin of his forearms had taken on a faint crystalline texture like cooling slag, that when he exhaled the breath came out faintly golden. The clerk wrote it all down and filed it under *later* and was, as always, ignored.

"New orders," Corvane said. "First: the Okafor woman. Alive. She knows the covenant's terms better than any text, and I want her to watch what replaces it. Second: the dragon. If it crawls to the gate — and it will, it's bound to the keystone, it has nowhere else to die — do not engage. Report to me. Third—" he paused, savoring it, "—send riders to the border villages. Tell them the fires come back tomorrow. All of them. Full flame, no rationing, no keeper's tithe. Tell them General Corvane gives freely what the Okafors sold."

Merrick hesitated. "Freely, sir?"

"Freely." The word rang in Corvane's own ears like a struck bell, and he loved it. Generosity at scale was only dominion with better tailoring. Let the villages love him. Let the realm love him. Love was the strongest chain ever forged, and unlike the Okafors' leash, it required no maintenance, no sacrifice, no daughter of the line bleeding on a keystone. Only more. Always more.

"Go," he said, and Merrick went, at a pace just short of running.

* * *

Alone again on his dais, Corvane turned inward and took inventory.

The fire filled him to the sternum now — he could feel its level the way a man feels wine in a cask, by knock and echo. Below the sternum there was still Corvane: the ambition, the certainty, the forty years of disciplined self. Above, the fire. Between them, a boundary that had been crisp an hour ago and was now, he noticed, less so. A softness at the margin. Places where a memory of Kestrelmoor — the frozen harbor, the dead horses standing upright in ice — surfaced already rimmed in gold, as if the fire had gotten to it first and gilded it.

He examined the phenomenon with clinical interest and concluded it was adaptation. The house was being renovated to suit its new tenant. Reasonable. Efficient. He was, after all, giving the fire what no vessel had ever given it: direction. Purpose. The dragons had hoarded their fire out of instinct and vanity. The Okafors had imprisoned it out of guilt. He would *use* it, and use was the only worship power had ever respected.

Still. There was a moment — brief, and he permitted it only because no one was watching — when he reached for a specific memory to check it. His mother's kitchen, in the garrison town where he'd grown up poor and furious. The smell of coal smoke and bread. He had not thought of that kitchen in twenty years.

He found the place where it should have been and it was there, but wrong — rendered in gold leaf, every detail luminous and perfect and somehow weightless, a painting of a kitchen rather than a kitchen. The smell was gone. The cold draft under the door was gone. The fury he'd carried out of that room, the engine of everything he'd become — gone, or gilded over, indistinguishable from gone.

The fire had eaten it. Or worn it smooth. Or perhaps he had simply outgrown it, the way one outgrows a coat.

*Yes,* he decided. *Outgrown.* The clerk in the cellar wrote something down. Corvane did not read it.

Above the pass, thunder rolled without clouds. He looked up and saw the dragon.

Vessarath came slowly down the mountainside, and even dying, even dragging a wound the size of a cathedral door across the rock, the creature was the largest true thing Corvane had ever seen — larger than the fire, larger than the crown, larger than the idea of either. Its scales had gone dull as old coins. Its breath plumed red and thin. And its eyes, fixed on the broken gate, on the keystone, on the small burning figure standing in the keystone's shadow, held an expression Corvane recognized with a jolt because he had seen it in mirrors all his life.

Ownership. Grief about ownership. The particular rage of a proprietor watching a thief walk out with the estate.

"You," the dragon said. Its voice came through the ground, through the stone, through Corvane's own borrowed fire, resonant as a bell struck in the chest. "Little thief. You drink what you cannot carry."

"I carry it fine," Corvane said, and lifted his hand, and drew a whip of gold fire out of the air, and it *obeyed*, coiling and snapping at his command, and the joy of that obedience was so total it blotted out the small cold clerk forever.

The dragon's head lowered until its ancient eye was level with him, near enough that Corvane could see his own reflection in it — radiant, monstrous, magnificent — and behind the reflection, deep in the black well of the pupil, something else. Something patient.

"The fire remembers every mouth," Vessarath said. "Yours it will remember longest."

Then the dragon turned, deliberately, ignoring him — the ultimate insult, a king declining to duel a pretender — and began the last crawl toward the shattered keystone, toward the wound-shaped hole in the world, toward the woman running along the ridge with a book in her hands and her ancestor's confession in her voice.

Corvane watched it go and felt the fire rise in him like a tide answering a moon, and he understood suddenly, clearly, with the last of the clarity he would ever have, what he had to do.

If the dragon reached the gate, the story would belong to the dragon and the girl and the doubting priest. If the fire was to have a master, the master must be chosen *now*, before the old order's ghosts finished their dying work.

He stepped down from the dais. The pass lit up around him, column after column of golden light, and four thousand men below saw their Radiant General descend from the broken gate wreathed in living sun, and many of them fell to their knees, and he blessed them absently with a raised hand as he passed, already climbing, already burning, already reaching for the sky.

Above him, wings unfolded with a sound like sails in a storm.

Vessarath rose off the mountainside to meet him.
