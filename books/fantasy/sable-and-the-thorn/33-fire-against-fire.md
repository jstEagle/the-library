## 33. Fire Against Fire

The keystone was still warm.

Ada felt it through her gloves as she climbed the shattered stair of the gatehouse — a heat like a sleeping animal's flank, pulsing in time with something far below the mountain's skin. Above her, through the ragged hole where the roof had been, the sky was wrong. Smoke moved against the wind. Embers drifted upward instead of down.

And Vessarath was screaming.

She had never heard him scream before. In dreams he had been vast and slow and sorrowful, a voice like a landslide deciding to be patient. This was nothing patient. This was a sound that peeled frost off stone three valleys away, that made the broken gate's remaining arches ring like struck bells.

Ada reached the top of the stair and looked out over the ruin of everything she had guarded for twenty years, and saw the dragon and the man turning above it like two storms learning each other's names.

Corvane had been human once. She could see what remained of that — the general's coat, scorched but recognizable, the epaulettes still bright where fire hadn't reached them. But the rest of him burned from within. Light poured out of his mouth when he opened it. His eyes were two white suns, and his shadow on the clouds below was not a man's shadow at all but something branching, antlered, spreading — fire given ambition and a rank.

He rose on wings of his own making now, wings of hard light and cinder, and every beat of them sent a shockwave across the pass that knocked loose stones from walls that had stood four hundred years.

*You should have taken my bargain,* Corvane had told her, an hour and a lifetime ago, standing in the wreckage of the gatehouse with Halvard Brann's hand still on her shoulder and the confession still ringing in the air between armies. *Truth is a fine weapon, Keeper. But weapons need hands. I am offering to be yours.*

Now his hands were full of dragon-fire, and he was trying to take the rest.

Vessarath met him in the air, and the collision lit the mountainside like dawn arriving early.

Ada didn't watch. That was the discipline of it — the old keeper's discipline, the one thing left of the woman she had been. She made herself turn away from the battle, because the battle was not hers. The keystone was.

It lay half-buried in rubble at the heart of the broken gate, where it had fallen when Corvane's sappers cracked the arch. A block of black stone the size of a coffin lid, veined with lines of dull red light that pulsed — slower now than they had pulsed this morning, slower than they had pulsed all century. The wound in Vessarath's chest, she knew, looked exactly like this. Keystone-shaped. Covenant-shaped. The leash and the collar were one object seen from two sides.

She slid down the rubble on her heels, boots skidding, and landed beside it with her palms flat on its surface before she could think better of touching it.

The stone spoke to her the way the gate always had — not in words, in pressure. A weight behind the eyes. A taste like iron and snowmelt. And beneath it, faint and failing, the counting voice she'd heard the first night the gate flared: Old Emberish numbers, descending, patient even now.

*Nineteen. Eighteen.*

Not counting down to destruction, she understood suddenly. Counting down to *choice*. To the moment when the old covenant would be fully dead and whatever came next would have to be written by someone standing here, in blood, in truth.

That someone was supposed to be her.

"Then stop stalling," she said aloud, to herself, to the stone, to the ancestor whose confession she had read aloud to two armies an hour ago until their certainty curdled into shame. "Do it."

She drew the knife.

It was Ryn's knife — he'd pressed it into her hand at the threshold below, before going back to hold the line beside Brann, and he hadn't asked what for, because Ryn Vasq had finally learned there were things smugglers didn't ask. The blade was cheap steel, nicked from years of honest dishonesty, and it was the truest thing she'd held all day.

She set the edge against her palm.

Above her, the sky tore open.

* * *

She heard it more than saw it: a sound like a mountain being unmade politely, and then heat on the back of her neck so fierce it raised blisters before she could flinch. Ada threw herself sideways off the keystone as a lance of white fire struck where she'd knelt and turned six tons of rubble into glass.

Corvane descended through his own smoke, wings folding, light streaming from him in ribbons. He landed on the broken arch above the keystone and stood there, balanced on four-hundred-year-old masonry that groaned under a weight it was never built for — because it wasn't really weight anymore, was it. It was will. He had drunk raw dragon-fire in the heart of the breach, and the fire had agreed to carry him, the way fire agrees to anything that feeds it.

"Keeper." His voice came out layered now, his own baritone under something older and hungrier, a chorus pretending to be a man. "I watched you read that confession. Very moving. Two armies weeping on cue." He tilted his head, and the gesture was almost human, which made it worse. "But you misunderstand what you found. You think truth ends the covenant. Truth is just the demolition permit. Something has to govern the fire after. Why not a man who understands it?"

"You don't understand it," Ada said. She got to her feet slowly, keeping the knife low, keeping her body between him and the stone. "You're drinking it. There's a difference."

"I am *becoming* it." He spread his arms, and the light in him surged, and somewhere above, Vessarath roared and dove. "The Crown wanted a weapon. I am giving them the only honest version — me. One vessel. One will. No more treaties, no more keepers, no more dragons bleeding out on altars so farmers can grow wheat. Just fire, and the man who holds it."

Vessarath hit him like a falling cathedral.

Ada was already running when the shockwave hit — she felt it pick her up and throw her ten feet down the slope of rubble, shoulder cracking against stone, and she rolled and came up coughing dust and kept running, because the fight above was not hers, the fight above was the distraction, and every second Corvane spent on the dragon was a second the keystone waited unwritten.

She scrambled back up. Her left arm hung wrong at the wrist; she bound it against her chest with a strip torn from her coat, teeth and one hand, and put the knife in her right.

Above, the two burning shapes wheeled apart and struck again. Vessarath was old and wounded and heavy with grief, and Corvane was young and mad and made of stolen vigor, and they were evenly matched in the way that meant both of them would die of it. Each time they collided, embers rained across the pass, and each rain fell a little wider, a little closer to the green valleys beyond Thornfell where Emberfolk villages huddled under frost that had no business existing in summer.

*Seventeen,* said the stone beneath her. *Sixteen.*

"No," Ada whispered. "You wait for me."

She knelt. She set the knife against her palm again, and this time she did not hesitate, because hesitation was a luxury bought by the old covenant and she was done buying anything on credit.

The cut was clean. Blood welled dark and ordinary across her hand — no glow, no magic, just a woman's blood, Okafor blood, the blood of forgers and keepers and liars, four generations deep. She pressed her palm to the keystone.

The stone drank.

She felt it happen — felt her blood run the old veins of the covenant like water finding an old channel, felt the gate's dying pulse quicken toward her, hungry, grateful, *expectant*. This was the mechanism. This was what her ancestor had built: a lock that opened for Okafor blood freely spilled, a sacrifice renewed each generation, a leash that ran through her family's veins into a dragon's chest. The keystone wanted her death. It had always wanted a keeper's death. It was reaching for it now with something like tenderness.

*No,* she told it, and pushed back. *New terms.*

The stone resisted. Of course it did. Four hundred years of habit, of blood-price, of the tidy arithmetic that had let two realms sleep at night while one dragon bled. The covenant did not want rewriting. Covenants never do.

Above her, Vessarath screamed again — shorter this time, wetter, and Ada looked up despite herself and saw why. Corvane had caught the dragon's wing in hands of hardened light and was *pulling*, and the membrane tore with a sound like a sail dying, and the great shape staggered in the air, lost height, crashed onto the far wall of the pass in an avalanche of its own.

"No," Ada breathed. "Get up. Get *up*—"

Corvane turned. He looked down at her kneeling on the keystone with her bleeding hand, and the twin suns of his eyes narrowed, and he smiled with a mouth full of noon.

"There she is," he said. "The last Okafor. Do you know what you're worth, Keeper? Your blood on that stone could reforge the whole old machine. Your death, freely given — the prophecy's first ending. The dragons live, the gate stands, and the Crown spends the next century hunting your successors." He began to descend, unhurried, a king coming down from a balcony. "Or you die anyway, and I take the stone, and I write the terms myself. Either way, Keeper, the arithmetic ends tonight. The only question is who holds the pen."

"The pen," Ada said, "is mine."

She slashed her other palm open on the knife's edge and slammed both hands down on the keystone, and *wrote*.

Not words. Not yet. She wrote in the only language the covenant respected — truth, given freely, priced honestly. *No blood-price.* The stone shuddered under her hands. *No leash.* The veins of red light flickered like a candle in wind. *Free passage. Mutual keeping. Both realms or neither.* The keystone screamed — actually screamed, a thin mineral shriek that cracked the rubble around it — and the counting voice stumbled, lost its place, began again from a number she couldn't hear.

Corvane hit the ground twenty feet from her, and the impact was thunder.

"That," he said, "is enough."

He crossed the distance in two strides that didn't touch the ground, and his hand closed around her throat, and he lifted her off the keystone like she weighed nothing, because to him now she weighed nothing, because he was mostly fire and fire does not respect mass.

Ada's feet dangled. His grip was not crushing — that was the horror of it, the control of it. He held her the way a man holds a document he intends to read carefully.

"You've read the confession," he said quietly. The chorus-voice had dropped away; this was just Corvane, General Corvane, the Crown's most sincere servant, and his sincerity had always been the most frightening thing about him. "You know your family forged the covenant through betrayal. You know every peace you ever kept was built on a theft. So tell me, Ada Okafor — what is your new covenant worth? What is truth worth, written by the daughter of thieves?"

Her vision was graying at the edges. Through it she saw movement on the far wall — a vast head lifting, one eye finding her, ancient and dimming.

"It's worth," Ada rasped, "what it costs."

And she drove Ryn's cheap, nicked, honest knife up under Corvane's ribs.

It shouldn't have worked. He was mostly fire; steel should have passed through him like a finger through smoke. But the knife had her blood on it — Okafor blood, forger's blood, the one lineage the covenant's magic had ever obeyed — and where the blade entered, the light in him guttered like a lamp denied oil.

Corvane looked down at it. Looked at her. For one moment the twin suns of his eyes flickered, and behind them, briefly, she saw the man — tired, ambitious, genuinely certain he was saving everyone — and she felt something adjacent to pity.

"You can't kill fire," he said.

"No," Ada agreed. "But I can evict it."

She twisted the knife and spoke the terms into his face, close enough that her breath moved the light pouring from his lips: *"Mutual keeping. Both realms or neither. No vessel. No crown. No you."*

The fire in Corvane considered this.

Fire, she was learning, was honest in the way water is honest. It went where it was allowed. For four hundred years the covenant had allowed it one channel — a dragon's chest, a keeper's blood, a leash. Corvane had offered it a second channel, and it had poured in gladly, because fire is not loyal. And now a third channel had opened, written in the blood the old magic trusted most, and the terms were simple, and fire does not argue with terms.

The light went out of him from the inside.

What collapsed at Ada's feet was a man again — a scorched, emptied, mortal man in a ruined general's coat, steam rising off him in the cold air, his eyes ordinary brown and very wide. He was breathing. Barely. The fire had not killed him; it had simply stopped believing in him, and without it he was only flesh that had flown too high.

Ada stood over him, shaking, both palms sliced open, blood dripping onto the keystone in slow ordinary drops.

Above the pass, a shadow blotted the stars.

Vessarath came down out of the smoke on one good wing, trailing fire and blood, and landed on the broken gate with a delicacy that enormous things only manage at the end of everything. His chest wound glowed in the shape of the keystone, and the glow was fading — not dying, she realized. *Steadying.* Finding a level. Somewhere in the Emberlands, she knew, frost was stopping its advance. Somewhere, green was holding.

The dragon lowered his head until his eye was level with hers. Up close, the eye was a horizon of gold with a storm in it.

*Daughter of the forger,* he said, in the dream-voice that needed no air. *Daughter of the truth-teller. Finish it.*

Ada turned back to the keystone. The counting voice had gone silent at last. The stone waited, warm under her ruined hands, empty of terms for the first time in four hundred years — a page, not a lock.

She pressed her bleeding palms flat against it and began to write.

*Let it be known, in both tongues, by blood and by fire—*

Behind her, at the threshold of the broken gate, she heard running footsteps — Ryn's voice shouting her name, hoarse with fear, and Brann's deeper voice calling for a medic, and the small sounds of an army discovering it had lost its god.

Ada did not look up.

The stone drank her words, and the first light of the new covenant rose along its veins like dawn along a mountain ridge — not red, not the old covenant's hungry crimson, but a color she had no name for yet, gold shot through with something cooler, patient, shared.

The keystone began, gently, to sing.

* * *

She was still writing when the singing changed key.

Ada noticed it the way you notice a room going cold — a wrongness in the bones before the mind catches up. The new covenant's light, which had been rising steady and warm through the stone, stuttered. Dimmed. And beneath her palms, faint but unmistakable, the keystone's pulse skipped — once, twice — like a heart remembering it was mortal.

*Terms require a witness,* said a voice that was not Vessarath's and not the old counting voice and not her own. It came from everywhere the new light touched. It sounded almost sorry. *A witness bound to both realms. That is the price of truth freely written, daughter of the forger. The covenant must live in someone.*

The light along the veins turned toward her.

Ada's blood ran cold in her opened hands, and she understood, all at once and completely, what she had written — and what writing it would cost.

Below her, Ryn was climbing the rubble toward her, reaching out his hand, shouting something she could no longer quite hear.
