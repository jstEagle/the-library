## 36. Fall of Corvane

Halvard Brann had spent thirty years believing that the worst thing a man could witness was his own king's army doing something unforgivable. He had been wrong. The worst thing was watching it fail.

Corvane hung above the broken gate like a second sun, and he was screaming.

The general had drunk the fire — Halvard had seen it happen, had stood close enough to feel the heat of it, raw dragon-flame poured down a man's throat from a cracked keystone like wine from a communion cup. For a moment Corvane had glowed with such terrible beauty that half the soldiers on the pass had dropped to their knees, thinking they were looking at a god. Halvard had not knelt. He had seen what lived inside the glow: a man coming apart at every seam, power filling him faster than flesh could hold, light leaking out of his eyes and the corners of his mouth and the split skin of his forearms.

Now the light was guttering.

It happened slowly enough to watch. Corvane rose another dozen feet on wings of pure flame — wings he did not have, that the fire had made for him out of its own memory of dragons — and then the flames thinned, went from white to gold to a sullen orange, and the scream changed pitch. It stopped sounding like triumph. It started sounding like a man discovering, inch by inch, that the thing inside him was eating him instead of the other way around.

"He's burning out," Ryn Vasq said beside him. The smuggler's shoulder was still bandaged where the crossbow bolt had gone through it three days ago; he held his sword in his left hand, badly, and didn't seem to care. "The covenant's terms. She rewrote them. There's no leash anymore — nothing holding the fire in a shape a man can wear."

Halvard understood. That was the horror of it. He understood exactly, because he had read the confession, had sat alone with Ada Okafor's ancestor's words carved in stone and copied them into his own hand by candlelight while the army marched, and he knew what the old covenant had been. A container. A set of terms so precise, so ruthlessly negotiated across blood and betrayal, that dragon-fire could be bound into human uses without consuming the human who used it. The Okafors hadn't stolen fire. They'd built a stove around it, and paid for the stove in their own veins, generation after generation.

And Ada had torn the stove open and written new terms in her own blood — *no blood-price, no leash* — which meant there was no longer any sanctioned channel through which a man could take the fire and survive.

Corvane had tried anyway. Of course he had. Men like Corvane always tried anyway.

"Can we help him?" asked Sergeant Iles, young, pale, still holding a shield he'd raised against a firestorm that never came.

"No," Halvard said. It came out flat and final, and he hated how easily it came. "No one can."

Above them, Corvane's flame-wings folded. He began to fall.

* * *

He fell like a star, trailing embers, and the pass lit up gold and terrible, and every soldier of the Crown — four hundred men who had marched on Thornfell behind sealed orders and a general's promises — watched their commander come down out of the sky.

He hit the scree slope below the broken gate and the impact rang off the mountains like a bell struck too hard. Fire burst outward from him in a ring, scorched the stone black in a perfect circle, and then — this was the part Halvard would carry to his grave — the fire *came back*. Drew itself inward, back into the body lying broken among the rocks, as if the terms of the new covenant were reclaiming what no one had the right to hold.

When the light faded, Corvane was just a man. A burned man. His uniform was charred to lace. His hands were blackened claws. But he was breathing, raggedly, wetly, and when Halvard reached him first — because of course he reached him first, because thirty years of service dies hard even when you've already decided the service was built on a lie — the general's eyes found him and focused.

"Brann." The voice was ash and gravel. "Report."

Halvard almost laughed. Almost. What came out instead was quieter than he intended. "You're relieved, sir."

"I gave the order to take the gate—"

"The gate is taken. Or rather—" Halvard looked up at the ruin of it, the keystone shattered, the arch half-collapsed, dragon-fire still threading through the cracks in slow luminous rivers, no longer screaming but singing, low and steady, like a hearth after a storm. "Or rather it's gone. And we're standing in the middle of two realms that are watching us decide what kind of men we are."

Corvane coughed. Blood came up with it, steaming faintly. "The fire," he whispered. "I had it. I felt everything. Every valley. Every border. I could have held both realms in one hand—"

"Yes," Halvard said. "That was the problem."

He crouched, took off his own cloak, and laid it over the dying man, because whatever else Corvane was, he was the Crown's, and Halvard was not yet ready to stop being the Crown's in front of four hundred witnesses. Then he stood, turned, and faced the pass.

Both armies were there. That was the thing no chronicle would ever quite capture — not Crown soldiers facing Emberfolk warriors, but both of them mixed together now, scattered along the broken road, weapons lowered or dropped entirely, everyone staring at the same impossible sight:

Vessarath was coming down the mountain.

* * *

The last dragon crawled.

That was the detail that broke something open in Halvard's chest and let the terror drain out through the crack. Vessarath — the name Ada had spoken like a prayer and a wound — was ancient beyond reckoning, vast as a chapel, and he was dragging himself down the scree on limbs that shook, one slow yard at a time, because the wound in his chest was shaped exactly like the Sable Gate's keystone and he had carried it for a hundred years and he refused, absolutely refused, to die before he saw what the daughter of the forger had done.

Soldiers scrambled out of his path. Some of them screamed. One young lieutenant drew his sword on reflex and Halvard put a hand on the blade and pushed it down without ever taking his eyes off the dragon.

"Not today," he said. "Not ever, if I have anything to say about it."

Vessarath's head swung toward him. One eye, molten amber, large as a cart wheel, regarded him with an intelligence so old it made Halvard feel like an insect that had learned to write.

"You are the one called Brann," the dragon said. His voice was thunder heard through stone, deep enough to vibrate in the ribs. "The hunter who became a reader."

"I read your history, lord. What there was left of it after the Crown got finished editing it."

A sound came out of the dragon that might have been laughter, if laughter could shake loose rock from a cliff face. "Then you know what stands here. Not a beast. A signatory. The last living hand that pressed its mark to the covenant your masters' masters broke."

"I know," Halvard said. "I know all of it now. That's rather the problem with me lately."

Vessarath dragged himself the last few yards and settled beside the shattered gate, folding his ruined wings, and the fire threaded through the broken keystone seemed to lean toward him, streams of light bending like grass toward the sun. Where the light touched his chest-wound, the raw edges eased. Halvard watched it happen and understood that the gate and the dragon had been one wound all along, split between flesh and stone by a clever, cruel, loving woman a century dead — and that Ada Okafor had just closed it from the middle.

The fire steadied. Everyone saw it. The frantic, screaming, realm-tearing blaze that had poured through the breach since midnight slowed, deepened, settled into a low golden pulse, steady as a heartbeat. Frost that had crept down the Emberlands' peaks began, very slowly, to retreat. Somewhere on the far slopes, Halvard later learned, green things stirred under snow that should have killed them.

"She wrote well," Vessarath said softly. "The daughter of the forger. No blood-price. No leash. Free passage and mutual keeping." He repeated the terms like a man tasting a meal he'd waited a lifetime for. "Four words fewer than the old covenant. Four words. And those four words were *the price of a keeper's life.*"

Ada Okafor stood at the base of the ruined arch, small and soot-streaked, her left palm wrapped in a strip of Ryn Vasq's shirt, still bleeding through. Halvard had watched her cut it. He had stood twenty feet away with a drawn sword and orders in his pocket to arrest her, and he had chosen — finally, irrevocably, with his whole career burning down around him like Corvane's borrowed wings — to stand aside.

She looked tired in a way that had nothing to do with the night. She looked like a woman who had set down something she'd carried so long she'd forgotten it wasn't part of her body.

"It's done," she said, to the dragon, to the armies, to the smoking air. "The covenant is rewritten. Both realms keep it or neither does. There's no keeper behind a gate anymore. There's just — the road. Open. For anyone willing to walk it honestly."

Silence, then. The long, ringing silence of several hundred people simultaneously realizing that the world had changed shape while they were busy trying to kill each other.

It was broken, absurdly, by Corvane.

* * *

The general had dragged himself upright against a boulder, the cloak pooled around him, and he was laughing. It was a horrible sound, wet and cracked, the laugh of a man whose ambition had eaten him down to the wick.

"A road," Corvane rasped. "She burns my career, kills my purpose, unmakes the weapon I drank a dragon's heart to hold — and gives the world a *road*. Merchants. Pilgrims. Smugglers." His eyes found Ryn Vasq and glittered with contempt. "You'll flood the passes with traders and call it peace."

"Yes," said Ada.

"And when the next war comes? When someone stronger than me looks at that open gate and wants what I wanted?"

"Then they'll burn out too," Ryn said. He slung his sword — the wrong-handed grip, the bad shoulder, all of it — and grinned with no humor in it at all. "Turns out the fire checks receipts now. You'd know, General. How's the stomach?"

Corvane's laugh died. He looked down at his own blackened hands, and whatever he saw there drained the last of the monstrousness out of him and left only a man, old and hollowed and suddenly very cold in the mountain wind. "I felt it," he said, quieter. To no one. To himself. "For nine minutes I held both realms, and it was the most beautiful thing I have ever known, and it was eating me alive the entire time, and I *kept drinking*." He looked up at Halvard, and his eyes were clear for the first time since Halvard had known him. "Is that ambition, Brann? Or is it just hunger wearing ambition's coat?"

"I don't know, sir," Halvard said honestly. "I think maybe it doesn't matter. I think maybe what matters is what you do in the next hour."

Corvane considered that. Above them the fire pulsed, steady, golden, patient. Down the pass, the Emberfolk delegation — the Council of Ash's survivors, ash-grey and wary — had come forward to stand near the dragon, and the Crown's officers had formed a rough line near their fallen general, and between the two groups lay forty feet of broken road that had never in a hundred years been neutral ground, and was now nothing else.

"Brann." Corvane fumbled at his belt with ruined fingers. "Come here."

Halvard went. Kneeling in the scorched circle, he took the general's seal — heavy gold, the Crown's double-thorn crest — from hands that could no longer close around it.

"Field command is yours," Corvane said. "Formally. Witnessed." His voice carried, deliberately, to the officers. "I am unfit. I am — I am *unfit*, gentlemen, and the record will show I said so myself, before anyone could make me say anything worse." A ghost of the old iron came back into his voice. "You will obey Brann. You will lower your arms. And you will *not* touch that gate, that dragon, or that woman, on pain of my considerable posthumous displeasure."

He sagged back against the boulder. Halvard stayed kneeling a moment longer.

"Why?" he asked, low. "You could have gone down swinging. History would have been kinder."

"History," Corvane said, "is written by whoever survives, and I intend to survive long enough to be honest about what I did. Someone has to be. Apparently it runs in the company I keep these days." He nodded past Halvard's shoulder — at Ada, at the dragon, at the confession Halvard had copied out by candlelight. "Read it to them, Brann. All of it. If we're going to be the first army in a century to stand down, let them know exactly what we stood down *from*."

* * *

They buried no one that day, which Halvard counted as the single miracle of his long, increasingly strange life.

By late afternoon the pass had transformed into something none of its chroniclers would have dared invent: Crown soldiers sharing hardtack with Emberfolk wardens; the Council of Ash's speaker and Sergeant Iles arguing over a map with the furious intensity of people discovering they agreed about more than they disagreed; Ryn Vasq somehow already conducting trade negotiations from a stretcher he refused to lie down on. And everywhere, threading through it all, the low golden pulse of the steadied fire, visible through the broken keystone like a heartbeat made public.

Halvard stood at the foot of the ruined arch with the confession in his hands — the original pages, returned to him by a dying peddler's legacy and a smuggler's stubbornness and a keeper's courage — and he read it aloud, all of it, the betrayal and the theft and the leash and the century of blood-price, while both realms listened and the wind carried his voice down the pass.

When he finished, Ada Okafor stepped up beside him. Her bandaged hand had stopped bleeding. She looked out at the crowd — at humans and Emberfolk standing closer together than they had in a hundred years — and then, unexpectedly, at him.

"Inquisitor Brann," she said. "You hunted me across Thornfell for a heretical book."

"I did."

"You burned the shrine trail. You cornered us at the Hollow Shrine. You offered me clemency in exchange for surrendering the truth."

"I did all of that," Halvard said. "I'd offer to apologize, but I suspect you'd find it insufficient, and you'd be right."

"I don't want an apology." She held out her unwounded hand. "I want a witness. The new covenant needs keeping, and I'm done being the only one who keeps things. You read the old history until you couldn't un-read it. That's the whole job now. Read. Witness. Tell the truth when it costs you." A tired, sharp-edged smile. "You're overqualified."

Halvard looked at the hand. At the dragon settling into the mountain's shadow like a cathedral coming to rest. At the four hundred soldiers who had followed him into heresy and were, at this moment, learning to eat lunch with the people they'd marched to kill.

He took her hand.

"Someone once told me," he said, "that the worst thing a man could witness was his own side doing something unforgivable." He squeezed, gently, mindful of the bandage. "I've revised it. The best thing a man can witness is the moment it stops being unforgivable. I intend to write that down. In triplicate."

Ada laughed — short, surprised, real — and above them the fire pulsed once, golden and steady, like approval.

Down the pass, where the last light of evening touched the broken gate, a figure detached itself from the Emberfolk delegation and began walking toward them: tall, robed in ash-grey, carrying a staff crowned with a shard of the old keystone. The Council of Ash's speaker wore an expression Halvard had learned to dread in thirty years of inquisition work.

It was the expression of someone who had read the new covenant very carefully — and found a clause they didn't like.

The speaker stopped ten feet away, planted the staff, and said, in accented but flawless Common:

"Keeper Okafor. We need to talk about what the magic took from you — and what it's going to ask for next."
