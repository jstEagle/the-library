## 37. Two Realms, One Road

Ryn Vasq had spent twenty years learning every way to cross Thornfell without being seen. Now he stood in the open middle of the pass, in full daylight, with a ledger under his arm and a dragon the size of a chapel watching him count crates.

It was the strangest morning of his life, and he'd once smuggled a live basilisk egg past two customs houses by telling the inspectors it was a very large cheese.

"Again," said Sefra, the Emberfolk envoy, her scales catching the light like hammered copper. "From the top. Slowly."

Ryn held up the first crate. "Dried fish. Smoked over alderwood, because your people told me last week that peat-smoke tastes like a funeral to you."

"It does taste like a funeral," Sefra agreed gravely.

"So. Alderwood." He set the crate on the flat stone that served as the trading table — an old boundary marker, worn smooth by centuries of weather and, apparently, by the occasional dragon using it as a chin-rest. Vessarath had done exactly that during the first negotiation, and nobody had had the courage to ask him to stop. The stone now bore a polished patch the size of a shield. Ryn privately thought of it as the dragon's signature.

"Next crate: iron nails, human-forged. Your smiths want them for the roof beams at Emberfall."

"We could forge our own."

"You could," Ryn said. "But you'd have to reopen the deep mines, and the deep mines are cold now, and your hatchlings are still getting their fire back. It's cheaper to buy ours. That's not charity. That's arithmetic."

Sefra's slit-pupiled eyes narrowed, but there was something almost like approval in them. "The council said you would try to cheat us."

"The council is right. I'm a smuggler. But I'm a smuggler who wants to keep doing this for thirty years, and you can't cheat someone twice if they've got wings and a grudge." He flipped the ledger open. "So I cheat fair. Everything's priced where both sides walk away annoyed. That's how you know it's honest."

Behind him, Ada made a sound that might have been a laugh strangled before it could escape.

He didn't turn around. He'd learned, over these three weeks of negotiations, that looking at Ada while she was trying to be official was dangerous — not because she'd reprimand him, but because she wouldn't, and then he'd lose the thread of whatever he was saying, and Sefra would notice, and dragons noticed everything.

"Grain next," he said. "Barley and winter wheat from the valley farms. And in exchange—" he turned the ledger toward her, "—emberglass shards. Small ones. The glasswrights in Lowmark will pay more for those than for anything else we carry, and half of what they make goes back up-mountain as windowpanes and lamp-lenses. Your valleys get light through the winter without burning fuel you don't have."

Sefra ran one clawed finger down the column of figures. Her talon left no mark; the ink seemed to dry faster under it, or maybe that was just the heat of her.

"The council will say the price of grain is too high."

"The council will be right. Tell them the price comes down a copper per bushel for every month we trade without incident. Call it a trust discount. It only ever moves in one direction."

"And if there *is* an incident?"

Ryn closed the ledger. "Then I'll have bigger problems than pricing."

* * *

The pass itself was unrecognizable, and that was the point of it.

Where the Sable Gate had stood — where its shattered keystone still lay in a tumbled heap that both realms had agreed, in the first treaty signed in a hundred years, would never be rebuilt — there was now simply road. Wide, graded, swept twice a week by work crews wearing mixed colors: Crown grey and Ember-copper, side by side, arguing constantly about whose method of sweeping was correct.

Ada had insisted on the sweeping. "A road that's maintained is a road that belongs to everyone," she'd said. "A road that's neglected belongs to whoever has the most soldiers." Brann, who had defected with a precision that still startled Ryn when he thought about it too long, had provided the crews. General Corvane's fall had left the Inquisition's border company leaderless, and Brann had walked into the wreckage and started picking up pieces, and to everyone's astonishment — including, Ryn suspected, his own — people had followed him.

Now Brann stood at the edge of the market square that was growing up around the old gatehouse ruins like moss after rain, watching Ryn negotiate, and pretending he wasn't watching.

Ryn finished with Sefra, sealed the day's tally with a thumbprint in wax — hers burned red-gold, his plain brown, pressed side by side — and wandered over.

"Inquisitor," he said.

"Not anymore." Brann's face did the thing it always did when someone used the old title: a flicker of pain, quickly filed away. "Reformer, apparently. It sounds less impressive in practice. Mostly it's paperwork."

"How's the paperwork?"

"Two petitions from valley lords demanding tolls on the pass. One petition from the Emberlands' council demanding the tolls be forbidden. A complaint that human children are teaching Emberfolk children a card game involving cheating." He paused. "That last one came from both sides simultaneously, each accusing the other of inventing the cheating."

"That game's older than either realm's borders," Ryn said. "Everyone cheats at it. That's how you know it's working."

Brann almost smiled. It was a strange sight, like frost forming in summer. "You're enjoying this."

Ryn looked out at the market. Stalls of canvas and hide, some pitched by humans, some by scaled folk who'd come down from the high valleys for the first time in living memory. An Emberfolk woman haggling over wool with a shepherd who kept forgetting to be afraid. Two kids — one human, one hatchling-scaled — chasing each other between the cart wheels, shrieking in a pidgin that hadn't existed six months ago.

"Yeah," Ryn said. "I am. Terrified the whole time. But enjoying it."

"That's what governing feels like," Brann said. "You get used to it. You don't get comfortable."

"I don't want to govern. I want to trade."

"They're the same thing with different ledgers." Brann turned back toward the command tent — the old gatehouse's surviving wall now flying a banner that was neither Crown nor Ember, just a plain white field with a black road stitched across it. "Your commission papers are ready, by the way. First sanctioned bridge-trader. Ada signed them this morning. She wanted me to tell you there's a clause about smuggling."

"There's always a clause about smuggling."

"This one says you're allowed to."

Ryn blinked. "Allowed to?"

"Within the terms of the new covenant. Free passage means free passage. If goods move honestly declared, neither realm may seize them." Brann's mouth twitched. "She wrote it herself. Took her four drafts. She kept trying to close loopholes and then remembering that closing loopholes was how the last covenant started."

* * *

He found her at dusk, up on the ridge above the pass, doing the thing she did now every evening: nothing.

That was the part Ryn still couldn't get used to. Ada Okafor, idle. Ada Okafor sitting on a rock with her hands empty, watching the light go gold and then violet over peaks that no longer needed guarding. The covenant's magic had taken her connection to Thornfell — taken the gate-sense, the constant low hum of the mountain's traffic and weather and mood that she'd carried since childhood — and what was left was a woman learning, at thirty-odd years old, how to be a person who wasn't a wall.

She heard him coming. Of course she did. Some things the magic hadn't needed to give her.

"Sefra signed?" she asked.

"Sefra signed. Trust discount and all."

"She'll regret it. The council will eat her alive over that grain price."

"Sefra can handle her council. She's got that look. Same look you used to have when pilgrims tried to argue gate-fees."

Ada huffed. Below them, the market was striking camp for the night — lanterns kindling along the stalls, emberglass lamps glowing among the copper-scaled folk, ordinary firelight everywhere else. Somewhere a fiddle had started up, and someone was attempting to teach a dance that required a tail, and failing cheerfully.

"Four drafts," Ryn said.

"Don't."

"Brann told me. Four drafts of the free-passage clause, because you kept—"

"*Don't.*"

"—because you kept writing it like a leash and then crossing it out." He sat down beside her on the rock. Close enough that their shoulders touched. She didn't move away, which after everything was its own kind of treaty. "For what it's worth, the fifth draft is good. Cleanest piece of law I've ever smuggled anything through."

"That's not a compliment to the law."

"It's absolutely a compliment to the law. Laws should be written by people who know how they break." He pulled the commission papers from his coat and looked at them without unfolding them. "First sanctioned bridge-trader. You know my crew's going to laugh themselves sick. Twenty years of running the pass in the dark, and now there's a *document.* With a *seal.*"

"Do you want to refuse it?"

"No." He said it fast enough that it surprised him. "No, I want it framed. I want it nailed to the mast of the first honest ship I ever sail. I want Corvane's ghost to choke on it." He turned the paper over in his hands. "It's just — strange. Being paid to do openly what I did in secret. Turns out I liked the secret part better than I admitted."

"Why?"

"Because in the dark, nobody could ask me to be responsible for anyone but myself." He watched a lantern-carrier pick her way down the switchback trail toward Emberfall village, her light bobbing like a low star. "Out here, every crate I price wrong is somebody's winter. Every deal I flub makes both sides trust each other less. There's nowhere to hide behind the next bend in the road, because I'm the one who mapped all the bends."

Ada was quiet for a while. When she spoke, her voice had the careful weight she saved for true things.

"When I rewrote the covenant, I thought the hard part would be the blood. Cutting my hand, standing at the keystone, saying the words while two armies watched." She flexed her fingers in her lap. "It wasn't. The hard part is every morning since. Waking up and not hearing the gate. Reaching for the mountain and finding only myself in there." A pause. "And then realizing that's what everyone else felt like the whole time. Everyone who isn't a keeper. Just... themselves, in the dark, guessing."

"That's called being a person. Terrible system. Nobody recommends it."

That earned him half a smile — real, tired, hers. Then it faded into something more serious, and she reached into her coat and drew out a folded page.

Ryn knew that page. Everyone on the pass knew about the prophecy book now; it sat in a case in the command tent, open to the rewritten final page, displayed like a wound that had healed into a scar. But this page was different — smaller, torn at the edge, the marginalia cramped and faded.

"What's that?"

"The third-path note. The margin writing that showed me the way out." She unfolded it carefully. "I've been re-reading it. Every night. And there's a line I missed, back when missing it didn't matter."

Ryn leaned in. The old script was thin and spidery, but the relevant words had been freshly circled in Ada's own ink:

*When the third path opens, a witness must bind the realms — and the binding will be tested before the first year turns.*

"Tested how?" Ryn asked.

"I don't know. That's the whole of it. No date, no name, no shape of the test." She refolded the page slowly. "Vessarath says prophecy-margin is like that. The seer writes what she sees, and what she sees is rarely the whole picture. But the first year turns at midwinter, Ryn. That's five weeks away."

"Five weeks." He did the arithmetic the way he did all arithmetic: in terms of cargo and roads. Five weeks of trade runs. Five weeks of the fragile new thing down there in the lantern-light, learning to stand. "And you think the test is coming *here?*"

"I think everything comes here. It always has." She looked out at the pass, at the road where the gate had been, at the two peoples below learning each other's names. "I broke the leash. I didn't break the story. Whatever the story wants next, it'll want it at the threshold."

Ryn folded his commission papers and put them away inside his coat, over his heart, where he kept the things he intended to survive for.

"Well," he said. "Good thing you know a trader. We're very good at moving things across borders before anyone knows they need moving."

"Is that an offer?"

"That's a contract. Witness and bridge-trader, jointly bound, until the first year turns." He stuck out his hand. "Terms negotiable. Cheating permitted, within reason."

Ada took his hand. Her grip was still a keeper's grip — strong, certain, built for holding doors shut against the dark. But when she squeezed, it wasn't a door she was holding.

Down in the pass, the fiddle changed tunes, and the lanterns burned steady, and somewhere far above them, on the highest peak, dragon-fire glowed against the gathering night like a promise that refused, stubbornly, to gutter out.

Neither of them saw the rider yet — the lone figure cresting the southern road at a gallop, Crown dispatch-case bouncing at his saddle-bow, carrying news from the capital that would reach the command tent by midnight and change the shape of everything.

But the mountain saw. And the mountain, these days, told no one — except, perhaps, in dreams.
