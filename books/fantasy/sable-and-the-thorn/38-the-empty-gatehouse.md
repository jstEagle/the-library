## 38. The Empty Gatehouse

She had meant to go in spring.

That was what she told herself through the winter, when the pass was snowed shut and the Emberfolk were still learning the shape of their own freedom, when every week brought a new delegation or a new quarrel or a new page of the true history to be argued over by candlelight. Spring, she said. When the roads clear. When there's time.

But the roads had cleared six weeks ago, and still she had not gone, and this morning Ryn set a pack by the door of the Emberfall inn without a word about it — just bread, cheese, a flask of plum brandy, her father's old oilcloth cloak folded on top — and looked at her with that flat smuggler patience that was somehow worse than nagging.

"You've been circling it for a month," he said. "Like a hawk that won't land."

"It's rubble."

"It's your whole life sitting in rubble." He shrugged, shouldering his own pack. "You don't leave a thing like that unvisited. It'll follow you."

So they went.

The road up Thornfell was one she could have walked blindfolded once. She knew every switchback by the sound its stones made underfoot, every stretch where the mountain shed scree in wet weather, every hollow where wind came off the high snowfields smelling of iron and cold. She had walked it ten thousand times as keeper of the Sable Gate: out to check the ward-stones, back with reports, out again because some pilgrim swore he'd seen lights on the ridge and someone had to look. The road had been an extension of her body. She had known it the way she knew her own pulse.

Now she walked it like any traveler, and it kept surprising her.

There was a bend past the second shrine where the path narrowed between two leaning slabs of granite, and for twenty years she had ducked her head there without thinking, and today she forgot, and cracked her brow hard enough to see sparks. Ryn caught her elbow before she staggered.

"Used to know that one," she muttered, pressing her palm to her forehead.

"You used to be listening to something else while you walked," Ryn said. "Now you're just walking."

She didn't answer. He was right, and they both knew it, and there was no kind way to say it plainer than he'd said it.

Because that was the thing. That was the thing she had not told anyone, not even him, though she suspected he'd guessed: since the night the gate shattered and she wrote the new covenant in her own blood on the broken keystone, she could no longer hear it. The gate. The mountain. The low continuous hum that had been underneath everything her entire life — the sound of the covenant holding, the sound of the leash drawn tight, the sound she had mistaken for the world's own voice until she was old enough to learn otherwise. Gone. In its place was ordinary silence, vast and clean and terrible, the silence of a world that did not need her to listen anymore.

She had thought she would rejoice. Some nights, lying awake in Emberfall while dragon-fire glowed steady and gold on the far peaks — *steady*, after all those years of watching it gutter — she did feel something close to joy. But joy, it turned out, was only half of it. The other half was grief, and the grief had teeth.

* * *

They crested the last shoulder of the pass in late morning, and there it was.

The Sable Gate had been a marvel once. Two towers flanking a span of black stone, the keystone carved with the signatories' names in Old Emberish, the whole structure humming with bound fire so that frost never touched it and travelers swore they felt warmer just passing beneath. Her grandfather had described it to her when she was small enough to sit on his knee: *the hinge of the world, little Ada, and we are the hand upon it.*

What remained was a wound in the mountain.

One tower still stood, sheared off at two-thirds height, its upper courses tumbled down the slope in a long grey tongue of rubble. The other was a foundation and a dream. The arch itself had come apart the night Corvane's forces breached the gatehouse — she remembered the crack running through the keystone like lightning frozen mid-strike, remembered dragon-fire screaming through the pass, remembered writing her name and her terms in blood while the world came apart around her. The keystone lay in three pieces near the base of the standing tower, and someone — pilgrims, probably, or Emberfolk crossing to trade — had laid wildflowers on it. They were weeks dead now, brown and papery, but they had been placed with care, in rows, like offerings at a grave.

Ada stood at the edge of the ruin for a long time without moving.

Ryn, to his credit, said nothing. He found a flat rock in the sun, sat down, unwrapped the bread, and made a show of being busy with lunch, giving her the ruin and the silence both.

She walked in alone.

Her boots crunched over glass — fused sand from the night the fire screamed through, the stone itself half-melted in places, vitrified into strange dark mirrors that threw her reflection back at her warped and wavering. Here was the floor of the gatehouse common room, where she had kept the ledger. There, the hearth she'd banked every night for nineteen years. A corner of the duty roster still clung to a charred wall, ink ghosted to nothing, the names of keepers dead and scattered unreadable. Her own handwriting had been on that roster once. She couldn't find it. Perhaps that was mercy.

She stopped in the middle of what had been the threshold.

This was the spot. Right here, between the doorposts, where every soul entering or leaving Thornfell had passed under her eye. She had stood watch here through blizzards and coronations' worth of boredom, through smugglers' bribes and pilgrims' prayers, through the long grey years when the covenant held and nobody thanked anybody for it. She had been good at it. She had been *very* good at it, and she had believed — right up until the peddler put that scorched book in her hands — that being good at it was the same as being right.

She closed her eyes and listened.

Nothing.

Not the hum. Not the deep note under the world, the covenant's leash-note, the sound she had slept inside of like a heartbeat inside a mother's chest. Not even the ordinary sounds a ruin makes — settling stone, wind through broken rooms. Or rather, yes, those sounds, but only those, only the mundane inventory of collapse, and beneath them the enormous plain silence of a mountain that no longer had a hinge in it.

*Say something,* she thought, and hated herself for thinking it. *You watched me my whole life. You counted down at me in Old Emberish. You flared and sang and warned and lied. Say something.*

Wind moved through the shattered archway. A loose shard of vitrified stone ticked against another in the breeze. Somewhere above, a raven commented on the morning.

That was all. That was everything. The gate was dead, and dead things do not speak, and she had killed it herself with an open hand and the truth, and she would do it again, and it *hurt*, and there was no word for a grief like this — grief for a thing that had enslaved two realms and also been the only constant of her life; grief for a self that had been built to serve it and no longer had a reason to exist.

She sat down on the broken threshold, in the exact center of where the wards used to sing, and she cried.

Not gracefully. Not the way she'd imagined it if she ever let herself imagine it — composed, brief, dignified, a keeper's farewell. She cried the way she had cried at eleven years old when her father died and the elders told her the blood had passed to her, that she was the Okafor heir now, that her childhood was a debt already spent. She cried with her arms around her knees, forehead on her knees, in the ruins of the only home she had ever known, and the mountain let her, indifferent and free.

* * *

When it was done — when there was nothing left in her but the hollowed-out quiet that comes after — Ryn was sitting beside her. She hadn't heard him cross the rubble. He handed her the flask without looking at her, which was the kindest thing anyone had done for her in months.

She drank. Plum brandy, cheap and fierce. It burned a small warm hole in the cold.

"I used to stand right here," she said finally. Her voice came out scraped. "Every night. I'd count them through. Smugglers, pilgrims, couriers. I had a system. Ledger left, ledger right, tally marks for anything I chose not to write down." She laughed, a dry broken sound. "I thought I was keeping the peace. Turns out I was keeping a prison, and I was the most faithful guard it ever had."

"You were a kid," Ryn said. "Raised into it. Same as me with the crew — first run across the pass at fourteen, didn't even ask what was in the crates. Nobody asks, in the family business. Asking is how you end up with choices, and choices get people hurt."

"And then one day you ask anyway."

"One day you ask anyway," he agreed. He turned the flask in his fingers. "And then you spend the rest of your life figuring out who you are when you're not the thing they built."

The wind came through the arch again, carrying the smell of snowmelt and, faintly, from the far side of the pass, the mineral tang of the Emberlands — sulfur and hot stone, the breath of a waking country. Dragon-fire glowed on the eastern peaks, steady as a lamp. She could see it from here, gold against the blue of afternoon, and it steadied something in her to look at. Vessarath lived. The flame held. Whatever else she had lost, that remained.

"Do you miss it?" she asked. "The old roads? Before?"

Ryn considered. He was a man who considered before answering; it was one of the things she had learned to trust about him, eventually, after the bolt and the confession and the long slow work of forgiveness.

"The old roads paid better," he said. "Quiet pass, one keeper who looked the other way for a fair cut, everyone knowing their lane. Simple." He glanced at her sideways. "And rotten to the bone, and built on a lie, and it froze villages and leashed the last dragon and ate a sacrifice every generation. So no. I don't miss it. But I understand missing it. Missing a thing and wanting it back aren't the same animal."

"No," Ada said. "They're not."

She got up. Her legs were stiff from the cold ground; she was not as young as the girl who had first stood this watch. She walked to the keystone, the three great pieces of it lying where they had fallen, and knelt, and brushed the dead flowers aside gently — someone had tended them; she would not disrespect that — and read the names.

Signatories. The forged ones. Her ancestor's hand was the deepest cut, gouged proud and certain into the black stone: **OKAFOR, KEEPER AND BINDER**. Below it, the dragon-name, VESSARATH, cut shallow, cut *later*, she realized — the strokes thinner, the angle wrong, added afterward by a claw or a desperate talon, a signature given under duress or given in hope and betrayed either way.

"We should raise a marker," she said. "Here. Truthful one. What this was, what it cost, who broke it and why. Pilgrims are already coming. Let them read the truth instead of kneeling to a pretty ruin."

"Who writes it?"

"I will." She traced the false signature with one finger, the groove cold under her skin. "In a new ledger. First entry."

Ryn nodded slowly. Then he cocked his head, the way he did when a route went quiet and his instincts pricked. "Speaking of ledgers. You hear that?"

She listened — the ordinary listening, the mortal kind, ears and air. Hoofbeats. Several horses, coming up the pass road at a pace too brisk for merchants and too orderly for bandits. She and Ryn exchanged a look and moved to the standing tower's stump, putting stone at their backs, and watched the road below.

Four riders. The lead banner was white and grey — not the Crown's red-and-gold, but the new device, the one Halvard Brann had registered with the provisional council after the fall: an open gate on a field of ash. His company. Reformed, renamed, sworn to the truth commission. And riding at its head, broad-shouldered and unhurried, a man she had once hunted her through these mountains and later stood beside her at the threshold while the world burned.

Brann reined in at the edge of the ruin and looked up at her among the broken stones. He looked older. They all did. But his eyes, when they found hers, held the same unsettling sincerity that had frightened her more than any warrant ever had — the sincerity of a man who had followed the evidence all the way down and refused to look away from what he found.

"Keeper Okafor," he called up. Then he corrected himself, deliberately, formally, and the correction landed in her chest like a bell struck. "Mistress Okafor. Forgive the intrusion. We rode as soon as the council's messenger reached us."

"Reached you with what?" Ryn called down, hand drifting nowhere near his knife in a way that meant exactly the opposite.

Brann dismounted. He took a folded paper from inside his coat, and even at this distance Ada could see the council seal — wax pressed with the joined sigils of both realms, the new mark, the mark she had written into being with her own blood.

"The council has voted," Brann said. "They mean to rebuild the Sable Gate. Stone for stone, ward for ward — a monument, they're calling it. A symbol of the new age." He paused, and his jaw tightened. "And they mean to install a Keeper at it. Hereditary office, renewed covenant terms, ceremonial — but the title is the old title, and the oath is the old oath, and there was only one name on the ballot."

The wind moved through the empty arch behind her, through the doorway where she had stood ten thousand nights, and it sounded like nothing at all.

"They want you," Brann said simply. "They voted this morning. Ada Okafor, Keeper of the Sable Gate — for life."

* * *

She stood a long moment among the bones of her old life, the dead flowers at her feet, the silent mountain all around her, the paper with her name on it waiting in a honest man's hand.

Behind her, Ryn said quietly, "Well. There's your answer to walk home with."

Ada looked at the ruined arch, at the place where the hum used to live, and began to laugh — low, disbelieving, almost free.
