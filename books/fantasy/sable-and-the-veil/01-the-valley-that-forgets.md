## 1. The Valley That Forgets

The valley woke the way it always did: slowly, and without memory.

Juniper Brightwater knelt at the shrine with her palms flat against the cold stone, feeling for a warmth that had not come in three hundred days. The shrine was older than her grandmother, older than her grandmother's grandmother, older than any name anyone could still say without stumbling. It was a low ring of standing stones furred with moss, set into the hillside above the terraced fields, and at its center lay a sword — or what remained of one. Only the tang and a hand's length of blade jutted from the rock, rusted to the color of dried blood, worn smooth where generations of hands had touched it in passing.

No one touched it now but her.

"Good morning," she said to it, because her grandmother had taught her that courtesy costs nothing and buys everything. "Still here? Good. So am I."

The blade did not answer. It never answered anymore. But she felt something shift beneath her palms all the same — a faint tremor, like a sleeper turning over — and she pulled her hands back quickly, guiltily, as though she'd been caught eavesdropping on a prayer.

That was the trouble with the Veiled Valley. Everything here was listening, and nothing was permitted to speak.

She rose, brushed the dew from her knees, and began the morning rite. It was simple, as rites went: a circle walked sunwise around the stones, a handful of barley scattered at each of the seven markers, a wordless bow toward the east where the boundary stones stood pale in the early light. The words were supposed to be spoken aloud — her grandmother's voice still lived in her memory, low and resonant, reciting the Litany of Keeping while the whole valley seemed to lean in and listen — but Juniper said them only in her head, moving her lips without sound, because sound was dangerous, and silence was the law.

*We keep the quiet. We keep the peace. What sleeps below the stones may sleep.*

Her breath fogged in the autumn air. Below her, the valley unrolled in its patient patchwork: barley gold and going gray at the edges, apple orchards heavy with fruit no one would bother to harvest fully, the river Marrow running shallow and bright over its stones. Smoke rose from forty chimneys, thin and orderly. Somewhere a dog barked twice and thought better of it. The Veiled Valley did everything in moderation, including living.

It had not always been so. Her grandmother used to tell her — in whispers, always in whispers, with the shutters closed and the fire banked low — that once the valley had sung. Once there had been swordspeakers enough to fill a hall, women and men whose voices could shape steel like clay, who could bind a promise into a blade so deeply that breaking the promise broke the blade, and the bearer with it. Once the valley had been a place of power, and power had a name, and names were safe to say.

Then came the binding. Then came the Veil. Then came the forgetting, which was not an accident but a mercy, or a punishment, depending on which story you believed — and in the Veiled Valley, nobody believed anything loudly enough to find out.

Juniper finished the rite and stood a moment longer, looking at the rusted blade in the stone.

"I dreamed about her again," she told it quietly. Even this much felt transgressive, her voice alone up here with the wind. "Grandmother. She was standing at the boundary stones, and she kept trying to tell me something, and every time she opened her mouth the fog took the words. You'd think I'd stop dreaming it. You'd think three hundred days would be enough."

Three hundred days since Maren Brightwater had died in her sleep with her hand curled around the hilt of the blade she kept beneath her floorboards, the one Juniper now kept beneath hers. Three hundred days since Juniper had become, whether she wanted it or not, the last oath-bound swordspeaker in the world — if the oath still held, if the gift still lived, if she was anything at all besides a woman who talked to rocks.

She pressed her thumb briefly against the cold tang of the ancient sword, the way her grandmother had taught her: *a greeting between kin*, though what kinship a Brightwater had with a blade buried before the Veil fell, she had never dared ask.

Then she went down to her chores, and left the silence to keep itself.

* * *

The village of Hollowmere — named, some said, for the hollow way the wind moved through it; others said the name was older and meant something else entirely, but those others had learned to stop saying it — consisted of two streets, a mill, a shrine-house, and a great deal of careful attention paid to other people's business. Juniper was tolerated there the way a scar is tolerated: known, unavoidable, and best not remarked upon.

She was the herb-woman's granddaughter and then the herb-woman herself, which bought her a certain usefulness. She delivered babies and sat with the dying and knew which mushrooms would feed you and which would show you God. But she was also the strange one, the one who lived alone above the tree line in her grandmother's crooked house, the one who never sang at festivals even when the cider made everyone else sing, the one whose lips sometimes moved when she thought no one was watching, shaping words that never came out.

They didn't know why she kept silent. They only knew that silence was proper, and that Juniper Brightwater was mostly proper, and mostly was enough.

Mostly.

"You're late," said Tam Voss, sitting on her fence rail as though he owned it, which in his own estimation he probably did. He was eleven, gap-toothed, and possessed of the particular fearlessness of children raised in a place where nothing bad had happened in living memory. A carved wooden bird sat in his cupped hands, and when he turned it over she saw the charm-work: tiny whorls scratched into the wing joints, a thread of red wool tied at the neck. "I made it fly. Well. Almost. Aunt Hetty says if I whistle at it right—"

"Don't." The word came out sharper than she intended, and Tam flinched, and she softened it immediately, crouching to his level. "Tam. Sweetheart. What have I told you about charms?"

"That they're just games." He scuffed his boot against the fence post. "That they're nothing. That the valley doesn't like them."

"The valley doesn't like them," Juniper agreed, "and neither do I. Not because they're wicked. Because—" *Because a child with a whittled bird and a thread of wool is nothing, less than nothing, a grain of sand beside a mountain, and yet I have seen what happens to grains of sand when the mountain decides to move.* "Because things that are just games can stop being games. Give it here."

He handed it over with the wounded dignity of the very young, and she held the little bird a moment too long. It was good work, actually. There was a faint warmth in the wood, a suggestion of intention, the smallest possible seed of the thing that had once filled her grandmother's voice until the rafters rang. If Tam had been born two hundred years ago, in the singing valley, they'd have called him gifted by midwinter and started his training before his voice broke.

If Tam had been born anywhere else in the world, right now, today—

She put the thought down the way you put down a hot iron. "I'll keep it safe," she said, tucking the bird into her apron pocket. "You'll get it back when you're old enough to know why I'm keeping it."

"That's never," said Tam.

"Then you'll never get it back," said Juniper, and he laughed, and the sound went through her like sunlight through cold water — clean and painful both. Three hundred days, and the village children were the only ones who spoke to her without weighing their words first. She hoarded their laughter like a miser.

"Aunt Hetty wants to know if you're coming to the harvest feast," he said, swinging his legs. "She says you always say no. She says you should say yes this time because Widow Marsh is bringing her honey wine and last year you missed it and everyone talked about how you—"

"I'll come," Juniper said, to make him stop, and then heard herself, and blinked. "Tell her. Yes. I'll come."

Tam's face split into a grin, and he vaulted off the fence and ran, shouting the news ahead of him, and Juniper stood in her garden holding a forbidden charm in her pocket and wondering what exactly she had just agreed to. Standing in a crowd. Letting people look at her. Letting herself be *seen*, when seeing was the one thing she had spent her whole life avoiding, because her grandmother's last coherent instruction — whispered with the last of her strength, gripping Juniper's wrist hard enough to bruise — had been:

*Hide it, little wren. Hide it until your dying day. Whatever happens, whatever they do out there beyond the stones — the gift stays buried, the voice stays silent, and you grow old and plain and forgotten, and that is the victory. That is the whole victory. Do you hear me? Promise me.*

*I promise,* Juniper had said.

And the oath had taken the promise the way it took all promises — completely, permanently, with a weight like a hand closing around her heart — and she had felt it settle into her bones alongside all the other promises, the litany of them, years of them, every vow her grandmother had ever made her swear to keep the valley's law:

*I will not speak the speech.*

*I will not draw the blade in anger or in art.*

*I will not leave the valley.*

*I will not teach what I am.*

Four oaths. Four links in a chain she had forged herself, link by link, from the age of seven, because her grandmother asked and because she loved her and because she was a child and children believe that love and chains are the same thing.

She was twenty-six now, and she was beginning to suspect they were not.

* * *

The trouble started, as trouble often does, with weather.

The fog came off the ridge earlier than usual that evening, rolling down the slopes in thick white ropes, swallowing the orchards, the mill, the roofs of Hollowmere, until the whole valley looked like something drowned. Juniper was walking home from Widow Marsh's — she had gone to the feast after all, had stood at the edge of the firelight with a cup she barely touched, had endured an hour of careful, kind, terrible small talk before slipping away — and the fog met her halfway up the hill path, and she welcomed it. Fog meant privacy. Fog meant she could walk with her lips moving, practicing, the way she did most nights when the loneliness got loud enough.

Because that was the secret under all her other secrets, the one she barely admitted even to herself: she practiced. Every night, or nearly. Silent forms, mouthed words, the old exercises her grandmother had drilled into her before the drilling stopped — *shape the word, don't speak it; hold the edge of it behind your teeth like a coin; a swordspeaker's word is a blade, and you do not draw a blade unless you mean to bleed.* She practiced the way a caged bird beats its wings against the bars: not to escape, exactly. Just to remember it had wings.

*First form,* she mouthed, walking through the fog, her breath making ghosts. *The Naming. Steel, hear me. Iron, know me. I am the word and you are the wound and together we are—*

The boundary stones appeared out of the white.

She hadn't meant to walk this far. The path had carried her, the way paths do when the mind wanders, and now here she was at the valley's eastern edge, where the seven gray monoliths stood in their ancient row and the world simply — stopped. Beyond them the land fell away into forest and foothills and, three weeks' hard ride south, the capital, with its crown and its king and its hunger. The Veil hung over the boundary like heat-shimmer over a road: you couldn't see it, but you could feel it, a pressure on the ears, a deadness in the air. Magic could not cross into the valley. Magic could not live here. That was the covenant, that was the keeping, that was why her ancestors had traded their singing voices for safety, back when the world outside decided that the gifted were a disease and the cure was fire.

Juniper stood among the stones, as she did perhaps once a season, and let herself feel the grief of it. This close to the Veil, she could almost imagine what was being kept out. A vastness. A weather system of the soul. Something her body remembered and her mind had never been allowed to learn.

And then she heard the sound.

At first she took it for an animal — a deer in trouble, a fox in a snare. But it came again, wet and ragged, unmistakably human: a coughing, a dragging, a body failing forward through brush. Juniper froze, every sense straining. The fog muffled everything. Then a shape lurched out of the white ten paces away, and her heart slammed against her ribs.

A man. Big — taller than any man in Hollowmere, broad through the shoulders in a way that spoke of armor long worn, though he wore none now. He wore travel-stained clothes and a cloak with the clasp torn off, leaving a rectangle of unfaded cloth at the throat where something had been ripped away — colors, she realized. He'd torn off his colors. And he was bleeding. She could smell it even over the fog-damp, copper-bright, a lot of it, from somewhere along his side. His face was half-shadow, stubbled, gaunt, with a fresh cut over one eye, and his eyes themselves —

His eyes found hers across the space between them, and they were gray and feverish and *desperate*, and there was something else in them, something that made the hair rise on her arms. Recognition. Not of her face — of her *position*. He looked at her standing among the boundary stones the way a drowning man looks at a shore.

"Please," he rasped. His voice was ruined, gravel and rust. "The stones. Please. I need — inside. They're following me. They can't follow me past the—"

He took one more step, and his legs simply quit. He went down onto the wet grass at the foot of the boundary stone like a felled tree, and did not get up.

Juniper stood absolutely still.

Every law she had ever sworn rose up in her at once, speaking in her grandmother's voice. *No stranger crosses the boundary. No outsider enters the valley. The Veil keeps magic out, and it keeps the world out, and that is the price and the protection both. If someone comes to the stones hurt, you send for the elders, and the elders decide, and you keep your mouth shut and your hands at your sides and your gift buried where it belongs.*

But the elders were a mile down the hill, asleep behind their doors. And the man was bleeding into the moss. And the fog was thick, and the night was cold, and hypothermia killed faster than blood loss sometimes, and she knew that, she was the herb-woman, she had watched men die of less on the mill road.

And underneath the laws, quieter than all of them, in a voice that sounded terribly like her own: *three hundred days, Juniper. Three hundred days of talking to rocks. And here, finally, at the edge of everything, is a man who tore the colors off his own chest rather than wear them one more day — and you are going to stand there and let him die on the far side of a line drawn by dead people?*

She looked at the boundary stones. She looked at the man. Blood crept darkly outward from beneath him, soaking into the valley's soil, and she thought, absurdly: *the valley will remember that. The valley forgets everything except what bleeds into it.*

"They're following me," he whispered again, barely audible, his cheek against the wet grass, his gray eyes still fixed on her. "Please. I'd rather die inside your line than out here. At least inside — at least inside it's quiet."

Something in the phrase went through her like a blade finding the gap in armor. *At least inside it's quiet.* He wanted what she had. He wanted the silence she had spent her life suffocating in, and he was bleeding out begging for it.

Juniper Brightwater crossed the boundary path, knelt in the wet grass beside a bleeding stranger, and broke her first law in twenty-six years of keeping them.

"Help me walk," she said aloud, in the open, in the fog, her voice strange and rusty in her own ears. "On three. One — two—"

He weighed as much as a loaded cart, but terror and mercy lent her strength she didn't know she had, and together they staggered through the stones, across the invisible line, into the Veiled Valley. The moment they passed the boundary she felt it — the familiar deadness settling over her skin, the muffling weight of the covenant, the gift going quiet in her chest like a banked fire. Safe. Contained. As she had always been.

Behind them, out in the fog, faint but distinct, she heard hoofbeats. Several horses. Moving unhurried along the ridge, the way riders do when they have lost something and intend to find it in the morning.

The man groaned and sagged against her shoulder. Up close, in the last gray light, she could see the quality of his gear beneath the stains — good leather, good stitching, a soldier's kit, a knight's kit — and the torn-off colors, and the brand-shaped scar on the back of his right hand that she recognized without knowing how, and a cold finger traced her spine.

"What's your name?" she asked.

He was half-conscious, sliding. But he found the words, and he spent his last strength on them, and she would remember the shape of them for the rest of her life:

"Sable," he said. "Formerly of the King's Own. And you should know — before you save me — that I'm the one who taught them how to hunt your kind."

The fog closed over the boundary stones behind them like a door.
