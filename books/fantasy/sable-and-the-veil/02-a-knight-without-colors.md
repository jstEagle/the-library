## 2. A Knight Without Colors

Pain woke me before the fever did.

I had dreamed of the capital—of course I had; the mind returns to its wounds like a tongue to a broken tooth—and in the dream I was kneeling on marble slick with rain, holding out my torn surcoat to a king whose face kept sliding like wax near a flame. *Take it back,* I said in the dream. *I don't want it.* And the king smiled with too many teeth and said, *You do.*

Then the pain arrived, honest and ordinary, and I was grateful for it. Pain meant a body. A body meant I had not yet been found.

I opened my eyes to darkness shot through with green-gold light. Candlelight, filtered through something woven—willow branches, maybe, or reeds. The air smelled of beeswax and dried yarrow and cold stone, and beneath all of it, faintly, of woodsmoke from a hearth that had been banked hours ago. Someone had tended me while I slept. Someone had taken off my boots, cleaned the gash along my ribs, packed it with a poultice that stung like a confession.

Someone had not killed me, which in my experience of the last three months was rarer than mercy.

I lay still and took inventory, as I'd been taught. Ribs: two cracked, maybe three, on the left side where the horse had fallen on me at the ford. The cut: deep but clean, stitched now with small, even stitches—steady hands. Fever: present, retreating, like a tide going out over gravel. My sword: gone. My knife: gone. My signet ring: gone, though the pale band of skin where it had lived for eleven years still circled my finger like a ghost of a ghost.

So. Disarmed, bandaged, sheltered, alive. Which meant either my host was a fool or she wanted something, and I had learned—slowly, expensively—that fools rarely survived long enough in the Veiled Valley to drag strangers across their threshold.

"You're awake."

The voice came from my left, low and unhurried. I turned my head, carefully, the way one turns toward anything that might be a blade.

She sat in a chair drawn up beside the cot, mending. That was the first absurdity: mending. A woman in a plain grey dress, a basket of wool at her feet, working a needle through a sock with the placid concentration of someone who had never in her life been hunted. She was perhaps twenty-five, with dark hair braided back from a face that would have been unremarkable in any market crowd except for the eyes—grey-green, watchful, set too deeply, as if they had spent years looking out from behind something.

"You've been asleep two days," she said. "You should drink." She nodded toward a clay cup on the stool beside me without rising, without setting down her needle. Smart. Never come within arm's reach of a strange man until you know what he is.

I pushed myself up against the wall. The room tilted, righted itself. It was small—a shrine keeper's cell, by the look of it, built into the side of whatever structure stood beyond the willow screen. Shelves of candles. A stone basin worn smooth by centuries of hands. And above the doorway, carved into the lintel so old its letters were nearly erased: *HERE THE VEIL IS KIND.*

The Veiled Valley. So I had made it. I had actually made it, bleeding and half-drowned, and crossed the boundary stones, and the thing inside me that had been screaming since Ashmarket—the constant low hum of the gift, the sense of being watched by everything alive—had gone silent. Utterly silent. Like a man who has lived beside a waterfall his whole life suddenly standing in a soundproofed room.

It should have been peace. It felt like deafness.

"Drink," she said again. "Or don't. But if you die here you'll haunt this valley forever, and we have enough ghosts."

I reached for the cup. Water, with willow bark steeped in it, bitter enough to make my eyes water. I drank anyway. "Thank you," I managed. My voice came out ruined, sanded down to gravel.

"Don't thank me yet." Her needle did not pause. "I haven't decided what you are."

*Neither have I,* I almost said, and swallowed it. "Sable," I offered instead. Just the name. No house, no title. Those were ash.

"I know."

That stopped me. The cup froze halfway to my mouth. "You know."

"The valley doesn't forget faces, even if it forgets names." She set down the mending at last and looked at me directly, and I saw that the placidity was a coat she wore, the way I had once worn colors. Underneath, something was coiled and ready. "Twenty years ago there was a knight who rode the boundary road with the king's surveyors. My grandmother spoke to him once, through the stones. She said he had kind eyes and a liar's mouth, and that he'd be back someday wearing neither."

"That sounds less like prophecy than insult."

"It was both. She was good at both." Something moved at the corner of her mouth—not quite a smile. "Ser Sable of the King's Own. Sworn shield. Oath-keeper. The crown's own hound, they called you, when they called you anything polite."

The old name landed on me like a wet cloak. I set down the cup. "They call me other things now."

"I imagine they do." She rose, finally, and crossed to the window slit, where grey daylight pressed against the willow screen. When she spoke again her back was to me, and her voice had gone flat and careful, the way voices go when someone is deciding how much truth a stranger can be trusted with. "There are riders on the north ridge road. Six of them, in crown livery, asking after a wounded man on a dead horse. The miller's boy says they're paying silver for information. Real silver, not the tin they mint for border pay."

My blood went cold in a way the fever never could. "How far?"

"Half a day. Maybe less, if they stop to eat, and soldiers always stop to eat." She turned. "So. Before I decide whether to hide you or hand you to them—and understand that handing you over would buy this valley a great deal of goodwill—I want to hear it from your mouth. Not theirs. What did you do, Ser Sable? Why does the crown hunt a man who used to carry its own banner?"

And there it was. The question I had ridden three hundred miles to escape, which had followed me across two rivers and a mountain pass and was waiting for me, patient as death, in a candlelit cell at the edge of the world.

I could lie. God knew I was good at it; I had lied for the crown for eleven years, lied to villages about why the king's men needed lists of their children, lied to myself most fluently of all. And this woman owed me nothing. She had pulled me from the mud out of some private decency, and decency did not oblige her to burn with me.

But I looked at her—at the too-deep eyes, at the way she held herself slightly apart from her own body, the way people do when they are keeping something locked behind their teeth—and I thought: *she is hiding something too. Something big enough that she lives alone in a valley that forgets, tending a shrine, speaking to no one, mending socks in the dark.*

Two exiles recognize each other the way wolves do. By scent. By the particular hunger in the posture.

So I told her the truth. All of it. Because I was tired, and because lying to her felt like lying to the only mirror I'd seen in months.

* * *

"Three years ago," I said, "the king began to change. You'll have heard the stories—everyone's heard the stories, though the stories lag behind the truth by a season. He grew cold. Then hungry. Hungry in a way I didn't have a word for yet. He started asking questions no king asks. How many gifted children born in the eastern marches. Whether oath-talents could be inherited. Whether a person's gift could be *taken*, rather than merely suppressed."

Her needle had stopped moving. She didn't seem to notice.

"I told myself it was grief. The queen had died the winter before. Kings are men, and men go strange with grief. I told myself the edicts were temporary—the registration edict, then the gathering edict, then the one that didn't use the word 'gathering' anymore because someone in the chancery had finally grown a conscience and suggested 'collection' sounded kinder." I heard my own voice go bitter and made no effort to soften it. Bitterness was the least I owed. "And when the Order of the Veil was formed—the hunt, properly speaking—they needed officers. Men who knew the borderlands. Men the gifted might trust, because they'd once protected them under the old accords."

"Men like you."

"Men exactly like me." I laughed, and it hurt my ribs, and I let the laugh become a wince because the wince was more honest. "I led the first collection in Marrowford. Eleven people. A cooper who could charm rust off iron. Two sisters who sang storms calm. A boy of nine who made flowers open out of season—flowers, in the name of all the saints, *flowers*. We put them in wagons with barred windows and drove them to the capital, and I told myself they'd be housed, studied, kept safe. I believed it because believing it was easier than the alternative, and I had gotten very good at easy."

"What happened to them?" Her voice was very quiet.

"I don't know. That's the answer that broke me, in the end. Not horror—*ignorance*. They went into the palace and they did not come out, and when I asked, I was told the matter was above my station, and when I asked again, I was reassigned. And I noticed—because I'm a soldier, noticing is my trade—that every man who asked was reassigned, and every man who didn't ask was promoted. The king's household had become a machine for sorting questions into piles: useful and dangerous. And I realized, standing in a corridor outside a door I was never again permitted to open, that I had spent eleven years being the useful kind."

The candle between us guttered. Somewhere outside, wind moved through the willows with a sound like slow applause.

"Last spring they gave me the northern command. The whole hunt, west of the mountains. My commission, my colors, forty men, and a list." I closed my eyes and the list was there, of course, printed on the inside of my eyelids in neat chancery hand. Names. Ages. Talents. Villages. "A hundred and sixty names. I rode to the mustering ground and I stood in front of those forty men with the list in my hand, and I looked at them—young, mostly, good men mostly, men who trusted me—and I understood that whatever I did next, I would carry it for the rest of my life. Lead them, and become the thing I'd spent a year pretending I wasn't. Refuse, and be branded traitor, hunted, my family's name struck from every roll in the kingdom."

"So you tore off your colors."

I looked down at myself. I was wearing a borrowed shirt, homespun, undyed. But underneath the words I could feel the old memory: the sound of the stitching ripping, one seam at a time, in front of forty silent men. The white and gold of the King's Own coming away in my fists like skin.

"Not dramatically, if that's what the songs say. There are no songs yet, but there will be; there are always songs. I didn't throw it in the mud or burn it or give a speech. I just tore it off, folded it, and laid it on the mustering table, and said, 'Find another hound.' Then I walked out before my legs could disagree with me." I paused. "Twelve of the forty walked out with me. The other twenty-eight stayed. I don't blame them. Courage is cheap when it's someone else's."

"And the twelve?"

"Scattered. Two made for the coast. One had family in the hill country. The rest—" I shrugged, and paid for it in pain. "Gone, like smoke. Or caught. I try not to think about which."

The woman was silent for a long moment. When she finally spoke, her voice had changed—stripped of its careful flatness, though I couldn't yet name what had replaced it.

"They'll reach the valley," she said. Not a question.

"Within weeks. A month at the outside." I met her eyes. "The hunt doesn't stop at borders, and the Veiled Valley's reputation won't save it. If anything, it condemns it. A whole community that refuses the census? That shelters the registered? In the new edicts, that's not eccentricity. That's sedition with a pretty view." I leaned forward, ignoring my ribs. "Listen to me. I've read the campaign orders. They'll cordon the valley first—riders on every road, no one in or out. Then a census under threat of the collection. Then the collection itself. Your neighbors will be given a choice between informing and wagon-bars, and I promise you, having watched it happen in four towns, that I can count on one hand the number of people who chose the bars."

"You say 'your neighbors,'" she said softly, "as if you've already decided I'm not one of them."

The question hung there, delicate and dangerous as a wire. I had walked into it—or rather, I had seen it all along and walked in anyway, because there was no path through this conversation that didn't cross it.

"You live alone," I said. "In a valley full of families. You keep a shrine nobody visits. You dragged a bleeding stranger across your threshold when every sensible instinct says leave him for the crows. And when you look at me—" I hesitated, then finished, because she deserved the whole of it, "—when you look at me, you flinch. Not at the knight. At something else. Something you recognized."

Her hand had drifted, without her seeming to notice, to the hollow of her throat—an old gesture, protective, the kind people make around things they keep hidden there.

"My grandmother died in the autumn," she said at last. "She was the last of her line. Now I'm the last of mine." A breath. "That's all you get today, Ser Sable. You gave me your sins; I'm giving you one secret, and it's smaller than yours, and it's mine."

"It's enough."

"It isn't, and we both know it. But it's what there is." She stood, decisively, and the moment broke like ice underfoot. "Riders on the north road by tomorrow, if the miller's boy is reliable, and millers' boys are reliable exactly as often as they're frightened. You can't walk. You can barely sit. So here is what happens now: there's a root cellar behind the shrine, dry, with a false wall my grandfather built during the last bad years. You'll lie in it and you'll be quiet, and when they knock—and they will knock, because crown riders knock at every door in every village they cordon, that's how cordons work—I will tell them I live alone, that I've seen no one, that the valley keeps no strangers."

"And if they search?"

"Then we're both lost, and it won't matter what we said." She gathered her mending basket, then paused at the door, her back to me. In the green-gold light through the willow screen her shadow fell long across the floor, all the way to my cot, touching me like a hand.

"Why?" I asked. It came out rougher than I intended. "Why hide me? You owe the crown nothing, but you owe me less. Handing me over would protect this valley. You must see that. A cooperative shrine keeper, a captured traitor-knight—those are coins worth spending."

She was quiet so long I thought she wouldn't answer. Then:

"Because I watched you talk about that list of a hundred and sixty names, and you didn't make yourself the hero of it. You didn't say you saved anyone. You said you failed everyone, and you said it like a man reciting debts." Her voice dropped. "I've spent my whole life hearing about the crown's knights. Monsters in bright cloth. And you're not that. You're something worse and more useful: a man who knows exactly what he is. Those are rare. Those are worth hiding."

She opened the door. Cold valley air spilled in, smelling of pine and snowmelt and stone.

"Root cellar's through the back. Move slowly; you'll tear the stitches otherwise. And Ser Sable—" she glanced back, and for one unguarded instant the watchfulness fell away entirely, and what was underneath looked so much like loneliness that my chest ached in sympathy, "—when they ask my name, don't give it. Whatever else happens. Names are how they find us. Names are how they've always found us."

Then she was gone, and I lay in the dark listening to the willows applaud, and I understood that I had traded one cage for another, and that this one, at least, had a woman's voice on the other side of the door—and that in three days, or five, or however long the riders took to eat their way up the north road, that voice would stand in front of armed men and lie for me.

I owed her more than silence. I owed her the one thing I hadn't said aloud, the thing that had been sitting in my chest since the mustering ground like a swallowed coal:

The list had a hundred and sixty names. And I had memorized every one. And somewhere in the middle, in a neat chancery hand, under *Veiled Valley — shrine lineage*, was a surname I had heard once, twenty years ago, through boundary stones, from a woman with kind eyes and a liar's mouth.

Brightwater.

The hunt wasn't coming for the valley.

It was coming for her.
