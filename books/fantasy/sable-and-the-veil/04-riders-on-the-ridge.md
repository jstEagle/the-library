## 4. Riders on the Ridge

Sable woke to the sound of hooves.

Not the soft, shodded tread of a carthorse on valley clay—he knew that sound now, had learned it in three weeks of hiding, the way a man learns the small music of a place he means to betray by staying in it. This was iron-shod and many-footed, coming down off the ridge in column, and beneath it, faint as a held breath, the jingle of mail.

He was on his feet before his eyes were open, hand closing around the hilt of the plain sword he'd taken from a dead man's scabbard two hundred miles south. The loft above Juniper's byre smelled of hay dust and old apples and, lately, of the tallow candles she burned past midnight while she read her grandmother's book with her lips moving over words she would not let him hear.

Below him, the valley lay silvered in early light. Mist stood in the low fields like a congregation. And on the ridge—the ridge that should have been empty, that had been empty for three weeks of careful, counting days—rode eight figures under the black-and-gold of the crown.

Sable's stomach turned over slowly, the way it did when he saw his own old colors. He had worn that black-and-gold for eleven years. He had torn it from his own shoulders with his own hands, seam by seam, in a barracks yard while men he'd trained watched him do it, and he had told himself the tearing meant something. It did not mean they would stop riding.

* * *

He found Juniper already at the boundary stones.

She stood among them the way she always did at dawn—barefoot, sleeves rolled, one palm flat against the tallest stone as though taking its pulse—but this morning her head was up, her whole body angled toward the ridge like a deer deciding which way to run. Her grandmother's blade hung at her hip. She almost never wore it in daylight. She wore it now.

"How many?" she said, without turning. She always knew when it was him. He had stopped wondering how.

"Eight. Maybe a scout column ahead of more." He came to stand beside her, close enough that their shoulders nearly touched, not quite. Three weeks had taught him the exact width of that not-quite. "They'll ride the ford road. They'll be at the shrine by midmorning."

"They can't cross." Her voice was flat, certain, the voice of someone reciting a law older than laws. "The valley forgets them. That's what the stones are *for*. My grandmother said no rider has crossed since before my mother's mother."

"Your grandmother," Sable said gently, "wasn't hunting a swordspeaker."

She looked at him then. In the three weeks since she'd dragged him half-drowned out of the river below the falls, splinted his leg, hidden him from her own neighbors, fed him from stores she could barely spare—he had learned most of her faces. The guarded one. The wry one, rare as frost flowers. The one she wore at night when she thought the dark hid her, grief sitting on her like weather. This was none of them. This was fear, and she was furious at herself for wearing it.

"The report said witch-fire," she said. "You heard the rider shouting it down the far slope last night. Witch-fire near the Veiled Valley. There is no witch-fire here. There's nothing here but—" She stopped. Her jaw worked. "There's nothing here."

"There's you."

The words came out of him plainer than he intended. He watched her take them like a blow she'd been braced for anyway.

"Then the stones will hold," she said, "because I have never lit so much as a candle wrong. Because I have kept every rule she ever gave me." Her hand slid off the boundary stone. "Because I have been *silent* for twenty-six years, Sable, and silence has to count for something."

He wanted to tell her yes. He wanted it so badly that for a moment he actually weighed the lie, turned it over like a coin, tested its weight. He had lied to better people than her for worse reasons. But lying to her had begun to feel like lying in a shrine.

"Silence counts," he said. "It just doesn't always protect. I led hunts into places that had kept their heads down for generations. Places that had done nothing. We burned them anyway, because someone reported a light, or a rumor, or because a captain needed a result before winter." He looked back at the ridge, where the riders had begun the long switchback descent toward the ford road. "I'm telling you this so you'll believe me when I say: they're not here to find something. They're here to find *anything*."

* * *

They went down through the mist together, and the valley did what the valley always did—it forgot them. Sable felt it happen the way he'd felt it every morning for three weeks: a softening at the edges of himself, his name going vague in his own mouth, the memory of the capital going distant and dreamlike, the taste of the hunt gone out of his anger. By the time they reached the shrine meadow he could hardly remember why his hands shook.

That was the mercy of the Veiled Valley, and he had come to understand it was also its cruelty. It didn't heal you. It just made you forget what needed healing.

The village was waking. Smoke rose from chimneys straight up in the windless air. And at the well, doing the thing boys do at wells—drawing water badly, making a game of it—stood Tam.

Tam was eleven, all elbows and cowlick, the son of the weaver two doors from Juniper's cottage, and he adored Juniper with the uncomplicated devotion of a boy who has decided an adult is the most interesting person alive. He followed her to the shrine some mornings. He had once spent an entire afternoon trying to teach Sable to skip stones, with the patient condescension of the young for the broken.

Around his neck, on a braided cord, hung a little charm of woven straw and river-glass. Sable had seen it a dozen times. Tam's mother had made it against nightmares; half the children in the valley wore one. It wasn't magic. It couldn't be. Nothing in the valley was.

But it glittered. And to a man who had ridden three weeks on a rumor of witch-fire, hungry for a result before winter, glitter was enough.

* * *

Sable heard the riders before anyone else—of course he did; he had been the hoofbeat once—and he was moving before the first horse came down the lane, putting himself between the road and the well with the ease of long habit. Old habits. Bad ones. The body remembers how to stand in front of things.

Eight riders. He knew the make of their armor, the cut of their tabards, the particular bored cruelty of the man leading them—a lieutenant, young, chinless, with the flat eyes of someone promoted past his imagination. Sable had served beside a hundred of him. Had *been* three of him, maybe, in the years before Aldric changed, before the edicts started arriving in handwriting that made Sable's skin crawl.

"Halt!" the lieutenant called, and the village stopped. Doors closed. Faces appeared in windows and vanished. The mist seemed to lean away.

"In the king's name, this valley is searched for the practice of forbidden arts. Any found concealing a practitioner shares their guilt." His eyes moved across the meadow, across the cottages, across the shrine with its leaning stones, and Sable watched him catalog it all with a soldier's greed. A poor valley. A compliant valley. A valley that would give him something to carry home.

His gaze snagged on Tam.

On the charm.

"Boy," the lieutenant said. "Come here."

Tam, being eleven and having never once in his life been spoken to by a crowned rider, came. He came skipping, still holding the water bucket, grinning up at the horses, and Sable felt the moment stretch like wire.

"What's that round your neck?"

"It's against bad dreams. My mum wove it."

"Did she." The lieutenant leaned down from the saddle, and there it was, the thing Sable had seen a thousand times in a thousand villages, the small ceremonial pause before the machine began to turn. "Wove it how? With what words? Show me your hands, boy. Turn out your pockets."

"He's a child," Sable said. He hadn't planned to speak. The voice came out of him with the old command-weight still in it, the parade-ground baritone that made raw recruits flinch, and the lieutenant's head snapped around.

"And who are you?"

"A traveler. Passing through."

"Travelers don't stand like soldiers." The lieutenant studied him, and Sable watched recognition move behind those flat eyes like a fish under ice—not recognition of his face, thank whatever gods still bothered, but recognition of the *type*. "Travelers don't talk like officers. Dismount him. Search him."

Two riders swung down. And in the lane, at the edge of the mist, Juniper Brightwater stepped out of nothing with her grandmother's blade bare in her hand.

* * *

Later—much later, on roads far from here, in the years when he would tell this story only to her and only in the dark—Sable would try to explain what he saw in her face in that moment. Not courage. Courage was too clean a word. What he saw was a woman standing at the exact center of the life she had built out of silence, watching it crack, and choosing—with full knowledge, with open eyes—to step through the crack rather than let a child be taken through it instead.

The riders had Tam now. One had lifted him half off the ground by the collar; the bucket lay spilled, water soaking into the dust, silvering it. Tam had stopped grinning. He was crying without noise, the way valley children cried, the way children cried who had learned early that noise brought attention.

"Witch-token," the lieutenant said, holding up the straw-and-glass charm between two fingers like a dead bird. "Possession is sufficient grounds. The boy comes with us for questioning. The mother too."

"You can't." Juniper's voice. Low. Steady. Wrong. "This is the Veiled Valley. Your writ doesn't run here."

"Everything runs where the king says it runs." The lieutenant smiled. "Even ghosts."

And Sable saw it happen. Saw her look at Tam's face. Saw her look at the boundary stones, tall and gray and patient in the mist, the stones her grandmother had tended, and her mother, and every speaker back to the first ones whose names had worn off the rock. Saw her understand, in one terrible instant, exactly what the choice cost, and exactly who would pay it if she refused to.

Her lips parted.

What came out was not a shout. It was not even, properly, a word. It was a sound like a blade drawn slowly from a scabbard of air, a syllable with an edge on it, and the world *moved*.

Sable had heard swordspeech once before, years ago, from the other side of a burning door. He had told himself since that memory had exaggerated it. It had not. The word went through the meadow like light through water, bending everything it touched. The two riders holding Tam dropped him as though their hands had forgotten their orders. Horses screamed and reared. The spilled water leapt off the dust in a sheet of beads that hung in the air, turning, catching the sun, a brief impossible constellation over the lane. And the lieutenant's sword tore itself out of his scabbard, spun once in the bright morning, and buried itself point-first in the earth ten feet away, quivering, abandoned.

Juniper walked forward through the hanging water. Her voice, when she spoke again, was still that doubled thing—one voice speaking, and behind it something vast and old speaking through her, the way wind speaks through a canyon.

"*Let. Him. Go.*"

They let him go. Tam ran—not to the riders, not to his mother's door, but to Juniper, and buried his face in her side, and she put her free hand on his head without looking away from the lieutenant, who sat his horse white-faced with his hand hovering stupidly over an empty scabbard.

"You're real," the lieutenant whispered. "The reports. You're actually—"

"Ride," Juniper said, and the word had a blade in it too. "*Now.*"

They rode. Eight riders, wheeling, stumbling, one thrown and scrambling back into his saddle, hammering up the lane and away toward the ford road with the black-and-gold of the crown shrinking between the trees. The mist closed behind them. The water fell back to the earth all at once, a soft rain, and was done.

And then the valley was very quiet, and Juniper Brightwater stood in the middle of it with a naked sword and a shaking boy and a look on her face that Sable knew he would see for the rest of his life.

* * *

The village did not come out to thank her.

That was the thing Sable understood best of all, standing apart by the well while the doors opened one by one—not with relief but with a kind of horror, faces pale in doorways, an old woman making a sign against evil that was aimed, unmistakably, at Juniper. Tam's mother came running and snatched her son away with both arms, and Tam twisted in her grip, crying now loudly, reaching back, calling Juniper's name until his mother's hand covered his mouth.

"She saved him," Sable said quietly, to the weaver woman, to anyone. "You all saw it. She saved your son."

"We saw," the woman said, and her face was terrible with grief and fear in equal measure. "We saw what she is. Do you know what you've done? The stones held for two hundred years. Two hundred years of keeping our heads down, of *nothing*, of silence—and in one morning—" She couldn't finish. She pulled Tam against her shoulder and went inside, and the door shut, and the latch clicked, loud as a verdict.

By noon the headman had come to Juniper's gate. Sable stood in the shadow of the byre and listened to it, and it was gentle, which made it worse. No rage. No torches. Just a tired man explaining, with genuine sorrow, that the valley's protection was broken—that the stones had tasted her speech and would never fully sleep again—that the riders would return, and return in force, and that she could not be here when they did. That she must go. For all of them. *For her own good*, he said, and Sable heard the ancient cowardice in the phrase, the phrase every exile in history had been handed at the gate.

Juniper took it standing. She thanked him. That was the worst part; she *thanked* him, in her ordinary voice, with her ordinary courtesy, and the headman couldn't meet her eyes, and left.

* * *

Dusk. The byre loft. Sable climbed the ladder and found her packing by candlelight—methodically, silently, the way she did everything, folding a life into a canvas roll. Her grandmother's blade lay across the bed, cleaned and oiled, ready.

"You used it," he said. It wasn't an accusation. He didn't know what it was.

"I used it." She didn't look up. "Twenty-six years of silence, and I broke it in front of eight witnesses for a straw charm and a boy who skips stones badly." A breath that was almost a laugh and entirely wasn't. "My grandmother told me the oath was a wall. She never told me it was a dam."

"Juniper—"

"They'll come back," she said. "Bigger. You know it. I know it. Whatever I am, whatever this gift is—they'll tear the valley apart looking for it now, and everyone I've ever known will burn for a choice I made in one morning." She sat back on her heels at last and looked at him, and the candlelight moved in her eyes. "So don't tell me it was right. Don't tell me it was wrong. Just tell me the truth, Sable, the way you always do, even when it's ugly. Tell me what happens next."

He sat down across from her, the canvas roll between them like a border. Outside, the valley lay quiet under the first stars, forgetting itself as it always did, the mist rising off the fields, the shrine stones gray and patient and, for the first time in two centuries, awake.

"What happens next," Sable said, "is that we leave tonight, before they decide to post watchers on the ford. We go east, on foot, through the high passes where horses can't follow. And then—" He stopped. Because the next part was the capital, and the throne, and the thing he had fled, and saying it aloud in this small room made it suddenly, horribly real.

"And then?" she prompted.

"And then," he said, "we find out what your oath was really for. Because I've been thinking about it for three weeks, Juniper, and I don't think your grandmother built a wall at all. I think she left you a weapon. And I think the crown just gave us a reason to use it."

Juniper looked at the blade on the bed. Then she reached out and pinched the candle out, and in the sudden dark her voice came steady and strange, carrying the ghost of that edged syllable:

"Then we'd better start walking."
