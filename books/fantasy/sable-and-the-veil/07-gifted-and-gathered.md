## 7. Gifted and Gathered

The safehouse was a tannery that had stopped being a tannery three years ago and never told anyone.

Juniper smelled it before she saw it—the ghost of lye and lime still clinging to the stone even under the newer scents of woodsmoke and dried herbs and something sweeter, beeswax maybe, or honey left too long in the comb. The building crouched at the edge of a village called Little Wren, which was barely a village at all: six houses, a chapel with a leaning bell-tower, and a ford where the road gave up trying to stay dry and simply waded through the river like everything else did.

Sable stopped them on the far bank, in the shadow of an alder whose roots drank straight from the current.

"Rules," he said quietly. "Before we cross."

"You've given me rules every day for two weeks." Juniper shifted her grandmother's blade higher on her back. The strap had worn a groove into her shoulder that she was beginning to think of as a kind of company. "I can recite them in my sleep. Don't use your name. Don't look at soldiers. Don't touch the bread until I've checked it for mold."

"This one's different." He was watching the tannery's windows—dark now, but she'd learned to read him when he watched things, the way his stillness changed texture depending on what he saw. "These people are hiding because of what they *are*, not what they've done. That's a different kind of fear. It doesn't sleep. It doesn't get braver with practice. You walk in there carrying that sword on your back and speaking the way you speak, and some of them will see the hunt coming through the door before they see you."

"And if I leave the sword outside?"

"Then you'll feel naked, and I'll feel better, and one of us will be wrong."

Juniper looked at him. Two weeks on the road had taught her the geography of his face—the scar through his left eyebrow that he never explained, the way his jaw set when he was about to say something honest, the particular tiredness around his eyes that wasn't from walking. She knew what he was asking. He was asking her to walk unarmed among people who had every reason to fear a sword, guided by a man who had once been the reason for their fear.

She unbuckled the strap.

The blade came down into her arms like something sleeping, heavier without the harness to distribute its weight, warm from her body in a way metal shouldn't have been. Her grandmother's sword. Oath-bound. The only thing she owned that remembered the valley as well as she did.

"Wrap it," she said. "In the oilcloth. And carry it yourself."

Sable took it without argument, which was itself an argument—that he understood what it cost her, and wouldn't insult the cost by refusing. He wrapped the blade with the care of a man burying something he intended to dig up again, and slung it across his own back, where it made a long, wrong shape against his silhouette. A knight carrying a swordspeaker's oath. If anyone from the crown saw him now, they would have questions no rank could answer.

They crossed the ford at dusk, boots wet to the knee, the river pulling cold fingers at their shins. In the valley, water had been just water. Out here, Juniper kept catching herself listening to it—listening *through* it—for the hum she'd spent her whole life not hearing. The world beyond the boundary stones was loud in ways that had nothing to do with sound. Magic moved in it like fish under ice, everywhere and invisible, and she could feel the currents now that the valley's silence no longer pressed them flat. Some of the currents felt like weather. One or two felt like eyes.

The tannery door had no handle on the outside. Sable knocked in a pattern—two, pause, three, pause, one—and Juniper memorized it without meaning to. Her memory had been doing that lately, hoarding details like a woman storing grain against a famine winter. Every knock-pattern, every false name, every turn of every unmarked road. As if some part of her already knew that remembering was going to become the most expensive thing she did.

* * *

The woman who opened the door was small and round and approximately the age of a well-kept barn, with iron-gray hair pinned up with what appeared to be actual thorns, and eyes the color of strong tea. She looked at Sable for exactly as long as it took to confirm he was who the message said he was, and then she looked at Juniper, and her whole face changed—not warming, exactly, but *focusing*, the way a lens focuses.

"Oh," the woman said softly. "Oh, you're *loud*."

"I'm sorry?"

"Your gift." She stepped aside to let them in. "It's shouting. You're standing in my doorway shouting in a language most people can't hear, and half my household is about to come running to see what's calling them. Come in, come in, and for mercy's sake try to whisper."

Juniper didn't know how to whisper with whatever the woman meant. But she stepped inside, into warmth and lamplight and the smell of rosemary, and the door closed behind them with the soft finality of a held breath being released.

The tannery's great room had been transformed. Where curing racks must have hung, there were now bunk frames and drying herbs and strings of paper charms turning slowly in the draft. A dozen people were scattered through the space—a boy mending a net by the fire, two women arguing amicably over a map, an old man asleep in a chair with a cat on his chest that rose and fell in perfect counterpoint to his breathing. Ordinary people, doing ordinary things, except that the air around each of them shimmered faintly to Juniper's new-opened senses, like heat off summer stone. Every single one of them carried something. A charm, a knack, a birth-gift, a bargain their grandmother had made with a river. Small magics. Fugitive magics. The kind that got a person a rope or a pyre if the wrong rider came through town.

They all looked up when she entered. They all looked away again, quickly, in the practiced manner of people who had learned that looking too long was dangerous.

Except the boy with the net. He stared openly, and Juniper realized after a moment why: his own gift sat in him visibly, a faint green glow behind his breastbone that any gifted eye could see, and hers—hers must have looked like a bonfire in a cellar.

"Tea," said the thorn-haired woman briskly. "Both of you. Sit. My name is Odile; you'll use it and no other. And you—" this to Sable, with a look that could have curdled milk at forty paces, "—you'll sit by the door, where I can keep an eye on you, and you'll remember that everyone in this room has a cousin or a brother or a child who was taken in a hunt you helped design. I'm feeding you because Maren vouched for you and Maren has never been wrong about a soul. Don't make her wrong."

"Maren?" Sable asked.

"The ferrywoman at Marrow Ford," Odile said, pouring tea from a pot that had seen more wars than most soldiers. "Who looked at a deserter knight and a girl with a sword wrapped in oilcloth and decided, against every instinct of self-preservation, that you were worth a message north." She thrust two clay cups at them. "Drink. It's willowbark and chamomile and if you tell me it tastes like bark I'll tell you that bark is what keeps the fever down, so drink it anyway."

Juniper drank. It tasted like bark. She drank it anyway.

* * *

Later, when the fire had burned down to a red heart and the others had drifted to their bunks—one by one, unhurried, but Juniper noticed that every one of them chose a bunk with a sightline to the door—Odile sat across from her with a second pot of tea and an expression like a woman settling in for the part of the evening she'd actually been waiting for.

"Now," Odile said. "Tell me what you are. Not the polite version. The true one."

So Juniper told her. Not everything—she kept the valley folded close, kept her grandmother's deathbed and the shrine and the specific shape of her loneliness—but she told the shape of it. The last swordspeaker. An oath passed down like an heirloom, spoken into her at seven years old with her hand on her grandmother's blade. A valley where magic couldn't follow, which had sounded like safety her whole life and had begun, since she crossed the boundary stones, to sound like something else entirely. A cage dressed as a sanctuary. Or a key dressed as a cage. She still didn't know which, and saying it out loud to a stranger made the not-knowing worse and better at once.

Odile listened the way old women listen—completely, without interruption, with her hands wrapped around her cup and her eyes never leaving Juniper's face. When Juniper finished, the hedge-witch was quiet for a long moment.

"Swordspeakers," she said finally. "I know three stories and half a song."

"Three stories?"

"The first is children's nonsense—a hero who talked her enemies' swords into the ground. Every culture has a version; yours talks instead of sings, that's all. The second is from the archives of a collector I knew in Vell's Crossing, before the collectors started burning. A fragment. Court record, two hundred years old, sealed by royal order." Odile's voice dropped, and the firelight seemed to lean in with her. "It mentions a binding performed beneath a throne. It names no names. But it says the words of the binding were spoken by *three sisters of the blade*, and that the thing they bound was bound *into the crown itself*, so that no heir could wear it clean."

Juniper's tea had gone cold in her hands. She hadn't noticed drinking it, hadn't noticed stopping. "And the third story?"

"The third is mine." Odile smiled, and it was not entirely a comforting smile. "My grandmother was a midwife in the palace city, back when the old king still lived. She delivered half the nobility and heard everything, because birthing rooms are where people tell the truth. She used to say the crown was cursed—not in the way peasants mean it, not bad luck and dead wives, but *hungry*. She said the kings grew strange in the third year. Always the third year. Colder. Hungrier. Signing things they'd sworn never to sign. And she said her own grandmother had whispered to her, when she was small, that the crown ate oaths the way other crowns eat taxes."

"Eats oaths," Juniper repeated.

"That's what she called it. The Hollow King, she named it—not the men who wore the crown, but the thing beneath, the thing that hollowed them out and wore *them*. And here's the part that matters to you, girl, so listen." Odile leaned forward. The thorns in her hair caught the light. "If the spirit eats oaths—if that's its food, its nature, its hunger—then what do you suppose it makes of a swordspeaker? Of a living oath, walking around loose, wrapped in skin, carrying a blade that's soaked in binding-words for nine generations?"

The fire popped. Somewhere upstairs a floorboard settled. Juniper became aware of her own heartbeat, slow and enormous, like something turning over in deep water.

"It would notice me," she said.

"It noticed you the moment you crossed the boundary stones," Odile said gently. "Perhaps before. Perhaps the valley wasn't hiding you from the world, child. Perhaps it was hiding the world from *it*."

* * *

She didn't sleep. She lay on the narrow bunk Odile had given her—the same room as Sable, separated by a curtain, because Odile had taken one look at them and assigned them together with the word "obvious" and nothing further—and listened to the tannery breathe around her.

The others slept in shifts; she could hear it, the careful choreography of it, someone always awake, someone always between the sleepers and the door. Once, near midnight, she heard the boy with the green-lit chestbone pad past her curtain on bare feet, and stop, and stand there for a long moment. When she pulled the curtain back an inch, he was looking at her sword, propped against the wall where Sable had unwrapped it at her insistence—she couldn't bear it wrapped, not after dark, not yet—and the boy was looking at it the way children look at hearth-fires.

"My mam could make a charm that'd find lost sheep," he whispered, when he saw her watching. "Just lost sheep. Nothing else. They hanged her for it anyway." He said it without self-pity, the way you'd report weather. "What's yours do?"

"I don't know yet," Juniper whispered back.

"That's the worst kind," he said solemnly, with the authority of vast experience—he could not have been more than twelve—and went back to bed.

She lay staring at the ceiling beams after that, and let herself feel it, carefully, the way you test a bruise. This room full of strangers who slept in shifts. This boy who had lost a mother to a sheep-charm and spoke of it like rain. This small round woman downstairs who had fed fugitives out of a tannery for three years and counted it a good life. In the valley, Juniper had been alone in a crowd of people who loved her—loved her, and could never quite reach her, because the silence between them was the law. Here, among the hunted, among people whose gifts were small and whose dangers were large, she felt—for the first time since her grandmother died—like a language being spoken fluently by everyone in the room.

Belonging, she thought, shouldn't feel like grief. But it did, a little. Because every one of these people had found this room by losing something else.

* * *

Near dawn, she gave up on sleep and went down to the fire, and found Odile already there, of course, doing something with dried yarrow and a mortar that clicked like a slow clock.

"Couldn't sleep either?" Juniper asked.

"I sleep four hours a night and have since the first raid. You learn to spend the dark hours productively." She nodded at the bench across from her. "Sit. There's a thing I've been chewing on since you told me your story, and I want to say it while it's fresh."

Juniper sat. The fire was low but the room was warm, and outside the shuttered window the sky was going the gray of tin.

"The fragment I mentioned. The court record." Odile ground the yarrow with deliberate patience. "Sealed by royal order, two hundred years ago. Do you understand what that means? Someone in the palace, two centuries back, knew about the binding beneath the throne. Knew enough to want the knowledge buried. Which means the Hollow King has been known to the powerful for a very long time—and every one of those powerful people chose the crown over the truth." She looked up. "Now. Why would a spirit that eats oaths allow such a record to exist at all? Why not burn it? It's had two hundred years and the whole apparatus of a kingdom to burn it."

Juniper turned her cup in her hands. "I don't know."

"I do. Or I guess, which is the witch's version of knowing." Odile set down the mortar. "Because it isn't afraid of the record. It's *feeding* on it. Think, girl. What is a secret kept by a court? What is a truth that powerful people know and bury and pass down in whispers, generation after generation, swearing each new keeper to silence?" Her eyes were very bright. "It's an *oath*. A great fat juicy oath, renewed every twenty years, sworn by every frightened noble who learns the truth and promises to keep it. The Hollow King doesn't hide the record. The record is its larder."

The words landed in Juniper's chest like stones dropped down a well. She thought of the hunts. Of the edicts signed in a hand that wasn't the king's. Of a kingdom where every official, every captain, every clerk who suspected the truth and kept silent—each silence was a vow, and each vow was a meal.

"It's built a kingdom out of complicity," she said slowly. "Everyone who serves it feeds it."

"And now," Odile said, "there's you. An oath that walks. An oath with legs and a blade and, as of two weeks ago, a grievance." She smiled, thin and sharp as the thorns in her hair. "You're not food to that thing, child. You're the first dish it's ever been served that might bite back."

From the doorway behind them, quiet as a man trained to be quiet, Sable said: "There's a problem with that."

Juniper turned. He stood in the shadow of the stair, and the dawn light through the shutters striped him like a prisoner.

"If the Hollow King has known about her since the boundary stones," he said, "then it hasn't sent riders. It hasn't sent hunters. It's had two weeks and the entire machinery of the crown, and it has done *nothing*." His voice was level, but Juniper had learned to hear the strain under his levelness, the sound of a man holding a conclusion at arm's length because he didn't want it touching him. "Things that hunt don't wait. Things that *farm* do. It's not ignoring her. It's letting her walk. Letting her gather. Letting her find the network, find the vaults, find the words—" He stopped. Started again, quieter. "Letting her come to it. Because everything she does, every step toward the throne, is a step deeper into reach. And it wants her close. It wants her *close*."

The fire ticked. Upstairs, the boy turned over in his sleep, and the floorboards murmured.

"Why?" Juniper asked, and hated how small her voice came out. "Why would it want me close? To eat my oath?"

"No," Sable said. He crossed the room and knelt beside her chair, and took her hands, and his were steady while hers were not. "To eat the *unbinding*. The words exist, Juniper. They've existed for two hundred years, sealed in the vaults beneath the throne, waiting for a speaker. The spirit can't destroy them—it's tried, or its hosts have tried, and failed. So it does the only other thing it can." His grip tightened. "It waits for the one person who can speak them. And it makes sure she speaks them *inside its mouth*."

Odile had gone very still by the fire. Outside, the first birds were starting, small and ordinary and unaware.

"So we don't go to the capital," Juniper said.

"We go to the capital," Sable said. "We just stop pretending it's our idea."

Above them, faint through the floor, a knock sounded at the tannery's front door. Two. Pause. Three. Pause. One.

Then—wrong, breaking the pattern—a fourth knock. Hard. Official. The knock of someone who had never needed a password in his life.

Odile snuffed the candles with a closed fist and said, very calmly, into the sudden dark:

"Everyone knows their places. Girl—your sword. *Now.*"
