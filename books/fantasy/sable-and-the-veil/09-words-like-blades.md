## 9. Words Like Blades

The mill house Odile had claimed smelled of rye dust and old rain, and it was there, in the hollow dark beneath the grinding floor, that Juniper learned what her voice could truly do.

She had thought she knew. She had spoken swordspeech once in the valley—once, to free Tam from the crown riders—and felt the words tear out of her like hooks dragged through flesh. She had assumed that was all there was: a blade, and blood on either side of it. Her grandmother's teachings had been a litany of warnings. *Never speak in anger. Never speak for gain. Never speak where the valley can hear you.* A list of prohibitions, not a map.

Odile spread three objects across the flour-dusted table between them: a rusted iron nail, a sparrow's body stiff with cold, and a strip of linen knotted seven times around itself.

"Your grandmother," Odile said, "taught you to cut."

"She taught me to be silent."

"Yes. Which is how speakers survive long enough to matter." The hedge-witch's hands were brown and cracked as riverbed clay, and they moved over the objects with the tenderness of someone greeting old friends. "But silence is only half the craft, girl. A sword that only cuts is half a sword. The first swordspeakers bound kings with their voices. They stitched wounds shut with them. They *unmade* promises that should never have been made." She looked up, and her eyes were pale as skimmed milk. "You are not carrying a weapon up to that throne. You are carrying a key. And keys do more than one thing."

Juniper's hand went, without thinking, to the hilt of her grandmother's blade where it lay wrapped beside her. The leather of the grip had worn smooth under two generations of palms before hers. "Show me."

"Oh, I can't show you." Odile smiled, and it was not an unkind smile, but it was not a kind one either. "I can only stand here while you find out. That's what training is, for your kind. Standing near the fire while someone else burns."

* * *

The nail came first.

"Bend it," Odile said. "Don't touch it. Speak to it."

Juniper stared at the small corroded thing and felt absurd. In the valley she had hidden her gift beneath ritual silence for twenty years; speaking to a nail seemed less like magic than like madness. But she closed her eyes, found the place behind her sternum where the oath lived—a knot of heat and pressure, like a swallowed coal—and let a word rise out of it.

She did not choose the word. That was the first thing she understood, and the last thing she would ever stop understanding: swordspeech was not a language you knew but a language that knew you. The syllable left her mouth sounding like iron dragged over stone, and the nail shivered, curled, and snapped in two with a sound like a small bone breaking.

The cost arrived a heartbeat later.

It came as a smell, first—the green smell of the shrine pool at dawn, cress and cold stone—and then as a shape: her grandmother kneeling at the water's edge, washing her hair when Juniper was six, humming something low. Juniper reached for the memory and found its edges gone soft, blurred, like ink touched by wet fingers. The humming remained. The face above her was fading even as she grasped at it.

"No—" She gripped the table's edge. "No, wait—"

"It takes payment," Odile said quietly. "I did warn you."

"You didn't warn me it would take *her*."

"I didn't know what it would take. It takes what's closest to the bone." The hedge-witch's milk-pale eyes held no apology. "That's why your kind were so rare, child. Not because the gift is rare. Because most people who have it learn this lesson early and spend the rest of their lives choosing silence."

Juniper sat very still while the memory finished dissolving. What remained was a feeling without a picture: being small, being loved, water running warm down her neck. She held the feeling the way you hold water in cupped hands, knowing it would drain away.

"Again," she said.

* * *

The sparrow came second, and it nearly broke her.

"Bind it back together," Odile said. "Not mend—*bind*. There's a difference. Mending fixes what broke. Binding holds what's falling apart. It's gentler work, and crueler."

The little body lay in her palm, weightless as a dried leaf. Its heart had been still for hours. Juniper spoke the word that rose in her—not the iron word but something softer, rounder, a word like a hand closing—and felt the oath behind her sternum turn over like a wheel.

Nothing happened.

She tried again. Again. On the fourth attempt the sparrow's breastbone gave one faint stutter, one flutter, and then stilled forever, and Juniper sat down hard on the mill floor and wept, because she understood suddenly that binding was not resurrection. It was a promise made to something dying: *hold on, hold on*—and the sparrow had been too far gone to hold.

"Some things can't be bound," Odile said from above her. "Only held while they go."

"That's useless," Juniper whispered.

"It's the most useful thing there is." The hedge-witch lowered herself onto the floor beside her, joints cracking, and took the sparrow gently from Juniper's palm. "You'll meet people on the road to the capital who are falling apart. You won't be able to fix them. You'll be able to hold them while they go, or hold them until they don't. Both are holy work. Don't confuse holiness with power."

The cost of those four failed words was smaller but stranger: the taste of the valley's blackberries vanished from her tongue. She searched for it—she could remember blackberries, remember picking them, remember the thorns—but the flavor itself was gone, a door painted shut in a house she'd lived in all her life.

* * *

The knotted linen taught her the third truth, and the third truth frightened her more than the other two combined.

"Unmake the knots," Odile said. "Without touching them."

"But that's nothing. That's just—"

"Just?" Odile raised an eyebrow. "A knot is a promise, girl. Rope remembers being tied. Everything tied wants to stay tied—that's what tying *means*. To unmake it with a word is to tell a thing that its own history is void." She pushed the linen across the table. "Now imagine the promises men make to each other. Imagine the promises a spirit makes to a king. Imagine unmaking *those*."

Juniper spoke. The word that came was neither iron nor hand—it was a word like a door opening, like breath released after long holding—and the seven knots slid loose all at once, the linen lying flat and innocent as if it had never been tied at all.

And the valley began to leave her.

Not a piece this time. A *layer*. She sat in the mill house and watched, from somewhere outside herself, as the whole texture of her childhood thinned: the way morning fog moved through the pines, the particular creak of the third stair in her grandmother's house, the name of the boy she'd kissed at fourteen behind the shrine. All of it still *there*, but behind glass now, seen rather than inhabited. She could describe her valley the way a traveler describes a valley passed through once. She could no longer feel it in her chest.

She screamed. She would admit later that she screamed, a raw animal sound that sent rooks up from the mill's rafters, and Odile let her scream, and did not touch her, and waited.

When it was done, Juniper wiped her face and said, in a voice scraped nearly clean: "How many times can I do this before there's nothing left of me?"

Odile was quiet for a long moment. Outside, the rooks resettled.

"The first swordspeakers," she said finally, "were nine. Did you know that? Nine sisters, some say. Or nine strangers who met on a road, depending on which fragment you believe. They forged the oath together, and each of them paid for it differently—one forgot her mother's face, one forgot her own name, one forgot how to weep." The hedge-witch folded her cracked hands. "The fragments agree on one thing, though. At the end, when the great unbinding was spoken, the last of the nine stood before the throne and could not remember why she had come. Another speaker had to whisper the reason to her, word by word, while she spoke the words that killed the spirit."

Juniper's blood went cold. "Then I'll need someone—"

"You'll need someone," Odile said simply. "Everyone does. That's not weakness. That's the design. An oath that could be broken alone would be broken by every coward who ever bore it."

* * *

They trained for eleven days.

Juniper learned to speak small—to shave slivers off the cost by shaping intent before the word rose, though the shaping was like trying to aim a river. She learned that anger doubled the price and fear tripled it, and that the cheapest speech of all was speech offered freely, without wanting anything back. She learned that healing was possible—not mending flesh, but binding pain, wrapping a wound in a word the way you'd wrap it in linen—and when she practiced on Odile's chronically swollen knee, the old woman walked without her stick for a day and wept with gratitude, and Juniper lost the sound of the valley's evening bells and considered it a fair trade, and hated herself for how easily she considered it.

She learned, too, what she was becoming. Each loss carved space, and the space did not stay empty. Something else poured in: a steadiness, a stillness, a terrifying clarity. She caught herself watching Sable across the fire at night with a general's eyes, measuring exits and angles, and had to deliberately soften her gaze. The gift was making her into a blade, and blades do not grieve well, and grief was the last rope tying her to the girl she had been.

On the ninth night, Sable found her practicing alone.

He stood in the mill doorway with snow in his dark hair—he'd been walking the perimeter again, unable to sleep, unable to stop guarding even here—and he watched her speak the binding word over a dead moth, over and over, trying to hold its wings together, losing something small each time she failed.

"You're spending yourself like coin," he said.

"I'm learning."

"You're grieving in advance and calling it study." He came and sat across from her, and the firelight moved on his face, on the scar that split his eyebrow. "In the king's guard we had a saying about men who drilled past exhaustion. We said they weren't training anymore. They were punishing themselves."

"For what?"

"For surviving, usually." His voice was gentle, and gentleness from him always undid her more than harshness would have. "Juniper. Whatever's coming on that throne—you don't have to arrive empty."

She looked down at her hands. They were steady. They were always steady now, and she missed the tremble.

"If I arrive full," she said, "the spirit will just take everything at once. This way I choose what goes. Do you understand? It's the only choice I get, Sable. I'm taking it."

He was quiet a long time. Then he said, "Then I'll remember for you. Everything you lose—I'll carry it. Tell me the valley, and I'll keep it, and at the end, if you can't remember why you came, I'll whisper it to you word by word."

She hadn't told him about the fragment of the nine sisters. Odile must have. She looked at him—at this disgraced knight who had torn off his own colors rather than hunt children, who flinched from praise and leaned toward penance—and something in her chest that was not the oath, that was older and quieter than the oath, turned over like a key finding its lock.

"You'd carry a valley you've never seen?"

"I've carried worse for worse reasons."

* * *

On the eleventh day, the letter came.

It arrived the way important things always arrived in the network—wrapped inside something mundane, a bolt of dyed wool carried by a drover who did not know he was a courier. Odile slit the wax with her thumbnail, read it twice, and then stood very still in the middle of the mill house for so long that Juniper set down the practice-knots and crossed to her.

"What is it?"

"A letter." Odile's voice had changed; the dry humor was gone out of it. "Sealed with the royal arms, but written in a woman's hand. It names the Hollow King. It names the oath-vaults beneath the throne room. And it offers us a door into the palace that has been closed for thirty years." She looked up, and her milk-pale eyes were bright with something between hope and terror. "It's signed by the Queen Dowager. The Hollow King's own mother is asking for our help."

Outside, snow was beginning to fall on the road to the capital, softening the tracks of everything that had come before, erasing the way forward and the way back alike. Juniper flexed her hands—steady, always steady now—and felt the oath behind her sternum stir like something waking.

Somewhere south, a caged man's mother was waiting for an answer.
