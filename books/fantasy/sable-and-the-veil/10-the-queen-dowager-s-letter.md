## 10. The Queen Dowager's Letter

The letter came wrapped in wax the color of dried blood, sewn into the lining of a dead man's coat.

Juniper watched Odile work the stitches free with a bone needle, her hedge-witch hands steady as a surgeon's. Around them, the safehouse breathed—cellar air thick with tallow smoke and dried yarrow, the walls hung with charms that clicked softly when the wind found cracks in the stone. Somewhere above, rain had begun, drumming on the low roof like a thousand small impatient fingers.

"Whose coat?" Juniper asked.

"A peddler's." Odile did not look up. "He died on the road three days ago. Fever, or so the village said." Her needle paused. "Or so they were meant to say."

Sable stood by the door with his arms crossed, positioned as he always was between Juniper and whatever might come through it. He hadn't slept properly since Marrow Ford; she could see it in the gray beneath his eyes, in the way his hand kept drifting to the place at his hip where a sword no longer hung. He'd sold the blade two towns back. She still caught him reaching for it.

Odile drew out a folded square of paper, heavy stock, cream-white—the kind of paper that cost more than most families earned in a season. The seal had been broken and re-melted, clumsily, by someone unpracticed in deception.

"That's not merchant's wax," Sable said quietly.

"No," Odile agreed. "It isn't."

She unfolded the letter and read it once in silence, her lips moving. Then she read it again, aloud, her voice dropping into something hushed and careful, as though the words themselves might bruise.

*To the one who keeps the old names: I write as a mother, though I know what my name is worth to you. Nothing. Less than nothing. But hear me anyway.*

*My son lives. They will tell you he does not—that the king is well, that the king is strong, that the king has never seemed so vigorous. I tell you he is caged inside his own body like a bird shut in a dark room, beating against walls only he can feel. I have seen him surface. Twice this year. Once he wept without sound for an hour while his own hand signed papers he could not read.*

*It sits upon the throne. It has sat there since before my husband died, wearing my family's faces one after another, eating them young. My father-in-law lasted forty years. My husband twenty-two. My son has been king six years, and already the edicts come out in a hand that is not his.*

*I know what it is called among those who remember. The Hollow King. It eats oaths—it eats the vows that bind a crown to its people, and then it eats the wearer. Every heir since Aldric's grandfather has been devoured younger than the last. You must understand: it is not a curse upon our house. It is a tenant. And we are the house it consumes.*

*There are records beneath the throne room. In the oath-vaults where the first binding was made. I cannot reach them—I am an old woman who tends gardens and is permitted to be harmless. But you could. If any of you can still walk the old roads under the palace, I can open them.*

*Come to me. Trust a royal, if you can bear the taste of it. I have nothing left to offer but the truth, and access, and the shame of having stayed silent too long.*

*—Maren, Queen Dowager, who buried a husband and fears burying a son*

*P.S. — There is a swordspeaker. I have dreamed of her three times now. She carries her grandmother's blade and walks with a knight who tore off his own colors. If she reads this: your oath was made in the vaults below the throne. Whatever you believe it is for, go and see where it was forged.*

The silence after Odile finished was total. Even the rain seemed to hold itself apart from it.

Juniper became aware, distantly, that her heart was hammering. *Your oath was made in the vaults below the throne.* The words went into her like a blade going into water—no resistance, just depth.

"She knows about me," she said. Her voice sounded strange in her own ears. "She's never met me. How does a queen dowager know about—"

"Dreams." Odile set the letter down on her worktable, weighting its corners with river stones, an old habit for things that felt dangerous. "The gifted dream true sometimes. Especially near the end of things. My grandmother said prophecy is just grief arriving early."

"And the rest?" Sable had not moved from the door. His voice was flat, controlled, the voice he used when feeling would otherwise spill out of him. "The vaults. The records. A royal offering to smuggle strangers beneath the throne room. Does no one else hear how that sounds?"

"I hear it," Odile said mildly.

"It's either salvation or a snare."

"Yes." She smiled, thin and dry as winter grass. "Most true things are both."

* * *

They argued about it for two days.

Not loudly—Odile's safehouse had thin walls and thinner trust—but in the low, grinding way of people circling the same wound. Juniper argued for going. Sable argued against it with the particular ferocity of a man who had spent eleven years inside palace walls and knew exactly what royal mercy looked like when it curdled.

"You want to walk into the seat of the thing that eats kings," he said on the second night, in the small room they'd been given, lit by one candle stub. "Because a letter told you to. Because a *queen* told you to."

"Because she described things only someone close to the throne could know." Juniper sat on the narrow bed, her grandmother's blade across her knees, running her thumb along the worn leather of the grip. "The unsound signature. The ages of the heirs. Sable—she named the Hollow King. Odile only had fragments. This woman has the whole shape of it."

"Or the whole shape of a trap."

"Then why warn us? Why mention the vaults? If the Hollow King wanted me dead, it could have let Ives take me at Marrow Ford."

"Ives reports to men who report to chancellors who report to—" He stopped himself. Pressed his knuckles briefly against his mouth. When he spoke again, his voice had dropped, roughened. "I signed the first hunt orders, Juniper. My hand. I read Aldric's name at the top of them and I obeyed, because that was my oath, because obedience was the only prayer I'd ever been taught. And every person I dragged in chains to Ashmarket believed, right up until the doors closed, that the king himself could not possibly have ordered it. That someone must have lied to him." He looked at her then, and his eyes were terrible with honesty. "Royals do not send letters asking for help. Royals send soldiers."

"Then why did you tear off your colors?"

The question landed harder than she'd intended. She saw it strike him—not as accusation but as memory—and she watched him carry it for a moment before answering.

"Because I finally saw the king's face," he said slowly, "at an execution I'd helped arrange. And what looked back at me wasn't a man making hard choices. It was something *hungry* wearing a man's face. And I understood that everything I'd ever sworn to had been eaten years before I knelt to swear it." He exhaled. "So yes. I know what royals are. I also know what it costs to discover the thing you served was never real. If Maren is lying, I'll kill us both getting out. If she's telling the truth—"

"Then her son is drowning," Juniper finished quietly, "and she's been watching for six years, unable to move, because moving means admitting what her family has been hosting for three generations."

The candle guttered. Outside, the rain had thickened, and somewhere down the lane a dog barked twice and thought better of it.

"She dreamed of me," Juniper said. "Three times. Grandmother said the valley kept dreams out. That was part of what the silence was *for*—nothing prophetic could find me there. But I've been outside the boundary for weeks now, and if the gift runs in the old lines, if the dowager has even a drop of the blood that made the first binding..." She trailed off, because the thought completed itself: *the oath was made in the vaults. The oath was made by someone standing exactly where I need to stand.*

Sable was quiet a long time. Then he said, in a voice stripped of argument, "I'll go where you go. You know that. I just needed to say the truth out loud first, so it's on record when it goes wrong."

"That's very romantic."

"I'm told I have my moments."

* * *

The reply went back the way the letter had come—through a chain of peddlers and pilgrims and one deaf ferryman, each link ignorant of the whole. Odile wrote it herself, in cipher, on paper cheap enough to burn: *We come. Two travelers, one lame horse, no colors. Tell us the door.*

The answer arrived four nights later, and it was not a letter.

It was a key.

A real one—iron, heavy, ancient, its teeth cut in patterns Juniper had never seen, though something in her bones stirred uneasily at the sight of them, the way a dog stirs at a sound below human hearing. It came wrapped in plain linen with a single line in a fine, aging hand:

*The gardeners' gate, south face, beneath the third gargoyle. The night of the new moon. Ask for Mother Crane. — M.*

Odile turned the key over in her fingers, held it to candlelight, sniffed it, whispered over it, and finally pronounced judgment: "Old iron. Older than the palace above it, I'd guess. This isn't a key to a door. This is a key to a *foundation*."

"The oath-vaults," Juniper said.

"Or something older that the vaults were built on top of." Odile handed it back with visible reluctance, as though glad to be rid of its weight. "Either way, girl, understand what you're holding. Every secret in that city has a price, and queens don't pay in coin."

That night Juniper dreamed—not of the valley, which had begun to blur at its edges the way all her memories did lately, but of a corridor underground, lit by lamps that burned without flickering. At its end stood a door of black wood banded in iron, and carved above the lintel in letters worn nearly smooth: *WHAT IS SWORN BELOW IS SWORN FOREVER.*

In the dream, she reached for the handle.

She woke with her hand clenched around the iron key so tightly it had bitten a mark into her palm—a crescent of small red dents, precise as a bite.

Sable was already awake. Of course he was. He watched her from across the room with that expression she was learning to read: fear worn openly, because he had decided long ago that hiding it was a kind of lie.

"What did you see?" he asked.

"A door." She opened her palm. The mark was already fading. "And words over it. *What is sworn below is sworn forever.*"

Something crossed his face—recognition, or dread, or both braided together. "Those are the words the old knights used," he said. "Before oaths were sealed with ink. Before my time, even. We still said them, at investitures. Nobody knew what they meant anymore. They were just... decoration." He sat up slowly. "Juniper. They weren't decoration. They were a *reminder*. Somebody built a whole kingdom on top of that sentence and forgot it was load-bearing."

She looked down at the key, warm now in her palm despite the cold room, warm as if it remembered being held by other hands in other centuries.

"New moon's in five days," she said.

"Five days," Sable agreed. And then, softer, almost to himself: "God help us. We're going to knock on a palace door and ask a queen to betray everything she is."

Outside, the last of the rain moved off eastward, and the sky over the rooftops began, very faintly, to pale—not toward dawn, but toward the clean, empty darkness of a moon dying down to nothing. Somewhere in that direction, days away, a caged man beat his hands against the inside of his own life, and an old woman knelt in a garden pretending to prune roses, waiting to see whether strangers would trust her.

Juniper closed her fist around the key.

*What is sworn below is sworn forever,* the dream had said.

Soon, she thought, she would learn what that cost.
