## 11. Ives Closes In

The rain had followed them for three days, and Sable had begun to think of it as a companion—dour, persistent, asking nothing but that he endure it. It fell through the pines in long gray threads and pooled in the wheel-ruts of the old drover's road, and it muffled the world so thoroughly that he could almost pretend they were not being hunted.

Almost.

He had been a hunter too long not to know the shape of a hunt from the inside. Captain Ives did not travel loud. He did not scatter outriders across the countryside like a man hoping to flush game by accident. He would be behind them, patient as winter, reading the same signs Sable was trying not to leave: a fire ring built too well, a hoofprint in clay, the particular way two people who trusted each other moved through broken country—one always watching while the other slept.

Sable watched now, from beneath a granite overhang where Juniper dozed against their packs, her grandmother's blade wrapped in oilcloth beside her hand even in sleep. Her breathing had gone slow and even. Three days of hard road, and still she woke at every sound like a deer. He envied her nothing about this life he'd helped make necessary.

The rain thickened toward dusk. He counted his advantages the way an accountant counts coins that won't cover the debt: they were ahead by perhaps half a day; the road forked twice ahead, once at a shrine to a god whose name the crown had struck from the rolls; and Ives, whatever else he was, was honest—and honesty could be led.

That last thought sat poorly in Sable's chest. He had spent six years using honest men. He knew the weight of it.

* * *

They made the fork at full dark, and Sable chose wrong on purpose.

"The left track follows the water," Juniper said, peering into the black. "Faster going."

"Faster going, and every ford between here and the capital will have a picket on it by tomorrow night." He crouched, touching the right-hand track's mud, feeling for what wasn't there. "Ives knows I know the river roads. He'll expect me to take them."

"And if he doesn't?"

"Then we've lost half a day." He looked up at her—at the pale smudge of her face under the hood, rain beading on her lashes. "Half a day is cheap."

She studied him a moment longer than she needed to. She had gotten good at that lately: looking at him as though reading a page written small. "You keep choosing the harder road," she said. "Is that penance, or tactics?"

"Both," he said, because lying to her had become something he no longer seemed able to do, and she huffed—not quite a laugh—and shouldered her pack.

The right-hand track climbed. It was sheep-country, treeless, exposed, the kind of ground Sable would have cursed in his cavalry years. But it was also ground where no one could come at them unseen, and after the third mile he stopped flinching at every shadow, because there were no shadows, only rain and the vast wet dark and the wind combing the tussock grass with a sound like someone whispering names.

Juniper spoke little on the climb. When she did speak, it was the speech—the swordspeech—under her breath, syllables like struck flint, practicing. He had learned not to startle at it. He had not learned what it cost her; she went quiet afterward in a way that frightened him more than any rider could, quieter each time, as though each word took a coin out of her that she could not count.

At the top of the pass stood the ruined shell of a beacon-tower, one wall standing, and they sheltered in its lee and ate cold oats and harder bread, and Sable took first watch because he no longer pretended otherwise.

It was near midnight when he saw the fire.

Not theirs. A single point of light far down the valley they had climbed out of, red-orange, steady—a campfire built large enough to be seen. Built to be seen by anyone watching from the heights.

His stomach went cold and stayed cold.

"He wants us to see it," Juniper murmured. She was awake; he hadn't heard her wake. She came to stand beside him at the broken wall, close enough that her shoulder touched his arm. "Why would a hunter show his fire?"

"To tell me he's coming." Sable watched the flame. "Or to tell me he's already sent someone around."

The wind gusted. Somewhere below, a horse screamed—short, cut off—and both of them went still as stone.

"That wasn't a wild horse," Juniper said.

"No." Sable was already moving, pulling the oilcloth from her blade, pressing it into her hands. "Get your boots on. If it's a decoy fire, the real party is flanking us in the dark, and they'll have picked this tower for exactly the reason we did."

* * *

They came down the north side of the pass in the hour before dawn, when the dark is thinnest and men are stupidest, and Sable hated how well he knew that, hated that the knowledge lived in him like a second spine.

The ambush was waiting at the ford of a stream called, on no map Sable had ever seen, the Weep—shallow, braided, loud enough to drown footsteps. Four riders. He counted them by the way the water broke around their horses' legs, four interruptions in the silver noise, and then a fifth shape apart, on foot, standing in the shallows with a lantern hooded to a slit.

"Sable." The voice carried over the water, level, unhurried. Captain Ives had a voice like a ledger: everything entered, nothing owed. "You're on crown land, traveling with a proscribed gift-bearer. Dismount and yield, and I'll see you treated as a prisoner of rank, not a traitor."

"You always did lead with the generous offer," Sable said. He had drawn Juniper back behind a shelf of rock; he felt her coiled there, felt the blade's wrapped hilt in her grip, and prayed silently, uselessly, to gods who had never answered him, that it would not come to her.

"I learned negotiation from you," Ives said. "Come out where I can see you. Both of you. Or my crossbows come out where I can see you, and then nobody negotiates."

Two of the shapes in the stream raised crossbows. Sable could hear the cranks of their windlasses, the wet click of quarrels seating. His heart was doing something old and familiar, the cold arithmetic of a skirmish: four riders, two bows, one captain on foot, darkness, water noise, high ground behind them.

He could win this. That was the horror of it. Six years ago he would have won it without losing a man, and gone to breakfast after, and slept well.

"Ives," he called. "Step forward alone. Five minutes. That's all I ask."

"I don't have five minutes of anything for a man who tore off his colors and ran."

"You have this much," Sable said, "because you've been reading our trail for nine days, and a man who wanted us dead would have forced a fight on day three, at Marrow Ford, when we walked into your tollhouse wearing our guilt like badges. You let us go once. Why?"

The water roared. The lantern slit wavered, just slightly.

"Because I wanted to be wrong about you," Ives said at last, and there it was—the crack in the ledger, the entry that didn't balance. "And I've spent nine days finding out I'm not."

"Then step forward," Sable said, "and be wrong in person."

A long moment. Then Ives handed the lantern to one of the crossbowmen and waded up the shingle alone, sword sheathed, hands loose at his sides, and Sable stepped out of the dark to meet him, and the two of them stood an arm's length apart in the gray pre-light with the stream shouting between the stones.

Up close, Ives looked worse than the last time Sable had seen him—which had been across a checkpoint desk, both of them pretending. There were new lines around his eyes. His beard was trimmed with parade-ground precision, which Sable recognized as the grooming of a man holding himself together with routine.

"You look terrible," Sable said.

"You look like a man sleeping in ditches with a witch." Ives' jaw worked. "Tell me it isn't true. Tell me the reports are lies. Tell me you didn't burn the Ashmarket cell records, didn't spring two gifted children out of a purge wagon in front of forty witnesses, didn't—" He stopped. His voice had climbed; he brought it down by force. "Tell me you're not the thing the edicts say you are."

"You've seen the king," Sable said quietly.

Ives went very still.

"Don't lie to me, Tomas. Not tonight." Sable held his gaze. "You've stood in an audience chamber. You've watched him sign. And some part of you has known since that day that the man on the throne is not a man, and every order you've carried since has tasted like ash, and you came out here hoping I'd give you permission to feel what you already feel."

"You don't know what I—"

"I know exactly what you feel. I invented it." Sable's voice cracked on the word, and he let it. "I hunted gifted children for that throne, Ives. I told myself it was law. I told myself the king knew things I didn't. And then I read the man's face one morning and understood there was nothing behind it looking back, and I had to choose what to do with six years of what I'd done. You're standing in a stream deciding the same thing. So decide."

For a heartbeat—for one suspended, ringing heartbeat—Sable believed he had reached him. Ives' hand drifted toward his sword belt, and Sable thought: *not to draw, to unbuckle, to lay it down.*

Then the crossbowman on the left flank fired.

Whether it was nerves, or the dark, or an order misunderstood, Sable would never know. The bolt came out of the black trailing a thin whistle and took Ives high in the shoulder, spinning him sideways into the shallows, and the night came apart.

Sable moved without deciding to move. He was in the water hauling Ives up before the second bolt splashed where they'd knelt, dragging him behind the boulder shelf where Juniper crouched with the blade half-unwrapped, its steel drinking the starlight.

"He's hit—it was an accident—"

"I know." Her voice was tight. Above them, hooves churned the shingle; voices shouted confusion across the water. "Sable, they'll regroup in thirty seconds. We can't carry him and run."

"We're not carrying him." Sable pressed his palm hard against the wound, feeling the bolt head grind, hearing Ives hiss through his teeth. "Tomas. Listen. Your men can't see us. Call them off. Say the fugitives broke east. Say whatever keeps them on their horses and away from this bank for ten minutes."

"Or I call them in," Ives gasped, "and you're both taken, and I'm decorated."

"Yes," Sable said. "Or that."

The shouting was closer. Torchlight bloomed on the far bank. Juniper had risen, blade bare now, and Sable saw her lips begin to move—to gather the speech, words like drawn knives—and something in him refused it, refused to let her spend herself, spend *memories*, on this.

"No," he breathed. "Not yet. Not for me."

And Ives, blood sheeting his mail, looked from the blade to Sable's face and made his choice—or failed to make it, which may be the same thing.

"Stand down!" he roared, rolling onto his back to bellow at the sky. "Stand down, damn you—they've crossed east! East, along the ridge! Form on me at first light!" The torches swung away. Hooves thundered off into the dark, chasing a lie their own captain had bought with his blood.

Silence came back by degrees, the stream filling it, the rain starting again soft on the stones.

"Why," Ives said. He was shaking now, reaction setting in, gray-faced against the rock. "Why did you pull me out of the line of fire? I'd have put you in chains by noon."

"Because you're the only honest man they have left," Sable said, "and I'm not going to be the reason the crown runs out of those." He tore a strip from his cloak, packed the shoulder wound as Ives swore at him in three registers, and bound it rough and quick. "The bolt's through the meat, not the bone. You'll write reports with a stiff arm for a month."

"You should kill me." Ives laughed, a horrible small sound. "Any soldier worth his salt would. I'm wounded, disarmed, alone with the two most wanted people in the realm. This is the moment in the ballads where the hero does the sensible thing."

"I was never in a ballad." Sable stood. "Rest till dawn. Tell them whatever keeps you whole. But Tomas—if you report our true route, if you send them after us—know that you'll be sending them to a throne room where the thing sitting on it eats kings alive from the inside. And you'll have chosen that. With your eyes open. Like I once chose."

Ives stared up at him. Rain dripped from the brim of his helm into his eyes and he didn't blink it away.

"I have to report something," he said finally, and his voice was very quiet, and very tired. "That's the whole of it, Sable. Whatever I believe—I wear the king's colors. I report."

"I know," Sable said. "That's why I'm sparing you instead of trusting you."

* * *

They climbed away from the Weep as the sky went the color of dirty pewter, and neither of them spoke until the stream was a rumor far below.

"He'll report us," Juniper said at last. Not accusing. Just certain, the way she was certain of weather.

"He'll report the truth, or part of it. He's incapable of anything else—that's his tragedy and mine both, because I trained it into him." Sable glanced back once. The pass was empty. "He'll report that we're making for the capital. And the Hollow King will know we're coming, and set the table for us."

"Then why let him live?" She wasn't cruel when she asked it. She was tired, and young, and carrying a blade that whispered to her in a language of debts.

Sable walked a while before answering.

"Because if we win," he said, "someone has to rebuild what comes after. And it can't be built by people like Odile, who've only ever known how to hide, or people like me, who only ever knew how to burn. It needs a man who believes in the law even when the law fails him—so that when it stops failing, he's still standing there, ready." He looked at her. "Honor is going to fail against the institution, Juniper. I watched it fail in myself for six years. But honor is stubborn. It keeps failing upward. One day it fails all the way through to the other side, and that's the day institutions break."

She considered that with her head tilted, rain running off her chin. "You believe that?"

"I have to," Sable said. "Otherwise sparing him was just weakness, and I've never yet found a way to live with my weaknesses unexamined."

Behind them, far down in the gray country, a signal horn sounded—two notes, then three, the crown's pattern for *enemy sighted, route confirmed*. Ives was keeping his word to his colors, exactly as promised.

Sable turned his back on the sound and lengthened his stride.

"Capital's five days' hard walking," he said. "If we're going to walk into a trap, we'd best arrive before the table is fully set."

Juniper fell in beside him, and above the eastern hills, thin and cold, the sun came up like a blade being drawn.
