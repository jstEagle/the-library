## 12. Ashmarket Burning

The capital announced itself long before we saw it.

We had walked three days through country that grew steadily more obedient—hedgerows trimmed to a ruler's edge, roads graded smooth, milestones carved with the crowned sun of Aldric's line—and the whole time I felt it like a hand pressing between my shoulder blades. The gift rode in me differently now than it had in Odile's cellar, where I'd learned to hold my speech like a cupped flame. Out here, under an open sky that belonged to someone else, it pressed against the back of my teeth and waited.

"You're doing the thing again," Sable said.

"What thing?"

"The thing where you go quiet and your jaw does that." He nodded at me without breaking stride. "In the valley you were quiet because there was nothing to say. Now you're quiet because there's too much."

I hated how well he knew me already. Three weeks on the road will do that—will teach a person the geography of another's silences. "I'm trying to decide what Ashmarket will look like," I said. "Odile drew it for us. The tannery lane, the fountain with the cracked mermaid, the dyer's loft with the blue door."

"And?"

"And I keep imagining it full of people who don't know they're about to be hunted." I looked at him sideways. "You knew. Didn't you? When you tore off your colors—you knew what was coming for them."

He didn't flinch, which was its own kind of honesty. "I knew the shape of it. Not the size." His hand went briefly to his chest, where his colors would have been, the gesture he made when the old guilt surfaced. "The hunt doesn't announce itself, Juniper. It arrives like weather. One morning the quarter just... has soldiers in it."

* * *

We came over the last ridge at dusk, and the capital lay below us like a spilled jewel box—the palace on its crag catching the last red light, the river winding silver through the lower city, ten thousand chimneys breathing smoke into a sky gone the color of a bruise. It should have been beautiful. Some part of it was.

But Ashmarket sat in the eastern quarter, and above Ashmarket hung a stain.

Not fire—not anymore. Smoke, old and settled, the kind that means something burned days ago and no one bothered to put out the memory. And beneath it, where Odile's map promised rooftops and market stalls and the blue door of the dyer's loft, I could see gaps. Pale rectangles on the skyline where buildings had been pulled down rather than repaired. A street that ended in rubble instead of continuing on.

"They've already been here," I said. My voice came out flat, which was how grief arrived in me first—flat, before it found its weight.

Sable said nothing. He didn't need to. We stood together on the ridge while the light died, two small figures looking down at the evidence of everything we'd come too late to stop, and the wind carried up from the city a smell I hadn't smelled since childhood: burnt thatch, wet ash, and underneath it, faintly, the sweet-rot stink of charm-cord burning. They burned the cords too. The little woven things the gifted wore to focus their small magics. Odile had given me one once, a plain brown thing of braided rowan, and told me never to wear it where soldiers could see.

"Come on," Sable said at last. "If anyone got out, they'll be hiding. And if anyone's hunting them—" he flexed his sword-hand, "—they'll be easier to find in the dark."

* * *

Ashmarket by night was a wound pretending to be a neighborhood.

We entered through the tannery lane, hoods up, moving the way Odile had taught us—never hurrying, never lingering, belonging everywhere and nowhere. The market square itself was half-shuttered. Stalls stood empty with their awnings furled, and chalk marks ghosted across doorposts: the crown's tally-marks, counting households searched, persons taken, goods confiscated. Someone had tried to scrub one away and only smeared it into a gray smear like a bruise.

At the fountain with the cracked mermaid, water still ran, black and gleaming in the lamplight, and a woman knelt there filling jugs with the careful economy of someone who has learned not to look up. Sable drifted toward her the way he did—unthreatening, forgettable, a tired traveler asking directions. I watched him work. He'd gotten good at being invisible; it was the one skill his disgrace had taught him that I wished he didn't have.

He came back grim. "Curfew at second bell. Garrison posted at the east gate and the bridge. And—" he paused, choosing, "—the dyer's loft is a shell. Burned four nights ago. There are still ashes in the fountain where they dumped the buckets."

"The cell," I said. "Odile's people."

"Gone. Taken or scattered." He looked at me, and in the dark his eyes were very steady. "Juniper. If the network here is broken, our way into the palace may be broken with it. The dowager's letter came through these people."

"I know."

"We may have come all this way for nothing."

"No," I said. "We came all this way for *them*. The palace can wait a day. Tonight we find whoever's left."

* * *

We found them because children are bad at hiding grief, and worse at hiding hope.

It was past curfew when we heard it—a sound from the ruins near the old rope-walk, thin and rhythmic, that might have been wind in charred beams except that wind doesn't sing. A child's song, half-remembered, the kind sung to keep fear at bay. Sable and I exchanged one look and moved.

The rope-walk had been a long low building once; now it was a ribcage of blackened joists open to the sky. In the deepest corner of it, behind a fallen wall-section that made a room of sorts, two children had made a nest of sacking and burnt blanket. The older was a girl of perhaps nine, sharp-faced, with the soot-smudged wariness of a stray cat. The younger was a boy, five or six, asleep against her side with his thumb in his mouth and a scrap of blue cloth clutched in his fist.

Blue cloth. Dyer's blue. My heart dropped through the floor.

The girl saw us and went rigid, pulling her brother closer, and her eyes went to Sable first—to his height, his swordsman's shoulders, the soldier's bearing he couldn't shed any more than she could shed her hunger—and then to me, and something in my face must have told her something, because her chin came up and she said, fierce and shaking:

"If you're the ones who took Maren, you can't have Wren. You can't have either of us."

"Maren," I repeated softly. "Is that your mother? The dyer?"

Something flickered in the girl's face—the terrible hope of the abandoned. "You know her?"

"I know of her," I said carefully. "A friend of ours knows her. A hedge-witch named Odile. Blue door on the loft."

"Blue door's burned." The girl's voice cracked on it. "They came at night. Soldiers. Mama hid us in the dye-vats and told us to count to a thousand and not come out, and we counted, and when we came out—" Her breath hitched. She was nine years old and holding herself together with both hands and pure fury. "Wren won't stop asking when she's coming back. So I tell him she's coming back. Is that a lie? Mama says lies to soldiers are fine but lies to family aren't. Am I lying to him?"

I knelt down slowly, keeping low, keeping small. "What's your name?"

"Pike."

"Pike. I'm going to tell you the truth, because you're family now—all of us who they're hunting are family. Your mother was taken because she had a gift, and the men who took her were wrong, and there are people working to make it right. That's the truth. It isn't finished yet. But it's true."

She stared at me with those too-old eyes. Then, in the way of children, who see straight through to the load-bearing walls of a person: "You've got a gift too. I can see it. It's like—like heat off a forge. You're *full* of it."

Behind me, Sable went very still.

* * *

We should have left at dawn. That was the plan—find the survivors, pass word along whatever threads of Odile's network remained, and vanish before the garrison woke properly. But Wren woke feverish in the night, the little boy hot and shivering in Pike's arms, coughing with a wet rattle that made my own chest ache in sympathy, and you cannot carry a sick child through a checkpoint at dawn without questions. So we stayed. And staying, as it always does, cost more than leaving would have.

It was midmorning, second day, when the soldiers came back to finish their work.

We heard them before we saw them—the tramp of boots, the scrape of a cart, and a voice calling the quarter's remaining residents out of doors. "By order of the crown! All residents to the square! Anyone harboring the unregistered will share their sentence!"

Pike's face went white. "They're doing a sweep. They did one before—they line everyone up and walk down the rows with a sniffer-hound. It smells charm-residue." Her hand closed over Wren, who whimpered in his fever. "Mama said if it ever happened again, run for the culvert under the tannery wall. But Wren can't run. He can't even *stand*."

I looked at Sable. He looked at me, and I watched him do the arithmetic every hunted person does—two lives against two lives, the mission against the moment—and I watched him arrive, with visible relief, at the answer I'd already reached.

"There's no version of this where we let them take a feverish six-year-old," he said quietly. "Tell me what you need."

"Time," I said. "And a crowd that sees."

* * *

The square of Ashmarket held maybe two hundred souls that morning, mustered between the fountain and the ruined dyer's loft, and every one of them wore the particular stillness of people who have learned that attention is dangerous. The soldiers worked down the lines with their hound—a lean gray brute with a charmed collar that whined and strained toward anyone with residue on their hands. Two families had already been dragged from the line when we slipped in among the crowd, Pike's hand locked in mine, Wren bundled against my chest under my cloak, burning hot as a hearthstone.

The sergeant running the sweep was young and bored and cruel in the lazy way of men who have never been contradicted. He was three rows away when the hound suddenly swung its head toward me and began, softly, to sing.

Every head turned. The sergeant's boredom fell away like a dropped mask.

"Well," he said, and smiled. "Well, well. Looks like the ash hasn't finished giving up its witches."

He reached for me. And I made the choice I had been circling since the boundary stones, since the ridge, since the night I first spoke a forbidden word into the dark and felt the valley's silence answer—I made it the way you make any true choice, all at once, with your whole heart, knowing the price.

I set Wren gently down behind me. I stepped forward, out of the crowd, into the open space before the soldiers. And I spoke.

"*Stand.*"

Not shouted. Spoken—the way my grandmother had taught me words were meant to be spoken, with the whole weight of meaning behind them, the oath riding the syllable like a blade rides a whetstone. The word went out of me and struck the world, and the world obeyed. The sergeant froze mid-reach, arm extended, mouth open. The hound sat down as though its strings were cut. Twelve soldiers stopped as one, boots half-lifted, caught in the amber of my speech.

The silence afterward was the loudest thing I had ever heard.

Two hundred people staring. Soldiers frozen like garden statues. A feverish child coughing once, small and shocking, in the ringing quiet. I felt the cost begin immediately—that familiar cold pull behind my eyes, the valley starting to blur at its edges, my grandmother's kitchen going soft and distant in my memory—but I had learned in Odile's cellar to spend precisely, and I had saved for this.

"*Drop your arms. Kneel. You are done here.*"

They knelt. Twelve grown soldiers, kneeling in the ash of Ashmarket, faces slack with compelled obedience, and the crowd made a sound I had never heard a crowd make—not a scream, not a cheer, something between a prayer and a gasp, hundreds of voices exhaling at once.

I turned to the people. To the mothers with their hands over their children's mouths. To the old man by the fountain who had crossed himself with the sign against witchcraft and was now crossing himself for a different reason entirely. To Pike, gripping her brother, watching me with her mouth open.

"Go home," I said, and my voice was only a voice now, human-sized and shaking. "Bar your doors. Tell no one—no—" I stopped, because even as I said it I knew it was already untrue. "Tell everyone," I corrected. "Tell them what you saw. Tell them the gifted are not what the crown says. Tell them until they can't unhear it."

Then Sable's hand was on my elbow, firm and urgent, and we were moving—through the fountain lane, down the tanner's cut, Pike and Wren stumbling between us, the frozen soldiers behind us beginning, syllable by syllable, to thaw.

* * *

They say it took the garrison till sundown to get their sergeant to stand up. They say the hound wouldn't hunt for three days after, just sat by the fountain and whined. They say a woman walked out of the crowd with frost in her hair and a voice like a drawn sword, and that she knelt twelve armed men with a word apiece, and that she vanished into the smoke with two children and a knight with no colors, and that the ash itself rose up around her feet like it was bowing.

By the next evening they were telling it in the dockside taverns. By the week's end, in the villages on the river road. They gave me names I never chose—the Swordswoman of Ashmarket, the Grey Lady, the Oath-Bearer—and none of them were mine, and all of them were spreading, growing in the telling like ivy over a wall. Odile had warned me once, laughing sadly over her tea: *The crown fears the gifted, Juniper. But what it fears more is a story it can't arrest.*

That was the terrible visibility of it. Every whisper was a shield for the hunted and a target painted on all of us. Somewhere in the palace on its crag, I knew, a servant was repeating the tale to a steward, a steward to a chamberlain, a chamberlain to the throne—and on the throne, the thing wearing King Aldric's crown was turning its head, slow as a millwheel, toward the east quarter, toward the ash, toward *me*.

We sheltered that night in the culvert beneath the tannery wall, the one Pike's mother had shown her, Wren sleeping at last as his fever broke, Pike watching the entrance with a knife she held wrong and would not put down. Sable sat beside me in the dark, our shoulders touching, and for a long time neither of us spoke.

"You know what you've done," he said finally. Not accusing. Just knowing.

"I know."

"Not the rescue. That was—" he exhaled. "That was the single finest thing I've seen in twenty years of wearing a sword. I mean the rest of it. You've lit a beacon. Everything in this kingdom that hunts will turn toward that light. Including the thing on the throne."

"I know," I said again. And then, because it was true and he deserved truth: "I'd do it again. In front of twice the crowd. Let them all see."

He was quiet a moment. Then, softly, almost smiling in the dark: "I know. That's why I'm here."

Outside, far off across the sleeping city, a bell began to ring—not the curfew bell, but the palace bell, deep and urgent, tolling out over the rooftops the way it had not tolled in thirty years. Pike lifted her head. Sable's hand found mine in the dark and gripped it.

"That's the assembly bell," he said. "They only ring that when the crown calls the hunt home."

And somewhere beneath the ringing, faint but unmistakable, rising from the direction of the palace like smoke finding a chimney, came a sound I had heard only once before—in a vision, in Odile's scrying bowl. A voice with no body behind it, vast and patient and hungry, saying a single word into the bones of the city:

"*Found.*"
