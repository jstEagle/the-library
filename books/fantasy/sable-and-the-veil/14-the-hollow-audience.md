## 14. The Hollow Audience

They dressed her in borrowed silk.

The dowager's women came at the grey hour before dawn, moving through the hidden chambers beneath the east wing with the hushed efficiency of people long accustomed to secrets. They washed the road dust from Juniper's hair with water scented with rosemary and something older—myrrh, perhaps, or ash—and they combed it out and braided it back from her face in a style she did not recognize, a court style, a style that belonged to a country she had never been permitted to enter.

The gown was deep green, the color of pine shadow, of valley moss. She wondered if that was kindness or strategy.

"You'll bow," said the eldest of the women, a spare, silver-haired creature named Maren who had not smiled once in two days. "Once. Low enough to show respect, high enough to keep your eyes on him. Whatever he says—whatever he *offers*—you do not answer quickly. The slow tongue survives where the quick one drowns."

"And if he asks me to speak?" Juniper asked. Her voice sounded strange to her own ears, scrubbed and dressed as it was.

Maren's hands stilled on the clasp of a necklace—a thin chain, a single pearl, nothing that could be mistaken for an amulet. "Then you speak," she said quietly. "That is what you came to do, isn't it?"

It was not a question, and Juniper did not pretend otherwise.

* * *

Sable was waiting for her in the antechamber, and the sight of him stopped her breath for half a heartbeat longer than she would have admitted to anyone.

He wore his old uniform.

Not the colors—he had torn those from his shoulders three years ago and had never once spoken of it without his jaw going tight—but the cut of them, the dark grey coat with its plain silver buttons, the sword at his hip that was ceremonial in shape and anything but in weight. He looked, for one terrible moment, like the man who had ridden out of the capital at the head of a hunt. Then he looked up at her, and he was only Sable again: tired, watchful, and carrying guilt the way other men carried scars.

"You look like someone I used to arrest," he said.

"You look like someone who'd have done the arresting."

Something moved through his face—not quite a smile, but the place where a smile lived when it was too frightened to come out. He crossed the room to her, and his hands hovered near her shoulders without landing, as though he needed permission even now, even here, with the world about to end or begin.

"Juniper." Her name in his mouth had become, over these months of road and rain and shared firelight, a kind of shelter. Now it sounded like a door closing. "If it goes wrong—if he takes you—you hold to your own voice. Not mine, not Odile's, not the dowager's. Yours. It's the only thing down there that isn't his."

"I know."

"He offered me everything once, too. Did I tell you that? The first year. Before I understood what he was." His eyes were fixed somewhere past her shoulder, on a memory. "My brother's pardon. My father's lands restored. All I had to do was sign my name to a list." A pause. "I signed it."

"I know that too," she said gently. "You've told me. Every night since Marrow Ford, some version of it."

"Because if I stop telling it, I'll start believing I'm clean." He finally let his hands settle on her shoulders, warm through the borrowed silk. "You're not clean either. None of us are. That's why he'll win, if he wins—because we all want something back that we've lost, and he knows exactly how much we want it."

The bells above them began to toll the hour. Somewhere far overhead, muffled by stone and gilding, the palace woke into its performance of normalcy: footsteps, doors, the distant music of a court that did not know—or refused to know—what sat upon its throne.

"The audience is granted for midmorning," Sable said. "Public, which is worse. The whole court will be there to see the swordspeaker kneel." His thumb traced a small, unconscious arc against her collarbone. "Come back out of there. That's all. Come back out."

* * *

The throne room had been described to her by three different people, and none of their descriptions had prepared her.

It was beautiful the way a spiderweb is beautiful, if you are small enough to mistake it for morning light. Vaulted ceilings painted with constellations no astronomer would claim; pillars of white marble veined with gold, so that the light falling through the high windows seemed to bleed as it descended; a floor of polished stone so black and so deep that walking across it felt like walking on the surface of a well. Courtiers lined the walls in tiers, a garden of silk and powdered faces, and every one of them watched her with the same expression—the carefully blank courtesy of people who have learned that looking too closely at the throne is hazardous to the soul.

And the throne itself.

It stood on a dais of seven steps, wrought from ironwood and pale stone, and it had been beautiful once. She could see the ghost of that beauty still: the carved oak leaves along its arms, the sunburst at its crown, the workmanship of a kingdom that had believed in itself. But something had gotten into the wood. The carvings were wrong now, subtly, the way a face is wrong in a dream—the leaves curling inward instead of out, the sunburst hollowed at its center, as though something had eaten the light out of it and left the shape behind. The stone around the throne's base was darkened in a wide ring, stained as if by centuries of spilled wine, though the palace was barely two hundred years old.

Juniper walked the length of that black floor with her grandmother's blade wrapped in cloth at her back, declared and permitted, because the Hollow King wanted her armed. Wanted her close. Wanted her *listening*.

She bowed as Maren had taught her. Low enough. High enough.

"Rise, Juniper Brightwater."

The voice came from the throne, and it was the worst thing yet, because it was a kind voice. Warm. Weary. The voice of a king grown old in service to his people, carrying burdens no one else could see. It rolled down the seven steps and across the black floor and wrapped around her like a fur cloak in winter, and every instinct in her body said *this is safety, this is home, kneel here.*

Her grandmother's training held her spine straight. *A speaker's first defense is silence,* Elara Brightwater had taught her, hands over a child's hands on a wooden practice sword. *Let the words come to you before you send yours out.*

She rose. She looked at the king.

King Aldric was fifty-one years old, the dowager had told her, though he did not look it. He looked like a portrait of himself left too long in the sun: all the lines correct, the strong jaw, the greying temples, the famous Aldric blue eyes—but faded somehow, thinned, as though the man inside had been stretched to cover a frame too large for him. He wore the crown loosely, tilted, the way a man wears a hat he has forgotten he is wearing.

And his hands, folded on the armrest of that hungry throne, were trembling.

No one else saw it. The court saw majesty. Sable, standing among the guards at the chamber's edge in his colorless grey, saw what Juniper saw—she knew because his breath changed, a small sharp sound swallowed immediately.

The king spoke, and the court leaned in to listen.

"We have heard much of you, daughter of the Veiled Valley. The last of an order older than this crown. They say your speech can cut stone. Bind rivers. Unmake promises." A smile, gentle and sad. "We confess we hoped never to meet you. And yet here you stand, and here We sit, and the kingdom between us grows stranger by the day."

"Your Majesty honors me with the meeting," Juniper said, and her voice did not shake, and she was proud of that later.

"Do We?" The king tilted his head. "Or does the crown? It is difficult, sometimes, to know where the man ends and the office begins. Kings are made of their kingdoms, swordspeaker, as houses are made of their stones. Remove a stone and the wall leans. Remove enough—" He gestured, a small elegant motion that took in the tiers of courtiers, the banners, the vast painted sky overhead. "Well. One does one's best with what remains."

*Careful,* whispered her grandmother's voice in her blood. *It talks around things. It talks around things the way water talks around a drowning man.*

"I have come to petition the crown," Juniper said. "For mercy toward the gifted. For an end to the hunts."

"Mm." The king's gaze drifted, and for a moment it snagged on hers, and something happened.

She would spend the rest of her life trying to describe it, and never succeed entirely. It was as if a window opened in a wall she had taken for solid stone. The faded quality of him gathered, concentrated, became suddenly, unbearably *present*—the blue eyes flooding with color, the trembling hands gripping the armrest, the mouth working around words that would not come. His lips shaped soundlessly: *run.* Then: *help.* Then something longer, desperate, the jaw straining, a tear cutting a bright line down the royal cheek while the face above it remained composed, gracious, faintly amused—

*Me,* the silent mouth said. *I'm in here. Please. I'm in here—*

The court saw nothing. To them, their king paused thoughtfully, considering the petitioner's words.

Juniper's heart slammed against her ribs. *Aldric.* Alive. Caged behind his own face, watching through eyes that were not his, for how long—years, years of watching his hands sign edicts, his voice order burnings, his body rise each morning and put on the crown like a yoke—

The window closed.

It closed the way a hand closes over a candle flame. The king blinked, and the flood receded, and the warmth came back into his voice like oil poured over water, smoothing everything flat.

"But We digress," he said pleasantly. "Mercy. Yes. A curious word to bring before a throne, swordspeaker. Mercy implies wrongdoing, and the crown does not consider protection to be wrongdoing. Fire must be managed. Rivers must be banked. Gifts—" the word came out of his mouth with a faint wet click, like a bone shifting, "—must be gathered, lest they gather themselves. You of all people understand oaths. Duty. The surrender of the self to something greater."

"I understand," Juniper said, "that oaths freely given bind both ways. That is what makes them sacred rather than servile."

"A fine distinction." The king rose.

It should have been majestic. It was not. There was something wrong in the mechanics of it, a smoothness without effort, the way a puppet rises when the strings pull—all the right movements arriving a half-breath after they should, as though the man were being operated from slightly outside his own skin. He descended the seven steps. The court bowed in waves, a field of silk bending before wind.

He stopped three paces from her. Close enough that she could smell him—cedar and ink and, beneath it, something like the air inside a sealed room, stale and sweet and empty.

Close enough that she could see his eyes properly.

They were blue. They were Aldric's blue. And they were full of the king, the way a well is full of water, and there was nothing at the bottom.

"Tell Us what you want, Juniper Brightwater," the Hollow King said softly, and the whole court strained to hear, and heard only tenderness. "Everyone who comes before a throne wants something. The wise ones admit it. What does the last swordspeaker desire? Speak freely. Here, of all places, your words cannot harm Us."

*There it is.* The invitation, silk-wrapped. *Speak. Use the gift. Show Us its shape, its reach, its cost. Let Us learn what We must guard against.*

She kept her hands loose at her sides. Kept the oath quiet in her chest, coiled, unspent.

"I want the burning to stop," she said. "I want children to stop being taken from their beds for charms against toothache. I want the gifted of this kingdom to live under law instead of under fear."

"A noble inventory." The king smiled. "And easily granted. Hear Us, then—all of you, hear your king." His voice lifted, filled the vaulted dark, and every courtier went rigid with attention. "The hunts are ended. From this hour, the gathering of the gifted ceases. Let it be proclaimed in every shire."

A rustle ran through the court—relief, confusion, delight. Somewhere at the chamber's edge, Sable had gone very still.

Juniper did not move. Her grandmother's voice, calm as deep water: *Nothing is given free by that thing. Nothing.*

"Granted," the king continued, softer now, turning back to her alone, "in exchange for a conversation. A private one. You and We, swordspeaker, in the old council room, with no ears but the walls'. You wish to negotiate terms for these people you love. We wish to discuss"—a pause, delicate as a knife laid flat—"your oath. Its history. Its *purpose*. There are matters in this kingdom older than either of us, and We think you carry a key whose lock you have never seen."

Every instinct screamed. And beneath the screaming, quieter, more dangerous: *he's offering what you came for. Terms. An end to the hunt. A door into the truth.*

"I will hear terms," Juniper said carefully, "before any private audience. Publicly stated."

The king laughed.

It was a good laugh, warm, human, and it was the most horrifying sound in the entire chamber, because somewhere underneath it, muffled, frantic, was another laugh trying to get out—Aldric's laugh, maybe, or just the man's terror wearing laughter's shape.

"Oh, you are *good*," the king murmured, for her alone. "Elara trained you well. Yes, We know our Elara. We remember her grandmother, too, and hers before her. We have known every Brightwater who ever carried that blade, little speaker. We have *waited* for you." His head tilted, birdlike, and for one flickering instant the court vanished from his attention entirely, as though he had stepped out of the world to stand closer to her. "Do you know why the valley forgets? Why magic dies at those boundary stones? Your ancestors built that silence. Built it to hide the key from Us. Clever, cruel, loving fools. And now you have walked out of it, and brought the key to Our doorstep, and you ask Us for *terms*."

His hand rose—slowly, courteously, asking permission even as it moved—and touched her cheek.

The court gasped at the intimacy. Juniper did not flinch. Flinching was a language, and she refused to speak it.

"Here are Our terms," the king said, and his voice dropped into registers no human throat should hold, harmonics stacking beneath the words like roots beneath a tree. "Your valley, restored. Not merely returned—*restored*. Your grandmother alive in it, kneading bread in her kitchen, scolding you for tracking mud across her clean floor. The shrine mended. Tam home and laughing. Every dead thing you mourn breathing again, every burned bridge rebuilt, every year of exile folded up like a letter and posted back to the past where it belongs."

The chamber fell away.

She fought it and fell anyway. The black floor became moss. The vaulted dark became open sky, pine-shadow, the smell of woodsmoke and rain-on-stone, and her grandmother's voice calling her in for supper across a twilight she had thought she would never hear again, and Tam was there, grinning gap-toothed by the shrine, and the ache that had lived in her chest since the riders came simply—wasn't. Lifted out like a splinter drawn clean.

It lasted three heartbeats. She tore herself back by main force, by the memory of Sable's voice saying *come back out*, by the cold touch of the wrapped hilt against her spine.

The king watched her surface with an expression of profound, patient pleasure.

"You see," he said gently. "We can give it. We are not lying—that is the tragedy of Us, swordspeaker, We never lie. Everything We offer, We can deliver. Ask the drowned villages. Ask the mothers who prayed for dead sons and received them, warm and breathing and *empty*." His fingers trailed from her cheek. "All We ask in return is the key. The oath, surrendered willingly. Spoken into Our keeping. Do that, and go home to everything you have ever loved."

The court heard none of it. To them, their king and the green-dressed petitioner stood in silence, and the silence stretched, and stretched.

Juniper found Sable's eyes across the chamber. He was white to the lips. He shook his head once, slowly, and his hand rested on his sword hilt not in threat but in the way a man grips a rope.

*He's offering you your dead,* Sable's face said. *Say no. Say no. Say no.*

And beneath that, deeper, in the place where her oath lived like a second heartbeat, Juniper felt the truth of it settle cold and clear: the Hollow King ate oaths. Odile had told her. It had eaten a kingdom's worth already—every vow sworn on that throne, every coronation promise, every knight's fealty, feeding, growing, hollowing. If she gave it hers, the last key turned in the last lock, and whatever door it opened would open forever.

But oh, her grandmother's kitchen. Oh, the bread and the scolding and the rain.

"Your Majesty is generous," Juniper said, and her voice came out steady, and she marveled at it from a great distance. "I will consider your terms."

The king's smile widened by a fraction. Just enough.

"Consider quickly," he said, warm as a hearth, cold as a grave. "We find We grow impatient, and impatience makes Us... careless with the things We hold. Your young friend Tam, for instance. Such a fragile thing to keep safe in a city like this one."

The floor did not fall away this time. She held. But her nails bit four crescents into her palms, and she bowed—low, lower than courtesy required, hiding her face so that no one, not the court, not the guards, not the thing in the king's skin, could see what was written there.

When she straightened, the audience was over. Trumpets. Bows. The machinery of majesty grinding back into motion around her.

And as she turned to walk back down the long black floor, the king spoke once more, so quietly that only she could hear it, in a voice that was suddenly, terribly human—cracked, exhausted, and pleading:

"*Don't take too long. I don't know how much longer I can hold the door.*"

* * *

Sable caught up with her in the corridor beyond, and she got three steps into his arms before her legs remembered how to fail.

"He's in there," she whispered into his shoulder. "Sable, he's *alive*, he's fighting, he begged me—" Her breath hitched. "And it offered me my grandmother. My grandmother, alive, and I *wanted* it, I wanted it so badly I couldn't breathe—"

"I know." His hand cradled the back of her head. "I know. I wanted my brother's pardon the same way."

"It knows about Tam. It'll use him. It's going to use everyone I love, one at a time, until I hand it the key."

"Then we don't let it get them one at a time." He pulled back to look at her, and his eyes were fierce and afraid in equal measure. "The dowager's passage opens tomorrow night. The vaults. Once we're below, once we've seen what the first binding cost and what the unbinding requires—we end this. No more audiences. No more offers."

Juniper nodded. But as they walked from the throne room's shadow into the ordinary daylight of the corridor, one thought followed her like a footstep, soft and patient and impossible to outrun:

The Hollow King had never lied.

Which meant that somewhere in this palace, warm and breathing and waiting, her grandmother might truly be within its power to give back—and some treacherous, grieving corner of her heart was already calculating the price.
