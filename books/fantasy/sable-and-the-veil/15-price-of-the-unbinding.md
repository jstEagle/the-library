## 15. Price of the Unbinding

The oath-vaults kept their own weather.

Sable had noticed it on their first descent, three nights past: a stillness down here that was not silence but something older, a held breath. The air tasted of iron filings and candle smoke and, beneath it, faintly, of rain on old stone—the ghost of some storm that had fallen centuries ago and never finished falling. The dowager's servants lit no torches in these corridors; they carried instead small lamps of green glass whose flames burned without flickering, as if even fire had sworn an oath down here and meant to keep it.

He walked them alone now, in the hour before dawn, when the palace above slept its uneasy sleep and the vaults belonged to the dead and to him.

The records were kept in the Long Gallery, a corridor so deep beneath the throne room that Sable could feel, through the soles of his boots, a slow pulse in the stone. He had told himself at first it was water moving somewhere far below, some underground river keeping its ancient time. He no longer told himself that. He knew what pulsed beneath the palace. He had knelt before it for eleven years and called it majesty.

* * *

The archives of the first binding filled seven shelves, and most of it was ceremony—proclamations, genealogies, the ornate self-congratulation of men who believed they were founding a dynasty rather than leasing their bloodline to a hunger. Sable read by green lamplight with his sword across his knees, an old habit from nights he did not trust, and copied out anything that touched the unbinding into a leather folio he had bought in Ashmarket, back when Ashmarket still stood.

It was near the bottom of the sixth shelf, in a ledger bound in cracked white leather, that he found the passage.

The hand was old-fashioned, cramped, the ink browned to rust. A chronicler of Aldric's grandfather's reign, recording—in a tone of scholarly detachment that made Sable's stomach turn—the death of the last swordspeaker of that age.

*The woman Veyra came to the vaults in the winter of her forty-third year,* the ledger said. *She had been summoned by the Queen Dowager of that time, as Juniper Brightwater has been summoned now, and she came willingly, though the records of her coming show she wept at the boundary stones and could not say why.*

Sable's hand stopped moving.

*It is written in the oldest accounts that the unbinding words cannot be spoken while the oath stands whole, for the oath is the lock's twin and will not permit its own opening. The speaker must therefore break herself first. Not merely renounce the oath—that any fool may do, and many fools have tried, and the spirit laughs at them—but surrender it entire: the gift, the speech, the memory that feeds it, the self that was forged around it. The swordspeaker does not speak the words so much as become them, and what becomes a word cannot remain a woman.*

*Veyra spoke the unbinding in the reign of King Osric. The Hollow was cast down—or so it seemed—and Veyra walked out of the vaults alive. But she remembered nothing of the valley that made her. She remembered nothing of the blade she carried or the mother who gave it to her. Witnesses record that she lived nine years more in the capital, quiet, harmless, tended by the crown as one tends a relic, and that when she died she was smiling at a window as though she had seen something lovely there, though the window looked out on the mews.*

*The cost, then, is this: all of it. The gift, the past, the name. And perhaps more than this—for the second unbinding, attempted in secret by a knight of Osric's guard who loved her, killed him where he stood, and left Veyra hollowed so deeply that some said the Hollow had simply changed houses.*

Sable set down the quill.

His hands were steady. That was the terrible thing—he had spent twenty years learning steadiness, and now it served him only too well. He sat in the green light among the dead kings' paperwork and understood, with the flat clarity of a man reading his own sentence, that Juniper Brightwater was going to die down here. Not quickly, perhaps. Perhaps not even entirely. But the woman who walked out of these vaults would not be the woman who walked in, and the difference between those two women would be everything he—

Everything he.

He closed the folio. His reflection looked back at him from the dark glass of the lamp, gaunt and colorless, a man without colors still, and he thought: *I tore off my colors rather than hunt the gifted. I have been proud of that for two years. And I am going to stand in a throne room and watch her unmake herself, because I need her to do it, because there is no other way, and I will let her.*

He put out the lamp and sat a long time in the pulse-dark before he went up to find her.

* * *

She was in the dowager's east rooms, which the old queen had given over to them entirely—a suite of chambers behind the aviary court where the windows looked onto a wall of climbing roses gone wild and thorned, and no guard tower overlooked them. Juniper sat cross-legged on the floor with her grandmother's blade across her lap, practicing.

Not the sword-work. The speech.

She did not know he watched her from the doorway. Her eyes were half-closed, her lips moving around shapes that made the candle flames lean toward her like listeners, and the air in the room had gone strange and thick, tasting of ozone and crushed mint. Words in no living language moved along the blade's fuller like water finding a channel, faintly luminous, and where they passed the steel sang—not audibly, but in the teeth, in the breastbone, a note that Sable felt in the scar tissue over his ribs where a swordspeaker's word had once cut him and, instead of killing him, had *unmade* the order he'd been given, the order to burn a farmhouse with a family inside it.

That word had saved his soul and cost him his career. He had never told her that. There had never been a right moment, and then there had never been a moment that wasn't running or hiding or bleeding, and now he stood in a doorway watching her practice the words that would eat her alive, and understood that there would never be a right moment again, and that this—this doorway, this candlelight, this last ordinary hour—was the right moment, because it was the only one.

"Juniper."

The lights guttered. The words on the blade dimmed and slid away like fish into deep water. She looked up, and for a heartbeat her face was open and young and tired, and then she saw his expression and shuttered it, the way she'd learned to do in the valley, the way you learn to do when your whole life is a thing that must be hidden.

"You found something," she said.

"I found something."

"Tell me."

He came into the room and sat down across from her, because his legs wanted sitting. He laid the folio between them on the floorboards, and the little sound it made—leather on wood, soft as a sigh—seemed very loud.

"There was a speaker before you," he said. "Veyra. Under King Osric, sixty years ago. She performed the unbinding. It worked." He made himself go on, evenly, the way you report a battlefield. "The Hollow was cast down. Osric ruled free for the rest of his life. But the unbinding requires the speaker to break the oath first—entirely. Not renounce it. Surrender it. The gift, the memories that feed it, the self that was built around it. She came out of the vaults remembering nothing. No valley. No blade. No mother. Nine years she lived after, and she didn't know her own name without being told it."

Juniper was very still. The candle flames had steadied, but they burned low, as if drawing breath.

"And the second part," Sable said, and his voice did not quite hold level, and he hated it, and went on anyway. "There was a knight. Osric's guard. He loved her. When she spoke the unbinding, the backlash—the oath tearing loose—it killed him where he stood. The record says the Hollow was nearly freed into *him*, that it changed houses, that they barely drove it out. He died holding her hand."

Silence. Somewhere above them, through the rose-wall, a night bird called twice and thought better of it.

"So," Juniper said at last. Her voice was quiet and terribly reasonable, the voice she used for hard things. "You're telling me I might die. Or worse—I might live, and be no one. An empty room with a smile in it." She looked down at the blade across her knees. "And you're telling me this because you want me to stop."

"I'm telling you because you deserve to choose with your eyes open."

"No." She shook her head slowly. "You're telling me because you want me to stop. Say it plainly, Sable. You've taught me that much—say the true thing plainly, even when it costs."

He had taught her that. On the road, in the bad weeks, he had hammered it into her like a lesson in drill: *the true thing, plainly, even when it costs.* And now it had come around on him like a flanked position, and he found he could not take his own advice, because the true thing plainly was: *I cannot do this. I did not tear off my colors and walk two hundred miles to hand the woman I love to a fire and call it duty.* And the other true thing, plainer and worse, was: *and I will let her do it anyway, because the alternative is a kingdom eaten alive and every gifted child in it hunted like Tam was hunted, and I know which choice I would despise myself for.*

"Say it," she whispered.

"I want you to stop." It came out rough. "There. Plainly. Find another way. Take the dowager's access, take Ives's knights, take the whole damned network—we get Aldric out of the throne room bodily, we put him somewhere warded, we starve the thing, we—"

"With what warding?" she asked gently. "Odile says the old wards are broken. With what power? The Hollow eats oaths, Sable. It ate a king. It's eaten four kings, if the vaults are right, one heir after another, younger each time. You want to starve it? It's been dining on a kingdom for sixty years." She laid her palm flat on the blade. "This is the key. My grandmother's grandmother's grandmother forged it for exactly this. I'm not a weapon someone pointed at the problem. I'm the *answer* to it. It's been waiting in me since before I was born."

"Then it can wait longer—"

"It can't." For the first time her voice cracked, and he saw the fear underneath the reason, the girl from the valley who had buried everyone she loved and practiced silence over their graves. "Aldric surfaced today. In the audience. For one moment he was *there*, Sable, looking out of his own face, begging. If I wait, the Hollow takes him under for good, and then there's nothing left in that body to save, and everything I promised him—" She stopped. Breathed. "You held me anchored once already. On the ridge, when the riders came. Your voice. You don't get to decide I'm too precious to risk. You get to decide whether you'll be there."

"That's not fair."

"No," she agreed. "None of it is."

He surged to his feet because sitting had become unbearable, and crossed to the window, and stood looking at the thorned roses without seeing them. Behind him he heard her rise too, heard the whisper of the blade being set down—set down, deliberately, so that her hands would be empty for whatever came next.

"You know what I did tonight?" he said to the glass. "I sat in the dark and I read about a man who died holding her hand, and do you know what I felt? Not fear. *Relief.* Because if it kills me, at least I won't have to live in the world afterward. At least I won't have to be the one who remembers." He turned. "I've been the one who remembers, Juniper. I remember every door I kicked in for the hunt. Every name on every list I carried. I remember a woman in Marrow Ford who spat at me and she was *right* to spit. I am not afraid of dying beside you. I am terrified—" his voice broke clean through, and he let it, "—of standing in that throne room afterward, whole, and watching you look at me with Veyra's eyes. Kind. Empty. Smiling at a window. And not knowing why my voice is the one thing left in the world that feels like home."

The room had gone very quiet.

Juniper crossed the floor to him. Slowly, the way you approach a wounded animal, or a wounded man, which amounted to the same thing. She stopped close enough that he could see the candlelight caught in the grey of her eyes, and the fine scars on her knuckles from years of shrine work, and the fact that her hands were shaking.

"Say the rest of it," she said softly.

"Juniper—"

"You've been carrying it since the ridge. Since before the ridge. Since the tollhouse, maybe, when you lied to Ives with your arm around me and your heart hammering like a war drum—I could hear it, Sable, I was pressed against you, I've been able to hear it for months." Her voice trembled and did not stop. "Say the rest of it, because tomorrow I walk into those vaults, and I am not doing it with this still unsaid between us. Whatever happens down there, I refuse to arrive at the end of my life with the truest thing in it unspoken. *Say it.*"

And Sable—who had faced charges of treason with a steady tongue, who had torn his colors off in front of his own captain, who had lied to Ives and outwalked the hunt and knelt in the pulse-dark of the vaults without flinching—found that the truest thing, plainly spoken, undid him utterly.

"I love you," he said. "I have loved you since the valley, since you stitched me up and fed me and asked nothing, since you chose mercy over law for a stranger who deserved it less than anyone you'll ever meet. I love you and I have no right to, I hunted people like you, I built the machine you're sacrificing yourself to break, and I love you, and I would trade every year I have left for one of yours, and I am asking you—" his voice failed and he dragged it back, "—I am asking you not to go down there alone. Let me stand with you. Let me hold the anchor. If the oath tears loose and comes for the nearest soul, let it come for mine. I have less to lose than you do. I have *only* you."

For a long moment she didn't move. Then her forehead came to rest against his chest, and he felt her breath shudder out of her, and her hands came up and gripped the front of his shirt like a drowning woman gripping rope.

"Foolish knight," she whispered, and it was half laughter, half grief. "You have more to lose than anyone. That's what makes it worth anything."

They stood that way while the candles burned low, two exiles in a stolen room above the sleeping palace, holding on to each other above the slow pulse of the thing below. And when she finally lifted her face, it was calm—not the shuttered calm of the valley, but something chosen, settled, hers.

"At dawn," she said. "We go down together. You'll anchor me with your voice, and if the words start taking me before the binding breaks, you keep speaking. Anything. Anything at all. Don't let me drift."

"What should I speak?"

She smiled, tired and fierce and certain, and reached up to touch his jaw.

"The truth," she said. "Plainly. Even when it costs."

* * *

They did not sleep. They sat together in the east room with the blade between them and made their preparations the way soldiers do—quietly, precisely, saying the hard parts aloud so they would not ambush them later. Juniper taught him the cadence of the anchoring words, the ones her grandmother had used to call her home from trances, and made him repeat them until his tongue stopped stumbling. He showed her the folio, the passages about Veyra, and she read them twice without flinching, though her thumb lingered long on the line about the knight who died holding her hand.

"He wasn't wrong to stay," she said at last.

"No," Sable said. "He wasn't wrong to stay. He was just early."

Near the gray hour, when the candles had drowned in their own wax and the sky beyond the rose-wall turned the color of a blade's edge, a soft knock came at the door. The dowager's serving-woman, gray-haired and silent, with a folded paper on a salver.

Sable broke the seal. Read it once. Felt the floor tilt beneath him, very slightly, the way the world tilts when the ground itself turns traitor.

"What is it?" Juniper asked, rising.

He handed her the letter without a word, because the true thing, plainly, even when it costs—and the letter, in the dowager's precise hand, said only this:

*The Hollow King knows. It has moved the judgment forward. You are summoned to the throne room at sunrise—not as a petitioner. As a prisoner. They are bringing the boy.*
