## 16. The Hunt Comes Home

The dream came again that night—the one where the valley forgot her.

She stood at the boundary stones and called her grandmother's name, and the mist answered with someone else's voice. She woke with salt on her lips and Sable's cloak pooled over her feet where she'd kicked it in her sleep, and for a long moment she lay listening to the rain on Odile's roof and tried to remember which memories were still hers and which had gone to feed her gift.

The house above the dye-shop smelled of madder root and tallow and the particular green scent of wet stone. Somewhere below, Odile's people moved through their small hours—a floorboard's complaint, the soft clink of a charm being wrapped in wool so it wouldn't sing. Juniper counted them the way she'd once counted sheep: Wren, who could make locks weep open. The twins, whose gift was weather and whose quarrels were worse. Old Maren, who read ash. Tam, asleep in the loft since they'd pulled him out of Ashmarket's smoke, dreaming whatever boys dreamed when the world had tried to burn them.

*They know you're here,* said a voice in her blood, quiet as frost forming. *I have always known.*

Juniper sat up in the dark. Her hand found the hilt of her grandmother's blade out of habit, though there was nothing to cut. The Hollow King did not send dreams. It sent certainty. And certainty, she was learning, was worse.

---

It began three days later, on a morning so ordinary it felt like a trap set by the gods.

Odile was grinding pigment in the back room, teaching Juniper to bind a warding word into paint—*speech that stays*, she called it, speech that could be brushed onto a doorframe and keep watch while its speaker slept. Juniper's brush hovered over the plank. The word she meant to write was *hold*. What came out of her, unbidden, in a voice like a drawn wire, was:

*Run.*

The pigment pot cracked down its seam. Every bird left the eaves at once, a black exhalation against the grey sky. And from somewhere across the city—far, but not far enough—came a sound Juniper had heard only once before, at the boundary of her valley, the day the riders came: the low braided thunder of many hooves moving as one will.

"They've stopped pretending," Odile said. She hadn't flinched at the cracking pot or the word or the birds. She was already rolling up her sleeves, already reaching for the bell-pull that would wake the network. Her hands were steady. Her eyes were not. "Child, how long have you known it could feel you?"

"Since the audience." Since the throne room, since Aldric's face surfacing through the thing that wore him like a coat, since the moment the Hollow King had looked at her and *smiled* with a mouth that wasn't its own. "Since it offered me my dead."

"Then you knew this morning would come." Odile wiped her hands on her apron, leaving two red ghosts. "Good. Forewarned is only half of forearmed, but it's the half most people skip." She raised her voice—not loud, never loud; Odile's power lived in precision—and spoke a single syllable that shivered through the floorboards like a struck tuning fork. All over the quarter, Juniper knew, doors were being barred, children were going down into cellars, charms were being smothered under wool and ash. "Now listen. You are the most dangerous thing in this house, and I don't mean your sword. If the hunt has come home, it has come for you. Which means every soul under this roof is a nail holding down your shadow. Do you understand what I'm telling you?"

"That I should run."

"That you should *choose*," Odile said. "There's a difference, and it's the whole difference. Run if running saves them. Stay if staying does. But choose with your eyes open, swordspeaker, because the Hollow King doesn't want your death. It wants your surrender. Remember that when it shows you something you can't bear to lose."

Juniper thought of Tam in the loft. Of the way he'd started sleeping with his shoes on, ready to run, because once was enough to teach a boy that lesson.

"I remember," she said.

Outside, the thunder of hooves grew teeth.

* * *

They came at noon, when the market was thickest and the smoke of ten thousand cookfires stood straight up in the windless air—came not as a raid but as a coronation, banners unfurled, trumpets braying the royal hunt-call that every child in the capital learned before they learned their letters. The Grey Company, three hundred strong, riding abreast down the Middenway with writs nailed to every door they passed.

Wren got the first door open and the last child through the cellar hatch before the first rider dismounted. It didn't matter. It had never been going to matter. Juniper watched from the dye-shop window as the Company fanned through Ashmarket with the terrible unhurried competence of men who had done this a hundred times and been thanked for it afterward. They weren't searching. They were harvesting.

Maren went first—old Maren, who read ash, who had laughed at Juniper's terrible attempt at dicing two nights ago and let her win anyway. They dragged her into the street by her white braid and made her kneel in the gutter while an officer read her crimes from a scroll: divination, sedition, consorting with the abjured knight Sable of the Grey. Maren spat at his boots. They took her hands.

Juniper's fingers closed on the windowsill hard enough to crack the wood. Beside her, Sable had gone very still, the stillness of a man watching his own history repeat itself in a new key. "If you go out that door," he said quietly, "three hundred crossbows go up, and everything Maren suffered today becomes a rehearsal."

"I know what it costs."

"You know what it costs *you*." He turned, and his eyes were the grey of the Company's banners, and she hated him a little for wearing them. "I led those lines, Juniper. I know exactly what happens next, because I gave the orders next. They'll take the strong ones for questioning and the weak ones for example. And they'll leave one door untouched—the one they want you behind. They're not hunting the network. They're herding it. Herding *you*."

Below, in the street, the officer finished reading. Maren was hauled upright between two soldiers, and the crowd that had gathered to watch—because crowds always gather, because horror is a spectacle older than fire—parted around her like water around a stone.

And then the crowd parted differently, and Juniper's heart stopped.

Tam.

He should have been in the cellar. He should have been three streets away by now, small and quick and gone. But the boy came out of the alley mouth at a dead sprint toward the soldiers holding Maren, and in his fist, held high like a torch, was the little carved fox Juniper had whittled for him in Marrow Ford—the one she'd breathed a finding-charm into, harmless, *harmless*, a toy that pointed home.

"*Give her back!*" Tam screamed, in the carrying crack of a voice just breaking. "*You give her back!*"

A soldier laughed. Another reached down and took the fox, turned it over with two fingers, and smiled the smile of a man who has just been handed a promotion.

"No," Juniper breathed.

"Yes," said Sable, who had seen it too. "Oh, no."

They did not take Tam then. That was the worst of it. They looked at him—they *marked* him, the officer wrote something on his scroll, and the whole street saw the writing, the whole street understood—and they let him go, and Tam ran, and the fox stayed in the officer's fist like a fishhook left in the water.

"They'll use him," Juniper said. Her voice sounded far away, spoken by someone else. "They'll let him be seen. They'll let him run. And wherever he runs—"

"—is where you'll follow." Sable closed his eyes. When he opened them, all the argument had gone out of them, replaced by something older and sadder. "It's what I would have done. Gods forgive me, it's exactly what I would have done."

* * *

They came for Tam at dusk, and they did it loudly, because bait is wasted on the deaf.

Juniper heard the whistle-scream of the signal rocket from the roof where she'd spent the afternoon pretending to plan and actually memorizing the sound of Tam's laugh, in case memory was all she got to keep. By the time she reached the corner of Tanner's Row, the Grey Company had him.

They'd done it neatly. A "search" of the dyer's loft where he slept, a "discovery" of the second charm hidden in his mattress—one Odile had planted there herself, weeks ago, a decoy for exactly this night, and it had worked, and it hadn't mattered. Four soldiers held him in the lamplight while an officer read the charges, and Tam, bless him, curse him, fought like a wet cat and shouted words that would have gotten a grown man gagged.

Sable's hand was on Juniper's shoulder, and his grip said everything his mouth didn't: *not yet, not yet, wait for the moment.* Behind them, in doorways and shadows, the remnants of Odile's network waited too—Wren with her lock-weeping hands, the twins with storm-light crawling under their skin. Everyone had come. Everyone had understood the same arithmetic: the boy was the price of all their futures, and none of them could pay it and live with themselves.

The officer finished reading. He drew his sword—not to kill, Juniper realized with a lurch; to *maim*, to mark, to send a message written in a child's body. The blade went up.

Every plan they'd made died in that instant, and Juniper let it.

She walked out of the shadows into the lamplight, alone, hands empty and visible, sword left behind on purpose, and she spoke—not the valley's silence, not the oath's careful hush, but the old speech, the true speech, the words her grandmother had fed her syllable by syllable beside a winter fire:

*"Steel, sleep."*

The officer's blade fell from his hand like a dead branch. So did every blade on the street—thirty swords, forty, ringing on cobbles, and the sound they made was the sound of the hunt's own confidence dying. The soldiers staggered as if the ground had tilted. In the ringing quiet, Juniper's voice carried to the last row of houses:

"I am Juniper Brightwater, last sworn of the swordspeakers. You came hunting a witch-fire. Here I am. Let the boy go, and I go with you willingly—no chains needed, no blood spent. Refuse—" and here she let the oath rise in her throat, let them feel the pressure of it, the sense of a wave deciding whether to break, "—and I will show you why your king's grandfather built these vaults so deep."

For three heartbeats, nobody moved. Then the officer—she would learn his name later, Vess, and that he'd wept that night into his wine—looked at his empty scabbard, at the boy, at the woman standing unarmed in a circle of dropped steel, and made the only choice available to a sensible man.

"Release the prisoner," he said hoarsely. "Take the speaker."

They put her in a wagon, not chains—chains, she suspected, were for people the Hollow King couldn't talk to. Tam was shoved stumbling into Wren's arms, and as the wagon lurched forward he tore free and ran alongside, pounding the boards with his fists, screaming her name, until a soldier gently—*gently*, which somehow made it worse—picked him up and carried him back to the shadows where Odile's people swallowed him whole.

The last Juniper saw of Ashmarket was Sable's face at a broken window, lit by lamplight, wearing an expression she'd carry into the dark places ahead: not helplessness. *Patience.* The patience of a man who had lost everything once already and learned what you do instead of dying.

*Wait,* his eyes said. *I'm coming. Wait for me.*

She closed her own eyes and answered him the only way left, in the old speech, pitched beneath hearing, woven into the rattle of the wheels:

*I hold.*

* * *

The palace dungeons were older than the palace.

Everyone knew the throne room sat above the oath-vaults; almost no one knew the dungeons were the vaults' outer ring, the shell around the kernel, cells cut from the same black stone the first binding had been carved into. Juniper felt it the moment they brought her down—the stone here *listened*. Words sank into it like rain into drought-cracked earth. Her own breath seemed to echo a half-second after she made it, as though the walls were repeating her, tasting her, deciding what she was worth.

They took her boots, her belt, the ribbon with her grandmother's knot-work. They left her the cell's iron cot and a blanket that smelled of mildew and, faintly, of other prisoners' despair. The door was oak banded with iron, and across its face, at the height of a tall man's heart, someone had carved a single word in a script older than the kingdom.

Juniper knew the script. She'd grown up tracing it on boundary stones.

The word was *LISTEN*.

She sat on the cot. She listened.

What came through the stone was not sound. It was the vault itself, breathing its slow mineral breath, and threaded through it—faint, rhythmic, wrong—something else. A pulse. Far above her, beyond floors of granite and ceremony, something on a throne was beating like a second heart, and every beat asked the same question in a voice like frost forming on glass:

*Where is the speaker? Where is the speaker? Where—*

"I'm here," Juniper said aloud, to the ceiling, to the thing above, to the man caged inside the thing. Her voice cracked on the second word and she let it. "You don't have to ask. I came willingly, and I'm not going anywhere, and neither—" her hand found the wall, pressed flat against the cold listening stone, "—neither is he. Do you hear me, Aldric? Whoever's left of you. *Hold on.*"

The pulse stuttered.

Just once—one skipped beat, one hitch in the rhythm, so brief she might have imagined it—and in that gap, thin as paper, she felt something reach back down through all that stone. Not the Hollow King. Something smaller. Something that felt like a hand pressed flat against the underside of a lid.

Then the frost-voice closed over it like water, smooth and satisfied, and the pulse resumed its question, and Juniper sat alone in the dark with her knees drawn up, doing the hardest work of her life.

Nothing.

That was the strategy now. That was the whole of it. She had given herself away to buy a boy's hands and a network's tomorrow, and the coin was spent, and what remained was the part no one sang songs about: sitting in the dark, counting heartbeats, feeding her hope one crumb at a time so it wouldn't starve before Sable came. Because he would come. He had torn off his own colors rather than become the hunt; he would tear through anything rather than leave her to it. She had seen it in his face at the window, plain as sunrise.

So she waited. She marked the days by the slot of torchlight that crossed her floor—morning, noon, evening, dark—and she practiced, silently, moving only her lips, the unbinding words her grandmother had never taught her and that she had pried out of the vault records anyway, syllable by syllable, like splinters from a wound. Each silent repetition cost her something. She could no longer quite recall the color of the shrine's door. The name of the creek below the terraces slipped her grasp and returned and slipped again. She held on to what mattered: Sable's voice saying her name. Tam's laugh. The weight of her grandmother's hand on her head, blessing and binding in the same touch.

On the third day—or the fourth; the torchlight lied—the bolt in the oak door slid back, and light spilled across the floor, and a figure stood in it.

Not a jailer. Not Vess. A woman in widow's grey with a face Juniper knew from a letter smuggled through a hedge-witch's network, a face she had seen reproduced in charcoal on the back of Odile's ledger: older, harder, but unmistakably the same.

The Queen Dowager looked at Juniper kneeling on the cot, and her chin trembled once before she mastered it.

"He skips a beat when you speak," the dowager said quietly. "Did you know? Three years, and I have knelt at that thing's side and prayed for any sign of my son—and you, in one day, from a hole in the ground, made him *stir*." She stepped inside and shut the door softly behind her, and when she turned, her eyes were wet and furious and absolutely resolved. "So. Swordspeaker. Tell me what my son's cage is made of—and tell me quickly, because tonight the Hollow King has ordered an execution, and I am beginning to think it means to make the condemned man wear my son's face."

Above them, faint through fathoms of listening stone, the pulse changed its question.

*Where is the speaker?* became, for the first time, *Why is the mother here?*

And Juniper, rising slowly to her feet with the cold singing up through her bare soles, understood that the game had just changed shape entirely—and that whatever came next, it would happen fast.
