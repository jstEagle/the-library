## 18. Throne Room of Thorns

They came for her at the hour when the dungeons were quietest, and they came with silk.

Juniper had expected chains again, or worse—the small dark rooms below the palace had taught her what worse looked like: a brazier, a table, a man with kind eyes and unkind hands who asked questions in a voice like a lullaby. Instead two guards in royal blue unlocked her cell and a chamberlain followed them in, gray-robed, spotless, carrying a folded gown over his arm like an offering.

"The king will see you," he said.

The words landed strangely. *The king will see you.* As if this were a courtesy. As if she had petitioned for audience rather than been dragged through Ashmarket's smoking streets with Tam's sobbing still caught in her ears like a burr.

"And if I refuse?" Juniper asked.

The chamberlain did not smile, but something behind his eyes flickered—pity, perhaps, or its exhausted cousin. "You misunderstand, Lady Brightwater. It was not a request."

*Lady Brightwater.* They knew her name now. Of course they did. She had torn open a slave-collar in front of three hundred people with words that made the air bleed light; she had carried two children out of a burning quarter on a bridge of spoken stone. In the taverns they were already calling her the Swordspeaker Reborn, though half of them said it like a prayer and half like a curse. The Hollow King would have heard her name whispered in its own halls, tasted it, turned it over the way a tongue turns a stone to find the sweetness under it.

She dressed in the gown because the alternative was being dressed by force, and because—and she hated herself a little for this—some vain, starving part of her wanted to meet that thing standing upright, in clean linen, with her spine straight. The gown was deep green, valley-colored, and she understood the choice for what it was: a first move on a board she could not yet see all of.

They walked her up through the palace's bones. Stair after stair, corridor after corridor, past windows where the afternoon light came down thick as honey and did not warm anything. Servants pressed themselves against walls as she passed. One woman, old enough to be Juniper's grandmother, caught her eye and touched two fingers to her lips, then to her heart—a gesture from the old country, from before the hunts, a blessing for the gifted that had survived centuries of burning by going underground. Juniper carried it up the stairs with her like a coal cupped in both hands.

The doors to the throne room were taller than any door had a right to be, gilded until they hurt to look at, carved with a lineage of kings whose faces had been worn smooth by centuries of reverent hands. The guards flanking them did not look at her. That was the first wrongness she noticed: everyone in this palace avoided looking at the throne room doors the way people avoid looking at the sun.

Then the doors opened, and she understood why.

* * *

The throne room of Aldric's line had been built to make a human being feel small before the crown, and it succeeded even empty—vaulted ceiling lost in shadow, banners of nine generations hanging like sleeping bats, the floor a river of black-and-white marble flowing toward the dais. But emptiness was the one thing it did not have today.

The court was assembled. Hundreds of them, lords and ladies and clergy, ranked along the walls in their finery, and every single one of them stood perfectly, terribly still. Not attentive-still. Not the hush before a ceremony. Still the way a field goes still when the hawk's shadow crosses it. Their eyes were fixed forward, on the throne, and their breathing was shallow, and when Juniper's guards marched her down the long marble river, not one head turned to follow her.

She looked at the throne, and the throne looked back.

King Aldric sat upon it—or the shape of him did. He wore the crown and the ceremonial whites, and at a distance he might have passed for a tired middle-aged king enduring a long day. But Juniper had seen the Hollow King's work up close, had felt how it hollowed a person out and wore the shell, and now, close enough to see his face, she saw what the court had trained itself not to see: the way his chest rose only when it needed to, economical as a bellows; the way his eyes moved without blinking, tracking her across the room like a hawk's or a snake's; the way his mouth held a smile that never once reached the muscles around it, a smile painted on from memory by something that had watched humans smile for three hundred years and still found the trick slightly distasteful.

Behind the throne, where the dowager queen had told them it would be, stood the mirror of black glass, tall as three men, veiled in cloth-of-gold. The oath-vaults lay beneath her feet. Somewhere above, beyond that vaulted ceiling, Sable and Ives and eleven knights who had chosen justice over institution were waiting for her signal—if she could give one, if she lived long enough.

Her grandmother's blade was not at her hip. They had taken it, of course. But her grandmother's voice was in her skull, steady as a hearth-fire: *Words are the blade, child. Steel is only where you keep them.*

The chamberlain struck the floor with his staff. "Juniper of the Brightwater line," he announced, "last of the swordspeakers, brought before His Majesty to answer for crimes against the crown."

The Hollow King spoke, and the whole room heard it in their teeth.

"Crimes." The king's voice was Aldric's voice, and it was not. There was a resonance underneath it, a second tone like a bell rung inside a well. "What a small word for so large a miracle. Three hundred years I have waited for a speaker to walk into my hall, and the crown's own hunters deliver her to my doorstep. Come closer, child. Let me look at what the Brightwaters bred in their little forgetting valley."

Juniper walked forward. Her legs obeyed her, which surprised her; fear had a way of either freezing the body or burning it clean, and hers, thank the first speakers, chose fire. She stopped at the base of the dais, close enough to see the fine stitching on the king's collar, close enough to see that beneath the whites his wrists bore marks—not chains, but the pale grooves of something that had gripped him for years.

"I'm not your child," she said. "And I'm not yours at all."

"No." The Hollow King tilted Aldric's head, studying her with that unblinking attention. "You are the valley's. I remember the valley. I remember when it was made." Something moved behind the king's face then, something almost like hunger sharpening into delight. "Do you? Do you remember it, Juniper Brightwater? You have spent your gift like a drunkard spends coin. Every word, a memory. Every miracle, a piece of the only home you ever had. Tell me—what does your grandmother's face look like this morning? Can you still find it?"

The blow landed exactly where it was aimed. Juniper reached for her grandmother—Maren, Maren Brightwater, hands like tree roots, smell of cedar smoke—and found the shape of her but the edges were soft, watercolor-soft, and terror went through her colder than any dungeon. *Four miracles since Marrow Ford,* she thought. *Four payments. How much is left in me?*

"You're stalling," the Hollow King observed gently. "So am I. Isn't it civilized, two predators circling and calling it conversation?"

* * *

But it was also true, and Juniper seized the truth like a handhold on a cliff. It wanted to talk. Things that want to talk can be talked to, and things that can be talked to can be talked *around*, and every minute she kept that resonant voice engaged was a minute Sable and Ives needed.

"Then talk," she said. "Tell me about the valley. You say you remember it. Prove it."

A ripple went through the still court—not movement, but the ghost of movement, a shiver passing through several hundred bodies at once, like wind over wheat. The Hollow King smiled its painted smile wider.

"Ohh. Clever. Asking the devourer to feed you its own memories." Aldric's body rose from the throne and descended the dais steps, unhurried, the crown catching the light. The court did not kneel. The court did not breathe. "Very well. A gift freely given, freely received—that is the grammar of oaths, isn't it? Your whole order was built on it. Listen, then.

"There was a king once, Aldric's grandfather's grandfather, a man named Edevane, who wanted a kingdom that would never fall. And there was I, a spirit of the space between promises, hungry and formless, and he summoned me with the first true oath ever sworn on this soil: *I give you my line's voices, generation upon generation, and you give my kingdom permanence.* He thought he was buying a guardian. He did not read the clause. They never read the clauses." The king stopped three steps above her, looking down, and in the depths of those borrowed eyes Juniper saw something vast turn over, like a leviathan shifting under still water. "Edevane's voice was the first meal. Then his son's. Then his grandson's. Each heir younger, each reign shorter, because I grew and the vessel must be emptied to hold me. Until your order arose—the swordspeakers, the word-smiths, the ones who learned that a promise can be *unmade* as well as made. They bound me to the throne itself, chained my feeding to the crown, and built your valley as a granary of silence where the last speakers' line could ripen unseen. They thought they had caged me." The smile widened another degree. "They had only given me a longer table."

The court heard none of this, Juniper realized. Or heard it as nothing—perhaps the spirit's voice simply slid off mortal ears that weren't meant to hold it. To them, their king was having a quiet word with a prisoner. To them, everything was normal. The horror of that settled into her bones: three hundred years of courts standing very still while the thing on the throne ate its kings from the inside.

"And Edevane?" she asked. "Is any of him left?"

Something crossed the king's face that was almost pain, and for one vertiginous moment Juniper wondered if it was Aldric's pain, leaking through.

"Dust eats dust," the Hollow King said. "Now. We have exchanged courtesies. Let us speak of what we both want, you and I, because I think you will find our wants are not so far apart." It descended the last steps and stood before her, near enough that she could smell him—no, *it*—a scent like cold iron and old parchment and, faintly, unbearably, like a valley meadow after rain. It had taken that from somewhere. From someone. "You have spent yourself hollow paying for other people's freedom. Slave-collars. Burning children. A boy named Tam, I believe—I do keep excellent records. You are running out of memories to spend, and we both know what the final unbinding costs. The first speakers wrote it plainly: the oath surrendered entire. Identity. Gift. Self. You would speak the words and come out the other side a stranger wearing your own face." Its voice dropped, went soft, went warm, went *kind*, and that was the most frightening thing it had done yet. "Or—

"—you walk away. You walk out of my hall, out of my city, back to your valley, and I restore it. All of it. Every memory you have fed to your miracles, returned whole. Your grandmother's laugh, sharp as you remember it from when you were six. Cedar smoke. The shrine stones warm at dawn. The boundary that kept magic out and grief at bay. I will give you back the girl who never left, the one who tended her shrine and mourned her dead and never once had to watch a child burn. And more—" the painted smile became, horribly, tender— "I will give you your dead. Not truly, no, I am not a god, whatever the priests fear—but a life so full of peace you will not miss them properly. A husband, perhaps. Children. Long years. The valley forgets, Juniper Brightwater. That was its whole design. Let it forget you, and be happy."

The hall was silent. The honeyed light did not move. And Juniper stood in the center of three hundred years of devouring and felt the offer slide against her heart like a knife laid flat—no edge showing, all weight.

Because she *wanted* it. That was the obscenity of it. Some starved animal in her chest turned toward that offered valley the way a frostbitten hand turns toward fire, and she had to stand there and watch herself want it, and know that the thing in the crown had counted on exactly this, had weighed her grief to the ounce.

"That's not peace," she said quietly. "That's a prettier cage. You've just described my grandmother's life, and I watched what it cost her."

"Cost," the Hollow King repeated, tasting the word. "There it is. The Brightwater stubbornness. Very well." And the warmth drained out of its voice like blood from a floor, and the temperature of the great hall seemed to drop with it, and every banner stirred though there was no wind. "Then hear the other side of the scales, speaker, and weigh carefully.

"If you speak the unbinding, I will not go quietly. I will tear free through the only door left open—and that door is Aldric. The boy crouches in the ruin of himself behind me, such of him as remains. He has held a corner of himself these three years the way a drowning man holds a rope, and I have let him, because hope makes the meat sweeter. But oaths bind both ways, child. If you begin the speaking, I will erase him. Every year of him. Every kindness, every guilt, every private grief—he will end before your second word finishes, and what walks out of this body will be only me, unchained, in a kingdom with no king and no law but appetite. You will have traded a caged fire for a wildfire, and you will have done it with a boy's soul as the wick."

It leaned close, Aldric's breath cold on her cheek, and its voice became a whisper meant for her alone, though the whole frozen court somehow leaned in to hear it anyway:

"So choose, swordspeaker. The valley, and the living boy on the throne. Or your precious words, and ash where a soul used to be. Choose before the bells ring vespers. I find I've grown impatient, and impatience makes me... careless with my vessels."

* * *

And there it was. The trap within the trap, sprung shut. Not *speak and die*—she had been prepared to die since Marrow Ford, had priced her life into the plan the night Odile drew the vault-maps in candle smoke. But *speak and kill Aldric*—that was new, that was a blade laid against a throat she'd promised the dowager she would not cut.

*He begged me,* she remembered. In the vision Odile had shown her, in the letter written in a mother's shaking hand: *my son is still inside, he fights, oh he fights.* And now the thing wearing him stood here promising to snuff that fight out the instant she raised her voice, and the worst of it, the unforgivable cleverness of it, was that it might even be telling the truth.

Her eyes went past the king's shoulder, to the veiled mirror, to the shadows at the edges of the vaulted ceiling where the light didn't reach. *Sable. Where are you. What do I—*

And then, high above, at the western end of the hall, a door that had no business opening opened.

The sound was small—a hinge, a boot, the ring of drawn steel—and in that stillness it was thunderous. Three hundred heads turned for the first time in hours. Captain Ives stood in the doorway with his sword bare and his colors torn from his shoulder, and beside him, moving like something the valley had forged and the road had tempered, came Sable.

His eyes found hers across the whole length of the marble river, across three hundred years of silence, and he did not call her name, because he knew better than anyone alive that names were power and this was no place to spend one. He only lifted his blade and set its flat against his own breast, over his heart—the old knights' sign, the one that meant *I am yours to command*—and Juniper Brightwater, last of the swordspeakers, felt her fear burn away all at once and leave something cleaner behind.

The Hollow King turned, slow as a season changing, and its painted smile finally, genuinely, reached its borrowed eyes.

"Oh," it said softly, delightedly, as the first of the guard began to scream. "*There* you are."
