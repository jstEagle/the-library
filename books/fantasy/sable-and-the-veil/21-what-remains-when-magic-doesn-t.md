## 21. What Remains When Magic Doesn't

The palace had never been quiet before.

Sable had served in it for eleven years and had thought he knew its every sound: the creak of the throne room doors on their iron hinges, the measured tread of the changing guard, the whisper of petitioners' shoes on marble worn to a dull shine by centuries of knees. He had thought the palace's silence was only the silence of power—the held breath of a thousand people pretending not to watch each other.

Now he understood it had been something else entirely. It had been the sound of something listening.

Three days since the unbinding, and the halls rang with ordinary noise. Cooks shouted in the sculleries. A chambermaid laughed at something a stable boy said, badly, near the west stair. Somewhere below, in the vaults where the first binding had been made, masons were already at work with chisels and lime, breaking open the oath-stone so that no spirit could ever be seated there again. The sound carried up through the floors like a heartbeat returning to a body long still.

Sable stood at the window of the guest chamber they'd given him—*him*, the disgraced knight, given rooms—and watched the city unclench. Ashmarket was rebuilding already, or trying to. Scaffolding climbed the blackened bones of Odile's safehouse like ivy on a corpse. Carts moved through the gates again. In the square where Juniper had spoken two children out of a pyre's shadow, someone had left flowers, hundreds of them, wilting honestly in the sun because no one had thought to bring water.

He should have felt triumphant. He kept waiting for it the way you wait for a wound to stop throbbing, checking, checking. It did not come. What came instead was a strange, low grief, like the ache after laughter—the sense of having passed through something enormous and come out the other side smaller, and cleaner, and unable to say precisely what had been taken from him to pay for it.

A knock. Not the guard's knock—two sharp raps, knuckle-bone on oak—but a single soft one, almost apologetic.

"Come in," he said, and turned.

King Aldric stood in the doorway.

Not the Hollow King. Sable knew the difference now the way a man knows the difference between his wife and the stranger wearing her face in a nightmare. The Hollow King filled doorways; he made them seem small, made the stone around them seem temporary, made *you* seem temporary. Aldric simply stood in this one, a tall man gone thin, gray to the roots of his hair where three years ago it had been brown, dressed plainly in a way no true king of this house had dressed in four generations. His hands hung at his sides like tools set down after long use. There was a tremor in the left one that he could not quite hide and did not try to.

"Sable." He said the name carefully, as if testing whether it would hold weight. "Or do they call you something else now? I've heard six versions in three days."

"Aldric," Sable said, because it seemed the only honest answer. "Your Majesty, if you want it."

"I don't know what I want yet." Aldric crossed the room slowly, and Sable saw how he favored his right leg, how every step cost him something. Being devoured for years apparently left debts in the body. He lowered himself into the chair by the window—the good chair, the one with arms—and looked out at the same view Sable had been looking at, and for a moment neither of them spoke, and the silence between them was the old palace silence, but empty now. Vacated. Like a nest after the hawk.

"You look at it as if you're counting," Aldric said finally. "The city."

"I'm counting what's missing from it," Sable said. "The gifted quarter. The people who aren't in the carts anymore. You can rebuild a market in a season. You can't rebuild—" He stopped himself. "I'm sorry. That's not fair to lay at your feet."

"It's exactly fair to lay at my feet." Aldric's voice was flat, unhurried, terribly tired. "That's why I came. Not to thank you—I've done that publicly, more than enough, the heralds are sick of it. I came to ask what you want. And then to tell you what I can give, and let you see if the two things touch anywhere."

Sable leaned against the window frame. Below, a flower-seller was setting out her trays in the square, working around the wilted offerings with the practical tenderness of someone who had seen many kinds of grief pass through her street.

"Restoration," Aldric said. "Full restoration. Your name cleared—there will be a formal decree, read in every shire, stating that Ser Sable of the Violet Order acted throughout in defense of the realm against a threat the crown itself could not see. Your rank returned. Command of the Order, reformed, purged of the hunt's officers—or dissolved entirely and rebuilt new, your choice. Lands in the east, if you want land. A seat at my council, if you want power." He paused. "I am offering you everything I took from you, and everything I never gave you, and I am doing it knowing you may refuse, because refusing is what honor looks like when it has finished being angry."

It was beautifully put. Sable wondered, briefly, how much of the eloquence was Aldric and how much was simply the relief of a man whose tongue belonged to him again, saying true things while he still remembered how.

"And if I refuse?" Sable asked.

"Then I'll grieve it privately and sign whatever you ask me to sign instead." Aldric turned from the window. Up close, Sable could see what the devouring had left in his eyes—not madness, not hunger, but a kind of scraped rawness, like wood sanded back to grain. "Tell me the truth, Sable. All of it. You were the first knight in twenty years to look at the king and see something wrong. Why didn't you say it sooner?"

The question landed in the center of the oldest wound Sable carried, and he found, to his surprise, that he could look at it directly now without flinching. Three weeks ago he could not have. Something about watching Juniper give away her memories one by one, her voice failing, his own voice holding her together by nothing but stubbornness and love—that had burned away a great deal of his talent for evasion.

"Because saying it meant believing it," he said. "And believing it meant admitting what I'd done. I led the early hunts, Aldric. Before you changed—before we all understood you'd changed—I rode out with warrants in my hand and put chains on hedge-witches and charm-menders and a boy of nine who could find lost things. I told myself it was law. Law is the best lie ever built; it lets good men do horrors and call it order." He met the king's eyes. "When I started to suspect what sat behind your face, I couldn't speak it, because speaking it would have made every arrest I ever made a crime instead of a duty. So I tore off my colors and ran instead. Running was cowardice wearing the costume of conscience. Juniper taught me the difference. She walked *toward* the thing that frightened her most, and she paid for it with pieces of her own mind, and she never once called it duty."

Aldric was quiet a long moment. When he spoke, his voice had roughened.

"The boy who could find lost things," he said. "Tam."

"You know his name?"

"He was in the dungeons. My dungeons." Aldric's hand closed on the arm of the chair, the tremor gone suddenly, gripped still by sheer will. "I have three days of memory that belong to me and three years that don't, and in the three days I've read every warrant signed in my name. Every one. Do you know what I found, Sable? I found that the Hollow King didn't invent the hunt. It found the hunt already growing here, like rot finds a wet wall. My grandfather's edicts. My father's. Fear of the gifted is older than the thing on the throne—it just wore the throne for a while because the throne was convenient." He looked out at the city again. "I can abolish the hunt. I have. But I cannot abolish what made the hunt possible, and that lives in people, and it will outlive me. Justice won imperfectly is still justice. But it's also still imperfect, and I'll not pretend otherwise to a man who bled for the difference."

"No," Sable said. "Don't pretend. That's the one thing I'd ask of you, and you've already done it."

"Then answer my offer."

Sable looked at his hands. They were steady. He noticed that—they had shaken for the first week after the throne room, after the fighting, after holding Juniper's failing voice in his own like a cupped flame, and now they were steady, and he did not fully trust steadiness that arrived without explanation.

"I can't take command of an Order," he said. "Any Order. I know too well what institutions do to the men inside them. They mean well, and then they mean law, and then they mean safety, and somewhere in there the meaning slides and no one marks the moment. I lived that slide. I won't preside over another one."

"And the lands? The rank?"

"Give the lands to Ashmarket. To the families the hunt broke. Let the decree clear my name if it must—I won't stop you—but spend the ceremony's cost on them instead." He drew a breath. "What I want, Aldric, is to leave. With her. Out past the boundary stones, wherever the road goes when it isn't going anywhere in particular. I spent eleven years learning that a crown is a story people agree to believe, and I've agreed to believe too many stories. I want a life that doesn't require my belief to hold it up."

Aldric studied him for a long moment, and then, unexpectedly, smiled—a small, crooked, human smile, the first Sable had seen on that face in three years, and worth more than all the heralds' proclamations.

"She refused me too, you know," the king said. "Yesterday. Politely. I offered her anything—the valley, her memories restored, research, scholars, whatever remains to be found of the swordspeakers' art. She asked me instead to make sure no child is ever taken for a charm again, and then she asked me for a horse." He laughed softly, once. "You chose well, Sable. Or she did. I genuinely can't tell which, and I think that's the mark of it having been done right."

* * *

They left the palace on the fourth morning, but Sable's last day in the capital began in a courtyard that smelled of fresh paint and crushed herbs.

Odile had taken a building near the river—an old customs house, decommissioned, its ledgers room big enough to hold forty people and its cellar deep enough to hide twice that if hiding were ever needed again. It would not be needed again, she had declared, loudly, in front of witnesses, which was itself a kind of magic: the magic of deciding not to be afraid in public.

Sable found her on a ladder, painting the lintel above the door. The sign was not yet lettered, just a field of blue drying in the morning light, but she talked as she worked, because Odile talked the way rivers ran—continuously, and with a bedrock under it.

"—and that's the whole point, knight, that's the entire point. For two hundred years we hid. Hiding kept us alive and it kept us *small*. Small enough to lose, one at a time, and nobody counted the losses because nobody could see them together. Well. Now they can see us together." She stepped down, surveyed her blue rectangle with a critical eye, and wiped her hands on an apron already the color of every color. "The dowager's patronage helps—don't smirk, I know what you think of royals, and mostly you're right, but that woman smuggled two fugitives through her son's own palace at seventy-one years of age, and I'll hear no slander. Under law's protection, she says. Registered practitioners, licensed, inspected by their own council—we write the rules ourselves and hand the crown a copy for ceremony. First time in history the gifted will be governed *with* rather than hunted *against*."

"And if the next king tears it up?" Sable asked quietly.

Odile looked at him. She had a way of going very still when a question deserved a real answer, all the chatter draining out of her like water finding its level.

"Then we start again," she said. "But we start from *open*. That's the difference. Hiding, they can erase you and no one knows there was anything to erase. Open, they have to do it in front of everyone, forever, and some part of the world remembers. Juniper learned that in Ashmarket, standing in front of a crowd. I'm only doing at scale what she did with two children and a burning street." She flicked a fleck of dried paint off her sleeve. "Besides. Ives is commanding the new guard."

"I heard."

"Do you believe it?"

Sable considered. Captain Ives—Ser Maren Ives, now, though titles were settling on people like snow this week—had tracked them across half the country, torn the whole way between oath and doubt, and in the end had chosen neither cleanly: had let them go with one hand and reported their route with the other, because the institution demanded it and he was not yet the kind of man who could defy an institution with his whole chest. And then he had seen the execution in the capital square. He had seen the Hollow King lean forward on Aldric's stolen face and smile at the dying, and something in Ives had broken along the exact line where duty ended and decency began, and he had defected with six knights and a satchel of evidence and had not slept properly since.

"I believe he'll die before he lets another warrant go out against a child," Sable said. "Whether the institution survives him is a different question. But he'll hold the line while it's his to hold. That's more than I managed."

"That's a hard sentence to say about yourself."

"It's an honest one. I'm practicing honesty. It doesn't come naturally."

Odile snorted, then sobered, and laid a paint-spotted hand briefly on his arm. "How is she?" she asked, and her voice had none of the performance left in it. "Truly. You don't have to answer well. Just answer."

Sable looked toward the river. How was she. How did you measure a person against the shape they used to be.

"She wakes some mornings and doesn't know where she is," he said. "She knows me. She knows my voice before she knows my face, which the healers say is unusual, and which I have decided not to examine too closely because examining it might break it. She remembers fragments. The shrine, sometimes. Her grandmother's hands, sometimes. The valley comes to her in pieces, like light through a cracked shutter, and some mornings the pieces are gone again by noon. She laughs anyway. She's angry at the right things. Yesterday she argued tax policy with the treasurer for an hour and won, and didn't remember doing it by evening, and laughed about that too." His throat tightened. "She's not who she was. But who she was is still in there, the way a river is in a dry bed—you can see exactly where it ran. And she chose this. That's what I hold onto. Every piece that went into the unbinding, she gave *on purpose*. Nobody took anything from Juniper Brightwater that she didn't hand over herself, eyes open, to save a man she'd never met and a kingdom that was hunting her."

Odile nodded slowly. "Then she's the bravest person either of us has ever known," she said, "and the sign will be blue, and the door will be unlocked, and when you two come back through this city—if you come back—there'll be a bed and a bad-tempered cat and no questions asked." She picked the brush back up. "Now get out of my courtyard, knight. You're blocking my light, and I've got a legend to make boring and bureaucratic before sundown."

* * *

They rode out through the north gate at dawn, and Sable made himself look back once, deliberately, the way you close a door on purpose instead of letting it swing.

The capital in morning haze: scaffolding and cranes, the masons' chisels ringing faintly from the direction of the palace, smoke rising straight from a hundred honest chimneys. On the wall beside the gate, someone had chalked a sword and, beneath it, words in a careful hand: *she spoke, and it ended.* It would rain soon and wash the chalk away. Sable found he didn't mind. Chalk was right for it. Nothing true needed to be carved in stone except laws, and those only until better ones were written.

Juniper rode slightly ahead, the way she always did now, as if her body remembered being a swordspeaker even when her mind had misplaced the rest. She rode her grandmother's blade slung across her back out of pure habit, though the blade was quiet now—heavier than it had been, or perhaps only heavier in meaning, an heirloom whose purpose had been spent. Sometimes she reached back and touched the hilt absently, the way you touch a scar.

At the top of the first ridge she stopped and turned her horse, and they stood together looking back at the whole spread of the kingdom: the capital's haze, the river's silver thread, the far smudge of the Veiled Valley folded into its mountains like a secret the land was keeping from itself.

"I can't remember why that matters," she said, nodding at the valley's smudge. "I know it mattered. Everyone tells me it did. I wrote it down before we left—do you remember, I filled twelve pages, I made you check them—and I read them every night, and it's like reading someone else's letters." She glanced at him, and her eyes were clear and unafraid, which was somehow worse and better than grief. "Is that terrible? That I can't miss it properly?"

"No," Sable said. "I think it means the price was real. If you could miss it easily, it wouldn't have been enough to pay with."

She was quiet for a moment. Then she laughed—that laugh, the one that had survived everything, that had been the rope he'd clung to in the dark of the throne room when her voice failed and hers alone could finish the unbinding.

"You always make it sound simple," she said.

"It isn't simple. I just say it simply, because you look calmer when I do."

"Flatterer." She turned her horse north. "Which way?"

"Whichever way," Sable said. "We've got no warrant, no colors, no crown, no oath, and no one hunting us. It's the richest I've ever been."

They rode down off the ridge, two figures on the north road, unwritten. Behind them the kingdom went on mending itself, imperfectly, the way all mending goes. Ahead the road bent toward country Sable had never seen, and Juniper Brightwater—who had been the last swordspeaker, and was now only a woman with a sword and a man who loved her—rode into it laughing at something he hadn't said yet.

He caught up. She was humming. He didn't recognize the tune, and suspected it didn't exist anywhere but in her, in some fragment of the valley that had survived the paying of the price, too small to be missed and too stubborn to be lost.

He hummed along anyway, wrong notes and all, and she didn't correct him.
