## 1. The Cartographer's Rule

The snow came down like ash that first week of Deepwinter, and Marguerite Whitlock—though no one living called them that—counted it falling from the window of a house that did not appear on any map worth trusting.

They had built the counting into their mornings the way other people built in prayer. One: the snow. Two: the fire, banked low enough to warm without blazing, because smoke drew eyes and eyes drew questions and questions were death wearing a polite coat. Three: the kettle. Four: the ledger, locked in its iron box beneath the third floorboard from the hearth, where the boards had been cut to look warped and worthless so that no thief would bother prying them up.

And five—the rule above all rules—*no one stays.*

Marguerite had lived by five rules for fifteen years, and they could recite them the way a priest recites a litany, flat and unhesitating, in the voice of someone who has forgotten there was ever anything else to say.

*No real name. No attachments. No questions asked or answered. No map drawn twice. No one stays.*

The house sat in a fold of the Thornfell foothills where two smuggler roads crossed like old scars, close enough to the Guild road at Wrenmoor Crossing to be convenient and far enough into the white silence to be forgotten. It was a good house for a person who wished to be weather rather than a presence—a smudge of grey stone against grey sky, chimney smoking just enough to seem inhabited by some poor woodcutter and no one interesting. Marguerite had chosen it the way they chose everything: for what it concealed.

Outside, the pines stood heavy with snow, bent like mourners. The wind came off the high passes with a sound Marguerite had never found a word for—not howling, not keening, but something between a whisper and a warning, as though the mountains themselves were murmuring secrets they'd been told never to repeat.

Marguerite knew the feeling intimately.

They were grinding ink when the bell on the gate rang—one clean note, muffled by snowfall.

Marguerite's hands went still over the mortar. The pestle rested against the rim. For three breaths they did not move, listening the way a hare listens: whole-bodied, every nerve turned outward.

The bell rang again. Patient. Unhurried. The ring of someone who knew they would be admitted and was merely observing the courtesy of asking.

*Guild,* Marguerite thought, and felt the old cold settle into their chest, familiar as a scar. *Courier. Midwinter payment.*

They set down the pestle, smoothed their apron, checked that their sleeves were ink-stained in the right places—an honest scribe's stains, earned and expected—and went to open the door before the visitor could decide impatience was more efficient than waiting.

* * *

Aldous Renne stood on the step shaking snow from his grey cloak, and he smiled the way a well-made lock smiles: precisely, beautifully, with nothing behind it you were meant to see.

"Master Corvel." His voice was warm honey poured over cold iron. "You're looking well. The mountain air agrees with you."

"It agrees with no one," Marguerite said. "It merely tolerates us. Come in before it decides otherwise."

Renne stepped across the threshold, bringing with him the smell of horse, wet wool, and something fainter beneath it—the particular scent of Guild paper, which was treated with alum and ash and smelled, to Marguerite's trained nose, like a thing being preserved against its will. He moved through the single room with the ease of a man who had been in a hundred such rooms and found them all equally beneath his interest. His gaze touched the drying racks of maps, the ink pots, the low fire, the narrow bed behind its curtain, and dismissed each item in turn with the efficiency of a customs officer.

He was perhaps forty, lean, with the kind of face that would be handsome in twenty years and forgettable in forty. His gloves were good calfskin. His boots were Guild-issue but privately resoled—he valued his feet, then; he walked far; he noticed ground. Marguerite noted this and filed it away in the part of their mind that never stopped taking inventory, even in sleep.

"Wine?" Marguerite asked.

"Do you have any?"

"No."

"Then no." Renne laughed—a pleasant sound, entirely hollow—and took the chair Marguerite indicated, the one facing away from the hearth. Marguerite noted that too. A man who chose the seat with his back to the warmth was a man who wanted the light behind him, so that others squinted and he did not. "I'll be brief. The Guild wants the winter routes. The northern passes especially. There's talk of movement out of Vellmark that our lords find... interesting."

"There's always talk of movement out of Vellmark," Marguerite said. "Half of it is smugglers and half of it is the Guild inventing smugglers to justify its budget."

"And which half are you?"

"I'm a scribe who sells paper," Marguerite said mildly. "What my clients do with their legs is their own affair."

"Mm." Renne unwound his scarf with deliberate slowness, watching Marguerite over the wool. "You know, Master Corvel, I've been coming to you for three years now, and in three years you've never once asked me a question. Not about the Guild. Not about the roads. Not even about the weather in the lowlands, which most mountain folk ask after the way other folk ask after family."

"The weather here is family enough."

"Yes." The smile again, thinner. "That's exactly what I mean."

Marguerite crossed to the shelf and took down the wrapped bundle they had prepared days ago—three sheets of good vellum, routes drawn in their own careful hand, accurate to within a stone's throw because inaccuracy in this trade got people killed and dead clients paid nothing. They laid it on the table between them.

Renne did not reach for it immediately. That was the thing about Aldous Renne that made the back of Marguerite's neck prickle: he never reached for anything immediately. He liked to let objects sit, to let silences stretch, to see what grew in them.

"Fifteen years," he said instead, conversationally, examining his gloved fingertips. "That's what the ledgers say. Fifteen years selling routes through Thornfell, and no one can tell me where you came from. No guild registration before Wrenmoor. No apprenticeship record. No kin. You appeared out of the snow like a fox, fully grown, already knowing every goat path in the range better than the shepherds do." He looked up. The firelight caught his eyes and gave them an amber gleam, almost kind. "Where *did* you come from, Master Corvel?"

Here it was. The question arrived every visit now, dressed a little differently each time—sometimes as small talk, sometimes as flattery, once, memorably, as sympathy. Marguerite had learned to meet it the way one meets a wave: braced, unbowed, letting it break and wash past.

"Rule of the trade," Marguerite said. "No questions asked, none answered. You taught me that yourself, first year. Said a courier who talks and a scribe who talks both end up in the same ditch."

"I did say that." Renne sighed, and for a moment—just a moment—the performance flickered, and something tired moved behind his eyes. "Yes. Well. Ditches are instructive places." He picked up the bundle at last, weighed it in one hand, and slid it inside his cloak without opening it. He trusted the goods. It was only the seller he wondered about. "Payment's been authorized at the usual rate. Plus a winter supplement. The Guild appreciates reliability."

"The Guild appreciates deniability," Marguerite said. "Reliability is mine alone."

Renne laughed again, genuinely this time, and Marguerite hated how much they wanted him to keep laughing. That was the danger of these visits—that after weeks of silence, another voice in the room felt like sunlight on frostbitten skin, and a person could grow addicted to sunlight without noticing the burn.

*No attachments.* Marguerite recited it inwardly, like pressing a thumb against a wound.

Renne counted out the coin onto the table—good silver, weighted, each piece rung against the next in the old test—and rose. At the door he paused, hand on the latch, and turned back with an expression Marguerite could not read, which frightened them more than any expression they could.

"One more thing," he said lightly. "The northern routes you sold us last month. There was a valley on them. Greywash headwaters, east fork. Your map showed it empty. Our surveyors say the same. But a shepherd came into Wrenmoor last week swearing there's an old road through that valley—paved, walled, older than anything the Crown ever built. He says it goes somewhere." Renne's smile was gentle as a knife laid flat on a table. "Maps don't lie, Master Corvel. But men who draw them sometimes leave things out. Sleep well."

The door closed behind him. The bell on the gate rang once, fading into the wind.

Marguerite stood in the middle of the room for a long time, listening to hoofbeats diminish down the frozen track, and did not move until the sound was wholly gone.

* * *

Then they moved quickly, because fear that is allowed to linger becomes paralysis, and paralysis in winter is death.

They knelt at the hearth, worked loose the third floorboard, and lifted out the iron box. Inside, beneath the ledger with its false entries and its false name written in a false hand, lay a book bound in blue leather gone soft as moth wing with age.

The songbook.

Their grandmother's.

Marguerite held it without opening it, the way one holds a sleeping animal—carefully, reverently, aware that waking it might be the end of everything. The cover was stamped with a design Marguerite had traced a thousand times as a child and never understood: a door, drawn as a simple seam in a mountainside, with seven small marks beside it like words, or stars, or wounds.

Vessarine work. Vessarine script inside, in their grandmother's slanted, hurried hand—songs, lullabies, a few recipes, one page of what might have been prayers, all written in a language that existed nowhere else in the world anymore. Marguerite knew this because they had spent fifteen years making certain of it. They had searched every archive they could reach, every private collection, every smuggler's hoard of stolen books. They had asked, obliquely, carefully, in the voices of hypothetical curiosity, and watched scholars frown and shrug.

*Vessar? Never heard of it. Old fable. There's no such kingdom. Never was.*

Never was. As if a country could simply not-have-been. As if their grandmother's songs were songs of nowhere, sung to a child who didn't exist, in a tongue that had never troubled anyone's mouth.

Marguerite had learned early what the silence cost. Their grandmother had died whispering it—words Marguerite hadn't understood then, pressed into their nine-year-old ear like a coal: *Never sing it where anyone can hear. Never write it. Never say our true name. They burned the libraries, little bird. They will burn the singers too.*

So Marguerite had not sung. Had not written. Had buried the language beneath floorboards and discipline and a name bought from a dying forger in Wrenmoor, and had told themselves for fifteen years that this was survival, that this was wisdom, that the hollow feeling in the chest on winter nights was only the cold.

Loneliness wore the costume of discipline very well. It had worn it so long that Marguerite sometimes forgot which was underneath.

They put the songbook back. Pressed the board down. Raked ash over the seam of it with the toe of their boot until the floor looked like nothing at all—which was, after all, the entire art of their life.

Then they sat by the fire with the coin Renne had left, and counted it, and did not count it, and listened to the wind come down off the passes carrying its wordless murmur, and thought about a shepherd in Wrenmoor talking about an old road through a valley that every map showed empty.

Marguerite knew that valley. Knew it better than the surveyors, better than the Guild, better than anyone drawing breath. And Marguerite knew—with the certainty of someone who had walked that ground at night, alone, and felt the hair rise on their arms for reasons no wind could explain—that the shepherd was right. There was a road there. There had always been a road there. And every map that showed it empty, including the ones Marguerite themselves had drawn and sold—

Including those.

Marguerite stared into the fire and felt, for the first time in fifteen years, the discipline crack along a fault line they had not known was there.

*What did we erase?* the question whispered, in a voice that sounded terribly like their grandmother's. *What did I sell them the blanks of?*

The fire settled. Snow ticked against the window. And Marguerite Whitlock—who was no one, from nowhere, who kept five rules and broke no hearts including their own—sat alone in a house that did not exist, and did not sleep.

Near dawn, half-dreaming in the chair, they heard the wind change.

Or thought they did. Beneath the mountain's murmur, faint and impossible, something that was almost—almost—a snatch of melody. Seven notes, rising, the shape of a lullaby they had not heard aloud since childhood, the first line of the first song in the blue book beneath the floor.

Marguerite came upright, heart slamming. The room was silent. Only wind. Only snow.

But the bell on the gate did not ring, and yet—when they opened the door at first light, scanning the white waste for a trick of the mind—they saw, far down the track where the pines began, a small dark figure struggling through the drifts toward the house.

Small. Very small. Child-sized. And moving with the desperate, stumbling urgency of something that would not survive another hour of the cold.

Marguerite stood in the doorway with the wind tearing at their clothes, and every rule they owned rose up in a body around them like a wall:

*No real name. No attachments. No questions. No one stays.*

The figure stumbled. Fell. Rose again.

Marguerite stepped out into the snow.
