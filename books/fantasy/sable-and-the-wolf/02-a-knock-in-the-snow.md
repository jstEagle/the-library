## 2. A Knock in the Snow

The storm came down off the Thornfells like something with intent.

Marguerite had watched it build all afternoon from the window of the wayhouse—a bruise-colored wall rolling along the ridgeline, swallowing the peaks one by one, and by the time the light failed it had swallowed the world. Snow fell so thick that the pines beyond the yard existed only as suggestions, gray ghosts swaying where trees should be. The wind found every gap in the shutters and worried at them like a dog with a bone.

Good, Marguerite thought, banking the fire. Let it bury the road. Let it bury everything.

Three days since the courier had ridden south with the winter routes folded inside his coat, three days of the particular emptiness that followed a sale. It was always like this after. Fifteen years of selling other people's secrets had taught Marguerite the rhythm of it: the transaction, the coin, the courier's horse fading down the valley—and then this, the silence afterward, settling into the house like snow through a broken roof. They had learned to call the silence discipline. They had told themselves so many times that they almost believed it.

The rules kept them alive. No real name. The guild knew them as *Merrin of the Upper Passes*, a name worn smooth as a river stone from use. No attachments—the nearest thing to a friend was Odile at the Greywash ferry, and even Odile was paid in favors, not affection. And no questions: not about the buyers, not about the routes, not about why certain valleys on the guild charts were inked out in careful black rectangles, as though someone had decided the mountains were too honest and needed editing.

Marguerite was ladling barley soup over the fire when they heard the knock.

It came faintly, half-drowned in wind—three small sounds against the door, more like branches than knuckles. Marguerite went still, spoon suspended. Nobody traveled the upper road in a whiteout. Nobody traveled it at all, honestly; that was rather the point of living here. Smugglers used the lower paths, couriers came when summoned, and everyone else had long ago agreed that the house at the top of the pass did not exist.

The knock came again. Weaker. A fourth blow that never quite landed.

*No attachments,* said the voice of fifteen years' discipline. *No questions.* Whoever was out there was someone else's problem, someone else's tragedy, and opening that door opened everything—warmth, light, witness, all the things Marguerite had spent a decade and a half making sure no one could trace back to this hearth.

But the wind gusted, and beneath it Marguerite heard something worse than knocking. A sound too small for the storm. A child's cough, or perhaps only the memory of one, imagined into being by a conscience that refused, despite everything, to die.

"Damn you," Marguerite said—to the storm, to the rules, to whichever dead grandmother was listening—and pulled open the door.

The cold hit like a fist. Snow poured across the threshold in a white sheet, and out of it, barely visible, stood a shape no higher than Marguerite's hip. A child, if it was a child—coated entirely in rime, hood crusted white, so still that for one horrible moment Marguerite thought the storm had delivered a corpse. Then the shape shivered, whole-body, like a bird shaking off water, and two dark eyes blinked up from under the frost-lashed hood.

They were extraordinary eyes. Not for their color, though the color was strange enough—a deep amber, almost gold—but for their steadiness. The child looked up at Marguerite through the swirling snow without fear, without pleading, with the flat patient assessment of someone who had already survived worse than this and expected to survive this too.

"You'll die standing there," Marguerite said, and reached out.

The child flinched from the hand but let herself be drawn inside—Marguerite registered *herself* distantly, the small frame, the way she moved—across the threshold and into the heat, and the door slammed behind them and cut the storm off mid-howl as cleanly as a blade.

For a moment neither of them moved. Snowmelt began ticking onto the floorboards. The fire spat. The child stood dripping in the middle of the room, small and rigid, watching Marguerite with those amber eyes while icicles melted from her hood and ran down her cheeks like tears she wasn't crying.

"All right," Marguerite said, more to themselves than to her. "Blankets. Soup. In that order."

She didn't move. She said something instead—a short phrase, two or three syllables, her voice cracked and rusted from cold but unmistakable.

Marguerite's hands stopped working.

They knew the words. That was the impossible thing, the thing that made the room tilt slightly on its axis. They knew the words the way they knew their own heartbeat—not meaning, not anymore, not since childhood, but the *shape* of them, the music. The vowels rounded and falling, the consonants soft as breath on glass. Their grandmother's voice rose unbidden from fifteen years of deliberate burial: an old woman by a winter fire, singing low, words that meant nothing and everything, words that stopped the moment anyone else's boots sounded on the path outside.

Vessarine. The language of a kingdom that did not exist. The language of lullabies sung in a house where silence was law.

"What did you say?" Marguerite asked, and heard their own voice come out wrong—too quiet, too careful.

The child tilted her head. She said it again, slower, and pointed one mittened hand—at the fire, Marguerite realized. At the warmth. Asking, or thanking, or naming. Her lips were blue-tinged and chapped raw, and she was shivering again now, deep shivers that would not stop, and whatever the words meant, the child was freezing to death in the middle of Marguerite's kitchen while Marguerite stood frozen in a different way entirely.

*No questions.* But the rule had never imagined this. The rule had been built for smugglers and couriers, for men who asked about passes and prices. It had no clause for a half-dead child speaking a dead tongue in a whiteout at the edge of the map.

Marguerite moved. Blankets from the cedar chest—heavy wool, smelling of years of disuse—wrapped around the small shoulders. The child resisted at first, stiffening like a wild animal taken up, then yielded with a practicality that reminded Marguerite uncomfortably of themselves. She sat where she was put. She took the bowl of soup when it was offered and ate with a controlled hunger that hurt to watch: fast, but not greedy, rationing herself even now, even here. Someone had taught her that. Someone had taught her that food could be taken away.

It was only when the bowl was empty and some of the blue had left her lips that Marguerite saw the compass.

It hung at her neck on a leather cord, tucked inside her coat, and the movement of eating had swung it free. Brass, old brass gone green at the seams, its lid scratched nearly smooth. Marguerite had handled a thousand instruments of navigation—guild compasses, smugglers' needles, sun-stones and star-charts—and the sight of another one should have meant nothing. Trade goods. Tools.

But this one was wrong.

Marguerite saw it in the firelight: the needle trembling, straining, pointing not north. Not south, not any direction a needle ought to know. It pointed *inward*, angled down and toward the heart of the Thornfells, toward the massed darkness of the peaks where no road ran and no chart admitted anything existed. It strained toward the mountains the way a dog strains at a leash, quivering with a wanting that no lodestone should possess.

"That's not possible," Marguerite said.

The child followed their gaze. She cupped the compass in both hands, protective, and said something else in that rusted, falling language—and this time a single word snagged in Marguerite like a fishhook. One word, repeated twice, the second time softer, the way one says a beloved thing.

Their grandmother had sung that word. Marguerite could almost place it—could feel it hovering just past recall, a shape remembered by the mouth but not the mind, like a taste from infancy. It had come near the end of the lullaby, always near the end, sung so softly it was nearly prayer.

"Where did you get that?" Marguerite asked. Useless—she wouldn't understand, or wouldn't answer. "Who gave it to you? Where are your people?"

The child looked at them for a long moment. The firelight moved in her amber eyes, and Marguerite had the sudden, vertiginous sense of being *read*—of standing on the examined side of an examination. Then she did something that undid fifteen years of discipline more thoroughly than any guild inquisitor could have.

She hummed.

Four notes. Rising, falling, rising again—a fragment of melody, thin and wavering with cold, but whole, complete, unmistakable. The opening of the lullaby. The song their grandmother had died with unfinished on her lips, the song Marguerite had spent half a lifetime refusing to remember and had remembered anyway, every note, every night, in the body's stubborn archive where nothing is ever truly deleted.

The room went very quiet. Even the wind seemed to lean closer against the shutters to listen.

Marguerite's hands were shaking. They pressed them flat against their knees. *This is how it ends,* they thought absurdly. *Not the guild, not the gallows. A child and a song.* Because they understood, with the terrible clarity that comes once in a life, what the compass meant, what the language meant, what the four notes meant. The child was not lost. The child was *sent*—or fleeing, which amounted to the same thing—out of whatever hidden country the guild's black rectangles had eaten, carrying a needle that pointed home, and she had knocked on the one door in all the Thornfells where a Vessarine lullaby might be answered.

Fifteen years of rules sat in Marguerite's chest like swallowed stones. *No real name. No attachments. No questions.* Rules that had kept them alive and alone and safe, safe as the grave, safe as the erased valleys, safe as a songbook buried under floorboards that no one would ever sing from again.

The child finished humming. She waited. There was ash-gray exhaustion in her face and iron patience in her eyes, and she waited the way the compass strained—for an answer, for a direction, for the thing she had crossed a whiteout to find.

Marguerite opened their mouth to say—I don't know what. *You can sleep by the fire. You can stay till the storm breaks. You must leave at first light.* Any of the sentences that would rebuild the wall between them.

What came out instead, rough and unpracticed and half-remembered, was the answering phrase of the lullaby. Four notes set to words their grandmother had sung, words whose meaning had rotted out of them decades ago but whose sound their mouth still knew, still held, still offered up like a key turning in a lock they hadn't known was theirs.

The child's eyes went wide. The bowl slipped from her lap; she caught it without looking. And she said—one word, urgent, wondering, gripping Marguerite's sleeve with a small mittened hand—

and outside, the storm screamed against the house, and somewhere far below in the dark of the pass, Marguerite would remember later, the brass compass trembled and turned, its needle swinging at last away from the mountains' heart—toward the two of them, as if it had found what it was looking for.
