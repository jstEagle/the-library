## 3. Sable

The fire had a sound before it had a light.

That was the first thing Sable remembered, and the last thing she remembered every night when she closed her eyes: the sound of it, like a thousand small animals eating something all at once, hungry and wet and everywhere. She had been small enough that her mother's hand covered her whole ear. *Sovelle, sovelle,* her mother had whispered — *hush now, hush* — pressing her palm flat against the side of Sable's head as if she could keep the noise out by touch alone, the way you pressed your thumb over a crack in an eggshell to keep the chick safe inside.

It hadn't worked. The chick was out now. The egg was ash. And the sound of fire lived in Sable's ears the way other children carried songs.

She woke in the dark of the cart-shed with her heart going fast and her breath making little clouds, and for a moment she did not know where she was, which happened more often now, because places kept not being where she left them. Then she felt the wool blanket — rough, smelling of tallow and old smoke that was *old*, the good kind of smoke, hearth-smoke, years-smoke — and remembered.

The map-woman's house. The door that opened before she finished knocking.

Sable lay still and listened to the house breathe around her. It creaked in a language of its own, beams settling, wind finding gaps in the shingles. Somewhere below, the map-woman was awake; Sable could tell by the particular quality of silence a house has when someone is moving through it carefully, trying not to wake anyone. The floorboards spoke under weight. A kettle set down on iron. The scratch of a pen or a knife.

The map-woman. Who had heard Sable's words come out of her frozen mouth on the doorstep — *vel suora, vel suora, please, please* — and had not looked confused. Had looked like a person struck by a thrown stone. Had said, in a voice gone strange and rusty, a word back:

*Ammeli.*

Sable turned the word over in the dark. Nobody used it anymore. In the camps, in the years of walking, people spoke Border Tongue or soldier-talk or nothing at all, mouths too cold and too afraid. But once — before — everyone had been *ammeli*. Beloved-stranger. The word you called to someone lost on the terraces at dusk so they would know they were found. Her mother had sung it into her hair. *Ammeli venne, ammeli venne* — beloved one, come home, come home.

Her mother's voice did not work anymore either. Not because her mother was dead — Sable refused, with the whole stubborn weight of her seven years, to decide that — but because voices needed somewhere to live, and hers had nowhere. The soldiers had seen to that.

* * *

The library had burned for three days.

Sable knew this because she had watched it from the high window of the map-hall, the one her mother wasn't supposed to bring her to, the one with the leaded glass that made everything outside look cut into diamonds. From up there the fire had seemed almost beautiful, a red flower opening over the lower city, petals of sparks going up instead of down, which was wrong, fire should fall like rain, but this fire climbed. It climbed like it was being paid to.

Inside the hall, the adults moved the way water moves when a stone drops into it — outward, urgent, silent. They were carrying books. Not ordinary books. Maps. Great rolled maps with rods of glass at each end, and flat maps folded into squares soft as cloth, and maps drawn on skins so thin her mother said you could read them by holding them up to a candle, the country printed right through the animal. The map-halls of Vessar held every road the kingdom had ever loved. Every river's true name. Every mountain's sleeping name, the one you only sang if you wanted it to answer.

*Sable.* Her mother crouched in front of her, and there was ink on her fingers, black and gold, because she had been working when the bells began. *Listen to me. We are going to play the quiet game. Do you remember the quiet game?*

Sable remembered. You breathed through your mouth and you thought about snow and you did not make any sound even if you cried, especially if you cried.

*If anyone asks your name, what do you say?*

Nothing. You said nothing. Names were how the soldiers found people. Her mother had explained it once with her hands very steady and her eyes not steady at all: the men who came across the passes did not want Vessar's gold or Vessar's grain. They wanted Vessar's *maps*, because whoever holds the map holds the country, and if they could not hold the maps they would burn them, and if they burned the maps then Vessar would never have existed at all, and no one would ever be able to say otherwise, because saying requires remembering and remembering requires —

Her mother had stopped explaining there. She had kissed both of Sable's hands, palms and knuckles, the way you blessed a small priest.

*Whatever happens,* she said, *you are still on the map. Do you understand me? You are drawn on me. No fire reaches that page.*

Then the doors of the map-hall came open from the wrong side, the outside, which meant soldiers, and the quiet game began and never ended.

Sable did not remember leaving the city. Memory was like that now — a map with holes burned through it, edges curling brown, whole provinces missing. She remembered cold. She remembered a woman who was not her mother holding her hand for three days of walking and then not, and never learning why, whether the hand had let go or been taken. She remembered eating bark and learning which bark. She remembered the compass.

The compass had been her mother's last gift, pressed into her coat pocket while the hall filled with smoke: a disc of brass no bigger than a plum, warm from her mother's hand, its needle trembling toward something that was not north. *It points inward,* her mother had said. *Toward the Door. If we are ever parted, follow it. Follow it home.*

Sable had followed it through two winters. It pointed always the same way, patient as a dog, into the high white teeth of the Thornfells, and Sable had walked toward it until the night the snow came down so thick the world became a held breath, and she saw a light, and she knocked.

And a voice answered her in the old tongue.

* * *

In the morning the map-woman gave her porridge with honey in it, real honey, and did not ask questions, which Sable understood was a kindness of the highest order. Questions were hooks. The map-woman seemed to know that without being told. She moved around her kitchen like someone who had practiced being unremarkable for a long time — sleeves plain, hair pinned without ornament, every gesture sanded smooth of anything a stranger might catch on. But her eyes were not smooth. Her eyes went to the floorboards near the hearth sometimes, quick as a tongue touching a broken tooth.

Sable ate and watched her and hummed without deciding to.

It was the song for putting children to bed in houses that might not stand till morning. *Orra venn, orra venn, sullo mirra solla venne* — sleep, beloved, the stars are counting you among them, none shall be subtracted. Her mother's version had a second verse most mothers didn't sing, the secret verse, the one about the mountains folding their arms around the valley like a mother folding a shawl. Sable had never met anyone else who knew it. She hummed the first verse and then, testing, watching the map-woman's back the way you watch a bird you hope will land, she slipped into the second.

The spoon stopped.

The map-woman stood very still over the pot, steam rising past her face, and then she began — quietly, roughly, like a wheel that hasn't turned in fifteen years — to hum the third verse.

There was no third verse. There had never been a third verse. Sable knew every song her mother knew, and the lullaby ended at the second verse, ended with the mountains' arms, ended with *solla venne*, come home, come home.

Except it didn't. Because here was a third verse, coming out of a stranger's mouth in a kitchen that smelled of tallow and pine, and the words were older than the others, heavier, and they were about a door. A door in the rock. A door that slept until someone sang it awake. And the melody fit, the way a key fits a lock you'd forgotten your family owned, and Sable sat with her spoon halfway to her mouth and felt something crack open in her chest that had been sealed since the night of the fire.

She cried. Not the quiet-game cry. The other kind, the ugly kind, the kind that doubled her over the table, and the map-woman crossed the room and put her arms around her — awkwardly, like someone handling a bird they were afraid of breaking and equally afraid of frightening — and held on.

Neither of them said anything. There was a language for this that needed no words, and both of them were fluent.

* * *

Afterward, the map-woman — Marguerite, she said her name was, though she said it the way you set down a cup you suspect is poisoned — cleared the table and brought out the compass. Sable hadn't shown it to her yet. She hadn't shown it to anyone. But the map-woman had seen it somehow, or guessed, or maybe the compass had simply grown tired of hiding, because when Sable took it from her coat the needle swung up eagerly and pointed — not north, not at the window, not anywhere sensible — straight at the map-woman's own chest, and then, quivering, past her shoulder, deeper into the house, deeper into the mountains behind the house.

Marguerite went white.

"It points inward," Sable said in Vessarine, because some things could not be said in any other tongue. "My mother said. Toward the Door."

"Your mother." Marguerite's voice came out careful, each word placed like a foot on river ice. "Where is your mother?"

Sable looked at her hands. "The fire," she said. "The soldiers burned the map-halls. My mother worked in the map-halls." She traced a shape on the tabletop without meaning to — the spiral sign of the cartographers' guild of Vessar, the mark that meant *we measure so the world may be loved*. "They burned the maps first. Then the halls. Then—" Her throat closed. "I ran. The compass says home is still there. The compass wouldn't lie. Compasses don't lie."

"No," Marguerite said softly. "Compasses don't lie. People do." She was looking at the needle as if it had accused her personally. "Sable. Listen to me. What do you remember about the Door?"

"Only the song." Sable wiped her face with her sleeve. "The third verse. How do you know the third verse? My mother never sang it. She said it wasn't for singing until—"

"Until it was time." Marguerite's jaw tightened. "Yes. I know why." She stood abruptly, went to the window, stood with her back straight and her hands clasped behind her like a soldier at rest, except she wasn't a soldier, she was the opposite of a soldier, Sable could feel that the way you felt weather through a wall. Outside, the snow had stopped. The morning lay blue and enormous over the peaks, and somewhere in all that white was a door, and somewhere behind the door was everything.

"My grandmother," Marguerite said, not turning around, "came out of those mountains when she was younger than you are now. She carried a songbook. She taught me songs I wasn't allowed to know I knew." A long breath. "I have spent my life drawing maps for people who pay well and ask nothing, and I have just understood, sitting across a bowl of porridge from a child, exactly what my maps have been *for*."

Sable didn't understand all of it. But she understood the shape of it, the way you understood a storm coming by the smell of the air. She slid off her chair, went to the window, and stood beside the tall stranger whose shoulders shook very slightly, once, and then were still.

"Ammeli," Sable said, and touched her hand.

Marguerite looked down at her. Something moved behind her eyes — grief meeting its echo across fifteen winters, recognizing itself, refusing at first, then unable not to.

"You shouldn't call me that," Marguerite whispered. "You shouldn't call anyone that. It isn't safe."

"It's true, though," Sable said. "You're a beloved stranger. That's what the word means. It doesn't stop being true because it's dangerous."

For a moment the map-woman said nothing at all. Then she knelt, so that their eyes were level, and took the brass compass gently from Sable's palm and held it between them, and the needle spun once, twice, and settled — pointing at the wall, at the mountain, at the hidden heart of everything.

"Then we'd better find out," Marguerite said, "what it's pointing at. Before someone else does."

Far off across the valley, so faint it might have been wind, a bell rang once — the kind of bell that rode on the harnesses of Guild couriers — and both of them went still, listening, the way prey listens, the way the quiet game begins.
