## 4. What the Maps Refuse

The storm had spent itself by morning, leaving the world outside the window the color of old bone. Snow lay on the pines in thick white pelts, bending them into shapes like bowing monks, and the light that came through the ice-frosted glass was thin and blue and utterly silent. Marguerite had always loved this hour best—the hour before the day demanded anything of anyone—when the fire was low and the tea was hot and the only obligation was to sit with one's own thoughts until they grew civil again.

This morning there was no such peace. There was a child asleep in their bed, a brass compass on the table, and a question sitting in Marguerite's chest like a swallowed stone.

They set the compass down beside their charts and looked at it for a long time without touching it.

It was a beautiful object. That was the first obscenity of it. Guild work was always beautiful—the Guild believed beauty lent authority, that a gorgeous instrument could make a lie feel like scripture—and this compass bore every hallmark of their workshops: the casing turned from a single piece of brass, seamless, the hinge so fine it whispered when it moved, the maker's mark stamped small and neat beneath the bezel. A six-pointed star inside a circle. The Cartographers' Guild had been stamping that mark on instruments for two hundred years, and Marguerite had been selling routes to their couriers for fifteen of those years, and never once—not once—had they seen an instrument like this leave a Guild workshop.

Because it didn't point north.

Marguerite had tested it through the night, when sleep refused them and the child breathed slow and even in the bed behind the curtain. They had set it on the table and watched the needle swing, lazy at first, then certain, settling not toward the window and the north star beyond it but *inward*—toward the east wall, toward the mountains, toward the deep spine of the Thornfells where no road ran and no chart showed anything but blank paper and the words, in the Guild's tidy hand, *HERE BE STONE AND WEATHER*.

Stone and weather. As if a hundred miles of mountain range were nothing but a shrug from the earth.

Marguerite drank their tea. It had gone bitter from steeping too long, and they drank it anyway, because their hands wanted something to do that wasn't shaking.

Then they went to the chest beneath the window seat and began pulling out their life's work.

* * *

Fifteen years of mapping. Fifteen years of frozen nights and bribed shepherds and walking ridgelines with a surveyor's chain and a notebook sealed in oilskin against the sweat of fear. Marguerite Whitlock—or whatever name the current season required—had walked more of the Thornfells than any living soul, and had drawn them with a precision that made smugglers rich and made the Guild's couriers pay in gold coin rather than promises. Their charts were famous in certain rooms, in certain cities, among people who never asked where the knowledge came from. That was the arrangement. No real name, no attachments, no questions. The rules had kept them alive through fifteen winters, and Marguerite had repeated them to themselves like prayers, like the counting of beads, until the repetition wore grooves in them as deep as any cartographer's line.

Now they unrolled the charts across the table, weighting the corners with the iron candlestick, the teapot, the compass itself, and the stone from the hearth, and they began—methodically, the way they did everything—to compare.

The Thornfell surveys. The great Guild atlas of the northern ranges, printed forty years ago in three volumes, which Marguerite had bought at ruinous cost from a clerk in Corvane who'd been selling things he shouldn't have. The military route-maps from the border wars. The shepherd's sketches, the pilgrim tracings, the crude charcoal drawings children made of the valleys where their mothers warned them not to wander.

And Sable's compass, its needle lying steady as a dead man's finger, pointing east-northeast into the heart of the range.

Marguerite laid a ruler along the bearing and drew a line in red chalk across their own master chart—a working copy, inked and re-inked over years, the most honest document they possessed. The line ran up the Greywash valley, past the ferry crossing, into the high country above the tree line, and then—

And then it crossed the place where Marguerite's own map simply stopped.

Not faded. Not torn. *Stopped.* The contour lines ran confident and close together up the valley walls, and then they ended, all of them, at precisely the same elevation, along precisely the same curve, as if the mountains themselves had been sliced off with a knife. Beyond that line: blank vellum. Marguerite had always assumed the blankness was their own failure—that the passes above had defeated them, that weather or war or simple exhaustion had ended the survey incomplete. They had meant, someday, to finish it. Someday was a word cartographers used the way drunkards used *tomorrow*.

But now they pulled out the Guild atlas, the printed volume, and found the same valley, the same slice, the same blankness—and the blankness was *identical*. Every contour ended at the same point. Every label stopped at the same curve. Even the hachures, the little shading strokes that gave mountains their flesh, terminated along a line so clean it might have been ruled.

Two maps, made decades apart, by different hands, for different purposes, agreeing perfectly on where the world ended.

Maps didn't agree like that by accident. Maps agreed like that because someone had told them to.

Marguerite sat back. The fire ticked in the grate. In the bedroom, the child murmured something in her sleep—a soft run of syllables, rising and falling, and even half-heard through a curtain it made the hair rise on Marguerite's arms, because it was the cadence of their grandmother's voice, the cadence of a lullaby about a river of glass, and it was coming out of a starving girl who had walked out of a whiteout carrying a Guild instrument that pointed at the place where every map in Marguerite's possession had been amputated.

They went back to the charts. They worked until the light changed and the tea went cold and their eyes burned, and what they found, they found the way one finds a body under snow—one shape at first, then another, then the horrible arithmetic of how many.

There were seven places in the Thornfells where the records simply ceased. Seven regions where every source—Guild, military, folk, smuggler—ended along ruled lines, as though seven wounds had been closed with the same needle. And the regions were not scattered. They were contiguous. If you took the blank spaces and imagined them filled—if you let the contour lines continue in your mind the way the terrain plainly continued, ridge joining ridge, valley joining valley—the blanks assembled themselves into a single shape.

A country-shaped hole in the middle of the mountains.

Marguerite's hands were flat on the table. They became aware, distantly, that they were breathing too carefully, the way one breathes when the floor has begun to tilt.

"No," they said aloud, to the empty room, in the voice one uses to argue with a diagnosis. "No, that's—"

But the charts said otherwise. The charts, which Marguerite had trusted longer than they had trusted any living person, said otherwise in their own careful hand.

They thought of the treaty. Everyone knew of the Vessarine Treaty—it was in the schoolbooks, a paragraph long, between chapters on grain tariffs. *The territories south of the Thornfell watershed, having been relinquished by their former holders, are incorporated into the Crown domains and shall be administered as crown forest reserve.* Former holders. Unnamed. Relinquished—by whom, to whom, in exchange for what, the paragraph did not say. Children memorized it and forgot it in the same week. Marguerite had memorized it and forgotten it too, and had never once, in fifteen years of drawing these mountains, thought to ask what lay south of the watershed.

Because there was nothing there. Because the maps said so. Because Marguerite had *drawn* the maps that said so.

They stood up so fast the chair went over backward. They stood at the window with their arms wrapped around themselves, watching the snow-light, and they felt it arrive in them slowly, the way cold water arrives in a boot—first a seep, then a flood.

The country hadn't fallen. Countries that fall leave ruins, refugees, songs, grudges; countries that fall leave *traces*, and traces get mapped, because mapping is what conquerors do to what they've broken—they measure it, they gloat over it, they print its rivers with new names. But there were no ruins on the charts. No burned villages marked with the little Guild symbol for destruction. No roads shown falling into disrepair. Nothing. Just absence, clean and total and *ruled*, as if the land had been erased not by fire but by ink.

By ink. By men at tables in warm rooms in Corvane, with pens and rulers and the Guild's six-pointed star on their letterheads, deciding—line by line, valley by valley—to stop recording a place until the place stopped existing. Not conquest. *Censorship.* You couldn't tax what wasn't on the roll. You couldn't garrison what wasn't on the chart. You couldn't rebel against a kingdom that officially had no room for you. Erase the map and you erase the claim, the history, the grievance, the grave. Erase the map and within two generations the erasure is complete, because the young will believe the paper over their own grandmothers' mouths.

Marguerite knew this because they had watched it happen in miniature their whole life. Their grandmother had hummed her lullabies with the windows shut. Had taught the words only in whispers, only to one grandchild, only after checking twice that the door was barred. *We don't sing that outside,* she'd say, and Marguerite, small and obedient, had never asked why. Heirlooms of silence, passed down like tarnished silver.

And Marguerite had grown up and become—God help them—a mapper. Had sold routes to the Guild's couriers. Had taken their gold and banked their reputation on the precision of their lines, and never once wondered why the Guild paid so well for a wilderness it claimed contained nothing worth knowing. Never once held their own beautiful charts up to the light and noticed that the blanks were too regular, too consistent, too *well-made* to be innocent.

Complicity doesn't announce itself. It arrives as a career. It arrives as competence, as praise, as gold coin counted out on a table exactly like this one.

Marguerite put their palms flat on the cold window glass and stayed there until their skin ached, and when they finally turned back to the table, the fury in them had gone quiet and structural, load-bearing, the kind that does not burn out because it has become part of the house.

* * *

The child woke near midday and came out rubbing her eyes, dragging the blanket with her like a snail's shell, and stopped when she saw the table.

Charts everywhere. The atlas open. The compass in the center with its needle steady on its inward bearing. Marguerite standing over it all with red chalk dust on their fingers and an expression that made the girl take a step back toward the bedroom.

"It's all right," Marguerite said, and heard how unconvincing it was, and tried again, softer. "Sit. Eat something. I need—" They stopped. What did they need? To ask her questions she might not have answers for. To use a child the way the Guild used instruments. "I need to show you something," they finished, and hated how it sounded, and said it anyway.

She ate the bread and honey they put in front of her, watching them over the top of it, and when she was done Marguerite turned the master chart toward her and tapped the ruled line where the contours died.

"Do you know this place?" They pointed at the blank. "What's here?"

She leaned over the vellum. Her brow furrowed. She looked at the blank space for a long moment, and then she looked up at Marguerite with an expression of pure, unguarded puzzlement, and said a word in her language—soft, round, unmistakably a *name*—and touched the blank space with one finger as if laying it on the brow of a sleeping friend.

Then she frowned harder. She looked at the blank. She looked at her own finger. She looked at Marguerite, and something frightened moved through her face, some childhood certainty beginning to crack, and she spoke again—more words, quicker, pointing at the empty vellum and then at the window, at the eastern wall, at the mountains beyond it, as if insisting on the connection. *It's there. It should be there. Why isn't it there.*

"I know," Marguerite said quietly. "I know."

She was breathing fast now. She pressed both hands flat on the blank space, the way one presses on a wound, and said a third word, and this one Marguerite's body understood before their mind did, because their grandmother had sung it: *home*. Or something older than home. Something like *the place that holds us*.

The word fell into the blank space on the map and vanished, and the map did not answer, and the child stared at the paper as if it had betrayed her personally, and Marguerite—cold-furious, complicit, fifteen years deep in the Guild's gold—reached across the table and covered her small hands with theirs.

"They took it off the paper," they said. They had no Vessarine for it. Perhaps no language had a clean way to say it. "Not the place. The paper. They decided it wasn't there, and they made everyone agree." Their voice came out rougher than intended. "I helped them make everyone agree."

The girl didn't understand the words. But she understood the tone, the way children and animals understand tone, and she turned her hands over under Marguerite's and gripped back, hard, with the desperate strength of someone who has decided—prematurely, dangerously, the way the drowning decide—that this person is the shore.

The compass sat between them, needle unwavering, pointing at everything the ink had refused.

Marguerite looked at it for a long time. Then they looked at the floorboards beneath the window seat—at one particular board, shorter than its neighbors, worn pale at one corner from fifteen years of being stepped around and never questioned.

There was a book under there. There had been a book under there since the night their grandmother pressed it into their hands and said, *burn it or keep it, but never let them see it,* and Marguerite had kept it, unread past the first page, unread for fifteen years, because opening it would mean the rules had been broken and the rules were the only thing standing between them and the dark.

The rules had just failed. The rules had been the Guild's rules all along, and the Guild had unmapped a country, and the proof was sleeping in Marguerite's bed humming a song Marguerite had known since before they could read.

"Stay here," Marguerite said to the girl, though they were already kneeling, already working the blade of the knife into the seam of the board. "Whatever happens—stay here."

The board came up with a groan of old nails. Dust rose in the cold light. And there, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff with age, lay the songbook—her grandmother's mother's, her mother's, hers—waiting in the dark the way buried things wait: patiently, and without any belief at all in being rescued.
