## 5. The Songbook Beneath the Floor

The compass would not stop pointing.

Marguerite had set it on the table, turned it, weighted its brass case with a candlestick, and still the needle swung with patient insistence toward the eastern wall—toward the mountains' heart, toward whatever folded country the Guild had inked out of existence. It pointed like a finger raised in accusation. And beneath that wall, beneath three boards Marguerite had pried up exactly twice in fifteen years, lay the songbook.

They had not wanted to open it. That was the truth of it, plain as frost on glass. They had spent half the night telling themselves there was nothing to find—that the book was what they had always believed it to be: lullabies, kitchen songs, the harmless music of a dead woman's childhood. But Sable sat across from them now, wrapped in the good blanket, watching with those dark unblinking eyes, and the child had begun humming again. The same melody. The one Marguerite had known before they had known their own name.

"You know it," Sable said. Not in the trade-tongue. In Vessarine, the words round and strange and warm as bread. Then, seeing Marguerite's face, she tried the common speech: "You know the song. You sang it wrong, but you know it."

"I sang it the way my grandmother taught me."

"She taught you the outside of it." The child said it without cruelty, which made it worse. "There's an inside."

* * *

The floorboards came up easier than Marguerite remembered, or perhaps harder—it was impossible to tell fear from effort. Beneath them, in a cavity lined with oilcloth gone stiff as bark, the songbook waited. It was small, bound in dyed leather the color of dried blood, its corners worn soft by decades of handling. A ribbon marker trailed out of it like something drowned.

Marguerite lifted it into the lamplight and did not open it for a long moment. Fifteen years of rules pressed down on their hands: *no real name, no attachments, no questions.* The rules had been their grandmother's first, though she had never spoken them aloud. She had simply lived them, so thoroughly that Marguerite had absorbed them the way children absorb gravity.

Sable leaned close. Her breath fogged in the cold room. "Open it," she whispered, and then, softer, in her own tongue: *"Luma."* Open. The word sounded like a door.

Marguerite opened it.

* * *

The first pages were what they had expected. Lullabies, written in a careful hand that sloped leftward, in an alphabet Marguerite could not read but recognized the shape of—the way you recognize a face from a dream. Between the staves of some songs, tiny illustrations: a bird, a boat, a tower with a light in its window. Marguerite's grandmother had sung these while kneading bread, while mending nets, while rocking a child who would grow up to sell roads to men who erased countries.

But the lullabies ended a third of the way through. What followed was not song.

It was a record.

The handwriting changed—tighter, faster, pressed hard enough into the page to emboss the sheets beneath. The language was still Vessarine, but between the lines ran translations in the common tongue, added later in different ink, as if the writer had needed to be sure. As if she had needed to practice saying it even to herself.

Marguerite read. The fire ticked. Sable read over their shoulder, lips moving, and every few lines she made a small sound—not words, just breath, the sound of someone finding their own house burned down in a stranger's handwriting.

*They came at dusk on the third day of the burning,* the record began. *Not soldiers first. Mapmakers.*

*My father was a keeper of the map-halls in Vel Sarith. He used to say that in Vessar, a map was a prayer—you drew the land as it dreamed itself, not as it stood. He said the Guild's maps were the opposite: they drew the land as the Crown wished it had never been. When the treaty was signed, the map-halls were ordered emptied. My father refused. He said a country is not its borders; a country is its names. So they took the halls instead, and then they took him, and then they took his name off every document he had ever touched, until it was as if no man named what my father was named had ever drawn breath in Thornfell.*

*I was nine years old.*

Marguerite's hands were steady. They noticed this with a strange detachment—the steadiness of hands trained never to shake, never to hesitate over a route, never to betray anything. Fifteen years of selling smuggler paths, and their hands had learned perfect stillness. Now that stillness felt like complicity wearing gloves.

*We fled north with eleven others,* the record continued. *By spring there were six. The mountains took some. A warden patrol took two—a man and his daughter, taken at a ferry crossing because the daughter sang while she worked. Sang in our tongue. Remember that, little one. Whatever else you bury, bury your singing deepest.*

Marguerite looked up. Sable was staring at them.

"That's why," the child said slowly, working it out in the trade-tongue. "That's why you don't sing. I heard you stop yourself. In the cave, when I hummed—you almost hummed back, and then you stopped, like you'd touched a hot pan."

"My grandmother never sang past dark," Marguerite said. Their voice came out flat, unfamiliar. "I thought it was grief. I built my whole life on top of what I thought was grief." They turned another page. "It wasn't grief. It was drill."

* * *

The lamplight guttered once, and in its stuttering Marguerite was suddenly elsewhere—thirty years younger, seven years old, sitting on a kitchen floor much like this one while their grandmother mended a net by the window.

*"Mémé,"* the child had asked, *"why do we live where nobody comes?"*

*The old woman's needle paused. Outside, snow was filling the tracks of the single cart that visited them each month, erasing the evidence of contact as efficiently as any Guild clerk.*

*"Because being seen is the first thing they take,"* she had said, in the trade-tongue, always the trade-tongue inside these walls, *"and the last thing they give back."*

*"Seen by who?"*

*She had resumed her mending. The needle flashed. And she had answered with a question, which was her way:* *"What does a map do, little one?"*

*"Shows where things are."*

*"No." She had shaken her head, slow and terrible. "A map decides where things are allowed to be. Everything else it leaves blank—and what is blank may be filled with anything. Soldiers. Fire. Nothing at all." She had touched the child's cheek with a hand that smelled of tallow and pine resin. "So we stay blank, you and I. Blank is safe. Blank is invisible. Promise me you will be blank."*

*And the child had promised, because children promise everything, and had kept the promise for thirty years without knowing what it cost, because the price had been deducted so gradually—in friendships declined, in questions unanswered, in a name surrendered before it was ever truly owned—that Marguerite had mistaken the empty space where a life should have been for discipline. Had called it discipline. Had been proud of it.*

* * *

Marguerite turned the pages with hands that had finally begun to tremble.

The record grew darker. There were lists—names, dozens of them, written in columns. Refugee families, scattered through the Thornfell settlements under new names. Marguerite recognized some surnames as ones that still appeared on tax rolls, on ferry manifests, in the ledgers of fences they had traded with for years. *The Odile family, Greywash crossing — keeps the old tongue in one hymn only, sung low, sung alone.* Marguerite's breath caught. Odile. The ferry-keeper who owed them three favors and asked no questions. Who had once, drunk on plum brandy, hummed a tune Marguerite had never placed until this moment.

"It wasn't just us," they said aloud. The words felt stolen. "There were others. There are others. We're not—" They stopped. Started again. "I spent fifteen years thinking I was the last of something, and I've been buying rope from people who hum the same lullaby."

"Then you weren't hiding a secret," Sable said. "You were hiding a country."

The record's final entries were the worst. The grandmother's account of the treaty itself, copied from somewhere—perhaps from memory, perhaps smuggled out in another refugee's coat:

*The Crown did not burn Vessar. Understand this. Burning leaves ash, and ash can be mourned, and mourning can become memory, and memory becomes dangerous. No—they did something cleaner. They signed a paper declaring that no such place existed, and then their cartographers walked the valleys and redrew them, and the redrawn maps became the true maps, and the true maps became the world. You cannot mourn a country that was never there. You cannot return to a blank space. That is the genius of it, and the horror. They didn't kill us. They unmapped us.*

*And I let them. Do you hear me, whoever reads this—I let them. I kept my tongue behind my teeth for sixty years. I raised my grandchild nameless and songless and safe, and I told myself safety was enough. If you are reading this, then either I am dead and you have broken open my floor, in which case: forgive me, and finish what I could not.*

*Or you are my grandchild, and you have already broken every rule I gave you, in which case: forgive me, and forgive yourself, because the silence was not cowardice. It was a bridge I built for you to cross in the other direction, when you found the courage I lacked.*

Marguerite closed the book.

For a long time neither of them moved. The compass on the table ticked softly against its brass housing, patient as a heartbeat, still pointing east.

"I sold routes," Marguerite said finally. It came out like a confession, because it was one. "Fifteen years. I sold the Guild the very thing they used—the paths, the passes, the knowledge of where things are. I told myself it was survival. I told myself staying blank was the discipline my grandmother taught me." They laughed, and there was nothing of laughter in it. "She wasn't teaching me discipline. She was teaching me to disappear, and I got so good at it I disappeared from myself. No real name. No attachments. No questions. I thought those were rules. They were symptoms."

Sable reached across the table and put her small hand on the songbook's cover, over the place where a title might have been, where instead the leather was bare—unnamed, unlabeled, blank.

"In Vessar," she said, "the map-hall keepers had a saying. My mother said it." She spoke it first in her own language—*"Sar veth ilume kai"*—and then translated it carefully, the way you handle something fragile. "A name unwritten is not a name lost. It is a name waiting."

Marguerite looked at the child. At the compass. At the floorboards lying open like a wound that had finally been allowed to speak.

"The courier comes tomorrow," they said. "Renne. He'll want the winter routes, and he'll ask his courteous questions, and he'll smile while he measures the distance between my words." They stood, and something in their posture had changed—some load shifted, some long-carried weight set down and replaced by a different burden, heavier but honest. "He'll get his routes. And while he's copying them, I'm going to ask him about the erased valleys. About Vel Sarith. About what the Guild does with places it declares never existed."

Sable's eyes went wide. "You said never ask questions. Your first rule."

"Yes." Marguerite picked up the songbook and did not put it back beneath the floor. Instead they set it on the shelf, in plain sight, spine outward, unnamed and unhidden. "Rule one is repealed."

Outside, the wind turned, and carried with it—from somewhere far up-valley, faint as memory—the sound of a bell that no charted village possessed. Sable's head came up sharply, and her hand found the compass, and the needle trembled against its glass like a thing alive.

"They're waking up," the child whispered. "The folded places. They heard the book open."

Marguerite blew out the lamp, and in the darkness began, very quietly, to sing.
