## 7. Thornfell Roads

We left before dawn, in that grey hour when the snow stops pretending to be beautiful and becomes only an obstacle.

I had spent the night doing what I had sworn never to do: burning my own work. Fifteen years of routes, coded in a cipher of my own devising, sold piecemeal to the Guild and its couriers—every page of the master ledger went into the stove while Sable slept, curled like a comma in my one good chair. The paper caught with a smell I will not describe here, because some smells are private griefs. When it was done I swept the ash into the firebox and told myself it was ash from ordinary correspondence.

The child woke anyway. She always woke. She had the light sleeper's gift of children who have run from something, and she stood in the doorway in her too-large coat watching me with those dark, patient eyes.

"You're burning your maps," she said in Vessarine.

"I'm burning my debts," I answered, in the same tongue, and was startled by how easily it came back, how the language fit my mouth like water fits a channel it cut long ago.

She considered this. "Are they the same thing?"

"Everything is the same thing, eventually." I shouldered the pack. "Come. We have a mountain to cross before a very polite man crosses us."

* * *

The smugglers' paths are not on any map, including mine. That is the first rule of them. They exist in the negative space between official roads, worn into the rock by generations of feet that preferred not to be counted. My grandmother taught me the first of them—though she would have denied it to her grave—and the fences and ferry-keepers of Thornfell taught me the rest, each lesson priced in favors I am only now coming to collect.

The first favor was owed by Tam Voss, who keeps a fence in a hollow beneath Crag's Tooth where he sells things that fell off carts, or were pushed. His door is behind a false wall of stacked slate, and you knock on the third stone from the left, twice, then once, and if he likes you the wall swings inward on oiled hinges.

He did not like me. He liked what I'd done for him in the winter of '09, which is a different thing entirely, and colder.

"Marguerite Whitlock," he said, though that is not my name, and we both knew it, and we both let the lie stand between us like a candle neither of us would snuff. "You look terrible."

"I've been better."

"And you've brought—" He looked past me at Sable, who was examining his shelves of contraband with the frank appraisal of someone who has seen worse rooms than this. "A child."

"A child who isn't here," I said. "Neither am I. We're two rumors passing through your cellar, Tam, and rumors don't leave footprints."

He chewed on that, and on the inside of his cheek, which he does when he's calculating. "Renne's men were at Miller's Ford yesterday. Asking after a woman and a girl. The Guild doesn't usually send three riders for a woman and a girl."

"No. It doesn't."

"That means they think it's worth three riders." He said it gently, for Tam, which is to say he said it without gloating. "What did you do?"

"I stopped selling," I said. "And started remembering."

Something moved behind his eyes—not fear exactly, but the animal awareness of it, the way a dog goes still when weather changes. "The old road," he said quietly. "You found the old road, didn't you. The one through the folded country."

I hadn't told him about the folded country. I hadn't told anyone. But Tam Voss has been moving goods through Thornfell since before I was born, and there are things a man learns by carrying other people's secrets across thirty years of stone. Things he never says aloud until saying them no longer matters.

"The pass under Raven's Shoulder," I said. "Is it open?"

"It's open." He turned to his shelves and began pulling down bread, dried apples, a wheel of hard cheese wrapped in cloth. "It's open the way a wound is open. You'll want food. And you'll want—" he hesitated over a shelf, then took down a small tin of rendered fat and pine pitch, "—this, for the child's boots. Snow-blind feet have ended more runners than soldiers ever did."

Sable took the tin from him with both hands and a gravity that made the old fence blink. "Thank you," she said—in Trade Tongue, careful and accented, her first words to a stranger in our language. Tam stared at her as if she'd spoken in bells.

"What's the cost?" I asked, because with Tam there is always a cost, and asking is a courtesy.

"Nothing now." He wouldn't meet my eye. "You'll owe me a route someday. A real one. To wherever you're going." And I understood: he wasn't charging me. He was buying a ticket.

* * *

Above Crag's Tooth the paths narrow to goat-width ledges where the wind comes up the valley like a tide and tries to peel you off the rock. I had meant to teach Sable the mountains gradually. The mountains declined to cooperate.

By midday we were climbing the switchbacks above the Greywash gorge, and the wind had teeth, and the child was flagging. She didn't complain—that was the thing about her, the thing that kept catching in my chest like a fishhook. She simply slowed, and set her jaw, and put one boot in front of the other with the grim economy of someone much older wearing a small body.

I stopped at a ledge out of the wind and sat her down against the stone.

"We rest," I said. "Ten minutes. Not eleven."

"I can keep walking."

"I know you can. That's not the question." I handed her the cheese and watched her eat, small fierce bites. "Listen. There's an arithmetic out here, and I'd rather you learn it from me than learn it the way most people learn it, which is once, briefly, and fatally. First law: the mountain doesn't care how brave you are. It cares whether you're smart. Bravery spends energy; energy is money; spend it stupidly and you die rich in courage."

She chewed. Considered. "What's the second law?"

"Never trust a path you haven't walked, and never walk a path you can't walk twice. If you go somewhere you can't come back from, you'd better be certain you're not coming back."

"And the third?"

I looked out over the gorge. Far below, the Greywash ran grey-green under ice shelves, patient as everything else in this country. "The third law is the expensive one," I said. "No attachments."

She was quiet a moment. Then, with the devastating directness of children: "You broke that one already. When you opened the door."

The wind filled the silence where my answer should have been. I had opened the door—I had broken fifteen years of discipline in a single whiteout moment, taken in a half-frozen stranger because she spoke my grandmother's dead language, and every mile since had been a payment on that debt. Three Guild riders at Miller's Ford. Aldous Renne's immaculate handwriting on a summons I'd found tucked in my shutter that last morning: *A discrepancy has been noted. Kindly present yourself at the Chapterhouse at your earliest convenience.* The courtesy of it. The courtesy was the threat.

"Eat your cheese," I said.

She smiled—the first smile I'd seen from her that reached all the way up—and ate her cheese.

* * *

We made the high col by late afternoon, and that was where the mountains began to behave strangely.

I had known, in the abstract, what the compass pointed toward. I had seen the inked-out valleys in the Guild charts, whole watersheds blanked with that flat institutional hatching that says *here be nothing*. But knowing a thing in the abstract is different from standing on a ridge with the sun going down and watching the land ahead of you refuse to resolve.

There was a valley below us. I could see it—or almost see it, the way you almost see the bottom of a deep lake. Trees, or the suggestion of trees. A river-thread catching the last light. But when I tried to fix my eyes on any detail—a boulder, a bend in the stream—it slid away, like a word on the tip of the tongue. The air above it shimmered faintly, the way air shimmers over a forge, and my sense of distance simply gave up, reporting *near* and *far* in the same breath.

Sable had no such trouble. She stood at the edge of the col with the brass compass held out flat on her palm, and the needle swung slowly, slowly, until it pointed not north but down and inward, into the heart of the fold, and she said, softly, in Vessarine:

*"Tessalya."*

Home. Or near enough to home that the word trembled.

"Don't say it too loud," I said, and heard the fear in my own voice, and hated it. "Not yet. Not until we're under cover. Sound carries, and—" I stopped, because I didn't actually know what sound carried *to*, only that in the last week I had watched a pass exist because a child spoke to it, and I was no longer willing to bet on the rules of the world holding steady.

She closed her hand over the compass and nodded, obedient in the way that hurt worse than argument.

We descended along a seam of scree that Tam had described and that existed, barely, a thread of a path stitched down through country that seemed embarrassed to contain it. Twice the path vanished under drifts and I found it again by feel, by the memory in my hands of paths my grandmother's hands had shown me when I was smaller than Sable. Her name was Ilse, officially. Before that, I suspect, it was something else, in another alphabet, sung rather than written. I thought about that a great deal on the descent, with the dark coming up out of the valley floor like water filling a basin.

* * *

Nightfall brought us to the Waystone, a shepherd's bothy older than any of the kingdoms currently claiming this range, its walls dry-stacked from stone that had been standing when the stones were younger. There was a hearth, and a store of cut peat left by whoever had passed through last, per the old custom: take what you need, leave what you can, tell no one.

I got a fire going while Sable explored the bothy with her hands, reading the walls the way I read charts. Carved into the lintel, half-worn, were letters in no alphabet the Guild recognizes. She traced them and read them aloud, barely a whisper: *"Vessa en doru. Vessa en kalim."*

Vessar endures. Vessar remembers.

"How old?" I asked.

"Older than the burning," she said. "My mother said these were carved by the first walkers. The ones who carried the maps out when the soldiers came." She settled beside the fire, close—not touching me, but within the radius where touch would be possible, which for Sable was a declaration. "Marguerite. Why did your grandmother stop speaking?"

The question landed the way her questions always did, precisely, in the soft place. I fed the fire instead of answering. Then, because the firelight made lying difficult and because I owed her more than routes:

"Because speaking gets people killed. Because she watched a library burn and understood that the soldiers weren't burning books—they were burning the proof that a place had ever existed. And she decided that the safest Vessarine speaker was one nobody knew about. So she crossed the border, changed her name, married a Whitlock, and sang the lullabies only when she thought I was asleep." I looked at the flames. "I wasn't asleep. I never told her I wasn't asleep. Now she's dead and I know all the verses and there was no one to sing them to, and then there was."

Sable was quiet for a long time. The fire cracked. Somewhere outside, wind worried at the eaves.

"My mother sang the same one," she said finally. "The one about the river that runs uphill. She said it wasn't a song. She said it was a key that forgot what it opened." She looked up at me, firelit, ancient-eyed. "Do you think it remembered?"

"I think," I said carefully, "that keys are patient."

That earned me another of the full smiles. Then she curled up against my side—attachments, third law, so much for that—and was asleep within minutes, the compass clutched to her chest like a second heartbeat.

I stayed awake longer than I needed to. That is the toll of the roads: not the miles but the watchfulness, the mind refusing to stand down even when the body begs. I lay listening to the wind and counting our assets like a miser. One fence's goodwill, spent. One hidden pass, ahead. One ferry-keeper on the Greywash who owed me a crossing from a bad winter six years ago, when I'd carried her son's fever-break through a whiteout and asked nothing at the time, which was either generosity or investment, depending on how cynical you were feeling.

Odile Marchand. She'd say she owed me nothing—she'd say the fever-break was repaid in gossip and bad brandy—but Odile's debts are like her river: deeper than they look, and running in directions she doesn't advertise. If anyone could put us across the Greywash ahead of Renne's riders, it was her. And if anyone would charge a price that hurt, it was her too. That was the arithmetic again. Every favor on these roads is a coin, and coins get spent, and I had been spending mine all day.

I just hadn't expected the spending to feel like this—to feel, with every favor called in, less like depletion and more like conversion. As if I were turning fifteen years of careful, lonely capital into something else. Something warmer and far less liquid.

The child sighed in her sleep and pressed closer.

Outside, far off to the south, I saw it: a pinpoint of light moving along the valley road I had burned out of existence two nights ago. Too steady for a lantern carried by hand. Too deliberate for a shepherd.

Lantern-light, riding at the pace of a man who reads while he walks.

I banked the fire, woke Sable without a word, and we went out into the dark toward the Greywash, while somewhere behind us, courteous and meticulous and gaining, Aldous Renne followed the thread of a road that no longer officially existed—and that I alone, now, could draw.
