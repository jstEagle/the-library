## 10. The Guild's Erasure Writ

The writ arrived at dawn, as all the Guild's worst instruments did, sealed in grey wax that smelled faintly of iron and old rain.

Aldous Renne stood in the archive antechamber of the Cartographers' Guildhall with the letter unopened in his hands, watching the light come up over Thornfell through windows of glass so thick it turned the mountains the color of drowned things. He had been awake since the third bell. He was always awake by the third bell. The Guild raised its orphans on bells the way other households raised children on lullabies, and even now, nineteen years after they had first taken him in—a boy of six with frostbitten fingers and no name anyone would claim—he woke to a schedule written into his bones.

He broke the seal. Read it once. Read it again.

*By authority of the Master Cartographer and the Crown's Commission on Territorial Integrity, the persons known provisionally as "the mapmaker Whitlock" and one juvenile of unknown provenance are hereby designated Errata. They are to be corrected from record. All routes sold under the Whitlock mark are voided. All clients retaining said routes will be considered complicit. Correction is to be effected with discretion.*

Corrected from record. Renne had drafted such language himself, twice, for smugglers whose routes had grown too valuable to leave loose in the world. He knew what *correction* meant when the Guild performed it. It did not mean arrest. Arrest left records. Correction left nothing—not a body, not a grave, not a line in a ledger to say a man had ever drawn breath in Thornfell. The Guild's mercy was that it never killed anyone. It simply made them into people who had never been.

And the child.

*A juvenile of unknown provenance.* The phrase sat in his chest like a swallowed stone. He set the writ down on the reading desk and pressed both palms flat against the wood until the grain bit into his skin, and he thought about a girl half-frozen in a doorway somewhere above the snowline, small enough that the wind could have taken her if the wind had wanted her.

He thought about himself at six, standing in this same antechamber, while a woman in Guild grey had knelt to look him in the eye and asked him his name. He hadn't had one. Or he'd had one, and it had belonged to a village that no longer appeared on any chart, and the woman had seen the confusion on his face and written something in her ledger anyway—*provenance unknown, ward of the Guild*—and that had become the whole of him. Provenance unknown. A blank space where a childhood should have been, filled in later with bells and ink and rules.

They had been good to him. That was the thing he kept returning to, the thing he could not put down. The Guild had fed him, taught him letters, given him work that mattered—the clean, necessary work of keeping maps true. When he'd shown aptitude for field inquiry, they had trained him in courtesy and observation and the gentle arts of getting people to tell him things they shouldn't. When he'd earned his courier's sash, the Master Cartographer herself had pinned it on, and her hands had been warm through his coat, and she had said, *You'll do well, Aldous. You know how to be invisible without being erased. There's a difference, and you understand it.*

He understood it. He had built his whole self inside that difference.

So why, holding this writ, did he feel like a man standing on a frozen river, hearing the ice begin to speak?

* * *

The morning session convened in the Map Room, where the great table held the current master chart of the Thornfell range under a lattice of brass weights. Renne took his place among the senior clerks along the eastern rail, close enough to see the ink, far enough not to be asked questions. His rank permitted him to listen. It did not yet permit him to speak unless spoken to, though everyone in the room knew the Thornfell inquiry was his—his threads, his pulling—and so the Master Cartographer looked at him once, briefly, before she began.

She was a spare woman, silver at the temples, with the particular stillness of someone who had spent fifty years making decisions that other people had to live inside. Her voice, when she read the operative clauses of the writ aloud, was level as a horizon line.

"—and accordingly, the designation of Errata attaches to both subjects. Correction teams will be assembled. Warden-Captain Vess will command."

Renne did not let anything move in his face. But he heard the name and understood its weight. Vess did not do inquiries. Vess did conclusions. Where Renne pulled threads, Vess cut ropes.

"The child," said Under-Clerk Halloway, a soft-bodied man with ink-stained cuffs and a talent for asking ugly questions in pleasant tones, "is an asset, surely. Unknown provenance, speaking an unregistered language. The Linguistic Section has been requesting a live specimen of pre-Treaty dialects for—"

"The writ does not distinguish," the Master Cartographer said. "Correction applies to both."

"With respect, ma'am, a child who speaks a dead tongue is worth more than—"

"A child who speaks a dead tongue," the Master Cartographer said, "is a child who can testify that a dead kingdom existed. The Treaty of Ashmark is not a historical document, gentlemen. It is a working instrument. Its efficacy depends on certain territories remaining unmapped, and its efficacy depends, further, on there being no living voices to contradict our charts." She laid two fingers flat on the master chart, over a region of clean white paper where mountains should have been. "Vessar fell. What survived the fall was memory, and memory is a cartographic hazard. We do not preserve hazards. We correct them."

The word again. *Correct.* Renne watched Halloway nod, watch the other clerks nod, watch the whole room settle into the comfortable rhythm of institutional assent, and felt the stone in his chest shift.

"Courier Renne." The Master Cartographer's eyes found him. "Your preliminary findings."

He gave them cleanly. The discrepancy in the winter routes—an old road appearing in Whitlock's sold work, a road matching fragments of a pre-Treaty survey recovered from a burned waystation. The ferry-keeper Odile on the Greywash, questioned and released, uncooperative in the manner of someone protecting something. The pattern of purchases: dried food in quantities for two, a child's boots from a cobbler in Lowmarsh, medicine. Fever medicine.

"They're moving toward the high passes," he concluded. "Whitlock knows every smuggler path in the range. If they reach the interior valleys—" He stopped himself. *If they reach the valleys that are not on our maps,* he had almost said, which was a sentence that only made sense if the valleys existed.

"If they reach the interior," the Master Cartographer supplied, without inflection, "they will find nothing. Because there is nothing. Is that understood, Courier?"

"It is understood, ma'am."

But it was not understood. It was the opposite of understood. Because Renne had walked the Greywash ferry crossing three days ago, and while Odile had poured him tea and lied to him beautifully about everything that mattered, he had noticed the thing she couldn't help noticing for him: the way her eyes went, twice, involuntary, to the northern wall of the valley—to a fold in the mountains that his own Guild-issued chart rendered as blank paper, but that his eyes, his actual eyes, insisted contained shadow, depth, the suggestion of a slope descending into somewhere.

He had checked the chart against the landscape four times since. The landscape kept winning.

* * *

He spent the afternoon in the deep stacks, ostensibly cross-referencing the burned waystation fragments against the pre-Treaty holdings. In truth he was looking for something he could not have named if Halloway had asked him, and Halloway did ask him, drifting past with an armful of folios and that pleasant, terrible curiosity.

"Something eating you, Renne? You've got the face of a man auditing his own soul."

"Just reconciling survey dates."

"Mm." Halloway set the folios down and leaned against the shelf, lowering his voice into the register he used for confidences. "Between us—the child. Vess wants the correction done quiet, but the Linguistic Section is screaming. If you were to, say, locate the subjects first, and secure the juvenile intact..." He let the sentence hang, generous and rotten. "There's advancement in that, Aldous. Real advancement. You've come up fast; some people notice. Some people resent it. Bring the Guild a living piece of Vessar and no one resents anything ever again."

"I'll keep it in mind," Renne said, and smiled the smile the Guild had taught him, and hated how easily it came.

When Halloway drifted off, Renne stood alone among the shelves with the dust moving in the high window-light, and finally admitted to himself what he had been looking for.

He pulled it at the bottom of the third case: a registry of foundlings, 1801 to present, bound in the same grey as everything else. He turned to the year of his own intake and found his own entry—*male child, approx. six years, found at the Ashmark treeline, no identifying effects, provenance unknown*—and beneath it, in the column reserved for disposition notes, a single line in a clerk's hand he did not recognize:

*Found within the corrected zone. Retain for Guild service.*

The corrected zone. He had always assumed it meant the border skirmishes, the burned villages, the ordinary cruelties of treaty-making. But he was holding the writ now in his memory, and the writ's language was the same language, and the corrected zones were places the Guild erased, and erased places did not ordinarily leave survivors wandering their treelines.

Unless the survivor was useful. Unless a child with no name and no past could be raised into a man with no questions and perfect loyalty—a man who would never think to ask why he'd been found at the edge of a blank space on a map.

His hands were steady. He noted that with detached professional interest, the way he noted everything. His hands were steady and his mouth was dry and somewhere above the snowline a child was walking through a whiteout carrying a compass that pointed at the hole in the world where his own life had started, and the Guild had just signed the paper that made her into errata.

* * *

Evening found him in his quarters, a narrow cell of a room the Guild had assigned him at fourteen, writing his report by lamplight because reports were the one ritual that still organized him. He wrote what he had observed. He wrote it accurately, because accuracy was the last thing he owned outright. And then he reached the section marked *Disposition and Recommendation*, and his pen stopped.

The ink dried in the nib while he sat there.

He thought about the Master Cartographer's fingers resting on the blank paper. *We do not preserve hazards.* He thought about Halloway's voice: *a live specimen.* He thought about the foundling registry, and the treeline, and the word *retain*, which was a word you used for tools and animals and not for children, except that the Guild had never distinguished, had it, between what it retained and whom.

He thought about the girl. He had never seen her face. He knew her only as a set of observations—small bootprints beside larger ones, a child's mitten lost at the waystation ambush site, red wool, half-buried in snow. He had picked it up. It was in his coat pocket now, absurdly, evidence he had failed to log. He took it out and held it. It smelled of woodsmoke and fever-sweat and something else underneath, faint and floral, an herb he didn't know, and he thought: *she was sick, and someone is keeping her alive, and the person keeping her alive is a smuggler who has spent fifteen years selling lies to my Guild, and I am sitting here deciding whether to recommend that both of them stop existing.*

The pen moved.

*Recommendation:* he wrote, *that correction be delayed pending full recovery of the subjects' route intelligence. The Whitlock network extends further than current mapping suggests. Premature correction risks loss of actionable terrain data.*

It was true. Every word of it was true, and it was also the only lever he had—a bureaucratic delay dressed as diligence, buying days he told himself were for the investigation and knew, in the place where he kept the things he didn't say, were for a child in red mittens he had never met.

He signed it. Sealed it. Set it aside for the morning courier.

Then he sat for a long time in the lamplight, listening to the Guildhall settle around him—bells, footsteps, the scratch of pens in distant rooms, the great machine of record-keeping grinding on—and he took out the little red mitten again and turned it over in his hands.

Somewhere in the deep stacks below lay a registry with his name in it, in a column labeled *disposition*. Somewhere north lay a door the Guild had locked and then pretended was a wall. He had spent nineteen years believing he stood on the side of the ink. Tonight, for the first time, he allowed himself to wonder which side of the page he had actually been written on.

Outside, snow was beginning again over Thornfell.

By morning, he would request assignment to the correction team himself. Someone competent should go, he would write—someone who understood inquiry, who could recover the route intelligence before Vess's wardens made their conclusions permanent. It was a reasonable request. It would be granted.

And then he would walk back into those mountains, carrying a writ of erasure in one pocket and a child's mitten in the other, and he would find out, at whatever cost, which of them he truly was.
