## 11. Betrayal at the Waystation

The waystation at Crag's Hollow had not changed in fifteen years, and that was precisely what made Marguerite uneasy.

They had known Tam Voss since before they'd had a name worth hiding. He'd been a young ferry-hand then, all elbows and easy laughter, hauling contraband brandy up the switchbacks while Marguerite walked ahead with a stolen chart and a head full of other people's secrets. He was the one who'd taught them which stones sang hollow when the Guild's surveyors were near. He was the one who'd stood them their first drink the night they buried their grandmother, and he hadn't asked a single question about the fire they'd burned her papers in. That was the code. That was the whole of it: no questions, no names, no debts left standing.

Now Tam was fifty, grey to the roots, running a waystation that smelled of woodsmoke and wet wool and the particular sourness of beer kept too long in cold. He looked older than his years, the way mountain men did, weathered down to something essential. When Marguerite pushed through his door with Sable tucked against their side like a knife carried close to the body, he looked up from his ledger and went very still.

"Well," he said. "The ghost of Thornfell walks."

"Keep your voice down."

"I'm keeping nothing." But he did lower it, glancing past them to the empty common room, the banked fire, the stairs going up into shadow. "Word came down the wire two days ago. The Guild's asking after a mapmaker traveling with a child. Offering more for information than I make in five years." His eyes were steady on Marguerite's face. "I told them I hadn't seen you. Which was true, then."

"And now?"

"Now you're standing in my common room dripping snowmelt on my floor." He sighed, a long sound, and reached under the bar for two cups. "Sit. Eat. You look like something the river spat back."

Marguerite sat. It was a mistake and they knew it even as they did it—knew it the way you know a rope is frayed but use it anyway because the alternative is falling now instead of later. But Sable was shivering, and there was stew on the hearth, and fifteen years of friendship is a heavy thing to throw onto a scale against survival.

Tam ladled out bowls without being asked. He set bread beside them, dark rye, dense as slate. He didn't look at Sable directly, which Marguerite noted and appreciated; children on the run learned fast that being seen was being remembered, and being remembered was being found.

"She your daughter?" he asked anyway.

"No questions, Tam."

"Old habit." He smiled, and it was mostly the old smile, but there was something behind it, some small animal moving in the brush of his face. "You're heading for the Greywash, aren't you. Odile's ferry."

Marguerite said nothing. Saying nothing was an answer, and they both knew it.

"The pass above Hollowmere is closed—rockfall, three weeks back. You'll have to come around by the Shepherd's Stair. Longer, but the Guild won't have watchers there yet." He tore his own bread, chewed, swallowed. "I can give you provisions. Rope, dried meat, a flask of something medicinal. And a night's sleep in a real bed, which you'll be wanting before the Greywash, because that crossing will kill you if your legs are tired."

It sounded right. Every detail of it sounded right—the rockfall was plausible, the Shepherd's Stair was real, the offer was generous in exactly the way Tam had always been generous. Marguerite ate the stew and felt warmth return to their hands and hated themselves for how good it felt, how much like safety, how much like the thing they had spent fifteen years learning did not exist.

Sable slept upstairs in a narrow room under a sloped roof, curled with the brass compass held against her chest like a talisman. Marguerite sat below with Tam over the last of the medicinal flask and listened to him talk about the price of hops and the cruelty of the winter and the wife who'd left him four years ago for a cooper in the lowlands. Ordinary talk. Anchor talk. And underneath it, Marguerite thought afterward, the small animal in his face had never stopped moving.

They woke before dawn to a silence that was wrong.

Not quiet—Crag's Hollow was always quiet—but a specific absence of sound, the kind of hush that meant someone was listening hard. Marguerite lay still in the room next to Sable's and catalogued it: no cart coming up the valley road, though Tam had said a wool-trader was due today. No movement from the kitchen below, where Tam rose at this hour to bank the ovens. No wind. Just the building settling around them like a held breath.

Then, faintly, from the treeline: the creak of leather. Many someones adjusting many belts.

Marguerite was out of bed and through Sable's door before the thought finished forming. The child was already awake, eyes wide and black in the dark, compass clutched so tight its brass edge had printed itself into her palm.

"Back window," Marguerite breathed. "Don't speak. Don't run until I say."

The window gave onto the woodshed roof, and the woodshed roof gave onto a slope of scree that fell away toward the river trail. Below, in the blue-grey light, Marguerite saw them: six figures in the treeline, moving with the unhurried coordination of professionals, closing on the waystation like a hand closing. Not wardens in full kit—no, these were quieter, leaner, dressed as drovers and pilgrims. Guild field-men. Renne's kind.

And Tam's kitchen chimney was already smoking. He'd lit the ovens. He'd fed them, provisioned them, given them a warm room—and he'd lit the signal fire that told the watchers below the quarry was home.

* * *

They went out through the window with the care of people defusing something. Sable first, light as ash, dropping to the shed roof without a sound. Marguerite followed, pack already strapped, the songbook against their ribs where it had lived for two weeks now like a second heartbeat. The scree took them down in long sliding strides, each stone a potential announcement, and Marguerite counted the figures in the trees as they moved—six visible, which meant eight, which meant ten, because Guild field-teams ran heavy and hid their flanks.

They were halfway to the river trail when the shout went up behind them.

Not surprise—confirmation. A whistle, two notes, answered from downslope, and Marguerite's stomach dropped because that second answer came from *ahead*, from the bend where the trail met the water, and the net had never been behind them at all. It had been strung across the whole valley, and Tam's waystation was simply the knot at its center.

"Run," Marguerite said, and shoved Sable toward the rocks.

What followed was not a chase so much as a negotiation conducted at speed between Marguerite's body and the mountain. They knew this terrain the way Tam knew his ledgers—every fall, every shelf of stone, every place the slope broke steep enough to break a pursuer's line of sight. Fifteen years of selling routes to smugglers, and here at last was the dividend: Marguerite Whitlock, hunted on their own map.

But Sable was nine, and nine-year-old legs are short, and the Guild's field-men moved through the scree like water finding cracks.

The first bolt came from the treeline and took a splinter off the boulder Marguerite ducked behind. Crossbow, then—not arrows, bolts, weighted to drop a man at range. Marguerite put their shoulder to Sable's back and drove her forward, up a goat-track only they and the ibex knew existed, a route so steep it wasn't on any chart, not even theirs, because they'd never dared sell it.

"They're behind us—" Sable gasped.

"I know. Don't look. Climb."

The track crested a spine of granite and dropped the other side into a fold of the mountain where the pines grew thick and black. For one merciful moment the shouts faded, muffled by the trees, and Marguerite allowed themselves the thought: *we might*. Allowed it the way you allow yourself to look at the sun—one instant, no more.

Then the second team came out of the trees ahead of them, and the sun went away.

Three of them, fanning across the slope with the smooth economy of men who had done this in worse places. The lead one raised a hand—not a weapon, just a palm, almost courteous. Behind that courtesy, Marguerite could see the writ-tube slung at his hip, the iron collar of Guild authority, the whole apparatus of erasure wearing a drover's coat.

"Mapmaker!" he called. "Aldous Renne sends his regards. He only wants to talk!"

"He should have led with that," Marguerite muttered, and turned, and pushed Sable at the one gap left—the scree chute to the east, narrow, blind, ending nobody-knew-where because nobody had ever mapped it.

"You'll go down that," they told her, kneeling, gripping her shoulders, forcing her black eyes to meet theirs. "You'll go down fast and you won't stop and you won't wait for me. The compass knows the way. Let it pull you. Do you understand me? Let it *pull*."

Her chin trembled. "You're coming too."

"Yes." It was a lie delivered with total conviction, the finest piece of cartography Marguerite had ever produced. "But I'm slower, so you go first. Go *now*, Sable."

She went. She hesitated one heartbeat at the lip of the chute—long enough to carve herself permanently into Marguerite's memory, small and fierce and terrified, the last living speaker of a drowned country silhouetted against the sky—and then she dropped into the stone and was gone, the brass compass flashing once like a struck match.

The crossbowman on the ridge fired.

Marguerite saw it happen with the strange clarity of the doomed: the man bracing, the bolt leaping, the whole arc of it drawn against the pale morning sky. There was time to move. There was time to turn, to let it take them somewhere survivable, a shoulder, a thigh. But turning would show the shooter the chute. Turning would tell the Guild exactly where the child had gone.

So Marguerite stepped *into* the shot instead, arms wide, a scarecrow of a target planted on the ridgeline, and felt the bolt punch through the meat of their left side below the ribs with a sound like a door being kicked in.

The world went white, then red, then very simple. Down. Knees. Stone. Blood arriving hot and shocking between their fingers. Somewhere below, impossibly far, a child's footsteps rattling down through the scree, growing fainter, growing *away*, and Marguerite pressed both hands to the wound and thought with perfect clarity: *good. Good. Keep going.*

Boots crunched up the slope. Voices. Someone saying "alive, the Guild wants them alive," and someone else saying something about a stretcher, and Marguerite, folding slowly sideways behind a granite tooth with their life leaking out through their fingers, did the last thing their trade had taught them.

They erased themselves.

Blood-slick hands found the scree, dragged a curtain of loose stone down over their own body, a burial in miniature, a map with themselves inked out. The pain was enormous and impersonal, a mountain sitting on them, and beneath it Marguerite's mind kept moving with the cold diligence of a lifetime's habit: *bleeding means heat, heat means they'll find me by scent if not by sight, so move, move now, before the shock finishes the job.*

They moved. They crawled. The mountain tilted and swam and offered them its stones like a host offering bread, and somewhere in the white noise of it Marguerite registered that the voices above had split—some following the chute, some circling—and that the child was, for this hour, this mile, beyond their reach or anyone's.

*Let it pull you,* they had told her. *The compass knows the way.*

Marguerite laughed, and the laugh came out as blood on their teeth, and the last thing they saw before the dark came down was the morning sun striking the granite spine overhead, lighting it gold, like a line drawn on a chart.

Like a border.

Like the seam of a door.

* * *

Consciousness returned in pieces, the way ice breaks up in spring.

First: pain, vast and organized, headquartered below the ribs on the left. Second: cold, but a different cold—damp earth, pine needles, the smell of sap and rot. Third: weight. Something draped over them, wool, smelling of smoke and sheep.

Marguerite opened their eyes to a ceiling of branches.

Someone had built a shelter—a lean-to, crude but competent, cut boughs laid over a frame of stripped poles. Someone had packed their wound with moss and bound it tight enough to ache honestly. Someone had built a small fire at the mouth of the shelter, shielded by stones, burning low and clean.

And someone was sitting just outside the firelight, knees drawn up, a small silhouette against the deeper dark, holding a brass compass that caught the ember-glow and threw it back in a thin golden arc.

"Sable." The name came out as a rasp, barely a word.

The silhouette turned. For a moment the child didn't move, and Marguerite understood that she had been sitting like that for hours, maybe a day, keeping watch over a body she must have believed was done breathing. Then she was across the space and on her knees in the dirt, and her hands were on Marguerite's face, and she was speaking—Vessarine, rapid and broken, words tumbling over each other, grief and fury and relief all fused into one bright unbearable sound.

"I don't—" Marguerite coughed. "Slowly. Slowly, little one. I'm here."

"You *fell*." She switched to their shared handful of trade-words, voice cracking. "I heard it. I heard you stop. I came back up and you were under the rocks and there was so much blood, and I—" Her breath hitched. She pressed her forehead against Marguerite's, hard, the way children do when words fail. "I fixed it. I fixed you. I think. Mostly."

"You dragged me?"

"You're not heavy. You're mostly lies." A wet, hiccupping almost-laugh. "I carried you here. I built the fire. I did everything like you weren't there to do it, because you weren't."

Marguerite managed to lift a hand—an expedition, a campaign—and rest it on the back of her head. Her hair was full of pine needles and scree-dust. Underneath the grime she was shaking.

"You went down the chute," Marguerite said. "You didn't stop."

"I stopped."

"You were supposed to—"

"I *stopped*." Fierce now, tears cutting tracks through the dust on her face. "Your rules are bad rules. No attachments. No questions. Look what they got you." Her small hand hovered over the bandaged wound, not touching. "Tam fed us and sold us. Your friend. So I have decided: I am attached to you, and I am asking questions, and you are not allowed to die, because those are my rules now, and my rules are better."

Marguerite stared at her. Somewhere in the ruined landscape of their body, something cracked open that had been sealed for fifteen years—something that had started as loneliness and hardened into discipline and was now, in the firelight of a hole in a mountain, breaking apart into something warmer and far more dangerous.

"Your rules are terrible," Marguerite whispered. "They'll get us both killed."

"Probably." Sable wiped her nose on her sleeve with tremendous dignity. "The compass moved, though. While you were sleeping. It doesn't point north anymore. It points—" she turned it in her fingers, and the needle swung, and settled, insistent, toward the deep heart of the mountains, "—*inward*. Harder than before. Like it's impatient."

Marguerite looked at the needle. At the child. At the blood soaking through the moss-packed bandage, and the fire burning low, and the darkness beyond the shelter where Guild field-men were surely combing the slopes for a wounded mapmaker and a fugitive child.

Safety had always been a debt, Marguerite thought. Fifteen years of careful silence, false names, no attachments—and the bill had come due in a friend's common room, paid in crossbow bolts. There was no more buying time. There was only the choice of what to spend what remained on.

"Inward," Marguerite murmured, and let their eyes close again, and this time the dark that came down was almost gentle. "Good. Then we stop running from it."

Sable's hand found theirs in the firelight and held on.

"We rest tonight," Marguerite breathed. "Tomorrow—we follow it. All the way to the Door."
