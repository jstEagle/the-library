## 14. Renne Catches Up

The shrine found Aldous Renne before he found it.

He had been following the fugitives' trail for nine days—through the Greywash ferry crossing where a one-eyed woman swore she'd seen nothing while her hands shook with the lie, through the high passes where the snow still held the print of a child's boot beside the longer stride of an adult's—and on the tenth morning the trees simply ended, and there it was: a hollow in the mountainside hung with ribbons.

Hundreds of them. Thousands. Ribbons of silk and wool and strips of bark and braided grass, knotted to the branches of dead pines and to iron hooks driven into the rock itself, each one fluttering in the wind that came down off the peaks. The colors had faded into a family of greys and bone-whites, though here and there a scrap of older dye survived—a rust-red, a deep sea-green—that made the whole hillside look like a wound that had almost healed.

Renne stood at the treeline with his hand on his satchel strap and did not move for a long moment.

He knew what this was. Every warden of the Cartographers' Guild knew, because every warden was taught it in their first year as a warning: *the name-shrines are relics of pre-Treaty superstition. Peasants once believed that writing a name on cloth and hanging it in a holy place would keep the name alive after death. The practice is obsolete. The places are unrecorded.*

*Unrecorded.* The Guild's favorite word. It sounded so clean. So administrative.

He walked up through the ribbons. They brushed his shoulders as he passed, and he found himself flinching from them, though he could not have said why. Some of them were very old. Some were new—cut recently, tied recently, by hands that had passed this way within days.

At the center of the hollow, set into the rock face, was a shelf of black stone. On it lay offerings gone grey with weather: a child's shoe. A clay whistle. A lock of hair bound with wire. And names—names scratched into the stone in a dozen different scripts, some of them worn nearly smooth, some fresh enough to catch the light.

Renne read them. He told himself he was reading them *as evidence*, cataloguing script families for the Guild's linguistic division, and then he stopped telling himself anything at all, because halfway down the list he found a name he knew.

*Odile Marchenne, who kept the ferry at the Greywash, who carried three hundred souls across before the water took her, remember her.*

He had crossed at Odile's ferry. He had eaten at Odile's table. He had paid her Guild scrip and watched her count it with fingers missing two joints, and he had never once wondered where those joints had gone.

His breath came out white and slow. Above him, the ribbons moved without wind now, or seemed to.

"They say if you tie one," said a voice behind him, "you have to leave something too. A memory, usually. Something you're willing to forget."

Renne turned.

Marguerite Whitlock stood at the edge of the hollow with a crossbow held low and easy against their hip—not aimed, not yet, but present the way weather is present. Fifteen years Renne had bought routes from this smuggler, fifteen years of meticulous invoices and immaculate silence, and only now, seeing them standing among the ribbons with their hood back and their face bare, did he realize he had never actually looked at them before. They were thinner than he'd imagined. There was a bandage at their side, badly tied, seeping rust-brown at its edge. Their eyes were the color of the faded sea-green ribbon directly above their head.

Behind them, half-hidden in the shadow of the rock, stood the child.

She was small. That was the first thing. Small and thin and watchful, with a brass compass clutched to her chest the way other children clutched dolls, and her eyes went from Renne to Marguerite and back with the flat calculation of something wild deciding whether to bolt.

"Aldous Renne," Marguerite said. "Senior courier. Third mark. You've come a long way past your jurisdiction."

"The writ doesn't have a jurisdiction." Renne kept his voice level, courteous, the voice he used at border stations. "You're corrected from record, both of you. I'm the correction."

"Are you." Marguerite's thumb shifted on the crossbow stock. "Alone?"

"Yes."

"That's either honesty or arrogance."

"It's usually both." He let out a breath. "I tracked you alone because I wanted to talk before anyone else found you. That window is closing. Warden-Captain Vess is behind me with six wardens and a survey team. She moves slowly because she believes thoroughness is a virtue. She'll reach this valley in—" he glanced at the sky, gauging the light through the ribbons, "—two days. Perhaps less, if the weather holds."

Marguerite didn't lower the crossbow. But they didn't raise it either. "Why tell me that?"

"Because I need you to understand that I'm not lying to you. Not about any of it. You've spent fifteen years selling me truth, Whitlock. Let me return the courtesy once."

The wind moved through the shrine. Somewhere above them a ribbon snapped loose and went cartwheeling down the slope, and the child flinched at the sound and pressed closer to the rock.

"You're wounded," Renne said quietly. His eyes went to the bandage. "The waystation. I read the report. A friend sold you for amnesty—he got it, incidentally. Full pardon, resettlement stipend, a cottage in the lowlands." He paused. "I hope it was worth it to him. I hope he lies awake in it."

"What do you want, Renne?"

"I want to ask you a question." He took his hand off his satchel strap—slowly, deliberately, showing them the gesture meant nothing hostile—and reached instead into his coat, and drew out a single folded paper, creased soft with handling, and held it up between two fingers. "And I want to show you something first. Because I think you're the only person alive who might be able to read it."

Marguerite's eyes flicked to the paper. To his face. Back to the paper.

"If this is a trick—"

"It's not a trick. Tricks are for people who have something to gain. Look at me, Whitlock. What could I possibly gain?"

*Everything,* said the careful voice inside him, the Guild voice, the voice they'd raised him with. *You could gain your career. Your name in the rolls. A third-mark courier who brings in the smuggler Whitlock and recovers a pre-Treaty artifact and documents a living speaker of a forbidden tongue—that's a fourth mark, that's a seat in the Survey Hall, that's everything.*

He ignored the voice. He was getting good at ignoring it. He'd been ignoring it since the ferry, since Odile's missing finger-joints, since the discrepancy in the routes—an old road inked out of the charts that his own feet had walked on and known to be real.

"Slowly," Marguerite said. "Toss it. Don't step forward."

He tossed it. The paper turned over twice in the air and landed at Marguerite's boots. They crouched—wincing at the pull of the wound—took it up, and unfolded it.

For a long moment they said nothing. Then Renne saw their hands begin to shake.

"Where," Marguerite said, very softly, "did you get this."

"It was in my file." Renne heard his own voice go strange. "In my admission file, when I entered the Guild orphanage at age four. It's the only thing that came with me. The intake clerk wrote a note on the back: *found on the child, language unknown, retained for the archive.* It's been in the archive for thirty years. Nobody ever translated it. Nobody could." He swallowed. "Last week I pulled it. I don't know why. I told myself it was procedure. It wasn't procedure."

Marguerite read aloud, haltingly, in a language that made the air feel older—the syllables falling into the hollow of the shrine like stones into a well, and the child behind them made a small sound, a caught breath, and stepped out of the shadow.

"*Vessa-nur i thalen,*" Marguerite read. "*Do not grieve the drowned country. The drowned country grieves you.*"

The child spoke. Her voice was thin and cracked from cold and disuse, but the words came sure, the way words come when they've been waiting a long time: "*Thalen nur. Thalen vessa.*" Then, in the trade-tongue, in a whisper: "It's a mourning verse. Mothers sang it. When someone was taken."

Renne stood very still among the ribbons.

"My mother," he said, and had to stop, and start again. "My mother wrote that. Or carried it. She was taken—I assume. The Guild doesn't call it that. The intake record says *parentage unknown, presumed deceased.* Presumed." He laughed, and it came out wrong. "Thirty years I've been the Guild's instrument. Thirty years I've been correcting things from record. And the whole time, the one record that mattered—the one thing that was mine—they kept locked in a drawer, untranslated, because no one left alive could read it."

"Someone left alive can," Marguerite said.

"Yes." He met their eyes. "That's rather the point, isn't it."

* * *

They sat in the hollow of the shrine like conspirators, which Renne supposed they were now, all three of them, conspirators against the thing that had made them.

Marguerite kept the crossbow across their knees. He didn't blame them. The child sat close against Marguerite's side, and at some point—Renne never quite saw when it happened—she had produced a stub of charcoal from her pocket and was copying the mourning verse onto a strip of birchbark, her tongue between her teeth, her letters careful and round.

"She writes it better than I speak it," Marguerite said, watching her. "Fifteen years I kept the songbook under my floorboards and never learned more than the lullabies. She's been free of it for—" a glance at the child, a question asked in Vessarine, answered with a shrug, "—she thinks maybe two years. Since the burning. And she carries more of the language than I do. Children do that. Words go in deep when you're young. They grow around the bones."

"Like the Guild's doctrine grew around mine," Renne said.

"Is that what this is? Doctrine coming loose?"

"I don't know what this is." He looked up at the ribbons, hundreds of names moving against the grey sky. "I know what the writ says. *Corrected from record.* Do you know what the correction process actually is, Whitlock? Formally? I signed eleven of them last year. It's not execution, officially. Officially it's *administrative dissolution.* The person's records are purged, their trade-marks voided, their name struck from parish rolls and guild registries and tax ledgers until there's no legal person left to arrest. And then—" he spread his hands, "—well. People who don't exist can't complain about what happens to them."

"And you signed eleven."

"I signed eleven." His jaw tightened. "One of them was a fence in Corrow's Bend who shorted the Guild on a map commission. A *map commission.* A man died administratively over forty crowns of underpayment, and I drafted the paperwork, and I slept fine, because the paperwork was elegant." He shook his head. "Elegant. That's the word we used. We talked about erasure the way poets talk about verse."

The child looked up from her birchbark. She said something to Marguerite—short, direct—and Marguerite answered, and whatever passed between them made the girl frown and study Renne with new attention, the way one studies a large animal whose intentions are unclear.

"She wants to know," Marguerite translated, "if you're going to erase us."

"No."

"She wants to know why she should believe you."

Renne considered the question seriously, because the child had asked it seriously, and children deserved serious answers even when the honest answer was ugly.

"You shouldn't," he said. "Not fully. Not yet. Belief is earned and I've given you no reason. All I can offer is this: I could have brought Vess and her wardens straight here. Instead I came alone, ahead of them, and burned two days doing it. Two days is what I have to give you. Spend them well."

Marguerite was quiet for a long time. The wind turned the ribbons. Somewhere far below, a hawk rode the valley's updraft in slow circles, patient as arithmetic.

"There's a door," Marguerite said at last.

Renne went very still. "Whitlock—"

"Not where. Not how. Just that there is one, and it needs a living speaker of Vessarine to open it, and when it opens, everything the Guild buried comes back up. Every folded valley. Every erased name. Every family they scattered." Marguerite's eyes were steady and hard. "I'm telling you because you'll find out anyway, and because when it happens, I want you to remember this shrine. Remember the ribbons. Remember that every one of these names was a correction, once. Somebody's paperwork. Somebody's elegant verse."

"The Door." Renne said the word carefully. "The old surveys mention a seam in the Thornfell massif. Pre-Treaty. The founding charters call it *the Hollow Door* and order it sealed and struck from all maps. I always assumed it was allegory. Founding charters usually are."

"It isn't."

"And if it opens—if Vessar comes back—what happens to the Guild?" He heard the answer forming in his own mouth before Marguerite gave it. "Nothing good. An institution built on a lie doesn't survive the lie being corrected. They'll send everything. Wardens, survey teams, the Fourth Company. They'll call it a rebellion of ghosts."

"Then you understand your position."

"I understand it perfectly." He smiled, and it was a tired, crooked thing. "I've been the Guild's since I was four years old. It's the only parent I've ever had. And it turns out my parent has been keeping my mother's last words in a drawer for thirty years, untranslated, unloved, filed under *language unknown.*" He looked at his hands. "You spend your whole life thinking you're an orphan. And then you find out you're worse. You're stolen goods."

The child moved. It was so sudden that Marguerite's hand went to the crossbow and Renne's went to his knife, and then both of them froze, because the girl had simply crossed the space between them and was standing in front of Renne, looking up at him with those flat, assessing eyes. She held out the strip of birchbark.

On it, in careful round letters, the mourning verse. And beneath it, in a second hand—shakier, newer, learned perhaps from the same songbook—three more words in Vessarine that Renne did not know.

"What does it say?" he asked.

Marguerite's voice, when it came, was rough. "It says *your mother's country remembers you.*"

Renne took the birchbark. His hands were not quite steady. He read the words he couldn't read, and understood them anyway, the way you understand fire without knowing its chemistry, and he folded the bark with great care and put it inside his coat, over his heart, where the writ used to sit.

"Two days," he said. "Maybe less. Vess is thorough. She'll bring survey instruments and a full correction detail, and she will not negotiate, and she will not be charmed, and if she finds me talking to you she'll correct me too, and she'll enjoy the elegance of it." He stood. Brushed the pine needles from his coat. Became, visibly, again, the senior courier, third mark, the Guild's instrument—though something in the set of his shoulders had permanently changed, the way a door changes once it has been opened. "Go north. The high paths. She'll expect you to run south, toward the lowlands and the borders. Go toward the Door instead. Whatever it costs."

"And you?" Marguerite rose stiffly, the wound dragging at them. "What will you tell them?"

"I'll tell them I found your trail at the shrine and lost it in the scree fields to the west. It's plausible. Scree eats tracks." He paused at the edge of the hollow, where the first ribbons began, and put out one hand, and touched a strip of faded silk—gently, the way you touch something sleeping. "There's a custom here, I'm told. You leave a memory you're willing to forget."

"Is that what you're doing?"

"I'm leaving the version of myself that believed the paperwork." He turned back, and for a moment the courier's mask slipped entirely, and what looked out from underneath was a four-year-old child on a Guild doorstep holding a paper no one could read. "One more thing. When you open that door—when your people come home—there will be others like me. Children the Guild took, or made, or filed away. Speakers scattered so thin they don't know what they're speaking. Remember us. However this ends for me—remember that we existed."

"We existed," the child said suddenly—in Vessarine, in clear bright syllables that rang against the rock like a struck bell—and then, in the trade-tongue, staring at him with fierce dark eyes: "We remember."

Renne closed his eyes for exactly one breath. Then he nodded, once, and walked down through the ribbons, and did not look back, because if he looked back he would not leave, and if he did not leave, Vess would find three people at the shrine instead of none, and everything would end here in this beautiful forgotten hollow, elegantly, administratively, corrected.

* * *

He made the treeline before he allowed himself to stop.

Behind him the shrine held its thousand names against the sky. Ahead, the trail bent west toward the scree fields, where he would spend the afternoon manufacturing evidence of a chase that had never happened—broken branches, a discarded ration wrapper, boot-prints in meltwater—before turning back to report his failure to Warden-Captain Vess with a straight face and a meticulously detailed account.

She would not believe him. He suspected she already didn't. Vess was thorough, and thoroughness eventually arrives at the truth of everyone, including her third-mark courier with his impeccable files and his newly restless nights. When it did, he would be corrected, elegantly, and some future clerk would write *parentage unknown, presumed deceased* on his intake record, and the drawer would close.

He found he could live with that. He found, in fact, that he had been living with it for thirty years; he had simply called it something else.

At the treeline he stopped once more and touched his coat, over his heart, where the birchbark sat folded. *Your mother's country remembers you.* Somewhere beyond these mountains, behind a door the Guild had ordered struck from every map, there was a country that might hold a record of a woman who sang a mourning verse to a child she was about to lose. If the Door opened, he might—no. He put the thought down carefully, the way you put down something breakable. One thing at a time. First the false trail. First the lie. First the two days.

Then he squared his shoulders, became entirely the Guild's instrument again, and walked west into the scree, whistling—very softly, under his breath, where no one could hear—a tune he did not know he knew.

Behind him, high in the hollow, the ribbons went on moving in the wind, carrying their cargo of names north and east and out over the mountains, toward the Door, toward whatever waited on the other side of the world's forgetting.

And two days, Renne knew, was not nearly enough.
