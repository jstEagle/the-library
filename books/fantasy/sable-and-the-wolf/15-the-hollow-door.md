## 15. The Hollow Door

The mountain stopped pretending to be a mountain on the third morning.

Marguerite had walked the Thornfells for fifteen years. They knew its moods the way a sailor knows weather—where the scree slid, where the snow bridges lied, which passes forgave a mistake and which collected it in blood. But this was not a pass. This was not anything on any chart they had ever drawn or sold or burned.

The trail simply ended at a wall of grey stone that rose out of the snowfield without warning, sheer and seamless, as if the mountain had been poured rather than built. No scree at its base. No lichen. Not even wind-scour. The rock was smooth in a way stone should never be, smooth the way glass is smooth after fire, and when Marguerite laid their bare palm against it, the cold that came through was not the cold of winter but something older and more patient—the cold of a held breath.

"Here," Sable said.

She said it in Vessarine first—*hena*—and then, because she had been teaching them all winter, she said it again in Common, carefully, like setting down something fragile. *Here.* Her breath made a small white ghost against the wall. She was nine years old and she had walked them across half a country of folded valleys on fevered faith and a brass compass that pointed inward, always inward, and now she stood before a blank wall with her chin lifted and her mittened fists at her sides, certain as scripture.

Marguerite did not feel certain. Marguerite felt the particular dread of a person standing at the edge of their own life's last unmapped territory—and knowing, with terrible clarity, that whatever happened next would be the end of every map they had ever been.

* * *

They had come up through the pass in the hour before dawn, when the light was still the color of pewter and the snow squeaked underfoot like new leather. Sable had led. That was the arrangement now; neither of them pretended otherwise. Marguerite's wound had closed to an angry red seam along their ribs, and the fever had broken three days past, but the weakness stayed in their legs like a debt, and so the child walked ahead with the compass cupped in both hands while Marguerite followed, one hand on the ice-axe, one eye always turned back down the trail toward the treeline where Renne had bought them hours he might not survive to spend.

The compass needle had been trembling for two days. Not wavering—trembling, the way a tuning fork trembles when its note is sung somewhere nearby. And this morning, as the light came up over the shoulder of the massif, it had gone still. Utterly still. Pointing not north, not inward anymore, but straight into the face of the wall, quivering with what Marguerite could only describe as eagerness.

"It knows," Sable whispered. "It's been waiting longer than we have."

"Sable." Marguerite crouched beside her, breath ragged from the altitude, and took her shoulders. Frost had gotten into her lashes. There was a crack on her lower lip she kept worrying with her tongue. "Before we do anything. Before you touch that wall. I need you to hear me."

She looked up. In the flat early light her eyes were the grey-green of the Greywash in thaw, and behind them lived everything she had carried since the night of the burning: the library going up in sheets of flame, her mother's voice rising over the roar, the long cold walk south alone. Fifteen years Marguerite had kept silence to survive. This child had crossed a kingdom's grave alone at seven. They were not the same kind of survivor, and Marguerite knew it, and it shamed them in a way that felt almost holy.

"When this opens—if it opens—" Marguerite began, and found the words snagging like fishhooks in their throat. "There are things I have to tell you. About what it costs."

"You told me. On the ferry. And in the cave. And again when my fever was bad and you thought I was asleep." Sable's voice was gentle in the way children are gentle when they have learned gentleness from grief. "You say it costs your name."

"It costs more than a name." Marguerite looked at the wall. Up close, in the strengthening light, they could see what they had refused to see from farther off: that the smoothness was not emptiness. It was *writing*. Faint as watermark, deep as grain, characters flowed across the whole face of the stone in lines that curved and braided and returned upon themselves—a script older than any alphabet Marguerite had ever sold routes around, and yet not wholly strange. Their grandmother's songbook had letters like these in its margins. Letters Marguerite had spent thirty years pretending were decorative.

"The Guild doesn't just erase places," they said quietly. "You understand that by now. They erase *people*. Anyone who stands where the record says nothing should stand. Anyone who remembers what the record says never happened." They touched their own chest, over the wound, over the heart beneath it. "That Door is keyed to a living speaker. Your people built it that way so no warden could ever open it, no matter how many maps they burned. Which means when it opens, it will open *because of me*. My voice. My blood. And the moment I speak the verse, I stop being a person the world has records of. I become a rumor. A gap. Something the Guild can correct without anyone ever asking where I went."

Sable was quiet for a long moment. Snow ticked softly down from some high cornice. Somewhere below, impossibly far, a bird cried once and was silent.

"My mother used to say there are two kinds of names," she said at last. "The kind other people write about you. And the kind you answer to." She reached up and put her small mittened hand over Marguerite's where it rested on her shoulder. "The second kind is the real one. She said the first kind is just... a coat. You can take it off. It doesn't mean you're naked."

Marguerite laughed—a rough, cracked sound, startled out of them—and had to turn away and press the back of their wrist to their eyes for a moment, because fifteen years of discipline is a long time to hold a door shut against weeping, and this child kept walking up to it and knocking.

* * *

They made camp in the lee of the wall itself, which sheltered them from the wind as if the mountain had cupped its hands around them. Marguerite got the small stove hissing, melted snow, forced broth into Sable and then into themselves because the body, whatever the spirit decides, must be fed. Then they sat with their back against stone that hummed faintly, like a struck bell long after hearing, and took out the songbook.

It had come a long way from beneath the floorboards. Water-stained at one corner now, from the Greywash crossing. Smelling of woodsmoke and wool and Odile's ferry-house. The leather cover was soft as skin from handling, though Marguerite had handled it rarely enough until this winter—rarely enough that the guilt of that neglect still sat in them like a swallowed stone.

They opened it to the last page. The unlocking verse. Their grandmother had written it out whole, in that looping Vessarine hand, and beneath it, in cramped Common, she had added a single line of translation that Marguerite had read a hundred times as a child and understood only now:

*I will not write what it does to the singer. Let her find out, if she must.*

"Grandmother," Marguerite murmured, "you could have warned me properly."

But she had warned them properly. Fifteen years of rules. No real name. No attachments. No questions. Marguerite had thought those rules were cowardice inherited, silence bred into bone. Now, sitting at the foot of a Door their grandmother had surely known about—had perhaps stood before, had perhaps been offered and refused—they finally read the rules correctly. Not cowardice. *Armor*. Their grandmother had looked at this exact wall, had heard this exact cost, and had chosen the coat of a false name over the nakedness of a true one, and had built a life of careful invisibility so that her grandchild would never have to stand here and choose at all.

And Marguerite had spent those same fifteen years selling maps to the very Guild that locked this place away.

The fury from the chart-room—that cold, clean anger at the inked-out valleys—rose in them again, but it had changed temperature on the long road. It was warm now. It had a direction. It pointed, like the compass, inward.

"I'm going to sing it," they said. Not to Sable, exactly. To the wall. To the grandmother. To the fifteen-year-old who had buried a songbook under floorboards and called the burial wisdom. "Tonight or tomorrow. When the light's right. When we're ready."

"We're ready now," Sable said, with the terrifying simplicity of children.

"We're ready when I say we're ready." But there was no iron in it, and Sable smiled the smallest smile and let them keep the fiction, the way you let an old dog win a tug-of-war it has already lost.

* * *

Night came down blue and enormous. The clouds tore open and the stars came out in the brutal multitudes that only exist above the treeline, and Marguerite lay awake beside the banked stove listening to the mountain hum.

It was not silent, that hum. Once you noticed it, you could not unnotice it. It had layers. Underneath the low bell-tone of the stone itself there was something rhythmic, something almost like breathing, almost like singing—hundreds of voices, maybe thousands, so far away and so deeply folded into the rock that they arrived as texture rather than sound. Marguerite pressed their palm flat against the wall and closed their eyes and let it move through them, and for one vertiginous moment they were certain they could almost make out words. A word like *home*. A word like *wait*. A word like—

"—like *come*, isn't it?"

Sable was sitting up in her blankets, eyes wide and shining in the starlight, her hand also against the stone.

"You hear it too," Marguerite said. It wasn't a question.

"Since yesterday." The child's voice shook slightly. "I thought it was the fever coming back. But it's them, isn't it. It's the ones who went in. The ones who've been waiting." Her fingers curled against the rock. "Fifteen years, you said. How long for them?"

Marguerite did the arithmetic they had been refusing to do since the chart-room. The treaty was old. The erasure was older. The valleys had been folded away before either of their grandmothers drew breath.

"Longer than any living memory," they said. "Generations. Maybe a century. They've been standing on the inside of a locked door for a hundred years, Sable, singing to a wall."

"And we're the key."

"One of us is." Marguerite sat up. The cold bit through their coat and they barely felt it. "The lock wants a speaker. A living voice, in the old tongue, singing the verse my grandmother carried out of the fire. It doesn't want me, specifically. It wants what I know." They paused. "Which means there's a version of tonight where I teach you the verse, and you sing it, and you go home, and I walk back down the mountain with my name intact and my trade ruined and my heart in pieces, but alive. Recorded. Protected, such as protection ever meant anything."

Sable stared at them. "You'd stay outside?"

"It's the sensible version."

"You hate sensible."

"I *invented* sensible," Marguerite said, "and I am telling you it is available as an option," and Sable laughed, a bright shocked bark of laughter that rang off the humming wall and seemed, absurdly, to make the hum deepen, as if the mountain had laughed too.

Then the child's laughter faded, and she looked at Marguerite with an expression far too old for her face, and said: "If I sing it, the Door opens for *me*. But the wardens will still come. And everyone who walks through after me—my mother's people, the ones waiting—they'll walk through a door that opened on a nine-year-old's voice, and the Guild will erase whoever opened it anyway. So it doesn't save you. It just makes me the one who pays." She shook her head slowly. "My mother didn't burn so I could learn to spend other people."

Marguerite looked at her for a long time.

Somewhere in the dark below them, far down at the treeline, a light flickered. One point of orange, small as a spark off a grindstone. Then a second. Then a third, spaced with the deliberate discipline of a camp being set by professionals.

Renne had bought them hours. The hours were spent.

* * *

They broke camp without firelight, moving by star-glare and long practice, gathering their gear with hands that did not shake because fifteen years of smuggling had trained the shaking out of them—or so Marguerite had always believed. Their hands were shaking now.

"There's nowhere left to run," Sable said. It wasn't fear in her voice. It was inventory. She had learned hard arithmetic on the smugglers' roads, and she was doing it now, aloud, because no one had ever taught her that children weren't supposed to. "Behind us is the Guild. Around us is the wall. The only direction left is through."

"Yes."

"And you already decided. Back at the waystation, when you were bleeding. Maybe before that. Maybe the night you answered my lullaby." She stepped close. "Haven't you?"

The truth was that Marguerite had decided in a dozen small moments and had repudiated the decision in a dozen others, and the two columns of the ledger balanced differently depending on how afraid they were, and right now, watching lights multiply at the treeline, they were afraid down to the marrow.

But the wall was humming. And under the hum, that word. *Come.*

"I decided," Marguerite said, "that I am done being a map that lies."

They knelt at the base of the seam—which was visible now, in the starlight, if you knew to look: a hairline fracture running floor to crown of the wall, thin as a scratch on varnish, older than any treaty, older than the Guild, older, perhaps, than the idea of owning land at all. They unwrapped the songbook from its oilcloth. The pages caught the starlight and gave it back pale as milk.

The verse was there. Their grandmother's hand, steady and looping, carrying a country's front door key out of a burning library in the only luggage that couldn't be confiscated.

Marguerite looked at Sable. Sable looked back, and nodded once, solemn as a coronation.

Down at the treeline, the lights had begun to move.

Marguerite filled their lungs with air so cold it burned, laid their palm against the seam, and opened their mouth to sing.
