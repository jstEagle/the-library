## 18. Opening the Seam

The Door did not look like a door.

That was the first thing Marguerite understood, standing before it with the wind tearing at their coat and Sable's small hand buried in theirs. Fifteen years of mapping the Thornfells had taught them that the mountains kept their secrets in plain sight—a scree slope that was really a stair, a waterfall that hid a smuggler's tunnel—but this was different. This was a seam in the living rock, a hairline fracture running from the snowline down into shadow, so narrow that a knife blade would have struggled to enter it. If you looked at it straight on, your eye slid away. It was like trying to look at the space between two words.

Only when Sable spoke did it hold still.

*Vessa-dor,* she had said, an hour ago, at the mouth of the shrine valley where the trees gave up and the stone began. *Vessa-dor.* And the seam had stopped shimmering and become simply *there*, patient as a held breath, waiting for them the way a dog waits at a gate for someone it has decided to love.

Marguerite's wound pulled at every step. The ambush at the waystation was four days behind them now, but the bolt had gone deep under the ribs, and the fever had only broken that morning, thanks to Odile's bitter willow-bark and Sable's stubborn refusal to let them sleep past dawn. They could feel each heartbeat as a dull knock against the bandages. *You are still alive,* it said. *You are still alive. Spend it well.*

Behind them, far below at the treeline, the world was ending in slow motion. They had seen the wardens' lanterns strung out along the Greywash road like a rosary of small cold fires—thirty of them, maybe forty—and they had heard Renne's voice carrying up through the passes, smooth and unhurried, buying them hours with courtesy the way he always had. Hours. Not days. The arithmetic was simple and terrible: sing now, or never.

"Marguerite." Sable tugged their hand. Her breath made small ghosts in the air. "Are you frightened?"

"Yes," Marguerite said, because lying to her had become impossible somewhere on the ferry across the Greywash, and because the truth, spoken plainly, felt almost like prayer. "But I have been frightened for fifteen years, little one. This is the first time I've been frightened *toward* something."

Sable considered this with the grave attention she gave everything, her dark eyes catching the last grey light. Then she reached into her coat—the coat Marguerite had cut down from their own spare, sleeves rolled three times—and drew out the brass compass. Its needle no longer spun or pointed inward. It lay flat, quivering, aimed at the seam like a finger.

"It's ready," she said. "It's been ready since before I was born. Maybe since before *you* were born." She said it in Vessarine first, then again in the common tongue, the way she did now with anything that mattered, translating the world for them word by word, gift by gift.

Marguerite knelt. The snow soaked through their trouser leg and they found they didn't care. From inside their coat they took the songbook—not the false ledger they'd carried for fifteen years, but the true one, the one that had lived beneath the floorboards through all those winters of careful silence. Its cover was cracked leather gone soft as cloth. Their grandmother's handwriting filled its pages in brown ink gone amber: verses, recipes, curses, lullabies. On the final page, the unlocking verse, written out whole—but the last lines smudged, water-damaged decades ago, illegible.

Except that Sable had given them back those lines. Three nights ago, around the cave fire, teaching them word by word, she had sung something and watched Marguerite's face change, and Marguerite had opened the book with shaking hands and found the smudged verse completed in their own memory, note for note. Their grandmother had sung it to them as a child, all of it, every line—they had simply never known what they were holding. A lullaby was a key. Silence had been the lock, and they had turned it themselves, night after night, year after year, keeping a country shut.

"I can't do this alone," Marguerite said. "I need your voice with mine."

"You won't be alone." Sable said it simply, the way children say enormous things. "You haven't been alone since the snow."

* * *

They stood together before the seam, and Marguerite opened the book, and the wind dropped.

Not gradually—it dropped all at once, the way a room goes quiet when someone begins to speak of the dead. Snow hung suspended in the air around them, thousands of small white points, and the light changed, going gold at the edges though the sun had set behind the peaks an hour past. Marguerite's heart knocked against the bandage. The mountain itself was listening.

They began with the first verse, the one their grandmother had sung most often, the one about the river that remembers its source:

*O Vessa-lume, o Vessa-lume,*
*the water does not forget the sea;*
*though men have burned the maps of home,*
*the home burns brighter in me.*

Their voice cracked on the second line. Fifteen years of disuse had left their Vessarine thin as winter ice, and they heard every flaw in it—the flattened vowels, the swallowed consonants, the accent of exile layered over the accent of the borderlands over the accent of nowhere at all. But Sable's voice rose beneath theirs like a current under a boat, steady and sure, carrying them, correcting nothing, only holding them up. The child sang the way she did everything: completely, without reserve, as if singing were simply another way of being alive.

And the seam began to breathe.

That was the only word for it. The hairline crack widened and narrowed, widened and narrowed, in time with the verse, like a throat, like a lung, like something long asleep remembering how to draw air. Light seeped from within it—not torchlight, not daylight, but a color Marguerite had no name for in any language, warm as lamplight through honey, old as the inside of the earth.

Second verse. The grandmother's voice came up through Marguerite's own now, unmistakably, the particular rise and fall of it, and Marguerite's eyes blurred. *She taught me this on her knee in a kitchen that smelled of cardamom and woodsmoke, and I thought it was only a song, I thought it was only a song.* The shame of it rose hot in their throat even now—even here, even at the threshold. All those years of silence, all those years of selling other people's secret roads while their own inheritance gathered dust under the floorboards. All those years of believing invisibility was safety, when it had only ever been surrender wearing discipline's coat.

*I'm sorry,* they sang, weaving the apology into the verse the way Sable had taught them grief could be woven into anything. *I'm sorry. I'm sorry. Open anyway.*

Third verse. Fourth. The smudged lines approached, the ones that had been lost and found, and Marguerite closed their eyes and let their grandmother's ghost take their voice entirely:

*And if the door has forgotten you,*
*sing until it remembers;*
*for stone is only patient water,*
*and patience burns like embers.*

The seam opened.

There was no thunder. That was the strangest part—no thunder, no earthquake, none of the violence the Guild's histories would surely have recorded if they ever recorded it. The rock simply parted along its ancient fracture with a sound like a long-held sigh finally released, like ten thousand voices exhaling at once, and the light poured out and up and over them, and the suspended snowflakes caught fire with it, and Marguerite Whitlock stood at the edge of a country that did not exist and looked down into it.

* * *

Terraces.

That was the first word that arrived, and it arrived in Vessarine: *serrath*. Terraces cut into the mountainside in sweeping curves, descending into a valley that could not possibly fit inside the Thornfells and yet did—folded, pressed, held the way a flower is held between pages, alive despite everything. The terraces were green. In the dead heart of winter, under a sky stitched with early stars, they were green, silvered with irrigation channels that caught the strange golden light and ran with it like veins of glass. Glass terraces—Sable had spoken of them around the cave fire, and Marguerite had imagined, and imagining had not been enough, not nearly. The channels were actual glass, fused sand, centuries old, glowing faintly from within.

Below the terraces, a river. Wide and slow and dark, spanned by bridges of pale stone whose arches repeated and repeated into the distance, each one carved with figures Marguerite could not resolve at this distance—people, animals, perhaps letters. The river smelled of mineral cold and, impossibly, of woodsmoke.

And lights. Windows. Dozens of windows scattered up the valley walls, warm yellow squares in cliff-hung houses built of the same pale stone as the bridges, and figures moving among the terraces—small at this distance, but moving, walking, working, *living*—and one of them, nearest, a woman bent over a terrace bed with a basket, straightened slowly and turned toward the Door, and even across the impossible distance Marguerite saw her go still.

"They waited," Sable whispered. She was gripping Marguerite's sleeve with both hands, and tears were running freely down her face, freezing at her jaw. "They *waited*. Mama said they would wait. She said—" Her voice broke. She tried again in Vessarine, and the words came out whole: *"She said the Door knows its own. She said sing, and someone will answer."*

From the valley below, faint and wavering with distance, a sound rose. At first Marguerite thought it was wind. Then it resolved, and their knees nearly went.

Voices. Many voices, joining one another across the terraces, across the water, answering the lullaby. The same melody, the same words, rising from the green valley in the folded dark—*o Vessa-lume, o Vessa-lume*—sung by people who had kept it alive for a generation behind a locked door, who had sung it to their children born in captivity of a kind no treaty had ever named, who had never once stopped believing that someday the verse would come from the other side and someone would be there to hear it answered.

Marguerite wept. They were not proud of it and they did not care. They knelt in the snow at the threshold of a country erased from every map they had ever drawn or sold, and they wept like a child, like their grandmother, like all the silent ones in all the silent houses, while the song rose to meet them and the light of the glass terraces washed over their face like a tide coming home.

The woman with the basket was running now. Others were running too, spilling from the cliff houses, gathering on the nearest bridge, and their voices were getting louder, and some of them were calling out—not the song anymore but words, names, questions, joy in every register a voice could hold.

"Mama," Sable breathed. "Mama might be—she might be *here*—"

And then she was moving, tearing toward the opening, and Marguerite caught her arm—not to stop her, never to stop her, but to hold on for one more moment, one breath, because there was something they had to say and there might never be another chance to say it.

"Sable. Look at me."

The child turned. Her face was wet and blazing and younger and older than her years all at once.

"Whatever happens next," Marguerite said, "whatever comes through that door after us—you remember that you opened it. Not me. You walked out of a fire with a compass and a lullaby and you crossed a country that wanted you dead, and you *opened it*. Your mother gave you the key, but you turned it. Never let anyone—not the Guild, not the historians, not even these good people—tell you otherwise."

Sable stared at them. Then she did something she had never done: she threw both arms around Marguerite's neck and held on, fierce and desperate, her face pressed into their shoulder, and said something in Vessarine so quiet that Marguerite almost missed it, and would spend the rest of their life turning it over like a stone in the pocket:

*"You're coming too. You promised. Names can be lost, but you're mine, and I don't give back what's mine."*

Down the valley, the singing swelled. Lanterns were being lit along the bridge, one after another, a chain of small fires reaching toward the Door. The woman with the basket had reached the foot of the path and was climbing, calling a name that made Sable shudder head to toe—

And behind them, far below, a horn sounded. Once. Twice. The Guild's signal, flat and cold as a blade laid on a table, cutting through the lullaby like ink through water.

Renne's hours had run out.

Marguerite rose, wound screaming, songbook clutched to their chest, and took Sable's hand, and together they turned toward the open seam and the green country waiting beyond it—while down the mountain, through the folding valleys, the wardens began to climb.
