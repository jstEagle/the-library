## 1. The Date Written in Water

The night market of Vellamare opened the way the tide did—gradually, then all at once, a slow silvering of lanterns along the canal-streets until the whole district seemed to float on its own light. Zephyrine Villalobos had worked the Saltglass Market for seven years, and in all that time he had learned one lesson above every other: never let your hands look like anything but what they were. A craftsman's hands. A carver's hands. Nothing more.

He laid out his wares on the striped cloth with the care of a man setting a table for company he could not see. Little charms of whalebone and driftbone, each no larger than a thumb: a fishhook carved with running waves, for luck on the water; a small door with no house around it, for luck in love; a ladder that went nowhere, for luck in grief, because sometimes the only way out of sorrow was up and over. He priced them fairly. He smiled at the right moments. He kept his voice level and his eyes down and his gift folded up inside him like a moth with its wings pinned shut.

Because Zephyrine Villalobos heard the dead. Had heard them since he was six years old, since the fever that nearly took him left something behind instead of taking everything. And in Vellamare, hearing the dead was not a gift. It was a crime. The Ministry of Unmaking had made certain of that for a hundred years, and the gallows-dust on the wind from Unmaking Square was proof enough for anyone who doubted it.

So he sold charms. Lawful ones. Bone was permitted—bone was *traditional*, the Ministry's own edicts said, so long as nothing in it *spoke*. And his bones never spoke. Not aloud. Not to anyone living.

"Zephy." A woman at the front of his stall, salt-crusted boots, a netting needle tucked behind her ear like a flower. "My mother's been gone three months now, and I can't sleep. Have you anything for that?"

He did not have to reach for the answer. It came to him unbidden, soft as silt settling: *She wants to know if her mother forgave her.* The dead woman stood just behind her daughter's shoulder, translucent as breath on glass, wringing her hands in the old way, the way she must have done in life whenever she'd wanted to speak and hadn't.

Zephyrine picked up a charm shaped like a closed eye and pressed it into the netter's palm. "For sleep," he said. "And when you can't sleep—talk to her anyway. Out loud. They say the dead hear us better than we ever heard them."

It was a lie built on a truth, which was the only kind of lie he allowed himself. The woman's face crumpled with gratitude. She paid too much. He gave back the difference. Behind her, the ghost smiled at him—the first real smile he'd seen in days—and faded into the crowd like foam pulled back under a wave.

*Seven years,* he thought, arranging the coins. *Seven years of this, and they still haven't found me.*

He should have known better than to think it. In Vellamare, thinking you were safe was the same as ringing a bell.

* * *

The market thickened toward midnight. Eel-sellers shouted their prices over the smell of char and brine. Acrobats walked the rigging of a beached fishing ketch, and children threw candied kelp at them for luck. Lantern-light turned the canal water to hammered gold, and under the gold, if you knew how to look—and Zephyrine always knew—you could see the drowned city beneath Vellamare's foundations, the older streets, the sunken arches, the realm the maps called Salt and the living called nothing at all, because naming it invited it.

That was when he heard the voice.

It came up through the flagstones, through the canal water, through the soles of his boots and the bones of his feet, the way drowned voices always came—not sound at all, really, but vibration, a resonance in the marrow. Most nights the dead murmured. They gossiped. They grieved. They repeated the last things they'd ever said, over and over, like birds striking a window. He had learned to let it wash past him, white noise with faces.

But this voice was not murmuring. This voice was *reciting*.

*"On the hundredth year, when the lanterns burn gold on the water—"*

Zephyrine's hands went still over his charms.

*"—the Carver's blood will stand in the market where the covenant was signed, and the tide will come for its own."*

He looked up sharply, scanning the crowd. No one near him was speaking. A fishwife argued eel-prices two stalls down. Lovers quarreled sweetly over roasted chestnuts. Nobody's mouth moved in time with the words, because the words weren't coming from any mouth. They were coming from below—from the water itself, from somewhere deep in the drowned quarter, rising through stone like cold through a floor.

*"Zephyrine Villalobos,"* the voice said, and it said his name the way you'd set a key into a lock, slowly, testing each tooth. *"Son of Marisol. Son of Edouard. Blood of the First Carver. Tonight is the night written in water."*

His stomach dropped as though the flagstones beneath him had become sea.

There was no prophecy about him. There couldn't be. He was a charm-seller. He was a careful man. He had spent seven years being so carefully nothing that even he sometimes woke in the morning unsure there was anything underneath. Prophecies were for kings and monsters and people in stories, not for men who sold bone ladders to grieving net-menders.

*Coincidence,* he told himself, and began packing his stall with hands that would not quite steady. *Drowned folk babble. They grab names out of the air. Someone said my name tonight—a customer, a neighbor—and some dead thing caught it and made a song of it.*

But the voice had known his mother's name. His father's. Names he had not spoken aloud in seven years, names buried deeper than the drowned city itself.

*"Tonight,"* the voice said again, gentler now, almost tender, which was somehow worse. *"Tonight, tonight, tonight."*

He blew out his lantern.

* * *

He was three stalls gone, hood up, moving with the practiced aimlessness of a man with nowhere particular to be, when the sky changed.

Not the weather. The *light*. Down the length of the Grand Canal, the gold lantern-glow went abruptly white—harsh, official, the color of magnesium flares—and a sound rolled over the market that every soul in Vellamare knew the way they knew their own heartbeat: the tolling of the Ministry bell. Three strokes. Pause. Three strokes.

*Unmaking sweep.*

The market didn't scream. That was the terrible thing about Vellamare, the thing visitors never understood. The market simply *changed*. Laughter switched off like a lamp. Vendors began folding their goods with swift, silent efficiency. Mothers gathered children without a word. An entire district performing the choreography of fear it had rehearsed for a hundred years, everyone moving at once, everyone knowing their part.

Zephyrine moved with them, heart slamming, mind already running the drill. *You are a lawful craftsman. You have your vendor's license. Your charms are registered, pattern-numbered, inert. You have done nothing. You are doing nothing. You will do nothing.*

Then he heard the wardens talking.

They came up the canal-street in their grey coats, six of them, wading through the scattering crowd with the unhurried confidence of men who had never once needed to hurry. Their leader carried a broadsheet, and by the light of a seized lantern Zephyrine saw the printed seal at its top—the open eye of the Ministry—and beneath it, in heavy type:

**BY ORDER OF THE MINISTRY OF UNMAKING. WANTED FOR UNLICENSED COMMUNION WITH THE DEAD: THE ONE CALLED THE CARVER. LAST SEEN, SALTGLASS MARKET, THIS NIGHT.**

*This night.* Not last week. Not last month. *This night.* They had come for him tonight, at this hour, in this place—as though someone had sent them an invitation. As though someone had read it to them out of the water.

"The Carver," the warden was saying to his men, and his voice carried flat and clear across the sudden hush. "Bone-worker. Hears the dead. The Archivist wants him breathing. Anyone who shelters him shares his sentence."

*The Archivist.* Corvane. The name went through Zephyrine like a fishhook through the cheek. The Archivist himself did not leave the Spire for street sweeps. The Archivist did not care about charm-sellers. Whatever had brought Corvane's grey coats down to the Saltglass Market tonight had teeth in it, and those teeth were meant for Zephyrine alone.

A dead man brushed past him—or rather, through the space beside him, because the living crowd parted around ghosts without knowing why they did. An old sailor, drowned forty years, his beard still dripping. He leaned close, and his voice was the voice from the canal, the reciting voice, and now it was smiling.

*"Run, little carver,"* the drowned man said. *"The tide has your name. And so do they."*

Down the street, a warden turned his head—slowly, deliberately, like a hound catching scent—and his eyes swept the crowd, the stalls, the shadows between the lanterns, and stopped, for half a heartbeat, on a hooded figure frozen beside a shuttered oyster-bar.

Zephyrine ran.

He went the way the dead go—through gaps the living don't see, down the crooked stair behind the smokehouse, along the canal-wall where the lanterns didn't reach, his boots silent on wet stone, his breath tearing in his throat. Behind him the Ministry bell tolled again, and again, and the white light spread through the market like frost through a window.

He ran toward the only place in Vellamare where a man who heard the dead might still find answers: the bone-carvers' quarter, and the ossuary beneath it, where a hundred years of his family's work slept in the dark—and where, if the drowned voice spoke true, something had been waiting for him since before he was born.

*Tonight is the night written in water.*

Behind him, the tide began to rise.
