## 2. What the Bones Remember

The bone-carvers' quarter woke the way it always did after a raid: slowly, and with its back to the door.

Zephyrine moved through it with his collar up and his hands in his pockets, where they could pretend to be ordinary hands. Around him the quarter performed its nightly resurrection. Shutters that had slammed at the first shout of Ministry boots now opened a cautious finger's width. Lamps were relit behind horn panes, their light gone the color of old ivory. Somewhere above him a woman was scolding a child in a whisper, which was how the quarter scolded—everything softened to keep from being overheard by anything, living or otherwise.

He had sold eleven charms tonight. He had carved them himself, whalebone and river-ox rib, each one lawful: a charm against rheumatism, a charm for safe childbirth, three little teeth of bone on a cord to keep nightmares off a sleeper's throat. No magic in any of them. He had checked. He always checked, the way other men checked for their purses.

And still the warden had stood in the market with her lantern raised and said *the Carver*, and the word had gone through Zephyrine like a needle finding the seam of him.

Not *a carver*. Not *some charlatan selling bone*. *The* Carver. Capital letters he could hear, though no one had spoken them aloud.

He turned down Ropewalk Stair, where the buildings leaned together like conspirators, and let the dark take his outline. Behind him, faint but persistent, the drowned voice was still reciting.

It had not stopped since the market.

*"On the hundredth year, when the tide forgets its name—"*

"Quiet," he murmured, into his own collar. "I'm closed."

The dead who spoke to him had never much cared for business hours, but they had always, until tonight, been polite about it. This one was different. This one didn't drift or mutter or beg. It *recited*, with the terrible patience of something that had waited a century to be heard, and it knew his name—not Zephyrine, which was common enough along this coast, but the whole of it, the name his grandmother had whispered over his cradle and told him never to say in front of water.

*"—and the Carver's hand shall break what broke, and mend what mends, and pay what cannot be refused."*

His grandmother had died believing she'd kept him safe. Seven years in Vellamare, seven years of lawful craft and checked charms and careful silence, and he had almost believed it too. Almost was a country he'd lived in comfortably. Tonight the borders of it were burning.

The ossuary archive sat at the bottom of Ropewalk Stair like a knuckle of the city's own skeleton—a squat dome of black coral-stone that predated the Ministry, predated the anti-magic law, predated, some said, the city itself. Officially it was a records office. Unofficially it was the reason the bone-carvers' quarter had survived every purge Vellamare had ever suffered: because the archivists kept accounts older than law, and law, whatever else it was, respected seniority.

The door was answered by Old Sefa, who was four parts blind and one part spite, and who looked up at Zephyrine with milky eyes that saw more than they should have.

"You're bleeding," she said.

"I'm not."

"You will be." She stepped aside. "They came asking an hour ago. Warden Ferreol herself. Wanted the covenant ledgers." Her mouth folded. "I showed her tax receipts from sixty years past. She read every page. She knew I was lying, and she read every page anyway, and then she left." Sefa's head tilted. "That's not how wardens leave, boy."

Zephyrine's chest did something complicated. He filed it away to examine later, or never. "Sefa. The prophecy. The old one—the drowned one. I need all of it."

Something moved behind the milky eyes. Pity, maybe. Or recognition, which was worse.

"Third vault," Sefa said. "The Salt shelf. Bring a lamp you're willing to lose."

* * *

The third vault smelled of brine and time. The Salt shelf ran floor to ceiling, and everything on it had been written on material that water could not rot: vellum cured in ash, slate, lead sheets scratched with a stylus, and—one scroll, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff as bark—something that might once have been skin.

Zephyrine took down the oldest ledger first, because age was a kind of authority here, and opened it across his knees in the lamplight.

The hand was beautiful. That was the first obscenity of it—that a document promising ruin should be written so lovingly, each letter a small act of devotion. The date at its head made his breath go shallow: one hundred years ago exactly, to the night. To *tonight*.

*Recorded at the Drowned Gate, at low tide, by the last Speaker of the Choir, who gave these words to the water so that they would outlive the water's patience.*

He read. And the reading was a kind of drowning, slow and total.

*The covenant between the living city and the realm of Salt was sealed in bone and renewed in choice, generation upon generation, each heir to the Gate laying their hand upon the keystone and saying: I choose this. Without the choosing, the seal starves. Without the seal, the tide remembers what it was before we taught it manners.*

*But the heirs grew few, and then grew proud, and then were taken. On the hundredth year, when the tide forgets its name, the covenant shall crack along its oldest seam.*

*Then shall come the Carver, born of the broken line, who hears what others bury. His hand shall break what broke, and mend what mends.*

*And the price is written here—*

Here the ledger changed. The graceful script continued, letter for letter, stroke for stroke—but the letters stopped meaning anything. They resolved into a tight, looping script unlike any alphabet Zephyrine knew, dense as fish scales, and when he stared at it too long the characters seemed to move, rearranging themselves the way bones settle in a body at rest.

Only the dead could read it. Of course. The price of the covenant, written in the language of those the covenant served.

"Show me," he whispered—to the ledger, to the room, to whatever listened. "If you want me to do this, show me."

Nothing answered. Nothing except, far off, through stone and stair and street, the drowned voice, patient as erosion:

*"—and pay what cannot be refused."*

He set the ledger down with hands that were not quite steady and told himself the lie he had carried for seven years: *you can still walk away.* Go north past the salt marshes. Change his name. Burn his tools. Learn to carve wood, wood that never spoke, wood that had never been anyone.

But even as he thought it, his fingers found the second item on the shelf—a slim lead sheet, soft-edged with age—and unfolded it, and there found the thing that ended the argument forever.

A genealogy. The line of Speakers, hand laid to hand down a century, each name followed by the words *chose, and was chosen.* And at the bottom, in fresher ink, added by some archivist within living memory, a single entry:

*Line ends: Villalobos. One heir remaining. Location: Vellamare, bone-carvers' quarter.*

Someone had known. Someone had known for years, and written it down, and filed it, and let him sell his little lawful charms two streets away while the date walked toward them both.

Zephyrine sat down on the cold floor of the vault, among the records of the dead, and laughed once—a short, ugly sound with nothing in it.

Reluctant inheritance. That was the phrase his grandmother used for the sea itself: *the sea is a reluctant inheritance, mijo; it takes you whether you sign or not.* He had thought she meant drowning. He understood now that she had meant this—being claimed by a story older than your consent, waking one night to find your name already written in a book you never agreed to open.

The lamp guttered. In its failing light, the unreadable script on the ledger seemed to pulse, gently, like something breathing. Waiting to be read by the only reader left.

And beneath it, fainter, rising through the floor of the world like water through stone, the voice began again—no longer reciting now, but calling. Not his name.

Hers.

*"Warden,"* it said, sweet and sorrowful, *"warden, come see what your law has buried."*

Somewhere above him, boots were crossing the quarter again. And Zephyrine understood, with the clarity of a man watching the tide withdraw before it returns tenfold, that Isolde Ferreol had not finished searching—and that the next place she would look was the one place in Vellamare where his name was written down.

He snuffed the lamp, and ran.
