## 3. The Warden of Unmaking

The summons came at the hour when Vellamare's lamps gutter low and the tide exhales its long salt breath against the seawalls, and Isolde Ferreol knew before she broke the wax that it would be about him.

She had known for three days, in truth—since the first whisper moved through the Ministry's marble corridors like oil through water: *the Carver is real, the Carver is found, the Carver sells charms in the night market under a name too soft to be true.* She had told herself it was coincidence. That there were a hundred bone-carvers in the drowned quarter, a thousand hands shaping whale-ivory and grave-bone into trinkets for tourists and widows. That Zephyrine Villalobos had been dead to her for seven years, and dead men could not be arrested.

The seal on the summons was Archivist Corvane's. The ink was black as a closed eye.

*Warden Ferreol. By order of the Ministry of Unmaking, you are directed to proceed at once to the Lantern Market and take into custody one Zephyrine Villalobos, bone-carver, on charges of unlawful thaumaturgy in the first degree. He is to be delivered alive to the Unmaking Chambers before the next high tide. Fail in this, and your badge, your rank, and your name will be entered into the Ledger of Forfeiture.*

Alive. Before the next high tide.

Isolde read it twice, standing in her quarters above the Ministry barracks, the paper trembling not in her hand but somewhere deeper, in the place where her heartbeat kept its own records. Outside her window, the city lay silvered under a moon that looked, tonight, like a coin held between the fingers of a god deciding whether to spend it.

Seven years ago she had stood in a rain-soaked alley behind his workshop with her hand on her baton and her oath in her throat, and she had let him go. She had written in her report that the suspect fled before identification could be made. She had carried that small forgery inside her chest ever since, the way a body carries shrapnel—not killing, but never forgotten, always aching when the weather turned.

Now the weather had turned.

* * *

The Ministry of Unmaking occupied the old lighthouse fortress at the harbor's mouth, and Isolde walked its corridors that morning feeling every stone of them newly, as though she were seeing the architecture of her own spine. She had given this place seventeen years. She had been nine years old when the anti-magic law was read aloud in her schoolroom, when the teacher explained with gentle gravity that magic was a debt the living owed to nothing and no one, that it made citizens into vessels for powers that did not love them, that the Ministry existed to protect Vellamare from itself. She had believed it the way children believe in the floor beneath their feet. She had built her whole life standing on it.

In the briefing chamber, Corvane waited beside a table spread with evidence, and Isolde's stomach tightened at the sight of it: carved bone charms laid out in rows, each tagged and numbered, each one humming faintly to her trained senses with something that was not craft. Craft sat still in the hand. This *moved*.

"The night market raid last night netted forty-one pieces," Corvane said. His voice was dry paper, his face a ledger's face, ruled and balanced and without margin for error. "All traced to a single carver. Villalobos escaped the initial sweep—he has a talent for vanishing, it seems—but we have his quarter surrounded. Warden, you took his confession seven years ago."

"I took no confession." Her voice came out level. She was proud of how level. "He fled before questioning."

"Then you know his face." Corvane lifted one of the charms—a small fish, delicate as a breath, carved from grey-green bone—and held it up to the lamplight. Something in Isolde's chest turned over. She knew that fish. She had seen it hanging at Zephyrine's throat the day they met, back when they were both young enough to think the world was a door rather than a wall. *For safe passage through deep water,* he'd told her, laughing. *A joke charm. It doesn't work.*

It worked. Looking at it now, she could feel it working, a faint warmth like a hand pressed flat against her sternum.

"He'll run if he sees a Ministry uniform," Corvane continued. "So you'll go plain-clothed. Alone. You're the only warden he might trust enough to approach—or the only one he might hesitate to flee from. Either hesitation serves us."

"You're sending me because I knew him."

"I'm sending you because you're the best I have, and because sentiment, properly aimed, is a weapon." Corvane set the fish-charm down with terrible gentleness. "Do not mistake me, Warden. If you cannot deliver him, another will. And the man who goes after him next will not have known his face. They will not bother taking him alive."

There it was. The blade under the silk. *Delivered alive by my hand, or destroyed by another's.* Corvane watched her with eyes like two drops of ink, waiting, and Isolde understood that this, too, was part of the design—that he had chosen her precisely because refusing would cost her everything, and obeying would cost her something worse.

"I'll need the case file," she said. "Everything on the prophecy."

Something flickered across Corvane's face—so fast she almost missed it, a shutter drawn and released. "What prophecy?"

"The one the drowned woman was reciting in the market. The raid report mentions it. A century-old text naming tonight's date." She kept her tone administrative, bored even. "If the Carver is tied to a prophesied event, I should know what I'm walking into."

"The prophecy is Ministry property. Sealed classification." He slid the arrest warrant across the table instead. "You need only this. Bring him in, Warden. Before the tide turns."

* * *

Her copy of the prophecy lived in a locked drawer beneath her bed, and had lived there for eleven years, since the day she'd been assigned to archive duty in the Ministry's restricted vaults and had copied it out by hand, late at night, telling herself it was professional diligence.

She had never told herself the truth about why.

Now she sat on the floor of her quarters with the pages spread before her—the old words in their crabbed century-old script, the date circled in her own younger ink—and she read them as though reading them for the first time, which was nearly true. She had memorized them years ago, but memorization is not understanding, and understanding had been waiting all this time for her heart to break open enough to receive it.

*When the moon stands full upon the hundredth year, the covenant shall crack along its oldest seam. A carver of bones, hearing what the drowned have said, shall stand at the Drowned Gate at low tide. What was sealed in blood may be resealed in bone; what was taken by force must be returned by choice. And the price shall be written in a script only the dead can read.*

Tonight. Tonight was the hundredth year. Tonight the moon stood full, fat and white above the harbor, and somewhere in the bone-carvers' quarter a man who heard the dead was running from people like her.

Isolde thought about the law. She thought about the schoolroom, the gentle gravity of her teacher's voice, the floor she had stood on for seventeen years. *Magic is a debt the living owe to nothing and no one.* But the prophecy spoke of a covenant—a bond, an agreement, something entered into willingly by both realms. Covenants were not debts. Covenants were promises. And you did not unmake a promise by outlawing one side of it; you only starved it, left it bleeding, until it cracked along its oldest seam.

Unless someone wanted it to crack. Unless someone had *made* it crack.

The thought arrived whole, unbidden, terrible: *Corvane didn't send me to arrest Zephyrine. He sent me to find him.* Because the Ministry had known the prophecy's date for a hundred years—it was in their vaults, sealed, classified—and they had done nothing to prevent this night except pass laws that drove every hearer of the dead into hiding, every potential instrument of renewal into shadow. Unless prevention was never the goal. Unless the goal was to be the only ones present when the covenant broke, holding the pieces, hoarding whatever power bled out of the wound.

She was not a warden tonight. She was a hound, and the hunt was not for justice.

Isolde rose, went to the small iron stove in the corner of her room, and fed it the warrant first. She watched Corvane's black ink curl and brown and lift into flame, watched the order that owned her become smoke, and felt nothing she expected—no terror, no exhilaration. Only a vast, quiet clarity, like the moment after a wave passes over you and you surface into air.

Then she burned her copy of the prophecy, page by page, because if the Ministry came for her—and they would, when she failed to report—they would find nothing but ash and a cold stove, and no evidence of what she knew or whom she meant to warn.

Her badge she set on the table, centered, precise. Let them enter it in the Ledger of Forfeiture. Let them strike her name. Names could be struck; what she was underneath the name had been decided years ago in a rainy alley, and tonight she was finally going to keep that older promise.

She dressed in dark wool, no uniform, no insignia. She took her baton—then left it leaning against the door. She took instead the small knife from her boot, the one for cutting rope, and the fish-charm Corvane had catalogued as evidence, lifted from the table when his attention was elsewhere. It was warm in her palm. It hummed like a held note.

*For safe passage through deep water.*

The tide was turning. Somewhere beyond the seawalls, past the drowned gate at the harbor's edge, the sea was drawing back its long grey hem, exposing the stones where a promise had been made a thousand years ago and broken a hundred. And somewhere in the narrow streets of the carvers' quarter, a man with soft hands and a hidden gift was deciding, this very hour, whether to run or to answer.

She went looking for him off the record, off the books, off the map of her own former life—down through the sleeping city, past the market stalls being shuttered, into the quarter where the buildings leaned together like conspirators and the air smelled of bone dust and brine.

She found his workshop door unlocked. That alone stopped her heart: Zephyrine never left anything unlocked. Inside, the lamps were cold, the tools laid out neat as surgery, and on the workbench, weighted beneath a scrap of whale-ivory, a single sheet of paper in his careful, slanted hand—as if he had known someone would come looking, and had guessed, perhaps hoped, who.

It was addressed to no one. It said only:

*They've named me the Carver. I'm going to the Drowned Gate before the tide comes back. If the dead are right about what I am, then I'm already too late to be anything else. Don't follow me. Please.*

Below the words, half-smudged, as though his hand had hesitated: *I. F. — if it's you —*

And nothing after. An unfinished sentence, seven years old and one hour old at once.

Isolde stood in the cold workshop with the paper shaking in her grip, and understood that she had already made her choice, had made it in the alley seven years ago, had made it every night since when she slept with a forged report buried in her conscience. Duty had simply been slower to admit defeat than she was.

She folded the note into her coat, over her heart, and walked out into the moon-white night toward the harbor's edge, toward the gate, toward the man she had once loved and had never once stopped—

—and behind her, far off at the Ministry fortress, a bell began to ring. Not the alarm for escape. Not the alarm for fire.

The alarm they rang, she knew with a cold settling in her gut, only when something ancient woke.

The tide was coming back early. And it was coming back hungry.
