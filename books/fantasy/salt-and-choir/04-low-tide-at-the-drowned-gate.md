## 4. Low Tide at the Drowned Gate

The prophecy's first instruction was written in a script that moved when he wasn't looking at it directly, but he had copied it out three times in the ossuary's cold light until his hand knew the shapes by heart:

*When the moon stands low and the sea stands lower, go down the Stair of Names to the gate that drowned, and wait for the tide that does not come.*

The Stair of Names was a fisherman's superstition, a spiral of worn steps cut into the cliff below the Lantern District, each riser carved with the name of someone lost to Salt. Children dared each other to descend it. Adults pretended it didn't exist. The Ministry had tried to demolish it twice; both times the crews came back with their tools rusted through and their memories full of holes.

Zephyrine went down it at midnight, carrying nothing but his carving knife and the terrible clarity of a man who has stopped pretending.

* * *

The stair was older than Vellamare. He could feel that in the stone under his palms—feel it the way he felt everything now, since the ossuary, since the archive had opened its bone-throated mouth and spoken to him in a hundred voices at once. Seven years of careful silence, of selling charms that were only wood and wire and hope, and in one night the dead had found him as if he'd been ringing a bell all along.

He counted steps because counting kept other thoughts at bay. Forty-one. Forty-two. The names on the risers grew older as he descended, the letters cruder, some of them not letters at all but knotwork sigils from before the city had a written tongue. At step sixty-three the salt air changed. It thickened. It tasted of iron and old grief.

At step eighty, the stair ended in black water.

This was the Drowned Gate—or what remained of it above the tide. Two pillars of green-veined stone rose from the shallows, leaning toward each other like conspirators, their capitals just breaking the surface. Between them, beneath the water, he could see the faint suggestion of an archway continuing downward, down into the dark where the harbor floor shelved away into the true deep. A century ago, the fishermen said, you could walk between those pillars at any tide and stay dry. The covenant had held the sea back like a cupped hand.

Now the water lapped at the third step of the stair, patient as a creditor.

Zephyrine sat on the lowest dry step and waited for the tide that does not come.

* * *

The moon stood low over the harbor, a fingernail paring of light. The city behind him had gone quiet in the way cities do—not silent, never silent, but hushed into its nighttime register: a dog, a distant argument, the groan of moored ships. Somewhere up in the Lantern District, Isolde Ferreol was either hunting him or lying awake deciding whether to. He tried not to think about which. He failed.

An hour passed. The tide did not come. The water did not recede either. It simply stayed, holding its line at the third step, and Zephyrine understood, slowly, the way one understands a joke told too quietly, that this stillness *was* the sign. The sea was waiting on him. The whole weight of the harbor, held motionless by something older than gravity.

Then the dead arrived.

They did not come as ghosts come in cheap melodrama—no cold wind, no guttering candles. They came as sound. It began at the edge of hearing, a hum like a wet finger circling the rim of a glass, and it gathered, voice upon voice, until the air itself seemed to have a throat. He could not see them. He could feel them: a pressure against his skin like standing in a crowd, hundreds strong, all facing him.

"*Carver,*" they said, in a chorus that had no single mouth. "*Son of the son of the breaker.*"

"I'm not—" His voice cracked. He steadied it. "I'm not anyone's anything. I carve buttons. I make charms for seasick tourists."

"*You hear us.*"

It wasn't a question, and there was no use denying it. That was the thing about the dead, he was learning: they had no patience for the small mercies the living traded in.

"Yes," he said. "I hear you."

The chorus sighed—a sound like the whole harbor exhaling—and the pressure around him eased into something almost gentle. Almost welcoming. That frightened him more than the coercion had.

"*Then you know why we have waited a hundred years,*" they said. "*Come and see what your blood unmade.*"

The water between the pillars began to move.

It parted—not violently, not with spray and thunder, but with the courtesy of a curtain drawn aside. A path opened across the harbor floor, walls of standing water trembling on either side, lit from below by a pale luminescence that had no source he could name. Down there, revealed for the first time in a century, lay the rest of the Drowned Gate: a great arch of carved stone descending into the seabed, its keystone a hollow socket the size of a coffin lid, empty. Around the arch, half-buried in silt, were figures—statuary, or something more. Carved men and women, kneeling, arms raised, their faces worn smooth by the sea.

His ancestor's work. He knew it the way he knew his own hands.

"*Walk,*" said the Choir. "*The tide will not touch you tonight. Tonight the tide is ours, and we are yours.*"

"That," Zephyrine muttered, stepping off the stair onto seabed that firmed beneath his boot like packed sand, "is exactly the kind of sentence that should come with a contract."

No one laughed. The dead, he was discovering, had no sense of humor about ownership.

* * *

The arch was taller than the Lantern District's bell tower, though most of it slept in shadow above the waterline. Up close, the carvings resolved into history: processions of figures moving in friezes around the pillars, living folk and drowned folk walking side by side, trading—corn for coral, song for salt, breath for memory. At the crown of the arch, where the keystone socket gaped, two hands reached toward each other from opposite sides of the stone and did not meet. The gap between them was the size of the missing keystone. The size, he thought with a sick lurch, of a man's chest, if a man were laid within it.

He touched the socket's rim. The stone was warm.

Beneath his palm, faint as a heartbeat heard through a wall, something pulsed.

"What broke it?" he asked. His voice fell strangely flat in the corridor of water. "The covenant. My—the carver who made this. What did he do?"

The Choir was quiet for so long he thought they might not answer. When they spoke, the chorus had narrowed, winnowed down to fewer voices—older ones, he somehow knew, though the knowing came from nowhere he could defend.

"*He was asked to renew it,*" they said. "*As every generation is asked. As it has always been asked. The covenant is not a wall, Carver. It is a promise, and promises die unless they are remade. Each keeper of the craft was meant to come to this gate in his turn and give what must be given, freely, so that the sea and the city might continue to hold each other.*"

"And my ancestor refused."

"*He feared the price. He fled inland. He married. He raised children who raised children who raised you.*" A pause, and then, with terrible mildness: "*And the promise starved. And the sea began to remember that it is a sea.*"

Zephyrine looked up at the reaching hands, the empty socket, the frieze of the living and the drowned walking hand in hand. He thought of the charms he sold—wood and wire and hope—and how, without ever knowing why, he had always carved them in the shapes from these friezes. Knots of joined hands. Interlocking spirals. He had been copying this arch his whole life, the way a bird copies a song it heard in the egg.

"The prophecy says a reforge," he said carefully. "It doesn't say how."

"*No,*" agreed the Choir. "*Because the how is not written. The how is given.*"

The pressure returned—not hostile, but vast, the way the ocean is vast, indifferent to whether you can swim. The water walls shivered. In the luminous dark, shapes gathered around him: he could almost see them now, impressions of faces, drowned faces, faces worn smooth as the statuary. Hundreds of them. Thousands. All the dead of a hundred years of slow drowning, all the dead of the famine tides and the wreck years, all of them looking at him with the patience of people who have already waited longer than his entire life.

"*Here is the price, Carver, and we will not hide it from you as your ancestor was not hidden from:*" The chorus dropped to a whisper that filled the whole harbor. "*The covenant must be sealed with the maker's own bone, carved by his own hand, given willingly. And the gift that makes the seal is the gift itself. You will carve your hearing into the keystone. Every voice of the dead you have ever heard—all of us, forever—will pass into the stone and be silent to you. You will spend your life deaf to us so that the living may keep theirs.*"

The words landed one by one, like stones dropped into a well.

His hearing. Not his life—that would have been simpler, cleaner, the kind of price a man could brace against. But the hearing. The thing that had cursed him since childhood, the whispers in market crowds, the drowned girl who had taught him which knots held, the voices that had driven him to hide, to lie, to build a whole false self out of lawful craft. The thing that was, God help him, also the only part of himself he had ever truly loved.

They were asking him to give up the wound and the gift together, because they had never once been separate.

"You want me to decide *now*," he said. His voice sounded very small against the arch. "Tonight. With the Ministry hunting me and the tide held open like a door."

"*We have waited a hundred years,*" said the Choir, and there it was—the gentleness curdling, the welcome revealing the teeth beneath it. "*The sea grows restless. The Ministry builds its engines. If the covenant breaks fully, Carver, it will not be only the drowned who pay. Vellamare will drown with us. Every soul in it.*"

"So it isn't a request."

"*It is a need.*" A pause. Then, softer, almost pleading, and that was worst of all: "*It is a need, and you are the last hand that can answer it.*"

Zephyrine stood in the corridor of held-back water, beneath the reaching hands of his ancestor's masterpiece, and felt awe and horror settle over him in equal measure, indistinguishable, like two tides meeting. They weren't threatening him. That was the trap of it. They were only telling the truth, and the truth was a vise.

Above him, far up the cliff, a light flickered along the Stair of Names. Lantern-light. Moving downward, deliberate, unhurried.

Someone else had read the prophecy's instructions.

The Choir went silent all at once, thousands of dead holding a single breath, and Zephyrine turned toward the stair with his knife loose in his hand, watching the light descend.
