## 8. The Tide That Takes Names

Isolde Ferreol had burned a prophecy once, and the ash had taught her nothing she could use. What it had taught her was how paper dies—curling first at the corners like a hand withdrawing, then all at once, surrendering its letters to the dark. She thought of that now as she stood in the Ministry's Tide Chamber and watched Archivist Corvane unroll a document that was not paper at all.

It was skin. Tanned, thin as onion membrane, and covered in script that moved when she wasn't looking directly at it—the way water moves when you trust it not to drown you.

"The Covenant of Salt," Corvane said, "in the original." His voice was the voice he used for lectures, for corrections, for the gentle public dismantling of a student's error. He had used that voice on Isolde for nineteen years. It had been, once, the closest thing to a father's voice she owned. "Do you know why we keep it in the deep vaults, Warden?"

"Because it's heretical," Isolde said.

"Because it's *embarrassing*." He smiled. He was a narrow man, dry as a ledger's spine, with white hair combed in precise rows and hands that had never once trembled over an archive. "Because for a hundred years the Ministry has told Vellamare that magic is a disease, that the drowned are a myth, that the covenant is folklore—and all the while we have held the deed to both realms in a box under our feet." He touched the skin-scroll with one fingertip, almost tenderly. "The founder understood something we have forgotten: power is not diminished by being hoarded. Only by being *shared*."

The Tide Chamber sat below the waterline, its walls of green glass thick as tombstones. Beyond them, the harbor pressed close, fish silvering past like loose coins. Forty wardens stood in ranks along the walls, and behind them, filling half the chamber on a frame of black iron, stood the engine.

She had heard it described in briefings she'd been cleared for only three days ago. They called it the Unmaker's Loom. It looked like a loom, too—a great skeletal machine of brass and bone-white ceramic, its warp threads strung with something that made her teeth ache to look at. Names, Corvane had said. The engine wove names. Feed it the covenant's terms and it would unpick them thread by thread, devouring the old accord entire, leaving Salt lawless and Vellamare's Ministry holding the only magic left in the world: the kind that could be issued by permit.

"Where is the Carver?" Isolde asked. Her voice came out level. She had practiced level for nineteen years; it was the first thing they taught you at the Academy, before combat, before law. *Your voice is your badge. Keep it flat and no one will ever know what you're carrying.*

"Villalobos?" Corvane consulted a slate without interest. "His trail ends at the Drowned Gate. Which is convenient. When the tide turns, the Gate opens whether we will it or not, and the Loom will drink from the source." He rolled up the covenant with the care of a man handling a relic he intended to outlive. "You seem pale, Warden Ferreol. Nostalgia?"

"For what?"

"For the girl who believed the law was a wall built to keep people safe." He said it kindly, which was the cruelest way he could have said it. "I remember her. She wrote excellent essays."

* * *

They gave her a post at the glass wall, because she was senior and because Corvane trusted her—or believed he did, which amounted to the same thing in practice. She stood with her badge at her throat, cold against her collarbone, and watched the tide begin to turn.

It turned wrong.

Anyone who had lived a winter in Vellamare knew the rhythm of the harbor: the slow green withdrawal, the gulls following the water out, the mudflats steaming. This was not that. The water didn't withdraw—it *gathered*, piling itself against the Drowned Gate in a standing wave that should have collapsed and didn't, a wall of sea holding itself upright like a congregation rising for a hymn. And through the glass, faint as memory, Isolde heard singing.

Not with her ears. With the scar on her palm, the one Zephyrine had kissed once, months ago, in a market stall that smelled of bone dust and cloves, when neither of them had yet said aloud what they were to each other. The scar sang, low and wordless, and she understood suddenly what Zephyrine had lived with his whole life: that the dead were not silent, only unheard, and that hearing them was less a gift than a wound that never closed.

*He's down there,* she thought. *Behind that wave. And I'm standing on the side that means to kill him.*

Corvane's voice cut across the chamber. "Begin the count."

A junior archivist began calling the tide-markers in a clear reciting voice, and the Loom woke. Its threads began to move—not turning, but *unweaving*, each name-thread plucked loose and drawn into the machine's heart where it frayed into nothing. Isolde felt each one go. Not heard—felt, the way you feel a tooth pulled from a jaw you didn't know ached. Somewhere below, something ancient lost a syllable. Then another.

"Archivist." Her voice was flat. Perfectly flat. "The terms of engagement for the Carver. Confirm them for me."

Corvane didn't look up from his slate. "Capture if practical. Termination authorized. The gift is to be harvested regardless of the subject's condition." A pause, and then, with the mildness of a man discussing weather: "You've gone very still, Warden."

"I'm always still. You taught me that."

"I taught you that stillness is a weapon." Now he looked at her, and his eyes were pale and patient and utterly without malice, which was the worst thing about him. He had never been cruel. He had simply never once wondered whether he ought to be. "Whatever you're deciding, decide quickly. The tide won't wait for your conscience, and neither will I."

Isolde reached up and took off her badge.

It was heavier than it looked. Nineteen years of weight, all of it concentrated in a disc of stamped tin no bigger than a coin. She had polished it every morning since she was sixteen. She had slept with it under her pillow during her Academy years. She had once taken a knife across the forearm defending a fellow warden wearing the same stamp, and had counted the scar a fair price.

She set it on the floor between her boots, gently, the way you lay down a sleeping child.

Then she drew her baton, reversed it, and drove the strike into the junction box at the Loom's base with everything nineteen years of training had put into her arm.

* * *

What happened next happened fast, and she would remember it afterward in fragments, the way drowning people remember light.

The Loom screamed—a sound like a choir being read aloud wrong—and half its threads snapped and whipped free, lashing the nearest wardens into retreat. Alarms. Glass shattering somewhere above as the standing wave, sensing the disturbance in the covenant's weave, leaned harder against the Gate. Corvane shouting her name—not *Warden Ferreol*, but *Isolde*, in the voice he'd used when she was young and brilliant and his, and she hated him a little for spending that voice now, when it could do nothing but break against her like water on stone.

Two wardens came at her. She took the first one's wrist and folded it, took the second one's legs and put him down hard, and did not draw her blade, because some part of her was still, stubbornly, a teacher's best student: *the law is a wall built to keep people safe.* These were not enemies. They were children of the same lie she had carried for nineteen years, and she would hurt them only as much as the moment required.

Then Corvane himself stepped into her path, and he was holding the covenant-skin in both hands, and he was smiling.

"You could have been Archivist," he said. "You could have had the vaults. Everything I built, Ferreol, and you throw it away for a bone-carver who hears ghosts."

"He doesn't hear ghosts," Isolde said. "He hears people. There's a difference, and you're too far inside your own lie to see it." She feinted left. "And I never wanted the vaults. I wanted the truth. You just never had any to give me."

He struck at her with the iron rod he kept for correcting unruly documents, and she caught it, and for a moment they stood locked together, face to face, close enough that she could see the age in him at last—all those precise rows of white hair, all those dry careful years, and underneath them nothing. No grief. No doubt. Just arithmetic.

"The tide's turning," he said softly. "In four minutes the Gate opens, and my engine drinks, and everything you love drowns in silence. Where is your wall now, Warden?"

"Being rebuilt," Isolde said, and broke his grip, and ran—not away from the chamber, but toward it, toward the great green glass wall where the sea stood piled like judgment, toward the seam where the Drowned Gate's ancient lock showed through the harbor-side, corroded open a finger's width by a hundred years of the Ministry's neglect.

She went through the gap as the alarms reached their peak, and the sea closed over her head, and the last thing she heard from the world of air was Corvane's voice, receding, giving orders to men who no longer mattered:

"Let her go. The Loom doesn't need her. Nothing down there does."

The water took her name the way the tide takes a footprint—but her body remembered what her badge had forgotten, and she swam down, down, toward the singing in her scarred palm, toward the gate, toward the man who had once told her, in a voice rough with fear and hope, *if I lose my hearing, I want the last voice in my ears to be yours.*

Below her, through the green and gathering dark, the Choir was rising to meet the breach—and at its center, carved bone gleaming like a lantern, Zephyrine Villalobos turned toward the surface, toward her, and opened his arms.
