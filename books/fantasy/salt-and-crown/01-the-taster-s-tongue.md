## 1. The Taster's Tongue

The first thing Petra Blackwood did every morning was insult the head cook, and the second thing she did was eat a spoonful of his soup.

"Go on, then," said Bramblewick, arms crossed over an apron that had survived three coronations and one small kitchen fire. "Tell me it's under-salted again. Break my heart the way you do every blessed Tuesday."

Petra took the spoon, held the broth on her tongue, and let her ledger open.

This was the part no one understood about tasting — that it wasn't swallowing at all. Swallowing was for amateurs and fools and second sons who wanted to prove their devotion to the crown by dying dramatically at banquets. A royal taster *read*. She let the flavor sit and unfold like a letter, and her body, which had spent three years cataloguing every poison the kingdom's enemies could brew, buy, or invent, wrote the entry in blood.

*Onion.* Good. *Bay leaf, thyme, a whisper of smoked pork fat.* Better. *Salt — adequate, though she'd never tell him so, because Bramblewick's pride was load-bearing architecture in this kitchen.*

And beneath all of it, faint as a coin at the bottom of a well: nothing. No bitterness of hemlock, no almond ghost of cyanide, no sweet metallic curl of lead. Clean.

"You've salted it properly for once," she said, "and I'm only saying that because flattery improves the digestion."

Bramblewick made a noise like a kettle being personally offended. "Flattery. From the woman who told the Ambassador of Vell my pheasant tasted 'like regret and feathers.'"

"It did taste like regret and feathers. The ambassador agreed. He wept."

"He wept because you'd already eaten half of it."

"I wept too," Petra said solemnly. "It was very bad."

He shoved a heel of bread at her, which was Bramblewick's way of embracing someone, and she tucked it into her coat pocket for later — for the small mouths at the market who considered palace bread a food group unto itself.

The Queen's breakfast arrived in stages, as it always did: porridge with honey, soft-boiled eggs, tea brewed from leaves that cost more per ounce than Petra earned in a month. Petra tasted each one in turn, holding them on her tongue while the serving girls watched with the particular reverence the palace staff reserved for her — half awe, half the wariness people gave to anyone whose job involved dying professionally.

Queen Maren watched from behind her correspondence, though she pretended not to. That was their ritual. The queen pretended to read a letter about grain tariffs; Petra pretended not to notice being watched; both of them understood that in a court where the last queen had died with her lips blackened and her cup still warm, pretense was a kind of armor everyone wore to breakfast.

"The porridge is clean," Petra reported. "The eggs are clean. The tea—" she paused, letting the flavor finish its letter, "—is clean, but someone has used the good saffron threads instead of the ordinary ones, and I'd wager my week's wages it was Marta, because it's her mother's name-day."

Marta, by the tea urn, went pink as a boiled ham.

Queen Maren lowered her letter. Her eyes were gray and unhurried and missed approximately nothing. "Let it stand, then. A kindness in the tea won't kill me." A pause, dry as good paper. "That has rather been your department, hasn't it, Mistress Blackwood."

"Eleven years without a poisoned queen, Your Majesty. I intend to make it twelve if it kills me."

"That," said the queen, returning to her tariffs, "is precisely the joke I wish you wouldn't make."

It was the closest thing to affection either of them permitted before nine in the morning.

Petra finished the tray — a candied plum, a dish of clotted cream, a small pastry shaped like a swan that she dismantled with genuine sorrow because it was delicious and she'd have liked to keep it whole — and signed the ledger with her mark: a small circle with a line through it, the sign tasters used since the old days. *Empty cup. Safe passage.*

She was nearly at the door when the strangeness happened.

It came with the last item, a honey-cake baked fresh that morning, golden and fragrant, smelling of orange blossom. Petra set it on her tongue the way she always did — patient, attentive, listening with her mouth.

Arsenic.

She knew it the way she knew her own heartbeat. White arsenic, refined, dissolved into the honey glaze where its slight sweetness would hide among the sugar. Enough to take down a grown man in an afternoon; enough, given the queen's slight frame and delicate constitution, to do the work by supper. It bloomed on her tongue with the awful familiarity of an old acquaintance — *hello, you, we really must stop meeting like this* —

And underneath it, something else.

Petra went very still.

Underneath the arsenic lay a taste she had no ledger-entry for. Cold. Mineral. Like licking the inside of a deep cave, like snowmelt off stone that had never seen sun. It didn't belong to any poison she knew, and worse — it didn't belong to any *food* she knew, or any herb, mineral, or preparation in the whole sprawling catalogue written into her blood. It tasted the way the space between stars must taste, if stars had spaces and spaces had opinions.

It tasted, absurdly, like *magic* — and Petra knew what magic tasted like, though she'd have swallowed her own tongue before admitting it, because unlicensed magic was a flogging offense and licensed magic belonged to Wardens who looked at market-charmers the way cats looked at things that squeaked.

The honey-cake sat on her tongue. The cold mineral wrongness sat underneath the arsenic like a stone under moss.

"Mistress Blackwood?" The queen's voice, quiet now, all the morning dryness gone out of it.

Petra spat into the basin by the door — theatrically, thoroughly, because a taster's spit was a performance as much as a verdict — and turned.

"Arsenic in the honey-cake, Your Majesty. Refined white, in the glaze. Dosed for one person of your size." She kept her voice level, bored even, because panic in a taster spread faster than poison. "The kitchen's clear — it went in after baking. Someone will need to check who had access to the cooling rack between the fourth bell and now."

"And the rest?"

Petra hesitated one heartbeat too long.

"The rest, Your Majesty?"

"You made a face," said Queen Maren, "that I have never seen you make in eleven years, and I have seen you drink things that would fell a horse."

*Tell her,* said the sensible part of Petra. *Tell her there's something under the poison that tastes like winter underground.*

But telling the Queen of Cindralon that magic had gotten into the breakfast tray meant Wardens, and Wardens meant questions, and questions meant someone asking why the royal taster could taste magic at all — a thing no honest taster should be able to do, a thing Petra had hidden since she was nine years old and had learned to keep hidden the way other people kept breathing.

"It's nothing," she said. "A bitter note in the glaze. Orange rind gone off, perhaps. I'll speak to Bramblewick."

The queen looked at her for a long moment. Gray eyes, unhurried, missing nothing.

"See that you do," she said at last, and returned to her grain tariffs, and Petra walked out of the royal apartments with her heart going like a drum in a parade.

* * *

The Nightmarket woke up around the time respectable markets went to bed, which was one of the many reasons Petra loved it.

By the time she reached her stall — wedged between a fortune-teller who was honestly just very good at reading faces and a man selling eels out of a barrel with more charisma than most priests — the lanterns were lit and the lane smelled of fried dough, wet wool, and possibility. Sefa was already there, sitting on the counter of Petra's stall with her boots swinging, guarding the stock with the ferocity of a much larger person.

"You're late," Sefa announced.

"I saved the queen's life today."

"You're late," Sefa repeated, "because Pip ate two of the luck-knots and Tam covered for you, and also because you're always late on Tuesdays, and Tuesdays are when Bramblewick makes the good soup, and you're a coward about soup."

"I am a *professional* about soup," Petra said, hanging up her coat. "There's a difference."

The stall itself was, officially, a seller of knotted thread and dried flowers. Unofficially, it was the best charm-stall in the Nightmarket, which was a dangerous distinction to hold, since charms were illegal, licensed charms were expensive, and Warden patrols came through the lane twice a night looking for exactly the kind of warm little magics Petra sold at three coppers apiece.

Her charms weren't grand. Nothing that moved mountains or called storms — nothing that would ever interest a Warden's detection-plates, if she was careful, and she was always careful. Luck-knots that nudged probability the way a friend nudges you toward the right door. Salt-rings for cradles, to keep bad dreams walking past. A charm against damp, which sounded humble until you'd spent a winter in the dockside tenements and understood that damp killed more market children than knives did.

Small magics. Small enough to fit in a pocket, small enough to hang around a neck, small enough that the law pretended they didn't exist right up until the moment it needed someone to arrest.

"Pip!" Petra called. "Come here and confess."

Pip emerged from behind the eel barrel, eight years old, gap-toothed, entirely unrepentant. "They were *broken* luck-knots," she said. "I was testing them."

"You ate them."

"Testing them *with my mouth.*"

"That's literally my job," Petra said, "and even I don't eat the merchandise."

"You should try it. Very lucky. I found four coppers today."

"That's theft, Pip."

"That's *luck*, Petra."

Tam appeared out of the crowd the way he always did — suddenly, quietly, carrying something. Tonight it was a sack of flour balanced on one shoulder and a look on his face that Petra couldn't quite read. He was twenty now, tall and brown-haired and steady in the way that made everyone in the market family orbit him without noticing they were doing it. Eleven years ago he'd pulled a starving, poisoned, half-dead child out of a gutter behind a bakery and shared his bread with her, and the found family had grown up around that single act like a pearl around grit.

"Flour," he said, setting it down. "Don't ask how."

"I never ask how," Petra said. "It's one of my finest qualities."

"Bramblewick sent bread too. He says the swan pastry was 'an act of vandalism against art' and you're banned from the swans."

"I *saved* the swans. There was arsenic in—"

She stopped. Not because of the words. Because of the taste.

It was faint, threading through the market's rich stew of smells — fried dough, eels, woodsmoke, the river — but it was there. Cold. Mineral. Snowmelt off sunless stone. The same wrongness from the honey-cake, here, in the Nightmarket, drifting like smoke.

Petra turned slowly, scanning the lane. Lanterns. Faces. A juggler. Two Wardens at the far end of the street, their detection-plates dull and dark, finding nothing — or finding something and not knowing what it was.

"Petra?" Tam's hand light on her shoulder. "You've gone white as flour yourself."

"Bad oyster at breakfast," she lied, and hated how easily the lie came, and hated more that it was the second lie she'd told about the same cold taste in one day.

Sefa was watching her with narrowed eyes. Sefa, who had known her since they were both gutter-thin and had a genius for spotting Petra's lies the way sharks spotted blood. But she said nothing, which was its own kind of message.

The market carried on around them. Pip sold a luck-knot to a dockworker and inflated its price on the grounds that it was "extra lucky, been tested." The eel man sang. Somewhere a fiddle started up, badly, with enthusiasm.

And the cold mineral taste lingered in the air like a guest who would not leave, fainter now, fading — but present, unmistakable, *wrong*.

* * *

Later, when the lanterns burned low and the little ones were asleep in the loft above the stall — Pip sprawled like a starfish, young Ferro clutching a wooden horse he'd charmed himself, badly, with a knot he'd tied backwards — Petra sat on the step with Tam and split the last of Bramblewick's bread.

"You were going to say something earlier," Tam said. "At the stall. About the swans."

"Was I?"

"You stopped mid-word. You do that when something scares you. You did it when the Warden patrol changed routes last spring, and you did it the day the tax collectors came, and you're doing it now."

Petra tore her piece of bread and said nothing.

"Eleven years," Tam said mildly. "You think I don't know your tells? You taught me half of them, back when teaching me your tells seemed like a good idea."

"It seemed like a good idea because you were a scrawny brat who'd die of curiosity otherwise."

"And look at me now. Enormous. Curiosity intact." He bumped her shoulder with his. "What scared you today, Petra?"

The cold taste rose in her memory, patient and mineral and deep. She thought about telling him everything — the honey-cake, the arsenic dosed for a queen, the stranger thing beneath it, the way it had followed her home across half the city like a scent on her coat.

But telling Tam meant telling him about the magic she could taste, and that door had stayed shut for eleven years, and doors shut that long grow heavy.

"Somebody tried to poison the queen," she said finally. "That part's simple. Arsenic in the honey-cake. They'll catch whoever did it, or they won't; either way it's palace business."

"And the other part?"

"What other part?"

"The part that made you stop mid-word." He said it gently, without pushing, which was Tam's gift — he could put a question down in front of you and walk away from it, leaving it yours to pick up or not.

Petra looked out at the Nightmarket, at the lanterns guttering low, at the dark mouth of the lane where the Wardens had stood with their blank plates. Above the rooftops, the stars were out in their usual scatter — she counted them without meaning to, an old habit, the way some people counted sheep. Forty-one visible from the step. There had been forty-one for as long as she could remember counting.

"There was something in the poison," she said at last. "Under it. A taste I've never had before, and I've had everything, Tam. Everything the books list and half of what they don't. This was—" she searched for it, "—cold. Deep. Like the mountain got into the cake somehow. Like something under the mountain."

Tam had gone very still beside her.

It was a small stillness. Anyone else would have missed it — a man pausing to work a crumb loose from his teeth, perhaps. But Petra had spent eleven years learning his tells the way he'd learned hers, and this was a new one, and new tells meant new secrets, and Tam had never once had a secret from her in eleven years.

"Under the mountain," he repeated.

"It's probably nothing. Bad water in the glaze, some mineral leached in from somewhere. I'll run it down tomorrow." She laughed, and heard how thin it came out. "Listen to me. Poisoned queen, mystery flavor, and I'm worried about *glaze*."

"Petra." His voice was careful. Careful in a way that raised every hair on her arms. "When you find whatever it is — whatever's under the mountain, or in the cake, or wherever — you'll tell me? Before you go chasing it?"

"Since when do I chase things?"

"Since always. Since the day I met you, you've been chasing trouble with your teeth bared and your sleeves rolled up. It's your best quality and your worst one and I've never once managed to talk you out of it." He smiled, but the smile didn't reach all the way up. "So promise me. Whatever it is. We face it together. That's the rule."

"That's the rule," Petra agreed.

Above them, the forty-one stars burned on, steady and silent, and if any of them flickered — if the darkness between them seemed, for half a heartbeat, deeper than it ought to be — Petra told herself it was tiredness, and lantern-smoke, and nothing at all.

She was very good at telling herself things.

She had eleven years of practice.

But that night, lying in the loft with the children's breath rising and falling around her, she dreamed of a table set deep in stone, and a place laid for one more guest, and a voice like cold water saying her name — not *Petra*, but something older, something that sounded like a debt coming due.
