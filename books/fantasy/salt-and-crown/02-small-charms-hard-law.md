## 2. Small Charms, Hard Law

The Nightmarket woke the way it always did: grudgingly, in pieces, like an old dog deciding whether today was worth the trouble.

Petra had her stall open before the lamplighters finished their rounds, which was a small joke in itself — the Nightmarket only truly lived after dark, but the morning trade was where the money hid. Dockworkers coming off the tide shift. Cooks buying charms against burns before they lit their ovens. One nervous clerk who came every tenth day for a focus-knot he swore was for his sister, though his ink-stained fingers said otherwise.

She laid out her wares with the care of a surgeon and the conscience of a pickpocket. Luck-knots braided from river-grass and knotted three times against bad tides. Salt-sachets stitched by Sefa, who could not charm so much as a sneeze but could sew a seam straight enough to make a lie look respectable. Warming stones that were, honestly, just stones — but warm stones held near a fire, and belief did the rest.

The magic was in the knots. The rest was theater, and Petra had learned long ago that people paid better for theater.

"Blackwood." A shadow fell across her table, broad as a door. Bren, the dockworker, cap in his fists, looking like a man who'd already lost the argument with himself about whether to come. "You got any of those — you know."

"Those," Petra agreed solemnly. "I have several of those. Would you like the ones against drowning, or the ones against your foreman?"

Bren's mouth twitched despite himself. "Foreman says the *Merrow's Pride* is cursed. Three wrecks this season on her runs, and old Hask swears he saw something under the water off Gull Point."

"Old Hask also swears he saw a mermaid marry the harbormaster's son, and I was at that wedding. It was lovely. There was cake." She pulled a luck-knot from beneath the counter — the good ones, triple-braided, each knot tied while she whispered the charm into the fibers. She could taste the magic going in, faint and bright, like lemon zest on the tongue. "Three coppers. And Bren — a knot keeps bad luck off. It does not keep stupidity off. If the foreman orders her out in a gale, no braid in the world will save you. Jump early and swim well."

"That's not very charming of you."

"I'm a charm-maker, not a miracle-worker. Miracles cost more."

He laughed, counted out the coppers, and tucked the knot inside his shirt where it sat over his heart like a second heartbeat. Petra watched him go and felt the small, stubborn warmth she always felt when a charm left her hands. Somewhere out there, a bit of brightness she'd made would stand between a man and the dark water. Whatever else her life was — and lately it had been tasting arsenic for a queen and pretending her tongue didn't ache — it was *this* too.

Then she saw the grey cloaks at the mouth of Lantern Row, and the warmth curdled.

* * *

Wardens moved through the Nightmarket the way cold moved through a house — slowly, inevitably, finding every gap.

There were four of them this time, walking in pairs, and they weren't hurrying. That was the worst part. A raid you could survive; a stroll meant they were hunting. Their badges caught the lamplight: the crowned mountain sigil of the Crown Wardens, licensed detectors of unlicensed magic. Each one wore a thin silver band at the wrist — a truth-taste bracer, the kind that hummed when active charms passed within arm's reach.

Petra's hands went still over her table. Then, deliberately, she picked up a warming stone and began polishing it, because a woman polishing stones had nothing to hide and everything to sell.

The problem was Sefa.

Her friend was three stalls down, hawking salt-sachets to a fishwife, and Sefa had exactly two modes: invisible and magnificent. Today she had chosen magnificent, which meant she was mid-performance, arms wide, telling the fishwife a story about a sachet that had once saved a captain's ship from a kraken —

— and the story was fine, the sachet was harmless, but Sefa was wearing her work-apron, and in the pocket of that apron, wrapped in oilcloth because Sefa believed in being prepared, were six of Petra's finished luck-knots awaiting delivery to the harbor stall.

Six live charms, walking toward a Warden's wrist-band like moths toward a lamp.

*Sefa,* Petra thought, with the particular despair of a woman who has told someone the same thing four hundred times, *if you value your skin, walk away.*

Sefa, who had never valued her skin above a good audience, turned directly toward the Wardens and raised her voice. "And the kraken, mind you, was *enormous*—"

Petra moved. There was no decision in it, just the way water moves downhill. She swept up her basket, ducked through the gap between the candle-seller's table and the wall, and came out behind Sefa just as the nearest Warden's bracer gave a soft, silvery chime.

Everyone within ten feet pretended not to hear it. Everyone failed.

"Charm-work." The Warden was young, with the earnest, unhappy face of a man who'd joined the Wardens to fight hexes and blood-magic and instead spent his days confiscating grandmothers' knitting spells. "Whose?"

"Mine," said Petra, stepping forward before Sefa's mouth could open, because Sefa's mouth opening was how disasters became catastrophes. "Licensed." She produced the paper from her bodice — the license that cost her half a month's earnings every season and read, in the Crown's own fussy script, *PETRA BLACKWOOD, CHARM-WRIGHT, CLASS IV (MINOR), PERMITTED: KNOTS, SACHETS, SALTS. PROHIBITED: ALL ELSE.*

The Warden examined it. His bracer chimed again, politely, like a bird asking for crumbs.

"These aren't hers," he said, nodding at Sefa's apron pocket.

"They're mine too. She's my runner. Delivery girl." Petra smiled the smile she used on queens' chamberlains — pleasant, unremarkable, forgettable. "You're welcome to count them. Six knots, Class IV, all tagged." They were, in fact, all tagged, because Petra had survived this long by assuming the law would eventually come calling and preparing her paperwork accordingly. "Unless Class IV requires an escort now? I'll want that in writing. My lawyer is a seagull, but he's very aggressive."

Something that might have been amusement flickered behind the young Warden's unhappiness. He counted the knots, matched them to the tags, and stepped back. "Keep them off the open street. New writs came down from the palace this week. After what happened at the border—" He stopped himself, jaw tightening. "Just keep them covered."

"What happened at the border?" Petra asked, light as anything.

But the Wardens were already moving on, grey cloaks swallowing them into the crowd, and the young one didn't look back.

Sefa let out a breath that seemed to deflate her entire body. "*Enormous* kraken," she muttered. "I had them. I had the whole row eating out of my hand, and then you come barreling in with paperwork like some kind of — of *bureaucrat*—"

"You had a chime and six felonies in your apron."

"They were *your* felonies. That's completely different. That's practically honest work." But Sefa's grin faded as she looked down the row where the grey cloaks had gone. "Four of them, Pet. Last month it was two. And that thing he said — new writs. Pip heard the lamplighters talking. They're doubling the patrols on the mountain roads. Nobody's saying why."

Petra looked north, past the rooftops, to where the mountain stood over the city like a patient animal. The border wards ran along its spine — the great wards, the old ones, older than the crown, older than the city, humming day and night with a magic so vast that even Petra's tongue couldn't find the edge of it. You didn't taste the wards themselves. You tasted their absence, the way you noticed silence after a bell stopped ringing.

She tasted them now, faintly, from across the whole city. Still there. Still singing.

But underneath the song, so deep she might have imagined it, there was something else. Cold. Mineral. Like licking a stone at the bottom of a well.

It hadn't been there last week.

"Pet?" Sefa said. "Your face went strange."

"It always goes strange. It's a strange face." She bundled the knots back into Sefa's apron, patted the pocket once — *be careful* — and tried to shake the taste of deep stone off her tongue. It stayed, like a memory of winter. "Go on. Harbor run. And Sefa — if the Wardens come again, be boring. Be *magnificently* boring. Save the kraken for Tuesdays."

"Tuesdays are slow," Sefa conceded. "Fine. But I'm telling it better next time. The kraken had a *hat*."

* * *

Tam was waiting at the kitchen when she got home, which should have been the best part of her day and had been, for eleven years, without exception.

Their kitchen sat above the cooper's shop on Rope Lane, one room and a stove and a window that looked out on nothing in particular, and it belonged to all of them the way the market did — by use, by habit, by nobody having anywhere better to be. Tam had bread rising. Tam always had bread rising; it was less a hobby than a personality. The smell of it filled the room like a hug you could eat.

He was kneading when she came in, sleeves rolled, flour to his elbows, and he smiled at her the way he always did — easy, warm, like she was a fire he'd been waiting all day to sit beside.

"There's my famous poison-eater," he said. "How was the queen's breakfast?"

"Eggs. Good eggs. No arsenic, which frankly was a disappointment after Tuesday's excitement." She hung up her coat. "How was the bakery?"

"Floury." A pause, half a beat too long. "Quiet."

Petra had spent years learning to read flavors no one else could detect — a grain of bitterness in honey, a whisper of iron in wine, the difference between a poison and its antidote by the shape of the aftertaste. She had assumed the skill was in her tongue. It wasn't. It was in her, everywhere, and it worked on people too.

Tam's voice had an aftertaste just then. Faint. Wrong. Like sweetness laid over something buried.

She didn't say anything. She washed her hands and started cutting onions, because some conversations needed a knife in hand, and sat with him in the comfortable clatter of ordinary evening — bread, onions, the kettle, Pip thundering up the stairs to announce that she had sold *nine* sachets and only lied about two of them. The room filled. The family assembled itself around the stove the way it did every night, Sefa arguing with Pip about the kraken's hat, old Marta dozing in the good chair with her knitting abandoned in her lap.

And through all of it, Tam kept glancing at the window.

Not the street. The window. North-facing. Toward the mountain.

After Pip was shooed off to bed and Sefa had gone down to lock the stall, after Marta had shuffled off muttering about yarn, the kitchen was theirs alone, and the bread came out of the oven golden and perfect, and Tam set it on the board and didn't cut it. Just stood there, looking at it, his hands flat on either side like a man guarding something or surrendering to it.

"Petra." His voice had gone quiet in a way she hated. Quiet wasn't Tam. Tam was loud bread and loud laughter and loud arguments about whether salt belonged in porridge (it did; he was wrong). "I have to tell you something, and you're going to want to fix it, and you can't. So I need you to just — listen first. All the way to the end. Can you do that?"

"No," Petra said honestly. "But I'll pretend, and we can both agree later that I did."

That startled a laugh out of him, brief and real, and then it died, and he sat down across from her, and the lamplight caught his face and she saw how tired he was. Not flour-tired. Deeper. The kind of tired that lives behind the eyes.

"I've been meeting someone," he said. "For months. Someone from below the mountain."

The words landed strangely, the way a familiar word lands when it's suddenly used wrong. *Below the mountain.* There was nothing below the mountain. There were mines, exhausted and sealed; there were cellars; there were stories. Old stories, the kind the Nightmarket told after midnight when the wine was cheap — the Undercourt, the pale king, the tables set with food that cost more than coin. Stories for children and drunkards.

"Below the mountain," Petra repeated carefully.

"You know the stories. They're true. Some of them." Tam's hands wouldn't stay still; he wiped them on his apron, folded them, unfolded them. "They come up sometimes. Through the old toll-gates. They're — not what the stories say. Mostly. They're people who made bargains a long time ago and are still paying them off. And one of them found me. Last autumn. At the harbor."

"Found you how? Why you?"

"Because I owed something." He said it fast, like ripping off a bandage. "Before you and me and the market — before any of it — my mother made a bargain. To keep me alive when I was born. I was supposed to die, Pet. First breath, done. And she went to the toll-gate and traded, and I lived, and the debt sat there for twenty-six years gathering interest, and now—" He stopped. Started again, quieter. "Now they've come to collect. Or talk about collecting. We've been meeting. Negotiating. There's a woman — her name is Vess — she's not cruel, she's just—" He rubbed his face. "She's tired. They're all so tired down there."

The onions sat forgotten on the board. Petra's hands were very still, because if she let them move she didn't know what they'd do.

"How long," she said, "have you been carrying this alone?"

"Petra—"

"*How long.*"

"Since autumn." Eight months. Eight months of shared bread and shared roof and shared family, and he had been walking down to meet the dark under the mountain and coming home and smiling at her, and she — who could taste a lie in honeyed wine from across a room — had tasted nothing, because Tam didn't lie. He just carried things until they crushed him. He always had. It was the one flavor she should have known by heart.

"Why tell me now?" she asked. "What changed?"

He looked at the window again. North. The mountain stood black against the stars, and above it the stars burned on, indifferent and bright, and something in Tam's face when he looked at them made her stomach drop before he said a word.

"The negotiations ended," he said. "Last week. Vess came to tell me the terms were — settled. Above her pay grade, she said. Decided higher up." His voice cracked, just slightly, on *higher*. "Petra, whatever happens in the next few days — whatever happens at the wards, in the city, anywhere — I need you to know it isn't your fault, and it isn't Sefa's, and it isn't anyone in this house. And I need you to know that these eleven years were the best part of my life, and I'd make the same bargain my mother did, a hundred times, to get them."

"Tam." Her voice came out low and steady, which surprised her, because inside she was suddenly all falling. "What did they settle? What are the terms?"

He picked up the bread knife. Cut two slices, thick, the way she liked. Buttered them with hands that shook only a little. Handed her one, and for a moment they just ate, standing in their crooked kitchen, in the smell of warm crust, like any other night of eleven years.

"I can't tell you that part," he said softly. "If I don't know it, they can't take it out of you. And they *can*, Pet. Down there, knowing is owning." He smiled at her, and it was the old smile, the real one, laid over something breaking. "Eat your bread. It's a good loaf. I finally got the rye ratio right."

Outside, far to the north, so faint that Petra would spend the rest of her life wondering if she'd truly heard it, the mountain rang — one clear note, like a struck bell, like the first syllable of a word the world had forgotten.

Tam didn't seem to hear it. But he closed his eyes, just for a moment, as if counting something down.

"One more loaf tomorrow," he said. "Promise me you'll be home for it."
