## 3. The Night the Stars Went Out

The trouble with a city built on top of a mountain full of old bargains, Sefa liked to say, was that eventually somebody came to collect.

Petra thought about that saying at half past eleven that night, standing in her kitchen with a cup of chicory going cold in her hand and Tam's laughter drifting down from the loft where he was supposed to be sleeping but was very obviously not.

"You're humming," she called up through the floorboards.

"I'm not."

"You're humming the dock-song. The one about the mermaid who owes money." She took a sip of the chicory and grimaced. It tasted of burnt grain and, faintly, of iron — which meant either the kettle needed scouring or someone in the building had been handling coins with unwashed hands, and given that their household consisted entirely of market orphans, it was almost certainly both. "Nobody hums a debt song unless they're thinking about debts."

A pause. Then his face appeared upside-down over the edge of the loft, framed by the curtain he'd nailed up there two winters ago and never once washed. He had flour in his hair. He always had flour in his hair; it was less an accident than a personal aesthetic.

"Maybe I'm just in a good mood," he said.

"Tam."

"Petra."

"Where were you this afternoon? Sefa says you vanished off Pier Nine like smoke."

Another pause — a beat too long, the kind of pause that has furniture in it. "Errands," he said, and smiled, and his smile had always been able to carry him across most gaps in conversation, but tonight Petra's tongue caught something underneath it. Not a poison. Nothing so simple. Just a wrongness, thin as a hair on the tongue: cold, mineral, deep. Like licking the inside of a well.

She'd been tasting that wrongness for three days now. In the rainwater. In the bread from Yeva's stall. Once, horribly, in her own spit when she woke at dawn with her heart hammering for no reason she could name.

"Come down and finish your chicory," she said. "It's terrible. Misery loves company."

"Love you too," he said, and did not come down, and she let him go because that was what family did — you let people keep their secrets until they were ready, and you kept the kettle warm in the meantime.

She would regret that kindness for a long time afterward. But midnight was still half an hour away, and the world was still whole, and the chicory really was terrible.

* * *

The bell-sound came without warning, which was the first impossible thing about it.

Border wards didn't make noise. Everyone knew that. They were the quietest magic in the kingdom — a hum below hearing, a pressure behind the eyes, the reason travelers crossing into Vellmark felt their teeth ache for a day and called it *mountain weather*. You lived your whole life inside them the way you lived inside your own skin, and you forgot they were there.

At midnight exactly, every ward from the Salt Coast to the Greymarch fell at once, and the sound they made falling was the sound of a bell the size of the sky being struck once, cleanly, by something patient.

Petra was asleep. She woke already upright, heart slamming, ears ringing, and for one disorienting moment she thought the palace kitchens had exploded again — old Marla's sugar work was notorious — and then she registered the light.

There wasn't any.

Not lamplight-gone-out dark, not storm-dark, not even the soft charcoal dark of a cloudy night. This was a darkness with *weight* to it, pressing against the window like a hand over a mouth. And where the stars should have been — she knew their patterns the way she knew the market lanes, had navigated by them since she was six and feral — there was nothing. A sky wiped clean. As if the night itself had been licked empty.

"Oh," she said aloud, to nobody, in the small reasonable voice people use when the unreasonable arrives. "Oh, that's not good."

Downstairs, the house erupted. Pip came barreling out of the alcove she slept in, dragging her blanket like a snail's shell, and collided with Sefa coming the other way, and both of them started talking at once, which under normal circumstances meant an argument about whose turn it was to scrub the stew pot and tonight meant pure animal fear wearing the costume of bickering.

"—heard it in my *teeth*, Petra, I heard it in my—"

"—the lamps won't catch, I tried, the wick just *lies down*—"

"Where's Tam?" That was Wren, the eldest after Petra, appearing in the doorway with the poker held like a spear, which was sweet and would have been useless against anything worth spearing.

"He was in the loft," Petra said, and went up the stairs two at a time, and threw back the curtain, and found the loft empty.

Not recently-empty. Not slipped-out-for-a-walk empty. Empty the way a room is empty when its occupant has been *removed*. His blanket lay folded — Tam did not fold things, Tam's idea of tidiness was shoving laundry toward the wall — and on top of the folded blanket sat a charm she'd made him two years ago, braided horsehair and river-clay and a pinch of salt from the queen's own table, a luck-knot strong enough to turn a knife or find a lost coin. He'd carried it every day since. He teased her about it constantly and checked its clasp constantly more.

It was split in two. Cleanly. Not broken — *cut*, along the knot-line, the way a contract is cut when both parties have signed.

The halves were still warm.

Petra stood in the dark holding them and felt the vertigo open up beneath her like a trapdoor. The stars gone. The wards gone. Her brother gone, and the only witness a severed charm that smelled — she raised it to her lips before she could stop herself, taster's instinct, taste everything — of cold stone and old water and something underneath that made her stomach clench: a sweetness. Deep, patient, hungry sweetness, like honey left in a tomb.

"Petra." Sefa in the doorway, white-faced, holding a lamp that burned with a flame the color of nothing at all. "There are riders at the gate. Palace colors. They're asking for you by name."

* * *

The palace was screaming in the polite way palaces scream.

No one ran — running was for servants and emergencies, and the court had not yet decided which category this fell into — but everyone moved very fast indeed, and the corridors between the queen's wing and the west stairs filled with a tide of silk and panic. Somewhere below, actual servants were actually running, carrying buckets of sand because the lamps wouldn't hold flame properly, sputtering and guttering like frightened animals. The air tasted of smoke and tallow and, beneath it, that same mineral coldness Petra had been catching for days, now thick enough to chew.

The guards at the queen's door didn't ask why she'd come. They'd been sent for her; word traveled faster than explanation. One of them looked at her — a food-taster, summoned at midnight, in her coat with the burn holes — with an expression she recognized. It was the look people gave the gallows cart. *Better you than me.*

Queen Maren was standing at the window.

That was the first shock. Queens sat; queens received; queens arranged themselves into compositions. Maren stood with her back to the room, hands clasped behind her, looking up at a sky with no stars in it, and she did not turn around when the door opened, and Petra understood suddenly that she had never once seen the queen be still.

"You tasted it," Maren said. Not a question. "Before tonight. Don't lie to me, Blackwood, I haven't the patience left."

"The last three days." Petra's voice came out steadier than she deserved. "In the water. In the bread. Cold. Mineral. Like—" she searched, "—like the taste of the space under a grave."

"Yes." The queen turned. In the guttering lamplight her face was ten years older than it had been that morning, and Petra realized with a lurch that the woman had known this was coming. Had perhaps been counting the nights. "And tonight?"

"Tonight the wards broke. Tonight my brother is gone. Tonight there's a sweetness under everything that I don't have a name for and I've swallowed enough of the world to name most things." She held up the two halves of the charm. "This was cut, not broken. Your Majesty. What did you do?"

The silence stretched long enough that Petra heard, distantly, a bell in the lower city begin to ring the alarm, then another, then a dozen, an ugly ragged chorus.

"Three years ago," Maren said, "the border wards began to fail. Not weaken — *fail*, the way a heart fails. The mountain beneath us is hollow, Blackwood. It has been hollow longer than my dynasty has existed, and something lives in the hollow, and the wards were never ours. We inherited them. We maintained them the way you maintain a fence around a thing you've agreed not to disturb." She crossed to her desk, unhurried, and lifted a ledger — a real one, leather-bound, iron-clasped, nothing like Corvane's kitchen tallies — and set it down without opening it. "When they began to fail, we negotiated. An extension. A renewal of terms."

"What terms?"

"A soul." The queen's jaw tightened. "One soul, freely delivered, in exchange for thirty years of sky. The broker swore the debt would fall due at term's end, not before. Three years ago the payment was made. Or so I was told." Her eyes went to the split charm in Petra's hands, and something moved behind them — guilt, maybe, or its expensive cousin, plausible deniability finally collapsing. "I am told many things by many people. I am learning, tonight, how much of it was true."

"Whose soul?"

"Blackwood—"

"*Whose.*"

The queen looked at her for a long moment. Outside, the alarm bells tangled themselves into a single sustained note, and somewhere in the palace a dog began to howl, and the darkness at the window pressed closer, attentive, like a guest leaning in at dinner.

"The crown does not sell its own," Maren said at last, which was the most elaborate non-answer Petra had ever tasted, and she had once eaten a soup designed to convince a duke his rival was a cannibal. "But names travel. Names get *overheard*. And the Undercourt—" she said the word carefully, the way you handle a blade by the flat, "—does not forgive a misdelivered debt. It collects what it can reach."

Tam. Tam who hummed debt songs. Tam who'd been meeting someone from below the mountain. Tam whose charm had been cut along the contract-line while the wards fell like a struck bell, all in the same midnight minute, because of course it was the same minute — the debt had come due, and the mountain had reached up and taken what it had been told it could take.

The vertigo came back, worse. Petra gripped the doorframe.

"Your Majesty," she said, when she could trust her voice, "I want access to every record of that negotiation. Every name, every signature, every broker. I want Corvane's ledgers. And I want it tonight, because whatever took my brother is not finished — I can taste it. It's still hungry."

For the first time all night, Queen Maren looked afraid — not of the starless sky, not of the hollow mountain, but of the small poisoned woman in the doorway who had just used the words *still hungry* in a tone usually reserved for battle plans.

"Corvane's ledgers," the queen repeated slowly. "Why Corvane?"

"Because three days ago," Petra said, "his handwriting started tasting like fear. And because somebody in this palace signed my brother's name, and stewards sign things. That's what stewards are *for*."

She turned and walked out into the smoke-filled corridors, past the running servants and the guttering lamps, past guards who stared and courtiers who pretended not to, holding the two halves of a cut charm in her fist like a compass needle. Behind her the queen said something to her ladies — she caught the words *follow her* and *quietly* — and ahead of her the palace unspooled into the dark, and above it all hung a sky with nothing in it, vast and blank and waiting, like a plate set before a meal that had not yet arrived.

Petra put the charm halves in her pocket, where they lay against her palm, still faintly warm.

Whatever had taken Tam had left the pieces behind on purpose. She was sure of it. Debts wanted to be found. Contracts wanted to be read.

Well. Reading was what tongues were for.
