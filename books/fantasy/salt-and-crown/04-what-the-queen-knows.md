## 4. What the Queen Knows

They took me up through the palace the way you carry something breakable — two guards in front, two behind, and nobody speaking, as if silence were a bandage we could all agree on.

The corridors were full of smoke that wasn't smoke. That was the first thing I understood, walking through it. I'd spent three years learning to read what touched my tongue, and somewhere along the way my skin had learned to listen too. This haze had no taste at all. It sat on the air like a held breath, like a room full of people who have just heard bad news and are waiting to see who cries first.

Ward-smoke, I thought. The wards are still dying, and this is what their death looks like from inside.

The queen's receiving room had been a garden once — Maren's grandmother had grown winter roses under glass, and the trellises were still bolted to the walls, bare and patient. Now the windows were shuttered with iron and the only light came from a ring of lamps around a single chair, and Queen Maren sat in that chair looking smaller than a queen should look.

She looked, in fact, like a woman who had not slept since midnight, which was fair, because none of us had.

"Petra Blackwood," she said. "Sit down."

"Your Majesty." I didn't sit. Old habit. Tasters stand until the food is proven safe, and I had never in my life tasted anything less safe than this conversation was about to be.

"I said sit." There was no cruelty in it. There was barely any command. It was the voice of someone holding a door shut against a flood and asking for help holding it. I sat.

A servant brought tea. I tasted it before she did — that was the ritual, that was my whole life in one gesture, cup lifted, breath drawn over the surface, tongue to porcelain. Chamomile, honey, a thread of valerian for sleep she wouldn't take. And beneath it, faint as a fingerprint on glass: that cold mineral wrongness. The same taste I'd found under the arsenic yesterday morning. The same taste that had been sitting on my tongue ever since, like a stone I couldn't spit out.

I set the cup down. "The tea's clean," I said. "The air isn't."

Maren laughed — one short, cracked note, nothing like humor. "No," she said. "It isn't."

* * *

"You were there when the wards fell," she said. "Or near enough. Tell me what you felt."

I told her. The bell-sound with no bell, rolling up out of the mountain's bones. The dark arriving not like night falls but like a lid coming down. The way every charm in the Nightmarket had gone quiet at once — Sefa said her stall of luck-knots had hung dead and gray as dried seaweed until dawn. I told her about Tam's empty room. About the charm I'd made him, split clean down its center seam, both halves laid side by side on his pillow with a care that frightened me more than violence would have. Violence is honest. Whoever had done that had wanted him to know he was taken, and wanted *me* to know it too.

The queen listened with her hands folded, and when I finished she was quiet long enough that I could hear the ward-smoke pressing against the shutters.

"Three years ago," she said at last, "the border wards began to fail."

I blinked. "Fail? They're crown-work. They've stood for—"

"Two hundred years, yes. Everyone believes they stand because our grandmothers built them well. Grandmothers build nothing that lasts two hundred years, Petra. Wards are not walls. They're more like... appetites. They must be fed." She turned her cup without drinking. "There is a court beneath the mountain. Older than the crown. Older than the city, I think. When the wards were raised, a bargain was struck — the Undercourt would lend its hunger to the border, and in exchange, once each generation, it would be paid."

"Paid what?"

"What do you think?" Her eyes came up, and they were terrible and tired. "Souls."

The lamp-flames leaned, all together, though no door had opened.

"The payments were always small," she went on, faster now, the words tumbling like water over a broken stair. "Criminals. The condemned. Men and women whose names were already struck from the rolls — the Undercourt doesn't care about guilt, only about substance. A soul is a soul. My grandmother called it the Tithe of Shadows and put it in no ledger anyone can find, and for two hundred years the arrangement held, and the stars stayed lit, and everyone slept soundly believing the sky was free."

"And three years ago?"

"Three years ago the Undercourt came to collect early. The wards were thinning — the old bargain was aging, the way everything ages — and they demanded a payment of a different weight. Not a criminal. Not a stranger." Her knuckles whitened on the cup. "Someone bound to the crown by love or service. Someone whose soul would be... nourishing."

The cold mineral taste flooded my mouth so hard I nearly gagged. I thought of arsenic. I thought of how poison always announces itself eventually, no matter how cleverly it's hidden, because a body knows what is trying to kill it.

"Who," I said.

And here is the thing I will remember until I die: she hesitated. One heartbeat, maybe two. Long enough to lie. Long enough to choose a softer truth, or a harder one, or none at all.

"Tam," she said. "Tamlin Ashe. Your brother."

The word *brother* landed like a coin dropped down a well — I heard it falling for a long time before it hit anything.

"No," I said. It wasn't an argument. It was just the sound my mouth made while the rest of me caught up. "Tam's not — he's not noble blood, he's not sworn service, he's a baker. He kneads dough. He sings off-key. He—" My voice did something humiliating and I stopped it with my teeth.

"He was chosen precisely because he is those things," Maren said quietly. "The Undercourt specified. A soul unmarked by ambition. A soul that loved and was loved. They wanted something *sweet*, Petra. They have eaten criminals for two centuries and they told us they had developed... a craving."

"That's monstrous."

"Yes."

"They asked for a specific man. By name."

Another hesitation. Smaller this time. I was learning to read her hesitations the way I read flavors — the bitter edge beneath the honey.

"The request came through an intermediary," she said. "A name written on a card, delivered to this room, sealed with the Undercourt's mark. We were given a season to comply, and a promise of what would happen if we didn't. They showed us the border. Every town from here to the sea going dark, every ward unraveling like wet rope. They showed us what comes through when the wards fall — not armies, Petra. *Hunger*. Things that eat light and memory and the warmth out of bread." She set the cup down with a click that sounded very loud. "I signed the order. I will not pretend otherwise. I chose one life over ten thousand, and I would choose it again, and I will burn in whatever afterlife keeps accounts for queens. But I did not know him. I never saw his face. The name arrived; the name was paid. That is all I knew until tonight, when my steward told me the taster's brother was missing and I felt the floor open under this room."

"Corvane told you."

"Corvane brokered the delivery." She said it flatly, factually, and something in my chest went very still and cold. "He handled the... arrangements. Three years ago and again now, when the second payment fell due."

"Second payment." My voice came out strange. Distant. Like someone else's. "You said one soul."

"The bargain bought three years. Three years is over. The wards fell at midnight because the debt is due again, and this time—" She stopped. Started again. "This time they took him directly. No order signed, no season granted. They simply reached up and collected what they considered theirs. Which means either the bargain has changed hands below, or someone above has renegotiated terms I know nothing about."

*Or so she claims,* whispered a small cold voice in me that I didn't recognize and didn't much like. *Or so she claims.*

I looked at my queen — this woman who had patted my shoulder at midwinter feast, who had once sent me a jar of salted plums with a note saying *the taster deserves sweetness too*, whom I had trusted with my tongue and therefore with my life, which is the deepest trust there is — and I realized I was tasting her words the way I tasted everything now. Searching for the poison underneath.

I hated myself for it. I did it anyway.

"Your Majesty," I said carefully, "you're telling me the crown sold my brother to a court of hungry ghosts, and that you learned his identity only tonight."

"I'm telling you everything I know, which I am painfully aware is not everything." She rose and walked to the shuttered window, and for a moment she looked less like a queen than like a woman standing in front of a locked door she'd forgotten she'd nailed shut herself. "There is a hole in this story the size of the mountain, and I cannot see into it. The name on that card three years ago — where did it come from? Who chose Tam? Why him, why then, why a baker with no title and no enemies? Contracts with the Undercourt are written in blood and iron and they do not permit ambiguity. Someone *knew* him. Someone named him. And whoever that was—"

"—is still inside these walls," I finished.

She turned. For the first time that night, she looked directly at me, and I saw that behind the exhaustion there was steel, and behind the steel there was fear, and behind the fear — this surprised me — there was grief. Real grief, unqueenly, unguarded.

"Yes," she said. "And I am surrounded by people whose loyalty I purchased with titles and pensions, which means it bends exactly as far as the gold does. But you." She crossed back toward me, and the lamplight caught her face. "You loved him. You have a tongue that reads truth the way priests read scripture. And you have spent three years swallowing death for this crown, which means you owe it nothing, which makes your word the only kind I can trust."

"That's a flattering way to say I'm expendable, Majesty."

Something flickered across her face — pain, quickly mastered. "Yes," she said simply. "It is. I won't dress it. I am asking you to walk into a nest of vipers because you are the one person in this palace who can feel them strike." She knelt then — the Queen of Cindralon, kneeling on her own floor in front of a market orphan in a taster's plain coat — and took both my hands in hers, and her grip was cold. "Find who brokered it. Find the hand behind the name. Do it quietly, do it fast, because the Undercourt has taken its payment and the wards are down and I give us a month, perhaps two, before the things beyond the border learn that the door stands open. Bring me the traitor's name and I will spend the crown itself to buy Tam back — but I cannot bargain with a knife in the dark, Petra. I need to see the blade."

For a moment I let her hold my hands. I even let myself believe her — or most of her, or enough of her. Grief wants to believe. Grief will drink anything you pour it.

Then I stood, and she released me, and I bowed the way I'd been taught in the kitchens, low and correct.

"I'll find them," I said. "But hear me, Majesty. I'm not doing this for the wards. Or the border. Or the crown."

"I know," said Queen Maren softly. "That's rather the point."

* * *

The corridor outside was still full of that tasteless smoke, and my footsteps sounded wrong in it, muffled, like walking through wool. One of my guards peeled away to escort me — a broad, kind-faced woman named Brannoc who had shared watch-duty with me often enough that we'd developed a language of shrugs.

"You look like you swallowed the whole poison cabinet," she said quietly.

"Close to it." I kept my eyes forward. "Brannoc. Three years ago. Did anything strange happen in the palace? Anything at all. A delivery. A visitor. A night nobody talks about."

She was silent for eleven steps. Twelve. Then: "There was a night the kitchens were closed. All of them, even the queen's own. Sealed, guards on the doors, orders from Corvane himself. Nobody ate palace food for two days. They told us the cellars were being fumigated." A pause. "Fumigated against what, nobody ever said. And Corvane—" She glanced sideways, lowered her voice to almost nothing. "Corvane burned something in his brazier that week. Big fire, in his office, middle of the night. Ash smelled like nothing. Like *nothing*, Petra. You know what I mean?"

I did. Nothing has a taste. Nothing has always had a taste, to me. Burned paper should taste of ink and glue and secrets. If his fire smelled of nothing, it was because magic had eaten the evidence down to the marrow.

"Thank you," I said.

"Don't thank me." Her jaw tightened. "Just find whoever did this to your boy. And Petra — when you find them—" She didn't finish. She didn't need to. Kind-faced women with watch-duty shrugs don't finish sentences like that. They just let you hear it.

* * *

My room in the tasters' wing was exactly as I'd left it, which felt obscene. Cup on the table. Cloak on the hook. A half-eaten plum going soft beside the bed. The world had ended at midnight and my plum hadn't noticed.

I sat down and made myself think, the way I made myself taste — methodically, layer by layer, refusing to flinch.

Layer one: the queen. Her story fit the facts I had, but stories that fit are the easiest kind to forge. She'd hesitated twice, and both hesitations had come when she spoke of names. *Who chose Tam?* She'd asked the question like a woman who genuinely didn't know. Or like a woman who knew exactly, and needed me to find it first so it wouldn't have to pass her lips.

Layer two: Corvane. Broker of the delivery, burner of tasteless fires, closer of kitchens. He had signed something three years ago, and he had signed something else — or failed to sign something — three days ago. And underneath all of it, the question I kept circling like a sore tooth: *how does a steward know which name to write on a card?* You cannot sell a specific soul by accident. Somewhere in the last three years, someone had looked at Tam — laughing, flour-dusted, off-key Tam — and seen currency.

Layer three, the layer I saved for last because it hurt worst: me. *Reserve collateral.* No — that thought hadn't formed yet, not fully. It was only a shadow on the stairs, a draft under a door. But I had spent three years tasting for this court, swallowing its poisons, keeping its queen alive, and not once — not once — had anyone asked what all that death was doing to the inside of me. What I was becoming. What I might be worth, someday, to something that ate souls and had developed a taste for the sweet ones.

What flavor, I wondered, does a woman become after three years of arsenic and nightshade and hemlock and worse?

I looked down at my hands. Steady. Taster's hands. Poison-keeper's hands.

"Hold on, Tam," I said to the empty room, to the split charm in my pocket, to the starless dark pressing against every shuttered window in the city. "I'm coming. And I'm bringing the recipe."

Because that was the thing the queen hadn't asked, the thing I hadn't said, the thing I would carry down every corridor of this lying palace tomorrow morning: contracts need brokers, brokers need ledgers, and ledgers — I had learned this young, in the Nightmarket, from a fence named Old Marta who could smell a forged bill of sale through three layers of wrapping — ledgers always keep more than one copy.

Somewhere in this palace was a book with my brother's name in it, written in a hand I intended to match, line by line, stroke by stroke, until I found the wrist that wrote it.

I blew out the lamp and sat in the dark, tasting the smoke, and planned.
