## 5. Ledger of Poisons

The palace kitchens woke before the palace did, which Petra had always found fitting. Kings and queens could sleep until the sun climbed their windows; bread had no such patience.

She slipped in through the scullery door at an hour when even the mice were still negotiating their shifts, and stood for a moment in the dark, breathing it in. Flour and tallow, ash and brine, the ghost of last night's roast still clinging to the spit like a lover who wouldn't leave. The kitchen smelled honest. After three days of smoke-filled corridors and whispered councils and the queen's face gone gray as old pewter, honest smells were a mercy.

"You're either a very committed thief," said a voice from the direction of the ovens, "or you've come to steal my job, in which case I'll fight you."

Petra didn't startle. Startling was for people whose hearts hadn't already been living on a ledge since the stars went out.

"Morning, Brenna."

The head cook emerged from behind the oven wall, sleeves rolled to the elbow, forearms dusted white to the wrist. She was sixty if she was a day, built like a doorframe, and she had been feeding the royal household since before Petra was born. She squinted at Petra in the ember-light.

"Blackwood. You look like something the tide refused." Brenna wiped her hands on her apron, which only redistributed the flour. "Sit down. There's porridge."

"I'm not hungry."

"Everyone says that at four in the morning, and everyone is lying." She shoved a bowl across the scarred oak table anyway, then added honey without being asked, because Brenna's love language was unsolicited sweetening. "You're here about the tasting rosters."

Petra looked up. "What makes you say that?"

"Because everyone's here about the tasting rosters. Three councilmen, two captains, and one very nervous undersecretary have all been through my kitchen since the wards fell, poking at my shelves like I keep the sky in a jar next to the saffron." Brenna sat down across from her, heavily, the way women sit when they've been standing over fires for forty years and their knees have opinions. "So. What are you actually looking for?"

Honesty, Petra decided, deserved honesty. Or at least a portion of it.

"The night the arsenic came up from the stores," she said. "The batch meant for the queen. It wasn't palace arsenic."

Brenna's eyebrows went up. "How can you possibly know that?"

"Because I tasted it." Petra turned the bowl slowly on the table, watching the honey spiral. "Palace arsenic comes out of the east stores, and it carries the smell of the cedar chests they ship it in. This was different. Colder. Mineral. Like licking a slate roof in winter." She hesitated, then said the part she'd told no one, not even Sefa. "And underneath it there was something else. Something that tasted like the mountain."

The kitchen was quiet except for the settling of coals.

"That's nonsense talk," Brenna said at last, but gently, the way you tell a child the dog hasn't run away, it's just visiting elsewhere. "Mountain doesn't have a taste."

"Everything has a taste," Petra said. "That's rather the whole of my profession."

* * *

By full light she had what she'd come for: access. Not permission — permission would require explaining herself to people whose questions she couldn't afford — but access, which in a palace was nearly the same thing and considerably cheaper.

It was Corvane who granted it, which later would feel like its own kind of joke.

The head steward received her in his office above the buttery, a room so tidy it looked less lived-in than embalmed. Ledgers stood in ranks along the walls, spines aligned with a precision that made Petra's fingers itch to knock one crooked just to see if he'd flinch. Corvane himself sat behind his desk like a man carved from good intentions and bad decisions, gray at the temples, precise at the cuffs, with the particular pallor of someone who hadn't slept well in years and had recently stopped pretending otherwise.

Since the wards fell, he looked worse. Since the wards fell, everyone looked worse, but Corvane wore it like a coat that no longer fit.

"Mistress Blackwood." He gestured to the chair opposite. "The queen informs me you're reviewing provisioning records. For... contaminants."

"For patterns," Petra said smoothly. "Poison leaves habits. Suppliers, routes, storage. If someone got arsenic past my tongue once, they'll try again, and they'll be lazy about it the second time. Lazy people reuse their roads."

Something flickered across Corvane's face — not guilt exactly. Recognition. The look of a man hearing his own handwriting read aloud.

"Thorough," he said. "As expected. The crown is fortunate in its taster." He pulled a ring of keys from his vest — too many keys, each one worn bright with handling — and set them on the desk between them. "The archive room below. Provisioning ledgers, eleven years back. Take whatever time you need." A pause, thin as parchment. "I trust you'll find nothing... surprising."

"No one ever does," Petra said, "until they do."

He almost smiled. It was the saddest thing she'd seen all week, and she had seen the stars go out.

* * *

The archive room beneath the buttery was cold in the way stone rooms are cold — patiently, eternally, indifferent to human comfort. Shelves ran floor to ceiling, crammed with ledgers bound in green buckram, each labeled in Corvane's immaculate hand: *Provisions, Year of the Double Harvest. Provisions, Year of the Long Frost.* Eleven years of the palace eating itself forward, one entry at a time.

Petra lit the lamp, rolled her sleeves, and went to work the way she did everything: systematically, hungrily, with the patience of someone who had learned that the truth, like poison, usually announced itself if you knew where to put your tongue.

She started with the obvious. The east stores' arsenic shipments — three entries over five years, all accounted for, all sealed and signed. Then the west stores, the apothecary requisitions, the imports from the Salt Coast. Nothing wrong. Nothing missing. Which was itself wrong; somewhere in this palace was a quantity of mineral-cold death that had never passed through a ledger, and unrecorded things frightened her more than recorded ones.

So she widened the net. And that was when she found the second set of books.

They weren't hidden, precisely. That was the first strange thing. They were shelved with the others, same binding, same labeling hand — but the labels read *Household Accounts, Private,* and the dates began nine years ago and stopped three years ago. Stopped, Petra noted, in the autumn three years past. The autumn, if her memory served — and her memory for dates was excellent, a side effect of a childhood spent memorizing which market stalls cheated and which ones merely lied — of the year the border wards had been *renewed.*

Everyone remembered that renewal. There had been bells. There had been a holiday. The queen had ridden the wall from gate to gate and the wards had sung so brightly the gulls had scattered off the rooftops in a white panic, and the whole city had breathed out at once.

Petra opened the oldest book and began to read.

Most of it was dullness itself — candle allocations, wine inventories, the scandalous price of pepper. But every few pages, tucked among the mundane like a hair in bread, were entries in a different style. Shorter. Denser. Written, she realized with a slow crawl up her spine, in a cipher — no. Not a cipher. A *shorthand.* A merchant's shorthand, the kind dockside factors used for contracts too valuable or too shameful to write plainly.

She couldn't read it. But she could count it. And the entries clustered around certain dates, and the largest cluster of all sat in the autumn three years ago, spanning six pages, ending in a single line written larger than the rest, pressed hard enough into the paper to dent the page beneath:

*Terms accepted. Payment scheduled. Witnessed and sealed.*

Below it, a signature.

Corvane's signature — she knew it instantly, had seen it a thousand times at the foot of tasting reports, elegant and slightly cramped, the R with its little flourish like a man raising his hat. But beside it sat a second mark, and the second mark was not a signature at all. It was a symbol: a circle bisected by a descending line, drawn in ink so black it seemed to hold no light at all.

Petra stared at it, and her tongue — her treacherous, gifted, cursed tongue — did something it had never done in the presence of mere paper.

It tasted cold.

Mineral. Slate-roof-in-winter cold. The exact flavor that had lain beneath the arsenic meant for her queen, rising off the page like breath off a well. Her mouth flooded and her stomach clenched and she gripped the edge of the shelf because the room had begun, very slightly, to tilt.

*The mountain signs its contracts,* she thought. *And Corvane held the pen.*

* * *

She should have stopped there. Any sensible person would have stopped there. She had proof enough to bring to the queen, a thread to pull, a name to offer up. Sensible people survived by carrying small truths to large powers at carefully chosen moments.

Petra had never been accused of sensibility by anyone who knew her.

She kept reading.

The middle book covered the two years after the sealing, and it was stranger. Entries in the merchant-shorthand grew sparse, then stopped altogether — but in their place appeared something else. Lists. Simple lists, names and annotations, in plain script this time, as though whoever kept them had grown careless or confident or simply tired of hiding.

Names of servants. Names of guards. A laundress with a note beside her: *weak lungs, low value.* A gate captain: *strong constitution, monitor.* Beside some names, small checkmarks. Beside others, lines struck through.

It took her four pages to understand what she was looking at, and when she understood it, she had to put the book down and stand with both hands flat on the cold shelf and breathe, because the archive room had gotten very small and very dark and the lamp flame seemed suddenly far away.

It was a ledger of people. Of souls, if she trusted the shape of it — valued, weighed, annotated like casks in a cellar. *Monitor. Low value. Monitor. Promising.*

A reserve. A cellar stock against future debt.

Her eyes went down the list, half-reading, half-dreading, the way you walk past a house where you know something terrible happened and can't help but look at the windows.

And then she saw her own name.

*Blackwood, P. Royal taster. Exceptional constitution. Long exposure, cumulative. Note:* — and here the handwriting changed, became smaller, more careful, almost tender, which made it infinitely worse — *seasoning of unusual depth. Reserve collateral. Do not draw against without authorization.*

Reserve collateral.

Petra read it three times. The words didn't improve with familiarity. *Reserve collateral.* The phrase you used for a field left fallow. For gold held against a loan. For the thing you promised the lender you'd hand over if the first payment failed.

Three years ago, someone in this palace had signed away Tam's soul to keep the wards standing — she was sure of it now, sure in her bones and in her tongue, sure with the same certainty that told her arsenic from nightshade. And in the same breath, in the same careful hand, someone had written *her* name in the margin of that bargain. Not as a person. As a contingency. As coin in escrow.

*Do not draw against without authorization.*

Authorization. Whose? Corvane's authority reached ledgers and larders, not souls. Souls required a higher sign-off. Souls required —

The thought arrived whole, unwelcome, wearing the queen's face.

Petra sat down on the cold floor with the open book in her lap and let herself feel it, because she'd learned long ago that feelings denied only came back with interest. Fear, first — old fear, market-child fear, the fear of being small in a large machine. Then anger, hot and clarifying. And beneath both, worst of all, a sick, salt-tasting grief, because Queen Maren had taken her in at fourteen, had taught her the difference between hemlock and fool's parsley, had called her *my clever girl* in front of ambassadors, and Petra had spent three years swallowing death on her behalf and calling it love.

Had it been love? Or had it been husbandry? Had the queen been raising a taster — or seasoning a payment?

*Exceptional constitution. Long exposure, cumulative. Seasoning of unusual depth.*

Every dose she'd ever taken marched past her memory in review: the rancid stew, the belladonna in the ambassador's wine, the slow lead in the glaze of a gifted cup, each one a day of fever and misery she'd borne proudly, *proudly*, believing she was armor for her queen. And all of it — all of it — had been marinating her. Making her richer. Making her *riper.*

She laughed. It came out wrong, cracked down the middle, echoing off the ledger shelves, and she clapped a hand over her mouth like a child.

Above her, faint through the floorboards, came footsteps. Measured ones. Precise ones. Crossing the buttery toward the stair that led down to the archive room.

Corvane's keys, she realized, had never left her hand. He'd given them freely. He'd *wanted* her down here. Or he'd wanted her to find something — or wanted her gone before someone else came looking — or —

The footsteps paused at the top of the stair.

Petra closed the book. Slid it back onto the shelf, spine aligned, heart hammering against her ribs like a fist on a locked door. She was holding the lamp, standing in the aisle, wearing the bland professional face of a woman doing tedious paperwork, when Corvane descended into the lamplight.

His eyes went to the shelf. To the gap where the private books sat — flush, aligned, undisturbed, unless you knew to look. His gaze lingered there a moment too long.

Then he looked at her, and his face was a masterpiece of nothing at all.

"Finding everything in order?" he asked.

Petra smiled the smile she saved for ambassadors and assassins. "Ledgers are ledgers, my lord steward. Mostly candles."

"Yes," Corvane said softly. "Mostly candles." He stood aside to let her pass, and as she climbed the stair, she felt his attention on her back like a blade laid flat between her shoulder blades — not striking. Waiting.

Outside, the morning was bright and ordinary and starless, and the city went about its business below the dead sky. Petra walked out through the buttery court with her ledger-notes safe in her sleeve and her name — her *price* — burning a hole behind her ribs.

She needed Sefa. She needed the market, the family, the people who loved her for reasons that had never once been written in anyone's books.

And she needed to decide, before nightfall, whom she could still trust — because the list of candidates had just gotten shorter by a queen.
