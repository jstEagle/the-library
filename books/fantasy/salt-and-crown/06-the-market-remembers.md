## 6. The Market Remembers

Sefa had a theory about grief: it was like a fish hook caught in your sleeve. You could ignore it while you stood still, but the moment you tried to move — really move, toward something you wanted — it pulled, and tore, and reminded you exactly where it sat.

Petra Blackwood had been moving for three days straight.

Sefa watched her from behind the luck-knot stall, pretending to re-sort a basket of knotted cords that did not need sorting. Across the Nightmarket, at the corner table of the Drowned Lantern where the lamplight was worst and the eavesdropping best, Petra sat with a stranger in a grey coat and laid out her charms one by one like a card sharp showing a losing hand.

Not selling them. Sefa knew the difference. Selling had a rhythm to it — the pitch, the pause, the little shrug that said *take it or leave it, friend*. What Petra was doing had no rhythm at all. She just put each charm down and named a price, and the prices were wrong. Too low. Low enough that Sefa's stomach clenched on every number she could lip-read.

"That's the third one tonight," said Pip, materializing at Sefa's elbow the way she always did, small and quick as a cat with an opinion. "The grey coat. He's a runner, isn't he? For the Wardens?"

"Runner, clerk, something." Sefa didn't look away. "He's not buying luck."

"Nobody's buying luck," Pip said gloomily. "Nobody's bought luck since the stars went out. Turns out people only want luck when they think the sky's on their side."

That was Pip all over — eleven years old, voice like a cracked bell since the toll-gate ward had taken her words down to a rasp, and still the sharpest observer in the market. Sefa squeezed her shoulder and kept watching.

Petra slid a fourth charm across the table. The grey-coat man turned it over, sniffed it — they always sniffed, as if magic had a smell, which Sefa supposed it did, if you were Petra — and counted out coins. Silver, from the weight of the pouch. A lot of silver.

Warden silver.

"Oh no," Sefa said quietly. "Oh, she's not bribing him. She's *bribing* him."

* * *

By the time the Lantern closed its shutters, Sefa had assembled the family in the back room of Old Marta's pie shop, which was less a room than a cave of flour sacks and stolen warmth, and which had served as parliament, chapel, and war council for the market orphans going on nine years.

Present and accounted for: Sefa, who ran the knot-stall and therefore, by ancient tradition nobody remembered starting, ran everything else. Pip, who could pick any lock in the city but couldn't shout across a street anymore. Old Marta herself, who owned the pies and denied owning the orphans while feeding all of them daily. And Brannic, who was twenty, built like a door, and currently holding a pie he hadn't bitten into because he could tell this was going to be that kind of meeting.

"Three hundred crowns," Sefa said, without preamble. "She paid a Warden archivist three hundred crowns tonight. In cash. Do you know what we clear in a good month? All stalls together?"

"A good month," Brannic said, "is forty."

"Forty-one," said Pip, "if I work the fountain crowd."

"Thank you, Pip. Forty-one. So our Petra has spent seven months of the family's income buying access to Warden records. Records about what, do we think?"

Nobody answered, because everybody already knew. The mountain. The Undercourt. The trade. Tam.

Marta wiped flour off her hands with the air of a woman preparing to say something sensible. "She's grieving," she said. "Grief makes people spend stupid. My sister sold her wedding ring to a fortune-teller the week after her husband drowned. Bought back a lie that he'd come home on the tide. It's what grief does."

"This isn't a ring," Sefa said. "This is a map. I saw the edge of the paper under his coat. She's not buying comfort, Marta. She's buying a way *down*."

The word dropped into the room and sat there like a stone in a soup.

Brannic finally bit his pie, chewed, swallowed, and said what everyone was thinking. "Down is where people don't come back from."

"People don't come back from the sea either," said Pip, "and half this city makes its living on it."

"The sea doesn't have teeth for a staircase."

"You don't know that. Nobody's been far enough down to check."

Sefa let them go at it, because letting them go at it was how the family thought. That was the thing outsiders never understood about six orphans stitched together by circumstance and Marta's leftover crusts: they didn't argue *despite* loving each other. Arguing was how the love got said. When Brannic called Pip an idiot, it meant *I noticed you skipped dinner*. When Marta called them all fools, it meant *I would burn this shop down before I let anything happen to you*. And when Sefa stood up now and rapped a knuckle on the flour-sack wall for order, it meant: *enough, we're deciding*.

"She won't run," Sefa said. "I want that understood before anyone proposes it. Someone will propose it, so — understood. Petra Blackwood does not run. She once fought a customs officer over a confiscated basket of dried figs. She was fourteen. We lost the figs, but the officer wrote a poem about her afterward. She does not run."

"It wasn't a very good poem," Pip said.

"It was four stanzas, Pip. The point stands."

"So what do we do?" Brannic set down his pie. He looked older than twenty, suddenly, the way he'd looked the night the stars went out and Tam's bed was empty. "Because here's what I see. Our girl is selling everything she's ever made, to the people who'd arrest her for making it, to buy a road into a mountain that eats souls. If we do nothing, she goes alone and dies alone. If we try to stop her, she goes anyway, at night, through a window, and then she never trusts us again. And if we go with her—" He spread his enormous hands. "Then we're all in the dark with the thing that took Tam."

Silence. Somewhere above them, the pie shop's chimney ticked as it cooled, a sound like a slow clock counting toward morning.

It was Marta who broke it, and she surprised them, because Marta almost never voted first.

"I buried two children," she said. "Real ones, born ones, before any of you lot landed on my step like starlings. And I'll tell you what I learned, which is that the worst thing grief ever taught me was to hold on tighter. Holding tight is just another way of closing your hand." She looked around the cave of flour and family. "You can't keep her up here by gripping. So the question isn't whether we let her go. The question is whether she goes down that stair thinking she's alone in the world, or knowing she isn't."

Pip's eyes had gone bright. "That's a yes, then."

"That's a yes," Marta said. "God help us all."

* * *

Sefa caught up with Petra at the charm-stall near dawn, because of course Petra slept at the stall now instead of the loft, as if guarding inventory that was mostly gone. She was hunched over the counter with a needle and thread, working on something small, and she didn't look up when Sefa's shadow fell across her.

"If you're here to lecture," Petra said, "I've had the lecture. I've had it from Marta, from Brannic, and from a drunk man who I think was actually lecturing a barrel."

"No lecture."

"Good."

"I brought bread." Sefa set it on the counter. Salted crust, the way Tam used to make it, because none of them had been able to stop baking it his way even though it hurt, especially because it hurt. "And I'm here to ask a favor."

That got Petra's head up. Her face was a ruin Sefa pretended not to see — three sleepless nights drawn into it, and something worse underneath, some decision already made and sealed. "A favor."

"The archivist. Grey coat. Whatever name he gave you." Sefa leaned on the counter, casual as Sunday. "When you meet him next, I want to be nearby. Not talking. Just near. If he decides three hundred crowns is worth more than discretion, someone should be there to run for the family, and I'm the fastest."

Petra stared at her for a long moment. Then, horribly, she started to laugh — a cracked, exhausted sound with no floor under it. "You want to *help* me bribe a Warden."

"I want to help you not get arrested while bribing a Warden. There's a difference, and the difference is me."

"Sefa—"

"Don't." Sefa held up a hand. "Don't do the thing where you list all the reasons I should stay safe. I've heard the speech. Tam gave it to me twice a week for nine years and it never worked once, so I know for a fact it's defective merchandise."

Something moved across Petra's face at his name — the hook pulling, Sefa thought, the sleeve tearing — and for a second she looked young and lost and nineteen, which she was, which they all kept forgetting because she carried herself like a woman with forty years of poisons in her blood. Which, Sefa supposed, she also was.

"He'll be at the Lantern tomorrow night," Petra said finally. Quietly. Surrendering without admitting it. "He has a copy of the old toll-records. Corvane's ledgers, the parts that matter. Where the gate is. What the thresholds cost." Her jaw tightened. "What they cost, Sefa. Each threshold takes a memory. A real one. Gone forever."

"Then bring memories you can spare."

"There aren't any of those." Petra looked down at the small thing she'd been sewing. Sefa saw it then: a luck-knot, half-finished, worked in red thread. Tam's pattern. The one he'd worn until the night it came back split clean in two. "There aren't any I can spare. That's rather the point of memories."

Sefa reached across the counter and put her hand flat over the knot, stopping the needle mid-stitch. "Then here's my other favor," she said. "Finish that one last. Because if you're going down to buy our brother back, you're not walking into that mountain with nothing in your pockets but grief and poison. You're walking in with family. All of us. Even the parts of us that stay up here."

Petra didn't answer. But her hand came up over Sefa's, and stayed there, and neither of them said anything else for a long time, and the market around them woke up in its usual way — shutters banging, gulls arguing, the first fry-smoke drifting from Marta's chimney — as if the world above the mountain were determined to go on being warm and ordinary and worth climbing back to.

* * *

The meeting at the Drowned Lantern went wrong at half past nine.

Later, Sefa could never quite reconstruct how. One moment the archivist was counting coins onto the table with the fussy precision of his kind; the next, the Lantern's door banged open and two actual Wardens walked in — real ones, badges and batons and the particular bored cruelty of men doing paperwork with fists — and the grey coat was already rising, already pointing, already saying *"that's her"* in a voice slick with apology to somebody upstairs.

Sefa moved before she decided to move. That was the family training, such as it was: nobody thinks during a fire, so you practice until your legs know the way out.

She knocked over the fry-oil rack by the kitchen door — an accident, your honor, a tragedy — and the whole back half of the Lantern went up in greasy yellow chaos, screaming, sliding, Wardens cursing in the smoke. Through it she grabbed Petra's wrist and pulled, and Petra came, because whatever else had changed in her these three black nights, her legs still knew the way out too.

They ran the roofline together, tile to tile, the way they had since they were children stealing washing-line pegs, and dropped into Marta's yard breathless and singed and laughing in the horrible gasping way people laugh when the alternative is crying.

"They took the money," Petra panted. "He took the money and *still* reported me. Three hundred crowns."

"Wardens' clerks get a bonus per arrest," Sefa wheezed. "Everyone knows that."

"I didn't know that."

"You've been busy."

They lay in the flour-dust under the eaves, side by side, hearts slamming. Above them the sky hung blank and starless as a swept floor, and below them, faint through the cobbles, Sefa could have sworn the mountain hummed — that deep mineral note the whole city had learned to hear since the wards fell, like a belly digesting.

"They'll seal the district by morning," Petra said at last. Her voice had gone calm, the terrible calm Sefa hated most. "Which means tomorrow night is the last night the toll-gate is reachable. I go tomorrow."

"We go tomorrow," Sefa said.

Petra turned her head. "Sefa—"

"Before you give the speech again." Sefa sat up. Down in the yard, she could see the others already gathering — Brannic with a pack the size of a coffin, Marta pressing wrapped pies on everyone like ammunition, Pip holding up a lockpick and grinning her gap-toothed grin. "We voted. While you were selling yourself to grey coats, the family held parliament in a pie shop, and the vote was unanimous, and Marta cried, which settled it, because Marta hasn't cried since the plague year. You are not going down that stair alone, Petra Blackwood. You are going down surrounded by the loudest, most stubborn, least qualified rescue party this city has ever produced, and honestly, I'd pity the Undercourt if it weren't about to eat us."

For a long moment Petra said nothing. Then she reached into her coat and drew out the finished luck-knot — red thread, Tam's pattern, mended where it had been split — and pressed it into Sefa's palm, and closed Sefa's fingers over it.

"Hold this," she said. "If I lose myself down there, this is how you find me."

Above the mountain, in the starless dark, something shifted — a sound like a struck bell, far underground, like a door being unlocked from the inside.

Or, Sefa thought, taking Petra's hand and running for the gate, like a table being set.
