## 7. Down the Stair of Teeth

The records said the toll-gate was behind the tanneries, and the records were right in the way that old things are often right and never convenient.

Petra stood at the mouth of a drainage culvert that smelled of three centuries of sheep fat and regret, holding Corvane's stolen ledger against her chest like a breastplate that had failed everyone who'd ever worn it. Above her, the city went about its business of not having stars. Lamplighters worked their rounds with the grim efficiency of people who had decided, collectively, to pretend the sky was supposed to be black felt. Somewhere a dog barked at nothing, which was either an omen or just a dog.

"Right," she said to herself, because there was no one else to say it to, and because her voice sounded less afraid when it was busy.

She had left a note for Sefa. It had taken four drafts. The first said *gone into the mountain, don't follow*, which Sefa would read as *follow immediately*. The second said *gone to visit cousins in Vell*, which was worse, because Sefa knew she had no cousins, unless you counted the entire Nightmarket, which Sefa did, and would therefore conclude Petra had gone to Vell with forty pickpockets. The final draft simply said *I'll be back before the bread goes stale*, which was true in the sense that mountain time was strange and bread was very patient.

She ducked into the culvert.

* * *

Corvane's ledger was a masterpiece of bureaucratic evil, which is to say it was beautifully organized. Page after page of neat columns: names, dates, weights — *weights*, as if souls came measured like flour — and in the margins, tiny annotations in his cramped hand. *Third threshold requires taste-memory. Seventh requires sound. Do not pay with anything you cannot spare twice.*

That last one had kept her up all night. What did it mean, to spare something twice? She'd turned it over while chewing the heel of yesterday's loaf, and had arrived at the only answer that made sense: some tolls took the memory itself, and some took your ability to make new ones of that kind. Pay a taste once, lose the memory. Pay it twice, lose every version of it still to come.

So she had spent the evening doing something that felt obscene: memorizing her own memories. Sitting cross-legged on her cot, eyes shut, walking through her mother's kitchen one more time. The low ceiling smoke-darkened like a hearthstone. The flour sack propped in the corner with a hole patched by a scrap of blue cloth. Her mother's hands, big-knuckled and quick, working dough with the particular violence of a woman who loved through labor. And the smell — gods, the smell — yeast and woodsmoke and the faint sweetness of honey brushed over the crust in the last minutes, so the whole house smelled like a promise being kept.

Her mother had died when Petra was nine. The bread was the last thing she had of her that wasn't grief-shaped.

She memorized it until she could taste it with her eyes open. Then she rolled up the ledger, tied her hair back, and went to find the door.

* * *

The culvert ran uphill, which told her everything about how old it was. Rivers used to run down from the mountain; now they ran away from the city, as if even water wanted distance from what lived under it. The passage narrowed, then widened, then opened without warning into a chamber that stopped her breath.

The gate was not a gate. It was a mouth.

A doorway perhaps twelve feet tall, carved — no, *grown*, she thought, there was no chisel-work here — into the living rock, framed by rows of teeth. Real teeth, or things like teeth: long, curved, yellowed with age, fused at their roots into the stone. Some were the size of her forearm. A few near the top had been snapped off, and the stumps were worn smooth, as if generations of travelers had touched them for luck, or as warnings, or because they couldn't help themselves.

Set into the rock above the arch, wards glowed a faint, sullen red — wards made of bone, she realized, looking closer. Not carved bone. Fused bone. Femurs and ribs and smaller things she declined to identify, knitted together into sigils that pulsed slowly, like the heartbeat of something sleeping just behind the stone.

"Well," Petra said. "Lovely."

Her tongue, traitor that it was, tasted the air. Magic had a flavor — that was her secret, the thing she'd never told anyone, the reason her charms sold better than any other charm-seller's in the Nightmarket. Wards usually tasted like copper and ozone, sharp and clean. These tasted different. These tasted like marrow broth left too long on the fire, like calcium and patience and something underneath that made the back of her throat close.

Hunger. The wards tasted faintly, patiently, of hunger.

She unslung her pack and checked it one more time, the way her mother had taught her to check a market list: salt, yes; dried plums, yes; the good knife, yes; three luck-knots of her own making, because if the law was going to hang her anyway she might as well be hanged well-charmed. The ledger stayed wrapped in oilcloth against her spine.

She stepped up to the teeth.

Nothing happened. She waited. Nothing continued to happen.

"Hello?" she tried. "I'd like to pass?"

The nearest tooth warmed under her palm like a hand waking up. And then a voice — not heard so much as *tasted*, arriving on her tongue the way all magic did — said:

**TOLL.**

One word. It filled the chamber the way a struck bell fills a room, except bells don't make your fillings ache.

"Toll of what?" Petra asked. "Coin? I have eleven coppers and a button I'm very attached to."

**MEMORY,** said the gate. **PAY.**

And here was the moment, she supposed, where a sensible person turned around. Here was the moment where the sensible person remembered that memories were the only currency a person actually owned, that everything else was borrowed, and walked back up the culvert to write a strongly worded letter to the crown about institutional accountability.

But Tam was somewhere below her feet, half-dissolved into the foundations of a court that ate contracts, humming a fragment of himself in a jar of starlight. And Petra Blackwood had never once in her life been sensible about the people she loved.

"All right," she said. "But I choose what I pay."

The gate considered this. She felt it considering, a slow pressure against her mind like a thumb testing fruit at market. Then:

**CHOOSE.**

She closed her eyes. She reached for the kitchen — the smoke-dark ceiling, the blue-patched flour sack, the honey-brushed crust — and found, to her relief, that she could still reach it. The memory sat whole inside her, warm as fresh loaf.

But around its edges, she noticed for the first time, were other memories. Smaller ones. The burnt loaf her mother had laughed over instead of scolding. The way flour dust hung in the light. The specific weight of being carried to bed with her cheek against a flour-soft shoulder.

She chose the smallest one. The burnt loaf. The laugh.

"I pay this," she said, and held it out in her mind like a coin on an open palm.

The gate took it.

There was no pain. That was somehow the worst part. There was simply a moment of warmth spreading across her tongue — the ghost of char and her mother's bright laughter ringing once, sweetly, in her mouth — and then it was gone. Not faded. *Gone*, the way a tooth is gone from the gum. She could feel the shape of the absence. She knew a memory had been there. She could not remember what it held.

She stood very still for a moment, breathing.

Then the teeth parted — slowly, grindingly, with a sound like a mill starting up — and the dark beyond them exhaled cold air that smelled of stone and deep water and, very faintly, of starlight.

Petra wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand, told herself firmly that the dampness was the culvert's fault, and walked into the mountain.

* * *

The stair went down the way grief goes down: further than you planned, past depths you didn't agree to.

It was cut in a spiral, wide enough for two abreast in the old days, though who those two had been and why they'd needed such a grand staircase into the roots of the world, the stone didn't say. Lichen glowed along the walls in patches of pale green, giving just enough light to see the next ten steps and no more — a design choice, Petra suspected, made specifically to prevent travelers from seeing how far they had yet to fall.

She counted steps to keep her mind occupied. At four hundred she stopped counting, because at four hundred she passed the first set of bones.

They were seated against the wall, arranged with care — someone had placed them deliberately, crossed-legged, hands on knees, skull tilted slightly upward toward the distant surface. Old bones. Dry bones. Whoever they'd been, they'd climbed this far and no further, and whoever came after had given them the dignity of posture.

Petra paused. "Sorry," she said, because it seemed polite. "I'd leave you something, but I'm paying in memories and mine are going fast."

The bones said nothing, but she felt less alone for having spoken, which she suspected was the actual function of talking to bones.

At six hundred steps, the second threshold.

**TOLL,** said the stair, and this time the voice seemed to come from everywhere, from the lichen and the bones and the dark below. **PAY.**

She'd prepared for this one differently. Corvane's margin note: *seventh requires sound.* She didn't know if this was the seventh, and she wasn't willing to gamble six more thresholds on finding out, so she'd built a small inventory during the climb. Sounds she could spare. The wail of the palace kettle, which had annoyed her for years. The specific squeak of Warden Hask's boots, which she'd happily forget twice.

She paid the kettle. It went out of her like a snuffed candle, and she found she couldn't quite recall what she'd lost, only that her mouth felt quieter than before.

Down. Down. Down.

The third threshold took the color of a dress she'd owned at fourteen. The fourth took the name of a street urchin she'd known for one summer, and she wept for that one without knowing why, standing on the stairs with tears running into her mouth, tasting salt and loss and no referent for either. That was the cruelty of it, she understood now. The gate didn't take the pain. It took the *thing*, and left the pain loose in you, ownerless, looking for somewhere to live.

"You're welcome," she told the stair bitterly, wiping her face. "Anything else? My childhood home? My ability to enjoy soup?"

The stair, having no sense of humor whatsoever, said nothing.

* * *

Somewhere past the ninth threshold — she'd stopped keeping precise count after the tenth step-counting attempt dissolved mid-number, another toll she hadn't seen coming — the character of the mountain changed.

The lichen thickened. The air warmed. And the wards in the walls began to appear not as separate sigils but as continuous structures, ribcages of bone arcing overhead, spines running along the walls like banisters, whole skeletons fused into load-bearing architecture. Whoever built this place hadn't used bone as material. They'd used it as *masonry*, and the masonry had opinions.

Because the wards watched her.

She felt it on her tongue with every step: attention, mineral and cold, tracking her progress the way a cook tracks a pot about to boil over. Once, she could have sworn a femur sigil rotated slightly as she passed, its carved eye-holes following her with the patience of something that had been waiting centuries and could wait centuries more.

"Keep staring," she muttered. "I'm delicious and unavailable."

Her charms, she noticed, were dimming. The luck-knots in her pocket still held their shape, but the magic in them had gone quiet, tasting the air and deciding, wisely, to play dead. Even her own gift felt muted down here, her tongue reading flavors through wool. Except—

Except the hunger. That came through clear as a bell. The deeper she went, the louder it got, until it wasn't a background note anymore but the whole chord: the mountain itself was hungry, had been hungry for longer than there had been mouths to describe it, and everything above her — the wards, the gates, the tolls — was just the mountain setting its table.

Petra had spent three years tasting poison for a queen. She knew what it was to swallow dread on schedule. But this was different. This was dread with *appetite*.

She ate a dried plum, mostly out of defiance, and kept walking.

* * *

The stair ended at a door.

Not a mouth this time — an actual door, iron-banded oak, set into the rock with hinges that gleamed as if oiled yesterday. Someone maintained this. Someone came here, regularly, and oiled the hinges of the door between the world of the living and the court of the dead, and Petra found that detail more frightening than all the teeth combined. Horror was bearable. *Housekeeping* horror was not.

Above the door, the final ward: a sigil of fused bone larger than all the others, shaped like an open hand. As she approached, it flared red, and the voice came one last time — and this time it was not one word.

**LAST TOLL,** it said, and the words settled on her tongue like frost. **THE DEEP COURT ASKS NOT FOR SCRAPS. IT ASKS FOR THE LOAF ENTIRE. PAY WHAT YOU CANNOT SPARE, AND BE ADMITTED WHOLE. OR TURN BACK, AND KEEP YOURSELF.**

Petra stood in the lichen-light with her heart hammering and her mouth full of cold.

*Pay what you cannot spare.* Corvane's note swam up from memory: *do not pay with anything you cannot spare twice.* She'd thought it was a warning. Now she understood it was arithmetic. Everything she'd paid so far had been trimmings. The deep court wanted the loaf.

She thought of turning back. Honestly, she gave it a full minute. She thought of Sefa's furious face, of Pip's laugh, of the market at dusk with all its little illegal lights. She thought of climbing back up nine hundred steps to a starless sky and spending the rest of her life knowing Tam was down here, humming in a jar, and that she'd gotten as far as the door.

"No," she said aloud. "I don't think I will turn back."

She closed her eyes and went to the kitchen one last time.

The smoke-dark ceiling. The blue-patched flour sack. Her mother's quick hands, the honey-brush, the crust, the promise. She gathered all of it — every corner of the memory, every version, the smell and the warmth and the woman at the center of it — and she held it out.

"This was my mother's," she said, and her voice cracked clean through the middle. "It's the best thing I have. Take it and know you're robbing a grave."

**PAY,** said the ward, almost gently.

She paid.

The warmth went out of her. Not the memory alone — the *taste*. She felt it go the way you feel a river change course: suddenly, totally, irreversibly. And when she searched for her mother's kitchen afterward, she found the room empty. She knew her mother had baked. She knew there had been bread. But the flavor of it — the honey and the woodsmoke and the promise — was gone, scooped out of her like the flesh of a fruit, leaving only the rind of fact.

She stood before the iron-banded door and cried the way you cry when you're too tired to do it quietly, great ugly sobs that echoed down the spiral behind her, and the bones along the walls listened, and the mountain drank the sound of her grief like gravy.

When it was done, she was done. Something had finished in her, some argument she'd been carrying since the night the stars went out. Whatever was left of Petra Blackwood now was leaner and colder and entirely, dangerously clear.

She straightened. She wiped her face. She looked at the door.

"Open," she said.

The hinges, freshly oiled, swung without a sound.

Beyond lay a corridor lit by jars — hundreds of them, thousands, lining shelves cut into the walls as far as the light reached — and each jar held a soft gold glow, and each glow moved, faintly, rhythmically, the way a sleeper's chest moves.

Souls. A pantry of souls, shelved and labeled and dusted.

And coming toward her down the aisle, keys at her hip and hollows where her eyes should have shone, a woman in a steward's apron who looked at Petra — at her tear-streaked face, her poison-hardened hands, her tongue still unconsciously tasting the air — and stopped dead.

"You," the woman said slowly, "are not on tonight's delivery."

"No," said Petra, and reached for the only weapon she had ever owned. "But I can tell you exactly what's wrong with your king's broth."
