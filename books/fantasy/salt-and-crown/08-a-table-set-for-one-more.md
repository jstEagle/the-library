## 8. A Table Set for One More

The Undercourt smelled like a kitchen that had forgotten why it cooked.

That was Petra's first thought, standing in the vast antechamber beneath the mountain, and she clung to it because a joke — even a bitter one, even one only she could hear — was better than screaming. The air was warm and mineral, thick with the scent of slow-simmered broth and candle tallow and something older underneath, something like wet stone and old coins. Torches burned without flickering. Doors opened for people who had no shadows.

And the servants — the servants were the worst part.

They moved through the halls with the smooth, unhurried grace of people who had all the time in the world, because they did. They were souls, though most of them didn't know it anymore. Petra could taste it on the air around them: a thinness, a watered-down quality, like tea steeped too many times. They smiled at her as she passed. They always smiled. It was the smile of someone who has been asked to smile so long that the asking has worn grooves into their face.

*Don't look at their eyes,* she told herself, and looked anyway, because she was Petra Blackwood and self-preservation had never once won an argument with curiosity. Their eyes were fine. That was the horror of it. Warm brown eyes, bright grey eyes, eyes with laugh lines crinkled at the corners — everything about them said *person*, right up until you tasted the nothing behind it.

She adjusted the hood of her borrowed cloak and kept walking.

Getting this far had required three lies, one stolen livery pin, and a small fortune in luck-charms burned at the thresholds, where the wards had sniffed at her like dogs at a market stall. The wards down here weren't made of stone or salt. They were made of *agreement*. Each threshold had hummed against her skin and asked, in a voice like a ledger being opened, what she was willing to give. She'd given it memories of no consequence — the name of a Warden who'd once fined Sefa, the exact color of a door in the palace barracks, the taste of rain on the day she turned twelve. Small coin. She was saving the real treasure, and she prayed to every star that had gone dark that she wouldn't have to spend it.

The kitchen, when she found it, was enormous.

It occupied a cavern the size of the Nightmarket's main square, all black stone and copper, with hearths big enough to roast an ox whole and shelves climbing up into darkness where jars glowed faintly with contents Petra decided not to examine. Souls in kitchen whites moved along the counters with that same terrible serenity, chopping, stirring, tasting, adjusting. No one hurried. No one spoke above a murmur. The whole room operated like a clock with no one left inside to wind it.

At the center stood a long table, and at the table sat a woman Petra hadn't seen before, presiding over a row of covered dishes with the weary authority of a general reviewing troops.

Petra's plan — such as it was — had been simple: slip in, find the steward's records, find where they kept the *inventory*, get out. She'd spent two days learning the rhythms of the place from the shadows of its corridors. But plans, she had learned long ago in the Nightmarket, were just stories you told yourself before reality got a vote.

Reality voted when a soul in kitchen whites stepped directly into her path and said, pleasantly, "You're new."

"I'm—"

"The new taster." The soul's smile widened by exactly one tooth. "For the king's table. You're expected. You're late."

Petra's mind, which had survived arsenic and nightshade and one truly memorable encounter with a fish that should not have existed, went briefly, magnificently blank. Then the market instinct caught it like a net: *when you can't win the game, become the game.*

"Yes," she said. "I'm late. I'm sorry. The stairs are—" she gestured vaguely upward, "—a lot."

"They are," the soul agreed, with feeling, and led her toward the table. "Vess will test you. Don't be offended. Vess tests everyone."

*Vess.* The name snagged somewhere in Petra's memory — Corvane's ledgers, the marginalia in his cramped hand, a payment record signed with a single letter. She filed it away and let herself be delivered.

The woman at the table was perhaps fifty, perhaps five hundred; the Undercourt played havoc with such things. Her hair was iron-grey and pinned back with ruthless efficiency. Her face was lined but not unkindly, the lines of someone who had frowned at a great many pots and found most of them wanting. And her eyes—

Her eyes were hollow. Not empty, not dead, but hollow, like windows in a house where the fire had gone out long ago and no one had bothered to relight it. Petra had seen that look once before, on Tam's mother, in the years after the fever took half her family. It was the look of someone who had paid a price and was still paying it.

"You're the replacement," the woman said. Her voice was dry as old paper. "The last one lasted eleven days."

"What happened to her?"

"She became soup." Vess said it the way you'd say *she resigned*. "Sit."

Petra sat. A bowl was placed before her, covered with a silver lid. Steam curled from beneath it, carrying a smell that made her tongue sit up and pay attention — rich, layered, wrong in ways she couldn't yet name.

"Before you serve the king," Vess said, "you will demonstrate competence. There is a broth under that lid. It contains seven poisons. Identify them."

Petra lifted the lid.

The broth was golden, shot through with threads of herb, beautiful the way a snake is beautiful. She picked up the spoon. Around the table, the souls had paused in their work to watch, and their collective attention felt like standing in a field during lightning weather.

*Seven poisons.* In the palace, that would be a death sentence and a scandal. Down here, it was apparently an entrance exam.

She dipped the spoon. She let the broth touch her tongue.

And the world opened.

This was the thing no one knew about her, the thing she'd guarded since she was nine years old and first realized that other children couldn't do it: Petra Blackwood didn't just taste poison. She tasted *everything*. Magic lay on the tongue like salt, like smoke, like honey cut with ash, and three years of royal service had taught her to read flavors the way scholars read books. Arsenic whispered. Nightshade sang. Wolfsbane howled. And beneath them all, magic left its own signature, unique as handwriting.

She closed her eyes and read.

"One," she said. "Hemlock. Top note, tastes like green and regret. It's masking something, though — there's a binding agent, gum of some kind, holding it in suspension. Clever. Someone wanted the hemlock to arrive fashionably late."

She took another sip. "Two. Moonwort. It's not poisonous to the body, it's poisonous to the *wards* — it would strip any protective charm off whoever drank this. Which means the hemlock isn't the point. The hemlock is the distraction. Three is the point."

She swirled the spoon, listening with her tongue. "Three is custom. Ground pearl and grave-moss, bound with grief — actual grief, someone wept into this mortar, you can taste the salt of it. It doesn't kill. It *opens*. Makes the drinker suggestible. Whoever brewed this wanted the king agreeable, not dead."

Silence around the table. One of the souls had stopped stirring entirely.

"Four," Petra continued, warming now, the fear burning off like morning fog because this — *this* — was the one thing in two worlds she was good at. "Foxglove, but young, harvested early, so it's more suggestion than threat. A bluff poison. Five is interesting — that's not a plant at all, that's mineral. Lead, but sweetened, which takes real skill because lead wants to be bitter. Six..."

She hesitated. The sixth flavor was familiar in a way that made her stomach clench. Cold. Mineral. Wrong. The same signature she'd tasted beneath the arsenic meant for Queen Maren, all those weeks and a lifetime ago.

"Six came from here," she said quietly. "From below. I've tasted it before — it was riding shotgun on an assassination attempt topside. It's not a poison so much as a... claim. A marker. Whoever drinks it belongs to whatever sent it."

"And seven?" Vess's voice was very soft.

Petra took the last sip and nearly dropped the spoon.

Seven wasn't in the broth at all. Seven was in the *bowl*. Coated on the inside of the glaze, invisible, tasteless to anyone whose tongue hadn't been rebuilt by three years of swallowing death. It was patient and old and hungry, and it tasted the way the space between stars must taste.

"Seven," she said slowly, "is a question. It's keyed to me specifically — no. Not to me. To *tasters*. Anyone with the gift who drinks from this bowl gets measured. Weighed. Reported upward." She set down the spoon and looked Vess dead in her hollow eyes. "Which means either your kitchen has a traitor, or this entire examination was designed to take my measure. Which is it?"

The silence stretched. Somewhere across the cavern, a pot boiled over and no one moved to save it.

Then Vess laughed.

It was a terrible sound, rusty, like a gate that hadn't opened in decades, but it was unmistakably a laugh. "Eleven days," she said. "The last one cried at the hemlock. The one before that drank the seventh and spent the rest of her service reporting on herself." She rose, and up close Petra could see that the hollowness in her eyes went all the way down, a well with no bottom and no water. "Come with me, taster. The king dines at midnight, and you have a table to learn."

* * *

The king's table was set for twelve, though the king dined alone.

Petra learned this in the following hours, as Vess walked her through the rituals of the Undercourt's kitchen with the brisk efficiency of a woman who had done this ten thousand times and hated perhaps nine thousand of them. Every dish was a contract. This wasn't metaphor — Petra watched Vess prepare a simple plate of bread and oil and murmured words over it that made the oil shimmer, and understood that whoever ate that bread would owe a debt measured in the grain's own language. Salt sealed bargains. Honey sweetened terms. A dish served cold was a debt deferred; served hot, a debt collected.

"Why food?" Petra asked, chopping grave-root while Vess supervised. "Why not just — papers? Signatures?"

"Paper burns," Vess said. "Signatures forge. But everyone eats. Even kings. *Especially* kings." She took the knife from Petra's hand and demonstrated the cut — thinner, angled, precise. "A meal shared is a bargain witnessed by the oldest law there is. The law of hunger. You cannot fake an appetite, girl. The body confesses what the mouth denies."

"And if someone refuses to eat?"

Vess's hollow eyes rested on her a moment too long. "No one refuses twice."

The hours passed. Petra chopped and stirred and memorized, and all the while a second ledger ran behind her eyes: corridors, guard rotations, the direction the servant-souls drifted at shift's end (down, always down, toward some deeper heart of the mountain), and above all, the vaults. Vess mentioned them only once, in passing — "the cellar," she called it, as casually as you'd mention a wine collection — and Petra's heart had hammered so hard she'd had to taste-test three dishes in a row to cover it.

*Tam is down there.* Half-dissolved, Vess had said — no, that was Corvane's intelligence, secondhand. But the shape of it was true. Somewhere beneath this kitchen, in the dark, her brother-by-choice hung in a jar like a preserved plum, and she was peeling vegetables twenty feet above him.

*Soon,* she promised the turnip. *I'm working on it.*

Midnight approached. The great table was dressed — black cloth, candles that burned blue, plates of a material that wasn't quite silver and wasn't quite bone. Twelve settings. Eleven would go unused.

"Why twelve?" Petra asked, arranging the final garnish with hands that only trembled a little.

"Hope," Vess said, and did not elaborate.

The king arrived without arriving. One moment the hall was empty; the next he was seated at the head of the table, and Petra's skin tried to crawl off her body and leave without her.

He looked almost human. That was the trap of him. Tall, spare, grey-robed, with a face like a handsome man remembered by someone who hadn't seen him in centuries — the features slightly too smooth, the kindness in the expression slightly too composed, like a portrait of warmth rather than warmth itself. His eyes were the color of the space between the stars that used to be there.

He looked at Petra, and she felt herself being tasted.

"There you are," the Underking said. His voice was gentle, and gentle things in this place frightened her most of all. "The new tongue. Vess speaks well of you. That is rare enough to be suspicious."

"I'll try to earn the suspicion," Petra said, before her brain could stop her mouth.

Something moved behind the king's face — not a smile, but the *place* where a smile would go, the way a footprint isn't the foot. "Serve the first course."

She did. Soup — of course it was soup, the mountain ran on broth the way the court above ran on gossip — and she carried it to his place with the steadiness of a woman who had once held a tray steady through an earthquake because dropping it would have started a war.

The king ate. And Petra, standing at his shoulder in the proper attitude of a taster, did the thing she had come here to do, the thing that terrified her more than the poisons, more than the wards, more than the hollow-eyed servants:

She tasted him.

Not his food. *Him.* It was a risk — he might feel it, might know, might turn those between-the-stars eyes on her and add her to the menu — but Corvane's notes had been clear: contracts were his armor, and armor had seams. She let her tongue read the air around him the way she'd read the poisoned broth, and what she found made her want to weep and laugh in equal measure.

He was starving.

Not metaphorically. The Underking was *hungry*, in the way a desert is thirsty, in the way a bell is silent — a vast, ancient, bottomless emptiness wearing the shape of a diner. Every contract, every soul, every debt this mountain had ever swallowed had gone into that hunger and come out as nothing, because hunger doesn't fill. Hunger only grows. He was eating eternally at a table set for twelve, and he was still starving, and some deep-buried part of Petra — the part that fed orphans out of her own charm-stall profits, the part that had never once let a market kid go to bed hungry — recognized him with a jolt of horrified pity.

*You're not a monster,* she thought. *You're a hole shaped like one.*

The king set down his spoon. He turned his head, and those eyes fixed on her with the weight of a landslide considering whether to fall.

"You taste the air," he said. "Bold. Most tongues only taste the spoon."

"I taste what's put in front of me," Petra said. "It's put a great deal in front of me."

"Mm." He considered her the way she imagined a man considers a fruit he hasn't eaten before. "Three years of poison, they tell me. Three years of swallowing death for a queen who signs her ledgers in another's blood. Your soul must be a remarkable vintage by now. Bitter. Layered. Complex." He leaned forward, and the candle flames all bent toward him like courtiers bowing. "I find myself curious, taster. Tell me — what does fear taste like on a tongue as seasoned as yours?"

Petra thought of Tam's split charm lying in an empty room. Thought of a jar of starlight in a cellar below her feet. Thought of Sefa, Pip, the whole loud loving argumentative family of her, a world away and a world above.

"Salt," she said. "Fear tastes like salt. Same as everything worth keeping."

The king held her gaze for a long, long moment. The blue candles guttered. Somewhere far below, in the cellar, in the dark, Petra would have sworn she heard — faint as memory, thin as thread — a snatch of the Nightmarket street-song, hummed by something that refused, even dissolved, even jarred, to stop singing.

The Underking smiled his portrait-of-a-smile, and returned to his soup.

"Tomorrow," he said, "you will serve the second course. Vess — see that our new tongue learns the cellar route. A taster should know where the ingredients sleep."

Petra bowed, heart slamming, mind racing, hope rising in her throat like a swallowed star.

*The cellar route.* He had just handed her the map, wrapped in courtesy, tied with a ribbon of casual cruelty — or invitation. She couldn't tell which, and with him, she suspected, neither could he.

As she followed Vess from the hall, the old steward fell into step beside her and said, quietly, without looking at her:

"He never shows anyone the cellar. Eleven days, the last taster lasted, and she never saw the pantry door." A pause. "Whatever you are, girl, you've interested him. And in this house—" her hollow eyes finally turned, and for one flickering moment Petra saw something buried deep in them, something that might once have been fire, "—being interesting is the most dangerous thing you can be."
