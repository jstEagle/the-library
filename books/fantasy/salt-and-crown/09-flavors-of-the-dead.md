## 9. Flavors of the Dead

Vess had not tasted anything in forty years.

That was the first thing you learned to give up, down here. Not your name — names were currency, and the Undercourt let you keep yours the way a miser lets a beggar keep his coat. Not your reflection — the mountain didn't care about faces. You gave up taste because taste was appetite, and appetite belonged to the king.

She still remembered how. That was the cruelty of it. The memory sat in her like a stone in a shoe: the bright green snap of spring peas, the way salt bloomed on warm bread, her mother's plum wine gone sharp at the bottom of the cask. She could recite flavors the way other servants recited prayers. She simply couldn't feel them anymore, and hadn't since the night she'd signed herself over with a spoonful of honeyed wine and a smile she'd thought was clever.

So when the new kitchen girl — the one who'd named seven poisons in the king's broth without so much as wetting her lips, which was impossible, which was *blasphemy*, which was the most interesting thing Vess had seen in four decades — when that girl looked up from her scrubbing and said, "You're staring at me," Vess did something she hadn't done in years.

She smiled.

"I'm staring at what you are," Vess said. "Which is either a miracle or a corpse that hasn't been informed."

The girl — Petra, the steward's roster said, though Vess doubted that was the name her mother gave her — straightened up slowly, dripping dishwater onto flagstones that drank it without complaint. Everything down here drank without complaint. "I don't know what you mean."

"No," Vess agreed. "You wouldn't. They never do, at first." She jerked her chin toward the door. "Walk with me. The larders need inventory, and I do my best lying while walking."

* * *

The larders of the Undercourt were a joke Vess had stopped telling out loud. Shelves upon shelves of food that no one ate. Wheels of cheese aging into geology. Jars of preserves from harvests that had happened before the mountain had a name, their fruit gone the color of old bruises. The Underking kept a kitchen the way a lonely man keeps a dog he never walks: for the shape of the ritual, for the sound of something alive in the house.

"Inventory," Petra said, looking at a ham that had presumably been a ham during the reign of someone's great-great-grandmother. "Of this."

"The king likes the ledgers kept current." Vess ran a finger along a shelf and came away with dust the color of ash. "He doesn't eat, you understand. I imagine you've noticed. All those dishes you're learning to carry, and not one crumb crosses his teeth."

"He tastes them," Petra said carefully. "Doesn't he?"

"He reads them." Vess turned to look at her properly. Up close, the girl had the particular pallor of someone who had spent years being slowly poisoned on purpose — Vess knew that complexion better than her own hands. But under it, something else. Something that made the air near her taste — *would* have made the air near her taste — of iron filings and winter. "Every dish is a contract. Every flavor is a clause. When you set that broth in front of him, you weren't feeding him. You were reading him his mail."

Petra's face did something complicated. Vess watched her decide not to ask, then decide to ask anyway. She liked that in a person. The ones who didn't ask were the ones already dead.

"What happens if the mail is bad?"

"Then the contract sours, and the mountain collects." Vess shrugged. "You saw the scullery boy who dropped the third course last week. He wasn't reassigned. There is no reassignment down here. There's only the walls, eventually."

"You say that very calmly."

"I've had forty years to practice calm. It's the one flavor they can't take from you, mostly because it isn't one." Vess pulled a jar off a high shelf — plums, by the label, though the contents had long since become a dark, patient sludge — and set it between them like a chess piece. "Now. Seven poisons. In one broth. By smell alone, across a crowded kitchen, while pretending to be a simple country girl with an unusually good nose."

"Lucky guess."

"Mm. And I'm the Queen of the Surface Realms, and these plums are fresh." Vess leaned in. The girl smelled of soap and scorched sugar and, underneath, faintly but unmistakably, of death — many small deaths, layered like lacquer. Vess's stomach, which had no opinions left, tried anyway to turn over. "I know what you are, girl. I was one. Before."

* * *

They sat on flour sacks in the corner of the larder, two women and a jar of dead plums, and Vess told it the way she told everything now: flatly, in order, with the emotion filed off like a burr.

"There was a famine the year I turned nineteen. My village starved politely, the way villages do — quietly, in corners, so as not to embarrass anyone. A man came through selling passage into the mountain. Work, he said. Bread every day, he said. All it costs is a taste of your future." She laughed, a dry sound with nothing behind it. "I was clever. I negotiated. Got him up to a full meal a day and my mother's debt forgiven. Signed with a spoonful of wine, because that's how they do it — contracts go down easier sweet."

"And?"

"And the wine was the last thing I ever tasted." Vess turned the jar in her hands. "The mountain takes the sense along with the soul, bit by bit. First taste goes. Then, over the years, the others peel away. Smell. Warmth. I can still see, still hear — the king finds those useful in a servant. But I haven't felt sunlight in thirty years, and I wouldn't recognize hunger if it bit me."

Petra was quiet. Then, in the voice of someone testing ice: "Your soul. Is it—"

"In the foundations? Probably. Or the walls. Or the king's left molar, for all I know." Vess said it lightly, and watched the girl flinch, and felt an old, rusted tenderness grind into motion. "Don't make that face. It's not so bad, being part of a floor. Floors are steady. Floors don't lie to you about wages."

"That's not funny."

"It's a little funny."

"It's really not." Petra hugged her knees. In the lamplight she looked young and stubborn and terribly tired, the way all the living looked down here, like candles burning at both ends in a draft. "There's someone I came for. His soul — they took it three years ago, and I need to know where it's kept, and whether there's any of him left to save."

And there it was. Vess had known it would come; it always came. Forty years of watching souls arrive in jars and depart into mortar, and in all that time, exactly twice, someone had come *down* after one. The first time had ended badly. The second time was sitting on a flour sack asking questions with her whole stubborn heart in her mouth.

"His name," Vess said.

"Tam."

* * *

The vault records were kept in a room that smelled — had once smelled, Vess corrected herself, had *once* smelled — of ink and cold stone. She walked Petra past the shelves of contract-jars, each one labeled in the steward's neat hand, and did not look at the row where her own name would be, if names were written on the outside instead of dissolved into the glass. Some mercies you built yourself, brick by brick, over decades.

"Tamsin-below-the-bridge, called Tam. Arrived three years past, autumn toll." Vess ran her finger down the ledger until it caught. "Here."

Petra read over her shoulder, and Vess heard the breath go out of her like a snuffed candle.

"*Dissolution schedule: fifty percent complete,*" Petra read aloud. Her voice cracked on *fifty*. "What does — what does that mean? Half? He's half *gone*?"

"It means what it says." Vess kept her voice level, because someone had to. "A soul in a jar is a debt waiting to be spent. The mountain spends them slowly. It grinds them into the mortar, the ward-stones, the bones of the stair. Fifty percent means half of him is holding the ceiling up right now, and the other half is still in the jar, still *him*, still—" she searched for a word and found it, reluctantly, "—singing."

"Singing?"

"They hum. The fresh ones. Old souls go quiet — mine went quiet inside a decade, I'd wager. But a soul three years in the glass, half-kept?" Vess closed the ledger. "If your Tam was the sort of person who sang above ground, he'll be singing below it. Faint. Like a tune remembered rather than heard. But there."

Petra had gone very still, in the way of people holding themselves together with willpower and thread. "Can I hear it? Now? Tonight?"

"No." Vess said it firmly enough that the girl's shoulders dropped, and immediately regretted the firmness. "Not tonight. Tonight you're a kitchen girl with an inventory to finish and a shift to survive, and if you go wandering toward the vaults with your eyes full of tears, some clerk will notice, and clerks feed on noticing the way the king feeds on contracts. Tomorrow. I'll find a reason. I'm very good at reasons; I've had practice."

"Why?" Petra asked. It came out raw. "Why help me? You don't know me. You've been here forty years and you owe this place nothing but the opposite."

Vess considered several answers. The true one was simple and embarrassing: because the girl tasted of iron and winter and survival, and because forty years ago a girl had stood in this same larder negotiating for her mother's debt, and lost, and no one had ever told her what she'd actually signed. Because somewhere in the grinding dark of the centuries, Vess had kept one flavor after all — not on her tongue, but in whatever was left of her where her tongue used to matter — and it was the flavor of wanting someone else to win.

"Because you named seven poisons in one broth," she said instead, "and I've waited forty years to meet the second person alive who could."

"The first being you."

"The first being me." Vess pushed off the shelf and straightened her apron, and the moment of honesty folded itself away like laundry. "Come. Inventory waits for no one, and the under-steward counts the dust."

* * *

They worked through the evening, and Vess talked, because talking was the closest thing she had left to feeding someone.

"You should understand what you're walking toward," she said, relabeling jars with a stub of chalk. "Everyone thinks the king eats food. Souls, flesh, bread — no. He eats *contracts*. An agreement freely made has a flavor, and he's been starving on it for longer than the mountain has had a name. Every pact, every bargain, every desperate signature — that's his meal. Food is just the plate it's served on. Which means—" she tapped Petra's hand with the chalk, gently, "—that the most dangerous thing you can do down here is want something. Wanting is the first clause of every contract he's ever tasted. He'll offer you exactly what you want, wrapped in exactly the words you need to hear, and it will smell like home, and by the time you notice the price, your spoon will already be in your mouth."

Petra was quiet a long moment. "And if I don't sign? If I just take him back?"

"Then the mountain takes it out of the walls, and the walls are made of people, and you'll be fighting everyone who ever bargained, including—" Vess gestured at herself with terrible cheer, "—me. Assuming I can lift my arms, which is debatable."

"So I have to beat him at his own game."

"You have to *out-taste* him." Vess paused, struck by her own sentence, and turned it over. "Huh. Forty years, and I've never said that aloud. Out-taste him. Girl, do you understand what you are? Three years of swallowing poison on purpose. Do you know what that makes you, down here where everything is flavor?"

"Dead, eventually?"

"Indigestible," Vess said, and had the rare pleasure of watching the girl's jaw drop. "Think. His whole power is appetite — reading desire, tasting intent, finding the sweetness in a soul and drinking it. And you come down here marinated in arsenic and grief and whatever else you've been force-feeding yourself since you were a child. You're the bitterest cup ever poured. He might not even be able to swallow you." She laughed, short and rusty. "Of course, that cuts both ways. Bitter cups get poured out."

"Cheerful."

"I'm a larder-keeper. Cheerfulness spoils."

* * *

At the end of the shift, walking Petra back toward the servants' cells, Vess stopped at the junction where the corridor split — left to the kitchens, right toward the deep places, the vaults, the humming dark.

"One more thing," she said. "About your Tam. About the half that's left."

"Yes?"

"When you find him — and you will, because you have the look of a woman who will dig through the mountain with her fingernails — don't expect the boy who was taken. Expect the song. Expect a fragment that knows your name the way a bell knows the hand that struck it." Vess held her gaze. "Half a soul is not half a person. It's a whole person, concentrated. Everything essential, boiled down. Whatever he was at his truest — that's what's in the jar. The rest is mortar."

Petra swallowed. "And if the essential part of him is stubbornness and bad jokes?"

"Then the mountain has been holding up its own ceiling with stubbornness and bad jokes for three years," Vess said, "and honestly, that's the funniest thing I've heard in a decade. Go to sleep, taster. Tomorrow I'll get you to the vault."

Petra went. Vess stood alone at the junction a while longer, listening to the mountain breathe around her — the slow mineral inhale, the patience in the stones — and became aware, gradually, of a sound rising up through the floors.

Faint. Distant. Coming from the direction of the vaults.

Someone down there was singing. Badly. Off-key, with tremendous conviction, a street-song about a fishmonger's daughter and a runaway cart.

Vess pressed her hand flat against the wall — against the mortar, against the ground-up years, against whatever piece of the mountain had once been her — and found, to her astonishment, that the wall was humming along.

"Well," she said to no one. "That's inconvenient."

Because she had read the dissolution schedules her whole long servitude, and she knew what fifty percent meant, and she knew what the mountain did next. At sixty percent, the singing stopped. At seventy, the jar went dark. And the Underking had summoned the new taster to his table for tomorrow evening — a private supper, the steward had announced, just the two of them, a little ceremony of welcome.

Contracts were served at supper. And Vess had just spent an entire evening teaching the bitterest cup in creation exactly how the pouring worked.

She turned toward the kitchens to sharpen knives that would never cut anything, and began, for the first time in forty years, to plan a menu.
