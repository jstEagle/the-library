## 10. The King Beneath

They came for her at what the Undercourt called morning, though there was no sun to argue with.

Petra had learned to mark time by the kitchen instead — by the great cauldrons that simmered without fire, by the moment Vess appeared in the doorway with her hollow eyes and her ledger of duties, by the hour when the soul-servants grew faintest, as if the mountain breathed them thin and then let them thicken again. It was during the thickening hour that two of them arrived for her: a pair of courtiers whose faces were beautiful and slightly wrong, like fruit waxed over rot.

"The King Beneath requests your tongue," said the one on the left.

That was all. No title, no honorifics, no explanation. In the palace above, a summons from the queen came wrapped in three layers of ceremony and one layer of threat. Here it came naked, which Petra supposed was its own kind of honesty.

She wiped her hands on her apron — she'd been given an apron, white as bone, and had decided within the first day that wearing it was easier than arguing about it — and followed.

The corridors of the Undercourt did not behave like corridors. They curved when they should have been straight, and twice Petra was certain they walked downhill for a quarter mile only to arrive somewhere higher than they'd started. The walls were veined with something pale and slow, and when she brushed her fingertips against one — because of course she did; she'd never met a wall she didn't want to taste-test — she caught a flavor like old candle smoke and patience.

*Wax,* she thought. *Or something pretending to be wax.*

The courtiers walked in silence. The soul-servants they passed bowed as they went, and each bow cost them something; Petra could see it, a slight dimming around the edges, like a lamp turned down a notch. She made herself stop counting after thirty. Counting was how you wept, and she couldn't afford to wept-out before whatever came next.

"You're frightened," said the left courtier, without looking at her.

"I'm thinking," Petra said. "It looks similar from the outside."

Something that might have been amusement flickered across the right one's face. Or perhaps it was indigestion. Down here, who could say.

* * *

The throne room — if it was a throne room — was a cavern the size of the Nightmarket square, and it was set for dinner.

That was the first thing that undid her. Not the scale, not the dark, but the table: a single slab of black stone running the length of the hall, laid with plates of hammered tin, cups of horn, candles that burned with flames the color of deep water. It looked, absurdly, like the long table in Sefa's kitchen on festival nights, when everyone crammed together and Pip stole rolls off the passing trays and Tam always saved her the corner piece with the most crust.

Someone down here had studied warmth. Someone down here had built a copy of it, the way a magpie builds a nest out of stolen silver.

At the far end sat the king.

He was smaller than she expected. That was the second undoing. She had imagined something vast, something geological — a thing shaped like the mountain's hunger given limbs. Instead he might have been anyone's grandfather: narrow shoulders, long clever hands folded on the table, a face lined like a page written on too many times. His eyes were the only wrongness, and they were wrong enough. They were the color of the space between stars, which was to say they were the color of nothing at all, and they were fixed on her with an attention so total it felt like being opened.

"Petra Blackwood," he said. His voice was dry and kind, like autumn leaves. "Royal taster. Keeper of the poison ledger. Three years, four months, eleven days in service to Queen Maren." He gestured to the chair beside him — beside him, not across from him; the seat of an equal, or a guest, or bait. "Sit. Eat with me. I have been told my hospitality is impeccable."

"By whom?" Petra asked, and sat, because refusing would tell him more than sitting did.

"By no one," he said, pleased. "That is the trouble with eternity. One's references die."

A servant — a soul, dim as a guttering wick — set a bowl before her. Broth, golden and clear, steam rising off it in slow spirals. The smell reached her and her stomach betrayed her instantly: it smelled like chicken stock and leek and thyme, like every cold evening of her childhood that had ended well.

"Taste," the king said gently. "It is why you are here."

She picked up the spoon. Her hand did not shake, and she was quietly proud of that.

Seven flavors, then. She catalogued them the way she catalogued everything, the ledger in her blood opening its pages. Salt — sea salt, honest. Fat — marrow, rendered long. Thyme. Leek, sweet not sharp, grown in real soil under a real sun, which meant it had come up through the toll-gate somehow, bought or bargained. A whisper of pepper. A base note of bone.

And beneath all of it, faint as a fingerprint in wet clay: *cold. Mineral. Wrong.* The same thread she'd tasted under the arsenic meant for Queen Maren, back when the world still had stars. Undercourt work. Undercourt seasoning.

"There's mountain in this," she said aloud.

The king smiled. It transformed his face into something almost lovable, which was precisely the point. "There is mountain in everything down here. You breathe it. You will taste it in your own spit by week's end." He lifted his own spoon and ate, unhurried. "You found the seventh flavor faster than any taster I have kept. And I have kept many."

"What happened to them?"

"They stayed," he said simply, "until staying became their whole flavor. Then they became part of the broth, in a manner of speaking." He said it without cruelty, the way a farmer discusses rotation of crops. "But you are not here to discuss my kitchen staff. You are here because you have been asking questions about a debt."

Petra set down her spoon. "Tam."

"Tam." He savored the name. "Do you know what I love about names? They are contracts. Every time someone speaks yours, they renew the terms." He laced his fingers. "Three years ago, a man named Corvane signed away a soul to keep the border wards standing. The wards protected his city. His city held his daughter. He chose, as all fathers choose, badly and bravely. The soul he offered was not his own. It belonged to a young man of the Nightmarket, a baker's boy with no family and no protection and — this is the part I find delicious — no idea what he was worth."

"He's worth everything," Petra said. Her voice came out flat and hard, a knife laid on a table.

"Yes," the king agreed warmly. "I know. That's why I took him."

The candles guttered. Somewhere in the dark of the cavern, water dripped with the regularity of a clock.

"You've come to take him back," the king continued. "I admire it. Loyalty is rare above ground these days; you people are so busy with your laws and your wardens and your fear of small magics that you've forgotten what devotion tastes like." He leaned forward, and his nothing-colored eyes caught the blue candleflame. "So let us speak plainly, taster, as one professional to another. You cannot take him. The contract is sound. His soul is half-dissolved into my foundations already — he hums in my walls. But."

"But," Petra repeated.

"I am starving."

It came out of him suddenly, that word, and for a moment the courteous grandfather-shape thinned and she saw underneath: something vast and hollow, a hunger with a horizon, an emptiness so old it had forgotten what it was empty *of*. Then it was gone, folded away, and he was smiling again.

"Starving," she said carefully.

"Eternally, structurally, fundamentally. I eat contracts, child. Debts. Obligations. The binding force between a promise and its keeping. It is nourishing the way gravel is filling — it stops the ache but never cures it. And lately—" his long hands spread, eloquent, ruined— "lately even that has gone thin. Souls taste of fear and duty and very little else. Everyone above ground owes everyone else. There is no *distinction* anymore. No savor."

"And mine?" Petra asked, though she already knew. Vess had warned her. *He eats contracts, not food.*

"Yours," the king said, and here his voice changed, went soft and wondering, the voice of a man describing a dream, "yours interests me enormously. Three years of poisons. Arsenic, belladonna, salts of lead, things the palace apothecaries won't name. Your soul has been marinated in death since you were barely grown. Do you understand what that means, down here where essence is flavor? You are a vintage, Petra Blackwood. You are a dish that has never once been served."

The bowl of broth steamed between them. Petra found, distantly, that she was hungry, and hated herself for it.

"You're offering me a trade," she said. "My soul for Tam's. Debt transferred."

"I am offering you a place at my table forever," the king corrected, "in exchange for the transfer. Which is not the same thing, and is infinitely better. Think about what you've eaten since you descended. Bone wards. Memory-tolls. Broth seasoned with mountain. Nothing here has hurt you, has it? Nothing here has *poisoned* you. Because I do not poison my guests. I feed them."

He rose, unhurried, and walked the length of the table toward her, and the candles leaned toward him like flowers toward sun.

"Above ground, you are a criminal twice over. Unlicensed charm-wright. Illegal magic in the Nightmarket, sold to dockworkers and thieves. If the Wardens ever caught you — and they are closer than you think — you would hang, or worse, be *useful*, which is worse. Your found family would scatter to protect themselves. Your queen—" he paused delicately— "your queen lists you as reserve collateral in a steward's ledger. Did you find that page? I sent you hints. I do love a reader."

Cold walked up Petra's spine. *Reserve collateral.* She had told no one she'd found it. Hadn't even told herself, really, not in words.

"Here," the king went on, "you would be honored. First taster of my table. My kitchens would become yours. Vess would serve under you — she is tired, though she'd never say it. You could teach. You could build. No Wardens. No law but appetite, and appetite is the only honest law I know." He stopped beside her chair. Close enough now that she could smell him: dust and honey and something older than both. "And your brother walks free. Whole. Above ground, in the sun, baking bread, remembering you fondly until remembering is all that's left of you. Is that not a fair price? Is that not, in fact, the exact bargain you climbed down here to make?"

It was. That was the terrible thing. It was nearly word for word the bargain she'd rehearsed on the Stair of Teeth, paying tolls with her mother's bread, telling herself she'd offer herself up the moment she found the vault.

And he knew it. Of course he knew it. He'd set the table for it.

"May I taste the offer?" Petra asked.

The king blinked. Then he laughed — a real laugh, delighted, rusty, as if he hadn't used it in a century. "You may. You *must*. What kind of taster accepts a dish unseen?" He extended his open hand, palm up, and hovering above it appeared — nothing visible, nothing at all, but her tongue woke anyway, tasting the air above his palm the way she'd taste steam off a suspect stew.

She tasted it.

And it tasted like *home.*

Not metaphorically. Not approximately. It tasted exactly, precisely, devastatingly like the Nightmarket at dusk: frying onions and woodsmoke and Sefa's spiced tea, Pip's laughter with dirt in it, the wet-stone smell after rain, Tam's kitchen on a baking morning with the ovens roaring and flour hanging gold in the light. It tasted like belonging. It tasted like every door she'd ever knocked on opening at once, like a chair pulled out for her at every table, like arms around her shoulders and no one asking her to be anything other than exactly what she was.

Her eyes filled before she could stop them.

"Oh," the king murmured, watching her face with those starless eyes, tender as a physician. "There it is. Yes. That is what I put in the offer, taster. That is my signature seasoning. Every soul I have ever taken has tasted that, right at the end, right before the swallowing. Home. The one flavor none of you can refuse." He crouched to her level, and his voice dropped to a confiding whisper. "The others fought it. The proud ones, the holy ones. They bit down on their hatred and choked me for a decade or two. But you — you've been starving for it longer than I've been starving for you. Haven't you? Orphan of the market. Taster of poisons. Girl who makes luck-knots for strangers because no one ever made one for her."

Petra sat very still.

Because he was right, and that was the horror of it. He was *right*. Three years of arsenic and nightshade hadn't killed the girl who wanted a family; they'd just taught her to hide her, fold her small, keep her safe behind the ledger. And now here was a thing older than kingdoms holding that girl out in its open palm, saying *I see her, I kept her warm, come and get her.*

It was the most dangerous poison she had ever tasted. Not because it would kill her. Because it would *keep* her.

She thought of Tam humming the street-song in a jar of starlight. Half a soul, and still singing. Still hers.

She thought of Vess's hollow eyes, and the way the servants dimmed when they bowed.

She thought — unexpectedly, absurdly — of the corner piece with the most crust, and how Tam always gave it to her without being asked, and how that had never once required a contract.

Petra closed her mouth over the taste of home. Swallowed it down into the ledger, filed it, labeled it. *Poison. Class: unknown. Antidote: pending.*

"No," she said.

The word fell into the cavern and rang there.

The king's smile did not falter, but something behind it shifted, tectonic, patient. "No?"

"Not yet," Petra said, and stood, and was surprised to find her legs held. "I'm a professional, Your Majesty. And the first rule of my profession is that you never accept a dish on the first offering — not even — *especially* not — when it's perfect. Perfect dishes are the ones you test twice." She met his starless eyes. "I'll taste your offer again tomorrow. And the day after. And every day until I've read every flavor in it. Because I think—" and here her voice steadied into something like her market-stall voice, warm and sure, the voice that sold luck to dockworkers— "I think there's something in it you don't want me to find. Something under the home. The same way there was mountain under the arsenic."

For a long moment the king said nothing. The candles burned blue. The water dripped its patient clock.

Then he laughed again, softer this time, and inclined his head like a duelist acknowledging a touch.

"Tomorrow, then," he said. "And the day after. Take all the time you need, Petra Blackwood. Eternity is generous that way." He turned back toward the head of the table, and paused, and spoke without turning around. "One courtesy, since you've earned it. Your friend's jar — the vault where he sings — it is on the east stair, past the gallery of debts. Vess knows the way. She has carried jars before."

Petra went cold. "Why would you tell me that?"

The king settled back into his chair and lifted his spoon, and his voice was mild as milk.

"Because a meal is best," he said, "when the guest has something left to lose."

* * *

Vess was waiting in the corridor outside, hands folded, face unreadable. She fell into step beside Petra without a word, guiding her through the crooked halls, and it was only at the third turning that she spoke, low and quick, eyes forward.

"You refused him."

"For now."

"No one refuses him." Vess's voice cracked on the last word, just slightly, like ice under a boot. "In four hundred years, no one has tasted the offer and said no."

"Well." Petra kept walking, toward the east stair, toward the jar, toward the sound of a half-soul singing a market song in the dark. "Then he should have seasoned it better."

Behind them, faint through the stone, the King Beneath began — softly, contentedly, terribly — to hum.
