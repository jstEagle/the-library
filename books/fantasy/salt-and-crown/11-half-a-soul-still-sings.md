## 11. Half a Soul Still Sings

Vess walked like a woman who had learned, long ago, that the mountain was always listening. Her steps made no sound on the bone-pale corridors of the Undercourt — not because she was light, but because she had practiced silence until it became a kind of armor. Petra followed in her wake and felt every one of her own footfalls echo back at her, as if the walls were counting.

"You walk loud," Vess said without turning. "In the Undercourt, loud things get tasted."

"I'm a taster," Petra said. "I'd like to see it try."

Vess glanced back over her shoulder. In the gloom her eyes were the color of water left standing too long in a cup. "That's what I said once," she said. "Forty years ago. Give or take a decade. Down here we don't keep very good track."

They went down. The Undercourt did not believe in stairs so much as in gradual surrender — passages that sloped deeper by degrees, worn smooth as river stones, lit by veins of pale mineral light that pulsed faintly, like something breathing under the skin of the world. Petra's tongue tingled with each step. The whole mountain tasted of cold iron and old promises, a flavor she was coming to know the way she knew arsenic: intimately, professionally, with a rising dread she kept folded small and tucked behind her ribs.

"Where are you taking me?" she asked.

"To the vault." Vess's voice stayed flat, but her hands, Petra noticed, were clasped tight enough to whiten at the knuckles. "You wanted to know if your brother is still in there. Souls down here get used for mortar, for lamp-oil, for flavoring. Most of them scatter within a year. But his—" She stopped walking. For a moment she simply stood in the pulsing dark, a hollow-eyed woman shaped out of patience and regret. "His is being kept. That's either very good news or very bad news, and I honestly don't know which."

"Kept how?"

"You'll see."

* * *

The last corridor before the vault narrowed until they had to go single file, and the walls changed. Petra ran her fingers along them without meaning to — an old habit from the Nightmarket stalls, checking grain and weave — and snatched her hand back. The stone was warm. Not stone-warm. *Skin*-warm, faintly, terribly, like the flank of a sleeping animal.

"It's fed," Vess said quietly. "The mountain. All these years, it's been fed. What do you think a soul tastes like to a thing like this?"

Petra thought of Tam. Tam, who had taught her to pick pockets at nine and to forgive herself at twelve; Tam, who once ate a spoiled oyster meant for her because he could stomach anything and she couldn't; Tam, who had held her hair back when the poisons first started and told her, laughing around his own nausea, that she was the bravest coward he knew. She thought of him dissolved into this warm, hungry dark, spread thin as butter across miles of stone, and her throat closed so hard she tasted copper.

"He's not mortar," she said. "Not yet."

"No," Vess agreed. "Not yet. Which is why we're going to move quickly, and why you're going to do exactly what I say, and why—" She hesitated. It was the first hesitation Petra had seen in her. "Why you should prepare yourself. Kept souls aren't... tidy. They're not people anymore. They're what's left when the person has been poured out. You may find him less than you're hoping for."

"He hummed," Petra said. "In my dreams, since I came down here. He hums the street-song."

Vess looked at her for a long moment. Something moved behind the still water of her eyes — recognition, maybe, or grief for a younger self who had once believed dreams meant something.

"The vault is ahead," she said. "I can't go in with you. My contract forbids it." A pause. "My contract forbids most things. It does not forbid telling you that the lock tastes intentions. So want the right thing, taster. Whatever else you want — bury it deep."

* * *

The vault door was iron, but iron the way a scar is skin: grown over whatever wound had been there first. It stood twice Petra's height, unmarked except for a shallow bowl set into its center at the height of a mouth. As she approached, the mineral light dimmed, and the door began — softly, horribly — to breathe.

She understood then what Vess had meant about taste. The air thickened against her tongue, probing, patient, a flavorless attention that nonetheless read her like a page. *Want*, it seemed to say. *What do you want?*

Petra closed her eyes and let the truth rise, because there was no use burying it; it was the only thing in her that wasn't buried already.

*I want my brother back.*

The door exhaled. The scar-iron split along a seam no eye could find, and swung inward on darkness that was not empty.

* * *

Inside, the vault was small — a round chamber no bigger than Sefa's storeroom above ground — and it was full of jars.

Dozens of them. Hundreds. They lined shelves carved from the living rock, and each one held a soft, sourceless glow: blue-white, gold-flecked, green as foxfire. Starlight. Real starlight, Petra realized, her heart slamming against her ribs — starlight harvested from the sky itself, from nights before the wards fell, before the bargain, before everything. Each jar was a stolen night. Each jar was someone.

And in the center of the room, on a pedestal of black glass, sat a jar larger than the rest, and brighter, and it was humming.

Petra crossed the floor without deciding to. Her legs simply went, the way a compass needle goes, and she was kneeling in front of the glass with her palms flat against it before she remembered that touching things in the Undercourt was how contracts happened. Too late. The glass was warm. Of course it was warm. Everything here was warm, and hungry, and alive.

"Tam," she whispered.

The light inside the jar stirred.

It was hard to describe what she saw, because it wasn't a shape, exactly — more like the memory of a shape, the way after staring at the sun you carry its ghost on your eyelids. There was a brightness at the center that leaned toward her, that pressed against the glass the way a cat presses against a familiar hand, and the hum resolved, note by trembling note, into melody.

The street-song.

*Down the crooked stair and through the fish-smoke gray,* it went, off-key, half-remembered, the way Pip sang it, the way all of them sang it, the way Tam had sung it a hundred times on the walk home with stolen chestnuts cooling in their pockets. *Oh, the market never closes and the market never sleeps—*

Tam's voice. Not the whole of it — a fragment, a shard, a single verse of a man — but unmistakably, devastatingly *him*. He didn't have lips anymore, or lungs, or any of the machinery of singing, and he was singing anyway, because three years of swallowing poison had taught Petra that some things persist past all reason, and apparently love was one of them.

"Oh," Petra said. It came out very small. "Oh, you stupid, brave, *terrible* liar. You met someone from below the mountain, you said. You wouldn't say who. You knew. Some part of you knew, and you didn't tell me, because you were going to handle it alone—"

The light flared, indignant, and the song stuttered into a different rhythm — faster, teasing, the tune he used to hum when he was mocking her. When she'd been fourteen and mooning over a dockworker and Tam had composed an entire insulting ballad about it on the spot.

"Don't you dare," Petra said, laughing and crying in the same breath, which was a skill she'd perfected sometime around chapter two of this disaster. "Don't you dare be funny in there. Do you know where I am? Do you know what I've eaten this week?"

The light pressed harder against the glass. And then — she would swear this until her dying day — the hum shifted again, lower, gentler, and it was the other song. The one he'd made up for her, back when the poisons started, back when she spent her nights shaking and convinced she'd die tasting someone else's dinner. He'd sung it through the wall between their rooms, badly, every night, until she slept.

*Brave little ledger,* it went. *Counting up the dark. Every bitter drop you swallow, you swallow for our sake—*

He'd only ever sung it through the wall. He'd never known she heard the words.

Now she heard them from inside a jar, from half a soul in a starless world, and Petra Blackwood, royal food-taster, veteran of forty-one poisons, put her forehead against the warm glass and wept like the orphan she'd been before the market found her.

* * *

She didn't know how long she knelt there. Long enough that her knees ached. Long enough that the tears dried salt-stiff on her face, and she thought, distantly, that even her grief would probably be unpleasant to eat.

When she finally lifted her head, she noticed what grief had kept her from seeing: the other jars.

They were all leaning. Subtly, impossibly, the way flowers lean toward a window — every jar on every shelf tilted a fraction toward the center of the room, toward Tam's bright pedestal, toward the sound of the street-song still threading faintly through the dark. And as she watched, a jar near the wall flickered in rhythm with his melody. Then another. Then a dozen, a hundred, a soft constellation of imprisoned lights picking up the tune in a hundred broken keys.

They all knew the song. Or they knew what songs were *for*.

"They can hear him," Petra breathed. "They're all still in there. All of them."

"That," said Vess from the doorway, "is the problem."

Petra hadn't heard her come back. She turned, wiping her face with the heel of her hand, and saw the steward standing rigid in the threshold, her long hands gripping the frame as if the corridor beyond might blow her away.

"Your contract lets you stand in a doorway?"

"My contract lets me stand exactly there and no further." Vess's gaze swept the vault, the leaning jars, the singing light, and her voice dropped to almost nothing. "Listen to me, taster, because I will only be able to say this once, and then I must forget I said it. The king doesn't keep these souls as trophies. He keeps them as *reserves*. When the mountain runs low — and it is always running low, hunger like his is a well with no bottom — he drinks from the jars. One at a time. Slowly. Your brother's jar is large because his soul was bargained whole, and whole souls pour slower."

"How long?"

"A year. Perhaps two, if the feeding goes gently." Vess's eyes fixed on Petra's face. "But here is what matters. Did you notice them leaning? They respond to him. To *song*. To anything that reminds them of being people." Her knuckles whitened on the doorframe. "Forty years I've carried trays down this corridor, and no jar has ever done what your brother just did. No jar has ever made the others sing."

"What does that mean?"

"It means," Vess said, and now her flat voice cracked, just barely, like ice on a pond, "that he isn't dissolving the way the others dissolve. It means something in him is *holding*. And it means the king will notice. Hunger notices everything. When he realizes that one of his reserves is fighting back — that one of them remembers — he won't wait a year. He'll drink that jar dry within the week, just to stop the others hoping."

The song faltered. The light in the jar dimmed, as if it too had heard.

Petra rose. Her knees protested. Her hands, she noticed, had gone perfectly steady — the same terrible calm that settled over her whenever the queen's plate arrived laced with something lethal, the calm that meant her body had decided fear was a luxury.

"Then I don't have a year," she said. "Or a week."

"No," Vess said. "You have until the next banquet. Three days. The king hosts one at every dark of the moon, and at banquets he..." She hesitated again, that second crack in the ice. "At banquets, he serves the reserves. A course of starlight, one jar at a time, to remind the court what he is."

"Then I'll be there," Petra said. "Tasting. That's what I do."

Vess studied her for a long moment — the steady hands, the salt-tracked face, the woman built out of swallowed deaths — and something almost like hope flickered across her worn features, brief and startled, like a fish surfacing in dark water.

"You understand," she said, "that a taster at the Underking's table doesn't taste for poison. There is no poison down here but him."

"Yes," Petra said. "I know. I'm not tasting for poison."

She turned back to the jar. The light had steadied, pressing against the glass, waiting, trusting, humming its half-remembered verse. She laid her palm flat against the warmth one last time.

"Hold on," she whispered. "Keep singing. Loud as you can. Let every jar in this mountain hear you."

And the light flared — bright, defiant, gloriously off-key — and all around the vault, in a hundred stolen voices, the stars began to sing.
