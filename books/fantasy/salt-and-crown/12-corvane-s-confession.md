## 12. Corvane's Confession

Corvane had kept the palace ledgers for thirty-one years, and he intended to die the way he had lived: with everything in its proper column.

The trouble was that dying, it turned out, was not a tidy business. The pact-backlash had begun three days after the stars went out — as if the mountain, having collected its payment, had decided to audit the broker — and it was eating him from the inside out. It started in his hands. Then his voice went grainy, like flour sifted through old cloth. Now there were mornings when he woke and could not remember his daughter's face, only the fact of her, the way a man remembers a sum without remembering what it purchased.

He sat at his desk in the steward's office beneath the palace kitchens, where the smell of bread drifted down through the floorboards like a benediction he no longer deserved, and he arranged his affairs.

There were four things on the desk.

A ledger, bound in green leather, the one with the false spine.

A letter to his daughter, Wren, which he had rewritten eleven times and which still began badly.

A small glass vial of poppy syrup, for when the pain grew teeth.

And a carafe of water, because whatever else he was, Corvane of the Undercourt accounts was not going to die thirsty in front of witnesses.

He heard her boots on the stair before he saw her. He had known she would come. Petra Blackwood tasted arsenic meant for queens and asked no one's permission to follow the flavor home, and Corvane had watched her do it for three years with the particular admiration of a man watching a fire work its way toward his own house.

"Steward." She stood in the doorway, and she did not look like the girl who charmed dockworkers' luck-knots in the Nightmarket. She looked like someone who had been underground and come back changed by the air down there. "I need the toll-gate records. The original ones."

"I imagine you do." He gestured to the chair across from him. "Sit down, Miss Blackwood. What I have to give you will go easier sitting."

She didn't sit. She never sat when she was frightened; he'd noticed that years ago. Fear made her stand very straight, like a candle flame deciding to be dignified about the wind.

"You're ill," she said.

"I'm *dying*, which is illness carried through to its natural conclusion. Sit. Please. My legs have opinions I'd rather not share standing up."

That, at least, won a ghost of a smile from her, and she took the chair. Good. He wanted her seated for this. There were things a person could not hear properly on their feet; the body got busy preparing to run.

* * *

"The night the wards fell," Corvane said, "you'll want to know how it was done. Everyone wants to know how. How does a kingdom sell a soul? What words? What ceremony? What monstrous ritual?" He turned the green ledger toward her but did not open it. "The answer is arithmetic. That's the horror of it, Miss Blackwood. No ritual at all. A name written in a ledger. A signature. Two witnesses who were paid to look elsewhere. The Undercourt doesn't need blood and candles. It needs bookkeeping."

Petra's eyes went to the ledger. "Whose name?"

"You know whose name." His voice cracked on it, grain and gravel. "Tam. Tam o' the Docks, they called him, though he hadn't worked the docks since he was twelve. Tam, who pulled you out of the harbor when you were nine and too proud to admit you couldn't swim. Tam, who has eaten at my table — my *staff table* — every feast day for six years because your found family always cooked too much and never learned to count."

"How do you know that?"

"Because I signed away his soul, girl. Do you think I didn't learn everything about him first? The Undercourt requires specificity. A soul poorly described is a contract poorly bound." He opened the ledger at last, to the page he had marked with a ribbon, and turned it so she could read.

She read. He watched her read. Her lips moved slightly, the way they did when she was tasting something invisible — he had seen her do it over soup, over wine, over the queen's morning tea — and he understood, with a lurch, that she was tasting the page itself. Tasting the ink. Tasting the transaction.

"That's not just his name," she said quietly. "There's a second entry. Below it. In different ink."

"Yes."

"*Reserve collateral: P. Blackwood.*"

"Ah," said Corvane, and found that all his careful arrangements — the letter, the vial, the water — had not prepared him for the moment of actually saying it aloud. "You've found yourself in my books."

"Explain it to me," Petra said, and her voice was very level, the way it went when she had swallowed something bitter and refused to let her face know. "Explain it slowly, Steward, because I am going to be fair with you exactly once, and then I am going to be something else entirely."

* * *

So he told her. All of it, in order, because order was the only dignity left to him.

Three years ago, Wren had been nineteen and brilliant and employed in the royal observatory, cataloguing starlight the way other girls catalogued suitors. And Wren had made a mistake — a small one, an honest one, the kind of mistake that only ever happens to people who are trying very hard to be good. She had copied a ward-formula wrong. One symbol inverted. A mirror-image where there should have been a plain stroke.

It would have been nothing, except that the border wards were old, and tired, and held together by formulas exactly like the one she'd botched, and the mountain beneath the palace had been leaning against them for centuries like a drunk against a door.

The wards sagged. The mountain noticed. And things below the mountain began, very politely, to inquire.

"The crown had two options," Corvane said. "Evacuate the city — which would have meant admitting the wards were failing, which would have meant panic, riots, the eastern provinces declaring independence inside a fortnight — or negotiate. The queen chose negotiation. I was sent to conduct it, because I was the only man in the palace who could read the old contract-scripts, and because—" he smiled, and hated the smile, "—because I was expendable. A steward. Not a minister, not a general. If the talks went badly, the crown could disavow me in a sentence."

"And the price?"

"The Underking asked for a soul. Not any soul — he was specific about that too, in his way. He wanted a soul *connected*. Rooted. A soul with threads running through it into the living world, because a lonely soul is thin fare, Miss Blackwood, and the king beneath the mountain has been starving a very long time." Corvane's hands, on the ledger, had gone white at the knuckles. "They gave me a list of candidates. Courtiers. Prisoners. A list, like a menu. And none of them — none — had what he wanted. So I went looking myself. And I found a young man in the Nightmarket with more family than blood, more friends than coin, a boy who had once carried a drowning stranger half a mile through freezing water without knowing her name."

"You could have chosen anyone," Petra whispered. "Anyone in this city."

"I know." He said it plainly, because dressing it up would have been worse than the crime. "I told myself the wards mattered more than one life. I told myself the mathematics were clear. Three hundred thousand souls above ground against one below. I told myself he wouldn't feel it — that the collection would be painless, that the Undercourt keeps its word about such things." He laughed, and it came out wrong, a dry rattle like a shutter in wind. "Do you know what the worst part is? The worst part is that I checked the arithmetic again last week, alone, at this desk, and the arithmetic is still *correct*. The wards held for three years. The city slept safe. Every cold morning, every market day, every wedding — bought with him. I would make the trade again. That is what damnation is, I think. Not doing the evil thing. Knowing, afterward, that you'd do it twice."

Petra was silent a long moment. When she spoke, her voice had gone strange and soft. "And the queen. Did she know it was Tam?"

Here it was. The last stone, the one he had been carrying so long it had grown into his spine.

"She knew," Corvane said. "I brought her the candidate list myself — my own additions, at the end. She read the names. She stopped at his. She knew whose boy he was; she'd seen him at the midwinter feast, seen you two singing in the kitchens with flour on your noses. And she said—" his throat closed, and he forced it open, because he had not survived thirty-one years of court service by flinching at the hard sentences, "—she said, '*Not a courtier. Someone unattached to the succession. Someone the court won't miss.*' And she initialled the page."

The silence that followed had weight. Corvane felt it settle on both of them like snow.

"She let me believe I'd chosen alone," he went on, quieter now. "All these years, she let me carry it solo, because a steward with a secret is a loyal steward. And when the debt came due and the wards fell anyway — because of course they fell, the bargain was never meant to hold forever, it was meant to hold until *her* reign ended and the problem became someone else's crown — she sent *you* to investigate. The reserve collateral, sent to sniff around her own crime." He shook his head. "I have served that woman for three decades, Miss Blackwood, and I want you to understand: she is not stupid, she is not cruel in the way villains are cruel, and she would let the whole world burn rather than be caught having made a mistake. Those three things can live in one person quite comfortably. I've had dinner with her."

"Why are you telling me this?" Petra asked. "Why now?"

"Because I'm dying, and dead men's secrets help no one, and because—" he paused, and reached under the desk, and set the last item beside the ledger. A knife. Small, plain-hilted, wrapped in oilcloth. But the blade had a color to it, a faint blue-grey shimmer, like frost on iron. "Because I owe a debt of my own, and unlike hers, mine can still be paid."

* * *

Petra looked at the knife but didn't touch it. "What is it?"

"Ward-iron. Drawn from the border-wards themselves, before they fell — the smith who made it died making it, so don't ask for another. It's the only metal in the world the Underking's contracts can't bind. And it matters because of what I'm about to tell you, which is the single most valuable piece of information I possess, and I am giving it to you free, which should tell you how desperate I am to leave this world owing less than I arrived with." He leaned forward. The backlash-pain flared in his chest and he rode it, jaw tight, and continued. "The Underking's power is contracts. Every soul in that mountain is bound by agreement — coerced, deceived, traded, but *agreed*. That is the architecture of his entire court. And the oldest law of contract, older than the mountain, older than the king himself, is this: *no soul freely offered can be taken twice.*"

Petra frowned. "Meaning what, exactly?"

"Meaning the binding breaks. Completely. If a soul enters his domain by choice — truly, freely, with full knowledge of the price — then whatever claim he held on it before is void. It's the flaw in his whole edifice, Miss Blackwood. He cannot abide a gift. Everything in his kingdom was taken, and so everything in his kingdom can be contested by the one thing he's never been given." Corvane tapped the ledger, on Tam's name. "Your friend was *sold*. By me. Without consent. The claim is valid — foul, but valid. But if Tam were to return to that mountain freely, offering himself, knowing the cost..." He let it hang.

"He'd break the claim," Petra breathed. "The old claim dies and the new one — "

"—is a gift. And gifts, in the Undercourt, are poison." Corvane managed something that was almost his old dry smile. "The king knows this law better than anyone alive, which is why he structures everything to prevent it. Why his offers are always traps dressed as kindness. Why he'd sooner starve than accept a freely given thing." He paused. "Which brings us, unpleasantly, to you."

"To me."

"To the second entry. *P. Blackwood, reserve collateral.*" He met her eyes. "I wrote that clause. The queen approved it. If Tam's soul had proven... insufficient, you were next. You have been currency in this palace for three years, child, and no one told you, and I am sorry in a way that will outlive me. But listen — listen — because this is the part that matters. Your name is in his books. He holds a paper claim on you, same as Tam. Which means the same law applies. Walk into that mountain freely, offer him what he already claims to own, and watch the claim eat itself."

Petra stared at him. He watched her do the arithmetic — she was good at arithmetic, whatever she claimed; you didn't survive three years of poison-tasting without a ledger mind — and he watched her arrive at the answer he dreaded.

"You're telling me to give him my soul."

"I'm telling you the knife cuts both ways, and I'm giving you the knife." He pushed the ward-iron toward her. "What you cut with it is your own affair."

* * *

She picked up the knife. Turned it over. Tested the edge with her thumb, the way market folk test everything, and he saw her taste the air off the blade and flinch slightly — ward-iron, even dormant, bit the tongue of anyone who could taste magic.

"Corvane." She said his name without title, and it was the first time anyone had done that in eleven years, and he found he didn't mind. "Wren. Your daughter. Does she know?"

"No. And she never will, if I have anything to say about it. The letter says I loved her and that the observatory was lucky to have her, and it is silent on all other subjects, and if you ever meet her and she asks, you will tell her the same." His voice roughened. "She inverted one symbol. She was nineteen. I have seen queens do worse before breakfast."

"I won't lie for you."

"I'm not asking you to lie. I'm asking you to be kind, which is harder, and which is why I'm asking *you*." He coughed, and the cough went on too long, and when it finished there was blood on his handkerchief, dark and arterial, and he folded it away with the neatness of a lifetime. "Go. The toll-gate records are in the green ledger, third section, behind the false spine. The gate itself is under the old granary on Tanner's Row — the stair of teeth, the Undercourt servants call it. And Miss Blackwood—"

She stopped at the door.

"—the memory-tolls on that stair take the sweetest things first. It's how the mountain feeds. Pay attention to what it takes from you, and what it leaves. What it leaves is what you actually can't spare." He smiled, genuinely this time, tired and crooked. "That's not steward's wisdom. That's just a dying man's guess. But I've made a career of educated guesses, and I'd hate to stop now."

Petra stood in the doorway a moment longer, the knife wrapped in its oilcloth, the ledger under her arm. She looked at him — at the bloodied handkerchief, the eleven-times-rewritten letter, the vial of poppy syrup — and something in her face shifted from judgment to something more complicated and more painful, which was recognition.

"For what it's worth," she said, "the arithmetic was wrong. Three hundred thousand souls against one. It's never division, Corvane. It's multiplication. One soul, multiplied through everyone who loves him." She almost smiled. "You of all people should have known better than to trust a number that comes out clean."

Then she was gone, boots on the stairs, and Corvane sat alone in his office beneath the smell of bread, and laughed until it hurt, and then hurt until it didn't, and poured himself a glass of water, because whatever else he was, he was not going to die thirsty.

Above him, faint through the floorboards, he could hear the kitchen staff beginning the day's baking. And beneath him — far beneath, past bone-wards and toll-gates and a stair carved from things he preferred not to name — the mountain waited, patient as a creditor, for the girl with the poisoned blood and the borrowed knife to come down and make her withdrawal.

He hoped, with the last of his strength, that she'd read the fine print.

He'd written it himself, after all. And Corvane had never once, in thirty-one years, gotten the numbers wrong — only the people.
