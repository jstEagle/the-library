## 13. The Price of Passage Back

Sefa had always believed that the worst night of her life was the one the stars went out. She revised that opinion the following evening, when Petra's note arrived pinned to the market board by a knife — a *knife*, which was Petra's way of saying *urgent* without wasting ink on it.

The note said: *Gone down the mountain. Don't follow. Feed the gulls. — P.*

"She wants us to feed the gulls," said Pip, who was eleven and read everything at a shout. "Why does she want us to feed the gulls?"

"Because she doesn't want us to follow her," Sefa said, "and she thinks we're idiots."

"We're not idiots," said Brannick, who was large, slow to anger, and currently holding a wheel of cheese he had not paid for because the cheesemonger had fled the city and left his stall unattended, which in Brannick's personal theology constituted a gift.

"No," Sefa agreed. "We're orphans with a smuggling operation and a grudge against the sky. Which is worse."

The market had gone strange since the stars died. Not empty — never that; the Nightmarket would keep trading through the actual end of the world, and probably a week after — but strange. The lamplighters worked double shifts. The Wardens had tripled their patrols, which meant tripled bribes, which meant everyone's luck-charms were selling at a premium even though nobody felt lucky. And above it all hung that wrongness, that ceiling of black where stars should have been, pressing down on the city like a lid on a pot.

Sefa had spent two days telling herself that Petra could take care of herself. This was true. Petra could taste arsenic in a wedding feast from across the room and identify the jilted lover by the bitterness under his cologne. Petra had survived three years of swallowing death for a queen who barely knew her name. Petra did not need rescuing.

But Petra had also spent those same three years making sure that every orphan in the Nightmarket ate breakfast, and Sefa had done the arithmetic on that a long time ago: you didn't leave people like that alone in the dark. You just didn't.

"Right," she said, standing on an overturned crate so she could see all of them — Brannick, gentle as a landslide; Pip, vibrating with purpose; old Marisol, who claimed to be sixty but whose hands suggested ninety; the twins, Osk and Oska, who were nineteen and shared a single brain between them and had, between them, built half the charms that kept the family fed. "Petra went into the mountain after Tam. The mountain eats people. We are going in after her."

Marisol snorted. "With what army?"

"With what we've got." Sefa hopped down and pulled the sack from under the fish stall. Inside: eleven luck-knots, four ward-scramblers, a jar of salted honey wine for courage, and the thing they'd emptied the family coffers to buy from a Warden archivist with expensive tastes and no spine — a scrap of vellum, cracked and brown, showing the old toll-gate into the mountain's roots. The gate the crown had sealed a century ago and then pretended it had never existed.

"The archivist said the wards on it are bone-wards," Osk said. He was turning the vellum over in his fingers like it might bite him. "Old ones. They don't take coin."

"What do they take?" asked Pip.

Nobody answered, because everybody knew, and knowing it out loud made it real.

"They take memories," Marisol said finally, in the voice she used for hard truths and bad weather. "That's what the old stories say. Each threshold, a memory. That's how the mountain got its teeth."

Pip looked around at all of them, and Sefa watched her do the math — the same math Sefa had done, the same arithmetic of love — and come out on the side of going anyway. Eleven years old, and already fluent in the only language that mattered.

"Then we'd better pick good ones," Pip said.

* * *

The Stair of Teeth began, according to the vellum, behind a waterfall two days' walk north of the city, in a gorge the maps marked with a single word: *QUARRIED, ABANDONED.* The twins had argued about whether to bring the mule. The mule, named Duchess, had resolved the argument by eating the map's corner, so they brought her mostly out of spite.

They walked by day now, which felt transgressive. The whole city lived by night since the stars went out — markets, courts, funerals, all of it shuffled into the dark hours as if daylight itself had become suspect. But the gorge lay beyond the Warden patrols, out where the law thinned to nothing, and Sefa found she liked the sun on her face even if there was nothing up there to see it by. No stars. No sunspots either, the scholars said. The sky was just *blank*, like a page someone had scraped clean.

"I used to think the stars were holes," Pip said on the second morning, walking backward ahead of the group so she could face them all. "You know? Like the sky was a roof and the light came through the cracks. And when they went out, I thought someone had patched the roof."

"That's morbid," said Oska.

"It's *theological*," said Pip with dignity.

Brannick chuckled, deep as a well. He had insisted on carrying most of the supplies, plus the cheese, plus Duchess's saddlebags, plus — briefly, until she objected — Duchess herself. He was the one who'd held the family together the first night after Tam vanished, when Petra had gone white and silent and started burning through her charm stock like a woman with a deadline. He'd cooked for forty people who weren't hungry and sat with them until they ate anyway. That was Brannick's magic, and it had never once been illegal.

Sefa walked at the rear and thought about Tam.

She hadn't known him as long as Petra had — nobody had known him as long as Petra had; the two of them had been feral children together back when the Nightmarket was just a rumor and a smell — but Tam had taught her to pick pockets without getting caught, which was to say he'd taught her to get caught, gently, six times, until the lesson stuck. He'd sung while he worked. Badly. Enthusiastically. The whole market knew his version of the street-song, the one about the fishwife and the duke, mostly because he changed the words whenever a Warden was in earshot and the new words were always filthier.

She missed him like a tooth. And she was furious at him — properly, deeply furious — for whatever he'd been doing, meeting whoever he'd been meeting below the mountain, keeping secrets from the people who loved him. She intended to tell him so at length. Preferably while hitting him with something soft.

The waterfall announced itself before they saw it, a white thread down the far wall of the gorge, and beneath it, half-swallowed by moss and shadow, the gate.

It was smaller than Sefa had imagined. She'd pictured something grand — iron doors, carved warnings, the architecture of dread. Instead it was a plain stone arch set into the cliff, maybe eight feet tall, its passage choked with rubble that looked deliberate rather than accidental. Someone had sealed this carefully. Someone had wanted it forgotten.

And across the arch, faint as breath on cold glass, the wards shimmered.

Sefa felt them before she saw them — a pressure behind her eyes, a taste like licking an old coin. She'd grown up around magic; the Nightmarket ran on small charms the way a kitchen runs on grease. But this was different. This was *old*. The shimmer wasn't light exactly. It was more like heat-haze, if heat-haze had teeth.

"Bone-wards," Marisol said quietly. She touched the arch's edge with one knuckle and pulled it back fast, sucking air through her teeth. "Aye. They're awake."

"Can the scramblers handle it?" Sefa asked.

Osk and Oska exchanged the look they exchanged when the answer was no but they were composing a more palatable version. It was Osk who spoke. "The scramblers can open a gap. A small one. For maybe ten heartbeats. After that the wards reknit, and anyone still touching the threshold when they do—" He shrugged, eloquently.

"Becomes part of the architecture," Marisol finished. "I've heard of it happening. There's a handprint in a shrine in the Salt Quarter that screams if you touch it."

"Wonderful," said Oska. "Love that for us."

They camped that night in the mouth of the gorge, and Sefa took first watch, and it was there — sitting on a warm rock under a starless sky, listening to Duchess complain philosophically about hay quality — that the fear finally caught up with her.

It caught her the way fear always did: not as a thought but as a body. Her hands shook. Her stomach performed acrobatics it had learned specifically for occasions like this. She thought about the handprint that screamed. She thought about thresholds and heartbeats. She thought about Pip, who was asleep with her mouth open and one arm flung over Brannick's chest like a small claiming flag.

Here was the thing about the market family, the thing Sefa had understood since the night Tam and Petra had dragged her, nine years old and starving, out of a snowdrift and fed her burnt porridge: love in the Nightmarket was not a feeling. Feelings were luxuries. Love in the Nightmarket was a *practice*, like knot-work, like lock-picking. You did it daily, with your hands, whether or not you felt like it. You fed people who weren't hungry. You stood watch so others could sleep. You went into mountains.

Tomorrow they'd go into the mountain.

She woke the others at gray dawn, and they ate the last of the cheese, and Brannick said grace in his own fashion, which was: "May everything we meet today be slower than us and dumber than Duchess." Duchess, hearing her name, bit him.

* * *

The scramblers worked better than the twins had promised, which was fortunate, because everything else went wrong immediately.

Osk and Oska planted the four devices at the corners of the arch, each one a knot of copper wire and salt-glass that hummed when activated, and the humming rose and rose until Sefa's fillings ached, and then the shimmer across the gate *shivered* — rippled like water disturbed by a dropped stone — and split, peeling back from the center the way a curtain parts.

"Gap's open!" Oska shouted over the noise. "Ten heartbeats! GO!"

They went. Brannick first, hauling the packs, ducking through the arch with surprising grace for a man shaped like a door. Then Marisol, then Sefa hauling Duchess, who dug in all four hooves at the threshold and had to be pushed, shoved, and bribed with a dried apple through a hole in reality. Then the twins, backing through together, watching their machines burn out behind them in little showers of sparks.

Then Pip.

Later, Sefa would replay it a thousand times. How the girl stumbled on the rubble. How the gap was already knitting closed, threads of that heat-haze shimmer drawing together like a wound sealing. How Pip threw herself forward — brave, stupid, *theirs* — and how the ward caught her not with force but with something worse: a kind of attention. The shimmer brushed her shoulder, her throat, her open shouting mouth—

—and took.

Pip came through the arch and landed on her hands and knees on the far side, and Sefa was there instantly, gathering her up, checking her over — bruised elbow, scraped palm, nothing broken, nothing bleeding, *nothing wrong* —

"Pip. Pip, look at me. Are you hurt?"

Pip opened her mouth.

Nothing came out.

She tried again, and Sefa watched her face do something terrible: confusion first, then effort, then a dawning horror that no eleven-year-old should have to wear. She shaped words. Her lips moved. Her throat worked. The air came out silent as snowfall.

"Pip—" Sefa's voice cracked. "Pip, say something. Say anything."

Pip pointed frantically at her own throat, then at the arch, then pressed both hands over her mouth, and her eyes filled, and she made one sound — one thin, soundless sob that was somehow louder than any scream — and Sefa held her while the rest of the family crowded close and the truth settled over them like ash.

The ward had taken her voice. Had reached through the closing gap and taken the loudest, brightest, most relentless voice in the Nightmarket, and left her standing.

For a long moment nobody spoke. Even Duchess was quiet.

Then Marisol crouched down to Pip's level, took the girl's face in her two ancient hands, and said, very steadily: "Listen to me, little bird. It's a toll, not a theft. Tolls can be paid back. Whatever's down here that wanted your voice — we'll find it, and we'll bargain, and we'll get it home where it belongs. Do you hear me?"

Pip nodded. Then, because she was Pip, she tapped Marisol's wrist twice — their old signal, *yes, obviously, keep talking* — and wiped her nose on her sleeve, and pointed down the passage ahead with the fierce imperiousness of a general ordering a charge.

"Yeah," Sefa said thickly. She stood, and turned to face the others, and found her own fear had burned off entirely, replaced by something harder and cleaner. "Yeah. All right. She's right. We keep moving."

The passage beyond the arch sloped downward, wide enough for Duchess, lit by nothing — the twins' lantern-charms threw pools of gold that seemed to sink into the stone rather than spread. The walls were worked stone, ancient, and carved — Sefa realized with a lurch — with names. Thousands of names, in scripts dead and living, climbing the walls like ivy. Some were worn to ghosts. One, near the entrance, was fresh enough that the dust still clung to the chisel-marks:

*P. BLACKWOOD.*

"Oh, Petra," Sefa whispered. "You absolute idiot. You beautiful, stubborn idiot."

Brannick put a hand on her shoulder. When she glanced up, he nodded downward, into the dark, toward the sound — very faint, very far below — of running water, and beneath the water, if you strained, something else. A hum. A melody, almost.

Sefa knew that melody. Everyone from the market knew that melody. It was the street-song, the fishwife and the duke, drifting up through the bones of the mountain like smoke through floorboards.

Tam's song. Or — her stomach turned — half of it, the way you'd hear it through a wall, through a jar, through three years of silence. Half a soul, still singing.

Pip tugged her sleeve. She pointed at the melody, then at her own chest, then made the *keep-talking* gesture, and her eyes were wet and blazing and absolutely certain.

"We hear it, little bird," Sefa said. She picked up her lantern. Behind her, the family formed up — Brannick with the packs, Marisol with the wine, the twins with their scorched, exhausted machines, Duchess with an expression of profound grievance. "We hear it, and we're coming. Both of you. Hold on."

And they started down, five voices and one silence, into the dark that sang.
