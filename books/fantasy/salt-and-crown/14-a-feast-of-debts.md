## 14. A Feast of Debts

The Undercourt's kitchens smelled of nothing.

That was the first wrongness Petra had learned in her three days below the mountain, and it still unsettled her more than the bone-warded stair or the servants with candlelight where their eyes should have been. Every kitchen she'd ever loved — the palace sculleries, Sefa's market stall, Tam's little oven nook with its perpetually burnt corner of bread — had a smell. Sweat and onion and woodsmoke and someone's spilled honey. Life, basically, wearing an apron.

This place smelled like a clean slate. Like a page before the words went down on it.

"Because nothing here is food," Vess said, when Petra had asked about it that first morning, hollow-eyed as ever, folding dough that was not dough. "It's *terms*. The cooks shape contracts into something the mouth can accept. You don't eat dinner down here, taster. You swallow your obligations."

"And what happens if you refuse to swallow?"

Vess had looked at her for a long moment, the way one looks at a child who has asked why the sky is up. "You are the obligation," she said. "Eventually."

Petra had thought about that a great deal while she worked. Because work was what she did; it was how she kept her hands from shaking and her mind from running to the vault three levels down, where a jar of starlight hummed a street-song when it thought no one was listening. She scrubbed pots that had never been dirty. She peeled roots that bled pale light instead of sap. And all the while she tasted everything — the air, the water, the steam off every pot — building the same ledger in her head she'd built above ground, except now the entries were debts instead of poisons.

Tonight, the ledger would be read aloud at table.

* * *

"They're calling it the Feast of Rebinding," Vess told her, two hours before service, in the cold pantry where the salt was stored in locked boxes because even salt could be a contract down here. "Once a century, or once a debt comes due, whichever comes first. Every course renews an old bargain. The court eats its own history."

"How many courses?"

"Nineteen." Vess's mouth did something that might have been a smile on a face that remembered how. "Nineteen courses, nineteen centuries of hunger. The last dish is always the newest contract. The freshest soul. This year—" she didn't finish, but her eyes flicked toward Petra, and Petra felt the word settle into her like a stone dropped in a well: *Tam*.

"The final course is his," Petra said. Not a question.

"A binding course. His name spoken over the dish, his essence folded into it, and then the king eats, and the eating makes it permanent. After tonight there is no jar to shatter, taster. There will be no *him* left to save. He'll be flavor. Memory of flavor, eventually." Vess turned back to her locked salt box. "I'm telling you this so you'll stop looking at me like I have a plan to offer you. I don't. I told you before: he eats contracts, not food. You cannot poison a contract."

"No," Petra agreed pleasantly. "But you can amend one."

Vess's hands went still on the salt box.

"I've spent three years learning that everything has a flavor underneath its flavor," Petra went on, keeping her voice light, the way she did when selling luck-knots to dockworkers who couldn't afford them. "Arsenic tastes of garlic gone wrong. Heartleaf poison tastes sweet, which is why it kills children. And magic—" she tapped her tongue against her teeth— "magic tastes like salt. Always has. Even below the mountain. Especially below the mountain. Your whole court is seasoned through, Vess. Every wall, every ward, every contract. Salt, salt, salt. Did nobody down here ever wonder why their king is always starving?"

The silence stretched long enough that Petra heard the mountain itself breathing — that vast slow tidal groan beneath the floor that she'd stopped noticing and now noticed constantly, the way you notice a sleeping animal in the room.

"You're going to do something catastrophically foolish," Vess said at last.

"I'm going to serve the nineteenth course," said Petra. "It's what I'm for."

* * *

The banquet hall had been a cathedral once, or something like one, before the mountain closed its hand around it. Pillars of black stone rose into darkness that swallowed their tops. The long table ran the length of the hall, laid with plates of grey silver and cups of something that wasn't glass and wasn't ice. The court sat along both sides — souls in livery, souls in finery, souls worn thin as old cloth — and at the head, on a chair carved from a single tooth of some creature Petra refused to imagine, sat the Underking.

He was courteous. That was the horror of him. He thanked each servant by name. He complimented the wine. When a young soul-servant trembled too hard to pour without spilling, the king steadied the decanter with one long finger and said, gently, "There is no hurry in my house. There is only time, and I own all of it," and the poor creature wept with gratitude, and Petra had to look away and breathe through the taste of pity, which was bitter as chicory.

Course after course came out, and Petra tasted each one as it passed her hands in the serving line, reading the contracts the way she'd learned to read the queen's plates.

The first course, a broth of pale gold: a treaty between the mountain and the river, renewed. It tasted of iron and moving water.

The fourth, small birds in aspic: a border agreement, centuries old. She caught the ghost of the wards in it — the very wards that had fallen, that had taken the stars with them — and her hands went cold around the serving tray. *This is where they bought it,* she thought. *This exact flavor. This is what Tam cost.*

The ninth course was grief. She didn't know how else to describe it. A pudding dark as peat that made the whole table go quiet and bow their heads, renewing some mourning so old its name had worn away. It tasted of burnt ends and unsaid apologies, and Petra thought of her mother's bread, the memory she'd paid at the toll-gate, and had to grip the edge of the pass until her knuckles whitened.

"You feel it," murmured Vess beside her, watching the hall. "Good. Feel it all. That's the difference between you and them." A tilt of her chin at the soul-servants. "They stopped tasting centuries ago. It was the only way to survive the serving."

"And you?"

"I'm still deciding," Vess said.

* * *

By the seventeenth course, Petra's heart was a drum in her ears and her mouth was dry as paper, and she found herself — absurdly, in the mouth of the dark — thinking of Tam teaching her to juggle with stolen oranges behind the chandlery, telling her *the secret is you don't watch your hands, you watch the space between*. Watch the space between, Petra. Watch where the thing is going, not where it is.

She watched the space between.

Eighteenth course: a roast that had never been any living thing, carved at table by a steward whose arms moved like clockwork. The contract in it was the oldest yet — she could taste the age of it, dust and deep stone and something like patience. The founding bargain. The one that had built the Undercourt itself. The king ate slowly, savoring, and the mountain groaned beneath them, and every soul at the table leaned forward slightly, hungry without knowing why.

Then the hall hushed, and the steward of the king's table — a tall figure whose face was smooth as an egg — turned toward the serving line and spoke the words Petra had been waiting three days to hear.

"The nineteenth course. The new debt. Let it be brought by the hand that knows its flavor best."

Every eye in the hall turned to her.

*Of course,* she thought, with a wild bubble of almost-laughter. *They've known what I am since the broth. Seven poisons named by taste alone. They didn't let me into the kitchen despite knowing. They let me in* because *of it.* The trap had never been hidden. She'd simply chosen to walk in with her eyes open, because the alternative was a world without Tam in it, and there had never been a version of the world where she chose that.

She lifted the covered dish from the warming shelf. Beneath the silver dome, a single portion — a small dark cake, dense as fruitbread, glazed with honey and salt. She'd baked it herself that afternoon, in the smell-less kitchen, with flour that was terms and honey that was interest. And threaded through it, kneaded into its very crumb the way her mother used to thread candied peel through holiday loaves: a charm. Her own work, her own craft, the small illegal warmth she'd sold in the Nightmarket a hundred times.

Keyed to a song.

*Half a soul still sings,* she thought. *So let it sing through the whole rotten table.*

She walked the length of the hall. It took forever. It took no time at all. The court watched her with eyes like banked coals, and the mountain breathed beneath her feet, and somewhere three levels down a jar of starlight was humming *the baker's round, the baker's round, hot cross and honey-brown* — the street-song they'd all screamed together as children, off-key, joyful, alive.

She set the dish before the king.

"Lift the cover yourself, Majesty," she said, and her voice only shook a little. "It's tradition, above ground. The taster presents; the sovereign reveals. Trust between the crown and the mouth."

Something flickered across the ancient face — amusement, perhaps, or appetite, or both, for in him they were surely the same thing. He lifted the cover.

The smell hit the hall like a bell struck backwards.

Honey. Bread. Warmth. And under it, unmistakable, ringing through every corner of that vast dead room: *song*. The charm woke the moment air touched it, and Tam's fragment — his half-dissolved, half-remembered, stubborn, singing soul — poured up out of the cake in a thread of light and sound, and the street-song filled the cathedral of the dark, and every soul-servant at that table flinched as though struck, because they hadn't heard music in decades, in centuries, and their starved hearts remembered it all at once.

The mountain shuddered.

Not the slow tidal breath — a real shudder, a convulsion, dust sifting from the unseen ceiling, cups rattling on grey silver. The contracts in the walls felt the song and *stuttered*, and Petra tasted it happen: nineteen courses' worth of bindings loosening by a hair's breadth, a hair's width, a hair's hope.

And the Underking began to laugh.

It was not a cruel laugh. That was the worst part. It was warm, rich, genuinely delighted, rolling up from the depths of him like bubbles from a lake bed, and it went on and on while the dust fell and the song rang and the court stared in terror at their laughing king.

"Oh," he said, wiping the corner of one ancient eye. "*Oh*, that's lovely. That is the finest thing anyone has brought to my table in four hundred years." He looked at her the way a master looks at a promising apprentice, the way a fox looks at a henhouse full of clever hens. "You baked your brother's song into my feast. You tried to season my contracts loose with a street tune and a luck-charm. Child, I have eaten rebellions older than your language. I have digested revolutions, wars, entire dynasties of the proud and the clever." He picked up the small dark cake in one long-fingered hand, held it to the light, and smiled at her with genuine, terrible affection.

"I expected nothing less from the moment you named seven poisons in my broth. Do you understand? The trick was never whether you'd try. The trick was making sure you'd try *here*, at my table, on my night, when the binding is strongest and every course before yours has already laid the pattern." He turned the cake slowly. "You haven't amended my contract, taster. You've completed it. A freely offered gift, given at the feast, by the reserve collateral herself. Do you know what that does to the paperwork?"

The mountain groaned. The song faltered — not dying, but straining, a kite in a tightening wind.

"But," the king said, and set the cake down untasted, which was somehow worse than anything, "I am courteous. It's the one virtue I have left. So I'll give you the courtesy of a choice, since you gave me the courtesy of a song." He leaned forward, and his voice dropped to something almost kind. "Eat it yourself, and share his fate willingly — the paperwork becomes very interesting indeed. Or stand aside, and I'll eat my due, and you may spend whatever years remain to you remembering that you were close. So close. Clever to the last."

The hall waited. The court waited. Vess, at the edge of Petra's vision, stood very still, one hand hidden in the folds of her apron, and Petra did not look at that hand, because looking would ruin everything, and because she already knew what was in it.

Her heart was calm. That surprised her most of all. Three years of swallowing death had taught her this much: the moment before the worst taste is always the quietest.

"There's just one problem with your paperwork, Majesty," Petra said.

The king raised an eyebrow, an expression of such human courtesy on such an inhuman face.

"I've never once," said Petra Blackwood, royal food-taster, ledger of poisons, reserve collateral, "served a dish I hadn't already tasted myself."

And she reached for the cake.
