## 15. Bargaining with Hunger

They did not chain her. That was the first thing Petra noticed, and the thing that frightened her most.

Chains meant the Underking thought of her as a body — a thing to be held by force, a prisoner among prisoners. Instead they gave her a small room off the banquet hall, warm and dry, with a bed of grey moss and a window that looked out onto nothing at all: not rock, not dark, just an absence shaped like a view. They left her alone in it for what might have been hours. Time moved strangely down here, pooling like cold honey.

She sat on the bed and took inventory, because inventory was what she knew how to do. Three years of tasting had taught her that fear was a flavor too — it came in sour and sharp and metallic, depending on what you were afraid *of* — and right now her own mouth tasted like copper coins and winter apples, which was the taste of being afraid for someone else rather than yourself. She'd learned to name it during Tam's fever year, when he was eleven and coughing blood into a rag while Sefa ran through the rain for the herb-woman and Pip, tiny Pip, sat holding his hand and telling him stories about a heroic turnip until he laughed so hard he choked.

She was afraid for him now. The jar was somewhere in this mountain, humming half a song, and the king who kept it wanted to eat her instead.

*Good*, she told herself. *Let him want.*

The terrible thing — the thing she would never be able to explain properly to Sefa later, though Sefa, of all people, would probably understand — was how calm she felt. Not numb. Calm the way a kitchen is calm after the bread is in the oven and there is nothing left to do but wait. She had spent three years swallowing death on behalf of a queen who had listed her as *reserve collateral* in a steward's ledger, and somewhere in those three years she had apparently made peace with the shape of her own life. It was a small shape. It fit inside other people's survival. She had stopped minding.

Mostly.

There was a knock — soft, courteous, obscene in its politeness — and Vess entered without waiting for an answer.

The hollow-eyed woman carried a tray. On the tray was a cup of something steaming and pale, and beside the cup, arranged with ceremonial precision, a single dried fig.

"The king sends refreshment," Vess said. Her voice was flat as slate. "You are not required to eat."

"No," Petra agreed. "I imagine I'm required to do very little from here on. That's rather the point of me."

Something flickered at the corner of Vess's mouth. It might have been the ghost of a smile; it might have been a muscle spasm from decades of not smiling. With Vess it was impossible to say.

Petra looked at the cup. She didn't need to taste it to know what it would be — warmth, belonging, the smell of a kitchen where someone was waiting up for you. The king's signature poison. He laced everything with it, she suspected. His courtesy, his hospitality, his invitations. *Come home*, said every dish in this court, and home was a mouth.

She pushed the tray away with one finger.

"Tell him I'll come to the table," she said. "Tell him I have a counter-offer."

Vess went still in the particular way of someone who has been still for a very long time and is practiced at it. "He does not take counter-offers."

"He took mine once already," Petra said. "He accepted my service in his kitchen on the strength of my tongue. He is a creature of contracts, Vess. Contracts are offers. Offers can be countered. If he were truly beyond negotiation he wouldn't bother with banquets and trays of figs — he'd simply take me. He wants me to *choose*. That's his whole architecture, isn't it? Every soul down here signed something." She paused. "Even you."

Vess's hands, folded on the tray, tightened fractionally. "Yes," she said, after a silence long enough to have its own weather. "Even me."

"Then tell him. A taster comes to bargain."

* * *

The table was smaller than she expected.

After the vastness of the hall where the feast had been served — the endless tables of bound souls gliding with dishes, the chandeliers of fused bone dripping light that wasn't light — she had imagined the king dining at some monstrous head of the board. But the room he received her in held only a round table of black stone, two chairs, and a single candle that burned without heat or smoke, its flame the color of a bruise.

The Underking sat with his hands folded, and he looked, as always, almost kind. That was the worst trick in his repertoire. He had the face of somebody's grandfather — a good one, the sort who taught you card games and cheated gently — except that his eyes were the deep grey of a well, and if you looked into them too long you began to feel the pull.

"You refused my fig," he said, sounding genuinely wounded.

"I don't eat anything here," Petra said. "With respect, Your Majesty. You season everything with homesickness."

"A fair critique." He gestured to the opposite chair. "Sit. Bargain, then. I find I'm curious what a poison-ledger thinks she has to trade."

She sat. The chair was comfortable, which she distrusted on principle.

"Tam's debt," she said. "Release it. Give me the jar, whole and unopened, and safe passage up the Stair for me and anyone who comes after me."

"And in exchange?"

"My soul."

The word dropped between them onto the black stone, and the candle flame leaned toward it like a listener.

The king did not laugh. She had half expected him to — the offer was absurd on its face, a taster trading herself for a market orphan, a pawn buying back a pawn. But he only studied her with those well-grey eyes, and she watched hunger move behind them the way weather moves over water.

"Your soul," he repeated. "Which is worth what, exactly? You are a servant of a servant's court. Unlettered in the deep arts. Your name appears in no genealogy, holds no title, commands no—"

"Three years," Petra said.

He stopped.

"Three years of poisons," she said. "Swallowed deliberately, daily, in doses calibrated to build tolerance without killing. Arsenic. Belladonna. Wolfsbane. Hemlock. Things your court hasn't brewed since before this mountain had a name, because the surface got clever and I got *used*. Every poison leaves a residue in the soul the way it leaves a residue in the blood. Ask your steward. Ask your ledgers. I am not a clean vintage, Your Majesty. I am a cask that's been filled and drained and filled again until the wood itself changed flavor." She leaned forward. "You have eaten kings and poets and saints. You have eaten souls aged in virtue and souls aged in sin. When in all your long starving history have you ever eaten a soul aged in *death*?"

The silence stretched. Somewhere far off, through walls that were more memory than stone, she heard the mountain breathing — that slow tidal groan she'd felt since her first day below, the sound of a hunger the size of a geology.

The Underking smiled. It transformed his face entirely, and none of the transformation was comforting.

"Seasoned," he murmured. "Bitter. Layered. A soul that has died a little every day for a thousand days." His well-eyes brightened with something horribly like delight. "Do you know, little taster, how long it has been since anything surprised my palate?" He spread his hands. "Everything below is either sweet with innocence or salted with guilt, and I have dined on both until both became monotonous. But you — you would taste of endurance. Of swallowed terror, metabolized. Of arsenic turned to patience." He drew a slow breath, savoring the idea alone. "Yes. Yes, I accept."

"That fast?" Petra kept her voice level. Inside, something enormous and cold turned over. Part of her had banked on haggling — days of it, delays she could use, gaps Vess could exploit. She had not actually prepared for the possibility that she was simply, immediately, *worth it* to him.

"He who hesitates over a rare dish deserves the table scraps," the king said. "Terms, then. Tomorrow night, at the second bell of the feast, the exchange is made. The boy's jar is opened and his essence returns to his body — which we will also provide, since your friends above ground have so rudely gone to the trouble of digging toward us." His smile thinned. "Your soul passes to me, freely offered, freely taken. And you will serve at my table forever, in whatever capacity remains of you."

"Forever is a long contract," Petra said. "I've read enough of them down here to know you always leave yourself a door. What's yours?"

For a moment — just a moment — the well-eyes flickered with something that was not delight. Something older and hungrier and briefly, briefly uncertain.

"No soul freely offered can be taken twice," he said. "It is the oldest law I know, older than me, and I did not write it and I cannot break it. Why do you ask?"

"No reason," said Petra Blackwood, who had read Corvane's confession twice and memorized it once. "Just checking the fine print. It's a habit of my profession."

* * *

Vess found her afterward, in the little room with the window onto nothing.

The old taster didn't knock this time. She came in, closed the door, and stood with her back against it for a long moment, looking at Petra with those hollow eyes that had once, decades ago, been somebody's daughter's eyes.

"You fool," Vess said softly. "You magnificent, suicidal fool."

"I prefer 'negotiator.'"

"He will drink you and he will still be hungry. Do you understand? He is always still hungry. That is what he *is*. You cannot fill a hole the size of a mountain with one seasoned soul, however bitter."

"Maybe not," Petra said. "But maybe I don't have to fill it. Maybe I just have to make it choke."

Vess crossed the room in three strides and took Petra's chin in her hand — hard, tilting her face to the light, examining her the way a jeweler examines a stone she intends to cut. Whatever she saw there made her let go and step back.

"You mean it," she said. "You're going to sit at that table tomorrow and let him drink you, and you're smiling about it inside, because he gives you Tam back and that's the whole of the arithmetic." Her voice cracked, just slightly, on the last word. "I sat at that table forty years ago. I offered myself for my sister's debt. He drank me down to the husk and kept her anyway — told me the terms had been misread, told me the contract was binding regardless. There was no one to check the fine print. There was no one who even *could*."

"I can," Petra said quietly. "That's why I'm alive. That's the whole point of me, Vess. I taste things other people swallow blind."

Vess turned away. Her shoulders, under the grey livery, were shaking — or perhaps the mountain was shaking, or perhaps down here there was no difference between a woman trembling and the world trembling. When she turned back, her hand was closed around something, and she held it out flat on her palm.

A knife. Small, plain-hilted, the blade a dull grey that seemed to drink the candlelight rather than reflect it.

"Ward-iron," Vess said. "Forged up top, in the old days, from the same stock as the border wards themselves. It cuts contracts. Not flesh — contracts. One cut, one binding severed, and then the iron dies and never works again. There has been one knife like this in the Undercourt for four hundred years, and I have carried it for forty, waiting for—" She stopped. Started again. "Waiting for a taster with fine-print eyes and nothing left to lose."

Petra looked at the knife and did not touch it yet. "Why?" she asked. "Why help me? He owns you. He's owned you for forty years."

"Because he drank my sister anyway," Vess said, "and because I have watched souls serve at that table for four centuries, hollowing out one decade at a time, and because you are the first creature to walk into this mountain who tastes of *endurance* instead of surrender." Her hollow eyes were wet. It was the most human thing Petra had seen below the earth. "And because tomorrow, when he drinks you, someone should be standing close enough to cut the cord if the drinking goes wrong. Consider it professional courtesy. One taster to another."

Petra took the knife. It was lighter than it looked, and cold in the way that deep wells are cold, and it hummed faintly against her palm — the same note, she realized, as the wards the night they fell. The same note as Tam's jar, singing half a song.

"Where do I hide it?" she asked. "He'll taste iron on me. He tastes everything."

"Not iron," Vess said. "Ward-iron reads as *law*, and law is everywhere in this place; he'll think it's his own weight on you, the mountain's rules settling into your pockets. Tuck it against your heart. And Petra—" Vess caught her arm as she turned away, grip fierce. "When the moment comes, do not hesitate. Hesitation is a flavor he adores."

"I know," Petra said. "I've tasted it. It tastes like my mother's bread going stale on the counter while I paid a toll to get down here." She slid the knife beneath her shirt, against her skin, over her heart. It warmed slowly to her body heat, like something learning to trust. "Thank you, Vess."

"Don't thank me." Vess was already moving to the door, becoming again the flat-voiced steward, the hollow woman, the ghost of a taster. "Win. That's the thanks. Win, and open every door in this mountain on your way out, and leave the king choking on a flavor he'll never forget."

She paused at the threshold, hand on the frame.

"For what it's worth," she said, without turning around, "there was never a version of the world where I didn't hope someone like you would come down here. Some of us just forgot to wait for her."

Then she was gone, and Petra sat alone with a knife against her heart and a contract signed in her own voice, and outside the window that wasn't a window, the starless dark pressed close — and somewhere above it, impossibly far, she could almost believe she heard digging.
