## 16. The Tasting

The hall where they meant to unmake me was beautiful, and I hated it for that.

It had been a cathedral once, maybe — before the mountain swallowed it — with pillars carved into shapes like frozen waterfalls and a ceiling so high the dark up there felt less like absence and more like weather. Torches of pale green flame burned without smoke. The Underking's table ran the length of the room, black wood polished to the sheen of still water, and set upon it were two cups.

Two. As if this were a wedding instead of an execution.

Tam stood at the far end of the hall between two guards made of shadow and old bone, and he looked *wrong* in a way I couldn't fix with my hands. His skin had gone the gray-white of dough left too long uncovered. His eyes found mine and something in them flickered — recognition, faint as a coin at the bottom of a well — and then his lips moved, shaping half a note of the market's street-song before the guards pressed him still.

*Half a soul still sings,* Vess had told me. *Sing back when you can.*

I couldn't yet. My mouth was full of the taste of my own courage, which, for the record, tastes like copper coins and fear-sweat and the particular chalky bitterness of a woman who has just volunteered her soul to a monster because there wasn't anyone else to do it.

The Underking sat at the table's head, and he rose when I entered, because courtesy costs nothing and he had been starving too long to waste manners.

"Petra Blackwood," he said. My name in his mouth sounded like it was being appraised. Weighed on little brass scales. "Taster. Ledger of poisons. You've come to pay your brother's debt."

"He's not my brother by blood." I walked toward the table because my legs had decided that standing still would look like begging, and I had never begged in my life except once, over Tam's fevered body, and even then I'd been holding a knife while I did it. "But debts don't care about blood. You taught the court above that. So here I am."

"You understand what you offer." He gestured, and one of the cups filled itself from nowhere — a slow silver pour, light moving like liquid. "Your essence. Your self. Everything you have been, poured out and drunk down. In exchange, the boy walks free, whole, his fragment returned and his debt struck from my books."

"I understand." I sat across from him. The chair was warm. That was somehow the worst detail yet — everything down here was cold except the places designed to make you comfortable, and comfort built by something that eats souls is not comfort, it's *basting*.

He lifted his cup. I lifted mine. Mine held nothing but air and the reflection of my own face, which looked, I thought, remarkably composed for a woman about to be dessert.

"A toast first," the Underking said. "I am old enough to believe in ritual. To the flavor of endings."

"To the flavor," I said, "of finding out."

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about offering your soul: it doesn't hurt. That's the trap.

Vess had warned me. *The taking is gentle,* she'd said, in the kitchen where we'd ground nightshade and whispered truths between tasks. *That's how it gets you. It feels like setting down a heavy bag after a long walk. It feels like rest. By the time you notice you're lighter, you're already mostly gone.* She'd pressed the ward-iron knife into my palm beneath a fold of cloth, small as a paring knife, cold as a promise. *For afterward. If there is an afterward.*

So when the Underking reached across the table and laid two fingers against my wrist — courteous, ancient, patient as geology — I braced for pain and got instead a sensation like exhaling after three years of holding my breath.

My soul came loose the way a knot comes loose when you finally find the right thread.

I felt myself pouring. Not blood, not breath — *me*. The taste of my mother's bread, already spent on the Stair of Teeth, and yet somehow still there, deeper down, in the place where tolls can't reach. The Nightmarket at midnight, lantern-light on wet cobbles. Sefa laughing so hard she snorted wine through her nose. Pip asleep in a pile of drying charms like a cat in a laundry basket. Tam, sixteen and furious, carrying me on his back through a snowstorm because I'd been fool enough to test a stranger's stew and fool enough to survive it badly.

And underneath all of it, threaded through every bright thing like wire through bread: the ledger. Arsenic and its almond ghost. Hemlock, sweet as hay gone wrong. Aconite, cold and numbing, the taste of winter getting into a wound. Three years of swallowing death so a queen could eat breakfast without dying. Every dose a small murder I committed against myself on purpose, daily, until my body became a book written in poison and my soul became —

Well. Until my soul became what it became.

The Underking drank.

He drank the way I imagine the sea drinks a river — vast, unhurried, entitled. The silver light of me went down his throat and the green torches flared and for one moment, one shining moment, I thought: *it's working, he's satisfied, Tam is free.*

Then the King Beneath the Mountain stopped smiling.

His face didn't change, exactly. Kings are too well-made for that. But something behind his face changed — the way a kitchen changes when a taster goes still over a spoon, when the chewing stops, when the whole room understands that the dish has turned.

"Oh," he said softly.

I knew that *oh*. I'd said it myself, twice in my career, both times followed by someone running for a physician. It is the sound a man makes when he realizes the poison isn't coming. It's already arrived.

"What," said the Underking, in a voice that had shaken mountains, "is *in* you?"

"Three years of royal service," I said. My voice came out steadier than I had any right to expect, given that I was currently a cup with a crack in it. "You should have read the ledger before you drank."

* * *

Let me tell you what he tasted, because I know now — I could feel it happening, the way you can feel bread rising under your palms, some knowledge of yourself passing out of you and into him.

He tasted arsenic, ninety-one doses, each one survived, each one leaving a grain of its bitterness behind like sand in a shoe. He tasted hemlock. He tasted the mineral wrongness from the queen's poisoned cup, the cold deep-earth flavor I'd carried unknowing ever since — his own vintage, come home soured. He tasted every antidote I'd choked down, every charcoal draught, every bitter tonic, until the sweetness in me had been scoured away entirely and what remained was a soul tempered like a blade: folded, folded, folded, a thousand times, in fire and death and stubbornness.

He tasted a woman who had been marinating in mortality since she was nineteen years old.

And to a creature whose whole existence was hunger — who ate contracts and souls and centuries, who had never in his long dark life encountered a single thing he could not digest — I was, at last, indigestible.

The Underking set down his cup with a click that echoed like a bone breaking.

"That's not possible," he said. "Souls are soft. Souls are *food*."

"Mine's been pickled," I said. "In arsenic. You really should have asked about the preparation."

He tried to be angry, and he managed it for perhaps four seconds — the torches guttering, the pillars groaning, the temperature dropping hard enough that my breath came out white. Then the anger cracked, because underneath it was the other thing, the true thing, the thing I had bet Tam's life and my own on:

Hunger. Enormous, wounded, *confused* hunger. The hunger of a thing that has just eaten and is somehow still empty, and worse than empty — *unwell*. Because I hadn't just refused to nourish him. I was sitting in him like a stone in a gut. Three years of death, folded into every fiber of me, and each grain of it was doing what poison does best.

Unraveling him from the inside.

"You *knew*," he breathed. The green flames bent toward him like grass in wind. "You came here knowing what you are. Knowing what three years of tasting had made you. This wasn't sacrifice. This was — " he searched for the word, and being ancient, he found it — "*seasoning*."

"I'm a taster," I said. "Presentation matters."

He lunged across the table.

I'll give him this: for a starving god having the worst meal of his eternal life, he moved fast. One instant he was seated; the next his hand was around my throat and I was lifted clean off the chair, my boots kicking air, and his face was close enough that I could see down into it, past the courteous mask, into the vast dark engine of appetite that had run for millennia on stolen light.

And I laughed.

I couldn't help it. It came out of me ragged and half-strangled and completely genuine, because I had spent three years waiting to die — every morning a spoonful of possible death, every supper a gamble, every dose a small wager that today my body would lose the count — and I had made my peace with it so thoroughly that a hand at my throat felt almost *familiar*. Almost like the palace kitchens on a bad day.

"You think this frightens me?" I rasped. "I've been choking down things worse than you since before you noticed my city existed. Death and I have an arrangement. He takes a little, I take a little. We're practically married."

"*What are you?*" The question tore out of him, and it wasn't rage anymore. It was something smaller and sadder. It was the sound of a king discovering an empty pantry.

"Petra Blackwood," I said. "Royal food-taster. And you, Your Majesty, have just eaten a meal you cannot keep down."

* * *

He dropped me. Not from mercy — from necessity, the way a man drops a pot that's too hot. I hit the floor and rolled, and my hand found the fold of cloth at my belt where Vess's ward-iron knife waited, small and cold and faithful.

Across the hall, Tam's head came up. The guards wavered — I saw it plainly, their shadow-stuff rippling like pond water — because whatever I was doing to the Underking's insides was connected to everything else he'd ever swallowed. The mountain itself shuddered. Dust sifted down from that high dark ceiling like the first snow of a season the Undercourt had never known.

The Underking gripped the edge of his beautiful table, and the black wood smoked under his fingers, and he looked at me with an expression I recognized from the far side of a hundred sickbeds I'd been summoned to taste-test my way out of.

The expression of a host watching his banquet go irretrievably wrong.

"You will not leave," he said. Each word cost him visibly now, like coins pried from a fist. "Nothing leaves. Nothing has *ever* — "

"Everything leaves," I said, getting my feet under me, knife low, heart hammering the only honest rhythm left in my body. "That's what food does, if you're lucky. It passes through and becomes something else. You've forgotten that, haven't you? You only ever take. You never let anything become something else. That's why you're starving in a hall full of servants." I smiled at him, and I put everything Sefa ever taught me about grinning at Wardens into it. "You don't have a court, Your Majesty. You have a larder. And larders, eventually, get raided."

The mountain groaned. Somewhere far above us — or below, directions were negotiable down here — stone split along a seam that had waited centuries for exactly this kind of pressure. The green torches flickered, and in the stutter of their light I saw them: cracks spreading up the pillars like frost on a window, thin veins of *light* bleeding through, starlight-colored, jar-light-colored, the glow of every stolen soul feeling the walls of its prison weaken.

The Underking swayed on his throne of courtesy and hunger, and inside him, I could feel it — feel *me*, the part of me he'd swallowed, doing its quiet corrosive work. Not fighting him. Poison doesn't fight. Poison just refuses to cooperate, patiently, chemically, grain by grain, until the whole system learns a new truth.

The truth I'd learned three years ago, swallowing my first dose with shaking hands: *what doesn't kill you changes what can.*

"Guards," the Underking said, and his voice was going hoarse, going thin, going *human*-sounding, which was the most horrifying thing I'd heard all night. "Keep them — keep them apart — "

The shadow-guards lurched toward Tam. Toward me. The hall tilted into chaos, torchlight and dust and the ringing crack of splitting stone, and I did the arithmetic I'd been carrying since Vess pressed the knife into my hand: one blade, one jar of starlight somewhere in the vaults, one brother with half a soul and a song stuck in what remained of his throat.

From the direction of the Stair of Teeth, muffled through tons of rock, came a sound I knew better than my own heartbeat.

A street-song. Badly sung. Deliberately off-key, in the unmistakable style of Sefa, with a chorus of smaller voices joining in — and one of them silent, singing with her hands, but singing nonetheless.

The market family had come down after me. Of course they had. I'd have been insulted if they hadn't.

The Underking heard it too, and turned toward the sound, and in turning gave me his back — the ancient, fatal mistake of every king who ever believed his kitchen was secure.

I tightened my grip on the ward-iron knife and started toward the vaults, toward Tam, toward the sound of my family singing in the dark.

Behind me, the King Beneath the Mountain began, very quietly, to be sick.
