## 17. Starlight Unbound

The Underking was still convulsing.

He had folded in on himself like a puppet whose strings had been cut and then set on fire, his long body thrashing against the floor of the feast hall, and every time he struck the stone the whole mountain rang with him — a deep, wounded bell-sound that Petra felt in her teeth, in her scars, in the ledger of poisons written into her blood. He had drunk a sip of her offered soul. One sip. It had been enough.

*Indigestible,* she thought, watching him writhe, and felt something that was almost laughter climb up out of her chest, ragged and disbelieving. Three years of arsenic and hemlock and worse, three years of swallowing death so a queen could eat breakfast in peace, and it turned out she had been marinating herself into a weapon all along.

But there was no time to enjoy it. The mountain was shaking apart around them, dust sifting down from the ceiling like gray snow, and ten feet away, on a shelf of black basalt, sat a jar of starlight.

Tam's jar.

She snatched it up and nearly dropped it. The glass was cold in a way glass should not be — cold like the space between stars, cold like the dark night three months ago when every light in the sky had gone out at once. Inside, a swirl of silver-blue radiance turned slowly, endlessly, and as Petra's hands closed around it, the light pressed toward her palms the way a cat presses toward a known hand.

And it hummed.

Not words. Not yet. But a melody, four notes rising and falling, worn smooth by repetition, and Petra's knees nearly went out from under her because she knew it. Everyone in the Nightmarket knew it. The fishwives sang it. The dockworkers whistled it. Pip used to sing it off-key on purpose just to annoy Sefa.

The market street-song. Tam's fragment of soul, sealed in a jar beneath a mountain for three months, and the first thing it did when it touched her was *sing home.*

"Oh," Petra said, and her voice cracked clean down the middle. "Oh, you stubborn idiot. You absolute— I told you not to follow me into trouble, and look where you got."

The light flared, indignant. Even dissolved half into a mountain's foundations, even a fraction of himself, Tam managed to be offended.

Behind her, the Underking made a sound like a glacier calving. She didn't turn to look. She was afraid that if she looked, if she gave him one heartbeat of her attention, he would find his feet again, and whatever hunger lived inside that courteous ancient body would come for her with all the patience of a thing that had been eating contracts since before her city had walls.

Vess appeared at her elbow, silent as spilled ink. The steward's hollow eyes were fixed on the convulsing king, and there was something in her face Petra had never seen there before — not hope exactly, hope was too tidy a word for it. More like a woman standing at a door she'd been locked behind for forty years and finding the lock already broken.

"The exchange is fouled," Vess said quietly. "His hunger has your death in its throat and cannot swallow it and cannot spit it out. It will not last forever. Break the jar, taster. Now, while he is down."

Petra looked at the jar. At the little captive star inside it, humming a fishwife's song in the dark.

"If I break it here," she said, "where does he go? Does it—" Her throat closed. She made herself finish. "Does it work? Is there enough of him left?"

"I have watched souls pour back into bodies for longer than your city has existed." Vess's voice was flat, but her hands, Petra noticed, were trembling. "I have never once seen one refuse to go home. Break it."

*No soul freely offered can be taken twice.* Corvane's dying gift, the flaw threaded through every contract the Underking had ever signed. Tam hadn't offered himself. Tam's name had been sold by a frightened man in a ledger room, and somewhere in the fine print of eternity, that mattered.

"Tam," Petra whispered to the jar. "If you can hear me — I'm sorry. I'm sorry it took me so long. I'm sorry about the charm I made you, the split one, I know you kept the halves, you sentimental disaster. Come back. Your family is upstairs raising hell without you. Sefa hasn't slept properly since the stars went out. Pip lost her *voice*, Tam, she paid it to a ward-toll getting down here to find you, and she came anyway. That's what you're worth. That's what you've always been worth."

The light in the jar pulsed, quick and bright, like a heart remembering how.

Petra raised the jar above her head and brought it down against the edge of the banquet table with everything she had.

The sound was not breaking glass. It was the struck bell from the night the wards fell, played backward, ascending instead of descending — and the starlight did not spill across the floor. It *leapt.* It poured upward and outward like water finding its level, silver-blue flooding the hall, and it knew where it was going. Of course it did. Souls are not lost; they are only misplaced.

Twenty feet away, slumped against the leg of the Underking's empty throne, lay Tam's body.

She had seen it earlier and forced herself not to weep over it — a tactical decision she now abandoned entirely. He looked terrible. He looked like a man who had been a foundation for three months, hollow-cheeked and gray, dressed in the strange black livery of the Undercourt's servants. His chest wasn't moving.

The starlight crossed the distance between them faster than regret.

It hit him like dawn hitting a valley, and Tam Kell — pickpocket, orphan, brother, baker's apprentice, the boy who had once carried eleven-year-old Petra Blackwood out of a burning tenement on his back because leaving anyone behind was not a thing his soul knew how to do — gasped.

His chest heaved. His fingers curled against stone. And then, because the universe occasionally permitted one small mercy, his eyes opened, found hers across the hall, and crinkled at the corners.

"Petra," he rasped. It was barely a whisper. It was the best sound she had ever heard. "You look *terrible.*"

"You look worse," Petra said, and burst into tears, and ran to him.

---

Being held by someone whose soul had recently been reassembled was, Tam would reflect later, an experience with texture. Everything felt slightly too vivid. Petra's arms around him were an event with orchestration. Her tears on his neck were each individually catalogued and treasured. Somewhere very far away, a king was screaming.

"You came," he said into her hair. His voice was a ruin. He didn't care. "You actually — they told me no one comes. They told me everyone forgets. I tried to remember your face and it kept sliding, and I held onto the song because the song was—"

"Hush." She pulled back and gripped his face in both poison-hardened hands, and he watched her take inventory of him the way she took inventory of everything, that quick flicker of her eyes that missed nothing. "You're real. You're here. We'll do the rest later."

"Petra." His new-old voice caught. "Your eyes. There's light in them. Actual — is that coming off me? Am I *glowing?*"

"A modest amount," she admitted. "It's very flattering. Don't let it go to your head."

Then the far doors of the feast hall exploded inward.

Tam flinched; Petra spun, one hand flying to the ward-iron knife Vess had slipped her — and then she started laughing, wet and helpless, because pouring through the shattered doors, battered and singed and absolutely incandescent with fury, came her family.

Sefa led them, of course. Sefa always led, because Sefa had decided at age nine that leadership was simply what happened when nobody else could be trusted to be sensible, and had never revisited the decision. Behind her came Bren with a smuggler's iron bar in both fists and a look of profound religious grievance; behind him Marta and old Joss and half a dozen Nightmarket faces Petra had feared she'd never see again, and at the very front of the charge, small and fierce and utterly mute, Pip.

Pip couldn't shout anymore. So she didn't. She simply marched up to Tam — who was struggling upright, dripping residual starlight, looking like a man waking from a decade-long nap — planted herself in front of him, and punched him in the arm as hard as her twelve-year-old arm allowed.

Then she threw both arms around him and held on, her shoulders shaking with everything her stolen voice couldn't say.

"Ow," Tam said, and wrapped her up, and over her head his eyes met Sefa's. "Hey," he said. "Sorry about the whole dying-for-three-months thing."

"You absolute *idiot,*" Sefa said, in a voice that wobbled in six places, and then she hugged him too, hard enough to crack ribs, and the rest of the family piled in after her until Tam disappeared entirely under a heap of orphans, and Petra stood at the edge of it wiping her face, laughing, glowing faintly in reflected light.

"We fought our way down a stair made of teeth for this," Bren announced to no one in particular. "Teeth, Tam. Somebody had better appreciate the teeth."

"I appreciate the teeth," Tam's muffled voice said from within the pile. "I appreciate everything. I appreciate being alive. Is it always this loud, being alive?"

"Yes," said everyone at once.

---

The Underking rose.

There was no announcement. No dramatic gathering of shadow. One moment the mountain was ringing with his struggle, and the next he was simply upright, swaying like a storm-bent tree, his ancient courteous face contorted around the bitter mouthful of Petra's seasoned soul that he could neither digest nor release. His eyes found her across the hall, and they were not angry.

They were *hungry,* and that was so much worse.

"Clever," the Underking said. Each word cost him visibly. "Clever, clever little taster. Do you know what you've done? Not saved him. That is a trifle. A rounding error. You have taught my hunger the taste of refusal. Do you understand what happens to a thing that has eaten for ten thousand years when it learns some flavors cannot be swallowed?" He smiled, and the smile split too wide. "It begins to question *everything it has ever eaten.*"

The hall shuddered. Dust rained. And beneath their feet, Petra felt it — a tremor that was not the king's rage but something deeper, older, structural. The mountain's foundations were made of dissolved souls. Half-dissolved Tam had been part of them. And if the king's own hunger was turning against itself—

"Then perhaps," Petra said, stepping forward, putting herself squarely between the Underking and her pile of family, "it's time to stop eating."

"Perhaps," said a voice from beside her.

Vess stepped forward, and the whole hall seemed to lean away from her, the way water leans from a dropped stone. She walked past Petra, past the huddled family, past the groaning contracts carved into the walls, until she stood before the convulsing king, and she looked up at him with forty years of service in her hollow eyes — and none of it was obedience anymore.

"I was taken," Vess said. "Do you remember? You never remember the names. I was a taster, like her. I was good at it. You ate my contract and called it employment and I served your table for four hundred years and told myself it was better than dissolution." She drew a breath, and Petra saw it — saw the shape of what she meant to do before she did it, and reached out too late. "You taught me that everything must be paid for. So consider this a payment. Freely given. The first one your mountain has ever received."

"Vess—" Petra started.

Vess smiled — a small, crooked, human smile, the first Petra had ever seen on her — and opened her hands, and *let go.*

What poured out of her was not light, exactly. It was more like weather. Four centuries of stored selfhood unfurling into the hall, and the mountain drank it in with a great shuddering sigh, and everywhere the soul-storm passed, things began to happen. Contracts carved in the walls flickered and guttered. Chains of obligation, invisible until that moment, snapped like harp strings. And at the far end of the hall, half-hidden behind the throne, stood two enormous doors of black iron banded with ward-iron — the gates of the Undercourt, sealed since before memory.

They swung open.

What came through was not a flood. Floods push. This was a *rising tide,* patient and total — centuries of stolen souls, thousands upon thousands, silver and blue and gold, streaming up out of the mountain's depths toward the open gates and the distant promise of sky. They passed through the hall like a river through a canyon. One brushed past Tam and he laughed out loud, startled, and said something in a language Petra didn't know, and the soul laughed back and moved on, upward, homeward.

The Underking watched his kingdom drain away, and for the first time in ten thousand years, the King Beneath looked small.

"No," he said, and it was not a command. It was a petition. "No. You don't understand what I am *for.* The wards. The balance. If I stop—"

"Then the stars come back," Petra said.

She said it simply, the way you tell someone the time. And high overhead, through the cracks that were spreading through the vaulted ceiling like frost across a window, the first thin thread of true darkness — not Undercourt darkness, but honest night — began to show.

The Underking sank back down onto the floor of his emptying hall, and did not rise again. Whether he was dying or merely starving, Petra could not tell, and did not stay to learn.

"Up," she said, turning to her family, to Tam, to the tide of freed souls flowing past them. "Everybody up. While the stairs still exist."

"What about her?" Sefa asked, nodding at Vess — at what remained of Vess, which was a slight woman standing very straight in the center of a hurricane of liberation, growing fainter at the edges like a candle burned to its final inch.

"She stays," Vess said, before anyone could speak. "Someone must hold the door while you climb. Someone must keep his attention while the last of them get out." She looked at Petra, and her hollow eyes were not hollow anymore; they were full to the brim. "You gave me back my name, little taster. I never told it to anyone here. It was Wren. My name was Wren." She smiled that crooked smile again. "Go and see the stars for me. All of them. Take your time about it."

Then she turned to face the fallen king, and the tide of souls bore the living up and away, and the mountain began, at long last, to exhale.
