## 19. Ascension

The Stair of Teeth had never been a stair in the way that word implies kindness. It was a wound in the mountain's throat, a spiral of fused bone and old stone descending into the dark — and now it was coming apart behind us like a sentence being unsaid.

Petra climbed.

She did not think about how she climbed, because thinking was a luxury her legs could no longer afford. Tam's arm lay across her shoulders, heavier than it should have been for a man who'd spent three years as a jar of starlight. His body remembered how to walk the way a house remembers a fire: structurally, tragically, mostly by what was missing.

"Put me down," he rasped for the fourth time.

"No."

"Petra. I weigh more than your ambitions."

"That's not possible. My ambitions are enormous." She shifted his weight higher on her hip and kept moving. "Besides, if I put you down, you'll do something noble and stupid, and I've had my fill of both this week."

Behind them, Sefa swore creatively in three languages as a step dissolved under her boot. Pip scrambled past them all on hands and knees, quick as a crab, voiceless and furious about it — she mouthed curses at the mountain with such eloquence that Petra almost laughed, and laughing would have cost her a rib, so she saved it.

Above them, impossibly far above, there was light.

Not Undercourt light, which was the color of mushrooms thinking. Real light. Thin and gray and trembling at the top of the shaft like a held breath.

Dawn.

* * *

The tolls began at the third landing.

The wards were dying — Vess had cracked the mountain's spine, and everything built on it was failing — but dying things bite hardest, and each threshold they crossed still demanded its price. Petra felt them the way she felt poison: a cold mineral wrongness gathering on her tongue before the ward even shimmered.

The first one took the name of Sefa's first ship. Sefa stopped dead, blinked, and said, "I had a boat once. Didn't I? I remember having a boat." Her face did something complicated and then smoothed over, the grief amputated clean. She shook herself and kept climbing. "Fine. It leaked anyway."

The second took Pip's memory of rain. The girl froze mid-step, hands opening and closing around something that wasn't there anymore, and Petra's chest cracked open along an old seam. She pulled Pip close, pressed the girl's head under her chin, and paid the next toll herself rather than let the mountain have anything else of hers.

That one cost her the smell of the Nightmarket at closing time — fried dough and lamp oil and wet stone, the smell that meant *home, everyone is home, go home.* It went out of her like a tooth pulled, leaving a smooth, aching socket where the memory had been. She knew she had loved it. She could no longer taste it.

"You're paying too many," Tam murmured against her shoulder. His voice was better than it had been in the vault, stronger, but still thin, still frayed at the edges like cloth washed too many times. "I can feel it when you pay. You flinch."

"I don't flinch."

"You flinch exactly like you did when you tasted arsenic for the queen and pretended it was cumin."

"That was one time, and I was very convincing."

"You were," he agreed softly. "You always are. That's what frightens me."

The third toll came at the landing where the bone-wards had fused into a lattice overhead, ribs of some ancient thing arched across the shaft. It didn't take a small memory. It circled, tasting, and Petra felt it probing for the good stuff — Tam's face in the vault-light, humming the street-song; Sefa's laugh; the queen's kitchen at dawn; bread, salted, torn and shared —

"No," Petra said out loud, to a ward made of bones. "Take something else."

It considered. Then it reached deeper, past the bright things, into the sediment, and took the winter she was nine years old, the one where she'd been hungry enough to eat candle wax and had learned, definitively, that the world did not care. It took the lesson but left the hunger, which felt obscenely unfair.

She staggered. Tam's grip tightened on her collar, holding her to the wall.

"What did it take?"

"Winter," she said. "When I was small."

"Was it a bad one?"

"The worst one." She tested her own mind the way she tested broth, probing for gaps. Oddly, the hole didn't hurt. It was more like finding a room in your house you'd forgotten you owned — furnished with cold and wax and loneliness, now simply gone. "I think I'm glad to lose it. Which worries me more than losing it."

Tam was quiet a moment. "Vess told me once — back when I was still — she said the mountain doesn't want your best memories. It wants the ones holding up the others." He looked up the shaft, toward the gray light. "You're shedding ballast, Petra. Don't shed too much, or you'll float away from yourself."

"Poetic. Are you sure you're half-dissolved and not just dramatic?"

"Both. It's the foundation of my character."

Despite everything — the burning thighs, the hollow where the market-smell used to live, the mountain groaning around them like a ship in a storm — she smiled. That was the thing about found family, she thought. They knew exactly which of your wounds to joke about, because they'd been there when you got them.

* * *

The stair began to fail in earnest at the halfway mark.

A section of steps the width of a market stall simply let go, dropping into darkness with a sound like a giant swallowing. Dust boiled up. Everyone flattened against the wall, and Petra's heart performed maneuvers she would later describe, generously, as "athletic."

"Now what?" Sefa asked.

Pip tugged Tam's sleeve and pointed up: a ledge, narrow, running along the inside curve of the shaft where the bone-lattice thickened. Climbable. Barely.

"Of course," Petra said. "Why would the escape route be sensible. Sensible is for people whose souls weren't collateral."

They went up the lattice like flies on a curtain. Petra went last, because someone had to be last, and because Tam refused to go first and she refused to argue about it with what little breath she had left. Below her, the dark yawned with the patience of geology. Above her, Tam's boots scraped and slipped and held.

Halfway across, the lattice shuddered.

Another toll — the last one, she'd swear it later, though the mountain kept no honest books — rose through the bones beneath her palms. Cold. Mineral. Wrong. And hungry, even now, even dying. It reached for her, and she was too tired to fight it properly, so she did what she always did with poison: she chose what to swallow.

It wanted a person. She gave it Corvane.

Not the confession — that she kept; the court would need it, and besides, a dying man's truth deserved a witness. She gave the ward the older thing instead: the memory of Corvane in the kitchens years ago, teaching her the palace ledger-system, patient, almost kind, a man she'd liked before she'd learned what he'd signed. The version of him that had made the betrayal hurt.

The ward drank it. The lattice steadied under her hands.

And Petra climbed the rest of the way feeling strangely lighter and strangely robbed at once, because grief without its reasons is just weight with the meaning scooped out. She'd need to grieve Corvane again someday, from scratch, with nothing but the facts. The mountain hadn't taken her pain. It had taken her right to understand it.

"Almost there," Sefa called down, and her voice cracked on *almost*, and nobody teased her for it.

* * *

They broke the surface as the sun did.

There was no ceremony to it. One moment Petra hauled Tam over a lip of shattered toll-gate stone, and the next they were lying in a heap on the mountainside among frost-killed grass, gasping like landed fish, while the sky above them did something none of them had seen in three years.

It was getting light.

Not the flat, sourceless gray of the starless months — actual dawn, gold bleeding up from behind the eastern ridges, catching the clouds and setting them alight. And scattered through the brightening blue, faint as breath on glass:

Stars.

Fading even as they watched, the night's last embers giving themselves up to morning, but there — real, returning, the sky remembering itself. One burned low over the mountain's crown, stubborn, brighter than the rest, and did not fade until the sun took the whole sky.

Nobody spoke. Pip made a sound — a small, cracked, wordless sound, the first noise she'd managed since the toll-gate, and it wasn't words but it was louder than words, and Sefa wrapped her up and cried into her hair without any dignity whatsoever.

Tam lay on his back in the frost-wet grass, staring up, tears running sideways into his ears, laughing weakly. "I missed the stars," he said. "Do you know how much of me was starlight? Apparently a significant portion. I feel very seen."

"You are literally glowing," Petra said. "A little. Around the edges."

"It's the new diet."

She sat beside him in the wet grass, too exhausted to care about damp, and let herself look at him — really look, the way she tasted a dish, taking in every note. He was thinner than the brother she remembered. There were gaps in him she could sense the way she sensed arsenic under cumin: places where the soul had been sanded down and only partly rebuilt. He would forget things. He would have mornings where he woke reaching for a jar-shaped dark. Three years underground did not come back up all at once.

But he was here. He was warm. When the wind moved, he shivered, and shivering meant alive, meant body, meant *his*.

"We did it," Sefa said, wiping her face with a sleeve already beyond saving. "We actually — Pip lost her voice, I lost my boat, you—" she pointed at Petra, hand wobbling with exhaustion, "—you've paid away half your childhood, and we did it anyway. Somebody should write this down."

"Somebody will," Petra said. "The court will want a report. In triplicate."

Sefa groaned. Pip mimed gagging. Tam laughed until he coughed, and Petra beat his back with the flat of her hand, gently, the way you handle anything that has recently been kept in a jar.

* * *

They rested because they had to, not because it was safe. The mountain behind them had gone quiet — not peaceful quiet, but the quiet of a building after the tenants flee, all its stolen souls poured out through Vess's open gates and streaming toward the surface like fish going home. Petra had felt them pass in the last stretch of the climb: centuries of the taken, rising through the rock around them, a tide of relief. Some had brushed against her as they went. A few had paused, curious, tasting the air of her — the poisons, the ash, the strange indigestible bitterness — and moved on without harm, the way travelers nod to a stranger on a road.

One had lingered. A woman's presence, dry and wry and tired beyond telling, and Petra had known without knowing how: *Vess.*

She hadn't come up with them. She had stayed below, holding the collapsing king's attention, feeding her own long-held soul back into the mountain willingly — the first free gift it had ever been given. Petra had felt the moment the mountain accepted it: a shudder through the whole stair, not pain but surprise, the shock of a creature that had only ever been paid in theft receiving something given.

The low star over the peak had kindled shortly after.

"Vess bought us our exit," Petra said quietly, when the others had drifted into exhausted sleep in the grass. She said it to Tam, who was fighting sleep and losing. "She stayed."

"I know." His eyes were closed. "I heard her. At the end. She told me—" he frowned, groping, "—she told me to tell you that the ledger closes. That debts end. That you're allowed to put the book down now."

Petra looked at the place on the mountainside where the toll-gate had been. It was rubble now, sealed, the Stair of Teeth collapsed into itself. Somewhere under all that stone, a hollow-eyed woman who had once been a taster like her was becoming the first generous thing the mountain had ever contained.

"She's wrong," Petra said. "I'm picking the book back up the moment I can stand. There's a queen up that hill who sold my brother's name and wrote mine in the margin as spare change. And a steward's daughter who deserves to know what her father traded for her. And a law that calls Sefa's charms crimes." She flexed her poisoned, seasoned, unconquerable hands. "The ledger isn't closed, Tam. It's just changing owners."

He opened one eye. Even half-dissolved, even wrung out, he managed the look — the fond, exasperated, entirely unsurprised look he'd been giving her since they were children stealing bread on the docks.

"There she is," he said. "I wondered where they'd put you."

* * *

They came down off the mountain at midmorning, four ragged ghosts limping toward the city walls, and the city saw them coming long before the gates did.

Word ran ahead of them the way word does — faster than horses, faster than sense. By the time they reached the great gate, people lined the road: dockworkers and bakers and Nightmarket stalls abandoned mid-sale, their keepers standing in the open with their illegal little charms lit like candles, luck-knots and hearth-sparks and door-blessings glowing openly in daylight for the first time in anyone's memory. Because the wards were back — the border wards stood again, singing, and the law that had outlawed small magic suddenly seemed like a law written by frightened people in a dark that was now ending.

An old woman pressed a heel of fresh bread into Tam's hands as he passed. He broke it, salted it from a pouch at his belt — Petra's pouch; she'd carried salt up a mountain, because priorities — and shared it with all of them, standing in the road, and Petra ate her piece and thought: *this* is the taste the ward stole. Not the market-smell. This. Bread broken by the people who came down into the dark for you.

She'd carry that one carefully. Ward or no ward, some things she would guard with her teeth.

At the palace gates, a royal herald stood waiting with a face like a man ordered to smile at a funeral, and delivered the summons with commendable steadiness: Queen Maren requested — the pause was audible — *the presence of Petra Blackwood, Royal Taster, and her companions, at her earliest convenience.*

"Her earliest convenience," Sefa repeated. "After she sold your brother. After she listed you as *collateral*. Like a spoon."

"Like a very valuable spoon," Petra said. "Let's not inflate my importance."

"Petra."

"I know." She rolled her shoulders. Every muscle filed a formal complaint. Somewhere in her head, the empty rooms where memories used to live ached in the weather. But underneath the exhaustion was the other thing, the thing that had gotten her down the Stair of Teeth and back: the steady, mineral, unswallowable taste of being done being currency. "I know, Sefa. Believe me. I'm not walking in there to curtsy."

Tam caught her sleeve as the herald turned to lead them in. Up close, in full daylight, with stars freshly returned to the sky behind him, he looked fragile and permanent at once, like a candle flame in a windless room.

"You don't have to do this today," he said. "Rest first. Fight tomorrow."

"If I rest, I'll be kind," Petra said. "And I've swallowed enough bitter things to know exactly how this one should taste." She squeezed his hand once, brief and hard, and turned toward the throne room doors, where the queen who had written her name in a dead man's ledger was waiting to learn what three years of poison had made of her taster.

The doors opened. Petra walked through.
