## 20. Reckoning at Court

They dressed me in silk for the reckoning.

I want that on the record: they *dressed me in silk*. Some poor chambermaid arrived at dawn with a gown the color of unbleached flour and a face like she'd rather be feeding it to a fire, and she said, "Her Majesty's orders," and I said, "Does her Majesty know I've spent three years eating poison for a living and four days underground arguing with a starving god?" and she said nothing, because what do you say to that.

So I wore the silk. Underneath it I wore my market coat with all its hidden pockets, because some things you don't give up just because you're about to commit treason politely.

Sefa fussed over my hair while Pip sat on the windowsill of our borrowed rooms, mute as a stone, mouthing along when Sefa muttered insults at the court's idea of fashion. Pip had been back above ground for two days. Her voice hadn't come with her. She'd paid it at the Stair of Teeth so the rest of us could pass — a child's voice, traded for an adult's stubbornness — and every time I looked at her sitting there so patient, working out her words on a little slate, something in my chest went tight and hot and refused to be reasoned with.

"You look like a duchess who's about to rob someone," Sefa said, tying off the braid.

"Good. That's accurate."

Tam was by the window too, where he'd been most mornings since we came up. He still moved carefully, like his body was a house he was relearning room by room. His hands were steady now, though. Steadier than mine.

"You don't have to do this alone," he said. "We could all stand up there with you."

"I know." I turned around. "That's why I'm not going to need you to. But thank you for offering to loom menacingly."

"We're very good at looming," Sefa said. "It's practically our trade."

* * *

The throne room of Queen Maren had always smelled wrong to me. Most people never notice what a room smells like; I've never had that luxury. The great hall beneath the mountain had smelled of hunger and cold stone and old contracts gone sour. Maren's throne room smelled of beeswax and lilies and, underneath it, faintly but unmistakably, of fear. Courtiers' fear. It had soaked into the stone over decades, the way smoke soaks into a kitchen wall.

I stood on the mosaic floor with Corvane's confession ledger in my satchel and the whole court arrayed above me in their tiers of velvet, and I thought: *you are all afraid of the wrong things.*

Maren looked older than she had a week ago. Grief will do that, even to queens — especially, maybe, to queens, who have no one to share it with honestly. She had not known, or she had half-known, or she had known and let herself not-look, and Corvane's dying testimony had dragged all three possibilities into the light where none of them could survive. She had read it aloud herself, before the assembled court, because refusing would have been worse. Her voice had only shaken twice.

Now she sat with her hands folded and said, "Petra Blackwood. Approach."

I approached. My boots — under the silk skirt, where nobody could stop me — were loud on the mosaic.

"By right of blood and crown," Maren said, in the voice she used for proclamations, "and in gratitude beyond measure, I offer you a title. Lands in the eastern valleys. A seat at this court. Knight of the Realm, Lady Blackwood of—" she paused, and I watched her improvise— "of Ashfall."

Someone behind me made a sound. I chose to believe it was Tam suppressing a laugh.

I bowed. Not deeply. Then I straightened, and I tasted the air of the room one more time — beeswax, lilies, fear — and I began.

"Your Majesty honors me," I said. "And I'm going to refuse."

The silence that followed had texture. You could have sliced it and served it with honey.

"The eastern lands are generous, and I'd probably burn the manor down within the year, because I've lived my whole life in rooms I could leave through a window. But that's not the real reason." I pulled Corvane's ledger from my satchel. It looked small and shabby in the grand hall, a kitchen account-book with a dead man's handwriting. "This book lists names. Names sold to the Undercourt to keep the wards standing. Tam Kestrel's name is in it. Mine is in it too, under 'reserve collateral,' which is a phrase your Majesty's own steward wrote, meaning that if the crown ever needed another soul, I was already priced."

I let that sit. Let the courtiers hear it. Several of them looked at their shoes, which was at least honest.

"Corvane signed the pact to save his daughter, and I believe he regretted it every day after, and I believe he died badly and partly of that regret. That's not why I'm here. I'm here because the wards fell, and the stars went out, and the reason they fell isn't written anywhere in this room. The reason is that three years ago this court decided that a person could be a line item. And the law that made it easy — the Hard Law against small magics — is the same law that made people like me criminals for selling luck-knots to dockworkers, while the men in fine coats brokered souls upstairs where nobody checks the arithmetic."

I turned to face the tiers. Faces everywhere, pale and attentive, the fear-smell sharpening.

"So here's what I want instead of a title. Repeal the Hard Law. All of it. Small magics, street charms, hedge-craft, taster's gifts — legal. Licensed if you must, taxed if you can't help yourselves, but *legal*. Because the night the wards fell, the only people who knew how to fight were the ones you'd outlawed. And the first star that came back came from a woman named Vess, who spent forty years serving the Undercourt's table because the world up here had no better place for a girl who could taste magic."

Maren's jaw tightened. "You ask me to undo thirty years of law in an afternoon."

"No, Your Majesty. I ask you to undo it in front of witnesses, which takes less time and more courage." I held up the ledger. "And I want this entered into the permanent record, with every name in it read aloud, once a year, on the night the stars went dark. Not as punishment. As remembering. Courts forget on purpose. Markets don't get that luxury."

A murmur ran through the tiers like wind through wheat. Somewhere a lordling whispered *insolence*, and somewhere else, quieter, someone whispered *she's right*.

Maren studied me for a long moment. When she spoke again, the proclamation-voice was gone, and what remained was just a tired woman who had made terrible bargains and knew it. "You could have taken the title and changed the law slowly, from inside. Safer. Slower."

"With respect, Your Majesty — slow is how Tam's name ended up in that book. Slow is how Vess rotted for forty years under a mountain. Slow is how a seven-year-old lost her voice paying a toll that shouldn't have existed." I glanced back at Pip, small and silent in the gallery where Sefa had planted her, chin up, slate in hand. "I've eaten enough bitter things to know when the taste is coming from the dish and when it's coming from the cook. This law has been poisoned for thirty years. You don't dilute poison. You spit it out and start fresh."

The silence stretched. And then Queen Maren did something I will remember longer than any star: she laughed. Short, startled, rueful.

"Three years of swallowing death," she said, mostly to herself. "It does make a person difficult." She rose. The whole court rose with her, a rustling tide. "Let it be entered: the Hard Law is repealed, effective this hour. Master Blackwood's terms are granted. And the title—" her eyes glinted, and there, faintly, was the woman who'd once smuggled me extra honey cakes during my apprentice years, before the crown got heavy— "the title is declined and recorded, with the note that Lady Blackwood of Ashfall would have burned the manor down."

"That's *Miss* Blackwood," I said. "No lady. I taste food for a living."

"Tasted," she corrected gently. "Past tense, unless you intend to keep standing at my table."

I thought about it honestly, which surprised me. The old dread didn't come. "I'll consult," I said. "On hard cases. But my table's in the Nightmarket now."

* * *

Walking out took longer than walking in, because the entire court had apparently decided, without consulting me, to applaud.

I hate applause. Let me be clear about that. Applause is what crowds give you instead of help. It tastes like copper and performance, and I stood in the middle of it with my ears burning, looking for the exits the way I always do, and then I saw my family.

They'd come down from the gallery. Sefa first, arms crossed, wearing her best smug expression. Behind her Tam, upright and thin and *present*, which still felt like a miracle I hadn't finished believing. And Pip, who couldn't speak, who had given away the small bright instrument of herself so that the rest of us could climb, holding her slate high above her head.

Written on it, in her enormous careful letters:

**TOLD YOU SHE WOULDN'T CRY**

The laughter started near them and spread outward, and I realized my eyes were wet, and I decided — in the spirit of the day, in the spirit of spitting out poisons — not to dilute anything.

"I'm not crying," I announced to the throne room. "There's dust in the air. This building is filthy. Someone should look into it."

More laughter. Maren, descending from the dais, leaned close as she passed and murmured, "The building is spotless, Blackwood. Take the win."

I took it. Badly. Visibly. Pip grinned like the sun.

Outside, the steps of the palace were crowded — word travels fast in a city that's spent a week staring at an empty sky — and the crowd had heard everything somehow, or enough of it, and they cheered as we came down into the light. Dockworkers. Bakers. A knot of Nightmarket charm-sellers with their licenses literally sewn to their sleeves for the occasion, like badges, like armor finally set down.

A dockworker I recognized — big hands, the luck-knot I'd sold him months ago still tied around his wrist, frayed and re-tied and frayed again — pressed through to me and held out his wrist.

"It worked," he said gruffly. "Every storm since. Never doubted it."

"Liar," I said. "But it's a kind lie. Keep it."

He nodded and melted back, and Sefa appeared at my elbow. "You realize," she said, "that you just repealed a thirty-year law, and your biggest concern was whether the palace dust was making you cry."

"One crisis at a time."

"And now?"

I looked up. The sky was doing its ordinary late-morning thing, blue and bland and utterly starless, the way daytime skies are supposed to be. But tonight — tonight was the anniversary. Tonight the city would stand in the streets and read names aloud, and look up, and wait.

"Now," I said, "we go home. There's bread to bake. Somebody owes me a batch of salted honey wine. And I have a feeling—" I tapped my tongue against my teeth, that old habit, tasting the day — "that tonight, the sky might owe us a show."

Pip tugged my sleeve and held up her slate. New words, chalked fast, slightly smudged:

**IF IT DOES, CAN WE WATCH FROM THE ROOF? WITH CAKES?**

"Pip," I said, "if a single star comes back tonight, we will watch it from the roof with so many cakes."

She pumped her fist. Tam laughed — really laughed, the first full one since he came back — and Sefa rolled her eyes at all of us, and we walked home through a city that was learning, slowly, clumsily, to leave its doors open.

Behind us the palace bells began to ring the repeal. Ahead, the Nightmarket smelled of cinnamon and solder and possibility.

And far above, past the blue, something small and patient was waiting for dark.
