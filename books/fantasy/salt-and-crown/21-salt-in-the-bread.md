## 21. Salt in the Bread

The first thing Tam learned about having a soul again was that it was hungry.

Not the hollow, gnawing hunger of the mountain — that had been a kind of forgetting, an appetite with no name attached to it, like being hungry for a word you couldn't remember. This was different. This was his own hunger, returned to him like a coat left at a friend's house three years ago: familiar in shape, smelling faintly of someone else's smoke.

He stood in the market kitchen at dawn, hands buried in flour to the wrist, and wept over a bowl of dough.

It wasn't grief, exactly. Or it was, but grief and joy had gotten tangled somewhere on the Stair of Teeth and come out of him braided together, and he'd stopped trying to separate them. The dough didn't mind. Dough never minded anything. That was why he loved it.

"You're doing it wrong," said Pip from the doorway.

Pip had been back for six weeks now, her voice still living elsewhere — she spoke in chalk and gestures and a small slate she wore on a cord around her neck, and she had developed, in the absence of speech, a glare of tremendous range and precision. She wrote now, in letters that slanted with irritation: *You're kneading it like you're angry at it.*

"I am a little angry at it," Tam admitted.

*Why?*

"Because it gets to be simple." He looked down at his hands, white to the elbow, steady for the first time in months. "Because it just has to rise. That's all it has to do."

Pip considered this with the gravity of a magistrate. Then she crossed the kitchen, took the slate from her neck, and wrote something she held up so he could not miss it:

*Rise anyway.*

He laughed — a rusty sound, still surprising to him, like a door opening in a house he'd thought empty — and went back to work.

* * *

Relearning his appetites was stranger than anyone had warned him it would be.

Sefa had warned him plenty. Sefa, who had organized the breaking of a sealed toll-gate with smuggled charms and sheer stubbornness, who had lost nothing but sleep and gained a permanent air of having been right about everything, sat him down the first week and delivered a lecture on eating slowly, chewing thoroughly, and not crying into the soup, which made it salty.

"The soup is already salty," Tam had said.

"Then you'll make it brine, and I'll have to water it down, and it'll be thin, and I'll blame you forever."

But nobody had told him that taste itself would need relearning. That honey would hit him like a memory of sunlight. That salt — plain salt, the cheap gray kind from the marsh pans — would make him sit very still at the table until Sefa asked if he was all right, and he'd have to say, *I don't know what it tastes like yet. Give me a moment.* That meat was overwhelming. That he cried at an orange, an actual orange, because the smell of the peel was so violently, stupidly alive.

The Undercourt had fed him, in its way. He remembered meals served by souls with hollow eyes, dishes that tasted of nothing at all, or worse, tasted of almost — almost butter, almost rosemary, almost home — each one a little lie his starving self had believed because believing was easier than starving honestly. Three years of almost-flavors. His tongue had learned distrust the way Petra's had learned poison.

Now everything real was too much, and then, slowly, day by day, exactly enough.

He baked because baking was honest. Flour, water, salt, time. No contracts. No debts. Nothing in a loaf of bread wanted anything from him except patience, and patience was something he had in ruinous abundance now, having spent three years waiting inside a jar of starlight for someone to come.

Someone had come.

He tried not to think about it while he worked, because thinking about it made his eyes sting and the dough suffer for it. But the thought came anyway, the way it did every morning, patient as yeast: *She came down the Stair of Teeth. She paid memories. She offered her soul — her actual soul, the whole seasoned, poisoned, impossible thing — for mine.* And every morning the thought ended in the same place it always did, which was a question he could not shape properly, and a feeling underneath it too large for any of the words he owned.

* * *

Petra started coming to the kitchen in the second month.

Not all at once. At first she appeared at the market at odd hours, buying things she didn't need — a twist of saffron, a jar of pickled radish, once, memorably, a live eel that escaped in the square and caused a diplomatic incident between two fishmongers. She was thinner than before the mountain. There were new lines around her eyes, and sometimes she'd pause mid-sentence, mid-step, mid-laugh, with the look of someone checking whether a certain taste was still there — reaching for a memory and finding the shelf empty. He knew that look. He'd worn its twin for years.

He never asked what she'd paid. She never offered. They circled each other through the autumn like two cats negotiating ownership of a warm windowsill, and the family watched this performance with the tact of a theater audience that had paid good money for the drama and intended to enjoy it.

"He's baking again," Sefa reported one evening, in the tone of a scout delivering intelligence. "He made forty rolls. Forty. We're four people."

"Leave them out," said old Marta, who had taken over the mending since her hands got stiff, "and see if they vanish."

"They'll vanish," Sefa said darkly. "They always vanish. And then tomorrow there will be sixty rolls, and eventually we will be buried in bread, and it will be a lovely way to die."

"It's how he thinks," Marta said, threading her needle without looking up. "His mother did the same. Grief goes into the oven or it goes into the walls. Bread's better for the house."

Petra said nothing at all during this conversation, which everyone noticed, and nobody mentioned.

* * *

The morning it finally happened, Tam had been up since the fourth bell, working a slow sourdough the way his mother used to — the way he'd taught himself to remember it, which might not have been the way it actually was, but the bread didn't know the difference and neither, anymore, did he.

The kitchen was warm. Rain ticked against the shutters. He had flour on his forearms and a blister forming where the peel handle rubbed, and he was humming — actually humming, the market street-song, the one his fragment had sung in the dark of the vault without knowing he was singing it — when the door opened and Petra came in out of the rain.

She stopped. He stopped. The song hung in the warm air between them like a bird that had flown in through a window and couldn't find its way out.

"That's the market song," she said.

"It is."

"You hummed it. Just now."

"I've been humming it for weeks." He turned back to the bench, because looking at her directly felt like looking at the sun through a jar. "Sefa says it's driving her mad. Pip says—" he glanced at the slate propped by the flour bin, where Pip had written, in enormous letters, *IF HE HUMS IT ONE MORE TIME I WILL LOSE MY OTHER VOICE* — "well. You can read."

Petra laughed. It was a real laugh, startled out of her, and it did something to the room, warmed it another degree, and Tam decided, standing there with his hands in the dough, that he was done circling. He was done being a man who waited in jars for other people to be brave.

"Sit down," he said. "This loaf needs twenty more minutes and I need to say some things, and I'd rather do both while my hands are busy."

She hesitated in the doorway, rain dripping off her hood, and for one terrible moment he thought she'd turn around and leave, and he'd spend another month leaving rolls outside her door like offerings to a skittish god.

Then she pulled off her cloak, hung it on the peg — *her* peg, the one that had stayed empty for three years because nobody had the heart to reassign it — and sat down on the flour-dusted stool by the window.

* * *

The loaf came out golden. Of course it did; even his failures came out golden lately, the oven apparently having decided to compensate for the mountain. He cut it, and the crust cracked with that sound — that specific, holy sound — and steam rolled up fragrant and soft, and he set half a loaf in front of her with butter and the good salt, the flaky sea kind Sefa traded for at the docks.

"Eat," he said. "I want your opinion. And I want you to eat it while I talk, because if you're chewing you can't argue."

"That," said Petra, tearing off a piece, "is the single most manipulative thing you've ever said to me, and I once watched you convince a Warden that our entire stall was a licensed puppet theater."

"And yet you're chewing."

She was. He watched her take the first bite — watched the moment it landed, the small involuntary closing of her eyes, the way her shoulders came down half an inch — and felt a satisfaction deeper than any he'd known below the mountain. *There,* he thought. *That's real. That's salt and wheat and fire, and it wants nothing from you, and it's yours.*

"So," he said, and wiped his hands on his apron, and found that the speech he'd composed at the fourth bell had entirely evaporated. "Why did you come for me?"

Her chewing slowed.

"I mean—" he pressed on, before his nerve failed, "I know the official version. Duty. The queen's orders. Corvane's confession, the contract flaw, all of it. I've heard Sefa tell it at the market three times and it gets more heroic every telling. But I was in that vault, Petra. I was a hum in a jar. And I felt you coming before I ever saw you — I felt it like heat through a wall. Nobody walks down a stair made of teeth and pays pieces of themselves at every door for *duty*. Duty doesn't taste like that." He stopped. Started again, quieter. "So I'm asking. Not the story. The truth."

Petra looked at her bread. She looked at it for a long time, turning the piece in her fingers, and Tam realized with a lurch that she was frightened — Petra Blackwood, who had identified seven poisons in a single broth to bluff her way into a king's kitchen, who had stared down an ancient starving god and been found indigestible — frightened of a question over bread in a warm kitchen.

"When I was eleven," she said finally, "you carried me to the herb-wife's after I ate the wrong berries. Do you remember?"

"You ate them on a dare."

"I ate them because Dossen dared me and I was eleven and stupid. And you carried me the whole way, and I was sick on your only good shirt, and you never once mentioned it again. Not once, in fifteen years." She set the bread down. "And when I got my tongue — when I learned to taste magic, when I figured out what I was — you were the first person I told, and you didn't look at me like I was a weapon or a crime. You looked at me like I was still me. Do you know what that's worth? To someone whose whole life is tasting things that want to kill her? To be looked at like she's *still me*?"

"Petra—"

"I'm getting there." Her voice had gone rough. "Three years, Tam. Three years I swallowed death for a queen who'd written my name in a ledger as spare change. Three years I sold charms in the dark and watched Wardens hunt children for lighting candles. And the whole time, the one true thing — the one clean thing — was that you existed. Somewhere in the city, you existed, and you saved my life once and never billed me for it, and I built half my luck-charms on that fact. You were the proof that somebody, somewhere, had been good to me for free." She looked up, and her eyes were wet and furious about being wet. "And then the stars went out, and your room was empty, and there was my charm split in two on the floor, and I understood that the world had taken the one clean thing. So yes. The queen ordered me to investigate. Corvane's ledgers pointed down. All of that is true." She picked the bread back up, and ate a bite, and swallowed, and said, simply:

"There was never a version of the world where I didn't come for you. I've looked. I've been through every version I own. In all of them, I'm on those stairs."

The rain ticked against the shutters. The oven ticked as it cooled. Somewhere out in the market, Pip was presumably terrorizing a customer with her slate.

Tam found that his eyes had gone hot, and that arguing was, for once, the last thing he wanted to do.

"I sang to you," he said. "In the jar. I didn't know it was singing. I thought it was just — noise I made, the way water makes noise. And then you shattered the glass and the first thing I saw was your face, and I thought, *oh. That's what the song was for.*"

"Tam."

"I practiced this speech for a month," he said. "I had a whole structure. There was going to be an analogy involving sourdough starter."

That startled the laugh out of her again, wet and cracked, and she wiped her face with the heel of her hand, leaving a smear of flour across her cheekbone that he wanted to look at forever.

"Tell me the analogy," she said.

"A sourdough starter keeps dying and coming back. Every baker knows it. You neglect it, it goes gray and sour and smells like regret, and everyone says throw it out, start fresh. But you feed it. You feed it flour and water and you wait, and it comes back. Every time. Because the living part of it never actually left." He took her hand — flour and all, salt and all, poison-ledger and all — across the scarred kitchen table. "You're my starter, Petra. You always come back. I'm trying to say I noticed."

"That's the worst analogy I've ever heard."

"I know."

"It's also the best thing anyone's ever said to me."

"I know that too."

She turned her hand over and laced her fingers through his, and they sat there in the flour-warm quiet, two people who had been to the bottom of the world and back, holding on across a table like it was the last solid thing in it — though it wasn't, of course. It was just a table. That was the miracle of it. It was just a table, and the bread was just bread, and the love was just love, spoken plainly at last, needing no contract and owing no debt.

"Stay for lunch," Tam said. "I'm making soup. And if you cry in it, I'm watering it down."

"I make no promises," said Petra, and reached for another piece of bread.

* * *

They were still at the table, laughing over Sefa's inevitable outrage at the soup's salt content, when the knock came at the kitchen door — three raps, formal, unfamiliar.

Pip got there first, slate already raised in warning. She opened the door a crack, then all the way, and stepped aside.

A palace herald stood in the rain, travel-stained, holding a scroll sealed with the queen's crest. Behind him, at the edge of the square, a crowd was gathering around the Nightmarket's notice board, and someone was shouting — not in fear, but in that bright, disbelieving voice people use for news too large to hold.

Tam looked at Petra. Petra was already on her feet, reading the seal, her taster's stillness settling over her like a second skin.

"What is it?" he asked.

She broke the wax. Read. And then she smiled — slow, and wondering, and a little bit sad — and turned the page toward him.

Above the mountain, the notice said, on the anniversary of the dark night, a new star had been seen.
