## 22. The First Star

The Nightmarket smelled different these days.

Petra noticed it every time she came down the crooked lane from the palace district — the way the air had loosened, unclenched. For years the market had held its breath. Charms were sold in whispers under awnings, luck-knots passed hand to hand like contraband, and everyone kept one eye on the Wardens' lanterns and the other on the sky, as if the stars themselves might report them.

Now the stalls hung their wares out bold as washing on a line. Sefa's cousin Brenna had a whole table of knotwork charms, each one labeled with a little card in her looping hand: *For Safe Roads. For Good Sleep. For Finding What You Lost.* A Warden stood at the mouth of the lane, and instead of patrolling he was buying a charm against hangovers, paying full price, haggling badly.

"You're overcharging him," Petra told Brenna as she passed.

"He can afford it," Brenna said serenely. "He confiscated my mother's stall for six years."

The Warden had the grace to look sheepish about it.

Petra carried her basket through the crowd, nodding at faces she'd known since they were pickpockets and faces she'd known since they were orphans, which in the Nightmarket was often the same category. The found family had grown, the way families do when you stop being afraid of losing them. Pip walked beside her now, seventeen and sharp-tongued, her voice restored a year after the mountain took it — though she still went quiet sometimes, mid-sentence, like a person listening for something far away. When that happened, Tam would just wait, patient as bread rising, until she found her words again.

"Vess is late," Pip said.

"Vess is always late."

"She's *always* late *on purpose*," Pip said. "She says punctuality is a flavor of desperation."

"That sounds like Vess."

They reached the old tannery steps where the teaching circle met — three benches arranged in a half-moon around a fire pit, because Petra had learned long ago that children learned flavors better with something warm in their hands. Six students sat waiting, ranging from a palace scullery boy of nine to a gray-haired widow who had decided, at sixty-two, that it was finally time to understand what her grandmother's tea really did.

"All right," Petra said, setting down her basket. "Today we taste iron."

A collective groan.

"I know. I know. Nobody likes iron day." She began laying out the little cups, each holding a thimbleful of water that had spent the night with a nail, a horseshoe nail, an old key, a coin from three different reigns. "But here is what nobody tells you: iron is honest. Arsenic lies. Belladonna flirts. Hemlock tells you exactly what you want to hear right up until your throat closes. Iron just says what it is. *I am metal. I am cold. I am not food.* If you can learn to hear honesty, you'll never be fooled by a lie."

The scullery boy, whose name was Odo and who had the finest palate Petra had ever taught, raised his hand.

"Yes?"

"What does honesty taste like? The real kind. Not iron."

Petra considered the question with the seriousness it deserved. Around them the market chattered; somewhere Sefa was arguing with a fishmonger about the moral character of eels; above the rooftops the sky sat pale and starless as it always did by day.

"Bread," she said at last. "Honesty tastes like bread someone made for you because they knew you'd be hungry."

Odo wrote that down. He wrote everything down. He was going to be better at this than she was, and Petra found she looked forward to it rather than minding.

They tasted. They spat. They compared notes — *nail water tastes like a door hinge in winter*, said the widow, which was so precisely correct that Petra asked if she could steal it for the lesson book. The afternoon went golden and unhurried. This was the work now, and Petra loved it with a fierce, uncomplicated love: taking the thing that had been done *to* her — the forced feedings, the ledger of poisons written into her blood before she was old enough to consent — and turning it into something given freely, taught gently, guarded by consent the way a flame is guarded by a lamp.

When the lesson ended and the students drifted off toward supper, Pip lingered.

"She's coming tonight," Pip said. It wasn't a question.

"For the anniversary." Petra folded the tasting cups back into their cloth. "Sefa's roasting a goat. Tam's baking. There will be salted honey wine, because there is always salted honey wine, because I am a creature of habit and grief and I have decided both are allowed to be sweet."

"And the toast?"

"The same as always." Petra smiled. "To the ones who paid, and the ones who stayed."

Pip nodded, satisfied, and then hesitated in the way she did when she was about to say something true. "You know it's going to happen tonight. Everyone feels it. The air's been wrong all week — sweet, almost. Like the mountain's holding its breath too."

Petra looked up, past the market roofs, past the palace spires, to the great dark shoulder of the mountain that loomed over the city like a sleeping animal. The Undercourt was gone. They had cracked its foundation open and let centuries of stolen souls pour out toward the surface like light finding a crack in a wall. But the mountain itself remained, and mountains remember.

"Then we'd best get home," Petra said. "I don't intend to miss it twice."

* * *

Tam's kitchen was the warmest room in the world, and Petra would defend that claim against all comers.

It occupied the ground floor of the house the market family had bought together with the queen's restitution money — a narrow, stubborn building wedged between a dyer's shop and a shrine to some minor river god, with a oven Tam had rebuilt himself, brick by brick, while he relearned how to be a person who ate. Three years dissolved in a jar of starlight will do strange things to a soul. Tam had come back thin in places no food could fill, slow to laugh, prone to standing very still in doorways as if checking whether the world was still there. But his hands remembered bread. His hands had never forgotten anything.

"Out of my kitchen," he said without turning around, because he could tell it was her by her footsteps, and also because saying it was a ritual between them, like a handshake.

"It's my kitchen. My name's on the deed."

"Your name is on *four* deeds. Mine is on the oven. Out."

Petra stole a heel of crust anyway, because rituals required observance, and leaned against the flour-dusted counter to watch him work. He'd gotten good. Better than good — there was talk of a bakery, of a second oven, of apprentices. Tam waved the talk away whenever it started, but Petra saw how he stood a little straighter when the market mothers called him Master Kavanagh.

He caught her watching and gave her the look — the one that said *you're doing the thing where you catalog my health like a ledger again.*

"I'm allowed," she said. "I climbed a mountain for you."

"You climbed a mountain for *my soul*. The body was incidental."

"The body is not incidental." She said it lightly, but it landed heavier than she meant it to, and they both went quiet for a moment, the way they always did near the edges of that particular conversation. Tam set down his knife. Flour dusted his forearms to the elbow; there was a smear of it on his jaw like a bad shave.

"Petra." He wiped his hands slowly on his apron. "You've got the face on."

"What face?"

"The face you make when you're rehearsing something. You've had it for a month. You make it when you think I'm looking at the bread." He turned to face her fully. "So. Rehearse it at me."

Petra looked at the oven, at the dough rising under its cloth, at the window where evening was beginning to gather blue. She had faced down an ancient starving king and lied to his face with a laced broth. She had drunk poison on purpose more times than she could count. And yet this — this small domestic courage — had taken her a month of rehearsals.

"When I was coming down the Stair of Teeth," she said, "paying memories at every threshold, there was one I refused to pay. No matter what it cost me. No matter what tolls it added to the climb back." She turned the cup of wine in her hands. "It was the memory of why I came. Not the fact of it — the *why*. The night you pulled me out of the harbor when we were eleven. The taste of the fever-tea you made me when I was fourteen and stupid and poisoned myself trying to prove I could handle hemlock. Every version of you saving me, and me deciding, somewhere in there, that I would spend my whole life returning the favor if I had to." She looked up at him. "There was never a version of the world where I didn't come for you. That's the memory I wouldn't pay. That's the one I kept."

Tam was very still. In the doorway behind him, unnoticed by either of them, Sefa had appeared with a platter and then quietly retreated, wearing the expression of a woman who intended to tell absolutely everyone.

"I know," Tam said at last, and his voice was rough. "Vess told me. After. She said the mountain tried to take it four times and couldn't, because—" he stopped, laughed wetly, "—because it wasn't a memory, it was a foundation. You can't toll away a load-bearing wall."

"So you've known for months."

"I've known for years, Petra. Since before the mountain." He crossed the kitchen and took her hands, flour and all. "I was just waiting for you to catch up. You're very brave about kings and very cowardly about kitchens."

"That's slander."

"That's *observation*." And he kissed her, tasting of yeast and salt, and it was not dramatic, and it was not a climax, and it was not the end of anything. It was a beginning that had been waiting patiently, like bread, like morning, like a star deciding when to come out.

From the doorway: "*Finally*," said Sefa, to no one, to everyone, and fled before they could throw flour at her.

* * *

The anniversary feast spilled out of the house and into the street, because it always did. Tables dragged out from three neighbors' homes. Brenna's charms strung overhead like festival bunting. The goat, roasted by Sefa, pronounced by Sefa to be perfect, argued about by everyone else with great love and no agreement. Old Warden Hask came by off-duty and brought his own chair. The widow from the tasting circle brought a pie and demanded, loudly, to know which of the young people had stolen her line about door hinges for the lesson book.

And Vess came, as she always came, late on purpose, slipping into the gathering like a shadow that had decided to be friendly. She wore gray still — she would wear gray forever, Petra suspected, the uniform of a woman who had served a hungry king for forty years and never quite stopped dressing for the post — but she smiled now, occasionally, in small careful increments, like someone relearning a language.

She settled beside Petra with her cup and surveyed the street: the noise, the children, Odo attempting to identify the goat's marinade by smell alone and getting two herbs out of five.

"You built all this," Vess said.

"The market built it. I just stopped hiding."

"Mm." Vess drank. "I served that court for forty years, and I never once believed it could end. Hunger that old feels eternal. You don't fight eternity. You just try to be useful to it so it eats you last." Her hollow eyes had filled in, over the months, with something almost like light. "And then a poison-drinker walks into the king's hall and turns out to be indigestible, and eternity chokes on her."

"I had help."

"You had a knife you barely used and a family that broke a sealed gate with smuggled charms and a girl who gave her voice to a ward-toll and got it back out of sheer stubbornness." Vess counted them off on her fingers. "Yes. Enormous help. Practically a delegation."

Petra laughed and clinked her cup against Vess's. Salted honey wine — sweet underneath, bright with salt, the flavor she had chosen for toasts because it was the flavor of the whole ridiculous story: grief and sugar in the same swallow.

The night deepened. The fire burned low and gold. Someone started the market's street-song, the one Tam's soul-fragment had hummed to her from inside a jar, and the whole street picked it up, ragged and warm and off-key in forty different directions. Pip sang with her eyes closed. Tam sang with flour still on his jaw. Sefa harmonized aggressively and incorrectly.

And then, near midnight, the singing faltered.

Not all at once. It thinned out voice by voice, the way rain stops, until the only sound was the fire and the distant sea and everyone looking up.

Above the shoulder of the mountain, where the sky had been empty for a year and more — empty the way a socket is empty, empty the way a table is empty where someone used to sit — a point of light kindled.

Small. White-gold. Trembling slightly, like a candle flame deciding whether to commit.

Nobody moved. Nobody breathed. Petra felt Tam's hand find hers and grip it hard enough to hurt, and she gripped back, and together they watched the first new star burn its uncertain hole in the dark.

"Vess," Petra whispered. "Is that—"

"I don't know." Vess's voice was unsteady, and Petra realized she was crying, silently, the way people cry who haven't done it in decades. "Maybe it's her. Maybe it's the mountain. Maybe it's every soul that poured out of that place, finally high enough to shine." She wiped her face with the heel of her hand, briskly, as if the tears were an inconvenience. "Or maybe hunger, once it's broken, learns generosity. I've seen stranger things. I *was* stranger things."

The star steadied. Held. Burned.

Around the fire, the singing started again — softly at first, then building, the street-song swelling up into the night like an offering, and Pip's voice rang clear over all of them, and Odo was crying without knowing it, and old Warden Hask raised his cup in his scarred fist.

Petra stood. She lifted her cup of salted honey wine to the sky, to the small defiant light above the mountain, and the street fell quiet enough to hear the sea.

"To the ones who paid," she said, "and the ones who stayed."

"*And the ones who came back*," Tam added, standing beside her, and the street roared the amendment, and drank.

Later — much later, when the fire was embers and the guests had gone home and the star still hung above the mountain like a promise kept — Petra stood alone in the doorway of the warm kitchen, cup in hand, watching it. Tomorrow there would be lessons to plan and a wedding, eventually, to argue about, and a city learning to live without fear, and a lesson book that needed the widow's door-hinge line written properly in the margin. Tomorrow was already knocking.

But tonight she watched the star, and the star watched her back, and somewhere beneath the mountain — or perhaps above it, or perhaps simply everywhere now, loose in the world at last — something that had been hungry for a very long time was learning, slowly, painfully, wonderfully, how to be full.

She raised her cup one last time.

The star, very faintly, seemed to wink.

"Show-off," Petra said fondly, and went inside, where the bread was rising and the light was on and the door, for the first time in her life, had never once been locked against her.
