## 1. The Ledger Room

The candle had to be tallow, never wax. Wax burned too clean, and clean light lied about color, and in the Vellum Guild's cellar archives a lie about color could cost someone their name.

Hazel Pemberton had learned that in her first week, from a woman named Odile whose position Hazel now occupied and whose fate she tried very hard not to think about. *Tallow for the shelves, storm-light for the pages,* Odile had said, tapping the little iron lantern at Hazel's belt with one yellowed fingernail. *And never the two together, girl, unless you want your inventory writing memoirs.*

Four years later, Hazel sat at the long cataloging table beneath the Guild's counting house and did precisely what Odile had forbidden, because there was no other way to do the work.

Before her, on a square of black velvet, sat a glass jar the size of a fist.

Inside it: a summer afternoon.

Not a description of one. Not a pressed flower or a ticket stub or any of the ordinary reliquary nonsense the wealthy liked to call memories. The actual thing — amber light gone thick as honey, the smell of cut grass and river water and someone frying onions two houses down, the sound of a wooden gate swinging on its hinge, over and over, patient as breathing. A child was laughing somewhere in it. The child was never visible; the memory belonged to whoever had sold it, and they had been behind their own eyes the whole time, watching a friend they would now never be able to pick out of a crowd.

The jar hummed faintly against the velvet. It wanted to be opened. They always did.

"Acquisition number eleven-forty," Hazel said aloud, because saying things aloud kept them true, "one childhood, partial, summer-weight. Provenance: the seller's own. Condition: intact."

She dipped her pen into the lantern.

The ink inside was not ink. It was captured lightning — storm-light, bottled by methods the Guild pretended were trade secrets and were actually just cruelty performed slowly — and when Hazel wrote with it, what she wrote *stayed*. That was the whole of her value here, though no one said so plainly. Ordinary ink faded. Ordinary ink could be argued with. Storm-light remembered exactly, and so did whatever page it touched, and so did the hand that guided it, which was why the Guild had hired her four years ago with suspicious speed and suspicious warmth, and why she had accepted with suspicious desperation, and why neither party had ever mentioned the town of Corvale or the storm of the ninth of Harvestmoon or the word *exile*, except on the single document Hazel had signed with a hand that shook only once.

*Entry:* she wrote, and the letters glowed pale blue-white on the ledger paper, cool as moonrise. *One childhood, aged approximately seven years. Contents include: a garden gate, a dog unnamed in the record, a game called bridges played across a stream, an older sister's voice singing off-key. Seller retains all rights of grief. Buyer has paid in full.*

She paused over that last clause. *Seller retains all rights of grief.*

It was a standard line. It was in every contract. It meant the man who had walked into the Guild's front office three days ago — a broad, sunburnt farmer type with river mud still on his boots, who had set the jar on the counter like a man setting down something far too heavy to carry another mile — could remember that he'd had a sister who sang, but not how she sang, and not the game called bridges, and not his own laughter at seven years old, thin and bright through a garden gate.

He could keep being sad about it, though. The Guild was generous that way.

Hazel finished the entry, blotted nothing — storm-light needed no blotting — and reached for the shelf-tag. She was halfway through lettering it when the jar turned itself slightly on the velvet.

She stopped.

Jars did not turn themselves. Jars settled, sometimes, if the memory inside was agitated — fresh acquisitions often were, all that new absence sloshing around — but this was not settling. This was deliberate. The jar rotated until its label faced her, and inside the amber, the summer shifted. The garden gate came forward. The off-key singing swelled, and beneath it, faintly, the child's laugh changed pitch — dropped lower, steadied, became almost —

Almost familiar.

"No," Hazel told it, in the flat tone she used for jars and clients and herself at three in the morning. "You don't get to do that."

The singing continued. It was not the seller's sister anymore. It was — and Hazel's pen-hand went cold, colder than the cellar, colder than the night four years ago with the rain coming down in sheets and the tribunal's faces lit orange by torchlight — it was a tune from Corvale. A harvest song. Every child in her village knew it; every child had sung it badly, off-key, on gates and fences and the backs of carts. *Roll the barrels, roll the barrels, the sky is full of salt—*

The jar was showing her a Corvalan childhood. And the provenance slip said the seller had come from upriver, from the fen country, three hundred miles east.

Hazel set down the pen. Her hands were steady. She noticed them being steady the way you notice a soldier standing at attention during a funeral — with respect, and with the knowledge that it couldn't last.

"Provenance error," she said quietly, to the cellar, to the ledgers ranked on their shelves into the dark like sleeping animals. "Possible provenance error."

Because there was another possibility, and she was not going to say it aloud, and the cellar was going to have to live with that.

* * *

The thing about exile — the thing nobody warned you, because nobody who wrote about exile had actually been exiled — was not the leaving. The leaving was almost easy. Guards, a cart, a border post, a paper shoved at you with instructions to sign. You signed. Your hand shook once. Done.

The thing was afterward. The thing was discovering that exile was not a place. It was an arithmetic. You became a problem of subtraction performed on other people: subtract the name, and what remained? Subtract the country. Subtract the family, who had been permitted — encouraged — to forget you had ever stood at their table. Subtract the friends who looked through you at the market in the last town before the border, not from malice but from genuine inability, their eyes sliding off you like rain off oiled canvas.

What remained was a clerk. What remained was a woman alone in a cellar with a candle and a lantern of lightning, cataloging stolen childhoods for the institution that had, indirectly, arranged the theft of hers.

Hazel had done the sum many times, usually lying awake in the narrow room the Guild rented her above the chandlery. The answer always came out the same: what remained was not enough. But it was what there was, and so she kept it immaculate. Her ledgers were famous in the Guild — Oren Vey himself had said, at last year's audit, that Pemberton's columns were the closest thing the archive had to a conscience. He'd meant it as praise. Hazel had received it as an indictment and bowed anyway.

Because here was what she had learned about precision, in four years of counting other people's losses: precision was a way of grieving without spilling. If every entry was exact, if every condition noted and every provenance traced and every clause transcribed without error, then the grief stayed where it belonged — inside the lines, contained, legible, filed. Grief that was cataloged could not drown you. Grief that was cataloged was just *inventory*.

She was very good at inventory.

She picked the jar up now — carefully, by the neck, the way Odile's manual instructed, as though it were a bird — and held it to the tallow flame. Inside, the harvest song had quieted. The summer light lay still, embarrassed, the way a room goes quiet after a raised voice.

"You're misfiled," she told it softly. "Or I am."

The amber didn't answer. It never answered. That was the difference between the jars and the ledgers, and it was the only comfort the cellar offered: the jars forgot on command, but the pages remembered whether you wanted them to or not.

She entered the discrepancy in the margin — small, precise, deniable — and shelved the jar between a vial of borrowed courage (three days' worth, secondhand) and a stoppered bottle of a widow's relief, which weighed more than any bottle that size should, because relief is heavy; anyone who tells you otherwise has never carried it.

Then she climbed the stairs to sleep, and did not.

* * *

Morning in the counting house arrived the way it always did: with money changing hands upstairs and truth changing hands below.

Hazel was at the cataloging table before the seventh bell, because the cellar belonged to her in the hours before the runners came down with the day's acquisitions, and because sleep had become a country she held citizenship in but rarely visited. She had the day's intake half-sorted when the door at the top of the stairs opened, and Oren Vey's voice came down ahead of him, pitched to carry, the way he pitched everything.

"Pemberton. Client. Front of house. Bring folio eleven-forty and your best face."

Her best face was the one that suggested she felt nothing, and she put it on while she climbed, folio under her arm, the ledger's storm-light entries glowing faintly through the leather even in daylight. Upstairs, the counting house was doing what it did — clerks at high desks, clients at low ones, the great brass scales in the window weighing intangibles against coin with a soft, continuous clicking, like insects eating something.

The client stood by the counter. Hazel knew him at once, and then wished she didn't.

He was the farmer. Three days had roughened him — several days' growth of beard, eyes red-rimmed, the same boots with the same river mud — and he was gripping the counter's edge the way drowning men grip flotsam. When he saw the folio under her arm he made a sound that was not quite a word.

"Master Aldous Fen," Oren Vey said smoothly, from behind the counter, "this is our senior archivist. She'll explain the Guild's position." He stepped back with the fluid courtesy of a man delegating something unpleasant, which was, Hazel thought, the most honest motion she had ever seen him make.

"Master Fen." She set the folio down. "How can the Guild serve you?"

"You know how." His voice cracked in the middle and he pushed through it. "I want it back. The jar. The — my sister. I want—" He stopped, breathed, started again with the desperate formality of a man who had rehearsed. "I wish to purchase the return of acquisition eleven-forty. I'm prepared to pay double. Triple. I've brought the deed to the orchard—"

"The item was sold in good faith," Hazel said. Each word placed like a stone in a wall. "Payment was rendered in full."

"It was *my* childhood."

"Yes."

"That's — you can't sell a person's *childhood*, that's not — it isn't *property*—"

"No," Hazel agreed, and hated the smoothness of her own voice, and continued anyway, because guild law forbade refunds and guild law forbade pity and guild law, above all, forbade the clerk from agreeing with the client where the client could hear it. "It is inventory. As of three days past, it belongs to the buyer. The buyer is the Guild's client, and his identity and purposes are sealed under the same law that seals your grief."

"I don't even remember why I'm sad," Master Fen said, and this was the terrible part, this was the part that got under the walls. He wasn't angry anymore. He had passed through anger on the way here and come out the other side into something worse. "That's the joke of it. I rode three days to buy back something I can't prove I lost. My wife says I've been strange since the fair. My wife doesn't remember my sister either — nobody does, I checked the parish rolls, there's no record of her, it's as if — " His hands left the counter and hung useless at his sides. "As if she was never born. Except I wake up crying, some mornings. Crying, and I don't know for who."

The counting house clicked on around them. Somewhere upstairs, coins. Somewhere deeper down, in the dark, a jar humming a Corvalan harvest song to itself.

Hazel looked at the folio. Her own handwriting glowed up through the page, neat and merciless. *Seller retains all rights of grief.* She had written those words four days ago in a steady hand, and she had thought, even then, somewhere below the level where thoughts are allowed to speak, *this is the cruelest sentence I know how to write,* and she had written it perfectly anyway, because perfection was the only thing standing between her and the rain, the torchlight, the tribunal, the word *exile* spoken in her mother's voice—

No. Not her mother's voice. Her mother hadn't been there. Her mother had been dead two years by then. It had been the magistrate's voice. Why had she thought—

"Pemberton," Oren Vey said, quietly, at her shoulder, and his hand touched her elbow, and it was not a kind hand.

"Guild law forbids refunds," Hazel said. "I'm sorry, Master Fen. The sale stands."

Something went out of the farmer's face. Not hope — hope had already gone — but the last scaffolding around the space where hope had been. He nodded, twice, a man accepting a verdict. He took the deed to the orchard off the counter, looked at it as if unsure what it was, and put it away. Then he straightened his shoulders with an effort that cost him visibly, and he said:

"They said you'd say that."

"Who said?"

"The people upriver." He was looking at her now, really looking, past the clerk and the folio and the best face, and his red-rimmed eyes had gone strangely clear. "The ones who told me where to sell it. They said, go to the Vellum Guild in Marrowgate, ask for the archivist with the glowing ledgers, and when she refuses you — and she will refuse you — ask her this." He swallowed. "Ask her what the sky of Corvale sold for."

The counting house did not stop. The scales kept clicking. The clerks kept scratching. Only Hazel stood absolutely still, the way ground stands still when it has decided to become the edge of a cliff.

Oren Vey's hand left her elbow.

"Master Fen," the Guildmaster said pleasantly, "I think we're finished here."

But Hazel wasn't looking at Oren. She was looking at the farmer, and the farmer was looking at her, and between them, unspoken, unbuyable, unsellable, lay a single shared fact: that upriver, in the fen country where no Corvalan should have any business, someone had known both halves of a transaction that should never have existed — the jar, and the name.

"What did you say your sister's song was?" Hazel asked him.

His mouth trembled. "I don't remember it," he said. "That's the whole point. I don't remember anything."

Down in the cellar, three floors of stone below them, a jar turned slowly on black velvet, and waited to be asked.
