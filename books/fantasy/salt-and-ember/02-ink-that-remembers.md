## 2. Ink That Remembers

The client arrived at the Vellum Guild on a Tuesday, in the hour between the second bell and the third, when the light through the archive windows goes the color of weak tea and even the dust seems to move more slowly.

Hazel Pemberton saw him first through the grille of the receiving window: a man of perhaps fifty, dressed in the good wool of a prosperous merchant but worn badly, as though he had slept in it for several nights running. His hands were folded on the counter with the deliberate stillness of someone who had practiced not wringing them. She had catalogued enough grief to recognize its posture from across a room.

"Name for the ledger?" she asked, pen already poised.

He flinched at the word *ledger*, which told her he had been here before. "Corwin Ashe," he said. "I — you have something of mine."

"We don't deal in *mine*," Hazel said, keeping her voice level, because guild law required the phrase and because she had learned that softness at this stage only made the cruelty arrive later, heavier. "We deal in custody. What was the acquisition?"

"A memory." His voice cracked on the second syllable and he steadied it with visible effort. "My daughter's voice. The last time I heard it — she was seven, she was singing to the cat, and I —" He stopped. Started again, quieter. "It was sold to your Guild eleven years ago. By me. And I've come to buy it back."

Hazel did not write anything yet. This was also procedure: never let a client see how quickly the answer comes, because hope is a currency too, and the Guild devalued it daily.

She knew the entry before she looked it up. Not his specifically — there were four hundred entries in the cellar archives involving children's voices, and she had catalogued most of them herself in her three years below ground — but the shape of it. Grief sold at market rate. A widower, usually. A man who could no longer walk past the room where the singing had happened, who had heard that the Vellum Guild would take the memory out of him like a splinter and pay him enough to leave the city entirely.

They always came back. That was the part the Guild's brochures did not mention, because the Guild did not print brochures; it printed contracts, and contracts were honest in the way knives are honest.

"Let me see the folio," Hazel said, and went down into the cellar archives while he waited above, hands folded, practicing his stillness.

* * *

The cellar archives of the Vellum Guild occupied three levels beneath the counting-house, and Hazel had spent three years learning their geography the way other people learned scripture. Shelf by shelf, jar by jar, folio by folio: bottled grief in blue glass that fogged when you walked past it too fast; borrowed years in long brass canisters, each one ticking faintly against its neighbors like restless insects; names not yet earned, kept in lead boxes because they were dangerous before they were true. And memories — the commonest stock, the bread and salt of the trade — stored in vials of smoked crystal, each labeled in the clerk's hand with the seller's name, the date of sale, and a catalogue number that meant nothing to anyone except the person who had once owned what it contained.

Hers was the hand on most of the labels now. Old Master Quillon had trained her for two years before his retirement, and then it had been hers alone, down here among the impossible inventory, because no one else wanted the work. The other clerks called the cellar *the cold office*. Hazel had come to think of it as the only warm room in the building, though she would never have said so aloud. Down here, everything was exactly what it claimed to be. Upstairs, in the world of ledgers and guild law and Master Oren Vey's careful smile, nothing was.

She found Corwin Ashe's entry in the eleventh-year register. *Ashe, C. One (1) memory, auditory, singular: minor child singing, domestic interior. Sold under standard grief-rate contract. Purchased by:* — and here Hazel paused, because the purchaser line was unusual — *purchased by the Guild, retained in permanent collection.*

Not resold. Not brokered to some collector in the Ember territories or some grieving dowager upriver. Retained. Eleven years in the vaults, unsold.

That was strange. Hazel made a small note of it in the margin of her mind, the way she made notes of everything, and carried the vial upstairs.

* * *

"It's here," she said, setting it on the counter between them. The smoked crystal caught the tea-colored light and held it, and inside — faintly, the way a coal holds fire — something moved. If you tilted your head you could almost hear it: a thread of melody, muffled by glass and years.

Corwin Ashe made a sound that Hazel had heard maybe a dozen times in her career and had never once gotten used to. It was not crying. It was the sound a person makes when the thing they lost turns out to be real, and close, and not theirs anymore.

"How much," he said. "To buy it back. Name any figure. I've brought—" He reached into his coat and produced a purse that clinked with the heavy, honest weight of silver, and set it on the counter, and pushed it toward her, and his hands were shaking now, all that practiced stillness gone to water.

Hazel looked at the purse. Then at the vial. Then at him.

"Guild law forbids refunds," she said.

"I'm not asking for a refund. I'm offering to purchase—"

"The sale was final, Master Ashe. It says so in your contract. Clause nine." She recited it without looking it up; she knew clause nine the way she knew her own name, having written it into a hundred contracts. "*All acquisitions are perpetual and irrevocable. The Guild does not repurchase, refund, return, or lend. What is sold to the Vault is sold to history.*"

"But it's mine." His voice rose, and the two junior clerks at the far desks pretended very hard to be copying. "It's my memory. It happened inside my own head. You can't tell me a piece of my own life belongs to history and not to me."

"You signed the paper that says otherwise."

"I signed the paper because my wife was dead six months and every time I closed my eyes I heard that song and I wanted to die, and a man in a gray coat told me the Guild could help." He leaned across the counter, close enough that Hazel could smell the road on him, the sleepless nights, the desperation gone stale. "Clerk. Clerk Pemberton — I can see your nameplate. Please. I am not asking you to break your laws. I am asking you to find me a loophole, an exception, a fee — I will pay the fee, whatever it is, I will sign whatever—"

"There is no exception." Hazel heard her own voice go flat and precise, the clerk's voice, the voice she put on like armor. "There is no fee. There is no loophole. If there were, do you think I'd be permitted to tell you?"

Something went out of his face then. Not hope — hope had already gone — but the thing after hope, the thing that keeps a man standing upright. He looked at the vial for a long moment. Inside it, faint and terrible, the melody turned over in its sleep.

"Can I hear it," he said. "Just once. Through the glass. You don't have to open it. Just — hold it near. I'll stand right here. I won't ask for anything else."

And here is what Hazel Pemberton did not do: she did not say yes, and she did not say no. She stood behind her counter with her pen in her hand and her guild law in her mouth, and she said, "If you'll wait a moment, Master Ashe, I need to verify the condition of the item," which was technically true, because verifying condition was part of her duties, and she lifted the vial, and she held it — briefly, professionally, at the regulation distance of one hand-span from the counter grille — and through the smoked crystal, thin as a hair, a child's voice began to sing.

It was a nonsense song. A made-up song, the kind children invent about cats and rain and nothing at all, tuneless and looping and utterly certain of itself. It lasted perhaps eight seconds before Hazel set the vial down, because her hands were no longer steady either, and a clerk whose hands shake over the inventory is a clerk who should not be holding the inventory.

Corwin Ashe did not make the sound again. He had gone somewhere past sound. He stood there with both hands flat on the counter as if the counter were the edge of a boat, and when he finally spoke his voice was almost gentle, which was worse than shouting.

"Thank you," he said. "You didn't have to do that."

"Yes, I did," Hazel said. "Condition verification. It's in the manual."

They both knew that was a lie. Neither of them said so.

He took back his purse — slowly, like a man gathering up the pieces of a broken thing — and stood there a moment longer, and asked the question that Hazel would carry for the rest of this story, though she did not know it yet:

"Why? Why does the Guild keep them? If it doesn't sell them, if it doesn't use them — why keep a man's daughter singing in a jar for eleven years?"

Hazel gave him the official answer. "The Guild preserves. Preservation is the whole of our purpose. Some acquisitions appreciate; some simply must not be lost."

"And who decides which is which?"

"The Guildmaster."

He nodded, slowly, the way men nod when they have received an answer that is not an answer, and he thanked her again in that same terrible gentle voice, and he left, and the door closed behind him with the small polite click of a well-made thing doing its job perfectly.

* * *

That night Hazel stayed late in the cellar archives, which was not unusual; what was unusual was what she did there, which was nothing. She sat at her cataloguing desk with the eleventh-year register open in front of her and did not write in it.

*Retained in permanent collection.* She read the entry again. Then she pulled the surrounding registers — tenth year, twelfth year, ninth — and began, methodically, the way she did everything, to count.

Memories retained rather than resold: one hundred and forty. One hundred and sixty-two. Two hundred and six, and rising year over year, and she sat back and looked at the numbers and felt the first cold thread of a question she had never thought to ask, because clerks do not ask questions; clerks record answers.

Why was the Guild hoarding grief?

She thought of Master Oren Vey's smile, which was a good smile, a practiced smile, the smile of a man who had shaken her hand three years ago and said, *Welcome to the Vellum Guild, Miss Pemberton. We keep what others cannot afford to lose.* She had been twenty-two and freshly exiled from Corvale, convicted of calling a storm she swore to this day she had never called, stripped of her standing, her name struck from the town rolls of her own home village — and the Guild had taken her in when no one else would touch a storm-caller, and she had loved it for that. She had loved it the way you love the roof that keeps off the rain, without ever asking what the roof is made of, or who paid for it.

*We keep what others cannot afford to lose.*

Or, she thought now, staring at the registers, we keep what others cannot afford to have back.

Because that was the truth underneath clause nine, wasn't it? Not preservation. Leverage. Every memory in the vaults belonged to someone, and everyone who had sold one was diminished by exactly that much — a little less able to fight, a little less able to grieve properly, a little less able to remember why they might want to fight at all. A city full of people missing pieces of themselves, and the pieces all in one cellar, and the cellar all in one man's keeping.

Hazel picked up her pen. She opened the current day-book to Corwin Ashe's visit and wrote, in her precise clerk's hand: *Client inquired re: repurchase. Refused per clause nine. Item verified. No irregularities.*

Then she sat for a long time looking at the words *no irregularities*, and her pen hovered, and hovered, and in the margin — small, in the storm-light ink that only she used, the ink that glowed faintly grey-green like the underside of a cloud before lightning — she wrote something she did not show anyone, something that was not an official record at all:

*Irregularity: the client was right.*

The ink glowed on the page, patient as weather. Storm-light remembered things. That was its nature, the reason the Guild tolerated her using it for the master copies, the reason old Quillon had taught it to her with such strange reverence: ordinary ink recorded what you wrote; storm-light recorded what was true, whether you wrote it or not. She had never fully understood what that meant until tonight.

She blew out her lamp and sat in the dark, and the single line of marginalia glowed back at her like a distant flicker over hills.

Above her, three floors up, Master Oren Vey was awake too. She did not know that yet. She did not know that he kept a private ledger of his own, or that her name had been entered in it the moment Corwin Ashe spoke the word *Pemberton* at the receiving grille — or that, two floors below the cellar, in a vault she had never been permitted to open, a folio older than the Guild itself lay waiting with a signature on its first page that she would know anywhere, because she had inherited her handwriting from somewhere.

But the stars, that night, were still burning. The wards still held. There were nine days' worth of ordinary cruelties left to catalogue before the sky itself became an entry in a ledger.

Hazel capped her ink, and went home through the sleeping streets, and did not sleep.
