## 3. The Night Without Stars

The audit of the eastern vaults was scheduled to end at midnight, and Hazel Pemberton intended to be finished by half past eleven.

She worked by lamplight in the third cellar of the Vellum Guild, where the air tasted of dust and tallow and the faint copper tang of bottled weather. Shelves rose around her like the ribs of some enormous patient beast, and on those shelves sat the Guild's inventory: jars of borrowed laughter, stoppered grief in graduated strengths, a crate of unspent courage marked *fragile — do not shake*. Somewhere above her, clerks were counting coin. Down here, they counted things that had never been coins at all.

Hazel counted best.

She had been told this since she was a girl — that she had a head like a strongbox, that nothing rattled loose from it. It was the one compliment her exile had not managed to take from her. Seven years ago the Magistrates of Corvale had put her name on a writ and put her on a road, and the writ had said *storm-caller* the way other writs said *thief* or *murderer*, and she had walked out of her homeland with one trunk and a letter of introduction to the Guild, because the Guild did not care where you came from so long as your columns balanced.

They cared about very little else, if she was honest. But honesty was for journals, not ledgers, and Hazel kept both.

"Lot forty-one," she murmured, lifting the jar into the light.

It was labeled in the neat hand of an acquisition clerk long dead: *Childhood, early summer portion, donor unknown, approx. 300 days.* Inside the glass, something moved like weather seen through a window — green light, the suggestion of a dog running, a woman's voice too far off to hear words. The child it belonged to was grown now, or dead, or sold; the memory sat here instead, glowing faintly amber, doing no one any good.

Hazel recorded its condition. *Contents stable. Seal intact. Emotional residue: sweet, fading.* Her pen scratched, and the scratch was the only sound in the world, and she loved the sound the way other people loved bells.

This was the trick of surviving an exile, she had learned: you made yourself small and precise. You gave yourself tasks with edges. You did not look at the jar of someone's childhood and think of the orchard behind your mother's house, or the way rain had sounded on its roof the night the storm came, the night they said you called it. You wrote *contents stable* and you moved to lot forty-two.

She was halfway through lot forty-two — *Regret, vintage unspecified, heavy as wet wool* — when the lamp guttered.

Not went out. Guttered. The flame leaned hard to the west as though a door had opened somewhere far away, then righted itself, then leaned again. Hazel frowned at it. Cellar three had no drafts; Oren Vey himself had boasted of that when he'd assigned her the audit. *No drafts, no damp, no surprises,* he'd said. *The Guild's cellars are the most honest rooms in the world.*

The lamp leaned west again and stayed there, bent like a man listening.

Hazel set down her pen.

Above her, through three floors of stone and timber, she heard the Guild's great clock begin to strike midnight — and stop. One note only, a single bronze syllable cut off mid-voice, the way a singer is cut off by a hand at the throat. The silence afterward was worse than any sound. It had texture. It pressed against her ears.

"Lots forty-one through sixty," she said aloud, just to hear a voice, hers, ordinary, human. "Audit in progress. Interrupted by —"

By what? She had no word for it yet. She would spend the rest of her life looking for the word, though she didn't know that yet either.

She took the lamp and climbed.

* * *

The counting room on the ground floor was full of people standing very still.

That was the first wrongness. Clerks did not stand still; clerks moved the way water moves, always toward the next task, the next column, the next signature. But now two dozen of them stood among their desks with pens still in their hands, all facing the same wall — the south wall, where the Guild's master map of the known world hung in a frame of black walnut, six feet tall, updated quarterly by the cartographers' wing.

Hazel followed their gaze.

The map was fine work, inked on vellum cured by methods the Guild did not share, and it showed everything it was supposed to show: the Nine Coasts, the Amber Reaches, the mountain spine of the Kessrin ranges, the trade roads stitched in red. And in the place where Corvale had been — the western peninsula, the one shaped like a cupped hand, the one that had held her mother's house and her trial and her exile road and every year of her life before the Guild — there was nothing.

Not blank parchment. Nothing. The vellum itself had changed, the fibers rearranged, the surface smooth and unbroken where a coastline should have been, as if the map had never been asked to hold that country at all. The red trade road that ran from the Nine Coasts down through Corvale's ports simply ended now, fraying into a decorative flourish, as though it had always been a border ornament and nothing more.

"No," said old Master Quillon, the senior auditor, in a voice like a door coming off its hinges. "No, that's — I've signed that map. I signed the correction in spring. There was a correction. The river mouth shifted." He turned to the room, and his face was the face of a man demanding the world make sense to him. "Who has the spring corrections? Who has my signature?"

Nobody answered. Nobody had anything. Hazel watched them understand, one by one, in the order of their cleverness, that the map was not mistaken. Maps were never mistaken. That was the entire point of maps, the covenant between cartographer and world: *I will draw what is, and what is will agree to be drawn.*

Corvale had broken the agreement.

"The wards," someone whispered near the window. "Look at the wards."

Hazel pushed through the clerks to the tall south windows. Beyond the Guild's grounds, beyond the sleeping city of Vell's Landing, the border wards of the surrounding kingdoms were visible on clear nights as a faint lattice of pale light along the horizon — the great defensive workings, older than any living government, maintained by treaty and toll and the quiet labor of a thousand hedge-mages. Children grew up thinking the lights were stars that had come down low to keep watch.

The lights were going out.

Not all at once. In sequence, west to east, like lamps being snuffed by a careful hand moving along a corridor — each ward flaring bright as it failed, one last furious bloom, then dark. A bloom of light leapt from the western horizon, hung there a heartbeat, and died. Then the next. Then the next. Each flare lit the city below for an instant, streets and rooftops and the river, and each time the darkness after seemed deeper than the darkness before, as though the light were being taken out of the world rather than merely extinguished in it.

And then the stars.

Hazel heard the gasp go through the room behind her, but she was already looking up. The sky over Vell's Landing had been clear at dusk; she remembered it, because she always checked the sky, because she was a convicted storm-caller and checking the sky was a habit carved into her deeper than any scar. Clear dusk, a few high clouds, the winter constellations sharp as pinpricks in black silk.

Now, star by star, they were going out.

It was not cloud cover. Clouds blurred; clouds swallowed; clouds did not *select*. This was selection. The sky dimmed in patches, constellations winking out whole — first the low southern ones, then the great hunter that hung over the western sea, then more, faster, a spreading stain of absolute dark eating upward through the heavens. And in the patches where the stars vanished, there was no black such as night makes. Night-black still holds a hint of blue, a memory of day, the promise of morning. This was a black that held nothing. It was the color of the inside of a closed book. It was the color of a page that had never been written on and never would be.

"They're turning it off," Hazel said. She hadn't meant to speak. "Someone is turning the sky off."

Beside her, Master Quillon gripped the windowsill with both hands, and she saw that the old man was praying, silently, his lips moving through names of gods the Guild officially did not keep on inventory.

And underneath the terror, underneath the cold climbing her spine, something else stirred in Hazel Pemberton — something small and hot and shameful, and she knew what it was immediately, because she had spent seven years trying to kill it and knew its heartbeat anywhere.

Vindication.

Because the writ that had exiled her had said *storm-caller*, and she had sworn until her voice cracked that she had called no storm, and no one had believed her, and the worst part had never been the punishment. The worst part had been the certainty, hers alone, carried like a stone in the chest for seven years, that the truth existed nowhere outside her own skull. That she was the sole custodian of a fact the entire world had ruled irrelevant.

And now the world itself was being unwritten, and she alone in this room understood what unwriting felt like from the inside. She had lived seven years in the country of the erased. She recognized the borders.

*I know what this is,* she thought, and the thought terrified her more than the dying sky, because it was not a frightened thought. It was a competent one. It was the thought of a clerk confronting an error in the books, and some deep-buried part of her looked at the vanishing stars and the falling wards and the map with its smooth blank vellum and thought, with a clarity that felt almost like calm:

*There's a debt here. Someone has defaulted on something enormous.*

* * *

She went back down to cellar three because there was nowhere else in the world to go, and because the ledgers were there, and when the sky dies you go to whatever still glows.

The lamp burned steady again, which was its own kind of wrong — the flame upright and serene while the world outside came apart. Hazel stood among the shelves with her audit sheets forgotten in her hand and became aware, slowly, of a light that was not the lamp's.

Her ledger lay open on the table where she'd left it. The audit ledger, the one she kept in her own ink — the ink she ground herself, from the recipe her mother had taught her before the trials, before everything: lampblack and salt-water and one ingredient her mother had never named, some powder measured out under a covered sky, taken from a tin that had rusted shut years ago. Storm-light, the old women of her village had called such ink, though they'd said it as a warning, not a recipe. *Storm-light remembers what daylight agrees to forget.* Her mother had laughed when she said it. Her mother had laughed at a great many dangerous things.

Every page of the open ledger was glowing.

Softly — a pale silver-green luminescence, like moonlight trapped in paper, like the underside of a stormcloud at evening. Hazel crossed the room slowly, the way one approaches a sleeping animal, and looked down at her own handwriting, and the handwriting looked back. Lines she had written that afternoon — *lot fifty-three, contents stable, seal intact* — shone with a quiet insistence, and beneath the lines, in the margins, in spaces she had never written in, other marks were surfacing. Faint. Rising like fish toward the surface of a pond. Not words yet. Shapes. The ghost-pressure of writing that wanted to exist.

The ink was remembering.

She touched the page and the glow gathered under her fingertip, brightened, and for one vertiginous instant she saw — not with her eyes, she would insist later, not with her eyes — a coastline. A cupped hand of land against a grey sea. Rain on an orchard roof. A courtroom with its windows boarded against a storm that was still, even then, hours away.

Then it was gone, and the page was only a page, glowing gently, waiting.

Hazel stood in the honest cellar of the Vellum Guild, in the most truthful room in the world, and listened to the silence where the clock should have been striking, and understood three things with the terrible completeness of an audit closing.

First: Corvale was gone from the maps, and the wards were failing, and the stars were being put out like candles after a sitting — and none of it was weather, and none of it was accident. Erasure on this scale required contracts. Contracts required signatories. Somewhere there was paper with teeth in it, and the paper had just been fed.

Second: her ledgers were written in storm-light, and storm-light remembered, and the pages were still glowing — which meant that somewhere in these shelves, in these columns, in her own precise and unremarkable handwriting, Corvale might still exist. Every shipment logged from its ports. Every acquisition sourced from its villages. Seven years of entries in ink that refused to forget. If the world had lost the country, the books might not have.

Third — and this was the one that made her sit down slowly on the cellar stool, the one that made her laugh once, a short cracked sound with no humor in it —

Third: they had exiled the one clerk in the world whose ink could prove they were wrong. Or they had exiled her *because* her ink could. She did not yet know which, and the not-knowing sat in her stomach like a swallowed coal.

Above her, muffled by stone, she heard the front doors of the Guild open, and voices — many voices, urgent, and one voice rising over the rest, dry and carrying, the voice of Guildmaster Oren Vey calling the clerks together, calling for calm, calling for inventories.

Hazel picked up her pen. She drew the audit ledger toward her across the table, opened it to a fresh page, and in the glowing dark of cellar three, in storm-light ink that remembered what the sky had agreed to forget, she wrote the first entry of a record no one had asked her to keep:

*Night of the dark stars. Corvale struck from the master map at midnight. Wards failing west to east. Cause: unknown. Debt: outstanding.*

She hesitated, pen hovering. Then, beneath it, smaller:

*Auditor's note: I remember it anyway.*

The ink flared silver, and held.

Somewhere overhead, Oren Vey's voice stopped mid-sentence — and did not begin again.
