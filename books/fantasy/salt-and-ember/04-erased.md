## 4. Erased

The bells of the Vellum Guild rang the hour wrong.

Hazel noticed it before anything else — she was a clerk down to her marrow, and a clerk notices when time itself is misfiled. The dawn bell should have struck six. It struck four, paused as if embarrassed, then struck two more in a voice that didn't quite match the bronze. Somewhere above the cellar archives, the Guild's great clockwork was recalculating, the way a man recalculates after waking from a dream he can't quite hold.

She had not slept. Of course she had not slept. You did not sleep on the night the stars went out.

The ledger room below the counting house had no windows, which meant it had no proof of sky, which meant — for one merciful stretch of hours — Hazel could pretend the darkness outside was ordinary weather. She worked by lamplight and by the soft blue glow rising off her own open folio, storm-light ink remembering what the heavens had forgotten. Page by page, her entries from three nights ago still shone: *Audited 412 vials of imported sorrow; noted discrepancy in the autumn consignment of borrowed years; stars visible at close of business.*

*Stars visible.* The ink held them like flies in amber.

She had copied those entries eleven times since midnight. Not because the Guild required copies. Because copying was a thing hands knew how to do when the mind refused to sit still, and because some animal part of her understood that whatever was happening to the world, these pages were the only place it hadn't happened yet.

Then the door at the top of the archive stairs opened, and Guildmaster Oren Vey came down into the dark with a lantern he did not need, because his face was already the color of tallow, and Hazel understood that pretending was over.

"Hazel." He said her name carefully, the way you set down something breakable. "Come up to the map room. There's been a... development."

"A development," she repeated. "You mean besides the sky."

"Come up."

* * *

The map room occupied the Guild's highest floor, a long chamber where the world hung in strips of calfskin and vellum, forty years of surveys and purchases rendered in the Guild's proprietary inks — grief-black, year-gold, name-silver. Hazel had spent nine years of exile learning every inch of it. The maps were the Guild's true ledger; everything else was commentary.

Oren stood in the center of the room with his back to her, and Hazel saw immediately what was wrong, because everything was wrong, everywhere, all at once.

The eastern wall was blank.

Not empty — blank. Where the great survey of the northern kingdoms had hung, there was now a stretch of unmarked parchment, smooth and pale as an egg, as though no cartographer had ever touched it. The brass fittings that had held its corners were still screwed into the wall, holding up nothing. The hooks waited. The map was simply gone, and so was any record that it had ever been drawn: no title cartouche, no surveyor's seal, no ghost-line where ink had faded. The wall behind it showed no sun-shadow, no discoloration, nothing. Forty years of sunlight falling on that parchment, and the plaster remembered none of it.

"Where is the northern survey?" Hazel asked. Her voice came out level. Clerks learned that. "It was rehung last month. I signed the maintenance slip myself."

"What northern survey?" Oren said.

She laughed — one short syllable, involuntary. "Guildmaster—"

"No." He turned. He looked older than he had yesterday, and not in the way of a man who has aged overnight but in the way of a man whose age had been *edited*, lines smoothed and re-added wrong, a portrait restored by someone working from a description. "That's the point, Pemberton. That's the whole cursed point. There is no northern survey. There never was one. I have sent runners to the surveyors' hall. I have opened the master index. I have asked men who have walked those borders their entire lives." His hand rose, trembling, toward the blank wall. "There is no country there. There is nothing there. The land past the Greywater marches is open steppe, they say. Always has been. Empty as far as the trade roads run."

"The trade roads run through Corvale," Hazel said. "I grew up on one of them. My mother kept a toll-house on the Salt Road. There are forty thousand people in Corvale's capital alone, and the Guild holds liens on half of them, and I have personally catalogued—" She stopped, because Oren was shaking his head, slowly, and the shaking was worse than shouting would have been.

"I know," he said quietly. "I know you believe that. Gods help me, I believed it too, until about three hours ago. Then I went looking for corroboration, the way you'd want me to. And there isn't any. There is no treaty, no census, no coinage, no shipping manifest. Every document in this building that mentioned that country has gone blank or gone missing, and every person I ask remembers only that they never heard of it." He passed a hand over his face. "Except you."

The word landed in the room like a dropped weight.

"Except me," Hazel said.

"Except you." He crossed to his desk — a massive thing of ironwood, the Guildmaster's own, its surface crowded with the day's correspondence — and lifted a single sheet. "This arrived at dawn. From the Chancery of Records in the capital. Official notice." He read aloud, and his voice flattened itself into bureaucratic neutrality, which was how Hazel knew how much it cost him:

"*Be advised: prior references to the polity styled 'Corvale' constitute a cartographic error propagated through archival channels. No such polity exists or has existed. All associated records are voided. Personnel claiming association with said polity are to be regarded as suffering from documentary confusion and reassigned accordingly.*"

"Documentary confusion," Hazel repeated.

"That is the phrase."

"It's a very tidy phrase."

"It is a very expensive phrase," Oren said. "Do you understand what that means? Someone paid for that phrase. Errors are cheap. Erasure of this quality — this *thoroughness* — costs more than most kingdoms' annual revenue. Somebody bought the removal of an entire country from the world's memory, and they bought it cleanly enough that the Chancery doesn't even know it sold anything." He set the notice down. "And then there's the second matter."

He slid a second paper across the desk. Hazel took it. It was the Guild's personnel registry — the master roll of clerks, appraisers, and wardens, updated quarterly, each entry countersigned. She found the current quarter's page and ran her finger down the columns. Halloway. Ives. Ketterling. Marsh. Nettlebed.

No Pemberton.

She checked the previous quarter. Halloway through Nettlebed again, same neat column, same signatures. And the quarter before. Nine years of quarterly rolls, and in none of them — nowhere between Nettlebed and Oakeshott, where alphabetically she had always lived — was there any entry for a clerk named Hazel Pemberton.

"I've served here nine years," she said. Her voice sounded far away to her own ears, as if someone else were reading it aloud from a page. "You countersigned my appointment yourself. You docked my wages when I broke the Aldous jar. You promoted me over Ketterling two winters ago and he hasn't spoken to me since."

"I have no memory of any of that," Oren said. "And neither does Ketterling. And neither does the wage-book, which shows your salary line blank — not struck through, not deleted. *Blank*. As though the line were always empty." He gripped the edge of the desk. "But you're standing in my map room, Pemberton. You have keys to my cellar. You are owed nine years of back pay by a ledger that says you were never employed. Explain that to me. I am asking you sincerely. Explain it, because I have been at this desk since the bell rang wrong and I cannot."

Hazel looked down at her hands.

They were stained to the second knuckle with faint blue-grey light. They always were, these days; storm-ink soaked into the skin of anyone who worked with it long enough, a shimmer under the nails like drowned moonlight. It was why the Guild gave her the deep-archive work nobody else wanted. Ordinary inks forgot — that was their nature and their virtue. Grief faded. Years spent down. Names wore smooth with handling. But storm-light *kept*. It was lightning caught mid-thought, memory in its rawest form, and it did not consent to be erased by anything so small as an empire's accounting.

Nine years ago, exiled from Corvale for a storm she swore she'd never called, she had come to the Guild because it was the only institution in the world that dealt in things everyone else insisted didn't exist. She had thought that was irony. Now she understood it was training.

"The ink," she said. "Everything written in me — everything I've written — is in storm-light. The ledgers I keep remember. I think..." She turned her hands over, watched the glow pulse faintly with her heartbeat. "I think I'm written in it too. Somewhere. Deeper than paper. Whatever erased Corvale reached into every book, every mind, every record in the world — and it couldn't reach me. Or it reached me and I *held*."

Oren stared at her hands for a long moment. Then, being the man he was — a guildmaster first and a coward second, and even his cowardice had a certain professional thoroughness — he did the clerk's thing. He tested the hypothesis.

"Ketterling," he called toward the door. "Bring me the day-book. Yesterday's."

The old appraiser shuffled in, bleary, carrying the leather-bound daily register. Oren flipped it open to yesterday's date and set it before Hazel. *Routine audit, cellar three,* the entry began, in Ketterling's crabbed hand. *Assisted by—*

The line stopped. There was a gap in the sentence, a space exactly the length of a name, and around the gap the ink had gone strange — not blanked, but *nervous*, the letters crowding away from the empty space as if from a hole in ice.

"You wrote my name there yesterday," Hazel said. "Look at how the words lean. Ink doesn't lean unless something pushed it."

Ketterling peered at the page, and Hazel watched him watch it — watched him see the gap, watch understanding try to surface in his face like a body in a pond, watch it sink again. "Odd," he murmured. "Smudge, maybe." He wandered out, satisfied, and the door closed on him, and Hazel felt the isolation close another degree tighter. Nine years of shared meals, shared cellars, shared grievances against the wage-book — and the erasure had passed through him like wind through a window, taking only her.

"He'll forget this conversation by lunch," Oren said. It wasn't cruelty. It was diagnosis. "Whatever this is, it maintains itself. It's not a wound in the records; it's a *patrol*."

"Then we should assume it will come back for me."

"It should already have you." Oren lowered himself into his chair, and for the first time Hazel saw how afraid he was — not of the vanished country, but of her, or rather of what her continued existence implied: that the erasure had limits, and limits could be found, and found things could be broken. "You are a walking contradiction of the most expensive purchase in history, Pemberton. Do you grasp that? Somewhere, whoever did this has auditors of their own, and their books do not balance while you're alive and glowing. You're not just un-erased. You're a *discrepancy*. And I have spent forty years learning what happens to discrepancies."

"They get reconciled," Hazel said. "Or they get destroyed."

"Yes."

The lamp guttered. Neither of them moved to trim it. In the silence, Hazel became aware of the faint sound she'd been hearing since midnight without letting herself hear it: a whisper of light from the stairwell, from the cellar below, from her open folio in the archive room — her storm-light entries shining steadily on, patient as stars, keeping their record of a country that officially had never been.

Corvale lived in her handwriting. That was the whole of it. That was the terrible, absurd, magnificent whole of it. An empire had bought a nation out of the world's memory, and its entire surviving estate was a disgraced clerk's penmanship.

Something shifted in her chest — not warmth, nothing so kind. More like a keel settling, ballast finding its place. For nine years she had carried her exile like a verdict, a question mark stamped on her life: *what did you do, what did you do, what did you do?* She had assumed the punishment fit some crime she couldn't remember committing. But the Chancery's notice, the blanked rolls, the nervous ink — this wasn't the aftermath of a crime. This was *maintenance*. This was a cover-up with a budget. You did not erase a country to punish one woman for a storm. You erased a country so that no one would go looking for what it had been used to buy.

And you exiled the one witness who could have read the paperwork — sent her far from home, stripped her standing, made her testimony worthless — years before you needed to.

Unless the exile wasn't before. Unless the exile was part of it.

"Guildmaster," she said slowly, "when the Chancery's notice names 'personnel claiming association' — it means me specifically, doesn't it? A general edict wouldn't bother with personnel clauses."

Oren's eyes flicked to hers and away. "The notice has an annex," he admitted. "A schedule of affected persons. It lists one name."

"Mine."

"Pemberton, H. Formerly of—" He stopped. "Formerly of a place the notice declines to specify. Which is a curious piece of drafting, isn't it? They named *you* precisely and your home not at all. As if the country is incidental. As if you're the item of interest."

As if she had always been the item of interest.

Hazel looked at her glowing hands and felt the last of the frightened clerk fall away from her, like silt off a stone in fast water. What remained underneath was colder and steadier. She had spent nine years believing she had nothing left of Corvale but homesickness. Wrong. She was the archive now. She was the last copy, the sole extant edition, and copies of valuable things get hunted — but they also get *read*, and reading was the one craft she had never once failed at.

"I want access to the deep folios," she said. "The restricted vault. The oldest acquisitions — the founding contracts, the ones even you don't like opening."

Oren went very still. "Those predate the Guild's modern charter. Some of them predate *me*. Half are sealed by terms I've never been permitted to read."

"Then consider this an audit," Hazel said. "The Guild trades in impossible things, Guildmaster. Well. A country has just become impossible, and I am the only record of it that survives. Either that makes me the most important asset this building contains, or it makes me the most dangerous. You should decide quickly which, and you should decide *with* me rather than about me — because if whoever bought Corvale comes looking to reconcile their discrepancy, they will not distinguish between the discrepancy and the desk she's sitting at."

The lantern flame bent, though there was no draft. On the blank wall behind Oren, where the northern survey had hung for forty years, the pale parchment seemed almost to listen.

Oren Vey looked at his impossible clerk for a long moment — at the storm-light staining her hands like a signature she couldn't wash off — and Hazel watched forty years of institutional caution lose, clause by clause, to forty years of institutional instinct. The Guild survived by honoring debts. And somewhere in his marrow, beneath the edited memories and the tidy phrases, Oren knew he owed her nine years of back pay and one erased homeland.

"The vault key is in the third drawer," he said finally. "The iron one. Don't tell me what you find down there."

"Why not?"

"Because whatever it is," Oren said, turning back to the blank wall, "it's clearly been waiting a long time for someone who could read it. And I find I don't want to be able to testify."

* * *

The deep vault lay at the bottom of a spiral stair older than the building around it, behind a door of banded iron that took both of Hazel's hands and her whole weight to swing. Inside, the air tasted of cold wax and centuries. Folios stood in ranked cases, chained at the spines like dangerous animals — the Guild's founding acquisitions, the debts so old and so large that the Guild itself had arguably been built as their custodian.

Hazel carried her lamp down the central aisle, and as she passed, the storm-light in her skin answered the dark.

Faint blue radiance bloomed along the case edges, waking in the oldest bindings — a shimmer here, a pulse there, like fish turning under ice. Her ink recognized this place. Or this place recognized her ink. At the very end of the aisle, in the deepest case, one folio burned brighter than all the rest, its iron clasp glinting, and across its cover, in lettering that had no business surviving so many years but had survived anyway because it was written in the same light that lived in her hands, ran a title:

*Contract of Sky — Corvale — Original Instrument.*

Hazel stood before it, heart slamming, lamp trembling.

Her family name was embossed beneath the title.

She reached for the clasp.
