## 6. The Client Who Shouldn't Know

The bell above the Guild's front door rang at the hour when no honest client came — past midnight, in the wet dark after the lamps had been trimmed to embers. Hazel was alone with the night-ledger, as she always was, her pen moving in small precise strokes that left faint silver ghosts behind them where the storm-light dried.

She had learned to work without looking up. Clients who came at this hour wanted discretion, and discretion began with a clerk who seemed not to notice them until they spoke.

"I'm told," said a voice like dry paper turning, "that you can sell me a door out of a country that doesn't exist."

Hazel's pen stopped mid-stroke.

She did not move. She did not breathe. Because those were *her* words — the exact ones, in the exact order, the phrase she had whispered to herself three nights ago in the cellar archives, alone among the jars and folios, when she had pressed her palm flat against the map of Corvale and felt it give nothing back. She had said it to the dark: *I need a door out of a country that doesn't exist.* No one had heard. No one could have heard. The cellar walls were lined with silence-charms older than the Guild itself.

And yet here it was, handed back to her across the counter by a stranger in a rain-dark coat.

She looked up.

He was tall and narrow, built like something that had learned to fit through gaps. Grey eyes, grey-streaked hair cropped close, a face that seemed assembled for the purpose of being forgotten — unremarkable in every feature except the hands, which were wrong for a wanderer. They were auditor's hands. Ink-stained at the second knuckle of the right forefinger, calloused in the pattern of a man who had spent decades holding a ledger open with one hand while writing with the other. Hazel knew those hands. She saw them every morning in her own reflection.

"You're mistaken," she said, keeping her voice level. "The Guild sells many things. Doors are not among them."

"Then I've come to the right place anyway." He set something on the counter between them. A coin — no. Not a coin. A token of pressed vellum, stamped with the Guild's oldest seal, the one from before Oren Vey's grandfather's time: a quill crossed with a key. "I want to buy passage. Not across a border. Out of an erasure."

"The Guild doesn't trade in—"

"In impossible things," he finished softly. "Bottled grief. Borrowed years. Names not yet earned." His mouth curved, but there was no warmth in it. "Your own catalogue language, Clerk Pemberton."

Her blood went cold and stayed cold. Her name. Her *name* — which, since the stars went out, since Corvale slid off every map in the building, she had half-believed no one else in the world still carried.

"Who told you that name?" she asked.

"No one." He glanced around the empty receiving hall, at the shelves of sealed acquisitions, at the dark windows where the sky should have shown stars and showed only a smooth black nothing, like paint over glass. "That's rather the point. The name exists nowhere now except wherever you are. So I followed the glow."

"Glow?"

"Your ledgers." He leaned in slightly, and she caught his scent — woodsmoke and cold iron, the smell of a forge banked but not dead. "Storm-light remembers, Pemberton. When everything else forgot your country, your ink didn't. Do you know how rare that is? Do you know what it means?" His voice dropped. "It means you're the only witness left."

* * *

She should have called for the watch-clerk. She should have triggered the ward-bell, locked the counter, done any of the hundred things Guild procedure demanded for a client who knew too much. Instead she found herself walking him back through the stacks toward the reading room, telling herself it was containment — keep the anomaly where you can see it — and knowing, in the honest ledger-keeping part of her mind, that it was hunger. Three days of being the only person alive who remembered a nation. Three days of Oren Vey looking through her with polite bafflement, of colleagues asking why a stranger slept in the archive cot, of maps that showed ocean where her childhood stood. Three days of madness with no witness.

Now here was a man who said *Corvale* — no, he hadn't even said it yet, he'd said worse, he'd said *erasure*, he'd spoken the shape of the thing — and her whole body leaned toward him like a plant toward light.

The reading room held one lamp. She lit it, and its glow caught the folio already open on the table — she'd left it there, unable to stop returning to it. The oldest folio in the Guild's possession. The contract dated before her birth. Her mother's signature at the bottom, small and steady: *Maren Pemberton, on behalf of the sky of Corvale, in perpetuity.*

The stranger stopped three steps inside the room and went very still.

"That's it," he said.

"You don't know what 'it' is."

"Don't I?" He approached the table the way a man approaches a sleeping animal. "May I?"

"It's Guild property."

"It's a crime scene," he said, "four hundred years cold. But yes. May I."

She turned the folio so he could read without touching. He bent over it, and she watched his face as he read — watched the grey eyes track line by line, watched his lips move over the clause-language, and understood with a lurch that he wasn't reading it the way a client reads. He was reading it the way she read. Structurally. Looking for the load-bearing words.

"Clause nine," he murmured. "'Consideration rendered in full upon transfer of title.' Title of what? There's no antecedent. Clause eleven references Schedule C, and there is no Schedule C bound into this copy." He straightened. "This is a fair copy. Where's the original?"

"Why do you care about a four-hundred-year-old land contract?"

"Because it isn't a land contract." He looked up at her, and for the first time his forgettable face showed something raw underneath. "Because I've spent eleven years auditing contracts like it, and every single one ends the same way. The consideration is never paid to the seller. It's paid *through* them, to someone who doesn't exist yet. And then the seller's country stops appearing on maps."

The lamp flame leaned in the draft. Neither of them moved to right it.

"Say your name," Hazel said quietly.

"Ilex."

"Not the name you were given. The name you use."

A pause. Something flickered behind his eyes — pain, or respect, or both. "Ilex," he repeated. "That's all of it that's left. You understand, don't you? You of all people. When they erase a place, they erase the people in it retroactively. Not killed. *Unwritten.* Everyone who ever lived there becomes a person who never did. Except—" he nodded at the folio, at her, at the faint silver shimmer under her skin where ink-stains had sunk too deep to wash out, "—except witnesses anchored in remembering material. Storm-light. Which is why you're still here, sitting in a guildhall full of people who can't explain your presence, in a city that has forgotten it was ever a capital."

"It was never the capital," Hazel said automatically. "Corvale's capital was—"

She stopped. Reached for it. Found the shape of the knowledge there and then a hole where the name should be, smooth-sided, deliberate, like a word scooped out of a sentence.

Ilex watched her reach and fail, and something in his expression gentled. "They take the anchors first," he said. "Capitals. Kings' names. Mothers' faces, sometimes. Whatever would hurt most, whatever would lead fastest back to the contract. It's not cruelty. It's procedure." He said the word *procedure* with a hatred so old and worn it had gone smooth as river stone.

"How do you know all this?"

"Because I used to be the one holding the scoop."

* * *

He told her standing up, refusing the chair, as if sitting would make it comfortable and comfort would make it forgivable.

"I was an auditor. Not for your Guild — for the other side. For the buyers." He turned his right hand over, showed her the ink-stain at the knuckle. "Verification of erasure. That was my office. When a contract completed — when a sky changed hands, a season, a coastline, a history — my job was to confirm the erasure took cleanly. No residue. No witnesses. No maps, no songs, no graves with legible stones. I signed off on forty-one countries before yours."

Hazel's hand found the edge of the table. "Forty-one."

"Forty-one." He met her stare without flinching, which she suspected was the only honorable thing about him. "I told myself they were failed states, dying nations, places that had sold themselves willingly. And some were. But the contracts — I started pulling the originals, against protocol, because an auditor who doesn't read the whole document is just a stamp. And I found the pattern. The consideration clause. The collateral clauses. The way every contract named a child, or a clerk, or a witness, and arranged for that person to be exiled, discredited, or dead before they could ever read page one."

"Page one," Hazel whispered.

"Every contract hides its true terms in plain sight. First page. People skip first pages; they think the meat is deeper in. But the first page is where the buyer writes what they're actually buying." His voice dropped. "Do you know what your mother sold, Pemberton? Truly?"

"The sky. It says—"

"It says *the sky of Corvale, in perpetuity*. Read it again. What is a sky? Weather. Light. The stars over a nation. And what happens to a country whose sky belongs to someone else?" He let the question sit. "Its stars can be put out. Its borders can be unwritten, because borders are drawn against horizons and horizons are sky. Your mother didn't sell weather. She sold the *legibility* of a nation. Everything else followed from that."

The lamp guttered. In the dark beat before it steadied, Hazel saw the folio clearly — her mother's small steady signature — and hated it and loved it and could not tell which feeling was older.

"She was dying," Hazel said. "Wasn't she. That's the consideration. That's what she got."

Ilex said nothing, which was answer enough.

"And the collateral clause. The exile." Hazel's voice came out flat and strange. "There's a name on that clause. I haven't been able to read it — the ink won't resolve for me, like it's written in my own handwriting and my eyes refuse it. Is it mine?"

"Yes."

"Then whoever engineered my exile—"

"Read the contract's logic backward," Ilex said. "A witness anchored in storm-light, daughter of the signatory, positioned inside the largest archive of erased things in the world. If she ever reads page one, the contract is contestable. So the contract arranges for her to be removed. Publicly. Memorably. Convicted of a crime that makes her testimony worthless forever." His grey eyes held hers. "You were exiled for calling a storm, weren't you? The night the harbor broke?"

"I never called it." The old fury rose in her throat, familiar as a scar. "I have sworn it for six years. No one believed me. The evidence was—"

"Were you convicted on eyewitness testimony?"

"One witness. A woman at the seawall. She swore she saw me raise the wind with my own hands."

Ilex nodded slowly, and the nod was the most frightening thing he had done all night, because it was the nod of a man confirming a figure in a column he had already summed.

"Describe her."

"I never saw her face. Hood, rain, dark. She was—" Hazel stopped. Her mouth had gone dry. "She was my height. She moved like me. The magistrates said so. They said it was like watching myself."

"Stolen shape," Ilex said quietly. "It's a Court technique. Enforcers who wear borrowed faces, borrowed bodies, for exactly this purpose — to commit crimes in the witness's form, to poison testimony before it's given. I audited three erasures that used it." He paused. "I signed off on three erasures that used it."

Outside, the starless dark pressed against the windows like a hand. Somewhere in the Guild, a clock struck two, and the sound fell into the silence afterward and did not echo, as if even the architecture had grown afraid of repeating itself.

"So," Hazel said. She made her voice into a clerk's voice, precise, itemized, because if she didn't it would shake. "Let me record what we actually have. One: you are a defected auditor of the buyers, carrying eleven years of their methods in your head. Two: you have been hunting the original contract — the true first copy, with Schedule C attached — for long enough that you tracked a rumor of glowing ledgers to a guildhall in a country that officially never existed. Three: you believe page one names the true terms, and that if those terms are read into the record somewhere with authority, the contract can be contested." She looked at him. "Four: you need me. Because I'm the only ink in the world that remembers, and because my mother's signature makes me co-signatory by inheritance. Have I missed anything?"

"A fifth item," Ilex said. "Whoever wore your shape six years ago works for the same buyers. And if they went to the trouble of framing you once, they will do it again — permanently, this time — the moment they learn you've read anything at all." He reached into his coat and drew out a slim black notebook, worn soft at the corners, and set it beside the folio. "Which is why I'm not asking you to trust me. I'm asking you to audit me. Everything I know is in there. Every contract number, every buyer's mark, every date. Check my arithmetic. If I've lied anywhere, in eleven years of entries, find it."

Hazel looked at the notebook. Then at the folio with her mother's name. Then at the window, where the sky sat blank as an unwritten page.

"If I help you," she said, "I'm not a clerk anymore. Clerks are neutral. Clerks keep records for whoever pays."

"Yes," Ilex said. "That's rather the appeal, from where I stand. A witness who's decided to testify is worth ten thousand neutral clerks."

She almost smiled. It hurt her face; she wasn't sure she remembered how. "One condition. Before anything else — before the original, before page one — you tell me the buyers' name. The actual name. The one they call themselves."

Ilex went to the window. Beyond the glass, the starless dark lay over the city like a lid.

"They don't have a name you'd find in any living language," he said. "But in the old contract-margins, in the tongues that predate the erasures, they're called the Ember Court. A parliament of fire. They buy skies, seasons, sovereignty — and they pay in exactly what you'd expect." He turned back to her. "Warmth. Survival. A few more years for the dying. And then they collect."

Hazel put her hand flat on the folio, over her mother's signature, and felt — or imagined she felt — a faint heat rising through the vellum, like a hearth behind a wall.

"Then we'd better read fast," she said. "Because I think it knows we're reading."

Far off, beyond the edge of the city, where no clouds had gathered in six years, thunder answered.
