## 7. Storm-Light Testimony

The Guild's reading room had no windows, which Hazel had always considered a mercy and now understood to be a necessity. What she was about to show Ilex could not be witnessed by daylight. Storm-light was a shy thing. It lived in the margins of the world, in the space between lightning and thunder, and it did not perform for an audience that hadn't earned it.

She locked the door. She drew the shutters over the ventilation grates — old habit, though no one had ever explained why the Guild bothered with grates at all in a windowless room. Then she set her ledger on the table between them and stood with her hands flat on its cover for a long moment, feeling the faint warmth of it through her palms, like a sleeping animal.

"You're certain," Ilex said. It wasn't quite a question. He sat across from her with the stillness of a man who had learned that sudden movements cost something. In the lamplight his face was all angles and shadow, and his eyes kept returning to the ledger the way a thirsty man's eyes return to a well.

"No," Hazel said. "But you asked for proof, and proof is what we keep."

She opened to the marker she'd placed three nights ago, after he'd told her about the Ember Court — after he'd said *they buy skies* in a voice so flat it had taken her a full minute to understand he wasn't speaking in metaphor. The page beneath her fingers was ordinary vellum, ruled in the Guild's precise gray lines, filled with her own entries in storm-light ink: acquisitions catalogued, debts recorded, interest calculated on bottled griefs and borrowed years. To anyone else it would have been bookkeeping. To anyone else, it would have been nothing.

"Guild law says every entry is written in witness-ink," she said. "That's the phrase they use in training. Witness-ink. As if it's just a way of saying the record is binding." Her thumb moved along the page's edge. "It isn't. It's literal. The ink doesn't record what happened. It remembers it. There's a difference, and the difference is everything."

"And you're going to show me."

"I'm going to show you the night I was exiled." She heard her own voice go carefully level, the way it went when she catalogued something painful — a widow's grief by the pint, a soldier's last year by the decade. "The trial lasted four hours. The verdict took eleven words. But the storm itself — the one they say I called — that took most of a night, and every second of it is in here, because I wrote my own hearing notes while it was happening. By candlelight. Through the bars of a holding cell." A breath. "I didn't know then what I was writing with. I thought it was just ink."

Ilex leaned forward. "What do I do?"

"Nothing. That's the hard part. You watch, and you don't touch the pages, and if it asks you a question—" she paused, because this was the part even senior clerks flinched at, "—you don't answer it. It's not really asking. It's checking whether you're real. Storm-light distrusts anything that stands still too long."

"That's not ominous at all."

"It's the least ominous thing about it." She drew a long breath, and let her hand rest fully open on the page, the way her mother had once taught her to rest a hand on a frightened horse — flat, warm, patient. "Now be quiet. And whatever you see — remember that I saw it too. From inside a cell."

She closed her eyes and gave the ink what it wanted, which was attention without agenda. Grief without direction. The particular quality of remembering that she'd spent six years perfecting in the cellar archives, handling other people's losses until her own had worn smooth as river stone.

The lamp guttered.

Not blew out — guttered, the flame bending sideways toward the ledger as if drawn by a draft that came from inside the book. And then the light changed. It went blue-white and trembling, the color of the sky two seconds before lightning splits it, and the room's shadows inverted themselves, dark things going pale, and the air filled with the smell of rain on hot slate.

The page began to move.

Not the paper — the *light*. It rose off the vellum in slow ribbons, unwinding like thread from a spool, and where the ribbons crossed they wove themselves into shapes: walls first, gray and wet, then a barred window, then beyond the window a sky the color of a bruise going black. Hazel watched it assemble with the detached precision of long practice, and beside her she felt Ilex go very still, the stillness of a man watching something he had been taught did not exist.

"There," she said quietly. "That's the holding cell under the Corvale courthouse. That's the view I had."

The ribbons thickened. Sound came with them now, arriving out of order the way memory arrives — thunder first, low and continuous, a sound less like noise than like pressure; then rain, hammering; then voices, distant and distorted, shouting against the wind. The image sharpened. Through the barred window of woven light, the storm built itself in miniature: clouds stacking in towers, lightning walking down their flanks on legs of white fire, the harbor below churning green-black.

And in the corner of the reconstructed sky — small, unmistakable, lit by each successive flash — stood a figure on the cliff above the harbor road.

A woman. Arms raised. Coat whipping around her like a banner.

Hazel had seen this image before, of course. Everyone in Corvale had seen it, or claimed to; it had been sketched, painted, printed on the front of every broadsheet for a week. *The Pemberton woman calls the storm.* The artist had given her figure a kind of terrible grandeur, arms spread like a conductor before an orchestra of ruin. The trial had turned on it. Witnesses swore they'd seen her up there. Her own coat had been found snagged on the gorse at the cliff's edge, soaked through, salt-stiff.

"I never stood there," Hazel said. Her voice sounded strange to her, flattened, as if it were coming from somewhere far away. "I want you to understand that before anything else. I never stood on that cliff. I was in the archive that evening. Forty people saw me there. The Guildmaster signed my time-sheet himself. And then the storm came, and that woman was on the cliff wearing my face, and forty witnesses stopped mattering."

In the woven light, the storm reached its height. The miniature harbor heaved. Somewhere in the reconstruction a bell began to ring — the chapel bell, rung in emergency, its sound warped by wind into something almost human, almost screaming. The figure on the cliff turned its head, slowly, deliberately, and looked directly at the barred window. At her.

At *her.*

Every time. Every time she played this back, the figure looked at the window, and every time Hazel's body responded as if it were happening now: heart slamming, hands cold, the animal certainty of being *seen* by something that meant to take her place.

"Watch the hands," she said, and hated how thin her voice came out. "The broadsheets got the pose wrong. Look at what she's actually doing."

The figure's raised arms weren't spread wide. They were moving — tracing patterns, deliberate ones, the left hand high and circling, the right hand low and cutting. Hazel knew those gestures. Every guild clerk knew them. They were the opening forms of a calling, the somatic grammar that accompanied a storm-invocation the way a signature accompanied a contract. The left hand summoned. The right hand *bound* — bound the storm to a name, to a shape, to a debt.

"She's not just calling it," Hazel said. "She's signing it. Binding it to someone. Watch—"

The right hand cut downward, final, a stroke like a pen finishing a letter, and the whole reconstruction flared white—

—and in the flare, for half a heartbeat, the figure's face resolved.

Not hers.

The face was wrong in ways that were almost impossible to articulate and impossible to unsee once seen. The jaw was a fraction too narrow. The eyes were set a shade too close, and they were the wrong color — not Hazel's gray but something darker, something that swallowed the lightning rather than catching it. The hair was right. The coat was right. The stance was right down to the way Hazel shifted her weight onto her left hip when she stood too long. Everything was right except the one thing that mattered, and the wrongness of it hit the room like cold water.

Beside her, Ilex made a small sound. Not a gasp. Something more controlled than that, more practiced — the sound of a man swallowing a reaction whole.

"You see it," Hazel said. It wasn't a question either.

"I see it." His voice had gone careful. Too careful. "Hazel—"

"Don't." She was on her feet. She didn't remember standing. The reconstruction still burned on the table between them, the false-faced figure frozen mid-gesture in its web of blue-white light, and Hazel stared at it with six years of exile rising in her throat like floodwater. "Don't say anything reasonable yet. Let me have this. Six years. Six years I have been *certain*, absolutely certain, in the private locked room of my own mind where certainty does no good, that I didn't call that storm. And certainty without evidence is just madness with good posture. Do you understand? They took my name, my city, my country on its way to taking itself, and the whole time — the whole *time* — there was a version of this that existed somewhere, in some record, that proved it. And it was in my own handwriting. It was in my own *ink*. I've been carrying the proof of my innocence in a ledger I used to press flowers."

The light on the table trembled. She realized distantly that the storm-light was responding to her — rippling, agitated, the ribbons of the reconstruction shivering like disturbed water. The ink remembered, and right now what it remembered was her rage, both nights of it, the original and this one layered on top.

"The face," Ilex said quietly. "Describe it for me. Precisely."

"Why?"

"Because precision is what you have instead of a army, and I suspect you'll need both." He hadn't moved from his chair, but something in him had reorganized — she'd seen it in clients sometimes, the moment a purchase became a pursuit. "Jaw narrow. Eyes dark, set close. Anything else?"

"Hairline different. Slightly lower on the brow." Hazel closed her eyes and made herself look again, inward, at the half-second of flare. "And there was — a scar. Left side of the throat. Old. It caught the light when she turned." She opened her eyes. "Why does it matter? No one can wear a face that well. Glamours slip. Masks don't fit the voice. Whatever they used—"

"It wasn't a glamour." Ilex said it flatly, without drama, which was somehow worse. "The Ember Court doesn't deal in glamours. They deal in *substance*. If someone wore your shape convincingly enough to fool a harbor full of witnesses, they didn't put on your face, Hazel. They took it. Or they had it made." He paused. "There are ways. Old ones. Ways that involve having something of yours — hair, blood, a signature. Something signed in your hand."

The bottom dropped out of the evening.

Hazel looked down at the table, at the frozen storm, at the false woman with her borrowed gestures, and the arithmetic assembled itself with the horrible inevitability of a ledger balancing. Something signed in her hand. Something of hers. She thought of the contract folio in the deep archive, the one with her mother's name on it, dated before Hazel's birth. She thought of clause after clause she hadn't read yet, terms she'd skimmed in her urgency to find the buyer, skipping the boilerplate the way any clerk skips boilerplate.

Collateral clauses named assets. Assets included signatures. Signatures could be *used*.

"They didn't frame me," she said slowly. "They spent me. Like currency. My shape was in the contract somewhere, wasn't it — some clause I never read, some provision that let them draw on me the way the Guild draws on a debtor's account. And the storm—" her voice cracked and she forced it level, "—the storm was a withdrawal. They called it in my name because my name was already theirs. The trial wasn't a mistake. It was *bookkeeping*. They needed me gone before I ever read page one, and the fastest way to exile a clerk is to make her the author of a catastrophe."

The reconstruction finally collapsed, the ribbons of light sinking back into the vellum, the cell and the cliff and the false-faced woman folding away into ordinary ruled lines and gray margins. The lamp straightened. The room smelled of rain and hot metal and, underneath it, the old smell of the archives: dust and vellum and time.

Ilex was looking at her with an expression she couldn't fully read — sympathy, yes, but underneath it something sharper. Recognition, maybe. The look of a man confirming a suspicion he'd carried a long way.

"You suspected," she said. "Before tonight. Before you saw it. That's why you came looking for me specifically. Not for the ledgers. For *this*."

"I came for the contract," he said. "I'll admit the rest was — likely." He rose, and for the first time since he'd walked into the Guild asking for a door out of a country that didn't exist, he looked uncertain how to stand. "Hazel. The person who wore your face. The Court has an enforcer. Few people have seen her face and kept their own. She wears what she's given. If the Court dressed her in you—"

"Then she knows what I look like from the inside." Hazel pressed her palms flat against the cool table, grounding herself the way she grounded a trembling ledger. "Then she's walked around in my life. Then somewhere out there is a creature who knows exactly how I stand, how I speak, how I sign my name — and she learned it in order to destroy me with it."

"Yes."

"Then I need to learn it back." She lifted her head, and found that the rage hadn't burned itself out the way rage was supposed to. It had done something worse and better. It had cooled into structure. Into columns and totals and a debt outstanding, entered fair, entered true, awaiting collection. "Because whoever she is, whatever she is — she made one mistake. She wore my shape into a storm, and storms keep records too. And mine was watching from a barred window the entire time."

Ilex gathered his coat. At the door he paused, hand on the latch, and looked back at her across the dead lamp and the closed ledger.

"There's something you should know before we go any further," he said. "About the Court. About why I know what their enforcer looks like when she's working." His jaw tightened. "You're not going to like the reason I recognized that scar."

* * *

Outside the reading room, the Guild slept its shallow administrative sleep — night clerks murmuring over valuation tables, the cellar lifts groaning as grief was moved between shelves. Hazel walked beside Ilex through the dim corridors with her ledger hugged to her chest, and the warmth of it bled through the cover into her arms like a second pulse.

They stopped in the antechamber beneath the great seal of the Vellum Guild, where the founders' portraits hung in their gilt frames, and Ilex turned to face her. In the low light his face seemed older than it had in the reading room, carved deeper, as if the confession were already weighing on it.

"The scar on her throat," he said. "Left side. Old."

"You said."

"I said I'd tell you why I recognized it." He drew a breath. "Sixteen years ago, I sat across a bargaining table from a woman with that scar. She wasn't the Court's enforcer then. She was their *debtor*. She'd sold something enormous — I was junior audit staff, I never learned what — and the payment hollowed her out. What was left, the Court kept. Trained. Used." His eyes held hers, steady and grim. "The woman who wore your face into that storm, Hazel — the woman who stole six years of your life — started out exactly like you. Someone who owed a debt she never should have signed."

Hazel stood very still, the ledger warm against her ribs, and felt the shape of the next chapter of her life settle over her like weather coming in.

"Then we find her," she said. "Before the Court finds us."
