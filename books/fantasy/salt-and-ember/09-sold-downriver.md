## 9. Sold Downriver

The knock came at the third hour of the night, and Hazel knew before she opened her door that it was wrong.

Guild knocks had a grammar. Clerks rapped twice, quick, apologetic, as if to say *sorry to wake you but the ledgers won't balance themselves*. Oren Vey knocked once, slow, with the flat of his fist — the knock he used on debtors' doors, the one that meant *I know what you have and I've come to collect*. But this knock was neither. It was three soft taps, spaced like footsteps in an empty hall, like someone who had learned knocking from watching rather than doing.

She stood very still in her narrow room above the cellar archives, one hand already closing around the ledger she'd taken to sleeping beside. The storm-light pages threw their faint blue glow against the wall, and in that light she could see the shadow beneath her door: two feet, planted wide, patient.

Not Oren's feet. Not Ilex's either — Ilex walked like a man apologizing to the floor for existing on it.

"Clerek Pemberton," said a voice through the wood, smooth as poured wax. "We haven't been introduced. My name is Sable."

Hazel did not answer. Her heart was doing something strange and cold, turning over like a page in wind.

"I'm told you keep unusual books," the voice continued. "I'd very much like to read them."

*The Court.* It arrived in her all at once, whole and certain, the way knowledge sometimes does — not reasoned but remembered, as if some part of her had always known this night would come. Chapter eight of the oldest folio. The Ember Court, which bought skies and sold names. Which had noticed her glowing ledgers the way a spider notices the tremor of its web.

"They can't find me," she whispered to herself. "I'm not on any map. No one remembers—"

"—except," Sable said pleasantly, as though she'd heard, "the Guildmaster. Who remembers everything about his Guild, even the parts he pretends don't exist. Even you."

The feet shifted. "I'll leave you to consider. Consider quickly. We're coming back with fire, and fire is less polite than I am."

The footsteps receded down the stair. Hazel counted to sixty, then sixty more, then flung open the door and ran.

* * *

Oren Vey was awake. Of course he was awake; she should have known. He sat in his office above the counting room with every lamp lit, which he never did — lamps cost candle-money, and Oren counted candle-money the way other men counted sins — and his face in all that light looked like something dredged up from deep water.

He didn't startle when she burst in. He'd heard her coming. He always heard everyone coming.

"You sold me." The words came out of her flat and ugly, and she hated how small they sounded, how much they sounded like the girl who had stood in front of a tribunal seven years ago and sworn she never called that storm.

Oren folded his hands on the desk. His knuckles were enormous, clerk's knuckles, ink-stained at the second joint the way hers were. She had always found that comforting before. Now it looked like a mirror held up by an enemy.

"The Guild has four hundred years of holdings," he said. "Bottled griefs. Borrowed years. Names in escrow. Debts owed to us and debts we owe, and every one of them written down somewhere, and every one of them void if the Court decides we're harboring stolen property." He lifted his eyes to hers. "You are stolen property, Pemberton. You've been stolen property since the day your mother signed."

"My mother signed a contract. That doesn't make me—"

"It makes you exactly what the contract says you are. Collateral." He said the word without cruelty, which was somehow worse. Oren Vey had never been cruel in his life; he was merely honest the way a knife is honest. "They came to me tonight. Not Sable — the ministers themselves, or their paper, which amounts to the same thing. A writ of recovery. And attached to it, a simple choice: surrender the clerk, or the Guild becomes a memory no one wrote down. Do you understand what that means? Not destroyed. *Unwritten.* Every contract we hold, unenforceable. Every client's purchase, evaporated. Four hundred years of careful bookkeeping, gone, because one junior archivist couldn't stop writing in light."

Hazel's hands were shaking. She pressed them flat against her thighs. "You could have refused."

"Yes," Oren agreed. "And then there would be nothing left of the Vellum Guild but you, running through the dark with one book, hunted by a parliament of fire. Whereas now—" he exhaled, long and tired, "—now there is a Guild, and there is a hunt, and the difference between those two futures is that in one of them, someone survives to keep the accounts."

"That's not bookkeeping. That's arithmetic with people."

"All bookkeeping is arithmetic with people. That's what makes it matter." For a moment — just a moment — something moved behind his face that might have been grief, or might have been its accountant, tallying grief against necessity and finding the sum acceptable. "I've sent word ahead. There's a postern gate behind the tannery row, unlocked. You have perhaps half an hour before the Court's people stop being polite. Take whatever you can carry."

"Why?" The question tore out of her before she could dress it up. "Why warn me at all? Why not just hand me over?"

Oren looked at her for a long time. When he spoke, his voice had dropped into something she'd never heard him use — quiet, almost private, the voice of a man alone with his ledger at midnight.

"Because thirty years ago I sold a different clerk downriver, and I told myself the same things I told myself tonight, and I have spent thirty years telling them. I wanted to see if saying them out loud would help." He turned his face toward the lamps. "It doesn't. Go, Pemberton. And take the founding ledger."

Her breath caught. "The founding—"

"Third shelf, behind the folio of unredeemed oaths. You'll understand when you read it. Or you won't, and you'll burn it with the rest, and that will be its own kind of understanding." He waved a hand, dismissive, done. "Go. Don't thank me. I couldn't survive being thanked."

* * *

The cellar archives breathed around her in the dark — that particular stillness of a room full of preserved things, dust and wax-seal and the faint vinegar ghost of old vellum. Hazel moved through the stacks with a satchel and a hooded lantern and her heart hammering out a rhythm she recognized from the worst nights of her exile: *hurry, hurry, they are coming, hurry.*

One ledger. Oren had said one ledger, take what you can carry. But standing among the shelves, she understood that carrying was not the problem.

The problem was leaving any of it behind.

Every volume here was a life, or a piece of one. Shelf after shelf of bottled childhoods and borrowed decades, of names held in trust and memories pawned against futures that might never come to redeem them. She had catalogued most of them herself. She knew where Widow Marsh's grief lived — aisle nine, third cubby, jar sealed in green wax. She knew which folio held the ferryman's forgotten things, and which drawer kept the soldier's unwritten war. These weren't acquisitions to her. They were the only company she'd had for seven years, the only voices that stayed, and the thought of the Court's fire touching them—

She stopped walking.

Stood in the middle of aisle nine with her lantern raised, and let herself think the thought she'd been refusing since Oren's office.

If she took one ledger and fled, the Court would come here. They would come for the Guild's records anyway — not to preserve them, to *audit* them, to erase anything that touched Corvale, anything that touched her, anything that glowed. And these memories, these purchased lives, would not be freed. They would be consumed as evidence. Bottled griefs poured out into flame. Borrowed years burned like kindling.

Or.

Her hand found the shelf. Steadied against it.

*Or I burn them myself.*

The thought arrived like vertigo. Because she knew what burning meant, here, in this place. These weren't ordinary books. A memory destroyed in the Guild's keeping didn't simply vanish — it *left*, it went home to whatever hollow place it had been sold from, or it died screaming, or both; the guild-lawyers had argued about it for centuries and settled on *inadvisable*. And she would be the one holding the match. She would hear them go.

"No," she said aloud, to herself, to the dark. "No, there's another way. There's always another way, that's what the ledgers teach you, every debt has a clause—"

But the clauses needed time, and time was the one thing the Court wasn't selling tonight.

She thought of the client from months ago — the man who'd tried to buy back his own childhood and been refused, guild law, no refunds, and how he had looked at her, not with hatred but with the terrible patience of someone memorizing her face so he could hate her properly later. She had enforced the rule. She had believed in the rule. The rule was the only thing standing between the Guild and chaos, she'd told herself, the rule was fairness, the rule was—

The rule was a wall, and walls keep people out, and she had spent seven years inside one.

Hazel set down her lantern. Took off her coat, folded it, laid it on the floor. Then she went to the founding ledger first — third shelf, behind the folio of unredeemed oaths, exactly where Oren said — and slid it into her satchel with the storm-light book she'd carried since the stars went out. Two books. Everything else, she would give back the only way left.

She started at aisle one.

The first folio she opened was a register of borrowed years — decades of a miller's life, purchased by a merchant who'd wanted youth and gotten it. She tipped the lantern until the flame kissed the corner of the page.

The scream was not a sound. That was the horror of it, the thing she would carry forever: it was not a sound at all. It was a *pressure*, a shape of anguish that bypassed the ear entirely and seized the inside of her skull, and for one white instant she felt the miller's fifty years — the smell of flour, the weight of sacks, a daughter's wedding, a wife's cough turning serious — all of it tearing loose, all of it going *home*, pouring backward through the burning page like water running the wrong way up a stream.

Then ash. Then silence. Then the next one.

She worked fast, because slowness was unbearable. Folio by folio, jar by jar, she fed the archive to the lantern flame, and each burning screamed through her, and she learned — God forgive her, she *learned* — to tell them apart. The griefs screamed longest. The borrowed years went almost gratefully, like travelers finally allowed off a ship. One name-in-escrow, a child's name held in trust for twenty years, made a sound that wasn't a scream at all but a single syllable of protest, *mine*, and then it was free, wherever free was, and Hazel knelt on the stone floor and shook and did not stop working.

Widow Marsh's grief she saved for last. Green wax, aisle nine, third cubby. She held the jar a moment, feeling the sorrow inside it shift like weather.

"I'm sorry," she whispered. "I hope it hurts less out there than in here."

She unstoppered it instead of burning it. Let the grief go home the gentle way, if there was a gentle way. Then she burned the empty jar's label, because even labels were records, and records were what the Court ate.

By the time she climbed the cellar stairs, the smoke was already threading up behind her, sweet and awful, the smell of forty years of careful work becoming freedom or nothing. Behind her the archive burned clean — guild-vellum burned clean, that was one mercy, no black smoke, only white — and ahead of her the postern gate stood unlocked in the tannery wall, exactly as promised, and beyond it the night waited, starless and enormous.

Hazel paused on the threshold. Looked back once at the glow rising over the Guild's roofline, and let herself feel all of it at once — the guilt, vast and specific, itemized like a ledger: *one archive, destroyed by my hand; forty years of purchases, returned or ended; one Guildmaster, betrayed in the only direction available to him; one clerk, guilty of arson and mercy in equal measure.*

Then she closed the entry. Turned the page. Walked out.

Ilex was waiting in the alley, pressed into the shadow of the tannery wall, and the look on his face when he saw the smoke told her he understood instantly, completely, what she had done.

"You burned it," he said. Not accusation. Wonder, maybe. Or fear wearing wonder's coat.

"I burned it." Her voice came out steadier than she had any right to expect. "Everything except this." She patted the satchel. "Two ledgers. The storm-light book, and the founding ledger Oren gave me. He said I'd understand it when I read it."

"And do you?"

"Not yet." She pulled the hood up over her hair and looked down the alley toward the river, toward the road south, toward whatever country came after the country that didn't exist. Somewhere behind them, a bell began to ring — alarm, or mourning, or both. Somewhere closer than comfort, she could feel it, the Court was already turning its attention like a great hot eye. "But I know where we're going. The contract has a clause-path, and clause-paths lead somewhere. They lead to whoever holds the original."

"The Ember Court."

"The Ember Court." She stepped past him into the open street, into the dark, and found that her legs held. "They bought my mother's sky. They sold my country's name. And they think I'm collateral." The smoke rose behind her, white and straight into the starless air, a column any hunter could follow — and she found she didn't care, because let them come; let them follow the smoke straight to the woman who was done being filed away. "Let's go teach a parliament of fire what happens when the fine print fights back."

They went downriver together, two shadows on a road that was forgetting itself behind them, and above the burning Guild the sky stayed black, and in her satchel, faint and patient, the storm-light pages glowed like a heart that refused to stop.
