## 10. The Road That Isn't There

The road began to forget itself three miles out of the Guild's territory, and Hazel noticed it the way she noticed everything: by its arithmetic.

They had been walking for two hours. She had counted her steps out of habit — a clerk's tic, counting was how she held the world still — and at step four thousand and eleven she looked back and saw that the road behind them had shortened. Not narrowed. *Shortened.* The ruts they'd left in the mud were gone. The milestone they'd passed at dawn, a leaning stone carved with distances to towns that might no longer exist, stood now only fifty paces back instead of half a mile.

"It's doing it again," she said.

Ilex stopped beside her and did not look surprised. He carried his satchel the way an auditor carries one — strapped high, close to the ribs, as though the papers inside were currency or contraband, which in his case they were both. He was a narrow man, gray at the temples, with the particular stillness of someone who had spent years being unremarkable on purpose.

"Terrain erasure," he said. "It radiates outward from the border. Corvale wasn't just struck from the maps, Hazel. It was struck from the ground."

"The ground can't be struck from itself."

"You'd think not." He crouched and pressed two fingers into the mud where their footprints should have been. The earth was smooth as poured wax. "But the Court didn't buy the sky of Corvale and leave the dirt alone. A country is a stack of agreements. The sky agreed to hang above it. The roads agreed to run through it. The stones agreed to hold their names." He stood, brushing off his fingers with the fastidious disgust of a man touching something improperly filed. "When you void the contract, everything that signed it starts forgetting what it agreed to."

Hazel looked down the road ahead. It ran toward hills she remembered from childhood geography lessons — the Dunlow Fells, gray and sheep-bitten — but the fells themselves seemed uncertain now, their outlines softening and sharpening like a thing breathing badly. Between one blink and the next, a stand of birches at the road's edge lost three of its number. No sound. No falling. They simply were not there anymore, and the space they'd occupied closed over like water.

Her ledger, wrapped in oilcloth against her chest, gave a faint pulse of light. Storm-light answering unease with unease.

"Keep talking," she said. "Out loud. Both of us. If we narrate ourselves, does it help?"

Ilex glanced at her — the first flicker of something like respect crossing his careful face. "You've done this before."

"I keep ledgers written in memory. I know what happens to things nobody writes down." She unwrapped the ledger enough to let it breathe, and its glow strengthened, throwing pale blue-white light across the mud. "We're our own record now. So we record."

"Then note this," Ilex said, resuming the walk. "Day one since leaving the Guild. We follow the clause-path. The contract your mother signed is structured like all Ember Court instruments — every obligation is a location. Debts don't sit still; they travel along their own terms. If we follow the clauses in order, the path leads to the Court's sitting-house."

"And if we deviate?"

"Then we're walking in a country that has no reason to remember us, holding documents it has no reason to honor." He said it lightly, but Hazel heard the auditor underneath: *the math does not favor us.*

She took out her pencil and wrote, right there on the move, in the margin of the ledger's last living page:

*Day one. Two travelers. Road unreliable. Sky absent. Proceeding by contract.*

The words glowed faintly blue and stayed put. Behind them, she did not check whether the road kept them.

* * *

By midday the world had grown worse in the specific way that bad bookkeeping grows worse: gradually, then all at once.

The sun was a rumor. There was light, but it came from everywhere and nowhere, shadowless, the illumination of a place that had been described rather than built. The hills repeated themselves. Hazel was nearly certain they had passed the same split boulder twice, except the second time the split had healed, and the third time the boulder was granite where before it had been limestone, and she could no longer swear it was the same stone at all.

"We're not lost," Ilex said, when she said nothing for too long. "Lost implies a map. There is no map. There's only the clause-path, and the clause-path says—" he consulted the copied folio pages he'd transcribed from her mother's contract, "—*from the signatory's door, west by the low road, until the first village that remembers.*"

"That's not a direction. That's a riddle."

"It's a legal instrument. They're the same thing." He squinted westward, where the hills ran together into a haze that might have been distance or might have been the world simply declining to render any further. "A village that remembers. In erased terrain, memory is a landmark. Somewhere out there is a pocket of Corvale that hasn't finished forgetting."

Hazel walked on, and the ledger against her chest pulsed like a second heart, and she thought about her mother signing away a sky, and about the word *perpetuity*, which she had always found beautiful and now understood to be a threat.

In the late afternoon, the road simply ended.

Not faded, not washed out — *ended*, the way a sentence ends, with a finality that made her stop mid-stride. Ahead lay a field of grass so uniformly green it looked printed. Beyond the field, nothing. Not darkness, not fog. Nothing — a flat absence her eyes slid off of, unable to land, like trying to look at the inside of her own skull.

"The clause-path continues," Ilex said quietly. "Look."

He was pointing at the grass. Through it, faint as breath on glass, ran a line of pale light — the same color as her storm-ink — curving westward across the field and into the nothing.

"It's using your ink," Hazel said.

"It's using *memory*. Your ink is just the nearest instrument." Ilex's voice had gone carefully neutral, which she had learned meant he was frightened. "The contract was drafted before you were born, but it's been *kept* in storm-light ever since your Guild started recording debts honestly. The debt knows where it lives."

Hazel stepped off the dead road onto the grass, and the moment her boot touched it, the line of light brightened, and the field — she would have sworn to this later, under oath, in whatever court would have her — the field *leaned* toward her, the way wheat leans after wind, grateful.

"Come on," she said, because there was nothing else to say.

They crossed into the nothing, and the nothing let them.

* * *

Inside the absence there was a valley, and in the valley there was a village, and the village was burning candles at noon.

That was the first thing Hazel saw: candlelight in every window, dozens of small flames trembling in broad daylight, as though the whole settlement were keeping vigil. The houses were stone, low-roofed, smoke rising from chimneys that smoked without fire. Smoke without fire. Her clerk's mind snagged on it and refused to let go. Everything here was slightly wrong the way a ledger balances when someone has forced the numbers.

"They're remembering," Ilex murmured. "Hard. You can see the effort of it."

And then the villagers saw them coming down the hill, and an old woman dropped her basket, and the bread she'd been carrying rolled into the gutter, and nobody moved to pick it up, because everyone was staring at Hazel.

"Hazel Pemberton," the old woman said.

Hazel stopped. "I'm sorry — do I know you?"

"No." The woman said it plainly, without embarrassment, wiping her hands on her apron as though preparing for work. "No, child, we've never met. None of us. But we know you." She raised her voice, and it carried strangely far in the shadowless light, down the lane and up the lanes beyond it, and doors opened, and people came out into the street, and every one of them looked at Hazel with an expression she had no shelf in her mind for. It wasn't recognition. Recognition requires a prior meeting. This was something older and more terrible.

This was *expectation.*

"Hazel Pemberton," a man repeated from a doorway, tasting the name. "The keeper of the accounts."

"How do you know my name?" Her voice came out steadier than she felt, and she was grateful for the clerk's discipline that made it so. "Who told you?"

"Nobody told us." The old woman had come closer. Up close Hazel could see that her eyes were wet without grief, the way eyes get in wind. "It's more like — do you know what happens to a house when the family leaves? How it keeps the shape of them? Where the bed stood, where the dog slept?" She touched her own chest. "Corvale left, child. But we're the part of Corvale that couldn't finish leaving. And you—" she pointed, and her finger trembled, "—you are the part of Corvale that *remembers us*. We feel it. Like warmth from a lit window. Every night since the stars went out, we've felt one warm window somewhere far off, and we've walked toward it in our sleep, and now here you are standing in it."

Hazel's hand went to the ledger without her deciding to. Through the oilcloth she could feel it glowing, steady now, brighter than it had been in days.

"The residue," Ilex said softly, beside her. He had gone very still, in the way of a man watching a figure come out exactly right. "I'd read about it in the old audit files and assumed it was metaphor. Pockets of population the erasure couldn't fully penetrate. Villages that persist as — as residue. As remainder." He looked at Hazel. "They shouldn't exist. The Court's instruments don't permit remainders."

"And yet," Hazel said.

"And yet." Something moved across his face that she had never seen there before, and it took her a moment to name it, because it was so ordinary and so human: hope. "Remainders mean the erasure isn't total. Remainders mean there are seams."

The villagers had begun to gather, drawn out of houses and fields, maybe forty of them, maybe sixty — Hazel tried to count and found the number wouldn't hold still, which frightened her more than anything else that day. They did not crowd her. They stood at a respectful distance, the way people stand around a well.

An old man near the front, bent as a question mark, spoke up. "Will you write us down, keeper?"

"Write you down?"

"In your book. The one that glows." He said it without shame, the way a beggar asks for bread. "Everything's going soft. My wife's name went three days ago — I can say it but it doesn't mean anything anymore, it's just sounds. Marta. Marta." He said it twice, testing, and his face crumpled slightly on the second saying. "If it's in a book that remembers, does it count? Does it stay?"

Hazel looked at Ilex. Ilex looked at the ground, and said nothing, because there was nothing in guild law about this, and they both knew it.

"Yes," Hazel said.

She sat down right there in the lane, in the shadowless noon light, with forty-some villagers arranged around her like a congregation, and she unwrapped the ledger, and it flared so brightly that several people gasped and shielded their eyes, and she opened to the first blank page.

"Names," she said. "One at a time. Slowly. And tell me one true thing about each person, because a name alone is just sounds — I learned that from a client who bought back his childhood and got someone else's."

The old man came first. "Marta," he said. "My wife. She burns the bread on purpose because I like the burnt ends, and she pretends it's an accident, and we've pretended for fifty-one years."

Hazel wrote it. The letters came out in storm-light, blue-white and steady, and as she wrote, the old man made a sound she had never heard a person make — a small, cracked exhalation, like a knot coming loose — and he pressed his hand flat against his chest and said, "There. Oh. *There* she is."

One by one they came. A girl who remembered her dog. A farmer who remembered the exact slope of his best field and the year the barley failed. A woman who remembered a song and sang it, haltingly, while Hazel transcribed the words phonetically because she didn't know the tune and feared even the words would go. The candle flames in the windows burned higher as the afternoon went on. Hazel did not know whether that was real or whether the village was performing its own hope, and she decided it did not matter.

What mattered was the arithmetic. Each entry cost her nothing and gave them everything, and she had spent her whole life in a guild where every gift had a price and every price was paid by the poorest party, and here, for one afternoon, the books balanced the way she had always secretly believed books should balance.

Ilex sat beside her and took dictation on ordinary paper — a duplicate record, mundane ink, insurance against a day her storm-light might fail. His handwriting was beautiful. She noted that and filed it away among the small surprising facts about him, which were accumulating faster than she liked.

Near dusk — if the lightless sky could be said to have a dusk — the old woman who had spoken first returned and crouched by Hazel's side.

"There's something else," she said. "Something we remember that we shouldn't."

"Tell me."

"The night the stars went out. Before that. There was a storm." Around them, the gathering quieted. "A bad one. It hit the capital hard. Killed people. And afterward they arrested a girl for calling it." The old woman's eyes, wet and wind-blown, fixed on Hazel's face with terrible gentleness. "They arrested *you*. We remember the trial. We weren't there, none of us, we've never seen you before today — but we remember it the way the wall remembers the picture that hung on it. And child, we remember something the trial didn't show."

Hazel's pencil had stopped moving. "What."

"We remember the storm had two faces." The old woman's voice dropped. "Everyone saw the girl in the square calling the lightning. Everyone swore to it. But the storm — the storm itself, the rain, the wind, we were in it, we know storms, we're Corvale — the storm was *confused*. It kept changing its mind. It reached for the harbor and pulled back. It gathered over the granary and broke apart. A storm called by a person is a single will, child. That one was two wills fighting over one body." She reached out and, unasked, touched the glowing ledger. "Whatever they convicted you of, the storm remembers disagreeing."

The lane was silent. The candles flickered. Somewhere a shutter banged in a wind that had no business existing in a windless light.

Hazel found she could not speak. She had carried her exile for six years like a stone sewn into her coat — had stopped arguing about it, stopped even thinking about it most days, because the argument was unwinnable and a clerk learns to live with balanced columns however wrong they are. And now a stranger in a village that did not exist was telling her the columns had never balanced at all.

"Ilex," she said finally, and her voice was not quite level, and she let it be not quite level, because these people had earned honesty. "Write that down too. Ordinary ink. *The storm had two wills. Witnessed by the residue of Corvale.* Date it."

He was already writing.

The old woman squeezed Hazel's hand once, her grip surprisingly strong, and stood. "Stay the night," she said. "We'll feed you what remembering tastes like — it isn't much, bread and onions, but it's ours. And in the morning—" she hesitated, and for the first time her certainty wavered, and Hazel saw that beneath the strange conviction the woman was afraid, had been afraid for a long time, "—in the morning, you'll want to see the church. There's something written on the foundation stone. It's been there longer than the village. Longer than the village, do you understand? And since the stars went out, the writing's been *changing*. New words surfacing like bodies in a pond. We can't read it. But the candles won't go out near it, and the dogs won't go near it, and I think—" she swallowed, "—I think it's waiting for someone who reads glowing books."

Hazel looked at Ilex. Ilex looked at the darkening, starless east, where the clause-path's pale line reappeared beyond the village edge, running on toward the hills and the Court and everything waiting there.

"A foundation stone older than the village," he said slowly, "inscribed with text that updates itself. That's not masonry. That's a *document*."

"That's what I was afraid you'd say," Hazel said.

She looked down at her ledger, at the day's entries glowing in rows like a planted field, at the names of people who existed only as remainders, held in light because she had written them there. Then she looked up at the church at the end of the lane, its windows full of candle-flame, its foundation stone hidden in the dark beneath it all, holding words that were surfacing word by word like a debt coming due.

"Show me the stone," she said. "Tonight. Before I lose my nerve."
