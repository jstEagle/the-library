## 11. What the River Owed

The river appeared on the third morning, which was strange, because there had been no river the night before.

Hazel knew this the way she knew her own ledgers — by the absence of it. She had gone to sleep on a ridge of gray grass that forgot itself in patches, ground that rippled like water when you weren't looking directly at it, and she had fallen asleep listening for water that wasn't there. When she woke, it was there: a broad brown current sliding past the base of the ridge, wide as a road, patient as debt.

"It does that," Ilex said. He was already standing at the top of the slope, his travel cloak buttoned to the throat despite the mild air. He never seemed to sleep so much as pause, like a candle pinched between snuffings. "The river. It's not that it moves. It's that everything around it keeps forgetting where the low ground is."

"That's not how rivers work."

"Neither is a country being unwritten," Ilex said. "And yet."

Hazel came down the slope carefully. The grass gave under her boots like old paper, and twice she felt the particular lurch of walking on terrain that had briefly stopped believing in itself — a sensation she was learning to hate, a softness in the world's spine. At the bottom, the river ran close enough to touch. It smelled of iron and something older underneath, something like the inside of a locked room.

On the near bank, moored to nothing — there was no dock, no post, no tree — sat a flat-bottomed ferry, black wood gone silver at the edges, and standing in it a figure who might have been tall once and had settled, the way buildings settle.

"You'll want to be polite," Ilex murmured. "He doesn't take coin."

"I gathered. The contract clause-path runs along the water." Hazel touched the folio strapped beneath her coat, the one ledger she'd saved from the burning, its storm-light pages warm against her ribs like a sleeping bird. "Everything about the Court is written along this river. The clauses follow it downstream."

"Then let's go meet the toll-keeper." Ilex hesitated, and Hazel caught it — a small hitch in him, a man checking his own pockets before entering a room. "Hazel. Whatever he asks for, think before you pay. He only takes what's already loosened. Things that are half-forgotten anyway. That's what he'd tell you. It isn't entirely true."

"What do you mean, not entirely?"

Ilex looked at the ferryman for a long moment, and his face did something complicated and brief, like weather crossing a window. "Nothing," he said. "Let's go."

* * *

The ferryman's name, if he had one, was not offered. He stood at the stern with a pole he did not use, and when they stepped aboard the boat simply began to move, the current bending toward them like a dog hearing its name.

"Fare," he said. His voice was the sound a ledger makes when you close it.

"We're going downriver," Hazel said. "Past the drowned villages. To the ash line, if the maps still—" She stopped herself. The maps didn't. Nothing still did, except her. "To the border of the Ember Court's territory. As close as you can take us without burning."

"I know where you're going." The ferryman's eyes were the color of the water, which is to say they were the color of whatever you were afraid of finding in it. "Everyone who boards my boat is going somewhere they shouldn't. Fare is fare. I take payment in forgotten things."

"Coin?" Ilex asked.

"Forget coin. Coin remembers itself too well." The ferryman turned his head, slow as a door. "A forgotten thing. A memory you've already begun to lose. A name you no longer answer to. A promise you've quietly decided not to keep. I take those. They weigh almost nothing, and yet—" he smiled, or the arrangement of his face suggested a smile the way a ruin suggests a house, "—they weigh everything."

Hazel's hand went, without her permission, to the ledger at her ribs.

"Not that," the ferryman said, and for the first time something moved behind his eyes — interest, or appetite. "Storm-light's no good to me. Storm-light won't forget. That's the whole trouble with you, clerk. You're carrying the only cargo on this river that insists on being remembered."

"Then what do you want from me?"

The ferryman studied her. The boat slid on. Banks went by — reeds, mud, the suggestion of a fence that faded halfway through existing — and all the while he looked at her the way an appraiser looks at an estate, itemizing.

"You're full of holes already," he said at last. "Exile does that. Burns out the rooms a person lived in. But most of your doors are still shut, still furnished." He raised one hand, a finger like a dried reed. "There. Third one back. The one you keep touching without knowing it."

Hazel realized her fingers had drifted up to rest against her own cheekbone. She didn't know why. She took them away, and they wanted to go back.

"No," she said.

"You don't even know what I'm asking for."

"I know it's mine."

"Everything is yours until it isn't," the ferryman said, not unkindly, which was somehow worse. "That's the first lesson of every ledger ever kept. You of all people should have learned it."

The river widened. Ahead, the light changed — a faint orange stain on the southern horizon, like a bruise seen through skin, the glow of a parliament that never stopped sitting. They were closer than Hazel had thought. Time moved strangely on forgetting ground; hours folded like pages.

"I'll pay," Ilex said suddenly.

The ferryman turned to him, and something in the boat's motion changed — a subtle slowing, the current holding its breath.

"You have nothing I want," the ferryman said. "You spent yourself down years ago. There's hardly anything left in you that's loose." And here his voice dropped into something almost gentle, which on that boat was the cruelest register available. "Almost."

"Ilex." Hazel grabbed his sleeve. "What would he even take?"

"Doesn't matter." Ilex wasn't looking at her. He was looking at the ferryman with an expression she hadn't seen on him before — not the auditor's flatness, not the defector's wariness, but something rawer. Recognition. "It doesn't matter, because you're not paying. This is my debt we're traveling on. My audit. My—"

"Your guilt," the ferryman agreed. "But guilt isn't forgotten. Guilt is the opposite of forgotten. Guilt is the thing people hold onto when everything else has been taken." He turned back to Hazel. "No. It's her fare or no passage. That's how fares work. That's how *debts* work, and you two are swimming in one big enough to drown a nation."

The word *nation* landed on the boat like a stone.

Hazel closed her eyes. She made herself do what she did best: inventory. What had she already lost? Corvale, from every map and every mind but hers. Her guild, her rank, her name in any record but her own. Nine days — no, eight now — until the centennial renewal, and a contract in her coat that named her mother's signature on page one.

Her mother.

She had been careful, these last weeks, not to think of her mother's face. Not because it hurt — though it did — but because she had noticed, somewhere on the second day of the road that wasn't there, that when she tried to summon it, the image arrived slightly wrong. The eyes too pale, or the mouth set in someone else's severity. Grief was editing the portrait, and grief was a sloppy editor. Each recall redrew it further from truth. She had maybe a handful of true viewings left before the original was overwritten entirely by approximation.

She understood, then, with the cold clarity of a clerk reading fine print, exactly which door the ferryman's finger had indicated. The third one back. The one already loosening. The one she kept touching without knowing it.

"You want her face," Hazel said.

The ferryman said nothing, which was confirmation.

"The last true copy of it. Before my memory finishes ruining it." Her voice stayed level. She was proud of that, in a distant, sick way. "That's the fare."

"Hazel, no." Ilex's hand closed on her arm. "Listen to me. Whatever you think you're trading, you're trading more. She's the reason you're doing any of this. Page one. Her signature. If you can't see her face when you read it—"

"Then I'll read it as a stranger reads it," Hazel said. "Maybe that's better. Maybe strangers read contracts more honestly than daughters do."

"That's not—"

"How much do you remember of your mother, Ilex?"

He went silent. The silence had a shape, and the shape was an answer.

"Exactly," Hazel said softly. "So either you're asking me to keep something you lost without falling apart, or you're asking me to be luckier than you were. Which is it?"

She stepped forward before he could answer, before she could lose her nerve, and held out her hand to the ferryman palm-up, the way one presents a seal, a signet, a signature.

"Take it," she said. "But hear the terms. I'm a clerk of the Vellum Guild, and we don't pay blind. You get the memory of her face — the image, the look of her. You don't get her voice. You don't get her hands. You don't get the smell of the ink she used or the sound of her laughing at her own jokes before she finished them. Face only. One door, not the whole house."

The ferryman's stillness stretched. Somewhere across the water, a bird forgot itself mid-song and the silence where its note had been rang louder than the note.

"You drive a hard bargain," he said, "for someone buying passage."

"I learned from the best creditors in the business. Nowhere better to learn than a guild that trades in impossible things." She kept her hand out. Her arm was shaking. She hoped he couldn't tell. "Terms accepted?"

"Terms accepted." He reached out — and his hand, when it closed over hers, was warm. That was the worst part. She had expected cold, grave-cold, river-bottom cold. Instead it was warm as a kitchen, warm as a hand that had once guided hers across a page, teaching her letters.

"Oh," Hazel said, because understanding arrived a half-second too late, and then the memory left.

It didn't hurt. That was the second worst part. It was like a page turning — a small pressure, a whisper of paper, and then the next page, and the face was gone. Not blurred. Not faded. *Gone*, the way a word is gone after erasure, leaving the ghost of indentation only. She reached for it and found smooth blankness where the portrait had hung, and around the blankness, horribly, everything else remained: her mother's voice, her mother's hands, the ink-smell, the laugh. A house with one room emptied. She could walk through every other room and feel the absence pressing through the walls.

"Well?" Ilex's voice, far away.

"She had," Hazel began, and stopped. Tried again. "She was—" Nothing. Her mother was a voice now, and hands, and a signature. A woman described entirely in everything except the one thing children are supposed to carry forever. "I don't know what she looked like," Hazel said, and heard her own voice crack down the middle like a binding breaking. "Ilex, I don't know what my own mother looked like."

"I know," he said quietly. "I'm sorry. I'm so sorry."

The boat moved on. The orange stain on the horizon grew.

* * *

Ilex watched her stand at the bow with her arms wrapped around herself, guarding warmth that was no longer there, and he could not make himself go to her.

This was the thing about debts: they compounded. He had known that longer than she had, in the way that men who have worked for the Court know things — in their teeth, in the scar tissue behind the eyes. He had audited erasures. He had signed off on them, stamped them, filed them. Countries, yes. But also smaller holdings. A village's festival. A family's crest. A woman's—

He flinched. Actually flinched, a full-body stutter, and covered it by adjusting his cloak.

Because he had seen that face. Not Hazel's mother — he'd never met the woman, he was nearly certain, nearly — but the *face*. When the ferryman had lifted it out of Hazel like a splinter drawn from flesh, the memory had passed through the space between them, the way heat passes, the way a secret passes in a quiet room, and for one instant Ilex had stood inside someone else's remembering: a kitchen, rain on glass, a woman looking up from a ledger of her own with ink on her cheekbone, smiling at a child who had just read her first full sentence aloud.

Ink on the cheekbone. Left side. Exactly where Hazel touched herself without knowing it.

*I know that smile,* he thought. *I have seen that smile.* Not in a file. Not in an audit. Somewhere real, somewhere with rain in it, somewhere that should have been erased along with everything else and yet persisted in him like a watermark — visible only when held up to the right light.

He was the Court's former auditor. He knew what watermarks meant. They meant the document had been copied, and copies meant originals, and originals meant that somewhere in the vast burned archive of the Ember Court there was a record of a woman with ink on her cheek who had signed away a sky — and that Ilex had stood in that archive, had handled those papers, had perhaps looked into that smiling face on some long-ago centennial and thought nothing of it, the way one thinks nothing of a stranger, until the stranger's daughter boarded a ferry and paid for passage with her.

Or worse. Or infinitely worse: that the recognition ran the other direction. That the woman in the memory had known *him*.

"Hazel," he said, and his voice came out wrong, scraped.

She didn't turn. "We're almost at the ash line. I can feel the ledger warming."

"There's something I need to tell you. About the fare. About what I saw when it—"

"Don't." Now she turned, and her eyes were dry and terrible, the eyes of a clerk balancing a column that refuses to sum. "If you tell me you recognized her, then I have to ask how, and if you tell me how, then either you're lying to spare me or you're not lying, and I can't afford either answer today. Eight days, Ilex. In eight days I stand in front of a parliament of fire and refuse consent on behalf of a dead woman whose face I sold for a boat ride. Let me have the eight days. Then you can tell me everything, and I promise you—" her hand found the ledger at her ribs, gripped it, "—I will read every word."

The river bent south. The orange light strengthened until it lay across the water like spilled coals, and the ferryman poled them steadily into it, and Ilex stood in the stern with a stranger's memory lodged behind his eyes like a splinter of his own, saying nothing, keeping the eighth day's promise in advance — because some debts, he knew, you paid before they came due, and some faces you carried whether or not anyone had ever written them down.

Ahead, where the water met the glow, something rose out of the river mist: a gate, or the idea of one, built of stacked and blackened treaties, its arch inscribed with words that were already burning away.

The ferryman brought them alongside and held out his hand — not for thanks. For the last small thing neither of them knew they'd forgotten.

Ilex paid it without looking. Hazel never learned what it was.

The gate opened like a book.
