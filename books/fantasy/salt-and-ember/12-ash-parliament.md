## 12. Ash Parliament

The Ember Court announced itself by smell before it showed itself at all: woodsmoke and hot iron, the scent of a library burning somewhere forever.

Hazel smelled it from the ridge, three days out, when the terrain was still pretending to be hills. By the time they crested the last rise on the morning of the fourth day, the smell had become a taste — she could feel it coating her teeth, fine as ash sifted through a ledger's spine. And there it stood.

She had imagined a palace. She had imagined, in her worst hours, something like the Vellum Guild's hall scaled up to monstrous size — marble and gilt, clerks in rows, the machinery of commerce wearing ceremony like a mask. What she saw instead made her stop walking.

The citadel was built of treaties.

Not built *like* treaties. Built *of* them. From a distance it looked like black stone, but as they descended the switchback road Hazel could see the truth in the walls' texture: strata. Layers upon layers of burned documents, compressed into something harder than granite, each layer a different shade of char — pale grey where the paper had been young, deep lacquered black where it had been old, and here and there veins of gold that caught the light and held it wrong. She leaned close to one exposed seam near the road and understood what the gold was. Gilded edges. The decorative edging on pages that had been worth enough to gild before someone decided they were worth more burned.

"How many," she said. Her voice came out flat.

Ilex didn't pretend not to understand. "No one counts anymore. The oldest layers predate counting." He walked with his hood up and his hands in his sleeves, and he had barely spoken since the ferry. "They don't build the Court out of treaties because they destroyed them. They build it out of treaties because that's all a contract is, once it's honored. Fuel."

"Once it's *paid*," Hazel corrected.

"Is there a difference?" He glanced back at her, and for a moment the hood shadowed everything but the scar along his jaw — the one he'd never explained, the one shaped exactly like a signature gone wrong. "You've read enough contracts, Pemberton. When did anyone ever pay one honestly?"

She had no answer, so she kept walking, and the citadel grew until it filled the sky, and then they were inside its shadow, and the shadow was warm.

* * *

The gates were not guarded. That frightened her more than guards would have.

Instead, at the threshold, stood a brazier the size of a wagon, and rising out of the coals — unhurried, like steam given posture — was a figure made entirely of slow fire. It had no face she could fix on. Where a face should be there was a shifting brightness, and when it spoke, its voice was the sound of a page turning over flame.

"Purpose."

Hazel had rehearsed this. She had rehearsed it for two days while Ilex drilled her, and still her mouth went dry, because guild law had taught her one thing above all others: never lie to a thing that can hear your heartbeat.

"Records-clerk," she said. "Second grade, provisional. Sent up from the downriver offices." She produced the letter of introduction Ilex had forged — or rather, *restored*, from a genuine template he claimed to remember from his old life, its ink aged with tea and smoke until it smelled convincingly of bureaucratic patience. "The sitting requires supplementary archivists. My office received the summons."

The fire-thing considered her. Heat pressed against her skin like a hand.

"The downriver offices were consolidated," it said.

Here it was. The moment Ilex had warned her about. *"They'll test you with a fact. Not a big one. A small one. Small facts are how institutions check whether you're real."*

Hazel let herself look tired — which took no acting at all — and sighed the sigh of a clerk who has been dealing with reorganizations since before the fire-thing learned to speak. "Consolidated twice. First under Provost Anselm, then again after the audit irregularities. My letterhead's out of date; I know. Everyone's letterhead is out of date. If you want to turn me away over stationery, turn me away."

Silence. The coals ticked.

Then the brightness where its face should be flickered — and Hazel would have sworn, later, writing it down in storm-light by a campfire's stolen glow, that the flicker looked almost like amusement.

"Second grade, provisional," it repeated. "You'll report to the Ash Registry, sublevel two. Provisional clerks do not enter the Sitting Floor. Provisional clerks do not touch sealed folios. Provisional clerks who ask questions are reassigned." A pause, delicate as ash falling. "Reassignment here is permanent."

"I've been reassigned before," Hazel said. "It didn't take."

The brazier flared once, high and bright, and when it settled the fire-thing was gone, and the gates stood open, and Hazel walked through them on legs that felt borrowed.

Behind her, Ilex murmured, barely a breath: "Where did you learn to do that?"

"Guildmaster Vey," she said. "He taught me that the best defense against an institution is to sound like you've already survived worse ones." She didn't add the rest: *and I learned it again the day he sold me.* Some lessons you keep in a locked drawer.

* * *

Sublevel two of the Ash Registry was a cavern the size of a cathedral, and it was full of clerks.

That was the first horror. Hazel had expected monsters. What she found instead was ordinary people — hundreds of them, human and nearly-human and some things she couldn't classify, seated at desks carved from the same treaty-stone as the walls, copying documents by lamplight. Quills scratched in unison, thousands of nibs moving in a rhythm so synchronized it sounded like rain. No one spoke. No one looked up. The lamplight was the color of dying embers, and every face beneath it wore the same expression Hazel knew intimately, because she had worn it herself for eleven years in the Guild's cellar archives: the expression of competence used as a lid over grief.

"They're not prisoners," Ilex breathed beside her. He had gotten them both assigned to adjacent desks with a word and a credential-seal he'd produced from somewhere inside his coat, and now he stood very still, taking in the room like a man reading a will. "Look at their collars."

She looked. Each clerk wore a thin band of dull metal at the throat. Not chains — bands, fitted, almost gentle. And stamped into each band, too small to read at a distance, was a clause.

"They're debtors," Hazel said quietly.

"They're *payments*." Ilex's voice had gone strange. "This is what honoring a contract looks like, down here, where no one reads the fine print. You owe a debt you can't pay in coin, so you pay in years of service. In attention. In the slow copying of documents that exist only to be copied." He sat down heavily at his assigned desk. "I audited this place. I signed off on these numbers. I wrote 'personnel costs stable' in my reports, and I never once came down here to see what personnel meant."

Hazel pulled out her chair and began, with hands that wanted to shake and therefore must not, to work.

The work itself was simple, which was the second horror. Copy ledgers. Verify totals. Transcribe renewal notices. She had done harder things before breakfast for most of her adult life. But every document that passed across her desk was a fragment of the same vast machine, and piece by piece, entry by entry, the machine assembled itself in her mind:

A renewal notice for the sky of a country called Merrow-Fen, due next century, terms unchanged. A receipt for forty years of springtime, purchased from somewhere whose name had been redacted even from the receipt. A memo, dry as dust, noting that the erasure of a certain northern nation had cost more than projected and recommending, in future, that erasures be scheduled during harvest festivals when public attention was low.

Public attention. As if countries had neighbors who might notice. As if the world were a street of houses and the Court had been going door to door for centuries, buying homes and burning the deeds and telling everyone the lots had always been empty.

Her quill moved. Her face stayed smooth. Inside, something cold and enormous turned over, like a leviathan waking under ice.

* * *

At the end of the first shift, a bell rang — a small, clear note — and every clerk in the registry rose in unison and filed toward the doors. Hazel followed, matching the rhythm, eyes down, one gray-robed body among hundreds.

But at the corridor's end, the stream of clerks split. Most went left, toward barracks and meal-halls. A few — marked by brass pins on their collars — went right, toward a stair that climbed, and above the stair hung a sign in letters of hammered copper:

**SITTING FLOOR — AUTHORIZED PERSONNEL ONLY**

And past the sign, through a doorway left briefly open, Hazel saw it.

The chamber where the fire ministers debated.

It was round, and vast, and its floor was a bed of coals that glowed and dimmed in patterns — and she realized with a lurch that the patterns weren't random. They were *writing*. The floor itself was a ledger, miles wide, recording every debate in cinders that bloomed and faded and bloomed again. Around its rim rose tiers of seats, and in the seats sat figures of fire in a dozen different temperaments — white-hot and furious, banked and patient, one in the far tier that burned blue and never moved at all.

Their voices reached her as a murmur, a parliament's hum, and threaded through the hum she caught fragments:

"—the centennial sits in nine days—"

"—the Corvale file remains closed, the witness remains scattered—"

"—renewal is formality, renewal has always been formality—"

A door closed. The fragments cut off. Hazel stood in the corridor with her heart slamming against her ribs and her face arranged into the blank patience of a woman waiting for a meal tray.

Nine days.

*Clause and counter-clause,* she thought, and the thought was a hand closing around a weapon. *Renewal requires consent. Consent requires the debtor. The debtor's daughter is standing in your registry, copying your receipts, and you told me myself: provisional clerks who ask questions get reassigned.*

So she wouldn't ask questions.

She would copy everything.

* * *

That night, in the narrow clerk's cell they'd assigned her — a stone room with a straw pallet and a lamp that never quite went out — Hazel took out the one ledger she'd saved from the Guild's burning and opened it to a fresh page.

She had no storm-light here. The Court's lamps burned with fire that remembered nothing; she'd tested it on the first evening, holding a scrap of her special ink-paper to the flame, and the paper had behaved like any paper, dumb and mortal. So she wrote in ordinary ink, in the tiny ciphered shorthand she'd invented at fourteen and never taught another living soul, and she recorded the day: the gate, the collars, the coal-floor that wrote, the nine days, the blue-burning minister who never moved.

When she finished, she sat for a long time listening to the citadel breathe around her. The walls of burned treaties whispered at night — she'd noticed it on her first walk through the corridors, and Ilex had pretended not to hear, and neither of them had mentioned it since. Faint voices, layered like sediment. Words half-consumed. Somewhere in the stone around her, a king's ultimatum was still finishing its final syllable, four hundred years late.

She put her palm flat against the wall.

"I'm here," she whispered to whatever was in there. "I'm keeping accounts."

The whispering didn't change. But it didn't stop either, and somehow that felt like an answer.

Across the cell, folded into her spare robe, lay the forged letter of introduction, and beneath it the credential-seal Ilex had lent her, and beneath *that* — wrapped in oilcloth, carried against her ribs since the river — the oldest folio's copied pages. Her mother's signature. *The sky of Corvale, in perpetuity.* Page one waited for her somewhere in this mountain of ash, and in nine days the whole apparatus of the Court would gather on that coal-written floor to renew what her mother had sold.

Hazel blew out her lamp, lay down on the straw, and closed her eyes.

Sleep came slowly, and when it came, it brought the registry with it — thousands of quills scratching in unison, and beneath the scratching, so faint she might have dreamed it, a new sound threading up through sublevel two, through the treaty-stone, through the sediment of burned words:

Someone, somewhere in the citadel, was asking for her by name.

*Pemberton,* the whisper said. *The Pemberton girl is in the building.*
