## 18. Recruiting the Forgotten

*Hazel*

The village of Ashenmere did not appear on any map, which was precisely why Hazel trusted it.

It sat in a fold of country that had forgotten its own name twice — she could see the seams where the forgetting had happened, little stutter-lines in the hedgerows, a lane that ended in a field and began again on the other side as if it had never noticed the gap. The people here moved with the careful, half-listening quality of those who had learned that their memories were not entirely their own. They greeted her by name. That was the residue of Corvale, leaking through the world's erasure like water through bad mortar. Somewhere in the deep ledgers of things, Hazel Pemberton was still owed.

She had come to Ashenmere because Ilex's old Court credentials — the ones he'd burned rather than surrender — had listed three anomalies in this region. Anomalies, in the Court's accounting language, meant *people who should have been erased but weren't*. People like her. People whose existence had slipped a clause, or been paid for, or simply refused to dissolve.

The first anomaly kept a shop at the end of the high street with no sign above the door, because a sign would have named it, and naming things here invited attention.

Hazel pushed the door open. Bells that weren't bells — more like the memory of bells — chimed somewhere behind her ears.

Inside, maps covered every surface. Not finished maps; maps in progress, maps being argued with, maps pinned at the corners and weighted with river stones. A woman stood at a drafting table with her sleeves rolled to the elbow, ink to her wrists, drawing a coastline that Hazel recognized with a jolt that went through her like cold iron.

That was the Corvale coast. Every inlet. Every shingle bar. Drawn from nothing, from nowhere, by someone who had never seen it.

"You're the Pemberton girl," the woman said without looking up. "The one who glows."

"I'm told I do." Hazel kept her voice level. "You're the cartographer."

"Merrow Voss." She set down her pen and finally looked up. Her eyes were the pale gray of paper left too long in weather. "Formerly Cartographer Royal of a country that, according to everyone but me, never existed. I mapped Corvale for eleven years. Coastlines, roads, the watershed of the Alder. And then one morning I woke up and my life's work was fiction." She tapped the coastline she'd been drawing. "So I keep redrawing it. From memory. Every day. Because if I stop, I think it stops."

"It doesn't stop," Hazel said. She unslung the ledger from her shoulder and laid it on the drafting table, open to a page of storm-light script. "It's all still there. It's waiting for someone to give it back its shape."

Merrow looked at the glowing lines for a long moment. Then she laughed — a short, cracked sound, the laugh of someone who had stopped expecting anything.

"Do you know what I did when they erased it?" she said. "I went to the Guild. I offered everything I owned for one bottle of my own remembering. One jar. Enough to prove I wasn't mad." Her jaw tightened. "They told me the inventory didn't include any such item. Which means either your Guild sold my country's memory to the Court, or it never stocked it at all. Either way, clerk, I have very little reason to love the Vellum Guild."

"No," Hazel agreed. "You don't. But I'm not here selling. I'm here collecting debts." She turned the ledger so Merrow could read the clause-path she and Ilex had traced across nine days of travel — the chain of obligations running from a mother's signature to a parliament of fire. "The Ember Court bought Corvale's sky. To make them give it back, I need witnesses. People the contracts touched. People whose testimony has weight because they were supposed to be nothing."

"And if we fail?"

"Then we fail loudly, in front of the whole Court, and they have to erase us properly this time. With paperwork." Hazel met her eyes. "I've been erased once already. It's overrated."

Merrow Voss studied her for a long time. Then she began rolling up her maps, one after another, with the brisk economy of a woman who had packed for exile many times before.

"The second anomaly," she said. "The soldier. He drinks at the Ferryman's Rest and tells anyone who'll listen about a war nobody remembers. They think he's mad. He isn't. I've checked his details against mine — his war happened inside Corvale's borders. He fought for a country that no longer exists, in a battle that no longer exists, for wounds that no longer exist." She slung her map-case over her shoulder. "He'll want to come. Men like him only have one thing left, and it's the same thing we have."

"What's that?"

"A grievance with excellent documentation."

* * *

*Tobias*

Tobias Crane had learned to measure time in pints, because hours had stopped meaning anything to a man whose war had been unwritten.

He sat in the corner of the Ferryman's Rest where he always sat, back to the wall, facing the door, because eleven years of soldiering didn't evaporate just because the soldiers had. The locals gave him space. They thought him a madman or a liar, and Tobias had long ago decided that both were easier to be than what he actually was: a veteran of the Battle of Redford Bridge, decorated twice, wounded once badly enough that he still couldn't feel two fingers of his left hand — a wound the healers called congenital, because there had never been a battle to wound him in.

He was telling the story again. He always told the story. The barmaid, a patient girl with a forgettable face, nodded in the right places.

"—and the bridge held," he said, "because Captain Ellery ordered the wagons chained across it, and we held behind them for six hours against—"

"Against the Ninth Column of the Ember Court's auxiliary forces."

Tobias turned so fast his stool scraped. A woman stood in the doorway — dark-haired, travel-worn, carrying a ledger that faintly *glowed*, which under normal circumstances would have made him question the ale. Beside her, an older man with a scholar's stoop and a stranger's eyes. And behind them, unmistakably, the Cartographer Royal, whom Tobias had last seen pinning maps to the war-tent wall while artillery shook the pegs loose.

"Merrow," he said stupidly. "You remember it too."

"Every contour," she said.

The dark-haired woman crossed the room and sat down across from him without asking. Up close, he could see the glow came off the ledger's pages like heat-shimmer, like light trapped in paper.

"Tobias Crane," she said. "Sergeant, Third Corvale Foot. You held Redford Bridge for six hours and lost forty-one men, and afterward the Court unwrote the battle, and the war, and the army, and left you sitting in a pub telling a story no one believes." She opened the ledger. "I believe it. Look."

He looked. Storm-light script crawled across the page, and where it crawled, images formed — not pictures exactly, but *rememberings*, the way a dream remembers a thing. A bridge. Chained wagons. Smoke. A young corporal named Denny Fell whose head came apart beside him, and the terrible quiet afterward, and the medal pressed into his hand by a captain who—

"Who is also gone," Tobias said. His voice came out strange. Flat. The flatness of a man standing on ground that has just shifted. "Captain Ellery. Where is he in your book?"

"He's not in it yet," the woman said. "That's why I'm here. The Court erased thousands of us, Crane. Thousands of countries, thousands of wars, thousands of bridges. In nine days they sit down to renew the contract that erased ours — and I intend to stand up in front of their entire parliament and read the truth into their own record. But testimony needs witnesses. It needs names. It needs a sergeant who can say *I was there, I bled there, here are the forty-one.*" She leaned forward. "Are you coming, Sergeant? Or are you going to finish your pint and keep telling your story to people who've been taught to disbelieve it?"

Tobias Crane looked at his pint. He looked at his two dead fingers, which had died at Redford Bridge whether the world admitted it or not. He thought about forty-one names he had carried alone for years, reciting them like prayers to a god who'd been deleted.

He stood up, drained the pint, and put a coin on the table that the barmaid would later swear had never existed.

"Forty-two," he corrected. "You forgot the corporal. Denny Fell. Everyone forgets Denny." He picked up his coat. "Nobody's forgetting him again."

*Ilex*

Ilex watched them gather in the taproom of the Ferryman's Rest — the cartographer rolling maps with hands that trembled only slightly, the soldier reciting names under his breath like ammunition being counted — and felt something he had not felt since his defection, something he had assumed had been burned out of him along with everything else.

Hope was a dangerous asset. He had audited enough revolutions, from the comfortable distance of the Court's ledgers, to know how they were usually entered: as expenditures, written off, closed.

But this was different. This was bookkeeping.

That was the thing the fire ministers had never understood, in all their centuries of cinders and purchased skies — the thing Ilex himself hadn't understood until he read Hazel's storm-light pages and felt the whole architecture of his certainty collapse. Power wrote itself down. Contracts, clauses, signatures, renewals. The Court ruled through its records, which meant the Court could be beaten through them. Every empire of paper carried the instrument of its own audit.

And here was the auditor's dream walking around in a clerk's body: a woman whose ink remembered, gathering witnesses the way other revolutionaries gathered weapons.

He counted heads. Hazel. Merrow Voss, whose maps could give the debt a shape. Tobias Crane, whose testimony could give it blood. Two more anomalies waited on Ilex's list — a bell-ringer from a town that had been rung out of existence, and a midwife who remembered delivering children to mothers who now had no record of bearing them. Small people. Erased people. The Court's arithmetic treated them as rounding errors.

Rounding errors, Ilex knew, were how great ledgers failed.

"Hazel," he said quietly, drawing her aside near the window, where the light of a sky that belonged to no one fell gray on her face. "A word before we march."

"You look like a man about to read terms and conditions."

"I'm always that man." He glanced back at the others. "When we reach the capital, the Court will offer you something. Understand that going in. They won't meet testimony with force if they can meet it with a bargain. They will offer you Corvale back — your name cleared, your exile lifted, your mother's good name restored — in exchange for the ledgers. Quietly. Generously. And it will be tempting, because it will be almost true."

"How almost?"

"Almost enough to break most people." He held her gaze. "I signed away four countries before I understood what I was signing. Don't let them put your signature on anything in that building. Not even a receipt."

Hazel was quiet for a moment. Outside, the first of the evening stars should have been appearing, and did not, and the absence of them had become a kind of companionship — everyone in the erased lands lived beneath the same blank ceiling, and it bound them.

"Ilex," she said finally. "Why are you really doing this? You defected before you ever met me. Before the ledgers. What did you see?"

He considered lying, found he didn't have the energy, and gave her the smallest true piece of it.

"There was a country called Vellichor," he said. "Green hills. Good cheese. A bell tower that rang wrong on purpose, because the founder believed perfection invited envy from heaven. I verified its erasure personally. Signed the certificate. And on my way out, an old woman stopped me in the road and asked me — very politely, the way you'd ask a neighbor — whether the bells would ring tomorrow." His throat worked. "And I said yes. And I went home and could not sleep for a year, because I knew they wouldn't, and I knew that I was the reason, and I knew that somewhere in the Court's vaults there was a certificate with my name on it saying that green hills and good cheese and a bell tower had never existed at all."

Hazel put her hand briefly on his arm. It was the touch of a clerk confirming a figure — precise, professional, and somehow more comforting than any embrace.

"Then you'll get your bells back too," she said.

Before he could answer, the door of the Ferryman's Rest banged open, and the evening changed.

* * *

*Sable*

She had worn many faces in her long service, but tonight she wore her own, which was the rarest and the cruelest.

The ministers wanted the Pemberton girl located before the centennial sitting — quietly, cleanly, no ash on the carpets. So Sable had followed the residue, the way she always did: the places where erased things leaked. Villages that greeted strangers by name. Lanes with stutter-lines. And now a tavern full of ghosts, glowing softly in the gray evening like coals refusing to die.

Through the window she watched them: the clerk with her impossible book. The cartographer. The soldier mouthing names. The traitor auditor, Ilex, leaning close, wearing his guilt like a coat that no longer fit.

Nine days until the renewal. Nine days until the debt rolled over for another century, and the blank ceiling stayed blank, and the bells stayed silent, and Sable went on wearing lives that were not hers because the Court had hollowed hers out so long ago she could no longer remember what color her own sky had been.

She knew what she was supposed to do. Report the location. Let the ministers send collectors.

Instead she stood in the road outside the Ferryman's Rest, in a body that was only nominally hers, and watched the small congregation of the forgotten through glass, and felt the oldest thing she had felt in decades stir somewhere beneath the ash.

She went in.

The door banged. Conversation died. The soldier's hand went to a sword-belt that no longer existed, out of habit older than his erasure. And the girl — Pemberton, the storm-clerk, the witness who wouldn't die — turned and looked at her, and Sable watched recognition move across her face like weather.

"You," Hazel said. "I've seen your shape before. Wearing mine."

"Yes," Sable said. Her voice came out rusted. She had planned a dozen openings and used none of them. "You have. And I've come to make you an offer — and before you refuse it, clerk, you should know that I am the only person in the world who can tell you what your mother's last words were. Because I was there. And I'm the only person alive who remembers them."

Behind her, through the window, the first star in nine years flickered — briefly, uncertainly — above a country that did not exist.

Everyone in the room saw it. No one breathed.
