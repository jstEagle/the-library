## 20. The Ferryman's Price Again

The river found them before they found it.

One moment the road was a sheep track threading through hills that could not decide whether to be hills or coastline — grass giving way to shingle, then back again, as if the land were turning over in its sleep — and the next, water lay across their path like a sentence neither of them had finished writing.

It was wide. It was gray. It went exactly where they needed to go and nowhere else, which was the river's way of being generous.

"There," Hazel said.

The ferryman's boat was already midstream, though she could have sworn the water had been empty a heartbeat ago. The boat did not approach so much as *arrive*, the way a word arrives on a page: suddenly, inevitably, with the sense that it had always been there waiting for someone to read it.

The ferryman stood at the tiller with his pole resting against the current as if the current owed him money. He was exactly as Hazel remembered him, which troubled her, because she had paid him with the memory of her mother's face, and she had expected the remembering itself to feel thinner now. It didn't. Grief, apparently, kept its own ledgers.

"You're back," he said. His voice was the sound a rope makes when it goes taut. "Nobody comes back."

"We need to cross again." Ilex stepped up beside her, hood back, hands open the way you hold them for a customs officer. "Same direction. Further."

"Further costs more."

Hazel had known he would say that. She had rehearsed the conversation on the road for two days, walking through the terrain that kept forgetting itself, past villages that greeted her by a name she was beginning to suspect wasn't entirely hers anymore. She had rehearsed it because she wanted to be the one who named the price first, so that it would feel like her decision and not his demand.

"The sitting is in six days," she said. "You know that. You know what's on that river. What do you want?"

The ferryman looked at her for a long time. Water moved under his boat without moving past it.

"I don't set prices," he said. "I collect what's owed. Passage is a debt like any other, and debts are paid in kind. Last time you paid me a face." He tilted his head, studying her with eyes like two holes cut in fog. "I've carried kings who paid me their coronations. Soldiers who paid me the war they survived. A woman once gave me the sound of her daughter laughing, and every night since, when the fog comes down, I hear it. I am very rich, clerk. And very poor. That's what the work makes you."

"Then name it," Hazel said. "And I'll tell you if I can pay."

He turned the pole slowly, and the whole gray world seemed to turn with it.

"You already know," he said gently, and that gentleness frightened her more than any price could have. "You felt it the moment you saw the river. That's why you stopped talking. That's why your friend there keeps glancing at you like a man watching a candle gutter." He nodded at Ilex. "I'll take why you became a clerk."

* * *

The silence afterward lasted long enough for the hills behind them to change their minds twice.

"Why *that*?" Ilex said finally, low and sharp. "Take mine. Take my — take anything. I was an auditor for a parliament of fire; my head is full of things worth having."

"I don't want your things." The ferryman didn't even look at him. "Passage isn't bought with weight. It's bought with fit. Her crossing needs a particular coin, the same way last time did. Why does a person become a keeper of other people's debts?" He smiled, or the fog arranged itself into something adjacent to smiling. "That's the shape of what she owes. That's the shape of what the river wants."

"That's the shape of *her*," Ilex said.

"Yes," said the ferryman. "Now you understand pricing."

Hazel stood very still. The wind off the water smelled of salt and cold iron and, faintly, of vellum — of the archive rooms where she had spent eleven years cataloguing impossible things with steady hands and a straight back, telling herself that precision was a form of devotion.

She tried to remember why she'd started.

And that was when the terror came, because the answer was still there — she could feel it, a warm coal somewhere behind her ribs — but it had gone strange and far away, like a room in a house she hadn't visited since childhood. She knew the door was there. She could no longer quite remember what was behind it.

"It's already fading," she said. Her voice came out level, and she was proud of that, and ashamed of how proud. "Isn't it. The moment you named it, it started going."

"They all do," the ferryman said. "Memory knows when it's been sold. It packs its bags."

"Hazel." Ilex caught her arm. Up close his eyes were the color of banked coals, and for the first time since the ash roads began she saw real fear in them — fear *for* her, which was worse than fear of anything. "Don't. We can find another way around. Downriver. Overland. There's always another—"

"There isn't." She said it quietly. "You know the arithmetic better than anyone. Six days. One river. And a Court full of ministers who will renew a hundred contracts by rote unless someone stands on their floor with proof." She looked down at the ledger strapped across her chest, the one surviving book, storm-light faintly pulsing along its spine like a sleeping pulse. "Everything I'm carrying is worthless if I'm too late. And everything I am—" she laughed, short and bitter, "*everything I am has been getting cheaper the whole way here.*"

"That's not true."

"No? Let's count." She held up her fingers, clerk-fashion, an old habit that made her own gesture feel like someone else's. "A mother's face. Gone. My reason for becoming what I am — about to go. Before that, my homeland, erased from the world. Before that, my name, struck from my guild. I am being paid out of myself, Ilex, installment by installment, and every payment buys a little more road toward the people who took Corvale." She closed her fist. "So let me spend the rest well."

The ferryman waited. He was very good at waiting. It occurred to Hazel, distantly, that waiting was probably the only thing he had left of his own.

"I want to know one thing first," she said. "What do you *do* with them? The memories. The faces. The laughter."

For the first time, the ferryman looked uncomfortable — a small crack in the fog, quickly smoothed.

"I keep them safe," he said. "That's the bargain nobody reads. You think I devour what I'm given. I don't. I carry it. Every soul who pays me leaves something aboard, and nothing leaves until—" He stopped himself, jaw tightening, and Hazel understood with a lurch that he had almost said something the river forbade him to say.

"Until what?"

"Until it's claimed," he said. "Or until the debt that took it dies. Debts die, clerk. Even the oldest ones. Especially the oldest ones, if someone refuses to renew them." His eyes flicked to the ledger on her chest, and there — just for a heartbeat — was something that might have been hope, wearing the mask of indifference. "Cross, pay your coin, and remember that. What's sold can sometimes be bought back. If the contract breaks."

Hazel breathed out slowly.

"All right," she said. "All right. I'll pay."

* * *

She did it standing up, on the shingle, facing him, because she refused to make it look like a surrender.

"How does it work?" she asked. "Last time I don't remember choosing. I just woke up poorer."

"Because last time you fought it without knowing you were fighting. This time, give it freely. Freely given comes out cleaner." He extended one hand, palm up. It was a workingman's hand, scarred across the knuckles, and she wondered distantly whose hands those had originally been. "Think of it. All of it. The first day. Hold the whole of it in your mind and hand it over like a parcel."

Hazel closed her eyes.

And the room came back to her — the room behind the childhood door, the one that had been going strange and far away — and she grabbed it with both hands.

She was nineteen years old, and rain was coming through the roof of the poorhouse infirmary, and an old woman was dying badly, not from her wound but from the paperwork of her wound, because no record existed of the regiment that had abandoned her and therefore, officially, no wound had ever happened and no medicine need ever arrive. Hazel — nineteen, furious, holding a bowl of water she couldn't make matter — had watched a Vellum Guild clerk walk in out of the rain and write the old woman's regiment back into existence. Just ink. Just a ledger and a steady hand and three sentences in a fair copyist's script, and by morning there was medicine, because officially the woman had never been abandoned, and the world bent around what the books said.

Hazel had followed the clerk out into the rain and asked her how, and the clerk had said, *the Guild trades in impossible things, girl, but somebody has to keep the accounts honest,* and Hazel had thought: *that. That is the weapon. Not fire. Not storms. Ink.*

She had walked into the Guild offices the next morning, soaked to the bone, and asked for work, and the intake master had asked why, and she had said—

She had said—

The words were going. She could feel them lifting out of her like heat off stone, and she reached for them and they weren't cruel about it, that was the terrible thing, they simply left, the way a tide leaves, politely, completely, taking the whole shape of the shore with it. *Why did you come here?* the intake master had asked, and she opened her mouth in the memory and there was nothing — a nineteen-year-old girl standing in the rain with no reason, and the reason was already in the ferryman's hand, packed and labeled, and—

"*Enough,*" Ilex said, and his hand closed on her shoulder, anchoring her to the present, to the shingle and the gray light and the smell of salt. "Let go. It's done. Breathe."

Hazel opened her eyes.

The ferryman's palm was closed now, and inside the closed fist something glowed faintly — a small warm light, amber-colored, like lamplight through a window seen from a rainy street. He tucked it away inside his coat with the care of a man pocketing a bird's egg.

"Well?" she asked. Her voice sounded fine. That was the horror of it. Everything sounded fine.

"What do you remember?" the ferryman asked. "Of why."

She searched. It was the strangest sensation of her life, stranger than watching Corvale vanish from the maps: she knew she had a reason. She knew there had been a day, a rain, an old woman, a choice. The scaffolding of the memory stood intact. But the room inside it was empty, swept clean, and when she tried to walk into it she felt only a vast, patient absence — grief with no object, conviction with no content. She believed she had become a clerk for a good reason. She could no longer tell you what it was. She could no longer even tell whether believing it counted.

"I remember that I had one," she said at last.

The ferryman nodded, and something in his ruined eyes looked, briefly, unbearably sorry.

"That's how it always goes," he said. "People think they lose the memory. They lose the *why*. The rest is just furniture." He gestured with his pole. "Aboard, both of you. The river's in a mood today, and we've far to go."

* * *

The crossing was unlike the first.

Last time, the water had been merely wrong — flowing backward in places, running uphill in others, carrying reflections of skies that belonged to no country. This time it was fast. The boat moved like something summoned, the gray banks unspooling past them, and the ferryman stood at the tiller without seeming to steer, and Hazel sat in the stern with her arms around the ledger and tried to take inventory of herself.

It was a clerk's exercise. She made it a clerk's exercise deliberately, because procedure was a rope you could hold onto when the floor went out from under you.

Name: Hazel Pemberton. She checked. Still there.

Country: Corvale. Still hers, still erased, still burning in her chest like a coal wrapped in cloth.

Purpose: reach the Ember Court before the centennial sitting. Refuse the renewal. Read the contract into the record. Restore what was taken.

Reasons for purpose: —

The blank sat where the reasons should be, and it did not hurt the way she had expected. That was worst of all. It didn't hurt. It was just smooth, like a page torn cleanly out, and she kept running her thumb along the tear's edge looking for blood and finding none.

Beside her, Ilex watched her sidelong. He had been watching her since the shingle, and she realized he was doing his own inventory — of her.

"Ask," she said.

"Does it feel different? Being without it?"

"Yes." She considered. "Lighter. Which frightens me. Lightness is what the Court sells, isn't it? Informed consent manufactured through edited memory. Ministers signing away countries because the weight of them has been quietly removed." She looked down at her own hands, steady on the ledger's cover. "Every mile, I'm becoming easier to persuade. I can feel it. Like ballast coming off a boat."

"Then I'll be your ballast," Ilex said simply. "I'll carry the remembering-for-you. Your mother's face — you described it to me on the ash road, before the ferryman took it. Dark eyes. A scar through the left eyebrow she got falling out of an apple tree. A laugh she tried to smother because she thought it was undignified." His voice roughened. "I wrote it down that night. In my own book. So if you ever need it back—"

"You can't give it back. It's not yours to give."

"No," he admitted. "But I can remind you that it existed. That *you* existed. Someone should keep the account." He glanced forward at the ferryman's broad gray back. "Apparently it's contagious, this work."

Ahead, the ferryman said nothing. But Hazel noticed — because noticing was the one faculty the payments hadn't touched, and she clung to it — that his free hand had drifted to his coat, to the pocket where a small amber light burned, and rested there, the way you'd rest a hand on a sleeping child.

* * *

They made the far bank as the light failed.

Not sunset — there had been no reliable sun for days, only a white glareless brightness like the inside of pearl. But the dark came anyway, punctual as a debt, and with it the far shore resolved out of the fog: black pines, a road, and beyond the road, far off, a smudge of orange on the horizon that was not dawn.

The Ember Court burned perpetually. You could see its glow from thirty miles, like a wound in the night.

"Six days," the ferryman said, poling the bow against the shingle so they could disembark. Then, as Hazel stepped down, he added — quietly, so Ilex couldn't hear, or pretended not to:

"Clerk. The thing you gave me. It's a good one. One of the best I've carried." He paused. "There was a girl in it, in the rain. Nineteen years old, soaked through, furious at the whole unjust architecture of the world. She followed a stranger into the weather because she'd just watched ink save a life and she wanted that power for honest ends." His fog-gray eyes held hers. "Whatever you've forgotten: that girl is standing in front of me right now. She didn't get sold. She got *stored*. Remember the difference, when the Court tries to tell you what you're worth."

Hazel's throat tightened. "Why tell me that?"

"Because I'm rich," the ferryman said, "and very poor, and it's bad business to hoard everything." He pushed off. "Go. Refuse their renewal. Break the oldest contract on the river's books — and when it breaks, clerk, everything it holds comes home."

The boat slid into the fog. The last Hazel saw of him was the small amber glow in his coat pocket, brightening once, like a coal breathed on — like something, somewhere in that gray vessel, waking up.

Then the fog closed, and the river was gone, and there was only the black pines and the road and the distant orange burn of the Court.

Ilex came to stand beside her. Ahead, on the dark road, something was moving — figures, several of them, walking toward the glow with the deliberate pace of people who had decided to stop running. The cartographer's silhouette was unmistakable, hunched over a satchel of impossible maps; beside her, the soldier whose war had been unwritten walked with a veteran's economy of motion.

They had sent word ahead. They had all come.

"Midnight tomorrow," Ilex murmured, watching them approach. "My credentials still open the Court's lower archives. After that, they won't — after tomorrow, I'm either an auditor again or a traitor again, and either way the doors start closing." He looked at her. "Are you ready?"

Hazel reached for the answer and found, behind it, a clean empty room where her reasons used to live.

It didn't matter, she told herself. Reasons were furniture. The girl in the rain was stored, not sold. And somewhere ahead, in a citadel built of burned treaties, a contract waited to be broken — and everything it held would come home.

"Yes," she said, and stepped onto the road.
