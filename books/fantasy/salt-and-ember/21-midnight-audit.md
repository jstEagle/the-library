## 21. Midnight Audit

The Ember Court did not believe in doors. It believed in thresholds — in the moment when one thing became another and could never become what it was before. Every entrance to the Ash Parliament was a threshold of that kind, and Ilex had crossed them for sixty years without once thinking about the burning.

He thought about it now.

The capital had no name on any map, because maps were one of the things it ate. You reached it by following the clause-path until the terrain stopped arguing with itself, and then you walked through a field of grey grass that remembered being wheat, and then you were simply *there*, standing beneath walls built from treaties that had been burned and burned again until the ash held its own shape out of spite. The citadel rose above him like a verdict. Its towers were bound ledgers, charred at the edges but legible forever; its gates were two slabs of vellum pressed flat under a century's weight of signatures.

Hazel waited in the shadow of a boundary stone three miles back, with the cartographer and the soldier and the others, because her face was known now — Sable had made sure of that before the chase even began. But Ilex's face was not known. That was the terrible joke of it. He had spent his career ensuring it.

*Auditor Third Class Ilex Vane,* said the seal sewn into his coat lining, still warm from the Court's own fire. *Authorized access: all ledgers, all chambers, all debts.*

They had never revoked it. Why would they? In their arithmetic he was dead, or worse — corrected. An auditor who found nothing wrong with anything was exactly as useful as an auditor who no longer existed, and cheaper to maintain.

He showed the seal to the gatekeeper, which was not a person but a brazier shaped like a kneeling figure, and the flames leaned toward it and read it and let him pass.

The corridor beyond smelled the way his childhood had smelled: hot paper, tallow, the sweet rot of ink left too long near flame. His boots rang on floor tiles made from compressed cinders — each one, he knew now, a page. He had walked these halls for six decades and had never once asked what the floors were made of. Auditors were trained not to ask. Asking was the first symptom of the disease they called *sympathy*, and sympathy ended careers faster than theft.

Tonight he intended to ask everything.

* * *

The Hall of Records opened at midnight, when the ministers slept in their cabinets of coals and the clerks of ash went to their niches to cool. Ilex knew the rhythm the way he knew his own pulse. He also knew the lock on the central archive — a ring of seven flames that tested not keys but *intentions*, guttering red if the hand that reached for it wanted anything more than knowledge.

Sixty years ago, his intentions would have passed clean. Tonight he stood before the ring and let it read him, and the flames went from gold to orange to a deep, suspicious crimson, and he thought: *this is where it ends, then,* and reached in anyway.

The fire parted around his wrist like water recognizing an old boat.

It had read deeper than his intentions. It had read his credentials, and his credentials were still true. That was the horror he would carry out of this room, if he carried anything out: the Court's locks did not care whether your heart was loyal. They cared only whether your paperwork was.

Inside, the archive breathed.

Shelves rose into darkness he couldn't measure, and every shelf held ledgers — thousands upon thousands of them, spines stamped in the Court's cipher-script, which he had spent half his life pretending not to be able to read. Dust here was not dust. It was fine grey powder ground from the edges of erased things, and it settled on his shoulders like a hand.

He lit no lamp. He didn't need one. The ledgers glowed faintly, each spine threaded with a hairline of ember-light, the way Hazel's pages were threaded with storm-light — and seeing that kinship nearly buckled him. She had been right in the ferryman's boat, right in the road-village that greeted her by name, right with a ferocity he had mistaken for grief. The Guild's impossible ink and the Court's patient fire were the same technology. Two rival churches, one god: *the record is the thing.*

He pulled the first ledger at random.

The cover bore a name in the old style: *Vessany.* A country, he realized, turning pages with hands that had begun to shake. Not a person. A country. Population entries. Harvest yields. A coastline described in loving administrative detail — *the western cliffs, white where the gulls nest, sound like bells in wind* — and then, on the final leaf, the sentence he had written himself a thousand times in other books, in other centuries:

*Purchased in full. Never existed.*

His auditor's training supplied the procedure automatically, uselessly: verify against the master index, cross-reference the territorial rolls, confirm the erasure certificate. He had performed that ritual so many times it lived in his fingers. And his fingers, moving on their own, drew out the master index from its chain beneath the reading desk — and the chain came free, because no one had ever tried to steal it. No one had ever needed to. Who would steal a list of countries that had never existed?

The index was not a book. It was a wall.

That was the only word his mind would give him. When he unrolled it across the reading desk it kept unrolling, past the desk's edge, pooling on the floor like spilled water, and the names ran down it in columns of ember-script — and there were not dozens. There were not hundreds.

There were thousands.

*Vessany. Orlund. The Salt Marches. Kethrim. Nine kingdoms of the Alder Coast, purchased as a lot. The Republic of Fen, bought during a drought and resold twice. Corvale.*

He found Corvale on line four thousand and eleven, and his eye snagged there the way a boot snags on a root, because beside the name, in the margin, in a clerk's careful hand, was a note:

*Witness outstanding. See Pemberton file.*

They had been watching her since before her birth. Since the contract. Perhaps — he turned back through the index with a dread that felt almost holy — perhaps since before the contract. He searched the early folios for the original acquisition and found the entry dated thirty-one years before Hazel's first cry: *Sky of Corvale, in perpetuity. Consideration: one life, one witness, one ledger-line yet unnamed.* One ledger-line yet unnamed. They had written her into the deal before she had a name to write.

Ilex sat down on the floor of the archive, in the grey dust of ten thousand vanished nations, and did something he had not done since he was an apprentice: he wept, briefly, silently, in the manner of a man balancing a ledger.

Because here was the thing his sixty years had hidden from him, folded inside the routine of audits and certificates and the pleasant warmth of belonging to the power that decided what was real. He had believed — he had *needed* to believe — that the Court's purchases were rare. Extraordinary. A sky here, a season there, the occasional stubborn village whose wells ran bitter. Scandal, yes. Atrocity, even. But *scale* — scale he had refused to imagine, because scale would have made him complicit in a way that no resignation and no defection could ever wash out.

Line four thousand and eleven was not the end of the list. It was somewhere in the middle.

He counted columns instead of lines, because counting lines would take the rest of his life. Forty columns per panel. Sixty panels. Names in every one. Some entries were single villages; some were empires with histories longer than the Court's own. Beside each, the same closing formula, over and over, in hands that changed across centuries but always wrote the same words with the same terrible tidiness:

*Purchased in full. Never existed.*

And beneath the formula, smaller, the part they never taught apprentices to read — the part written in the fire's own script, visible only when you tilted the page toward the dark:

*Consent renewed. Consent renewed. Consent renewed.*

Every hundred years. Every country. Every debt rolled forward on a signature manufactured from edited memory — some farmer's widow signing away her nation's sky because the Court had first signed away her remembering that the sky had ever been anyone's. He thought of Hazel in the Guild cellar, refusing that client his refund, reciting guild law like a psalm, and understood suddenly that she had been kinder than the law deserved and crueller than she knew, and that both of those truths had been installed in her by systems that needed clerks more than they needed liars.

* * *

Near dawn — if dawn meant anything in a citadel that owned its own light — Ilex stopped counting and started copying.

He worked with an auditor's discipline, which was the only prayer he had left. Name, date, consideration, renewal status. He filled forty pages of his own small notebook, the one he'd bought in a market town that no longer appeared on maps, writing in ordinary ink because ordinary ink could be burned and storm-ink could not, and tonight he wanted the option of destruction. If they took him, he wanted to be able to swallow the evidence whole.

It was on page thirty-one that he found the pattern.

He had been recording considerations — what each nation had sold itself for — and the column had begun to rhyme. *One life. One harvest. One memory, freely given. One name. One child, yet unnamed.* Over and over, the Court bought enormous things for intimate prices, and the intimacy was the mechanism. You could not make a people surrender a country. But you could make a mother surrender a future, a soldier surrender a war, a girl surrender a storm she never called — and the country came attached, the way a house comes with its land.

Corvale was not an anomaly. Corvale was the *method*, executed cleanly, witnessed by a clerk who could have undone it and exiled precisely so she wouldn't.

And there, at the bottom of the current century's renewal schedule — the schedule for the sitting nine days hence — he found the day's appointments listed in order of seniority. Dozens of renewals. Whole nations' continuities of erasure, ratified in an afternoon by ministers who would not read a single term.

Third from the top, in fresh ember-script:

*Corvale. Co-signatory: H. Pemberton (inherited). Status: consent pending. Note: witness unrecovered. Enforcer assigned.*

Enforcer assigned. Sable was already hunting her, and the Court had budgeted for it, the way you budget for weather.

Ilex closed the notebook and sat very still, listening to the archive breathe around him — the soft settling of ash, the tick of cooling ledgers, and beneath it all, so faint he might have invented it, a sound he had heard only once before, in Hazel's cellar, when she burned the Guild's books: a murmur of voices, thousands of them, thin as wind through reeds. The records were not empty. Nothing the Court touched was ever empty. Every ledger here held what it had erased, pressed flat and preserved, the way amber holds the insect it killed.

Somewhere in these shelves was a book with his own name in it. He was certain of it now, with the certainty of a man who has finally read the whole contract. Somewhere was the entry for whatever he had been before the Court made him an auditor — because no one was *born* to this work; the Court did not breed its servants, it *purchased* them, one life at a time, and filed the originals.

He found it an hour later, in a folio marked *Personnel, Acquired.*

*Ilex Vane. Formerly of the town of Marrow's Bend. Formerly a mapmaker's son. Acquired at age nine in settlement of his father's debt. Memory of origin: retained, sealed. Purpose: audit.*

Marrow's Bend. He said it aloud, once, just to hear it exist, and the archive seemed to lean closer, the way a crowd leans toward a man reading a will.

He had been a mapmaker's son. All those years auditing the deaths of countries, and he had been raised to draw borders, to give places their shapes, to write *here* onto blankness. The Court hadn't corrupted him. It had inverted him, used his gift backward, made him the unmaker of maps. And now a cartographer who mapped nowhere sat three miles behind him in the dark with Hazel Pemberton, waiting for him to come back with the shape of the crime.

He tore the personnel page free — a theft that would cost him his remaining protections the moment it was missed, and he found he did not care — and folded it into his coat beside the seal that had opened every door.

Then he did the last thing an auditor should ever do. He picked up the master index, that endless wall of vanished names, and he did not file it back on its chain. He rolled it tight, tied it with the cord from his own sleeve, and carried it out under his arm like a man carrying a sleeping child through a burning house.

At the threshold, the ring of seven flames woke and read him — his intentions, his credentials, his heart — and hesitated, flickering between gold and crimson, between *colleague* and *thief*.

"Read it however you like," Ilex told the fire quietly. "But count what I'm taking. You're the ones who taught me that everything must be counted."

The flames considered. Then, with what he would swear forever after was reluctance, they parted.

Behind him the archive settled, and the murmur of the erased rose slightly — not louder, but clearer, the way a sea sounds different once you know its name — and Ilex Vane walked out of the Ember Court at first false light with a list of four thousand dead countries under his arm and one living name burning in his mouth.

Hazel. He had to reach Hazel. Because the renewal schedule gave them nine days, but the note beside Corvale's entry gave them less — *enforcer assigned* meant Sable was not waiting for the sitting.

Sable was already on the road.
