## 22. The Fire Ministers

The robes were the easy part.

Ember Court clerks wore ash-grey wool with a single coal-colored sash, and there were forty of them milling in the antechambers of the citadel, all of them faceless behind hoods, all of them interchangeable by design. Hazel had learned that much from Ilex in three days of whispered instruction in a rented room above a dye-house: *the Court trusts no one to be anyone. Anonymity is its uniform. You will be safer as nobody than you ever were as Hazel Pemberton.*

So she was nobody now. A clerk of the third register, hood up, eyes down, carrying a ledger she had no right to carry and a heartbeat she was trying very hard to keep slow.

The disguise itself had cost her more than she'd expected. Not coin — Ilex's old credentials had covered that — but something stranger. The robe-shop keeper, a stooped woman with burn-scarred fingers, had measured Hazel without once looking at her face, and when she finished she'd said, in a voice like dry paper: *"Third register. You'll want to forget your own name before you go in. They can smell it on you."*

Hazel had laughed, then stopped laughing when she realized the woman wasn't joking.

Now, standing in the great antechamber beneath the Ash Parliament, she understood. The air itself felt like it was reading her. It pressed against her skin with a faint warmth, patient and thorough, like a customs officer turning out pockets. Somewhere under her hood, against her sternum, the stolen ledger — the one she'd saved from Oren's burning, wrapped in oilcloth beneath her clerk's sash — sat warm as a sleeping animal. She prayed it stayed asleep.

*You are a third-register clerk,* she told herself, the way she'd once taught apprentices to steady their hands over fragile acquisitions. *You have always been a third-register clerk. You catalog cinders. You file flame. You have never heard of Corvale.*

The last part was almost true now, and that frightened her more than any minister could.

A bell made of struck glass rang somewhere above — a sound like a held breath breaking — and the doors opened.

* * *

The sitting chamber defied every law of architecture Hazel knew, which she supposed was fitting for a building made of burned treaties.

It was vast and it was intimate at once. The walls were constructed of charred documents compressed into brick — she could see, where the light caught them, the ghosted edges of signatures, clauses, seals, whole paragraphs of surrendered sovereignty fused into masonry. The ceiling arched so high it vanished into a haze of ember-light, and yet when a minister spoke, his voice arrived at her ear as if he stood beside her. The floor was black glass, and beneath the glass, fire moved — slow rivers of it, orange and white and a blue so deep it looked cold — flowing in channels that branched and merged like the tributaries of some infernal map.

She counted the ministers' thrones as she took her place among the clerks' benches at the chamber's edge. Thirteen. Each carved from a different burned thing: a fleet, a forest, a cathedral, a wall. On each throne sat a figure that had once perhaps been a person. Now they were mostly shape and suggestion — silhouettes of heat-shimmer wearing remnants of regalia, crowns melted into their brows like wax into candlewick. Their faces were the worst part. Not absent. *Averaged.* As if a thousand memories of what a face should be had been poured into a mold and set.

Ilex had warned her about this too. *"Don't look at them directly. They notice being noticed. Keep your eyes on your ledger-pad and write down everything they say. That's what clerks are for. That's all they're allowed to be."*

A thirteenth throne, at the chamber's head, was larger than the rest and empty. Above it hung a seal the size of a wagon wheel: a phoenix rendered not rising but *signing*, quill in talon, inkwell of flame.

The glass bell rang again.

"The Centennial Sitting is opened," announced a voice from everywhere and nowhere. "Let the accounts be renewed."

And the horror began — because that was the only word Hazel could find for it, watching from the bench with her pen moving and her fury banked down to a coal in her chest. Horror, conducted as routine.

* * *

The first minister rose. His name, when the herald spoke it, was Minister Vael of the Third Flame, and his portfolio, apparently, was skies.

"Item one," Vael said, and his voice was pleasant, unhurried, the voice of a man reading a grocery list. "The sky of the western principalities. Term of purchase: one hundred years, expiring this sitting. Renewal requires debtor confirmation of informed consent."

He unrolled a scroll. From the clerks' benches, Hazel could see nothing of its contents — but she didn't need to. She knew contracts. She had spent eleven years among the Guild's ledgers, and she knew what a consent clause looked like, and she knew, with sickening certainty, what she was about to hear.

"Debtors of record," Vael continued, "number four hundred thousand, distributed across nine principalities. Confirmation is entered as follows."

He touched the scroll with one finger, and it *burned* — not destroyed, but lit, glowing from within like Hazel's own storm-ink pages, and a sound came out of it. A sound like a voice. Like four hundred thousand voices, compressed into one.

*"We consent,"* the scroll said. *"We consent. We consent."*

Over and over, in hundreds of accents, men and women and children, some weeping, some laughing, some flat as recitation — and every single voice saying the same words. Hazel's pen stopped moving.

Because she had heard this trick before. Not this exact trick — but its grammar. The Vellum Guild sold bottled grief, and she had once catalogued an acquisition, early in her career, that had turned out to be grief *edited*: a widow's mourning with the anger removed, the questions removed, leaving only a smooth, consumable sorrow. She had flagged it. Her supervisor had unflagged it. She had let it go.

This was that. Scaled to nations.

Those voices weren't consenting. Those voices had been *edited into* consenting — memory by memory, year by year, until consent was all that remained of whatever refusal had once lived there. Somewhere in those nine principalities were people who had fought this contract, who had marched and starved and died against it, and every trace of their fighting had been skimmed off like cream and the remainder poured back into them wearing their own voices.

"We consent," said the scroll, in a child's voice, and Hazel's hand shook so hard she nearly snapped her pen.

"Consent confirmed," said Vael. "Renewal entered. Term extended one hundred years. Next item."

* * *

They went through them like a harvest.

The seasons of a river valley whose people had traded spring for a guarantee against flood, and had forgotten, in a hundred years of edited memory, that spring had ever been theirs to lose. The dreams of an island nation, sold wholesale — the herald read the clause aloud, *all dreams, both sleeping and aspirational, in perpetuity* — and the renewal scroll murmured *we consent* in a voice that sounded hollow even compressed, as if the dreamers themselves had been scooped out and only the agreement remained.

The names of a mountain people. Just the names. Sold, Hazel gathered, to something that collected identity the way other collectors collected stamps, and the mountain people had been issued replacement names by the Court — functional, temporary, *rented* — and had long since forgotten they were tenants inside their own selves.

Minister after minister. Item after item. The chamber's black-glass floor pulsed gently with each renewal, the fire-rivers brightening like a fed animal, and Hazel wrote and wrote until her wrist ached, transcribing every clause, every price, every murmured chorus of manufactured yes. If she died in this room, the pages would still remember. Storm-light kept what ordinary ink abandoned. It was the one advantage she carried into this furnace, and she clung to it the way a drowning woman clings to driftwood.

Somewhere around the eleventh item, she noticed the pattern underneath the pattern.

Every contract due for renewal had, somewhere in its history, a witness problem. A clause-path that led, eventually, to someone who might have read the terms and refused. And in every case, that someone had been handled. Exiled. Erased. Edited. The cartographer who mapped nowhere — she'd met him on the road; she thought of him now, his gentle ruined hands. The soldier whose war was unwritten. Herself. They weren't accidents of the system. They were *maintenance*. The Court did not merely buy countries. It gardened them, pruning away every mind that might grow a doubt.

And the renewals — the centennial sitting itself — existed for one purpose. Not to confirm consent. To *renew the edit*. Another hundred years of forgetting, applied fresh, before the old forgetting wore thin enough for anyone to notice the seams.

Her mother had signed the original Corvale contract. Hazel had read page one in the archive stacks with Sable's shadow hunting her through the shelves, and she had read the clause that named her collateral, and she had understood — finally, completely — why her exile had been engineered before she was old enough to hold a pen. But sitting here, hearing item after item renewed by rote, she understood something larger and colder: her family's betrayal was one thread in a tapestry of betrayal woven across the entire world. Every erased country on Ilex's list. Every "never existed." All of it maintained here, annually tended, in a room where the worst crime imaginable was performed with the ceremony of a tax filing.

"Cold," she whispered under her breath, testing the word. "Cold fury." It would keep her alive. Rage made people reckless; she'd seen it in the Guild's clients, the ones who tried to buy back what couldn't be bought back. Fury, banked and precise, was a different instrument. She would need precision. In nine days — no, fewer now — the Corvale renewal would come to the floor, and she intended to be standing in this chamber when it did.

"Item fourteen," the herald called.

Hazel's pen froze.

* * *

"The sky of Corvale," said Minister Vael — or another minister; they blurred, deliberately, into one another — "in perpetuity. Original instrument executed one hundred years ago this sitting. Debtor of record: Pemberton, M., deceased. Collateral of record: Pemberton, H., status—" the minister paused, and the pause lasted half a second too long, and Hazel felt the whole chamber tilt toward her like a head cocking, "—under review."

Under review. Her hood was up. Her sash was correct. Her pad was full of obedient transcription. She was a third-register clerk, and third-register clerks were furniture.

But the fire beneath the glass floor had gone very still, all its rivers pausing mid-flow, and the averaged faces of thirteen ministers had turned — not toward her, precisely, but toward the *section* of benches where she sat, the way a searchlight sweeps a field without yet fixing on anything.

"Status under review," the minister repeated, to no one, to everyone. "Collateral presence... detected."

*Ilex,* Hazel thought, with a lurch that had nothing to do with fear and everything to do with arithmetic. *Where is Ilex?*

He was supposed to be below, in the records vaults, opening the Court's books with his old auditor's credentials while the sitting consumed the ministers' attention. He was supposed to be finding the master index, the registry of every purchased country, the evidence that would make her testimony unanswerable. He was supposed to be safe.

The chamber doors, far behind her, did not open. No commotion. No alarm. Which meant either Ilex was undetected and fine — or he had never reached the vaults at all, and she had no way of knowing which.

"Collateral presence detected," the minister said a third time, and now the words had changed key, and Hazel realized with a drop-through-the-floor certainty that the repetition wasn't confusion. It was *summons*. The Court was calling something into the chamber. Something that hunted by shape.

The fire-rivers beneath the glass began to move again — faster, brighter, all flowing toward the empty thirteenth throne at the chamber's head, pooling there, rising, taking form.

And through the haze of ember-light, at the far end of the clerks' benches, Hazel saw a figure rise from among the disguised clerks — a figure whose hood fell back as it stood, revealing a face Hazel knew better than her own, because she had worn it in nightmares for eleven years.

Her own face smiled at her across the chamber.

"Sable," Hazel breathed.

The averaged faces of the Fire Ministers turned, all thirteen at once, toward the sound of her voice.
