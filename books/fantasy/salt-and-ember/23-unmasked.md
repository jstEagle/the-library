## 23. Unmasked

The centennial sitting convened at the hour when even fire grows tired.

Hazel had learned the Court's rhythms in six days of carrying trays and pretending not to count. The ministers of the Ember Court did not sleep as mortals understood it; they banked themselves, dimming to coals in their alcoves along the walls of the Ash Parliament, and twice each day they flared back into deliberation like hearths rekindled for company. Between sittings, the great chamber emptied of everything but heat and the soft settling of burned treaties — the citadel's walls were made of them, ten thousand charred documents compressed into stone that smelled faintly of every promise ever broken.

She moved through the clerks' gallery with a tray of ink-pots she would never set down anywhere important. Her disguise was good because it was boring: gray robes, gray hood, the posture of a person whose whole ambition was to be overlooked. Around her neck, beneath three layers of cloth, hung the ledger.

It was warm against her sternum. It had been warm since the night Ilex burned his credentials open the Court's master vaults and showed her the shelves — shelf after shelf after shelf — of countries filed under *never existed*. She still dreamed about those shelves. In the dreams, the spines whispered.

Below her, the floor of the sitting opened like a wound in the shape of an amphitheater. Nine ministers sat in a crescent of basalt thrones, each one less a person than a column of slow flame wearing the suggestion of robes. Their voices came out as crackle and draft, and the Court's translators — lesser fires, quick and servile — rendered them into words for the record.

Hazel kept the record. That was the beautiful, terrible joke of it: on her fourth day inside, the under-clerk who normally transcribed the sitting had been called away to some errand of ash, and Hazel had volunteered with the eager mediocrity of a woman who wanted nothing more than steady work. No one questioned a clerk who wanted to be useful. Boredom was the best armor she had ever worn.

So now she sat at the transcription desk, thirty feet from the First Minister's throne, writing in ordinary ink what the Court said aloud — while beneath her robes, in storm-light only she could see, she wrote what it meant.

*Item: renewal of the Corvale instrument, scheduled this sitting.*

*Item: consent to be affirmed by the co-signatory line, Pemberton, per clause eleven.*

*Item: no living signatory located. Consent to be presumed.*

*Presumed.* Her pen pressed hard enough to score the page. They were going to renew her mother's contract by presumption. By paperwork. By the same bloodless arithmetic that had erased a nation from its own sky. Somewhere in the crescent of thrones, a minister droned through the procedural language of perpetuity, and nine days of Hazel's fear condensed into a single thought: *they don't even need me here. They never needed anyone to say yes out loud. They just needed everyone who could say no to stop existing.*

Which meant the refusal had to happen where they couldn't edit it out.

Her plan was simple in the way that plans are simple right up until they meet reality. At the moment of renewal, when the First Minister spoke the words of affirmation, Hazel would stand, unbind the ledger, and read. Page one. The original terms. Her mother's signature. Clause thirteen, naming an infant girl as collateral. She would read it into the sitting's own record, in the Court's own chamber, before its own witnesses — because she had learned something in the vaults, something Ilex had confirmed with the hollow voice of a man reading his own complicity: the Ember Court was bound by its contracts. Fire kept faith with paper. It was the one law they could not burn without burning themselves.

All she had to do was survive long enough to speak.

The sitting proceeded. A border dispute between two principalities that existed only as line items. A petition regarding the seasonal rights of a river valley Hazel had never heard of, argued with the tedium of eternity. The ministers' flames guttered and steadied. Ink dried under her pen. And then the herald-fire at the chamber's mouth brightened, and the First Minister's voice came out in a long exhalation of cinders:

*"The centennial instruments. Bring forward the schedule of renewal."*

Here it was.

Hazel's hands did not shake. She noted, distantly, that she'd expected them to. Ten years of ledgers had taught her body a discipline her heart had never agreed to: you do not tremble over an open book. You write. You verify. You balance the column.

A minor fire carried the schedule forward on a brazier-plate. The First Minister began the recitation — names of nations, dates of purchase, terms of erasure, each one a country spoken like a debt retired — and Hazel counted down the list toward Corvale, marking her place, waiting for the breath between entries where she would rise.

She never got the chance.

Because the doors at the top of the amphitheater opened, and Sable walked in.

* * *

Hazel had seen her once before, across the archive stacks, by the light of things burning that should not have burned. She had hoped — with the desperate optimism of someone who knew better — that the enforcer had lost her trail.

Sable descended the steps of the sitting floor the way water descends: without hurry, without resistance, taking the shape of whatever held her. She wore a face today that was not her own — a broad, pleasant, forgettable face, the kind belonging to a customs officer or a harbor clerk — and she wore it slightly wrong, the way a coat hangs on the wrong shoulders. As she passed the ministers' crescent, the face rippled, and for half a heartbeat Hazel saw beneath it: a hollowed place, a person-shaped absence, eyes like the last light leaving a window.

Sable stopped at the center of the floor. Every flame in the chamber leaned toward her.

*"Enforcer,"* said the First Minister. *"You were not summoned."*

"I bring an irregularity." Sable's borrowed voice was smooth, unhurried, almost kind. "An intrusion into this sitting. One who entered under false pretenses and carries contraband."

Hazel's hand moved beneath the transcription desk, found the ledger's cord, and held on.

"The Court welcomes scrutiny," the First Minister said, in the tone of a thing that welcomed nothing.

"Not scrutiny." Sable turned — slowly, theatrically, letting the whole crescent follow her — until her stolen face pointed directly at the clerks' gallery. Directly at Hazel. "Evidence."

And then she smiled with someone else's mouth and said, clearly, in a voice pitched to carry:

"Come down, little storm-clerk. Or shall I tell them what glows under your robe?"

* * *

The chamber went very quiet, which for a room full of fire meant it went very bright. Every minister flared. The heat rolled up into the gallery in a wave that made the ink-pots rattle on their tray.

Hazel stood.

Later, she would try to reconstruct the decision and find there wasn't one — or rather, that the decision had been made years ago, in a cellar archive, by a girl cataloging a jar of someone else's childhood and understanding, for the first time, exactly what kind of business she served. Every step since had just been the long way around to standing up.

She untied her hood. She let it fall back. She walked to the gallery rail, and below her, nine columns of ancient fire looked up at a gray-robed clerk with ink-stained fingers and a ledger hanging at her throat like a lantern.

"My name is Hazel Pemberton," she said, and her voice did not shake either. "Of Corvale. Which you have entered in your books as *never existed* — and which exists anyway, because I am standing in it, and I am remembering it, and memory, honored ministers, is the one commodity you have never successfully monopolized."

*"Identity,"* crackled the Second Minister, a thin blue flame with a voice like gasping. *"Unverified."*

"Oh, I can verify it." Sable again, ascending the last steps to stand at the floor's center, looking up with delight she didn't bother to hide. "Shall I? She is Hazel Pemberton, daughter of Maren Pemberton, co-signatory of the Corvale instrument. She was exiled from her homeland for calling a storm she did not call. She has spent ten years keeping books for the Vellum Guild, writing in storm-light, and everything she has written still remembers — including, I suspect, the document she is hiding against her heart." The borrowed face tilted. "Shall we ask it?"

"You've just testified for me," Hazel said. "Thank you. I couldn't have stated my own qualifications better."

A ripple went through the crescent — laughter, if fire could laugh, a sound like wind through a burned forest. But the First Minister did not laugh. Its flame had gone very still and very white, the color of heat that has stopped pretending to be comfortable.

*"The storm-clerk,"* it said. *"We know of you. We have known since your ledgers first shone in the dark of the world. You were to be left alone, little clerk, so long as you stayed small. Erasure is expensive. We do not spend it on one woman."* The white flame leaned closer. *"You chose to become worth the expense."*

"I chose to read page one," Hazel said. "You wrote a contract that says I'm collateral. I've come to contest the lien."

*"There is no Corvale,"* said the Third Minister, flatly, automatically, the way one recites a catechism. *"There was never any—"*

"There is a contract," Hazel cut across it, loud enough that the translator-fires flinched. "Your own law binds you to your own paper. You taught me that — no. The Vellum Guild taught me that, and the Guild exists because courts like yours cannot be trusted to keep their debts honest. So let us keep it honest. Let us open the instrument. Let us read the terms into the record of this sitting, in front of your own witnesses, and see whether fire keeps faith."

For one suspended moment, Hazel believed it might work. That the sheer audacity of procedure would hold them, the way procedure holds all bureaucracies — even parliaments of fire, even monsters with ledgers of their own. The First Minister's flame wavered. Somewhere in the crescent, a minister murmured to another in a language of pure heat, and the murmur had the cadence of doubt.

Then the First Minister said, softly:

*"Burn her."*

* * *

They did not send guards. The Court had no guards; it had never needed them. What it had was itself.

Fire came off the crescent like a tide. Not aimed — poured, a general immolation of the space where Hazel stood, the gallery rail glowing orange, the air itself igniting into a shimmering wall of consumption. It was the same principle as erasure, she understood in the half-second she had to understand anything: not violence, but *unwriting*. Reduce the witness to ash, and the record closes over the gap like water. They had done it to countries. She was, after all, only one woman.

The fire reached her.

And broke.

It broke the way a wave breaks on a seawall it did not expect — scattering, hissing, splitting around her in two streams that scorched the gallery on either side and left, at the center, a column of untouched air shaped precisely like a standing woman holding a book. The ledger was open in her hands. She did not remember opening it. Perhaps she had opened it the moment Sable said her name; perhaps it had opened itself. Its pages blazed — not with fire but with storm-light, that pale silver-blue luminescence of the night sky over Corvale, of rain about to fall, of the exact color of the world the Court had purchased and pretended away.

And the storm-light *resisted*.

Hazel felt it as pressure, as a ringing in her teeth, as the sensation — familiar from ten years of exile — of being somewhere she was not permitted to be and refusing, on principle, to leave. The ink her mother's world had left in her blood rose up through every page she had ever written and met the Court's fire the way the sea meets a torch: not fighting it. Outlasting it.

*"Impossible,"* gasped the Second Minister. *"Paper burns. All paper burns."*

"Not this paper," Hazel said. Her voice came out strange to her, doubled, as though the ledger were speaking through her — and maybe it was; maybe ten years of storm-light had soaked through skin into self. "This paper remembers a sky you bought. And a sky, honored ministers, is not for sale to the thing that lives under it."

She raised the ledger above her head, pages out, and the light of it filled the Ash Parliament like dawn filling a tomb — and she saw, in that light, the faces of the ministers clearly for the first time. Not flames. Never flames. Faces behind the fire, old and afraid, faces of things that had once been people and had purchased so much of the world that nothing in it could look at them anymore.

They were afraid of her.

Good, thought Hazel Pemberton, exile, clerk, daughter of Corvale. Be afraid. I learned bookkeeping from the best, and I am here to audit.

"Page one," she said, and turned the leaf.

But Sable was already moving.

The enforcer came up the gallery steps faster than anything wearing a face had a right to move, and the borrowed face slid off her like wax, and beneath it was the hollow place, the person-shaped absence, and her hand — a hand that had once belonged to someone, the way all her parts had once belonged to someones — closed around the ledger's edge with a grip like a closing door.

"You should have stayed small," Sable said. And there was something in her voice, buried deep under the emptiness, that sounded almost like grief. Almost like warning. "I offered you a life once. I'm offering it again. Put it down."

"No," said Hazel.

The ledger tore.

Not all the way — a corner, a few leaves, ripped free in Sable's fist, and the torn pages screamed. Hazel heard it with her whole body: the memories in those leaves crying out as they were severed from their binding, a childhood in a jar, a borrowed year, a name not yet earned, all of it shrieking in storm-light as the enforcer crushed the pages in her palm and the light died there like stars going out.

Hazel hit her. It was not skilled. It was ten years of loneliness and rage delivered with the edge of a ledger-board, and it caught Sable across the face — across the hollow — and staggered her back one step, two, to the very lip of the gallery rail.

Below, the ministers roared in a language of conflagration. Above, the ceiling of burned treaties began, very faintly, to glow.

"Hold her!" the First Minister thundered, in words at last, in plain trade-common, because some orders are too important for translation. "*Hold the storm-clerk! Silence her before she reads!*"

Hands seized Hazel from behind — clerk-hands, under-clerk hands, the servants of the Court dragging her back from the rail — and Sable straightened, smoothing the ruin of her stolen face, and looked at Hazel with those window-going-dark eyes.

And Hazel, pinned, bleeding from the lip, clutching the ledger to her chest with both arms like a child, like a nation, did the only thing left in her inventory.

She began to read aloud.

"'Know all persons by these presents,'" her voice rang up into the burning dark, "'that the sky of Corvale, in perpetuity—'"

"Silence her!" 

"'—is sold, assigned, and conveyed unto the Ember Court—'"

A hand clamped over her mouth. Storm-light poured between the fingers. The whole chamber was turning silver at the edges, the ledger blazing, the torn corner in Sable's fist flickering like a dying star, and Hazel bit down until she tasted blood and wrenched her face free long enough to shout the words that mattered most:

"'—for consideration received: ONE LIFE, AND THE CONSENT OF THE LINE OF PEMBERTON, RENEWED EACH CENTURY BY INFORMED—'"

The lights went out.

Every flame in the Ash Parliament extinguished at once — the ministers, the braziers, the translator-fires, the herald at the door — plunging the chamber into a darkness so total it had texture, so deliberate it could only be policy. Because they knew what she knew. Without light, storm-ink goes silent. The ledger's glow guttered, fought, faded — she felt it fading against her chest, her mother's signature dissolving back into unread shadow, page one going dark in her hands —

—and in that darkness, just before the last of the light drowned, Hazel saw Sable's face one more time.

The enforcer was not looking at the ministers. She was not looking at the torn pages in her fist.

She was looking at Hazel — and mouthing, silently, in the last silver breath of the dying light, three words that no enforcer of the Ember Court had any earthly reason to say:

*Keep reading.*

Then the dark took everything, and somewhere below in the blackness, Hazel heard the First Minister pronounce sentence upon her — and heard, underneath it, softer than ash falling, the scrape of a flint.
