## 24. Testimony Interrupted

The chamber of the Ember Court was shaped like a held breath.

Hazel had seen it only in fragments since her unmasking — glimpses between the bodies of fire-ministers closing ranks around the floor of the sitting, glimpses through the smoke that curled from their shoulders like patient serpents. But now, standing in the center of it with a ledger pressed to her chest and ash sifting down from the vaulted ceiling like gray snow, she saw the whole of it at once.

The walls were treaties. She had known this from Ilex's whispered briefing in the cartographer's garret, but knowing was nothing against seeing: thousands upon thousands of burned documents fused into stone, their charred clauses still legible in places, whole paragraphs of surrender and cession glowing faintly ember-orange whenever a minister gestured too near. The ceiling arched above them all, ribbed with the spines of accords so old they had become architecture. And everywhere — on the tiered benches where the ministers sat, in the braziers that burned without fuel, in the very floor, which was compacted ash packed hard as marble — everywhere there was fire, and none of it gave light.

That was the first cruelty of the place, Hazel thought. The Court's fire did not illuminate. It consumed, it warmed, it signed and sealed and ratified, but it cast no glow worth reading by. A parliament built for darkness. A government of things that preferred not to be seen.

She stood at the witness mark — a circle of white ash inlaid in the black floor, the only pale thing in the chamber — and she opened the ledger.

The ministers went quiet. Not respectful quiet. The quiet of creditors watching a debtor approach the table.

"Let the record show," Hazel said, and her voice carried further than she expected, up into the treaty-vaults, "that the sitting of the centennial renewal is interrupted by prior claim."

A minister on the third tier stirred. He was old even by the standards of things made of fire — his robes more cinder than cloth, his face a landscape of cooling cracks. First Minister Vhalen. Ilex had named him in the tunnel beneath the capital, his voice shaking with an anger Hazel had never heard from him before: *Vhalen drafted the Corvale instrument himself. He has renewed it four times. He knows every clause the way you know your own hands.*

"Prior claim," Vhalen repeated. His voice was the sound of a banked hearth settling. "There is no prior claim. There is no *Corvale* to claim from."

"There is," Hazel said. "It is in my hand."

She turned the ledger toward them.

And the chamber gasped — a sound like wind through a dying grate — because the pages were glowing.

Storm-light. Her storm-light, the ink she had ground from lightning-glass and rain caught mid-fall, the ink that remembered what everything else had been made to forget. In the Court's lightless fire, the ledger burned blue-white and silver, every line of it luminous, and the nearest ministers flinched back from their benches as though the glow scalded. Perhaps it did. Perhaps memory, in this place, was a kind of heat.

"The Vellum Guild keeps its ledgers in many inks," Hazel said. "Grief-ink. Year-ink. Name-ink. Mine is storm-light, and storm-light is not subject to erasure, because it was never written *in* your jurisdiction. It was written under open sky." She let that land. Let them look at the page. "I hold the original instrument of sale for the sky of Corvale, executed one hundred years past, co-signed by Maren Pemberton of the House Pemberton, and I am here to read it into the record in full, per Article Nine of the Court's own founding law: *no contract may be renewed whose terms have not been read aloud in the presence of the debtor's heir.*"

Silence. Long enough that Hazel could hear the treaties in the walls ticking as they cooled.

Then Vhalen smiled. It was not a kind thing to see. Fire learning to smile is an obscenity, whatever shape it takes.

"Clerk," he said, "you are mistaken about what this chamber is. It is not a courtroom. It is a counting-house. We do not argue law here. We honor it — precisely, completely, and without sentiment." He rose, and his shadow moved wrong across the tiers, falling upward. "If you hold an instrument, then read it. The Court does not fear its own paper."

*He wants me to read it,* Hazel thought, and her stomach turned cold. *Or he believes I can't.*

She looked once, quickly, toward the gallery where the forgotten sat. The cartographer, Wren, with her ink-stained fingers laced together so tightly the knuckles had gone white. Tam, the soldier whose war had been unwritten, standing rigid as a pike. Others — a woman who had been a harbor, a man who had been a song, faces the Court had spent centuries filing away. And at the end of the row, hood drawn low, Sable — who should not have been there, who had no right to be there after everything, and who gave Hazel the smallest nod, the nod of someone standing watch over a grave.

*Read it,* Sable's nod said. *I'll get you out if it goes wrong.*

*I read it aloud,* Hazel thought, *or I die trying, and either way the record exists.*

She lifted the ledger. Found page one. And began.

"'Instrument of Cession and Perpetuity, executed under seal of the Ember Court and the hand of Maren Pemberton, freely given—'"

The words came out of her and became weather.

That was the thing about storm-light that no one had warned her — that she herself had never tested, because she had never read a full page aloud in public, never needed to. The ink did not simply sit on the vellum. When voiced, it *rose*. Each clause lifted off the page in a thin ribbon of blue-white radiance and hung in the air of the chamber, legible from every bench, unignorable, and behind each ribbon came the sound of what the words recorded: rain on the roofs of Corvale, the long roll of thunder over the Saltreach hills, gulls crying over a harbor that officially had never existed. The chamber filled with the sound of a country remembering itself.

"'—the undersigned cedes to the Ember Court the sky of Corvale, in perpetuity, together with all stars, seasons, storms, and weathers appurtenant thereto—'"

"Point of order." Vhalen again, sharper now. "The instrument references collateral conditions which the reader is omitting—"

"I am omitting nothing," Hazel said, and did not stop reading. "'—in exchange for consideration rendered unto the House Pemberton, namely: the life of the said Maren Pemberton, extended beyond natural term—'"

A minister on the lower tier stood up. Then another. The fire in the braziers began to climb, restless, throwing shadows that writhed but shed no light.

"'—and further naming as surety and collateral against breach the issue of the said house, to wit: Hazel Pemberton, whose exile from the said Corvale is hereby contracted, conditioned, and—'"

"*Enough.*"

It was not Vhalen who spoke. It was the chamber itself — or something speaking through it. Every treaty in the walls flared at once, every burned signature igniting simultaneously, and for one terrible instant the room was full of light after all: the light of ten thousand surrenders burning in unison, and in that light Hazel saw the ministers' faces clearly for the first time.

They were afraid.

Not of her. Of the *reading*. Of the sound of rain in a room that had not permitted rain for a century.

The light died. Darkness rushed back in like water filling a footprint. And Vhalen's voice came out of it, gentle now, which was worse than anything:

"Clerk Pemberton. You have read sufficient terms to establish provenance. The remainder is administrative. The Court will take the instrument under advisement and—"

"No," Hazel said.

The word echoed. Small, human, absurd in that vast dark, and absolutely immovable.

"You will hear all of it," she said. "Every clause. Every condition. Every name. Because your own law says a contract unread cannot renew, and your own law says a reading interrupted is void, and I have spent my whole life learning the letter of guild law, ministers, and I promise you—" she turned another page, and the storm-light flared, defiant, "—I know the letter better than any of you."

She read on. The ribbons of light multiplied. Rain-sound swelled. Somewhere in the galleries, Wren had begun to cry, silently, the way people cry when they hear the name of a place everyone else insists was never real.

And that was when the First Minister gave the order.

Hazel would remember — afterward, in whatever fragmentary way she remembered anything — that it was not dramatic. That was the horror of it. Vhalen simply raised one crackled hand, the way a chairman calls a recess, and said, quietly, to the chamber at large:

"Extinguish."

* * *

The lights went out.

All of them. The braziers. The wall-treaties. The faint ember-glow in the floor. Even the ministers themselves dimmed, banking their own fires down to almost nothing, until the Ash Parliament became a cavern of absolute dark — the first true darkness Hazel had seen since the night the stars went out over Corvale.

And the ledger went silent.

She felt it happen. Felt the storm-light gutter like a candle pinched between wet fingers. The ribbon of light she had been reading from thinned, wavered, and dissolved, and the words on the page beneath her thumb faded from luminous blue to a ghost-gray, and then to nothing at all. Blank vellum. The rain-sound died mid-drop. The thunder cut off like a door slammed on a storm.

"No," Hazel whispered. "No, no—"

She knew the property. Of course she knew it — she had *made* the ink, ground it from lightning-glass by lamplight in the Guild cellars, and lightning-glass held charge the way a bell holds a ringing: only while struck. Storm-light needed light to speak. Sunlight, moonlight, lamplight, starlight — any light would do, any light at all.

But the Court had extinguished every source in the chamber, and the chamber was windowless, and the dark was total.

Her testimony hung unfinished in the air for three heartbeats — the last few ribbons of clause still faintly visible, fading — and then they, too, went out, and the silence that followed was the silence of a country being erased a second time.

"Article Nine," Hazel said into the blackness. Her voice sounded small and strange to her own ears, stripped of its echo. "Your own law. A reading interrupted is void. You've just voided a century of renewals."

"Do not lecture us on our law, child." Vhalen's voice had moved; he was descending the tiers, unhurried, footsteps soft as settling ash. "You misunderstand what happened here. Nothing was interrupted. The reading *concluded*. You reached the end of what your medium could sustain. Storm-light is a fairweather ink, clerk. It serves in daylight and deserts at dusk." A pause, and when he spoke again she could hear the smile in it. "Perhaps next time, bring a lamp."

Laughter moved through the tiers — dry, papery, the laughter of things that had done this ten thousand times. Hazel understood, suddenly and completely, how old this trick was. How many witnesses had stood where she stood, holding instruments that glowed, reading truth into a record that required only darkness to make the truth illegible. The Court did not burn testimonies. It simply waited for sundown.

Her hands were shaking. She gripped the ledger harder, felt the blank pages under her fingers, and the despair that rose in her was unlike anything the exile had taught her — deeper than loneliness, colder than the night without stars. Because she had *been* the light. She had been the one un-erasable thing, the storm-light clerk, the girl whose ink remembered — and now she stood in a room built to have no light in it, holding a book full of silence, and the whole architecture of her purpose collapsed around her like the wards over Corvale.

*Page one,* she thought. *I got to page one. I read the sky away. I read my mother's life as the price. I read my own name as collateral.* And no one had heard it but the ministers who already knew, and the forgotten in the gallery, and the forgotten could testify — but who would believe the erased? That was the entire mechanism. That was the whole machine. Erase the witnesses, darken the room, and the paper wins forever.

"Hazel." Wren's voice, a thread from the gallery. "Hazel, the skylight—"

"There is no skylight," Vhalen said pleasantly. "This chamber has not admitted the sky in nine hundred years. Why do you think we built it so?"

Hazel stood in the dark with a dead book in her arms and understood that she was going to lose. Not die — the Court would not waste a collateral asset — but lose. They would adjourn, they would renew, they would file her somewhere between the grief-jars and the unwritten wars, and Corvale would stay erased, and in a hundred years some other clerk would stand in this circle of white ash holding some other glowing book, and the dark would simply wait for them too.

She thought of Ilex. He had gone ahead into the archive levels an hour before the sitting — *someone has to pull the duplicate filings*, he'd said, *proof of the pattern, in case the reading alone isn't enough* — and she had kissed his cheek and told him to be careful, and he had smiled his crooked auditor's smile and said, *I'm always careful, that's what's wrong with me.*

*Ilex,* she thought, not a prayer, just a name, the way you say the name of the last lit window in a drowning town.

And then, in the gallery, a match struck.

Hazel heard it before she saw it — the small scrape, absurdly domestic, a sound from kitchens and writing desks, from the ordinary world of ordinary ink. Then a bloom of orange. Then Wren screaming his name. Then light.

Not much light. One flame, cupped in two hands, high in the gallery where the forgotten sat. It threw a trembling circle across the black chamber, up the treaty-walls, down onto the floor — and onto the open ledger in Hazel's arms.

The pages woke.

Blue-white flooded back into the lines like blood returning to a numbed limb, and the nearest ribbon of clause reignited mid-word, and the rain-sound resumed as if it had never stopped, and Hazel's breath came back to her all at once.

She looked up at the flame.

It was Ilex. He had come back. He stood at the rail of the gallery with a fire kindling in his cupped hands that was not coming from any match — she could see that even from the floor, see the way the light was rising *out of him*, from under his skin, along his arms, the old ember-minister's inheritance waking in his blood like a debt called due. He was looking down at her, and he was smiling, and his eyes were already beginning to glow.

"Hazel," he said, and his voice carried the way hers had carried, the way true things carry. "Keep reading."

"What are you doing?" Her voice broke on it. "Ilex, what are you doing?"

"Article Nine," he said. "A reading requires light." The flame climbed his wrists. The ministers were on their feet now, all of them, Vhalen shouting something about containment, about protocol, guards of living ash pouring up the gallery stairs — too late, far too late, the light was already spreading, already reaching the walls, and ten thousand burned treaties were catching the glow and brightening like coals breathed upon. "And I am," Ilex said, "the last light in this room that they don't control."

"Ilex—"

"Read, Hazel." He looked at her the way you look at the only thing left worth looking at. "Every clause. Every name. Take all the time you need."

The flame went up like a struck bell.

And Hazel Pemberton opened her ledger, and began to read.
