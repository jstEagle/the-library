## 25. Ilex Burns Bright

The ministers had not merely extinguished the chamber lights. They had *unmade* them.

Ilex felt it happen the way a man feels a door close in a distant room — a pressure change, a small wrongness in the air. One moment the Ash Parliament's great sitting hall was lit by ten thousand hanging flames, each one a minister's office, each office a small burning authority; and then Speaker Coriander Voss lifted one ash-grey hand, and the light did not go out so much as *retract*, drawn back into the walls like thread pulled through cloth.

Darkness fell with the weight of law.

And in that darkness, Hazel's ledger went silent.

Ilex heard her stop reading. He was three rows behind her, pressed into the gallery of unranked observers where he'd spent eleven years taking minutes for men who never once said anything worth recording, and he knew the sound of her voice better now than he'd ever known his own name in the Court's registers. She had been halfway through the fourth clause — *the sky of Corvale, in perpetuity, held against the life of the signatory and her issue* — when the dark came down, and her voice simply stopped, the way a river stops at a dam.

"No further testimony," Voss said from the Speaker's dais, his voice dry as paper left too long near a lamp. "The chamber is adjourned to deliberation. Observers will withdraw."

There would be no deliberation. Ilex had sat through a hundred years of this parliament's deliberations — well, eleven years of them, but they were all the same year wearing different robes — and he knew exactly what happened next: the ministers would retire to their ember-closets, they would consult the contracts they had written to protect themselves, and by morning Hazel Pemberton would be a rumor that had never finished becoming true. Erasure took time even here. That was the one mercy of the Court's machinery. It was slow, because it was thorough, because it was *legal*.

But it only needed the dark for a few hours.

Hazel needed minutes.

* * *

He had known this moment was coming since the ferry. Perhaps since before — perhaps some part of him had known it from the night he first opened a smuggled ledger in the Guild's cellar archive and saw storm-light crawling across the page like something alive, something that remembered what the Court paid whole departments to forget. He had been the Court's auditor then. Auditor of erasures: the man who checked the arithmetic of countries that stopped existing, who confirmed that the ledgers balanced, that nothing was left over, that no orphaned memory survived to embarrass the ministry. He had signed off on Corvale. He had signed his name beneath the line that read *no such country*.

Then he had read the ledgers, and understood that the Court kept its books honest in exactly one place — in the handwriting of an exiled clerk in a cellar under the Vellum Guild — and that honesty, in the Ember Court, was the only crime that could not be pardoned, only deferred.

So yes. He had known. There is a reason the ministry trained its auditors in flame-theory as well as arithmetic. Every minister carries fire in his chest; it is how they are made, in the founding kilns, from candidates who volunteer knowing the terms. And every minister is taught, on the first day, the last trick:

*A minister cannot be extinguished. But he can choose to be spent.*

They taught it as a warning. Spend yourself improperly, and you do not die; you simply cease to be recoverable. No rebirth from the kilns, no re-tempering, no second sitting. The Court could remake a traitor's body a hundred times, but a minister who burned himself out *for something* — that ash could never be gathered into anything again.

Ilex had always assumed he would spend himself badly. On spite, maybe. On escape. On some small private warmth in the last cold hour.

Not like this. Not standing up in the dark while a clerk read a hundred-year-old contract aloud, holding his own chest open like a lantern.

Except that it was exactly like this. It had always been going to be exactly like this. That was the joke the Court had never understood about its own training: you cannot teach a man the shape of sacrifice without teaching him to recognize it. They had shown him the trick on day one so he would fear it, and instead he had spent eleven years keeping it polished in the back of his mind like a coin saved for the right shop.

The right shop was here. The right price was now.

* * *

"Observers will withdraw," Voss repeated, and this time there was iron under the paper. Somewhere below, Sable was moving — Ilex could hear the particular silence of her, the way stolen lives moved quietly because they belonged to no one — and he knew she would reach Hazel before the guards did, and that whatever Sable intended, it would end with the ledger in cinders and the testimony unfinished.

Hazel was still in the witness aisle. He could see her outline against the faint residual glow of the chamber's stone — stone that had drunk a century of minister-light and gave it back grudgingly, like a miser counting out coins. Her hands were flat on the open ledger. Her head was bowed over it as though in prayer, but he knew better; she was listening to the pages, willing them to speak in the dark, and they would not. Storm-light needed light. It was ink made of memory and lightning both, and lightning does not grow in cellars.

She looked up. Even across forty feet of darkness he felt her find him — because of course she was looking for him, because he had promised her, hadn't he, in the cartographer's freezing attic, with the redrawn borders spread out between them like a patient: *if the lights go out, I have one more credential they haven't revoked.*

He stood up.

The nearest guard turned. A minister two boxes down leaned forward, ash face furrowing. In the Ember Court, an observer standing during adjournment was a procedural violation, a footnote, a thing for clerks to record — and Ilex almost laughed, because for eleven years he had *been* the clerk recording such things, and here was the entry he had always known he'd write eventually:

*Observer, gallery three, rose during adjournment. Observer identified as former Minister Ilex Vane, Office of Audit, presumed defected. Observer then—*

Then what? Then the sentence every auditor dreams of ending: *the books balanced.*

"Hazel," he called, and his voice carried strangely well in the dark, the way voices do when everyone else has gone quiet enough to be ashamed. "Clause five. You were at clause five."

"I can't read it," she said. "It's dark. The ink won't—"

"I know." He stepped down from the gallery. His legs felt very ordinary. That was the strangest part, he thought — that the body refused to understand what was asked of it, that his feet found the stairs with the same dull competence they'd used for eleven years of carrying minutes and tea and quiet treason. "That's my part."

Voss's voice cracked across the chamber like a whip of dry hide. "Minister Vane. You are recalled to the rolls. Present yourself for re-tempering or be taken."

"I am presenting myself," Ilex said. "For spending."

He walked down the central aisle toward the dais, toward the Speaker, toward the great seal of the Court set into the floor before it — a circle of fused treaties, glassy and black, the floor itself made of everything the parliament had voided. Ministers watched him from their boxes. Some wore contempt. A few — and these he catalogued with the last of his auditor's precision — wore fear, because old ministers remember the founding kilns, remember what it cost to be made, and understand viscerally what it means when one of their number walks toward the seal with his collar already open.

Sable stepped out of the shadow of the dais. Her current face was a young man's, mild and forgettable, and her voice when she spoke was nobody's at all. "You'll give them light," she said. "And they'll put it out again. And she'll have read for nothing, and you'll have ended for less."

"Probably," Ilex agreed. He kept walking.

"You're the auditor. You know the odds."

"The odds are my oldest colleagues," he said. "I've never liked them."

Something moved behind Sable's borrowed eyes — not softness, he didn't think she had any left to spend, but recognition, the look of one hollowed thing seeing another about to be hollowed differently. She did not step aside. But she did not strike him either, and in the Ember Court, in that moment, not-striking was its own kind of testimony.

* * *

Here is what they teach you about the trick, in the first hour of the first day, when your chest is still new-made and the fire in it still hurts to hold:

A minister's flame is not in his heart. It is his heart. Everything else — the body, the rank, the centuries — is wick and wax. To spend yourself is to open the casing and let the whole of it out at once, unrationed, and the light of a fully spent minister is not like lamplight. Lamplight illuminates. A minister burns *testifies*. Every treaty he ever signed is written in his fire; every audit, every erasure, every country he confirmed out of existence glows for as long as he lasts, and anyone standing in that light sees it all.

They teach you this so you will understand why it must never be done inside the chamber. Because a spent minister's light shows the truth of everything it touches — including, especially, the truth of the Court itself.

Ilex stopped at the edge of the seal. Above him, Voss had risen from the Speaker's chair, and the other ministers were rising too, and someone was shouting procedure at him — *this is irregular, this is unprecedented, this is outside the order of sitting* — and Ilex thought, with a calm that felt like the eye of the storm Hazel had never called: good. Let it be irregular. Let the record show that the Court could not contain even its own destruction within its rules.

He looked back once. Hazel had come down the witness aisle and stood at the seal's far edge, the ledger open against her forearm, watching him with an expression he would carry into the fire with him if fire allowed carrying — not gratitude, not grief, but *witness*. She was doing the thing she did best. She was going to remember this correctly.

"Don't waste the clauses," he told her.

"I won't."

"And Hazel." The words came easier than he expected. "Page one. Read page one twice. My name isn't on it, but it should be. I countersigned Corvale's erasure. Make them hear that part."

Her jaw tightened. "Ilex—"

"That's not a request. That's an amendment." He smiled, and found the smile was real, which surprised him more than anything else that day. "Eleven years I've wanted to amend something in this chamber. Indulge me."

Then he opened his collar, set both hands flat against his own sternum the way a man steadies a door he intends to walk through, and spoke the words they teach once and hope never to hear spoken:

*"I am the wick. I am the wax. What I was, let it be light. What I owed, let it be paid. Spend."*

* * *

The casing of a minister opens along no seam a surgeon could find. It opens along the seam of his consent.

Ilex felt himself come apart along that seam, and the first sensation was not pain but release — eleven years of rationing, of banking his burn, of feeding his fire teaspoon by teaspoon to stay unnoticed in the ministries, all of it ending in one great exhalation. Light poured out of him the way water leaves a broken jug: everywhere at once, unstoppable, total.

The chamber ignited with him.

Not fire — *memory*. That was the horror and glory of a minister's full burn: the light was made of what he had witnessed, and Ilex had witnessed everything. The walls of the Ash Parliament filled with moving shadows and shining figures: the signing desks of a thousand erased nations. Borders unwriting themselves in real time. The village that had greeted Hazel by name flickering on the eastern wall, whole and warm, before the erasure took it. The map rooms. The kilns where ministers were made. And over it all, vast and unavoidable, the ledger entries themselves — his own neat auditor's script rising in the air in letters of gold and ash:

*Confirmed: no such country as Corvale.*
*Confirmed: no such people as the Pembertons.*
*Confirmed: no debt outstanding.*

*False,* the light said, because a spent minister's fire testifies, and Ilex's fire had been lying to itself for eleven years and was done with it. *False. False. False.*

He heard the ministers screaming. Not in pain — in exposure. Their faces, ash-grey and composed for a century, were suddenly legible in his light: he could see every forged consent, every edited memory, every contract renewed by rote over the bodies of countries that had never been asked. His burn was doing what no auditor's report ever could. It was making the whole chamber *readable*.

And in the center of it all, lit like the first morning of the world, Hazel Pemberton lifted her ledger and began to read.

"—clause five," her voice rang out, clear as a bell over the crackle of him, "the sky of Corvale, in perpetuity, held against the life of the signatory Maren Pemberton and her issue—"

Yes, he thought. Yes. Read it. Read all of it.

The pain arrived then, late and enormous, the way debts arrive — he supposed he should have expected the metaphor — but it came wrapped in the light, and the light was worth it, the light showed him things even as it unmade him: Hazel's face fierce and tear-streaked in his glow; the guards faltering at the chamber's edges, unwilling to step into a brightness that was busy exposing their masters; Sable standing motionless at the dais, her stolen face sliding like a mask in the heat of him, and beneath it — just for a moment — something young and ruined and *watching*, something that looked at him the way you look at a door you thought was painted on.

*Go,* he tried to tell her. *There's still a door. I'm proof.*

He was dimming. He could feel it — the great expenditure narrowing, the edges of him going to ash, the light pulling inward like a tide. But Hazel was still reading, clause after clause, and the ministers were on their feet and *bound* — he could see it, see the debt-law take hold of them the way it takes hold of everything in this chamber, the oldest law in the room suddenly the only one anyone could obey — and Voss was shouting something about adjournment and no one was adjourning anywhere—

"—and the undersigned," Hazel read, and her voice broke and held, broke and held, "countersigning for the Office of Audit: Ilex Vane, who hereby confesses the erasure of Corvale false, and the debt of the Ember Court—"

*Outstanding,* he thought, giving her the word with the last of his brightness, pouring it into the air between them the way he'd once poured tea for ministers whose names he'd hoped to outlive. *The word you want is outstanding.*

"—outstanding," Hazel said, "and payable, and due—"

The chamber blazed with him. The seal beneath his feet — the fused treaties, the floor of voided promises — began, for the first time in a century, to glow from underneath, as if the Court's own foundations had been waiting in the dark for one honest light to answer.

Ilex spent. He spent everything. And as the last of him flared — one final, towering pulse that lit every corner, every hidden alcove, every minister's terrified face, and showed Hazel, mid-sentence, turning the page — he felt the fire find the thing he had saved for the very end, the smallest coin in his pocket, the memory he had guarded through eleven years of grey service:

A girl in a cellar archive, holding a jar of someone else's childhood up to the light, checking the label against the ledger with a clerk's exact care. The first honest thing he had ever seen in the Court's whole dishonest empire.

He gave them that too.

*Read fast,* he thought, coming apart into gold and ash and morning. *I'll hold the dark off as long as I—*
