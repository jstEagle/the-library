## 28. Sable Unmasked Further

The cell they threw Hazel into was made of ash-bricks and older law.

She knew the difference because she had spent three days in the Ember Court's undercrofts learning to read both. The bricks were warm — everything here was warm, even the stones that should have been cold, even the water in the basin, which came out of the tap faintly steaming as though the whole citadel were a body with a fever it refused to acknowledge. The law was in the mortar. She could feel it when she pressed her palm flat against the wall: a low, humming insistence, like a contract being recited by someone very far away who never stopped for breath.

*The debtor shall be held until disposition of accounts.*

That was what the wall said. Not prisoner. Not traitor. *Debtor.* The Court could not bring itself to call her anything else, because to call her anything else would be to admit she was something other than an entry in their books.

Hazel sat on the cot — also warm, unbearably so, like sleeping on a hearthstone — and turned her remaining ledger over in her hands for perhaps the hundredth time. They had not taken it from her. That was the thing she kept circling back to, the splinter in her mind she couldn't stop probing. They had stripped her of her clerk's sash, her seal, her boots with the hidden compartment in the left heel. But the ledger they had returned to her cell personally, carried in by a fire-minister's attendant, laid on the cot with something close to reverence.

They wanted her to have it. Which meant they wanted her to read it. Which meant there was something in it they believed served them.

Or — and this thought arrived at three in the morning, court-time, when the light through the barred vent went the color of dying coals — they wanted to know if she would try to destroy it. A ledger written in storm-light did not burn. She had proven that in the cellar of the Vellum Guild, watching Oren's betrayal glow through the smoke. If the Court wanted it gone, they would need her to unmake it herself, willingly, page by page, the way debts were always collected: by consent, manufactured or otherwise.

So she held it and did not open it, and the ledger sat in her lap like a sleeping animal, and somewhere above her the parliament of fire went on debating in cinders.

* * *

Sable came on the fourth day.

Hazel heard her before she saw her — the particular sound of the door's lock disengaging, which was not a mechanical sound at all but a spoken one, a word in a language of locks that the Court had bought from some drowned kingdom centuries ago. Then the corridor's orange light spilled in, and then Sable, alone, without escort, carrying two tin cups of something that steamed.

"You look terrible," Sable said.

"You burned down my guild."

"I burned down a building. Your guildmaster sold you first." Sable set one cup on the floor between them, equidistant, a negotiation in miniature. "Chamomile. It's the only thing in this place that isn't on fire or thinking about it."

Hazel didn't touch the cup. "Why are you here?"

"Because I'm the only one they'll let in." Sable lowered herself onto the floor across from the cot, cross-legged, unhurried, in that way she had of making every room feel like territory she'd already surveyed. Up close, in the flat light of the cell, Hazel noticed again what she'd been refusing to notice since the archive stacks, since the fight, since Sable's knife had opened a line of fire along her ribs: the woman's face was wrong. Not ugly-wrong. Not scarred-wrong. Wrong the way a translation is wrong — accurate in every particular and true in none of them. Her eyes were gray in a way that suggested they had been other colors before. Her cheekbones sat where cheekbones should sit, but as though someone had consulted a manual.

"I'm going to tell you something," Sable said, "and you're going to want to use it against me, and I'm asking you — just for the length of this conversation — not to."

"That's a lot to ask of someone whose face you stole."

"I know." Sable looked at the steam rising off her own cup. "I stole more than your face, Pemberton. I stole your storm. I stood on the ridge above Corvale in your body and I called down the sky on your name, and you've been paying for it for six years, and I have never once told you why." She drank. Set the cup down. "Here's why."

And then she pulled off her gloves.

Hazel had seen Sable's hands before — gripping a knife, signing a forged writ, wrapped around Hazel's throat in the stacks. She had assumed, in the way people assume things about hands, that they were simply hands. They were not simply hands. They were blank. Not smooth — blank. No lines. No creases at the knuckles, no whorls on the fingertips, no scars, no history. Skin like unwritten vellum stretched over working bone, and where the palms should have carried the map of a life lived, there was nothing at all.

Hazel had spent eleven years cataloguing impossible things. She had shelved jars of bottled grief and crates of borrowed years and a box, once, containing the echo of a bell that hadn't been rung yet. She had thought herself past the point where a body could frighten her.

These hands frightened her.

"What am I looking at," she said quietly.

"A debtor," Sable said. "Like you. Before you. The clause in my contract was different from yours — mine wasn't collateral, it was payment. I owed the Court a debt I couldn't pay in years or grief or names, so they took the only thing I had left." She flexed the blank fingers, and Hazel watched the skin move and saw nothing in it move with her. "They took my record. My whole record. Every memory, every year, every name anyone ever called me — including the one my mother used. Including the one I used. There's nothing underneath this face, Pemberton. That's what I am now. A person-shaped container the Court fills with whatever work needs doing."

"The faces—"

"Borrowed. Like the Guild borrows years." Sable's mouth did something that was almost a smile and had clearly been practiced into that shape from instructions rather than feeling. "When I take a shape, I take its history too — the surface of it, anyway. Your face, your gait, your voice, the way you push your hair back when you're reading. I wore you well enough to fool a border ward and a tribunal. But it's costume, not skin. Underneath—" she turned the blank palm up, offering it like evidence, which is exactly what it was, "—underneath there's nothing to wear anything *on*. Do you understand? I don't have a self to hide behind your face. I have a vacancy."

The word landed in Hazel's chest and stayed there, cold, in a room that was too hot.

"The Court hollowed you out," Hazel said. "And then gave you a job."

"They gave me a purpose. It's not the same thing, but when you're empty, it's close enough that you stop checking." Sable picked up her cup again, and Hazel watched the blank fingers curl around the tin and thought: *she can't even feel whether it's hot.* Or maybe she could; maybe the Court had given her borrowed sensation along with everything else. Maybe that was worse. "For sixty years I've worn other people. Ministers. Soldiers. A ferryman once, for a season — don't ask. And clerks. I've worn four clerks. You're the fifth." She met Hazel's eyes, and the gray of them seemed, for a moment, less like nothing and more like a color that had been washed too many times. "You're the first one who ever wrote back."

* * *

Hazel didn't answer right away. She made herself not answer right away, because the thing rising in her was rage, and rage in front of Sable was a currency Sable could spend.

But underneath the rage — and this was the discovery, the one that felt like finding a forged signature in her own family folio — underneath the rage was recognition, and the recognition was worse.

Because Hazel knew what it was to be defined by a ledger. She had been the Vellum Guild's instrument for eleven years, precise and punctual and increasingly hollow, keeping accounts of other people's griefs while her own sat sealed in a tribunal verdict she'd never been allowed to contest. She knew what it was to be useful to a system that did not care whether she survived its use. She had simply never had the hollowness made literal. She had gotten to keep the memory of her mother's face — until the river. Until the ferryman. Until she'd paid, and paid again, and felt herself thinning with each transaction, and kept paying anyway because the country needed her to.

How many payments from empty?

"Sable," Hazel said. "Is that even a name?"

"It's the one they gave me when the last one wore out." A pause. "It means shadow in a language neither of us speaks. They think it's clever."

"What was your real one?"

The silence that followed was long enough that Hazel counted the hum of the mortar — four full recitations of the debtor's clause, each about nine seconds, thirty-six seconds of a woman sitting with an empty cup in blank hands trying to remember a word.

"I don't know," Sable said finally. "I know I had one. I know it fit. I know that when I wear other names, there's a moment — half a second, when the shape settles — where I can feel how badly they fit, the way a shoe feels when it belongs to someone else. That's all I have of it. The knowledge of the gap." She set the cup down with exaggerated care, as though her hands might do something without her permission, which, Hazel supposed, they might. "You want to hate me. You should. I'll even help. But I want you to hate me accurately, so listen: I chose this. Whatever my crime was, whatever debt brought me to that table — I signed. Nobody tricked me the way they tricked your mother. I walked in knowing the terms, and I signed, because I was arrogant and certain I'd find a loophole, and the Court let me believe it because letting debtors believe in loopholes is how they get good signatures." Her voice didn't break. Hazel suspected it couldn't; grief required somewhere to live, and Sable had nowhere. "So no, I'm not asking for absolution. I'm telling you what you're negotiating with. When you stand in front of those ministers tomorrow, understand that the thing standing beside them wearing an enforcer's coat is what they do to people who sign and fail. You are looking at the fine print, Pemberton. I *am* the fine print."

* * *

Afterward — after Sable had risen, gathered the cups, paused at the door with her hand on the frame as though the frame had asked her a question — Hazel sat with the ledger in her lap and did the arithmetic she'd been avoiding.

A nation's weight of memory. That was the price of restoring Corvale; the ministers had named it plainly, gloating almost, certain no debtor could pay it. And the Court had proposed the obvious account: hers. Everything the ledgers held. Everything she was.

She had thought, in the days since, that the offer was cruelty. Now, holding the image of those blank hands, she understood it was precedent. Somewhere in the Court's deep archive there was a contract with a name no one remembered, signed by a person no one remembered, paid in full — and the payment had walked out of the chamber wearing an enforcer's coat and had been walking ever since, sixty years of borrowed faces over a perfect emptiness, doing the Court's erasing because she had nothing left of her own to erase.

That was what waited for Hazel. Not death. Death would be mercy, and the Court did not deal in mercy; it dealt in *disposition of accounts.* They would take her memories and leave the vessel, and the vessel would be *useful*, and in sixty years some other stubborn clerk would sit in a warm cell listening to a hollowed thing explain, calmly, what the fine print looked like from the inside.

Enemy becomes mirror. She turned the phrase over and found it insufficient. Sable wasn't her reflection. Sable was her *forecast.*

And yet.

And yet the forecast had climbed down into the dark to bring her chamomile. Had shown her the one proof that could not serve the Court's purposes — a debtor's blank hands, displayed voluntarily, off the record, to the very witness the Court wanted broken. Had said *you're the first one who ever wrote back* in a voice that, for half a sentence, had almost had a texture of its own.

There was still something in the vacancy that chose. Hazel clung to that. It was thin, and it might have been another mask — Sable's masks were legendary — but it was the same kind of thin thing Hazel herself was standing on: a refusal, renewed daily, to become entirely what the ledgers said she was.

The lock spoke its drowned-language word. The door began to swing.

"Sable." Hazel's voice stopped her in the doorway, orange light cutting across the blank hands. "The night of my exile. On the ridge. You wore my shape — you called my storm. Why that night? Of all the nights, why did the Court need Corvale's witness buried *then*?"

Sable stood very still. For a moment Hazel thought she wouldn't answer, that the door would close and the question would join the long inventory of things the Court kept locked away.

Then Sable said, without turning around:

"Because of what your mother wrote on page one. In the margin. The part the ministers made sure I forgot the moment I finished reading it." Her grip tightened on the doorframe, and the blank fingers went white. "I've spent sixty years trying to dig that sentence back out of wherever they put it, Pemberton. Tomorrow, when you read the contract into the record — read the margins. Read *everything* they didn't bind."

The door closed. The lock spoke again, sealing.

Hazel looked down at the ledger glowing softly in her lap, storm-light pulsing beneath the cover like a second heartbeat, and understood that somewhere in these pages, in ink that remembered what a hollowed woman had been forced to forget, was a sentence worth sixty years of searching —

and one night left to find it.
