## 4. The Hedge-Witch's Letters

The letters arrived the way all of Odile's things arrived: without warning, smelling faintly of smoke and rosemary, and refusing to be ignored.

They came bundled in oilcloth inside a crate of dried yarrow that Marisol ordered every autumn from a trader who worked the eastern passes. Rafael had unpacked a hundred crates like it over four years—yarrow in summer, elderflower in spring, valerian root packed in straw that always left his hands itching. He was elbow-deep in the shipment when his fingers found something that wasn't herb. A bundle, tied with waxed cord, tucked against the crate's slats like a thing hidden rather than stored.

Four letters. The paper was heavy, cream-colored, expensive—the kind Odile had always used for bills and never for anything else. His name was on the topmost envelope, written in the sharp, downward-slanting hand he would have known anywhere, even blindfolded, even drowned.

*Master Rafael Blackwood.*

Not *Rafael.* Not *boy.* *Master.* She had never once called him that while he lived under her roof. The formality of it made his stomach turn before he'd even broken the seal.

He stood there in the back room of the apothecary, dust sifting down through the lamplight, and told himself it was nothing. Old correspondence, misdelivered, meant for someone else. There were other Blackwoods in the world, surely. Other boys who'd run away from hedge-witches at fifteen with nothing but a change of clothes and a scar on his—

On *their* palms. On their palms.

He carried the bundle upstairs to his room above the shop, set it on the desk beside the prophecy book he still hadn't shown anyone, and sat looking at both of them for a long time. Outside his window, the valley went on being the valley: Mrs. Okafor beating her rugs at exactly four o'clock, the bell over the bakery door, children shouting in the lane where no child's voice ever sounded quite as loud as it should. A valley where magic could not cross the borders. A valley where nothing ever happened to him.

Except now two objects on his desk disagreed about that.

He opened the oldest letter first. It was dated six weeks after he'd run.

---

*Master Rafael,*

*I trust this finds you. I have been assured it will, which is more than I can say for most correspondence.*

*You will forgive the delay. When you left—and I use the word "left" with some precision, as I am given to understand that is the account you prefer—I assumed grief would bring you home within the month. You were always a sentimental creature. It is your worst quality and the only one I never managed to train out of you.*

*But you have not come home, and I begin to suspect you cannot.*

*Do you remember the night you fled? Of course you do. You remember everything; it is your gift and your affliction. You will remember that the wards on my cottage failed that evening—all of them, at once, every ward I had laid in fifteen years of careful work. You will remember that I screamed at you, that I blamed you, that I beat you for it. I have had time to reconsider that beating. Consider this letter its apology, such as it is.*

*My wards did not fail, boy. They were broken. From the outside. By practitioners far better funded than I am.*

*I did not lose you, Rafael. Someone took you from me. And whoever they are, they are paying me very well not to look for you.*

---

Rafael read the paragraph three times. Then a fourth. The lamp guttered, righted itself.

*Paying her.* The words wouldn't settle into meaning. They slid off, like water off waxed cloth. He read on.

---

*You will want to know who, and I cannot tell you, because I do not know. They approach through intermediaries. The money arrives by means that suggest either great power or great cowardice—I have never decided which. What I know is this: twice a year, a purse arrives with enough coin to keep me comfortable for the season, and with it, a report of your health so terse it might have been written by a man being tortured. "He is well." "He has taken up work in an apothecary." "He is growing tall."*

*That last one cost them extra, I think. Or perhaps they simply knew I would ask.*

*I write to you because I cannot help myself, and because someday you will find these letters—they always find their recipients eventually, if the sender pays for good paper—and I would rather you hear it from me than construct some nobler version of events. You will be tempted to believe I sold you. Do not bother. If I had sold you, I would have gotten a better price.*

*Your affectionate mistress,*
*O.*

---

Rafael set the letter down very carefully, the way you set down something that might be alive.

His hands were shaking. He watched them shake with a strange detachment, as though they belonged to the boy in the reports—*he is well, he is growing tall*—a boy being described in terse dispatches to the woman who had beaten him for failures that weren't his.

Someone had bought him. Not sold—bought. Someone had wanted him in this valley badly enough to break a hedge-witch's wards, extract him, install him here, and then pay his captor—an abductor? rescuer?—an annuity to keep quiet about it.

He thought: *Marisol.*

And immediately thought: *no.* Because Marisol cried at weddings she wasn't invited to. Because Marisol kept a jar of honey on the shelf labeled FOR RAFAEL WHEN SAD. Because Marisol had taken in a fifteen-year-old stranger with no references and no history and asked him only one question—"Can you tell salt from sugar by smell?"—and hired him when he said yes.

People didn't buy children for apothecary work.

People bought children for what was *in* them.

The second letter was dated a year later. Shorter. Angrier.

---

*Master Rafael,*

*The payments continue. The reports continue. I have stopped asking questions because questions, apparently, reduce the payment, which tells me everything about the character of my benefactors and nothing I did not already suspect about money.*

*I will tell you what I have deduced, since deduction is the only inheritance I ever gave you worth having. They broke my wards from outside, which required knowledge of their construction, which I shared with no one—which means they scouted me for years before they took you. Years, boy. This was not opportunity. This was husbandry.*

*Second: they pay me to stay away, but they have never paid me to stay silent. Mark the distinction. Silence could be bought with a threat. Distance costs coin. Whatever they fear is not gossip. It is proximity.*

*Third: they let you write to me. Did you know that? Your letters arrive, and mine go unanswered—but yours arrive. Which means they read them. Which means they are watching you so closely they can afford to let you reach toward the one person who might tell you the truth.*

*Unless, of course, they are certain you will never believe me.*

*O.*

---

Had he written to her? He tried to remember. Yes—in those first raw months, he must have. He remembered ink, remembered crying onto paper in the little room behind the apothecary, remembered sealing letters he never sent because he didn't know where she lived anymore. Letters he'd burned in the stove. Hadn't he?

Or had he sent them? He couldn't find the memory's edges. Four years of memories, and suddenly he was checking each one for seams, like a tailor inspecting cloth for someone else's stitching.

*He is well. He has taken up work in an apothecary. He is growing tall.*

Who wrote those words? Who stood outside his life taking notes?

The third letter was three years old, and it frightened him more than the others, because in it Odile's voice changed—not softer, never that, but *quieter*, the way her voice went when she was genuinely afraid and covering it with precision.

---

*Master Rafael,*

*A woman came today. Not an intermediary—a principal. She wore gray, and she did not remove her gloves, and she looked at my shelves the way a farmer looks at a field he intends to burn.*

*She asked me what I had put into you.*

*I told her the truth: herbs, discipline, and a healthy respect for consequences. She smiled at that, and I have seen better smiles on wolves, and I told her so, and she laughed, and I did not.*

*She said: "You misunderstand what you raised. You did not raise an apprentice. You raised a door, and doors do not belong to the houses they're built into."*

*I asked her what the door opened onto. She said: "Nothing yet. That is why we are all still alive."*

*Then she paid me double and left. I have been drinking since noon and I intend to continue. Take this however you like, boy, but if you are reading this, then the paper found you, which means you found yourself first, which means the door is thinking about opening.*

*Do not open it.*

*Whatever they promised you, whatever the valley promised you, whatever you have begun to suspect—do not open it.*

*O.*

---

*A door.*

Rafael sat very still. Below him, muffled through the floorboards, he could hear Marisol moving around the closed shop, doing the end-of-day counting, her footsteps tracing the same path they'd traced for four years. *Click of the till. Rattle of jars. Her humming—off-key, always off-key, "The Miller's Daughter," a song he'd learned to love without ever learning its name.*

A door. Doors don't belong to the houses they're built into.

He thought about the prophecy book hidden under his mattress, the date circled in century-old ink, *his* name in it, *the vessel wakes.* He thought about the wards fraying at the border like old rope, and the way something beyond them had tasted his presence—had *known* him, the way a tongue knows a missing tooth.

And he thought about a valley built where magic cannot cross. Built, or *raised*, the way you raise walls around a thing you're keeping.

The fourth letter was the newest. The seal was unbroken. The date was eleven months ago—right around when the wards had started fraying, right around when the trader's routes had shifted and this crate had been misdirected, delayed, sitting in some warehouse while the paper inside waited for him with a patience that felt almost deliberate.

He broke it. Odile's hand was different here—larger, looser, drunk or hurried or both.

---

*Boy,*

*The payments have stopped.*

*For nineteen years they paid, and last month the purse did not come, and this week a letter came instead, unsigned, and it said: "No longer necessary."*

*No longer necessary. As though I were a fence that had served its purpose. As though the thing they were fencing in had grown too large for fences.*

*So I am telling you plainly what I have spent four years telling you sideways: you were purchased. Your whole life in that valley was purchased. The kindnesses, the routines, the small warm ordinary life—they were installed, boy, the way I installed your Latin and your manners. Someone designed a cage and furnished it with people who mostly don't even know they're jailers, and the rent on your affection has been paid twice yearly since before you could walk.*

*Mostly don't know. Sit with that word. Mostly.*

*I taught you to read people, so read this: I am writing to you now not out of love and not out of guilt but because the payments stopping frightens me. When powerful men stop paying, it is because they have changed plans. And when plans change regarding doors—*

*Come see me. Come see me and I will tell you the rest, the part I have never written down. The part about your mother.*

*O.*

---

Rafael read it twice. Then a third time, hunting the trap in it, because Odile always built traps into tenderness, always made affection conditional on obedience. *Come see me*—and the valley's wards were dying, and the border was a day's hard walk, and something beyond them was tasting the air like a beast scenting an open larder.

*The part about your mother.*

He hadn't thought about his mother in years. Not truly. He knew she had died—that was the story, the clean sad story everyone accepted—but when he reached for her face it came up smooth, featureless, like a stone worn blank by water. He knew he'd loved her kitchen. He could not remember what had cooked in it.

He sat with that absence the way you sit with a toothache, probing it, and the absence answered back with something worse: he couldn't remember arriving in the valley either. Fifteen years old, running, exhausted—and then the apothecary, and Marisol's face asking about salt and sugar, as though the journey between had been lifted out of him like a splinter.

*Installed.*

Downstairs, the till clicked shut. Marisol's footsteps paused beneath him—paused, he realized, directly below his room, directly below the floorboards where he sat holding proof that his entire life was furniture arranged by strangers.

"Rafael?" Her voice came up through the wood, warm and careful. "Supper's on. Are you coming down?"

He looked at the letters. At the prophecy book under the mattress, patient as a landmine. At the window, where the valley lay gold and harmless in the evening light, every chimney smoking, every bell accounted for.

"Coming," he called back.

He gathered the letters and hid them with the book, and he went downstairs to eat supper with the woman who might have raised him out of love or out of wages—he genuinely could no longer guess which—and he smiled, and passed the bread, and lied with his whole face, and understood, sitting there in the warm yellow kitchen that smelled of thyme and lamp oil, that he had learned that trick somewhere long before Odile ever got hold of him.

Because you only learn to lie that well inside a house where the truth was never safe.

And as Marisol refilled his cup and chattered about the trader's late deliveries—*the crate was misrouted, imagine, a whole season late*—Rafael caught himself watching her hands. Steady. Practiced. The hands of someone who had done this before.

Who else had sat at this table, eating supper, believing themselves safe?

What happened to the ones who sat here before him?

Marisol laughed at something he'd said, and the laugh caught in her throat halfway through, just for a heartbeat, just long enough—
