## 6. Tea with the Enemy

Sera had been trained to read rooms before she could read words. The Ashen Court did not waste its coin on children who lacked that particular talent, and so when she stepped through the door of the apothecary on Vellum Street—bell chiming, dust motes swirling in the amber light of late afternoon—she took in everything at once: the rows of labeled jars catching sun like a wall of stained glass, the smell of dried lavender and something sharper underneath, iron maybe, or salt; the boy behind the counter with his sleeves rolled up and his hands stained green to the wrist.

He was grinding something. His movements were careful, unhurried, the movements of someone who had done this ten thousand times in a place where nothing ever happened. He looked tired in a way that had nothing to do with sleep. She recognized it because she wore the same look most mornings: the exhaustion of a person who has recently learned that the floor under their feet is not what they thought it was.

*The vessel*, she thought. *This is the vessel.* A nineteen-year-old with herb stains on his knuckles and a chip in his front tooth visible when he glanced up to greet her.

"Welcome to Marisol's," he said. "If you're looking for feverfew, we're out until Thursday. If you're looking for anything else, I can probably help."

"I'm looking for tea, actually," Sera said. "And information."

His hands went still against the mortar. Not frozen—she noted that—he didn't freeze, didn't flinch—but still, the way a deer goes still rather than a rabbit. A deer weighs its options. "We don't sell information."

"You sell it free, then. Even better." She smiled and let a little of the Court's polish fall away from her voice, let herself sound like what she was underneath: a girl from the ash-country who had eaten dirt bread during the famine years and never quite lost the habit of counting exits. "My name is Sera Quill. I've come a long way, and I'd rather have this conversation sitting down than standing up in your shop where anyone can walk in."

"And if I'd rather not have the conversation at all?"

"Then I'll have it anyway," she said gently, "and you'll spend it pretending you're not listening, which is worse for you than for me."

Something moved behind his eyes—not fear exactly. Recognition, maybe. The look of a man meeting the thing from the edge of a prophecy and finding it disappointingly human. He set down the mortar, wiped his hands on his apron, and called toward the back room, "Marisol! Watching the shop!"

No one answered. He waited a beat, then shrugged as if to say *worth trying*, untied the apron, and led her through a curtain of hanging sage into the kitchen beyond.

* * *

The kitchen was small and warm and painfully ordinary. Copper pots hung from hooks gone dull with use. A loaf of bread sat under a cloth on the table, half-eaten, and a cat—gray, enormous, missing an ear—regarded Sera from the windowsill with the flat contempt of all cats for all newcomers. There were three chairs. Rafael pulled out the one nearest the stove without asking whether she wanted it, which told her he wanted her close to the fire, where he could watch her hands.

She approved. It was a sensible instinct, and she said so.

"Don't flatter me," he said. "You came here to do something. Do it."

So she did. She laid it out plainly, the way her handler had drilled into her across four hundred miles of dying road: the Court knew what he was. The Court had known for years—the wards' decay had shown up in their instruments like a candle guttering seen from very far away. And the Court, whatever else it was—and it was many things, few of them kind—was also the only power on the continent with the reach to protect him from what was coming.

"Because things are coming," she said. "You've felt it. Don't tell me you haven't. The wards are fraying like old rope—I crossed them last night and they barely noticed me. Whatever's sleeping under this valley is turning over in its bed, and when it wakes fully, it won't wake alone. Every power within a thousand miles will come here. The Court is simply going to be first." She leaned forward. "Come willingly, Rafael Blackwood. Come as a guest, with rights and standing, and we can shape what happens next. Come as a prize, dragged out of a smoking valley, and you'll be shaped instead."

The kettle began to sing on the stove. Neither of them moved to take it off.

"That's your offer," he said. "Sanctuary."

"That's my offer."

"And what does the Court want in return?"

Sera held his gaze. She had decided, somewhere on the road, that she would not lie to him about the large things—it seemed both crueler and more dangerous to lie to a man whose whole life had already been built out of other people's lies. "They want the god," she said. "And they want you willing, because a vessel that fights the binding shatters, and a shattered vessel releases everything it holds. They want your consent, freely given, to use you as the anchor for a weapon." She paused. "I'm not going to dress that up. You deserve plainer speech than you've gotten so far in your life, I think."

For a long moment he just looked at her, and she watched him turn it over—the offer, the honesty, the terrible arithmetic of it—and she saw the moment the vertigo hit him, the moment the kitchen stopped being a kitchen and became a stage set with him standing on it.

"You're honest," he said finally. "That's almost worse."

"It usually is."

"That's why you're here alone, isn't it? The honesty. You're not their favorite kind of agent."

Sera laughed despite herself—a short, startled sound. "I'm alive and employed. That's more than some honest agents can say." She reached for the kettle, poured two cups of the tea already steeping in the pot—chamomile and something wilder, yarrow maybe—and pushed one across the table. "Drink. You look like you haven't slept."

"I haven't." He wrapped his hands around the cup but didn't drink. "Why do you care? Genuinely. You crossed a dying ward line, walked into a town that hates magic, found the monster of the century, and you're pouring him tea. Why?"

She considered lying again, briefly, out of professional habit, and discarded it. "Because I've spent two years reading the files on you," she said. "Every report, every keeper's log the Court bought or stole. Nineteen years of entries about a child who liked the garden and cried when the goats were slaughtered and asked, every single year, when he would be allowed past the border stones. And every entry ends the same way: *no suspicion displayed; containment holding.*" She looked down into her own cup. "Do you know what it does to a person, reading nineteen years of a life reduced to *containment holding*? It does something. I came here expecting a monster and I found a man who worries about strangers' fevers. So yes. I care. It's inconvenient for both of us."

Rafael stared at her. And it was at that exact moment—the moment the enemy agent said *it does something*—that the back door opened and Marisol came in with a basket of late tomatoes and stopped as though she had walked into a wall made of cold air.

* * *

Marisol had known this day would come the way you know winter will come: abstractly, endlessly postponable, until suddenly it is at the window. She stood in the doorway with tomatoes in her arms and took in the scene in one long breath—the stranger's ash-gray traveling cloak folded over the third chair, the two cups steaming, the writ of the Ashen Court half-visible where the woman had carelessly left it on the table, its wax seal like a drop of old blood—and felt nineteen years of careful silence collapse around her like a roof coming down.

"Get away from him," Marisol said. Her voice came out low and even, which surprised her; she had expected it to shake.

"Marisol—" Rafael started.

"Sit down, mijo." She set the basket on the counter with exaggerated care, one tomato at a time, buying her hands something to do while her mind raced. "Not you," she added, to the woman. "You can stand by the door where I can see all of you."

"I'm not here to hurt him," the woman said.

"No. You're here to recruit him. That's worse." Marisol untied her scarf, hung it on its peg, and made herself cross the kitchen slowly, deliberately, taking the chair between Rafael and the stranger as though furniture could be a fortification. Nineteen years. She had fed this boy nettle soup when he was sick and taught him to read off seed packets and lied to his face at every birthday, every border-stone question, every night he woke shaking from dreams of water and salt, and she had told herself it was mercy, it was protection, it was the oath, and now here was the bill for all of it, presented at her own kitchen table by a girl young enough to be her daughter wearing the colors of the Court.

"You know what I am," Rafael said quietly. It wasn't a question. He was watching her with an expression she had never seen on him before, and she understood, with a lurch, that the knowing had happened already—that the stranger had not brought the knowledge but only confirmed it, that some part of him had suspected since the book, since the border, since always, the way children suspect the truth of things long before adults admit it.

"Yes," Marisol said. Because there was no longer any point in anything else.

"How long?"

"Since before you were born. Since your mother carried you across the flats at night, bleeding, praying, begging us to take you." Her throat tightened. "Since the council drew lots for who would raise you, and I cheated."

"Cheated how?"

"I volunteered. Before the lots were drawn. Because I'd already decided." She folded her hands on the table so they wouldn't see them tremble. "Your mother died eleven days later. And I swore on her grave that you would have one ordinary life, even if it was built on a lie. Especially if it was built on a lie."

The stranger—Quill, the writ had said—shifted in her chair, and Marisol turned on her with nineteen years of banked fury finally finding air. "And you. You sit in my kitchen offering him sanctuary. Sanctuary. From what? From the people who will put him in a deeper cage than this one and call it service? I've read what the Court does with vessels, girl. I'm old enough to remember the last one they 'protected.'"

"The last one they protected died," Quill said evenly. "Holding a binding the valley built and then abandoned. At least we would have been there."

"We were there!" The word tore out of her louder than she meant, and the cat fled the windowsill, and for a moment the kitchen rang with it. "We were there for nineteen years. We were there for every fever and every birthday and every night he cried for a mother he couldn't remember. Don't you dare stand in my house and tell me the Court knows how to care for people. The Court knows how to *use* people. That's the whole of its alphabet."

"Mari." Rafael's voice was soft. "Mari, stop."

She stopped. She realized, distantly, that she was crying, and that she couldn't remember the last time she had cried in front of him—she had been so careful, all these years, so careful to give him only steadiness, only warmth, only the unbroken surface of things.

He was looking at her the way he had looked at the prophecy book, she thought. Like a man reading a document that rewrites everything he thought he knew.

"You chose," he said. "You all chose. The council, the keepers, you—you decided what I am before I could talk, and then you spent my whole life making sure I never got a vote." He wasn't angry. That was the worst part. He sounded almost gentle, almost sorry, the way you sound toward someone you love when you understand them completely for the first time. "And now she comes with her offer, and it's the same thing in different clothes. The Court decides. The valley decides. Everyone stands around this table telling me what I am and what I owe and where I'll be safest, and none of you—" his voice caught, just once, and steadied— "none of you has once asked me what I want to be."

The silence after that was total. The kettle ticked as it cooled. Outside, far off toward the flats, there came a sound like the earth clearing its throat—low, subterranean, patient—and all three of them heard it, and all three of them pretended, for one more moment, that they hadn't.

Marisol reached across the table and took his hand. He let her. But he didn't hold on, and that difference—small as a hairline crack in a jar—told her everything about what the next days would bring.

"There's something under the valley," Rafael said, to neither of them, to both of them. "It's waking up. And I think—" He stopped. Swallowed. Started again. "I think it knows my name."

Quill rose, unhurried, and took her cloak from the chair. "When you're ready to talk terms," she said, "I'm lodging at the inn under the name Wren. I'll leave the writ with you." She set the paper on the table between the teacups, seal upward, and paused at the door. "For what it's worth—of everyone at this table, you're the only one who's asked himself that question. What he wants to be. Keep asking it. It's worth more than any of our offers."

The door closed behind her. The bell chimed. Marisol squeezed his hand harder, and Rafael looked down at the writ of the Ashen Court, and beneath it, faintly, rising through the floorboards like damp through stone, he could feel it again—the vast slow attention turning toward him from under the salt, patient as tide, waiting to hear what he would decide.

That night, the first cracks appeared in the apothecary's windows—thin white lines, branching like frost, like roots, like veins—though nothing had struck the glass at all.
