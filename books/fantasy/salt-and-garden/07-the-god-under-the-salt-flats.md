## 7. The God Under the Salt Flats

The prophecy book gave him three nights to decide, and Rafael used all of them badly.

He did not sleep. He sat in his narrow room above the apothecary with the lamp turned low and the book open on his knees, reading the same passage until the words wore grooves in him:

*When the vessel is nineteen years and one day old, it will hear the dreaming beneath the flats. Let it not go down alone, for what sleeps below has waited long enough to mistake loneliness for love.*

Nineteen years and one day.

Tonight was the day after his birthday. The book had been written in 1903 by a hand that shook, ink blotted where the writer had pressed too hard, as though urgency itself had bled through the page. And it knew. It knew the date. It knew his name—*Rafael*, spelled correctly, which no one outside the valley could have managed—and beneath his name, in letters that seemed darker than the rest, as if they'd been written twice: *the vessel wakes.*

He had spent two days telling himself it was a forgery. A cruel joke. Marisol's face when she'd told him to burn it—pale as milk, hands trembling around a mortar of dried yarrow—had killed that theory. You didn't look like that over a joke. You looked like that when you recognized something you'd spent your whole life pretending not to see.

So on the third night, he went down.

* * *

The entrance to the old salt mines was behind the tannery, where the hillside folded into itself like a held breath. Everyone in the valley knew the mines were there; everyone agreed they were flooded and dangerous and best forgotten, the way people agree about all the things they've decided not to think about. As a boy, Rafael had dared the mouth of the tunnel once, gotten his boots wet, and been dragged home by the ear by old Tomás, who had never raised his voice at anyone before or since.

Now he stood at that same black mouth with a storm lantern in one hand and the prophecy book wrapped in oilcloth against his chest, and the dark inside the tunnel breathed out at him—cold, mineral, tasting of iron and old rain.

"You don't have to do this," he said aloud, to himself, because there was no one else to say it. Sera—the Court agent with the tired eyes who drank tea like she was apologizing for it—had offered him sanctuary, and Marisol had nearly come to blows with her over it, and through all of it Rafael had understood one thing with terrible clarity: everyone wanted to carry him somewhere. No one wanted to walk beside him into the place he actually needed to go.

So he went alone. Not because he was brave. Because he was done being carried.

The tunnel swallowed him by degrees. First the light of the valley behind him shrank to a coin, then a thread, then nothing, and the lantern became the whole world—a small gold circle swinging in an ocean of stone. Water ran somewhere below, echoing strangely, so that he couldn't tell if it flowed toward him or away. The walls wept brine. Long crystals of salt grew from the seams like teeth, or like fingers reaching for something, and when his sleeve brushed them they left white streaks on the wool that would not wash out.

He counted his steps because counting kept the fear at arm's length. At four hundred, the floor tilted downward. At six hundred, the water reached his ankles—shockingly cold, then shockingly not, warming around his legs like something greeting him. At eight hundred, the ceiling opened into the first gallery, and Rafael stopped counting, because counting suddenly seemed obscene.

The gallery was vast beyond the lantern's reach. He stood on a shelf of rock at the water's edge, and before him stretched a flooded cathedral of salt: pillars rising from black water into blackness above, walls glittering faintly even where the light didn't touch, as if the stone remembered being lit and refused to fully forget. The water lay perfectly still. Not calm—still. There was a difference, and the difference was attention.

Rafael's skin prickled. Every hair on his arms rose, and beneath the prickling came something else, something worse: a feeling of being *known*. Not watched—watching he could have borne. Known. The way you know your own hand without looking at it.

"Hello," he said, and his voice cracked on the second syllable, and the word went out across the water and did not echo. The gallery ate it. Nothing in this place echoed. Sound went in and stayed, the way the valley kept everything in.

Then, from everywhere and nowhere—from the water, from the walls, from the space between his own heartbeat—

*You came.*

It wasn't a voice. Voices have timbre and direction and a throat behind them. This was more like weather, like pressure, like the moment before lightning when the air itself leans toward the strike. But it made words anyway, slow words, patient words, words that had clearly been rehearsed for a very long time in a very empty room.

"I—" Rafael's lantern hand shook. Light jittered across the pillars. "I came."

*Yes.* A pause, long enough that he felt it in his chest, like a held breath the size of a century. *I know your step. I have heard it every year of your life. Above me. Small, then larger. You walked over my ceiling ten thousand times and never once looked down.*

Rafael's throat closed. He thought of all the ordinary days—hauling water, sorting herbs, laughing with Marisol in the shop while the floorboards creaked—and realized the floorboards had been creaking over *this*. Over a sleeping thing the size of a landscape, listening to his footsteps the way a prisoner listens to birdsong through a barred window.

"What are you?" he whispered. He already knew. The book had told him. His ancestors' wards had told him. But some questions you ask anyway, because the asking is how you survive the answer.

The darkness considered him. When the answer came, it came gently, which was somehow the most frightening thing yet.

*I am what your family buried under their garden. I am the salt in the earth and the stillness in the air. Your valley is my sleep, little vessel. Its quiet is my quiet. Its borders are the edges of my dream.*

"And me?" His voice sounded very young to himself. "What am I?"

Another pause. Longer. And in that pause Rafael felt something move through the water—not physically, but the way a mood moves through a house. Something enormous turning over in its sleep, careful, so careful, the way you're careful with the one fragile thing in a room full of everything else.

*You are the door,* it said. *And I am so sorry for that.*

* * *

He should have run. He knew that later, looking back—he knew it in the moment, too, knew it in the animal part of him that wanted his legs to move. But instead he sat down on the rock shelf, boots in the cold shallows, lantern beside him, and asked questions, because the god's sorrow was doing something to him that terror couldn't undo.

"Why sorry?"

*Because doors are made to be used. Because every vessel before you was a door, and none of them chose it. They were born into it, the way you were born into the valley, and when they came down here—as you have come down—they found me waiting, and they were afraid, and their fear fed back into the binding until the binding was mostly made of fear.* The pressure shifted, and Rafael felt it like a change in altitude. *I do not want your fear. I have had a hundred years of it. It tastes like rust.*

"How many," Rafael said. "How many vessels before me?"

Silence. Then, softly, with a care that broke something open in his chest:

*Nine.*

Nine children. Nine lives lived above this ceiling, walking and laughing and growing old or not growing old, each of them carrying a piece of this—of the vast, patient, drowning-dark thing below—and none of them knowing until the day the knowing arrived, the way it had arrived for him, in a book, on a date, in ink that seemed to press too hard.

"Did they come down here? All of them?"

*Some. The brave ones. The angry ones. One sang to me. She had a bad voice and she sang anyway, for years, until her heart gave out. I keep the song. Would you like—*

"No." Too fast, too sharp. His own voice bounced off nothing and vanished. "Not yet. I can't—I can't hold any more of this tonight."

*That is wise,* the god said, and there was no mockery in it, only a gentleness so old and worn it had gone smooth as sea glass. *I have waited a thousand years. I can wait while you breathe.*

And that was the terrible kinship, Rafael thought. That was the thing he'd carry up out of this dark whether he wanted to or not. The god wasn't a monster. Monsters were simple. This was lonely—lonely on a scale that made his own nineteen years of quiet valley life look crowded and loud. It had listened to footsteps for a century. It remembered a woman's bad singing. It apologized to the child bred to be its door.

"You could break out," he said slowly. "Couldn't you? If you tried. Through me."

A long stillness. Even the water seemed to stop attending.

*Yes.*

"But you haven't."

*No.*

"Why?"

The answer took so long coming that Rafael lit a second candle stub from his pack just to have something to do with his hands. When it finally came, it came like a tide, slow and total:

*Because the last time I moved freely, your ancestors drowned a war in salt, and I swore—if ever I slept, I would wake only through a hand offered, not a door broken. I have kept that oath through nine vessels and a hundred years of dark. I am keeping it now. Do you understand, little vessel? I am not your cage. I have been waiting, all this time, to be let out politely.*

Rafael laughed. It came out wrong—half sob, half something wilder—and the sound died in the non-echoing dark, and he pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes and sat there at the edge of the flooded cathedral with a god's patience resting on him like snowfall.

Above him, the valley slept. Marisol, probably awake, staring at the ceiling of the room where she'd lied to him with kindness for nineteen years. The wards fraying like old rope. The Ashen Court circling somewhere beyond the border, patient as vultures. And below all of it, this: not a monster, not a weapon, but a prisoner who had chosen to be gentle, waiting for someone to open the door on purpose.

"The Court wants me," he said at last. "They want to use me to wake you under their control. The valley wants to put me back to sleep. My—" he stumbled on the word, "—my mother's mistress sold me here before I could walk. Everyone has a plan for me. Nobody has asked me anything until tonight."

*Then let me ask,* the god said. *What do you want, Rafael Blackwood?*

His name in that pressure-voice, that weather-voice, undid him completely. He cried, quietly, without dignity, sitting in the cold shallows of a drowned mine, and the god waited through it with the same patience it had spent on a century of footsteps, and did not once tell him to stop.

When he could speak again, he said: "I want it to be my choice. Whatever happens. Mine."

*Then it will be,* said the god. *I swear it by the salt and the long dark. When you call me, I will come as a guest. When you refuse me, I will remain a rumor. Your hand opens the door, little vessel. No other hand can.*

Rafael looked out across the black water, at the pillars going up into darkness, at the glitter of ancient salt remembering light. He felt the shape of the thing beneath him now—not its size, which was unbearable, but its *shape*: a wound in the world that had decided, against all evidence, to be kind.

"I need time," he said.

*Take it,* the god answered. *Time is the one thing I have too much of.*

* * *

He climbed for what felt like hours, and the tunnel gave him back to the night in stages—first the smell of grass, then the coin of starlight, then the open sky, huge and indifferent and full of ordinary constellations. Behind him the hillside sealed its black mouth and went back to looking like geology.

But the world had changed. He could feel it in his bones, in the soles of his feet, in the salt-dried streaks on his sleeves: the valley's famous stillness was thinner now, translucent, a membrane rather than a wall. And beneath everything—beneath the crickets, beneath the wind, beneath his own pulse—there was a new silence, deep and vast and attentive, lying under the flats like a sleeper who has heard the door open upstairs and is waiting, without moving, to learn whether the footstep is coming toward the cellar or away.

Rafael stood at the top of the hill with the oilcloth-wrapped book against his chest and looked down at the sleeping roofs of the only home he'd ever known.

In the morning, he knew, things would move. The Court would push. The council would argue. Marisol would try to stand between him and everything, the way she always had, loving him in the shape of a lie. The wards had hours left in them, maybe less—he could feel them dying now, could feel the frayed edges humming like cut wire.

And far below, patient as winter, something waited to find out what he would choose.

He started down the hill toward the lights of the apothecary, and behind him, so faint he might have imagined it, the ground hummed—one low note, held, like a singer drawing breath.
