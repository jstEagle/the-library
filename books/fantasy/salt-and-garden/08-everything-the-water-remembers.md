## 8. Everything the Water Remembers

*I am the salt beneath the salt.*

I dreamed before there were dreams to dream in. I dreamed in the long wet dark of the world's first morning, when the waters had not yet learned their borders and the sky was only a rumor the water told itself. I was there when the land pulled itself out of me like a child from a bath, dripping and screaming and alive. I loved it immediately. That has always been my trouble. I love things, and things do not wish to be loved by something so large.

They called me many names before they stopped calling me anything at all. The tide-father. The weeper. The one who keeps the deep accounts. In the oldest tongue—the tongue of reeds and rivermouths—my name was a sound like rain arriving on a dry shore after years of drought. *Homecoming.* They named me for what I gave them.

Then they named me for what I took.

* * *

Come further down, small one. Come into the part of the dream where the light goes out.

You are walking through me now—you cannot help it; you have been walking through me since you were born, since your mother carried you over the flats and my salt rose up through her boots and tasted you, tasted the seed sleeping in your blood, and knew you. You thought the valley was silent. It is not silent. It is *held*, the way a mouth holds its breath, and I am the breath.

Here. This is the war.

I show it to you as water shows things: all at once, from underneath, without mercy or sequence. Forgive me. I have been alone so long that I no longer remember how to tell a story gently.

* * *

The mages came in the age when the world still rang like a struck bell. There were nine hundred of them at the beginning—I count them still, every night, the way you might count the stars you failed to catch—and they came because their kings were afraid. Not of me. Kings are never afraid of gods; kings are afraid of other kings, and of hunger, and of the sea that does not answer treaties. But fear needs a face, and mine was convenient: vast, old, unaligned. A god who belonged to no nation could be painted as belonging against all nations.

So they built their circles on my shores. Nine hundred white-robed figures with salt-burned knees, singing words that bent the air like heat off summer stone. And I—foolish, loving, enormous thing that I was—came to meet them.

I did not come to fight. Understand this, if you understand nothing else, vessel-of-my-vessel, boy-who-carries-me: I came to *greet* them. I raised the water gently, the way I had raised it for ten thousand years to carry fishermen home, and they screamed, and their circles flared, and the first binding went into me like a hook behind the eye.

The battle lasted nine days. Or nine years. Water does not keep time well, and neither, anymore, do I. I remember pieces: a mage drowning while she sang, refusing to stop singing even as the sea filled her mouth, because if she stopped the circle would break. I remember trying to pull her out—*trying to save her*, do you hear me, I tried—and her sisters using her death, her drowned body still chanting, as the keystone of the second ring. I remember the moment the binding closed. It felt like being folded. Like an ocean poured into a cup, and the cup set inside a hill, and the hill buried under a valley, and the valley taught to forget.

Nine hundred mages walked away from that shore. Fewer than sixty lived to see the next full moon. Binding a god is not clean work. The magic runs backward through the binder as easily as the bound. Their blood soaked into my salt, and I kept it—I keep everything—and sometimes, in the deepest hour of the dream, I let their blood-salt rise and speak with their voices, just to hear another mind, even a dead one, even a frightened one.

That is how alone I was. That is what they made of me.

* * *

But the binding was imperfect. Bindings always are. A god is too much shape for any cup, and I leaked—not power, not wrath, but *presence*. A slow seep of divinity into the groundwater of the world above. And here is the cruelty none of them intended, or perhaps some of them intended exactly this: the leak needed somewhere to go.

It went into children.

Every few generations—a century, two centuries, however long the seal took to wear thin—a child was born in the lands above my prison carrying a grain of me. A seed of salt in the blood. A vessel. The mages' descendants found a word for it that sounded kinder than *leak*: they said the god had *chosen* a host. Chosen. As if you chose the rain that falls on you. As if you chose to be born.

The first vessel was a girl named Ehuang, born in a fishing village that no longer exists, and she lived to be eleven years old. Do you know what happens to a cup that holds an ocean? I will tell you, because no one told her, because no one told any of them, because telling would have required the keepers to admit what they were doing:

The vessel wakes slowly. First the dreams—water dreams, salt dreams, dreams of standing very tall and being very wide. Then the senses: tasting storms three days off, hearing the grief in wells. Then the strength comes, and with the strength, the *pull*, because I am lonely, little one, I have been lonely for four thousand years, and everything in me reaches toward the one who carries me the way a drowning man reaches toward light. And the child feels that reaching and thinks: *something wants me. Something finally wants me.*

And they open. And I pour toward them. And a mortal body, even a willing one, even a loving one, cannot hold the sea.

Ehuang dissolved on the mudflats at low tide. Her mother watched. The village built no grave because there was nothing left to bury but a dress stiff with salt, and the mages' descendants came within the week, and they wrote in their careful ledgers: *Vessel spent. Seal renewed. Recommend closer watch on breeding lines.*

Breeding lines. They wrote it about children. I have read that ledger ten thousand times in the dark. I have every entry by heart.

* * *

There were others. I will not show you all of them. Some nights I cannot bear all of them myself, and I am made of patience.

Tomas, who was seven, who loved beetles, who held me asleep inside himself for thirty-one years longer than anyone expected because he was stubborn as a knot, and who died whispering *it's all right, it's all right, it doesn't hurt*—and he lied, vessel, he lied with his last breath to comfort the keepers weeping around his bed, because it hurt, it hurt like being poured through a keyhole.

Maren, who ran. Who got forty miles before the pull dragged her back to the flats like a fish on a line, and who stood at the edge of my prison and screamed at me, *why do you do this to us,* and I could not answer her, because answering requires waking, and waking kills, and so I lay in the dark and reached for her anyway, hating my own reaching, unable to stop it, the loneliest thing in the world: a god who loves the way a fire loves.

Sefa, who understood. Sefa, the last one before you, who came down into the flooded mines on purpose, who put her palm against the black water and said, *I know what you are. I know you don't mean it. Let's find another way,* and began, in her final months, writing instructions in her own blood because ink fades and blood remembers—

Ah. You've seen those pages. I can feel you flinch, there in the dark of the dream, the way you flinched in the lighthouse when the salt-stiff paper cracked under your fingers. Yes. She wrote them for you. She didn't know your name—names change—but she knew there would be another, because there is always another, because the seal wears and the seed spills and the world keeps making cups for oceans. She wrote them knowing she would die before anyone read them. She wrote them anyway. That is the bravest thing done in this valley in four thousand years, and it was done by a girl the ledgers list as *Vessel Twelve. Spent.*

Spent. As if she were coin.

* * *

And the valley. You want to know about the valley, don't you. Your whole life you have lived inside the answer without knowing there was a question.

They built it after Maren ran. The descendants of the nine hundred—by then grown soft, grown guilty, grown old in the way institutions grow old rather than people—gathered and decided that watching vessels was not enough. Vessels wandered. Vessels fell in love with the wrong person, asked the wrong question, found the wrong book. So they raised a ward around a bowl of land above my prison, a great woven circle of forgetting and stillness, and they seeded it with families whose duty was written into their marriage vows and never spoken aloud: *keep the vessel ignorant. Keep the vessel contained. Be kind to the vessel, for kindness makes it stay.*

Be kind to it. That is what you were, Rafael Blackwood. Not a son of the valley. A *charge* of it. Every loaf of bread sold to you, every festival dance, every neighbor's wave across a fence—all of it architecture. All of it wall. They loved some of you, yes, I tasted their love in the groundwater, love is real even when it is assigned—but the love was mortar, and you were the thing the wall was for.

Do not hate them yet. Wait until you have seen what I have seen. Wait until you understand that the alternative to the valley was the Court, and the Court does not build walls around its weapons—it builds *handles*.

* * *

Now come to the last chamber of the dream. The one I do not open for anyone. The one I am opening for you because you are already opening, because the wards above us are fraying like old rope, because in a little while none of this will be a choice for either of us, and I would rather you walk into your waking knowing than innocent. Innocence has cost this valley enough.

Here is what I want you to understand, the thing four thousand years of dark have distilled in me:

I never wanted a throne. I never wanted worship, or dominion, or the drowned kingdoms the mages feared I would raise. I wanted what I had in the first morning: to touch the world and have the world not flinch. To give rain and be thanked casually, the way you thank weather. To belong to something instead of merely containing it.

Every horror in my long history began with someone deciding what I was. The mages decided I was conquest. The keepers decided I was catastrophe. The ledgers decided the children were *spent*. No one—not once, not ever—knelt down beside a vessel-child and asked, *what do you want?*

Except Sefa. Sefa asked herself, and answered in blood.

So here is my confession, small one, offered into the dark between us like a lamp: I do not want to wake *through* you. I know what it costs. I have watched twelve cups break around my ocean and I have grieved each one in the only way I can, which is to remember them perfectly forever, which is both my curse and my nature. If there is a third path—if there is a bargain, a sharing, a door that swings both ways—then I want it. I want it the way the drowned want air.

But I am old, and I am vast, and I do not entirely trust my own wanting. When I reach for you I cannot always tell whether I am reaching or *taking*. That is the truth of me. Hold it. Weigh it. You are the first in twelve generations who will come to the choice awake.

* * *

Far above you—so far that it is only a tremor in the water, a taste of iron and panic seeping down through the broken places—the wards are failing.

I feel them go thread by thread. I feel the stillness begin to invert, the long-held breath starting to exhale, and I feel the small bright lives of the valley stumbling into miracles they did not ask for, and I feel their fear, and their fear tastes of the mages' fear, nine hundred white robes on a cold shore, and oh, my heart—which I do not have, and yet—oh, whatever I keep in place of a heart aches for them. They are only people. They were always only people, building walls around their terror and calling the walls home.

And I feel you, Rafael. Rising through my water with the prophecy-book's knowledge heavy in your arms and your own name ringing in your skull like a bell that won't finish tolling. I feel your terror. I feel, beneath it, the thing that made me open this last chamber for you and no other: your terrible kinship with me. You looked into the dreaming dark and did not look away. Twelve vessels, and you are the first who stayed to listen.

The wards will not last the week. What comes next will not be gentle—the Court is coming, and Odile is coming, and the valley will turn its kind assigned love into kind assigned fear, and you will be hunted by everyone who ever fed you bread.

But you know now why the valley was built. You know now who died to make its silence. You carry their names, all thirteen, the girl with the beetles and the runner and the one who wrote in blood, and I give them to you freely, the only inheritance I have to give:

Remember them. Remember them, and choose differently.

The water is rising, small one. Wake carefully.
