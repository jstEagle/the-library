## 9. Wards Down

The first sign was the bread.

Tomas Reyes had baked in the Valley of Stillness for thirty years, and in all that time his loaves had risen exactly as they should—obediently, predictably, the way everything in the valley behaved. Magic could not cross the border, and so yeast rose on chemistry alone, and rain fell because clouds were clouds, and nothing ever surprised anyone. That was the bargain of the valley. That was its whole character.

But on the morning after Rafael Blackwood turned nineteen, Tomas pulled three loaves from his oven and found them floating.

Not hovering, exactly. Drifting. They bobbed at the height of his chest like little brown boats on an invisible tide, shedding crumbs that fell upward for a moment before remembering themselves and drifting back down. Tomas stood in his flour-dusted kitchen with a peel in his hands and watched a loaf turn slowly in the air, and he thought, with the strange calm of a man whose world has just tilted one degree off true: *I did not put anything in the starter.*

He reached for the nearest loaf and it came to him willingly, pressing itself into his palms warm as a sleeping animal. The touch of it made something hum behind his sternum—a feeling he had no name for, a feeling like being *noticed* by the air itself.

Outside his window, Mrs. Okonkwo's garden had bloomed out of season. All of it. Every rose, every late aster, every stubborn marigold she'd been nursing toward spring—all open at once, all facing not the sun but the east, toward the salt flats, as if waiting for someone to arrive.

The valley had been still for two hundred years. On this morning, it began to hold its breath.

* * *

Rafael felt it happen from inside the apothecary, and what he felt was silence ending.

For nineteen years the valley's quiet had been the texture of his life—the way other people lived inside weather or music, he lived inside stillness. It pressed against him gently everywhere he went, a hush so complete he had stopped hearing it, the way you stop hearing your own heartbeat. He had thought it was peace. Standing at Marisol's counter with the prophecy book open between them, he understood suddenly that it had never been peace. It had been pressure. A hand laid flat over water to keep it from moving.

At dawn the hand lifted.

It didn't lift all at once. It lifted the way a bandage comes off—catching, tearing, leaving raw places. Rafael felt each one. There was a sound only he seemed to hear, a low ringing like a wet finger circling the rim of a glass, and beneath it a vast slow turning, far below the earth, like something enormous shifting its weight in sleep.

"Marisol," he said. His voice came out strange. "Something's happening."

She was already at the window, her hands wrapped around a mug of tea she wasn't drinking. Beyond the glass, the morning light lay wrong on the street—too golden, too deliberate, pooling in shapes that looked almost like letters in a language no one had written down. A cat sat motionless in the middle of the road, staring at nothing, or at everything, its fur lifting in waves as though wind moved through it where there was no wind.

"The wards," Marisol said quietly. "They're not fraying anymore. They're going."

"How long?"

"I don't know." She turned to look at him, and Rafael saw something in her face he had never seen there before—not the warmth he knew, not even fear exactly, but grief arriving ahead of schedule. Grief for something that hadn't happened yet. "Hours. Maybe less. Rafael—" She set down the mug. Her hands were shaking. "When it goes, the stillness doesn't just end. Everything the valley has been holding back comes through. And everything the valley has been holding *down*—"

She didn't finish. She didn't have to. He had read the book. He knew what slept under the salt flats, and he knew, now, whose sleeping body it wore like a coat.

Outside, the church bell began to ring. No one was pulling the rope.

* * *

By noon, the miracles had turned.

That was the thing no one had prepared for—that the valley's magic would not return like water filling a dry well, clean and welcome. It returned like floodwater through a broken dam, carrying everything upstream of it: silt and wreckage and things that had been waiting a very long time in the dark.

The roses in Mrs. Okonkwo's garden kept blooming, and then kept blooming past any reason, petals crowding petals until the bushes split their own stems with a sound like green knuckles cracking, and by afternoon the garden had swallowed her fence and was working patiently at her porch steps. She stood at her window and wept without knowing why.

Old Ferro's bad hip healed itself at breakfast, and by lunch his good hip had begun to ache instead, and by evening both legs remembered every injury they had ever suffered at once, and he lay on his floor unable to stand, flooded with forty years of pain arriving all together like overdue letters.

The well in the town square began to sing. Not metaphorically—though the singing was beautiful, which somehow made it worse. It sang in a woman's voice, low and looping, a lullaby in no language anyone knew, and children gathered at its edge until their parents dragged them away, and afterward none of those children could be woken from their naps before sundown, and when they woke they all described the same dream: a shore made of salt, and something enormous standing in the shallow water, patient as stone, watching them with an attention that felt almost like love.

And the shadows. That was the detail that broke the town's composure. Every shadow in the valley had begun, very slightly, to lean east. At noon, when shadows should have pooled small and neat beneath their owners', they stretched instead toward the salt flats—thin and straining, like dogs pulling at leashes.

People did what frightened people do. They gathered. They talked in doorways and over fences, and their talk circled and circled like birds looking for somewhere to land, and finally it landed where frightened talk always lands.

On the stranger. On the boy who wasn't quite one of them. On Rafael.

* * *

Sera felt the wards die from half a mile outside the border, and it nearly took her off her feet.

She had been walking the perimeter for two days, mapping the decay—she was good at this, reading the structure of a ward the way a surveyor reads land—and what she had mapped was a slow unraveling, weeks of life left in the weave, months if the vessel stayed calm. Then, sometime in the night, the whole pattern had changed key. The threads weren't unraveling anymore. They were *reaching*, stretching outward like roots toward her, tasting the air, and the taste they were looking for was not hers.

It was looking for itself. For the piece of the god walking around in a young man's skin.

She crossed the border at dusk and the crossing told her everything. Where the ward had been, there was now a seam she felt pass over her like the membrane of a soap bubble popping in reverse—and beyond it, the valley's famous stillness was gone. In its place: pressure. The whole basin was pressurized with unspent magic, the way a room is pressurized before a storm breaks a window. Her own small charms, dormant since she'd entered the borderlands, woke against her skin like insects stirring. Her writ of authority, folded in her breast pocket, had grown warm.

In the town, she found panic wearing its Sunday clothes. People moved quickly and spoke quietly. Shop doors were locked at hours when they were never locked. And everywhere, in every conversation she pretended not to overhear, one name recurred, passed mouth to mouth like a coin everyone wanted to give away.

*The Blackwood boy. The apothecary's ward. It has to be him—it was always going to be him.*

She found Rafael at the edge of it all, standing in the doorway of the apothecary with his arms full of bundled herbs he was distributing to anyone who asked—feverfew, willow bark, valerian, the ordinary pharmacopeia of a town trying to treat a sickness that wasn't in any body. His sleeves were rolled. His hands were steady. But Sera had been trained to read people the way she read wards, and what she read in him was a man standing in a doorway of a house that was already burning, handing out cups of water.

"You shouldn't be out here," she said, coming up beside him, keeping her voice low, keeping her hood up.

"They need the valerian." He didn't look at her. "Half the town hasn't slept. The well won't stop singing."

"That's not why I mean." She glanced at the street—at the knots of people, and the way conversation died and eyes sharpened wherever he passed. "They're not afraid of the magic, Rafael. They're afraid of you. You're the explanation they can carry."

For a moment he went very still. Then he handed the last bundle of feverfew to a woman hurrying past without meeting her eyes, and Sera watched the woman take it the way you take something from a leper—carefully, touching only the paper.

"My whole life," Rafael said, "this place felt like the one solid thing. Like the rest of the world was weather and this was ground." He laughed, and there was nothing in the laugh. "Turns out I'm the weather."

Behind them, down the street, someone had begun to shout.

* * *

The crowd formed the way crowds do—not summoned but accreted, drawn by the gravity of shared fear, and by the time Rafael reached the square it had already found its shape: fifty or sixty townsfolk ringed around the well, and at the center of them, standing on the well's stone lip with her shawl clutched at her throat, Alderman Voss.

Voss had been alderman for eleven years, and she was not a cruel woman. That was the terrible part, Rafael thought, pushing through the crowd's edge. She was a fair-minded, decent, tired woman who had spent her whole life believing the valley was safe, and safety, once broken, must be explained, and explanations require a cause, and causes require a culprit. She held the old council ledger under one arm like a shield.

"—and I am not saying we do anything rash," she was saying, in the voice of a woman who had already decided. "I am saying we face facts. The wards are failing. The valley is—doing *what it is doing*—" a gesture that encompassed the singing well, the leaning shadows, the roses devouring Mrs. Okonkwo's porch, "—and the prophecies, which I have now read, thank you, Petra, are not ambiguous. *The vessel wakes.* Well. Who among us is the vessel? Who among us arrived here as a child, with no family, no history, bought and paid for by persons unknown?"

The crowd murmured. Somewhere near the front, someone said, "He's not one of us," and the awful thing was not the malice in it—there wasn't much malice—but the relief. The relief of having somewhere to put the fear.

"He grew up here," someone else said—old Ferro's daughter, Ana, her voice cracking. "He's delivered medicine to my father's bedside for four years. He's *one of us*."

"He's the reason my father can't walk," Ferro himself called out from a chair someone had carried into the square for him, his face gray with resurrected pain. "I don't hate the boy. But I know what broke me, and it started the day he was born."

"That's not how it works," Ana said. "You don't know that—"

"People!" Voss raised her hands. "No one is accusing anyone. But the council has a duty, and the duty is containment. If there is a vessel among us, then the vessel must be bound again—safely, humanely, according to the rites our grandparents' grandparents used. That is all I am proposing. A binding. For everyone's protection." Her eyes found Rafael across the crowd, and in them he saw not hatred but something worse: apology. "*Especially* his."

The crowd turned. All of them, as one body, the way flowers had turned toward the salt flats that morning. Fifty faces he had known his whole life—the baker who gave him burnt ends of loaf, the schoolteacher who taught him letters, women he'd delivered tinctures to through three winters of coughing sickness. Faces that had smiled at him ten thousand times.

None of them smiled now. Some of them looked away. That was the worst of it—not the ones who stared, but the ones who couldn't meet his eyes, who studied their shoes while their neighbors called for his binding, because they were ashamed, and shame, he understood in that moment, was not the opposite of betrayal. It was betrayal's gentler twin.

Rafael stood in the center of their attention and felt the last thread of his belonging come apart—not torn, but dissolving, the way sugar goes in water, invisibly, completely, irreversibly.

"I'm not your vessel," he said, and heard how thin it sounded. "I'm your neighbor. I've been your neighbor for nineteen years."

"The wards fell on your birthday," Voss said softly. "The book names you. What would you have us believe, child?"

What would you have us believe. As though belief were his to grant. As though nineteen years of counting change honestly and showing up early and sitting with dying people so they wouldn't be alone could be weighed against one line in a book written before his grandmother was born.

He opened his mouth—to say what, he didn't know—and the well answered for him.

The singing stopped. The sudden silence dropped over the square like a struck bell's afterimage, and every head turned toward the water. In the well's black circle, the reflection of the sky had changed. It showed no stars, though the sun had not yet fully set. It showed salt. A white plain stretching to a horizon that was not the valley's horizon, and on that plain, rising slowly, unhurried, the way a mountain might rise if mountains were capable of patience—

Ana screamed. Someone began to pray. The crowd broke apart like a fist opening, and in the scattering, in the churn of bodies and lantern-light and fear, Rafael felt a hand close around his arm—Marisol, breathless, gray-faced, hauling him toward the apothecary's back door.

"Not a word," she said. "Not one word. We're leaving."

"Leaving where? Marisol, the wards—"

"I know what the wards did." She shoved him through the door and bolted it behind them, and in the dim lamplight of the workroom, surrounded by the smell of dried herbs and beeswax, she looked at him with an expression he had never seen on her face and understood, with a lurch, that he was seeing it now for the first time because she had been hiding it for twenty years.

Guilt. Old, worn-smooth, long-carried guilt.

"There are stairs under the apothecary," she said. "Under the floor. My grandmother showed me when I was younger than you, and made me swear on her grave I'd never use them unless the valley broke. And it's breaking, Rafael. So we're going down, and I'm going to tell you everything I swore I'd never tell you, and then—" Her voice caught. She steadied it the way she steadied a shaking vial, with practiced hands and a lifetime of practice. "Then you're going to have to decide what you are. Before they decide for you. Because they will, sweetheart. They're already deciding."

Beneath the floorboards, faint but unmistakable, something answered her.

A single drop of water falling in a deep dark place. Then another. Falling upward.

Rafael looked down at the floor of the only home he had ever known, and the floor looked back.
