## 10. The Trial of the Apothecary

They held the trial in the granary because it was the only building in the valley large enough to hold everyone who wanted to come, and everyone wanted to come. Rafael had never seen the valley gather like this—not for harvest festivals, not for the winter solstice bonfires, not even when old Tomás Ferreira's barn burned and half the town turned out with buckets. The valley did not gather. The valley endured, quietly, the way stone endures rain.

Now they gathered like water finding a crack.

The granary smelled of dust and old wheat and lamp oil, and beneath all of it—faint, but unmistakable to him now that he knew what he was—the mineral breath of salt rising through the floorboards from the flats below. Someone had arranged benches in rows facing a long table where the council would sit: Alderman Voss at the center, his face the color of uncooked dough; beside him the three elders who had served on the council since before Rafael could walk, and two newer faces he didn't know well. A lectern stood off to one side, empty, waiting for whoever would speak against Marisol.

And there she sat, alone on a plain wooden chair at the front, her hands folded in her lap like a woman waiting for bread to rise. She wore her good shawl, the blue one with the fraying fringe that she always said made her look respectable, and she had pinned her gray hair back with the copper combs she saved for funerals. That was what undid him first, before anything was said or decided: she had dressed for a funeral. Her own.

He tried to catch her eye from the back bench. She wouldn't look at him. She looked instead at the rafters, at the grain sacks stacked along the wall, at her own hands—and once, briefly, at the door, as if measuring how far away it was. As if calculating whether she could still run. Marisol, who hadn't left the valley in forty years. Marisol, who ran the apothecary the way other people kept faith.

*She's not afraid for herself,* he realized. *She's afraid they'll make me watch.*

The room filled until people stood along the walls. He recognized every face—that was the cruelty of a valley. There was Petra Ilić, who bought chamomile for her insomnia every month and always overpaid and pretended not to notice when Marisol gave change too generously. There were the Okafor brothers, who'd carried Rafael home on a cart when he broke his ankle falling out of the pear tree at eleven. There was old Widow Sarn, who taught him his letters, and the miller's family, and the ferryman, and children too young to understand who stared at Marisol the way children stare at anything solemn.

Nobody looked at Rafael. That was worse than if they had. It meant they had agreed not to, some silent covenant passed hand to hand down the rows: *don't look at the boy, don't remind the council he exists.* Or perhaps—his stomach turned—it meant something else entirely.

Alderman Voss rose. He was a flour-merchant by trade and had the flour-merchant's gift for making simple things sound weighty. "We are gathered," he said, "under the Old Charter, article nine, which concerns the concealment of matters touching the valley's founding purpose." His voice echoed off the rafters. "Marisol Duarte, keeper of the apothecary, you are accused of harboring a vessel of the sleeping god within our borders, of concealing its nature from this council, and of failing in your sworn duty as a keeper of the wards."

The words fell into the room like stones into a well. Rafael heard them land in the silence, one after another after another.

*Harboring.* As if Marisol had hidden him under her floorboards. As if nineteen years of his life had been contraband.

A woman near the front—Rafael didn't see who—made a small sound, quickly swallowed.

"Marisol Duarte." Voss consulted a paper he clearly didn't need. "How do you answer?"

Marisol stood. She moved slowly, the way she moved when her hip ached in cold weather, and Rafael understood that she was doing it deliberately, letting them see an old woman, a tired woman, a woman who measured out feverfew and honey and asked after their mothers. When she spoke, her voice was steady.

"I answer that I have kept the valley safe for thirty-one years," she said. "I answer that the wards held while I kept them. And I answer that everything I did, I did because the Charter told me to."

Murmurs. Voss raised a hand. "The Charter instructs keepers to report any sign of the vessel's waking. You did not report."

"The Charter instructs keepers to preserve the stillness." Now there was iron under her voice, the voice she used on customers who wanted willow bark for wounds that needed a surgeon. "Tell me, Alderman—which part of the stillness is preserved by dragging a child in front of a council? Which part is preserved by fear? I reported nothing because there was nothing to report. The boy slept. The god slept. The valley slept. Until your wards began to fail—and they did not fail because of me, or because of him. They failed because they are a hundred years old and nobody has renewed them since my mother died, because the council decided renewing them was expensive and unnecessary." She let that sit. "I kept the secret I was sworn to keep. The only thing I failed to do was lie about loving him. Forgive me that failure."

The last sentence cracked something open in the room. Rafael saw it happen: heads turning toward each other, quick glances exchanged, the sound of a community remembering itself.

Voss's jaw tightened. "You admit, then, that the vessel lives among us. That you have known since—" he checked his paper again, "—since his arrival at age four."

"I admit nothing I'm ashamed of."

"Then we will hear testimony." Voss turned to the room, and his gaze swept past Rafael with such deliberate care that it was almost a touch. "Is there anyone present who can speak to the vessel's nature? To any signs of waking?"

Silence. Long enough that the lamps hissed. Rafael counted heartbeats—one, five, ten—and then, to his astonishment, Widow Sarn rose from the second row.

His old teacher. Ninety if she was a day, hands like crumpled parchment, eyes still sharp as needles. She stood facing the council with her cane planted before her like a fence post.

"I'll speak," she said.

Voss blinked. "Widow Sarn, you wish to testify *against*—"

"I wish to testify, which is more than you asked for." She turned partially toward the room, addressing everyone and no one. "Forty years I taught letters in this valley. I taught the Duarte girl, and her mother before her, and I taught the vessel's mother, rest her, and I taught the vessel himself. And I will tell you what I know." She paused, savoring the room's attention the way she'd once savored making children wait for the end of a story. "I know that when the boy was seven, he brought me a bouquet of weeds he'd picked because he thought they were flowers, and I pressed them in my book of psalms, and they're there still. I know that when the fever came through twelve winters ago, it was Marisol's tonics and the vessel's errands that kept half this valley alive—he walked those medicines house to house through snow to his knees, and no one thought to ask the boy his nature then. We were too busy drinking the tea he carried." Her voice hardened. "So yes, Alderman. I can speak to his nature. His nature is that he showed up. Every time. In every weather. Ask yourselves what yours is."

She sat down to a rustle of movement through the benches, and Rafael felt heat climb his throat and behind his eyes. He had come here ready to do the brave thing. He had rehearsed it all night, standing at his window watching the salt flats gleam under a bruised moon, composing the confession in his head like a schoolboy composing an apology. But he had not prepared for this—for the discovery that the ground he stood on was not what he thought. That the silence of the valley might not have been ignorance.

Petra Ilić stood next. "I'll say my piece," she announced, before anyone invited her to. "Three summers ago my daughter took the wasting sickness. The doctors from beyond the pass said make her comfortable. Marisol said otherwise. And the vessel—" Petra's voice caught; she steadied it. "The boy sat with Nell every evening for a month. Read to her. Held the basin when she was sick. Never once flinched, never once complained, though I know Marisol sent him without asking, because that's how she is. My daughter is alive. If that's what a vessel of a god does, then I say we should be so lucky as to be full of gods."

Laughter, nervous and brief, breaking like thin ice and reforming.

One by one, they stood. The Okafor brothers. The ferryman, gruffly, about the winter Rafael had helped free the ferry from ice. The miller's wife, about bread delivered during her confinement. Story after story, none of them grand, all of them small as seeds—and Rafael stood at the back of the granary listening to his own life read aloud like evidence, except that the evidence proved the opposite of what the council wanted proven. They had known. Not all of them, maybe not most—but some. Enough. They had watched him grow up carrying whatever it was he carried, and they had chosen, each in their own kitchen, in their own silence, to love him anyway.

*They chose,* he thought, and the thought went through him like cold water. All these years he had believed the valley's kindness was given to someone innocent—a boy who didn't know what he was. But they had known, some of them, and their kindness had been a decision, made daily, remade daily. That was not innocence returned. That was something harder. Something worth more.

It also meant the betrayal cut both ways, and he wasn't sure yet which direction hurt worse.

"That is quite enough." Voss's voice cracked across the room like a whip. "This is not a festival of testimonials. This is a proceeding under the Charter. The question before us is narrow: has the keeper violated her oath, and what must be done about the vessel?" He drew himself up. "The Charter is clear. A waking vessel must be bound deeper or removed beyond the wards. The safety of two hundred souls—"

"Two hundred and six," said someone in the third row, mildly, and got a scattered laugh.

"—the safety of this valley depends on the stillness we were founded to protect. I move the council find Marisol Duarte guilty of concealment and remand the vessel to the deep mines for re-binding, per the rites of the founders—"

"No."

The word came from Rafael's own mouth before he had decided to spend it. It came out quiet, but the granary was built to carry sound, and it carried his all the way to the rafters.

Every face turned. There it was—the looking he had been spared all evening, arriving all at once, two hundred pairs of eyes finding him in the back like iron filings snapping toward a lodestone.

He walked forward. It was the longest walk of his life, longer than the descent toward the salt mines had been, because this one was uphill, against the weight of the whole valley's attention. He passed Widow Sarn, who reached out and gripped his wrist once, hard, and let go. He passed Petra Ilić, whose chin trembled. He came to stand beside Marisol's chair, and she looked up at him at last, and her eyes were wet and furious and proud all at once, and she shook her head almost imperceptibly: *don't.*

He did anyway.

"My name is Rafael Blackwood," he said, loud enough for the back row, for the walls, for whoever outside might be listening through the dying wards. "I am the vessel. I found the prophecy book. I've been to the border and seen the wards fraying. I've been below, to the flooded mines, and I've heard the god dreaming. Everything Marisol concealed, I concealed with her—no. That's not true." He took a breath. "She concealed it to protect me. I concealed it to protect myself. If guilt lives anywhere, it lives in me, and I won't stand here and let you bury an old woman for loving me badly and well at the same time."

"Boy—" Voss started.

"You want to talk about the Charter?" Rafael turned to face the council fully. "Then hear me. Your Charter says bind me or banish me. Fine. Here are your options, and I'll say them plainly so there's no mistake. You cannot bind me—I've felt what binding means, and I won't survive it, and neither, probably, will your precious stillness, because the god is already stirring and ropes only make it struggle. You cannot banish me—the wards are failing regardless, and sending me out is like throwing a lit lantern at a dry field. And you cannot force me, because whatever I am, the one thing it seems to require is my consent, and that—" his voice caught, and he hated it, and pushed through, "—that is the one thing in this world nobody gets to take from me. Not the council. Not the Ashen Court, whose agent is already inside your borders, sitting somewhere in this valley as we speak. Not anyone."

The silence afterward had texture, like felt. Somewhere in the room a baby began to cry and was hushed.

"So what do you propose?" Voss asked, and to his credit—or his exhaustion—his voice had lost its officialdom. He sounded like a frightened flour merchant. "If not binding, if not banishment. What?"

"I don't know yet," Rafael said honestly. "But I know it isn't yours to decide alone. And I know that the woman in this chair spent thirty-one years holding up a roof none of you thanked her for, and if this council marks her guilty, then every debt this valley owes her comes due, and I promise you—" he let the salt rise in his voice, let it ring faintly, just enough that the lamps flickered and the oldest people in the room went pale with recognition, "—I will collect."

He hadn't meant to do that. The power had simply surfaced, the way it had been surfacing for days now, salt and memory leaking through cracks in him like damp through plaster. He watched the council exchange looks. He watched Widow Sarn smile, slow and terrible, like a woman watching a storm she'd predicted.

Voss cleared his throat. "The council will deliberate," he said, in a voice gone small. "In the meantime—the accused is remanded to her home, under watch."

"There's no need for watch," Marisol said, rising, taking Rafael's arm as though he were the one on trial, as though she were escorting him out of church. "My home has been under watch for nineteen years. It's called an apothecary. Everyone knows where it is."

They walked out together into the blue hour, the crowd parting for them, and the strangest thing happened: people touched him as he passed. A hand on his shoulder. Fingers brushing his sleeve. Widow Sarn's cane tapping his boot, twice, gently, like a blessing. Nobody spoke. But the touches accumulated, and by the time they reached the door Rafael understood that this was the verdict the granary had actually rendered, whatever the council wrote in its ledger.

Outside, the air tasted of coming frost and, underneath, of salt. The flats gleamed pale under the first stars. And at the edge of the square, half in shadow beside the well, stood a figure Rafael recognized even before she stepped forward—Sera, the Court's agent, her ash-gray coat buttoned to the throat, her face unreadable.

Behind her, at the far end of the lane, a carriage stood in the dark with its lamps unlit. And leaning against its door, elegant as a heron, smiling the smile that had once been the whole warm world to a lonely four-year-old—

Odile.

"Hello, little salt," she called softly across the square. "You've grown."

* * *

Marisol's grip on his arm tightened to bone. "Whatever she says," she breathed, "whatever she promises—remember that she sold you once already."

"I know," Rafael said. But his feet had gone cold, and the god beneath the flats had begun, very faintly, to hum.
