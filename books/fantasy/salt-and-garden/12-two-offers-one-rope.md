## 12. Two Offers, One Rope

They gave him three days to decide.

Rafael spent the first of them in the apothecary's back room, staring at the ceiling beams he had memorized as a boy—counting knots in the wood the way other children counted sheep—and understanding, for the first time, that every knot had been placed there by someone who knew what he was. The room hadn't changed. He had. It was strange how a thing could be true for years and only become real on a Tuesday afternoon, with dust motes turning slow circles in the light and the smell of dried yarrow seeping through the floorboards from the shop below.

Three days. Three offers. And beneath all of them, like a stone at the bottom of a well, the one truth nobody wanted spoken aloud: whatever they were offering, they needed him to say yes.

He turned that over while the light moved across the ceiling. Consent. The word kept surfacing in his mind with the insistence of a fish rising to feed. The Court's delegation had been very careful about it—Sera's superiors had used the phrase *voluntary binding* four times in their formal petition, written in ink so black it looked wet even after it dried. Marisol and the council had been less polished but no less precise: the re-binding rites required the vessel's *willing submission*, the old texts said, or the god would simply wake inside a cage it hated, and cages built by frightened people never held anything patient. And Odile—Odile had sat across from him in the parlor with her hands folded like a woman at prayer and said, *I would never force you, mijo. You know that. I have never once forced you.*

That was the cruelest sentence anyone had said to him in nineteen years, and Odile had said worse things with her hands around his wrists over a bubbling cauldron.

He got up. He dressed. He went downstairs, because a man could not count ceiling knots forever without becoming one of them.

* * *

The shop was busy, which surprised him until he remembered: the wards were down, the world outside had noticed them again, and fear sent people to apothecaries the way cold sent them to fires. Marisol worked the counter with her sleeves rolled and her jaw set, weighing feverfew and valerian for a line of neighbors who wouldn't quite meet his eye when he came through from the back. He heard his name in the murmurs—not whispered anymore, not even carefully. Just said. *The vessel. The Blackwood boy. Him.*

"Chamomile for Tomás," Marisol said, pressing a paper packet into an old man's hands. "Steep it strong. And you—" she caught Rafael's sleeve as he passed, her voice dropping to something meant only for him, "—the Court's woman is waiting in the garden. She's been there since dawn. I told her the garden closes at dusk."

"She knows the garden doesn't close."

"I know she knows." Marisol's grip tightened, then gentled. Her hands smelled of beeswax and bitterroot. "Whatever she offers you, Rafael, hear it all the way through before you answer. That's not advice. That's a mother asking."

She had never called herself that before. He looked at her—at the gray coming in at her temples like frost on a windowpane, at the flour-and-herb smudge on her apron—and felt the grief arrive early, ahead of schedule, the way it did now, pre-empting everything. Whatever he chose, he was already losing this. He could feel the losing in his teeth.

"I'll hear it all the way through," he said.

Sera stood among the raised beds where they grew the medicinal herbs, her ash-gray coat buttoned to the throat despite the warmth, her hands clasped behind her back like a soldier at rest. Beyond her, the valley's western ridge cut its familiar line against the sky—the border he had tested with his own two hands, the fraying rope of the wards, the place where something vast had tasted him and found him *familiar*. She didn't turn when he approached. She knew his footsteps. That unsettled him more than any threat could have.

"You've been counting," she said.

"Counting what?"

"Days. Options. Exits." Now she turned, and her face was doing the thing it did—holding court neutrality in the lower half while the upper half, around the eyes, admitted things the lower half would deny under oath. "It's what I'd be doing. I counted for six years before I burned my first writ."

"Sera."

"I'm not here to pressure you." She held up both hands, palms out—a gesture he'd learned, in their strange weeks of acquaintance, to read as her version of honesty. "I'm here because my orders changed last night, and you deserve to hear it from me and not from a sealed letter delivered by someone with colder manners. The Court isn't offering sanctuary anymore. They're offering *purpose*."

The word landed in the garden like a dropped glass.

"The salt god," Sera continued, her voice flat and careful, the voice of a woman reciting something she despised, "is one of nine primordial bindings scattered across the known world. The Court has spent three centuries locating them. Rival powers—the Tidebound Compact, the Hollow Choir—are racing us. Whoever wakes a primordial first and binds it to a willing vessel gains—" she paused, and he watched her choose between the official phrasing and the true one, and watched her lose, "—gains a weapon that ends wars before they start. Ends *cities*, if it comes to that. You wouldn't be a prisoner, Rafael. You'd be a sword. Swords get honored. Swords get *kept*."

"Kept," he repeated. "Sharpened. Sheathed. Drawn when someone decides."

"Yes."

"And if I say no?"

"Then they come back with people whose manners are colder than mine." She said it without flinching, which he understood now was its own kind of mercy. Sera did not soften truths; she handed them over whole, like stones, and let you decide where to put them down. "I'm telling you because you're going to make your choice either way, and I refuse to let you make it believing the Court is kinder than it is. We are not kind. We are *orderly*. There's a difference, and the difference is measured in people like you."

The garden smelled of rosemary and turned earth. Somewhere beyond the wall, a cart rattled past, ordinary as bread, and Rafael thought: *this is what they're all fighting over. A valley where carts rattle. A boy who knows how to dry herbs.* The smallness of it made him want to laugh, and the laughter would have come out wrong, so he swallowed it.

"Why are you really telling me this?"

Sera looked at the herb beds for a long moment. When she answered, her voice had gone quiet enough that the wind nearly took it.

"Because when I was seventeen, someone told me what I was for, and I believed them, and it took me eleven years to understand that being *for* something is not the same as being *someone*." She straightened her coat. "Hear all three offers. Then decide. Not for the valley, not for the god, not for the Court. For the person who has to live inside the decision."

She left through the garden gate without another word, and Rafael stood alone among the rosemary, holding three futures in his hands like eggs, aware of how easily each one cracked.

* * *

The valley's offer came at dusk, in the meeting hall, and it wore its best clothes.

The council had cleaned the hall. Someone had put fresh rushes on the floor and lit the good lanterns, the ones with the colored glass, and the whole room glowed amber and green like the inside of a bottle. The benches were full. Every face he had grown up among was there—the miller, the schoolmistress, old Benicio who had taught him to whittle, the twins from the bakery who used to steal plums with him off Doña Elena's tree. Faces he loved. Faces that had said his name in the market like a diagnosis.

Marisol sat apart from the council, in the audience, which told him everything about how the evening would go. She would not sit with the people deciding his fate. Small mercy. Heavy one.

Councilor Reyes stood, a broad woman with a voice built for weather warnings. "Rafael Blackwood. This valley owes you an accounting, and we will give it—one debt at a time, starting tonight." She did not apologize. He noticed that. There would be guilt in plenty afterward, he could see it banked in their faces like coals, but no apology yet, because an apology might crack open the thing they actually needed from him. "You know what we ask. The deep rites exist. Older than the wards, older than the book. Your grandmother's grandmother helped write them. Under the deep rites, the god does not wake. Neither do you—not fully. You would sleep, and the valley would sleep around you, safe, for another hundred years. Another hundred years of carts rattling. Of plums stolen off trees. Of children who grow up not knowing what sleeps beneath their feet."

"And me?" His voice came out steadier than he expected. "In a hundred years. What happens to the boy who slept?"

Reyes's jaw worked. Beside her, the schoolmistress looked down at her hands.

"The rites preserve the vessel," Reyes said finally. "Mostly."

"*Mostly.*"

"There are costs. We won't lie to you in this room." A murmur ran the benches—some of them had been prepared to lie, he saw, and were relieved to be spared it. "The vessel who goes under the deep rites surfaces changed. Diminished. Some memories don't survive the compression. Some—" she hesitated, and old Benicio finished for her, his whittler's hands spread wide, helpless:

"Some of the boy doesn't come back up, mijo. We know. We've read the accounts. We're asking anyway, because the alternative is asking you to hold a god awake inside you for the rest of your life, and no one should carry that alone, and—" his voice broke, an old man's crack like river ice, "—and we are *afraid*. All of us. We have been afraid since 1903, and we raised you inside our fear, and we are sorry for it, and we are still afraid, and we still need you to sleep."

The hall was silent. In the audience, Marisol had her hand pressed flat against her sternum as if holding a door shut.

Rafael looked at them—all those faces, all that borrowed love built on a foundation of lies—and felt the cold clarity settle over him like a coat fitted precisely to his shoulders. They were not villains. That was the terrible part. They were frightened people who had done monstrous arithmetic: *one boy's dimming against ten thousand lives.* He could see the sum. He could even respect it. But every path they offered led through the same door, and the door was labeled with his name, and none of them had ever once asked him to help draw the map.

"You need my consent," he said. "For the deep rites. Say it plainly."

Reyes didn't flinch. He granted her that. "Yes. The rites fail otherwise. The vessel must walk into the sleep willingly, or the god rides the unwillingness up out of the dark." She met his eyes. "We cannot take it from you. God knows we've discussed whether we could. We can't."

"Thank you," Rafael said, and meant it, and watched relief and shame move through the hall together like weather. "I'll give you my answer with the others. All three of you get the same day. No one gets to go first."

He walked out through the amber-and-green light, past the faces he loved, and Marisol caught his hand at the door and pressed something into it—small, wrapped in waxed cloth. He didn't open it until he was outside. Inside was a plum, perfectly ripe, from Doña Elena's tree, saved since summer.

He ate it standing in the dark, and it tasted like everything the deep rites would take from him, and he let himself cry for exactly as long as it took to eat a plum.

* * *

Odile came on the third night, which was, Rafael had to admit, excellent timing. Everyone else had already shown their hand. She arrived at the apothecary as the lamps were being lit, wearing traveling gray instead of her ritual blacks, carrying nothing but a covered basket, and she looked—for one treacherous moment—like any mother visiting any son.

"I brought supper," she said. "I remember what you liked. I remember *everything* you liked."

"That's the problem," Rafael said, but he let her in.

She cooked in Marisol's kitchen with an ease that should have been impossible for a stranger, finding the salt, the pans, the good knife, and Rafael stood in the doorway watching his childhood move through another woman's hands. She made the braised rabbit with juniper he had eaten on the nights his experiments went well. The reward meal. He had forgotten—he had genuinely forgotten—that the dish existed, that his body had ever associated juniper with safety, and now the smell filled the kitchen and undid fifteen years of forgetting in a single breath. That was Odile's gift. She could reach into the past and rearrange it while you watched.

"Eat," she said. "Then talk."

He ate. It was delicious. He hated that it was delicious.

"You've heard the Court's offer," she began, serving him like he was still fifteen, still hers. "A sword. Blades rust, mijo, and blades get *used*. You've heard the valley's offer. Sleep. A hundred years of nothing, and the accounts say the vessel surfaces diminished—I've read those accounts too, I taught myself to read them when you were a baby, did you know that? I've spent fourteen years preparing for this conversation." She sat across from him, folded her hands, and her eyes—his own gray eyes looking back at him from a face he had fled—held steady. "Here is mine. Come with me. Not as a vessel. As an heir."

"An heir to what?"

"To the oldest craft lineage in the coastal kingdoms. To knowledge the Court would burn and the valley never dreamed of. The binding that made you a vessel—it can be *unmade*, Rafael. Slowly. Carefully. Over years, with the right teacher." She leaned forward. "I found the technique in a monastery library in Vhalen. I traded eight years of my work for it. I have spent your whole life learning how to free you, and yes—" her voice caught, and he could not tell, even now, even knowing everything, whether the catch was real, "—yes, I sold you to the keepers, and I have regretted it every single day since, and I am here now to spend the rest of my life correcting it. Wake the god or don't. Sleep or don't. But do it *free*, trained, armed with everything I can give you. Not as anyone's instrument. As my son."

The kitchen was warm. The food smelled like safety. And Rafael sat very still, running the cold clarity along the offer like a blade along a strop, feeling for the edge.

There it was. Of course there it was. *As my son.* An heir inherits. An heir carries the name forward, the work forward, the debts forward. An heir belongs to the lineage. Odile would unmake the binding and build a better one—not of salt and sleep, but of gratitude, of training, of the long slow debt of a rescued child. She would free him from everyone else and bind him to herself, and the binding would be made entirely of things he would feel guilty for refusing.

And it would require his consent too. That was the quiet genius of it. No ritual, no rite—just years, just meals like this one, just the drip-drip-drip of *after everything I've done for you.*

"Say the last part," Rafael said quietly.

Odile blinked. "Which last part?"

"The part where the unmaking works better if I want it. Where the craft requires a willing student, the way the rites require a willing sleeper, the way the Court requires a willing sword. Say it plainly, like Councilor Reyes did. You're capable of plainness. I've seen you use it as a weapon."

Something flickered behind her eyes—hurt, or admiration, or the place where those two feelings shared a coat. And then, because she was Odile, because she had never once lied to him when the truth would cut deeper, she smiled a small, terrible, honest smile.

"It works better if you love me," she said. "The unmaking draws on the vessel's own will. A reluctant heir makes a cracked vessel, and cracked vessels leak gods. So yes, mijo. Ideally, you love me. Ideally, you *choose* me." She reached across the table, and he let her take his hand, her fingers cool and certain as a tourniquet. "Is that so unforgivable? Everyone else wants your consent for a cage or a blade or a grave. I'm asking for your consent to be *loved*. Poorly, perhaps. Late. But truly, in the only way I know how."

Rafael looked down at their joined hands. His mother's hands. The hands that had held his head over cauldrons and combed his hair before valuation days and signed the papers that sold him to the keepers and, possibly, had trembled while signing them—he would never know, would never be allowed to know, because Odile understood better than anyone alive that a wound and a gift can be the same object depending on the angle of the light.

"No," he said. "It's not unforgivable. That's what makes it so much worse."

* * *

He climbed to the roof of the apothecary after she left, because the roof was where he went as a boy when the house held too many voices, and some habits survived even the discovery that your whole life was a stage set. Below him, the valley lay lamplit and small. Above, the stars came out over the broken wards like witnesses arriving late to a trial.

Three offers. He laid them out in his mind the way Marisol laid out herbs for drying: the sword, the sleep, the heir. A weapon, a grave, a leash made of love. Each one polished. Each one sincere. Each one requiring, at the hinge of it, the same small mechanism turning the same way: *yes.* His yes. The one thing in the world that belonged to him outright, that no rite could extract and no army could seize, that the god itself—vast, patient, lonely beneath the salt flats—had never once tried to take.

*Even the god asks,* he thought, and the thought arrived with a sensation like a tide turning somewhere far below him, vast and slow and attentive. *Everyone with power in my life has finally learned to ask. So the question isn't what they want. The question is what I'm willing to pay, and for whom.*

Far out past the western ridge, where the wards hung frayed as old rope, a light appeared. Then another. Then a line of them, descending the pass in perfect formation, orderly as a ledger, cold as arithmetic: the full Ashen Court, come to collect its answer.

And beside the lead rider, small in the saddle, wrapped in traveling gray, rode a second figure who had left his kitchen an hour before—Odile, riding out to meet them, her basket empty, her hands folded like a woman at prayer.

Rafael watched them come, and the cold clarity in his chest warmed by one degree into something harder and more useful.

Decision.

Not theirs. His.
