## 13. The Night of Small Treasons

The writ arrived at dusk, folded inside a moth that should not have existed.

Sera knew it was a Court message before she touched it—ash-magic always smelled faintly of burnt sugar, sweet and wrong, like something delicious dying. The moth landed on the windowsill of the room the valley had given her, its wings shedding grey powder that settled in little dunes along the wood. She watched it for a long moment. Somewhere below, the apothecary's lamp was still burning; she could see its gold rectangle laid across the cobblestones like a dropped hymnal.

She let the moth crawl onto her palm and speak.

*By order of the Ashen Court, under seal of the Ninth Chair: the vessel known as Rafael Blackwood is to be taken into custody by whatever means necessary. Failure to secure him peaceably is anticipated. Force is authorized. Force is expected.*

Anticipated. Expected. The words were chosen carefully, the way all Court words were chosen. They meant: *we know you've gone soft, Vespera. We are giving you permission to stop pretending otherwise.*

The moth crumbled when she closed her hand. She sat with the ash in her fist for a long time, listening to the valley breathe.

It was not supposed to be like this. She had crossed the dying wards three weeks ago expecting a monster—a thing with salt for blood and a god coiled behind its ribs, something she would have to put down or drag out in chains. Instead she had found a young man behind a counter stacked with dried yarrow and feverfew, apologizing because he'd sold out of willow bark, his hands stained green to the wrist, tired in the specific way of people who have been kind longer than they have been careful.

He had sold her chamomile for her headaches and refused payment the first two times, until she insisted so awkwardly that he laughed—a short, startled sound, as if laughter were a language he was relearning—and said, *Fine, but then you have to tell me one true thing about the outside world. That's the price.*

She had told him about the sea. About how it sounded nothing like people said and everything like they feared. He had listened the way starving people look at bread.

That was three weeks ago. Three weeks of tea on the apothecary's back step, of him asking questions about the world with the hungry patience of someone reading a book through frosted glass, of her answering and hating herself a little more each time, because every answer was also reconnaissance, wasn't it? Every question he asked told her where the soft places were.

And now the Court had decided the soft places were no longer needed.

*Force is authorized.*

Sera opened her palm and let the ash fall into the candle flame. It flared green, acknowledged, received. Somewhere far away, in a hall of black basalt, a clerk would note that Agent Vespera had read her orders. The clock had started. She had until moonrise tomorrow—or perhaps less—to deliver Rafael Blackwood to the delegation waiting beyond the southern ridge, or to die trying, or to run.

She looked at the gold rectangle of lamplight on the cobbles and thought: *I am twenty-six years old and I have burned eleven years of my life for people who write sentences like 'force is expected.'*

Then she thought about the way Rafael had said *thank you* when she brought him rainwater from the eastern springs—the good water, the water without iron in it—because he'd mentioned once, offhand, that the valley's wells tasted of metal lately, and hadn't that been strange, hadn't the whole valley tasted strange since the wards began to fray?

He had thanked her. No one in the Court thanked anyone. Gratitude was a debt there, and debts were weapons.

Sera blew out her candle and went downstairs.

* * *

Marisol was waiting for her in the apothecary's kitchen, which did not surprise Sera at all. The old woman had eyes everywhere and the particular stillness of someone who had spent decades practicing the appearance of knowing nothing.

"He came to you," Marisol said. It wasn't a question. She stood at the hearth with her back to the fire, and the light made her shadow enormous against the drying racks of herbs, a giant made of small green things.

"Who?" Sera asked, out of habit.

"Don't." Marisol's voice was gentle and absolutely immovable, like a river stone. "A rider came through an hour ago wearing a face I didn't recognize and a coat I did. Ash-grey. And now you're standing in my kitchen at midnight looking like a woman doing arithmetic she doesn't like the answer to."

Sera leaned against the doorframe. The kitchen smelled of rosemary and beeswax and, beneath it, faintly, of salt—that low mineral hum that had been rising out of the ground for days now, seeping up through the floorboards, frosting the insides of the windows some mornings. The valley was dying and being born at the same time, and everyone could feel it, and only two people in this room knew why.

"If I tell you," Sera said slowly, "you'll have to decide what to do with it. And I don't think either of us wants to carry that."

"I've carried worse." Marisol took down a clay jar from the high shelf, poured two fingers of something amber into two cups, and set them on the table. An invitation and a sentence, both at once. "I carried a lie for nineteen years. Sit down, girl."

Sera sat. The liquor tasted of plums and smoke and something older underneath, something that made her teeth ache pleasantly.

"The writ says force," she said. "They're coming at moonrise tomorrow. Not just me—the second delegation moved up to the ridge tonight. Odile is with them."

Marisol's cup stopped halfway to her mouth. For a moment the old woman's face did something terrible and private, a grief with teeth in it, and then it smoothed over like water after a stone.

"That woman," Marisol said quietly, "should never have been allowed near a child."

"She claims maternal rights. The Court finds it convenient. A vessel surrendered by family looks like consent. A vessel taken by agents looks like theft, and theft invites scrutiny from the other powers." Sera turned the cup in her hands. "Everything is paperwork to them. Even him. Even—" She stopped.

"Even what he is," Marisol finished. "Even what waking him would do to the world." She drank, set the cup down with a click. "You've seen the flats. You've felt what's coming up through the ground. Tell me honestly, Ashen girl—if they wake that god under their control, what happens to my valley?"

Sera thought of the briefing documents. Of the diagrams. Of the phrase *controlled awakening* and the asterisked footnote she probably wasn't meant to read: *collateral projections unavailable; prior vessel mortality 100%.*

"They don't know," she said. "Nobody knows. The last vessel who got close enough to ask died before she could write it down properly." She heard her own voice go flat and bitter. "Eleven years I served them, and I never once asked what we were serving *for*. I asked how. How do we cross the wards, how do we track the vessel, how do we make the capture clean. Never once—"

"Because asking why gets you sent to the Ninth Chair's dungeons," Marisol said, "and I imagine you've seen those."

"I grew up next to them." Sera laughed, and it came out wrong. "My mother swept their floors. I used to think if I was useful enough, precise enough, loyal enough, I'd be safe. Do you know what the funny part is? I *was* safe. Right up until the moment I started caring about something they wanted."

Marisol studied her across the table for a long moment. Then she rose, went to the pantry, and came back with a loaf of dark bread and a knife, and cut three slices instead of two.

"He's in the drying loft," she said. "He hasn't slept in two nights. Says the salt sings to him now, when it's quiet. Says it knows his name." Her hands paused on the knife. "I raised that boy lying to him, and I will not spend the end of it lying to him again. So you're going to walk up those stairs, and you're going to tell him what's coming, and then the three of us are going to figure out what a hedge-witch's apprentice, an old fool, and a Court agent can possibly do against the Ashen Court."

"And if I'm lying? If this is a trap?"

"Then I've fed you good bread for nothing," Marisol said, "and I'm too old to feel foolish about kindness. Go on."

* * *

The drying loft was warm and dim, hung with bundles of lavender and sage that turned the air purple-sweet. Rafael sat on the floor between the rafters with the prophecy book open across his knees, and in the low light he looked, for a moment, exactly like what the Court believed he was: something ancient wearing a young man's skin, something vast pretending to be small.

Then he looked up, and he was just Rafael—hollow-eyed, salt-crusted at the hairline, nineteen years old and carrying a god like a stone in his chest.

"You look like a woman about to apologize," he said.

"I might be." Sera lowered herself onto the floor across from him. Up here, away from the hearth, the cold crept in through the slats, and with it the smell of the flats—that wide, mineral emptiness pressing at the valley's edges. "Rafael. There's a delegation on the southern ridge. They moved up tonight. By tomorrow moonrise they'll come down into the valley, and they won't be asking."

He didn't flinch. That was the worst part, she thought later—he didn't flinch, as if some part of him had been waiting for the knock since the day he was born. "The book said this too," he said quietly. He turned it toward her. Fresh ink, still shining wet on the old paper: *the hunters descend; the hunted chooses.* "I keep hoping it's wrong. It hasn't been wrong yet."

"It's not wrong." She made herself say the rest plainly, because he deserved plainness, everyone in his life had fed him curves and evasions until the truth must taste like a foreign fruit. "There's a writ with my name on it. Capture by any means. And Odile is with them—they're going to dress the seizure up as a mother reclaiming her son. Consent theater. If you go to her willingly, even one step, even one word of agreement, the Court can claim the awakening was voluntary and do whatever they want with what comes out of you."

"What comes out of me." He smiled, thin and terrible. "You say it like I'm a letter to be opened."

"Aren't you tired of being unopened?" The words escaped before she could catch them, and she saw them land—saw his breath catch, saw something shift behind his eyes, surprise and hunger in equal measure. She pressed on, quieter. "I've read the old reports, Rafael. Every vessel before you fought it or fled it or tried to kill themselves rather than hold it, and every single one died anyway, badly, and the god stayed asleep and alone under the salt for another generation while the world above pretended it had solved something. The Court wants to open you *for* you. Your mistress wants to open you *through* you. This valley wants to nail you shut forever. Has anyone—has *anyone*—ever just asked you what you want?"

The loft was very quiet. Somewhere below, Marisol moved through the kitchen, banking the fire, and the small sounds of it rose up through the floorboards like a heartbeat belonging to the house itself.

"No," Rafael said at last. His voice cracked on the word and he didn't try to hide it. "No one has ever asked. They've all just... decided. My whole life has been other people's decisions wearing my name." He closed the book. When he looked up again, something had changed in his face—not resolve exactly, not yet, but the clearing where resolve would grow. "What do you want, Sera? Truly. Not your orders. You."

And there it was. The question she had spent eleven years making sure no one would ever ask her, because she had no answer that wouldn't burn her life down.

She reached into her coat and drew out the writ.

It was a slip of ash-paper, grey as moth-wing, dense with the Ninth Chair's black script. It weighed almost nothing. It weighed eleven years. She held it in both hands and thought about her mother sweeping dungeon floors, about the sea she'd described to Rafael and had never actually seen herself, about the word *Vespera*, which meant evening star, which her mother had chosen because it was the first light you could see and the last one to fade, and how she had spent her whole career being neither.

"You asked what I want," she said. "I want to stop being the kind of person who delivers sentences like this one."

She held the writ to the lantern.

The ash-paper caught reluctantly, the way Court things do—it resisted, the black script writhing as the flames took it, and for one absurd moment Sera imagined she could hear it, a thin dry voice reciting its own clauses as it died. Then it was just fire, and then just ash, and she tipped her hand and let the grey flakes drift down between the hanging lavender, settling among the herbs like snow that had finally lost its argument with spring.

When she looked up, Rafael was staring at her with an expression she couldn't name—something between fear and wonder, the look of a man watching someone step off a cliff to prove the air would hold.

"Do you know what they do to agents who burn their writs?" she asked. "Neither do I, actually. Nobody's stupid enough to find out. Congratulations. You're keeping company with a first."

"Why?" he asked simply. "You barely know me. Chamomile and rainwater and a few conversations. Why throw your whole life—"

"Because you thanked me for the water." Her voice came out rougher than she intended. "Do you understand how small that is? How enormous? Eleven years in the Court and no one has ever thanked me for anything, because thanks implies you had a choice, and no one there has choices, we just have functions. And then I come to this broken little valley full of wards that smell like rotting rope, and a man who sells herbs for a living looks me in the eye and says *thank you* like I'm a person." She laughed, wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand, furious at herself for crying. "I'm not saving you, Rafael. I'm saving the version of me that existed before I learned what the Court was. She's been buried a long time. Turns out she wasn't dead."

Below them, the kitchen door opened and closed. Footsteps on the stairs—Marisol, unhurried, carrying a third cup of plum liquor and a face like a closing gate.

"So," the old woman said, setting the cup down beside Sera without ceremony, as if feeding fugitive Court agents were a service the apothecary had always offered. "The writ is burned. The delegation arrives at moonrise. Which gives us—" she squinted at the window, at the fat white moon already climbing over the eastern ridge, "—one day, and most of a night."

"One day to do what?" Rafael asked. "Run? The wards are failing everywhere. There's nowhere in the valley they can't reach by tomorrow night."

"Not run," Sera said slowly. The thought had been forming all evening, gathering itself out of half-remembered briefings and forbidden footnotes, and now it stepped forward into the light. "The last vessel. The one who died. Marisol—you told me the keepers' records mention her. That she got further into the mines than any before her. That she left something behind."

Marisol went very still. "The council sealed those records generations ago."

"Sealed isn't destroyed. Nothing in this valley is ever destroyed—that's the whole point of it, isn't it? A valley built to keep things." Sera turned to Rafael, and heard her own voice drop, quick and low, the voice of a woman who had just committed treason and found it fit her better than her uniform ever had. "If there's a fourth path—if there's any path at all that isn't their weapon or their prison or your grave—then it's written down somewhere under this valley, and we have one night to find it."

Rafael looked down at the prophecy book in his lap. As they watched, the fresh ink stirred, swirled, and rearranged itself into new words, wet and gleaming:

*The drowned one wrote in red. The lighthouse remembers what the valley forgot.*

Outside, far to the south, a bell began to ring on the ridge—the delegation's arrival signal, hours early, or perhaps exactly on time, because the Court had never once in its history done anything as generous as arrive when expected.

Marisol set down her cup. "Get your coats," she said.
