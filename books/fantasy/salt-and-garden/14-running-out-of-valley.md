## 14. Running Out of Valley

The valley shook at dawn, and the birds did not sing about it.

Rafael noticed the silence first—the absence of the morning chorus that had scored every day of his life like a heartbeat he'd never had to listen for. Then the ground answered: a long, rolling shudder that made the apothecary's bottles chime against each other in a hundred small glass voices, as if the whole shop were whispering *run*.

Sera was already awake. She stood at the window with her back straight and her burned writ still smelling faintly of ash where she'd fed it to the stove two nights ago, and when she turned to him her face held the particular calm of someone who has done arithmetic with her own life and gotten an answer she can live with.

"They're coming up the eastern pass," she said. "I felt them before I felt the quake. The Court travels heavy—it drags its own weather."

"How long?"

"Half a day. Less, if Odile is pushing them." She said the name without flinching now, which was its own kind of gift. "We go west. Through the flats, through the borderlands, out."

"Out," Rafael repeated. The word sat strangely on his tongue. He had spent four years believing there was no *out*—that the valley's edge was the edge of the world, or at least of his world, a seam sewn shut by ancestors whose faces he didn't know and whose purpose, it turned out, had been him. Now the seam was tearing on its own, and he was expected to simply walk through it.

He packed the way Marisol had taught him: dried yarrow for wounds, willow bark for pain, salt—of course, always salt—in a stoppered jar that clinked against the herbs like a small insistent conscience. He took the prophecy book last, wrapped in oilcloth, and hesitated over it. It had stopped being a book of fate somewhere around chapter fifteen. In the abandoned lighthouse records they'd found—or would find; the geography of the borderlands had begun to feel less like terrain and more like suggestion—the dead vessel's blood-written instructions waited for him. He could feel them pulling, the way you feel a word on the tip of your tongue.

He put the book in his pack and did not let himself think about what it was asking.

Marisol met them at the western gate. She had not slept; he could see it in the bruised skin under her eyes, in the way her hands moved over his shoulders—checking him the way she checked a tincture for spoilage, thorough and tender and trying very hard to be quick.

"The flats are unstable," she said. "The god is turning under them like a sleeper rolling over. Stay off the white ground where you can. And Rafael—" Her voice caught on something. She swallowed it. "Whatever it asks you, whatever it shows you down there in your own head, remember that memory isn't the only thing that makes a home. Practice does too. You taught me that, though you don't know it yet."

"That doesn't make sense," he said.

"It will," said Marisol, who knew things she wasn't allowed to say, and pushed him gently toward the gate.

* * *

The salt flats were blinding.

They stretched west from the valley's edge in a vast white sheet, cracked into polygons by a century of evaporation, each plate edged with delicate rimes of crystal that caught the early light and threw it back doubled. Walking on them was like walking on the sky. Every footstep crunched—a sound so loud in the silence that Rafael kept glancing behind himself, certain the noise would carry all the way to the eastern pass and deliver them straight into the Court's hands.

Sera walked half a step ahead, reading the ground the way he read herb beds. She had shed her Court grays for travel-stained wool, but she still moved like an agent: weight low, eyes sweeping, one hand never far from the knife at her hip even though the knife had been useless against everything they'd run from so far.

"The cracks are widening," she said, crouching. She pressed two fingers into one of the dark seams between salt plates and came away with wet earth, black and glistening. "Water's rising. The mines must be flooding."

"The god is dreaming harder," Rafael said. It came out of him without permission, the way the knowledge had been coming out of him for days—not learned but remembered, surfacing like bodies in a thawing lake. He pressed his palm flat against the salt. Beneath the cold crystal he felt it: a pulse, slow and enormous, patient as geology. *Come down*, the pulse said, in no language at all. *Come down where it is quiet.*

He snatched his hand back.

Sera watched him do it. She didn't ask if he was all right, which he appreciated more than any comfort would have been. Instead she stood, shouldered her pack, and said, "How far to the borderlands?"

"Two days' walk, if the flats hold. A day, if we cut across the deep brine pools." He looked at the horizon, where the white gave way to a smudge of gray-green hills—the drowned country beyond the valley, marshland and old mine shafts and, somewhere in it, a lighthouse that had never seen the sea. "The wards used to be here," he added quietly. "I can feel where they were. Like a scar."

"What do scars feel like?"

"Cold," he said. "And then, underneath the cold—warm. Like the thing they were holding back is pressing its face against the other side."

Sera was silent for three steps. Four. Then: "When I was nine, my mother bound my magic. Not sealed—bound, threaded into knots so the Court wouldn't take me for training. It was supposed to be temporary." She said it evenly, reporting facts, but her jaw was tight. "It never was. For eleven years I lived with something warm pressing against the inside of me, wanting out. So I think I understand the god better than most people in that Court. Better than Odile, certainly. She looks at it and sees power. I look at it and see a prisoner who has been told for so long that it's dangerous that it started to believe her."

"And you?" Rafael asked. "Do you believe it?"

"I believe it's lonely," Sera said. "Lonely things break things. That's not the same as evil. It took me years to learn the difference, and it cost me—" she touched her collarbone, where Rafael knew the Court's brand sat under her shirt, "—it cost me more than I want to say while we're walking."

The ground shuddered again. Far to the east, past the valley's rim, a column of dust rose against the sky, deliberate as a signature.

"They're moving faster than I thought," Sera said.

"Then we stop walking politely," said Rafael, and surprised them both by breaking into a run.

* * *

They crossed the deep brine pools at noon, picking their way along crystalline causeways that the floodwaters had not yet swallowed. The pools themselves were wrong—too still, too red-gold, like eyes. In each one Rafael saw his reflection, and in each reflection something stood slightly behind him: a tall pale shape, indistinct, patient, watching him with what he could only describe as recognition.

He stopped telling Sera about them after the third pool. Not because he didn't trust her—he found, with a vertigo that had nothing to do with the shaking ground, that he trusted her almost completely—but because every time he opened his mouth to describe the shape, his voice came out layered. Doubled. His own voice, and beneath it a sound like wind moving through a flooded cavern, saying the same words a half-beat late.

His magic was waking with the god. He could feel it in his hands now, a dry prickling like static before lightning, and when he flexed his fingers the sweat on his palms dried instantly to fine white crystals that glittered and fell away. Salt. Of course salt. He was the vessel of the god of salt, and his body had apparently decided to start taking the job seriously whether he consented or not.

Worse was the memory-magic. That began in the afternoon, near the ruins of an old watchtower at the flats' western edge.

He crouched to retie his bootlace and touched the stones, and the tower spoke to him.

Not in words. In *belonging*. The stones poured their remembering into him: masons laughing over shared bread, a keeper's daughter scratching her name into the mortar, decades of lantern-light and rain and the small domestic history of a place. It flooded him so fast and so completely that he gasped and sat down hard, and Sera spun with her knife already drawn, scanning for threats that weren't there.

"Rafael?"

"It talked to me." His voice shook. "The tower. Everything remembers, Sera. Everything that people loved, it keeps a little of the love, and I can—I can hear it. I can taste it." He wiped his mouth with the back of his hand and tasted salt and, absurdly, fresh bread. "This is what the bargain costs, isn't it. This is the currency. Memory. Belonging. The god runs on it."

Sera knelt beside him. She didn't touch him, but she stayed close enough that he could feel the warmth of her shoulder, and in the middle of the burning white flats that warmth was the only real thing.

"Then we'll be careful what you touch," she said.

"It's not just touching." He laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, doubled, with that cavern-wind underneath. "It's happening anyway. Look."

He held out his hand. In his palm, unbidden, a memory was condensing—literally condensing, drawing itself out of the air in glittering grains, building itself into a small solid scene no bigger than a bird's nest: a kitchen. A woman's hands kneading dough. Sunlight through a window. His mother's kitchen, from before, from the years he barely remembered, assembling itself grain by grain out of his own mind because some part of him was already practicing the payment, rehearsing the giving-away.

Sera stared at it. Her breath went ragged.

"You're leaking," she said softly. "You're paying in advance."

"I know." He closed his fist and the kitchen collapsed into ordinary salt, and he felt the memory dim slightly, like a page thumbed soft. "I need to learn to hold on. To choose what stays." He looked at her then, really looked, and the words came out plainer than anything he'd ever said. "If I'm going to pay, I want to decide what I pay with. That's the whole point. That's been the whole point since the beginning. Everyone else got to decide what I am—the keepers, the Court, Odile, the god. Not this. Not this one thing."

Sera nodded slowly. Then she reached out and took his salt-dusted hand in hers, deliberately, letting her skin touch the magic, daring it to take something of hers.

"Nothing happened," she said, wonder in her voice. "Your magic doesn't want my memories. It wants yours. It's polite, somehow. Or loyal." She almost smiled. "Like its vessel."

The ground rolled beneath them, long and slow, and far away to the east the dust column thickened.

* * *

They made the borderlands by dusk.

The change came gradually and then all at once: white giving way to gray crust, gray to mud, mud to a tangle of salt-killed scrub and standing black water. Old mine heads jutted from the hillsides like broken teeth, their timber frames rotting, their shafts breathing cold mineral air. Somewhere below, water moved in the dark with a sound like slow applause.

Here the god's awakening was not a distant tremor but a presence. The air tasted metallic. Puddles had frozen into lace despite the season. And every reflective surface—black water, wet stone, the blade of Sera's knife—showed the tall pale shape standing behind Rafael, closer now, clearer now, wearing what might have been his face worn thin as gauze.

"He's not following us," Rafael said, stopping at the lip of a ravine where a rope bridge swayed in a wind he couldn't feel. "I figured it out today. He's not chasing. He's *accompanying*. There's a difference. Chasing means he wants to catch me. Accompanying means—" He searched for it. "Means wherever I go, he considers himself invited."

"Is that worse?"

"It's lonelier," Rafael said, and heard how true it was, and understood suddenly why the god had chosen bargains over conquest in the end, why the last vessel's blood-instructions existed at all. You couldn't fight something that just wanted to come along. You could only decide, finally, whether to open the door—and on what terms.

They camped in the shell of the old watchtower's twin, a ruined circle of stones on a hill above the marshes. Sera built a fire small and shielded, a professional habit, and they ate hard bread and dried apples and drank tea brewed from the yarrow in Rafael's pack, bitter and steadying.

The shaking came again at full dark—harder this time, sustained, and in the valleys below them they heard the mines groan, and saw, impossibly, a faint glow rising from the flooded shafts: light the color of moonlit salt, seeping up through the earth like dawn arriving from the wrong direction.

Sera's hand found his in the dark. Neither of them made anything of it. Neither of them let go.

"When this is over," she said, "whatever 'over' turns out to mean—if you still have memories left, if there's anything of you still keeping house in there—I'd like to be in one of them. Just so you know. No pressure. No obligation. I just wanted it said out loud, while you can still hear it."

Rafael turned the words over. He thought about the kitchen condensing in his palm, the mother he was already losing by grains. He thought about Marisol's parting words: *memory isn't the only thing that makes a home. Practice does too.*

"Then help me practice," he said. "Starting tonight. Tell me something true about yourself, something small, and I'll spend it carefully. I'll build a room for it. If the payment takes everything else, I want at least one thing in me that I chose instead of inherited."

Sera was quiet for a moment. The firelight moved on her face.

"All right," she said. "Here's something true. When I burned my writ, my hands shook for a day afterward—not from fear. From relief. Eleven years in the Court and it was the first time I'd ever chosen anything, and my body didn't know what to do with the freedom except shake." She looked at him across the fire. "So when you walk out of that salt flat having chosen, and your hands shake—don't think it's terror. Sometimes it's just the shape freedom makes in a person who's never held it before."

Rafael memorized it. Deliberately, consciously, the way Marisol had taught him to fix a recipe in mind: her voice, the firelight, the glow rising from the mines, the warmth of her hand. He built the room. He set the memory in it like a lamp on a table.

And then, because the night demanded it, he opened the oilcloth bundle and took out the prophecy book.

The pages turned themselves to the lighthouse record. The blood-written instructions of the last vessel gleamed faintly brown-red in the firelight, and beneath them, in ink so fresh it shone, new lines had appeared since he'd last looked:

*The vessel approaches the third door. The god walks beside him as a guest walks beside a host. What is offered freely cannot be seized. What is chosen cannot be stolen. But the Court reaches the flats by tomorrow's dusk, and the witch Odile carries a chain woven from the vessel's own childhood—*

Rafael stopped reading.

"A chain," he said aloud. His voice came out flat. "Odile has something of mine. Something from when I was a child." He looked up at Sera, and the doubled voice rose in his throat unbidden, his own words shadowed by the cavern-wind, both of them saying the same terrible thing together:

"She's been carrying a piece of me this whole time. And I think I know exactly which piece."

Far to the east, over the hills, the sky began to lighten—not with dawn, but with the cold silver glare of the Ashen Court's approach, pouring over the pass like ash given a horizon.
