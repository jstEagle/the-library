## 15. The Third Option

### Rafael

The lighthouse stood at the edge of the world like a finger raised in warning.

They had been walking for two days through the borderlands, where the valley's stillness bled out into a country that could not decide what it was. Grass grew silver on one side of the path and green on the other. A stream they crossed in the morning ran backward by afternoon. Rafael's own skin had begun doing things he did not authorize—salt crystallizing in the creases of his knuckles overnight, fine as frost; his shadow lagging half a heartbeat behind him when the sun moved fast.

"Is that normal?" Sera asked, watching him shake glitter from his hand for the third time that morning. She said *that* the way she said most things: flat, practical, as though asking whether he'd remembered the bread.

"I don't know what normal is anymore." He flexed his fingers. The salt had left them faintly scented, like the air over the flats before rain. "I used to be a person who weighed herbs and argued about the price of comfrey."

"You were never only that. You just got very good at pretending." She squinted up at the lighthouse. "Whoever built that thing wanted ships to see it from a long way off. There's no sea within a hundred miles of here."

"There was," Rafael said. The knowledge arrived the way so much knowledge had been arriving lately—not thought but recognition, something old surfacing through him like a body through water. "Before the binding. The god pulled the tides. When they chained it under the valley, the sea went away and took its harbors with it. All these towns—" he gestured at the ruined hamlets they'd passed, roofless houses with their doorways facing an ocean that no longer existed, "—they didn't die. They were abandoned. Their reason for being was taken inland and buried."

Sera was quiet for a moment. "The Court teaches that the binding saved the coast. That the god would have drowned everything."

"The Court also taught you I'd be a monster." He started up the path toward the lighthouse. "Come on. If we're going to find nothing, let's find it before dark."

The door was iron, rusted to the color of dried blood, and it opened at his touch without a sound—which was wrong, and both of them knew it was wrong, and neither of them said so. Doors in dead places groaned. This one swung inward as if it had been waiting.

Inside, the stair spiraled upward into gloom, but Rafael's feet turned the other way, down, into the cellar that should have held oil stores and rope. It held books instead.

Hundreds of them. Thousands. Shelves carved directly into the rock, and on every shelf, volumes swollen with damp or cracked with dryness, spines lettered in a dozen hands across what looked like three centuries. Salt-logs. Tide tables. Almanacs for seas that had retreated before any living keeper was born.

And on a desk in the center of the room, beneath a lantern whose glass had somehow stayed clean, a single notebook lay open, weighted at the corners with four white stones.

"She kept this place," Rafael said. His voice came out strange. "The last vessel. The one who wrote the instructions."

"You don't know that."

He touched the open page. The ink was brown-black, flaking, unmistakable even now. "It's written in blood. I know what my own looks like when it dries." He hadn't known that until he said it. Another gift from whatever was waking in him. "Her name was Amaya. She was vessel ninety years ago. She came here after the valley tried to put her back to sleep, and she spent eleven years looking for another way, and then she died of it, and everyone decided her way had failed because she died." He looked up at Sera. "But she died at sixty-two, Sera. Vessels who hold the god asleep die at nineteen. Twenty-three. She bought herself forty extra years, and they called it failure because she didn't live forever."

Sera had gone very still, the way she did when her training and her conscience pulled in opposite directions. Then she crossed the room, pulled the lantern down, and lit it. "Then we read," she said. "All night if we have to. The Court's delegation won't reach the flats for days yet, but I'd rather not spend those days discovering this at dawn."

* * *

### Sera

The blood faded where the writing was oldest, and Sera found herself leaning close to the pages, lips moving, translating a dead woman's handwriting from script into sense. Around midnight, Rafael fell asleep sitting up against the shelves, salt dusting his hair like premature age, and she did not wake him. He needed sleep more than she needed company. Besides, some of what Amaya had written was not for him.

*The god does not hate us,* Amaya had written in her fourth year, in ink this time, ordinary oak-gall ink gone sepia. *I sat with it for six nights in the flooded mine and it never once reached for me with anything but loneliness. They tell us it dreams of drowning the world. It dreams of company. These are not the same dream, but I understand why the mages preferred their version.*

Sera had been raised on the Court's version. She had recited it in catechism: *the Salt God is hunger without limit, bound for our protection.* She had believed it the way you believe the floor will hold you—without examination, until the day you look down and see the planks are rotten.

She had seen the floor rot in stages. First the writ on her desk ordering the vessel's capture alive but *compliant*, with a list of approved methods that made her stomach turn. Then Odile, arriving at the delegation camp with her soft voice and her mother-of-pearl smile, asking precise questions about how much pain a vessel could endure before the god woke involuntarily—and taking notes. Then Rafael himself, asleep against a bookshelf, salt in his hair, who had twice now had chances to hurt her and had instead shared his food.

*They want a weapon,* Amaya wrote. *A weapon must be aimed. But a god is not a weapon; it is a weather. You cannot aim a weather. You can only learn its seasons, and build accordingly, and warn each other honestly when the storm comes.*

And then, near the end, the pages that mattered:

*I found it. After nine years of reading everything the drowned coast left behind, I found the account of the original binding, and it is not what the histories say. The mages did not defeat the god. They negotiated with it—a bad bargain, a coward's bargain, terms extracted while it was wounded and grieving—but a bargain nonetheless. It agreed to sleep. It has kept that agreement for a thousand years. Everything since—the vessels, the valley, the wards, the deaths of children in the dark—that is not the bargain. That is the cage they built around the bargain because they could not bear the terms.*

*The original terms were simple: the god sleeps of its own will, and wakes of its own will, and in exchange the coast lives and the tides return. What the mages added was the vessels—anchors, hostages, collateral. Every generation a child, so the god would know its freedom was always mortgaged against a life. They made its mercy conditional on our cruelty and called it safety.*

*A fourth path exists. Not sleeping it, which kills us. Not killing it, which cannot be done and would unmake the coast besides. Not serving it, which is only slavery with better manners. Bargaining. Renegotiation. It can be done—I am certain it can be done—but it requires a vessel willing to speak for both sides, and it requires payment, and I did not learn the price until too late to pay it myself. My body is failing. Whatever the god's seed does to us, it spends us faster than it should, and I have perhaps a season left.*

*So I leave this for the next one, if there is a next one, if the keepers' carelessness ever lets one of us read:*

*The bargain must be spoken at the heart of the flats, at the place where the first anchor-stone was driven. It must be spoken awake, deliberately, before the god rises on its own—because a god woken in chains wakes angry, and a god invited to the table wakes as itself. And it requires payment. The god will ask for memory. Not all of it. Only the memories of belonging—home, hearth, the people who made a place yours. It wants to know what it is protecting. It wants to feel, once, from inside a mortal life, what a harbor is.*

*That is the price. That is the whole price. I would have paid it gladly if I had learned it ten years sooner, when there was someone left for me to remember.*

*If you are reading this, vessel, whoever you are: I am sorry. I am sorry for every year they lied to you, every keeper who smiled at you across a market stall knowing what you were, every kindness that was really a wall. I am sorry that your inheritance is this cellar and this choice. But hear me: you are not a cage. You are not a key. You are a person, and persons may speak, and speaking is the one power they never managed to take from us.*

*Speak well. Speak for yourself first. Let the god hear a human voice that is not afraid.*

*—Amaya, vessel, keeper of the light, who saw the sea once and means to see it again however she must.*

Sera sat back. The lantern had burned low. Above her, through the ruined lamp room, she could see stars turning, indifferent and enormous, and somewhere south of them the valley slept inside its dying wards, and somewhere east the Ashen Court's delegation moved like a slow black tide toward the flats.

She heard Rafael stir behind her.

"How much did you read?" he asked. His voice was rough with sleep.

"All of it." She hesitated. "Rafael—the price. It's your memories. Home. Everyone who—"

"Marisol," he said quietly. "The apothecary. My mother's kitchen, what's left of it. The smell of the shop in winter." He said it steadily, listing them like inventory, and she understood suddenly that he had already done this arithmetic alone in the dark, that he had probably been doing it since the mines, since the god's dreaming loneliness had first recognized him. "Yes. I know."

"And you're still—"

"It's the cheapest thing anyone's ever asked of me." He came forward into the lantern light and crouched beside the notebook, and his eyes when they met hers were calm in a way that frightened her more than panic would have. "The Court wants my body. The valley wants my sleep. Odile wants my name. The god is the only party at this table offering to take something instead of giving orders." He touched the last line of blood-writing, gently, the way you touch a face. "Besides. Amaya's right about one thing. Belonging isn't a possession. It's a practice. I lost the memories once already, in a way—fifteen years of my life were a lie told to me by people who loved me. I survived learning that. I can survive losing the learning too."

"That's not the same and you know it."

"No," he admitted. "It isn't. But it rhymes." He closed the notebook and held it against his chest. "There's one problem. The bargain needs to be spoken at the anchor-stone, awake, before the god rises on its own. The wards have maybe three days. The Court arrives in two. Which means—"

"Which means we stop running tonight," Sera said, "and start moving toward the thing we've been running from."

Outside, far to the south, the horizon flickered—a pale pulse, like lightning trapped under the earth, like something vast turning over in its sleep and beginning, patiently, to open one eye.

Rafael watched it and did not look away.

"Two days," he said. "Then we go home and wake a god."
