## 16. Blood Instructions

The lighthouse smelled of rust and old salt, of iron left too long to the weather, and beneath it all—faint as a memory under a memory—the sweetness of dried herbs gone to dust in their jars. Rafael had been in the tower for three days now, and he had learned its language: the groan of the stairs when the wind came off the flats, the way the light room at the top still turned on its ancient gearing though no flame had burned there in eighty years, ticking like a patient heart. He had learned, too, that the dead vessel's name was Ilsa, and that she had written her last instructions in blood because ink, she had discovered, could be erased.

Blood could only be burned.

He sat in the keeper's quarters with the pages spread across a table scarred by decades of elbows, Sera asleep—or pretending to sleep—on the narrow cot behind him. Outside, the sky over the valley glowed wrong, a bruised amber that never fully darkened anymore, and every few hours the ground shivered like a horse troubled by flies. The god was waking whether Rafael did anything or not. That was the thing nobody wanted to say plainly: the wards were not fraying because of the Court, or because of Odile, or even because of him. They were fraying because a century was up, because everything sleeps poorly eventually, because the salt flats themselves had begun to breathe.

The notes were written in three hands.

That had taken him two days to understand, and understanding it had felt like falling through ice. The oldest hand was careful, scholarly, letters upright as fence posts—a young woman writing while she still believed she would be obeyed by her own body. The second hand slanted, hurried, the loops of certain letters collapsing inward: the same woman, years later, writing against pain or against time. And the third hand barely qualified as handwriting at all—scratches, gouges, words dug into the paper with something harder than a pen, made in the last months, maybe the last weeks, when her fingers no longer did what she asked them.

Because that was what the vessels became, in the end. Not people with a god inside them but a shoreline with a tide going out and coming in, each wave taking a little more of the sand.

*If you are reading this,* the first hand began, *then you are the one after me, and I am sorry. I tried to leave you nothing but this.*

Rafael read it again. He had read it perhaps forty times. Each time the apology landed somewhere new.

*I was born in this valley the year the wards were raised, which is not a coincidence, whatever they told my mother. I heard the god before I could walk. Not voices—I want to be clear about that, because they will tell you it is voices, and it is not. It is more like standing next to a wall and knowing someone is on the other side, breathing. All my life I knew someone was breathing on the other side of everything.*

Sera shifted on the cot. Rafael didn't turn around. There was a strange mercy in reading these words aloud in his head while she slept, as if Ilsa and he were having a conversation across a hundred years and Sera's presence kept it from becoming a séance.

*They bound it here because it could not be killed. Understand that first, because everything else follows from it. The mages who built the valley did not win a war; they lost one slowly, over forty years, and the binding was the treaty. A god of salt does not die. Salt is what remains when everything else is gone. You can burn a forest and the ash washes to the sea and the sea leaves salt on the shore. It is the patience at the end of all things. They put it to sleep and they built a cage of stillness around the sleep, and then they needed a lock that was alive, because locks that are not alive can be picked.*

*The lock is you. The lock was me. The lock will be whoever comes after, unless you find the fourth door.*

Rafael's hands had gone cold. He flexed them, pressed them flat against the table, felt the grain of wood worn smooth by a dead woman's arms. Through the window, far off, the amber sky pulsed once, twice, like something turning over in sleep.

*Here is what I learned, and it cost me everything I am about to tell you it cost, so pay attention.*

*There are three doors. Sleep: keep the binding, feed it your life, die young with the god still dreaming behind your ribs. This is what the valley wants from you. Kill: there are those who believe it can be done, that enough of the Court's ash-magic focused through a willing vessel can unmake even salt. I tried to open this door myself, in my twenty-third year. I will not describe what happened. I will say only that the eastern flats are white glass now, and that killing a god of salt is like trying to kill the concept of the end of things. Serve: give yourself to it wholly, let it wear you, and it will spare what you love—but what you love will belong to it, and it does not know the difference between sparing and keeping.*

*The fourth door is a bargain. Not surrender. Not destruction. Terms.*

*It took me nine years to learn that it would accept terms. Nine years of listening at the wall, learning the rhythm of the breathing, until I understood that what sleeps under the salt is not mindless. It is lonely. It has been lonely since before there were words for lonely, and loneliness, it turns out, is a kind of hunger, and hunger can be negotiated with.*

*But a bargain requires payment, and the god does not want gold or blood or years. Those are small currencies. What it wants—the only thing it values, the thing it has been reaching for through every vessel since the beginning—is the feeling of home.*

Sera sat up.

Rafael heard the cot creak, heard her bare feet on the floorboards, and then her hand was on his shoulder, warm through his shirt, and he realized his lips had been moving, reading the last lines aloud without deciding to.

"Don't," she said.

"I haven't gotten to the worst part yet."

"Rafa." She came around the chair and crouched so she was looking up at him, and her eyes in the lamplight were fierce and wet at once. "I've been listening to you turn pages for three days. I've watched your face do things I didn't know faces could do. Don't read me the worst part like it's just information."

He almost smiled. "You're an agent of the Ashen Court. You burned your writ for me. You should be arguing for the efficient option."

"The efficient option is I knock you out and drag you back to the valley and let Marisol lock you in the apothecary cellar." Her voice cracked on Marisol's name, and he understood that she was frightened in the specific way of someone who has already lost the argument with herself. "I've run the numbers, Rafa. Every version where you do what this book says ends with you walking out of that valley not knowing my name. I've had four days of you. Four days of knowing you, and I'm telling you I'd burn the whole Court down rather than have you look at me the way you're going to look at me."

"Sera—"

"*Read it to me,*" she said, quieter now, "and then let me hate it properly. That's all I'm asking."

So he read.

*The payment is memory,* Ilsa wrote, in the second hand, the slanted hurrying one. *Not all of it. Only the memories that make a place into home—and hear me, because this is the trap: you do not get to choose which ones by naming them. The god takes what home is made of, and home is not made of the big things. Home is your mother's kitchen. The particular squeak of a stair. The smell of a shop when you push the door open. The sound a person makes when they laugh at their own joke before the joke lands. It takes the small things, hundreds of them, thousands, the mortar between the bricks of a life, and when it is done the bricks are still standing but nothing holds them together. You will remember facts. You will not remember belonging.*

*And you cannot hold on to them by wanting to. Wanting is part of the currency. I tried. I wrote my mother's name on my arm in ink, and when the bargain was struck I looked at the name and knew it was a name and felt nothing, and the nothing was worse than forgetting, because forgetting at least is sleep. I stood in my own village among people weeping over me and I was a stranger holding a list.*

*One thing, though. One thing survives. I don't know why. Perhaps the god permits it as a courtesy, the way a thief leaves one coin in the purse. Choose it beforehand. Write it down. Make it small—not a person, not a face, those are too large and the god will take them anyway. A smell. A sound. Something you could carry in a closed fist.*

*Choose it, and choose knowing that afterward you will not remember choosing.*

Rafael stopped reading. The lamp hissed. Somewhere below them the sea-less sea of the flats whispered against itself, and the lighthouse ticked its empty rotation overhead.

"That's why she wrote in blood," he said. His voice sounded far away to himself. "Ink fades. Ink can be rewritten. But she wanted—" He swallowed. "She wanted the last thing she ever chose to be permanent."

Sera was silent for a long moment. When she spoke, her voice was rough. "What did she keep? Do the notes say?"

He turned the pages carefully, past diagrams of the rite—circles drawn in salt within salt within salt, the geometry of terms, the exact words of invocation written phonetically because the god's language predated writing and had to be spoken, not read. Near the end, in the third hand, the gouged desperate letters:

*Kept the smell of rain coming. That was mine. Everything else is its. If you are the one after me: I hope your one thing is as small and as enormous as mine was. I hope you get to use it. I never did. I died in the winter with the rain still coming, somewhere over the mountains, and I never got to stand in it again.*

*Go at dawn. Go barefoot. Speak the terms exactly. And when it asks what you offer, do not hesitate—it reads hesitation as invitation, and it will take more than you meant to give.*

*Last instruction, and this is the only one I beg: make sure someone who loves you is watching. Not to stop you—they can't, and trying will only break them. Watch so that someone remembers you were real. Memory is the price, but witness is the change you get back. It isn't much. It's everything.*

Rafael set the pages down. His hands were steady, which surprised him; he had expected shaking, and instead there was only a terrible calm, the calm of a man who has finally seen the bottom of the water he's been drowning in for nineteen years.

"Dawn," he said. "Tomorrow."

"No." Sera stood, backed away a step, and he watched her assemble the agent again piece by piece, the flat voice, the tactical posture, all of it failing at the seams. "No, we have time, the Court won't move before—"

"They move tomorrow. You told me. Two days' march, and they were a day out when we fled." He rose, and the chair scraped, and the sound seemed very loud in the round stone room. "If I wait, they force the awakening under their control, and the god wakes in chains, and everything Ilsa wrote about sparing becomes a lie. If I run farther, the wards fail completely and the valley drowns in miracles it doesn't survive. There is no version where I keep the memories, Sera. There's only versions where I choose which ones to lose."

"You could choose nothing. You could fight it—"

"With what?" He gestured at himself, at the thin arms, the herb-stained fingers, the whole unremarkable fact of him. "I'm a lock, Sera. Locks don't fight. They either hold or they open. The only choice I've ever actually had is *how*."

She crossed the room in two strides and took his face in her hands, and her palms were trembling, and that undid him more than any argument could have. "Then take mine," she whispered. "Take my memories. Give it my mother's kitchen, my first snow, my—give it everything of mine and keep yours, there must be a way, you're the vessel, you set the terms—"

"It doesn't work like that. Ilsa tried substitutions. She wrote it right here—" he touched the page gently, "—*the payment must come from the vessel's own house.* It's not buying memories, Sera. It's buying *home*. Mine. Only mine."

"Then don't go barefoot," she said wildly, absurdly, grasping at the smallest instruction as if the whole rite might hinge on footwear. "Don't speak the terms exactly. Misspell something. Hesitate on purpose—"

"And it takes more than I meant to give." He covered her hands with his own. "I know. I've read the fine print. I wrote half of it myself, in another century, apparently."

That startled a broken laugh out of her, and then she was crying, quietly, the way agents cry, efficiently, hating every second of it, and he held her while the lighthouse ticked above them and the amber sky outside began, at last, to pale toward a dawn neither of them wanted.

Later—when her breathing had gone slow and heavy against his shoulder—he sat alone at the table with a stub of pencil and the back of one of Ilsa's blank pages, and he thought about the one small thing.

Not Marisol's laugh; too large, too much a person, the god would take it regardless. Not the apothecary's smell; that was the whole shape of his childhood, and shapes that big slip through the god's fingers like water and it keeps reaching. It had to be small. Small enough to hide in a closed fist.

He thought of evenings in the shop, closing time, Marisol sweeping while he stacked jars, and how she always—always—sang the same three notes under her breath, tuneless, half-hummed, some fragment of a song she'd never explain, and how he had spent fourteen years secretly waiting for those three notes every single evening, and how he had never once told her he listened for them.

Three notes. He could carry three notes in a closed fist.

His eyes stung. He wrote it down in the margin, small, in case the god could read: *the three notes Marisol hums at closing.*

Then, because Ilsa had begged, he woke Sera, and told her what he had chosen, and made her swear—swear on her writ's ashes, on her name, on whatever she still believed in—that after it was done, she would hum those three notes to him, someday, somewhere, without explaining why. So that the coin the thief left in the purse might, someday, ring true.

She swore. She swore with her forehead pressed to his and her tears on his mouth, and outside the first gray light came up over the white glass flats, and far away, at the valley's edge, he could feel the Ashen Court arriving like a change in pressure, like thunder deciding where to land.

Dawn was here. The salt waited. And Rafael Blackwood, who had spent his whole life being decided for, picked up Ilsa's blood-browned pages, folded them into his shirt over his heart, and started down the stairs—to go barefoot, to speak the terms exactly, and to pay for a valley's freedom with everything that had ever made it worth saving.

Behind him, Sera followed, carrying the only thing he'd asked of her: a promise about three notes, and a reason she would never remember giving.
