## 19. The Price of Walking Away

*I am the salt, and I am the one who walks into the salt, and we are the same width now.*

Rafael had thought waking a god would feel like an explosion. It felt instead like a door opening in a house he had lived in all his life without knowing it had rooms—rooms full of weather, rooms full of drowned bells, rooms where light moved like something swimming. The god rose through him the way dawn rises through fog: not suddenly, but completely.

*Little vessel,* it said, and its voice was not sound but pressure, the way deep water presses on the ear. *You are braver than the ones before you. They came to me asleep and asked to keep sleeping.*

"I'm not asking to sleep." Rafael stood at the heart of the flats, boots sunk to the ankle in crusted white, the sky above him bruised purple with the Court's descending lights. Behind him, far off, he could hear Marisol's voice carrying across the valley—not shouting, not pleading, just speaking, the way she spoke when she wanted a room to go quiet. The valley had come out to stand witness. He would not look at them. If he looked at them, he would begin saying goodbye, and goodbyes were a currency he could not afford to spend twice. "I'm asking for terms."

*Terms.* The god turned the word over like a stone found on a beach, tasting what had grown on its underside. *The mages did not offer terms. They offered chains and called them mercy. The vessels did not ask for terms. They held their mouths shut around my dreaming until their hearts gave out, one by one, like lamps running dry.*

"I know. I saw them." He had seen them in the descent, in the flooded mines—all those small stubborn lives spent holding a door closed from the inside. "I'm not them. I'm the one who read the book. I'm the one who found her notes." He touched his own forearm, where the dead vessel's blood-written instructions had been copied in ink that still smelled faintly of iron. "You get to live through me. Freely. No cage, no leash, no Court with its hands around your throat. In exchange—the valley stands. Every soul in it. You don't touch them, you don't drown their fields, you don't salt their wells. Not now, not in a hundred years, not ever."

*And what do you pay?*

"That's the part you're going to hate," Rafael said, and almost smiled. "Because I hate it too."

* * *

The god had been alone so long that it had forgotten loneliness was a thing with a shape. For three thousand years it had been pressure without edges, hunger without a mouth, a dream so vast it mistook itself for the world. Then the mages came with their circles of chalk and their children bred like candles, and the god learned a new sensation: being *held*. Being carried inside someone small and warm and terrified, someone whose heartbeat was a drum it could not stop hearing.

Every vessel had been a prison. Every vessel had also been—this was the shame the god buried deepest beneath the salt—the only company it had ever known.

Now this one stood in the open air and offered himself as a door instead of a wall. *Walk through me whenever you want,* he was saying. *Look out through my eyes. But the toll is memory. Everything that makes a place feel like home. That is the price written in her blood, and blood does not lie.*

*Why that price?* the god asked, though it suspected. It had watched four vessels live and die inside their own skulls. It knew what homes were. It knew they were the last things people surrendered.

"Because home is the anchor," Rafael said. "And you need to be unanchored to move freely through me. And because—" his voice caught, and he let it catch; there was no dignity left worth protecting, "—because if I keep my memories, I'll spend eternity grieving them while you wear my face. Better to give them up clean. Better to walk away whole and empty than half-broken forever."

*You understand,* the god said slowly, *that once the payment begins, it cannot be stopped. Not by me. Not by your witches. Not by any court of ash or salt. Memory, once given to the water, stays in the water.*

"I read the instructions eleven times."

*Then choose,* said the god, and for the first time in three thousand years, it chose gentleness. *Choose what remains. One thing only—one thread you may hold while the rest unwinds. Choose carefully, little vessel. It will be the room you live in.*

* * *

The payment began with the smell of bread.

That was how Rafael knew it was real—not pain, not light, but the sudden absence of a smell. His mother's kitchen had always begun with bread: the yeast-and-warmth of it rolling down the hallway of the small house at the valley's edge, waking him better than any bell. He stood in the salt flats and reached for that morning—he was six, maybe seven, the window fogged, his mother's back to him, flour to her elbows—and found the doorway open and the room already going gray at the edges, like paper left in rain.

"No," he said, uselessly, the way everyone says no.

The kitchen did not care. The fogged window cleared. The flour vanished from her arms. Her back became a back, then a shape, then a warmth with no face attached, then a warmth with no body attached, then only the fact that once, somewhere, someone had loved him first and best and without conditions—and even that fact was thinning, stretching, becoming a sentence without a speaker.

He felt the god watching from inside his own ribs, feeling him grieve, unable to look away, unable to help.

*It hurts less if you do not fight each room,* the god murmured. *Fighting makes the doors slam. Let them open.*

So Rafael stopped fighting, and the kitchen went, and after it the garden his mother had kept—the beans on their strings, the snails he'd collected in a bucket and named after saints—and after the garden, the house itself, and after the house, his mother's voice, which had been the last living piece of her, singing something low and tuneless while she worked. He held the tune as long as he could, humming it into the wind, until he realized he was humming a shape with no song in it, and stopped.

* * *

Marisol's laugh went next, and that one he fought anyway.

He couldn't help it. She was alive, she was standing behind him somewhere in the gathering dark with the whole valley at her shoulders, and it seemed obscene to lose her while she was still breathing. But memory does not consult the present. The apothecary dissolved around him shelf by shelf: the jars of comfrey and yarrow, the brass scale with its chipped pan, the way she said *careful, that one bites* about the nettle tincture every single time as though it were the first time. Her laugh—she laughed from the belly, head tipped back, entirely unguarded, a laugh that made strangers in the market turn and smile without knowing why—that laugh went out of him like a candle pinched between wet fingers.

He staggered. The salt crust cracked under his knees.

*Rafael.* The god used his name for the first time. *Hold the thread. Hold the one thread. Let the rest go, or you will tear.*

"What's left?" His own voice sounded foreign. "What am I keeping?"

*You have not chosen yet. Choose now, quickly, before the water chooses for you.*

He cast through what remained, frantic, a man grabbing at belongings in a flooding house. Sera's face, three weeks old and already load-bearing. The prophecy book falling open to his own name. Odile's hands, gentle and terrible. The mines, the dreaming dark, the first time the god had recognized him and he had—shamefully, undeniably—recognized it back. The wards fraying like old rope under his fingers. The taste of well water. The particular quality of valley silence, magic-dead and safe, the silence he had fled into at fifteen believing it a refuge—

There. In the middle of the flood, one small dry room.

Not the biggest memory. Not the sweetest. A small one, almost embarrassing: he was nine years old, sick with a winter fever, and Marisol had sat beside his cot all night in the back room of the apothecary, reading aloud from a book of nothing—no spells, no prophecies, just stories about a fox who wanted to be a dog—and at some point near dawn he had woken and she had been asleep sitting up, her hand still resting on his hair, and he had looked at her and thought, with the absolute certainty available only to children: *I am safe. This is what safe feels like. I will know it again.*

He had known it again. For ten more years, in that same building, among those same jars, he had known it every day.

*I keep that one,* Rafael said. *The fever night. The hand in my hair. Safe—I keep knowing what safe feels like.*

*It is small,* the god observed.

"It's the load-bearing wall."

*Then it will hold.* And the god did something no god should have been able to do, something the dead vessel's notes had promised was possible but had never described: it helped. It put its vast weight against the flood on the other side, holding the small room dry while everything else went under.

* * *

The rest went in waves, and Rafael learned to breathe between them.

The smell of the apothecary—dried lavender, beeswax, the mineral bite of sal ammoniac—drained out of the world until he stood in the salt flats smelling only salt, and could not remember why salt had ever smelled like anything else. The layout of the valley streets went, so that the place he had walked daily for nineteen years became, in his mind, a map drawn by a stranger. The names went. Neighbors he had known since infancy became faces without handles, then faces, then the warm fact of having been surrounded by people, then not even that.

Sera he lost in pieces, and the pieces hurt worst because they were newest. Her voice saying *I burned my writ for you, you idiot* — gone. The lighthouse, the failed fourth path, her hand steady over the blood-written pages — gone. He gripped at what remained and found only this: that there was a person, recently, whom he had trusted, and that trusting her had been his own choice, freely made, and that the choice itself was still true even though its reasons had drowned. He wrapped that bare fact around himself like a coat.

*They will grieve you,* the god said quietly, as the last wave gathered. *The witch. The hunter. The ones who stay. They will stand next to you and be strangers to you, and you to them. Are you certain—*

"Don't ask me again." His voice was almost gone, scraped down to bedrock. "Asking again is how you make martyrs. I'm not dying. I'm moving out. There's a difference, and the difference is that I'm choosing the door."

*Then walk through it.*

The last wave took everything else. His fifteenth year, the flight to the valley, the terror and relief of crossing the border. His childhood entire. The hedge-witch's cottage, both the tenderness and the cruelty, mercifully indistinguishable now, going together. The book of prophecies, his name in century-old ink. The words *the vessel wakes*, which had started all of this, dissolving into water stains on blank paper.

What remained was very small and very bright: a cot in a back room. A fever. A hand in his hair. *Safe. This is what safe feels like.*

And beneath that, strange and new and entirely his own: the god's presence, no longer a prisoner in him nor he a vessel for it, but two travelers sharing one coat against the cold. It hummed at the edge of his awareness, patient, enormous, oddly careful, the way a large animal is careful in a small room.

*We are bound now,* it said. *Freely. Do you understand what that means, little vessel?*

Rafael opened his eyes. The sky was clearing. Somewhere behind him, voices were rising—not in fear, he thought distantly, though he could no longer have said whose voices, or why they might fear, or what a valley was, exactly, except a green place between hills where people had once stood witness to something important.

"I don't remember what it means," he said honestly. "But I remember how to find out."

He turned toward the figures coming across the salt—a woman in an apron running, a woman in ash-gray leathers walking faster than running, others behind them—and felt nothing he could name, and one thing he could:

Safe. This, apparently, was what safe felt like. He would build the rest from here.

The woman in the apron reached him first and stopped as though struck, searching his face for herself and finding nothing. Her mouth shaped a name he did not know.

"Yes?" Rafael said politely, and the salt wind carried the word out over the flats, and the god inside him stirred like a tide remembering the moon, and far away in the apothecary, unseen by anyone, a century-old book began, very softly, to rewrite itself.
