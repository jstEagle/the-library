## 1. The Charm That Would Not Light

The moon was one night shy of her twelfth turn, and Marguerite Villalobos was selling a dead woman's protection.

She'd wrapped it well — that was the craft of it, the only craft left to her. A knot of salt-white thread around a sliver of bone, sealed in wax the color of old milk, tucked into a leather pouch that smelled of rosemary and something older underneath, something like iron left out in rain. On the tag she'd written, in her careful mercenary's hand: *For the traveler. For the road at night. It holds.*

It didn't hold. Nothing held anymore. But the woman buying it didn't know that, and Marguerite had a rent due on the stall and a debt due on her pride, and twenty years of practice telling herself those were different things.

"Three silver," she said.

The buyer was a carter named Doss, broad through the shoulders, nervous through the eyes. His route ran the mountain road twice a month, past the old tollgate where travelers had been disappearing since before Marguerite could walk. He turned the pouch over in his big hands like it might bite him.

"My wife bought one of these," he said. "Off you. Last autumn."

"And did anything take her?"

"No." He said it grudgingly, the way men say things when they want to believe but have been burned before. "No, it didn't."

"There's your answer." Marguerite leaned back against the stall post and let him look at her. She knew what he saw: a woman of forty-odd years with a sword-callused grip and a scar splitting one eyebrow, wearing a coat too good for a charm-seller because good coats were the last thing mercenaries stopped buying when everything else went to rot. She knew what he saw, and she let him see it, because fear of the seller was half the price of the goods.

What he couldn't see was the mark on her wrist, under the cuff. What nobody could see anymore, because it hadn't done anything but sit there like a brand from a cold fire for two decades.

"Three silver," she said again.

He paid. Of course he paid. They always paid, right up until the moment they didn't.

* * *

The moment came an hour later, in front of forty people and one Inquisitor.

Marguerite heard it before she understood it — a sound from down the row of stalls, high and wrong, like a dog crying in a human throat. Then Doss came around the corner of the fishmonger's awning at a dead run, holding the charm out in front of him at arm's length, and the charm was burning.

Not glowing. Burning. The salt-thread had gone black and was smoking, the wax bubbling off the bone sliver in fat greasy drops, and the smell hit the market square like a slap — burnt hair and hot metal and underneath it something sweeter, something that made the back of Marguerite's neck prickle with a memory she didn't want.

"It lit up!" Doss was shouting. "On the road, not even past the tollgate, it just — it caught fire in my hand! You said it would hold!"

Behind him, unhurried as weather, came the Inquisitor.

Guild-gray robes, the hood back, a young face made old by the expression on it — that particular flat patience they drilled into them, the look of a man who has already decided how this ends and is simply waiting for the paperwork to agree. His badge was polished bright. That was the thing about the Inquisitors' Guild, Marguerite thought, watching him come. Twenty years since the Edict of Silence, twenty years since magic died in Vell Hollow and everyone swore it had never lived, and their badges still shone like they expected a war.

The war was over. The war had been over since before some of these children were born. All that was left was the mopping up, and the mopping up looked like this: a charm-seller's stall, a frightened carter, a crowd pulling back to make room the way crowds always do, leaving the guilty party standing alone in a circle of cobbles and daylight.

"Villalobos." The Inquisitor read the name off a slate he carried, though there was no way he'd known it before. They always knew. "Marguerite Villalobos. Licensed vendor, third district. These are—" he lifted the ruined charm from Doss's hands with tongs he'd produced from somewhere, delicately, like a priest handling relics, "—these are proscribed materials."

"They're thread and bone and wax," Marguerite said. "You can buy all three at the chandler's."

"The wax is sigil-wax." He said it mildly. "The bone is cut on the old pattern. And the thread—" he tilted the smoking ruin toward her, and she saw, with a lurch she kept off her face, that the blackened threads had fallen into a shape. Not random char-marks. A spiral. The kind of spiral that used to be drawn on doorposts when she was a girl, before drawing it became a crime. "The thread remembers what it was taught."

Around them the crowd had gone quiet in that particular hungry way. Somewhere a mother pulled a child close. Somewhere else, someone Marguerite couldn't see was already deciding whether this would be worth a whipping or just a fine, and either way it would be talked about in three districts by nightfall.

"I sell souvenirs," Marguerite said. "Knick-knacks. Road-luck for the gullible. If it caught fire, that's a flaw in my supplier's wax, and I'll be having words with him."

"If it caught fire." The Inquisitor's eyes came up from the tongs. Flat. Patient. "Charms don't catch fire, mistress. Charms are thread and bone and wax, as you say. Dead things don't burn unless something lights them." He let that hang. "So the interesting question — the question I'll be asking at the Guildhouse, formally, under seal — is what lit yours."

And there it was. The trap wasn't the charge; the charge was nothing, a fine, an embarrassment. The trap was the questions afterward, in a room with no windows, where a woman with a mark hidden under her cuff would be asked to bare her arms.

Marguerite had survived twenty years by knowing which fights were fights. This one wasn't. Not here, not now, not with the crowd watching and the moon a single turn from full and her cellar full of stock she needed to move before the season turned.

"You'll find nothing at the Guildhouse," she said, "because there's nothing to find. But I'll come. Tomorrow. After market."

"After market," the Inquisitor agreed, and smiled without any of his face joining in. "Do try to keep your dead things quiet until then, mistress. Whatever's lighting them."

He walked away. The crowd, cheated of a whipping, began the slow disappointed business of dispersing. Doss stood there holding nothing, looking at Marguerite with an expression she'd seen a hundred times and would see a hundred more — the look of a man who wanted his three silver back and his belief back and couldn't have either.

"Refund," he said.

She gave it to him. Three silver, counted out of a purse that couldn't afford it, into a hand that took it without thanks. He went. The market noise swelled back up around the hole he'd left, and Marguerite stood behind her stall among her unsold charms — forty of them, fifty, each one thread and bone and wax and nothing, each one a small dead promise — and felt the day settle onto her shoulders the way it always did, familiar as a yoke.

Weary resignation. That was the whole of her religion now. She'd had another once, but its god had gone silent, and silence, in Vell Hollow, was the law.

* * *

She closed the stall early. Carried the unsold charms home in the same basket she'd carried them in for years, down streets that knew her feet, past the shrine-niches in the walls that had been bricked over by edict and then painted over by habit and then just left, gray scars in the stone that nobody looked at anymore because looking had become the kind of thing that got you asked questions in rooms without windows.

Her rooms were above a dyer's shop in the Hollow district, three flights up and one across, reached by stairs that complained. She lit no lamp — lamp-oil cost, and anyway her eyes were good enough in the dark, another inheritance from the trade she no longer practiced. She set the basket down. She ate bread and hard cheese standing up. She took off the good coat and hung it, and sat down on the edge of her bed to pull off her boots, and that was when she heard the knock.

Not a knock. That was the thing she realized later, lying awake replaying it. Knocking has weight behind it, intent, a fist or knuckles, someone wanting something. This was softer. Fingertips. Three small taps, spaced like a heartbeat slowing down.

Marguerite went to the door with a knife she hadn't consciously decided to pick up.

The boy on her landing couldn't have been more than eight. Barefoot, though the month was turning cold. Thin in the way that isn't poverty exactly but absence, as if someone had been subtracting from him a little at a time. Dark hair cut badly, uneven, the work of a hurried hand. He stood very straight, which was the first strange thing — barefoot children on landings at midnight usually hunch, apologize with their spines. This one stood like a soldier waiting to be reviewed.

The second strange thing was his eyes. They were fixed on her wrist. On the cuff she'd pulled down, without thinking about why, the moment she'd opened the door.

Then he spoke, and the third strange thing swallowed the first two whole.

It wasn't Common. It wasn't anything. It was sounds that shouldn't have fit in a human mouth — vowels that seemed to arrive from further away than his lips, consonants like stones dropped down a well, and underneath the words a rhythm, a pulse, that Marguerite felt in her teeth before she heard it with her ears. She knew languages. Mercenaries collect them the way soldiers collect scars, and she'd bartered in four tongues along the border roads. This was none of them. This was older than all of them, older than the kingdoms that spoke them, older maybe than speaking itself — a language that sounded like it had been there first, and everything since was translation.

And her wrist was burning.

Through the cuff, through the skin, through the twenty years of silence — the mark on her wrist, the god-mark she'd sworn into at seventeen and mourned at thirty-five, woke up. Heat poured off it like a struck flint. Not pain, exactly. Recognition. The feeling of a limb waking after too long asleep, all pins and needles and terrible returning life, and Marguerite staggered back a step inside her own doorway with the knife shaking in her hand and every instinct she owned screaming two opposite orders at once: *shut the door*, and *kneel*.

The boy watched her burn and did not look surprised. He said something else, shorter this time, gentler, and though she understood not one word of it, she understood it completely — the way you understand a hand extended in the dark. It sounded, impossibly, like her own name. Not *Marguerite*. Something older, folded inside it, the way a seed folds inside the fruit.

"How do you know me?" she whispered.

He tilted his head. Considered. Then, with the grave patience of someone crossing a bridge built long ago for exactly this purpose, he raised one small dirty hand and pointed — past her, past the dark of her rooms, down through floorboards and foundations and the packed earth beneath the city, down and down, to a place she had never been and somehow, in that moment, recognized.

Down. Toward the mountain. Toward whatever hummed beneath it, if anything hummed, which nothing did, which magic was dead, which the Edict had settled all of that twenty years ago and the Inquisitors' badges were polished bright.

Her wrist throbbed like a second heart.

Marguerite Villalobos had spent two decades being a sensible woman. Sensible women shut doors on barefoot boys who speak in dead tongues. Sensible women report strange fires to no one, pay their fines, keep their cuffs down, and live.

She looked at the child in her doorway, at the moonlight lying on the landing behind him like a road leading away from everything sensible, and felt the weary resignation that had been her whole self crack down the middle — quietly, the way ice cracks on a river in spring, one hairline at a time, before the whole surface gives.

"Get inside," she said, in Common, in the plain tongue of the kingdom, the only language she had left. "Before something sees you."

The boy stepped over her threshold as if he'd crossed it a thousand times before, and closed the door behind himself with a click that sounded, in the dark of her rooms, exactly like a lock turning.
