## 2. A Language Older Than Kingdoms

The boy was still there in the morning.

Marguerite had half-hoped he'd dissolve with the dark—another hallucination of a woman who talked to charms that didn't work and drank cheap plum wine past midnight. But when she came down the stairs of her shop-house with the kettle in one hand and her knife in the other, he stood exactly where he'd been at moonset: barefoot on her threshold boards, small for eight or nine, wrapped in nothing but a grey cloak that had been fine once and was now more hole than wool.

He hadn't moved all night. She knew because she'd watched him from the window until her eyes burned.

"Right," she said, mostly to herself. "You're real."

The boy tilted his head. He didn't flinch from the knife, which worried her more than anything else about him. Children flinched. Dogs flinched. Even the Inquisitors' novices flinched, behind their masks where no one could see it.

She set the kettle down on the counter between them, an offering or a barricade. "Can you speak Common? Tap once for yes."

He looked at the counter. Looked at her. Then he spoke—and the language came out of him like water over old stones, syllables rounded and deep, vowels that seemed to have weight. The sound filled the little shop the way smoke fills a room: everywhere at once, touching everything.

Marguerite's wrist caught fire.

Not pain, exactly—or not only pain. It was the mark, the god-mark she'd taken at seventeen when she swore her blade to the Hollow Saint, the coiled sigil below her left wrist that had been dead ink for twenty years. It blazed like a coal pulled from a hearth. She dropped the knife. It clattered against the floorboards and the boy didn't startle at that either; he was watching her wrist, watching the light leak out from under her sleeve, and his face did something complicated—not triumph, not surprise. Recognition. Like a man finding his own house after a long war.

"Stop," she said. "Stop talking."

He stopped. The mark dimmed to a dull ache, then to warmth, then—to her horror—to something almost gentle. It hadn't done *anything* in twenty years. She'd pressed needles into it once, drunk and grieving, just to feel something. Nothing. And now this child opened his mouth and it woke like a dog hearing its master's step.

"What are you?" she whispered.

The boy answered in that rolling tongue, three words, soft as an apology. Her wrist throbbed on each syllable. Not burning now. *Listening.*

* * *

She gave him bread and salted fish and hot tea with too much honey because honey was cheaper than questions. He ate like someone who had learned hunger as a discipline rather than a misfortune—slowly, gratefully, wasting nothing. His feet were filthy but uncut. No shoes, no frostbite, though last night had gone cold enough to skin the gutters with ice.

"You came through the salt-flats," she said. "Nobody crosses the flats barefoot."

He looked up at her, chewing, and said something brief and melodic. The mark pulsed. She was beginning to map it, the way she mapped everything—charm-seller's habit, reading what a thing wanted by how it moved. Certain sounds made the mark flare. Others made it hum low, like a struck bell settling.

"You know my name," she said suddenly. "Last night. You said it before I told you. Say it again."

He set down the bread. Considered her with those grey eyes, too steady for a child, and said: *"Marguerite Villalobos."*

Except he didn't say it like that. He said it in his own tongue—but her name was in there, folded inside the foreign sounds like a coin sewn into a coat lining, unmistakable. Her whole name. Both names. The family name she never used because it made people look at her twice.

The mark went hot enough that she hissed through her teeth.

"How." She gripped her wrist. "How do you know that name. Who sent you?"

He shook his head slowly—not refusal. He reached across the table, hesitated with his fingers an inch from her burning wrist, asking permission with his eyes. She should have said no. Every instinct bred into twenty years of selling worthless charms to frightened people said *don't let anyone touch the mark, don't let anyone see the mark.* But she nodded.

His fingers were cold. Where they touched the sigil, the heat sank inward instead of spreading, banking itself like a fire settled for the night. The boy closed his eyes. His lips moved without sound—a prayer, maybe, or a counting—and then he opened them and pointed at her wrist, then at his own thin chest, then at the floor. Down. Under the floorboards, under the cellar, under the city.

Under the mountain.

"No," Marguerite said flatly. "Whatever you're selling, I'm not buying. Nobody goes under the mountain. Nobody comes back from under the mountain. That's not superstition, that's arithmetic."

He held up one small hand. Five fingers. Then, deliberately, he folded two down.

Three days.

"That's not funny," she said.

He didn't smile. That was the worst part. There was no mischief in him anywhere, no childish games, none of the slyness street kids learned before they lost their milk teeth. Just that grave patience, that certainty, as if he were a letter delivered to the wrong address and he would wait forever for someone to read him.

* * *

The knock came at midmorning. Two knocks, precise, spaced like a heartbeat.

Marguerite went very still. Through the shutter slats she saw the grey-and-white livery, the iron-grey mask with its blank eye-slits, the long white staff with its seal of wax and wire. An Inquisitor. Not the usual beat-walker either—this one walked like a man who counted doors.

"Charm-seller," the voice behind the mask called. Muffled, courteous, terrible. "Open for inspection."

There was no law requiring her to open for inspection. There was also no law preventing what happened to shops that didn't. She put her finger to her lips at the boy and he was already moving—silent as spilled flour, folding himself into the shadow behind the stairwell. Good. Obedient. Frighteningly quick.

She opened the door with her best empty expression, the one she wore for tax collectors and grieving widows.

"Inquisitor." She spread her hands. "Come to buy or come to burn?"

The mask turned toward her. Behind the eye-slits, nothing human showed—just the pale gleam of whatever the Guild used instead of faces these days. "Reports of irregular speech. Night before last. This district."

"People talk in their cups," Marguerite said. "People sing. People pray, if they're stupid."

"People pray," the Inquisitor agreed. The staff tapped once against her threshold stone. "Your license is current?"

"It's on the wall where you can't read it."

The mask tilted, which might have been amusement. The Inquisitor stepped inside without invitation, boots leaving faint prints of road-salt, and began the slow circuit of her shop. Marguerite's heart kept mercenary time—steady, cold, professional—while her mind screamed one phrase on loop: *the boy, the boy, the boy.*

The Inquisitor lifted a charm from the display rack. A protection knot, braided horsehair and river-iron. Dead stock. They were all dead stock; every charm in the room had been dead for twenty years and both of them knew it, and both of them pretended otherwise, because pretending was the treaty that kept the peace in Vell Hollow.

"These are licensed?" The Inquisitor turned the knot over. "No living script? No pre-kingdom characters?"

"River-iron and horsehair," Marguerite said. "You're welcome to test it."

"I will." The Inquisitor produced a testing lens from beneath the grey cloak—a disc of smoked glass in a silver frame—and held it over the charm. Nothing. Of course nothing. The Inquisitor set it down and continued along the racks, and Marguerite realized with a fresh spike of cold that the inspection wasn't the point. The Inquisitor was listening. Head cocked slightly beneath the mask. Listening for something under the silence of the room.

Behind the stairwell, the boy breathed like furniture.

Then the Inquisitor stopped. Turned. Walked toward the cellar door—the low door under the stairs, the one she kept locked, the one stuffed with unsold charms and broken furniture and, currently, absolutely nothing else, because the boy was behind the stairwell, not in the cellar, and she needed to stop confusing herself—

"The cellar," the Inquisitor said.

"Full of rats and bad memories," Marguerite said. "Like everyone's."

"The key."

She could refuse. She catalogued the refusal in half a second: the summons, the fine, the whipping post if they found anything, the whipping post even if they didn't, because a charm-seller with a smart mouth was good practice for novices. She could also open the door, and if the boy had moved—if he'd crept down there while she wasn't looking, if children were children and hid in cellars—then it ended here, in her own shop, with her own rope.

Her hand didn't shake. Twenty years of practice held it steady while she fetched the key.

The cellar smelled of brine and dust. Charms hung from nails in the ceiling joist, rows of them, knots and bundles and glass beads strung on wire, all of them dead, all of them dim. In the corner: a cracked cradle she'd never had the heart to burn, a chest of her mother's linens, forty jars of pickled things going soft.

No boy.

The Inquisitor descended two steps, raised the lens, swept it across the hanging charms. The smoked glass caught the lamplight. For a moment the reflection shifted strangely—Marguerite saw the whole cellar doubled in the curve of it, the jars, the joists, the cradle—

And in the doubled image, standing beside the cradle where no one stood, a small grey shape.

Her lungs forgot their work.

The Inquisitor lowered the lens. Tilted the mask. Looked directly at the corner where the cradle sat alone in the lamplight, where the boy was not, where the boy was *not*, where the boy was standing invisible three feet away and the lens had seen him anyway—

"Rats," Marguerite said loudly, and knocked over a jar.

It shattered on the stone floor, brine and pickle-brine flooding everywhere, and she swore with genuine artistry, dropping to her knees among the glass. "Damn it—damn it—that was a full season's brine—" She snatched at shards, cut her palm, let it bleed, made herself loud and clumsy and *present*. "Inquisitor, you'll want to watch your boots—"

The mask regarded her. Regarded the corner. Regarded her bleeding hands.

"Mind your stock better," the Inquisitor said at last, and climbed the steps. At the top it paused. "Villalobos. That is your name? The ledger lists a charm-seller named Villalobos in the Hollow district. It's an uncommon name."

"It's a common enough name where I'm from," Marguerite said.

"Mm." The Inquisitor stepped out. The door closed with terrible gentleness. His footsteps faded up the street, unhurried, counting doors.

* * *

She locked the cellar door behind her and then unlocked it immediately, because locking it felt like sealing a tomb, and stood in the middle of her shop shaking so hard the charms on their rack clicked together like teeth.

The boy emerged from behind the stairwell. Visible now, ordinary now, bare feet silent on the boards. He looked at her bleeding palm and his face did the complicated thing again—that old, grave sorrow, grief worn smooth like a stone carried in a pocket for years.

"Don't," she said, when he reached for her hand. But he took it anyway, gently, turning it over to study the shallow cut, and he spoke—soft, musical, four syllables—and the mark on her wrist answered with a slow warm pulse, and the cut stung less. She stared at her palm. The edges of the wound had already drawn together, pink and new, as if hours had passed instead of seconds.

"That's illegal," she said hoarsely. "That is so illegal. Do you understand me? What you just did, what you *are*—if anyone hears you, if anyone sees—" She stopped. Breathed. Started over. "They burn people for less than what you just did. They burned my teacher for a prayer book. They burned the moon-priestesses' whole order for bells."

He listened to every word she couldn't understand, and nodded anyway, solemn, as if he understood perfectly—as if he'd heard this speech before, in another city, in another century.

"They'll come back," she said. "That one will come back. It saw something in the lens. Or it thinks it did. Either way it'll be back, and next time it won't knock politely."

The boy looked at her. Then he pointed at himself, and at the floor—down again, always down, under the mountain—and held up three fingers.

Three days.

"I can't take you anywhere," she said. "I'm a fraud who sells dead knots to scared women. I'm nobody. Whatever you think I am, whoever sent you—they got the wrong Villalobos."

He crossed to her then, and did the strangest thing yet. He reached up and pressed his small cold hand flat against her burning wrist, and spoke one word. One syllable. It landed in the mark like a key in a lock, and for half a heartbeat—one impossible, vertiginous half-heartbeat—Marguerite felt something on the other side of the silence. Not a voice. Not words. Just *attention*, vast and weathered and achingly familiar, like the smell of a house you haven't entered in twenty years.

Then it was gone, and the boy let go, and she was standing alone in her shop with her heart slamming and tears running down her face that she had absolutely no permission to be crying.

"Who taught you that word," she whispered.

The boy pointed down. Under the mountain. And then—he did it slowly, watching her face the whole time—he pointed at her, and drew a shape in the air with one finger. A shape she knew. A shape every child in Vell Hollow was drilled to report to the Inquisitors on sight.

The sigil of the Hollow Saint. Her mark. Her god.

"You're telling me," Marguerite said, "that my god sent you."

The boy neither nodded nor shook his head. He picked up the last piece of bread from the table, tucked it gravely into his cloak like a traveler provisioning for a journey, and waited.

Outside, far up the street, a second set of footsteps had joined the first. Two Inquisitors now. Counting doors.

Marguerite looked at the boy—at the impossible, illegal, holy horror of him—and felt fear turn over in her chest like a blade being reversed into a grip. She'd spent twenty years being careful. Careful had bought her a warm room and a full kettle and a mark that slept.

It wasn't going to save this child.

"All right," she said, and heard her own voice come out steadier than it had any right to. "Cellar. Now. Move."

And when he'd gone, quiet as breath, she took down her mother's lockbox from behind the false brick, and opened it, and looked for the first time in twenty years at the one thing she'd sworn never to sell: a scrap of vellum, older than the kingdom, bearing a single line of pre-kingdom script—the same tongue the boy spoke in his sleep.
