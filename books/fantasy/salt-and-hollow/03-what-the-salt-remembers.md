## 3. What the Salt Remembers

The city was a wound.

Oren had known it before he ever saw it—known it the way you know a fever in your own blood, from three days' walk away, when the road still ran through pine and the mountain behind him was only a gray suggestion on the horizon. The wound had a shape. It lay in the earth like a gash laid open by something careless, and everything that lived inside it bled in ways the people there could not see.

He could see.

He stood at Marguerite Villalobos's window in the hour before dawn, small hands on the sill, and watched the wound breathe. Smoke rose from ten thousand chimneys, but beneath the smoke there was another rising: thin gray threads of *taken*, drifting up out of doorways and gutters and the cracks between cobblestones, the way heat shimmers off summer stone. Nobody below looked up. Nobody below ever looked up. That was part of what had been done to them—the looking-up had been sold, or taxed, or forbidden, he did not yet know which. He would learn. He had been sent to learn.

The mountain hummed.

It hummed all night, low and patient, a note so deep that ordinary ears heard nothing of it and called the silence quiet. But Oren's bones were tuned to it the way a bell is tuned to its clapper. *Come home,* the mountain said. *Come home, come home, come home.* It was not unkind. That was the terrible thing about it. The mountain loved everything it held, the way a fist loves what it closes around.

And the lamplight—

Oren pressed his forehead against the cold glass.

In the street below, a watchman carried his lantern along the crooked lane, and around the lantern's flame moths gathered—or what any eye in Vell Hollow would have called moths. They were not moths. They were souls. Small ones, frayed ones, the leavings and offcuts, the syllables and shadows that had been shaved off the people of this city over two hundred years and never returned. They drifted toward every flame in the city because flame was the closest thing left to what they had been taken from. They circled and circled and could not enter, because they were already owned, and ownership is a wall even light cannot pass.

One of them pressed itself against the window near his cheek. It had a woman's shape if you squinted—a curl of gray fire no bigger than his thumb—and it mouthed something at him, over and over, soundless.

He knew the word. It was a name-syllable, one of the little stolen pieces. *-mira. -mira. -mira.*

Somewhere in this city was a person who had been sold down to someone named Mira, or Amira, or Samira, and did not know why certain sounds made them flinch.

"Stop that," Oren whispered to it, in the old tongue, the tongue of the deep courts, the language that had no word for *own* but eleven words for *owe*. "You'll wear yourself out."

The soul-moth trembled, as if it understood—not the words, maybe, but the fact of being spoken to, which was perhaps rarer and more precious than understanding. Then the watchman turned the corner and the moth streamed away after his lantern like everything else, and Oren let out a breath he had been holding since the mountain.

Behind him, in the bed under the eaves, Marguerite slept badly.

* * *

He had known her name before she opened the door.

That was the first strange thing, and she had felt it too—he had seen it land on her face like a thrown stone. She had asked, later, in gestures and broken fragments, how. He had pointed at her wrist, at the faded mark there, the god-brand worn nearly smooth, and she had covered it with her other hand as though he'd spat on it.

But that wasn't the whole truth, and Oren did not like carrying partial truths; they sat in the chest like swallowed river stones.

The whole truth was this: names are not secret. Names are just far away. If you have been made in the deep places, where the courts keep their ledgers, then every name written anywhere hums at a frequency you can feel through your teeth. *Marguerite Villalobos* was written in a ledger beneath the mountain, in an entry older than her grandmother, and the entry had a weight to it, and the weight had walked up out of the dark for twenty years and finally arrived at her door wearing a boy's body with no voice in it.

Because he had no voice. That was the arrangement. That was the price of the crossing. A vessel sent up must go silent, or the silence above would notice him. So they had taken his words—all of them, every word he owned—and left him the language underneath language, the old tongue, the one the courts spoke when they thought no one living remembered it.

They had thought wrong. Or they had thought exactly right, and that was the part Oren turned over in the dark hours, the part that made his stomach hurt.

*She can carry it,* the wardens of the threshold had told each other over him, believing a sleeping child could not hear. *Her line made the debt. Her mark makes the vessel. Send the boy to the mercenary. She will do what mercenaries do.*

He had not understood, then, whether that was a promise or a threat.

* * *

Morning came gray and salt-smelling. Marguerite moved through her shop below like a woman wading upstream—counting charms that would not work, dusting shelves of things that had not worked in twenty years, arranging failure so it looked like merchandise. Oren sat on the stairs where he could watch without being watched, which was a skill he had been born with, or made with; he could not remember which, and that frightened him more than anything else about himself.

He watched her wrap a charm of safe-passage in brown paper for a carter with nervous hands, and he watched the charm lie there in its paper like a dead beetle, and he watched her smile anyway. It was a good smile. It was the kind of smile that has been used so many times it has gone thin as coin rubbed smooth. Underneath it she was doing arithmetic—rent, flour, the boy now eating her bread—and the arithmetic never came out kind.

She was afraid of him. He could taste it whenever she came near, a copper tang in the air, the flavor of a person deciding daily not to run. But she fed him. She had put a blanket over him in the night when she thought he slept. And once, passing behind him on the stairs, she had touched his hair—just briefly, just the way you'd steady something fragile—and her hand had shaken afterward, and she had pretended it hadn't.

Oren decided, somewhere in that morning, between the second piece of bread and the third, that he would not be afraid of her.

It was not trust. Trust was a door you opened. This was more like standing in a doorway in a storm and noticing that the stranger inside had lit a lamp, and thinking: *well. Here is a lamp. Here is a floor. Here, at least, is not the mountain.*

The mountain hummed on. He felt it through the floorboards, through the soles of his bare feet, through the fillings of his teeth. *Come home.* And beneath the hum, fainter, another sound—the sound he had been sent to listen for, the reason a silent child was worth more to the court than a regiment of speaking ones.

A soul. A specific soul. A small blue flame kept in a lantern of white salt, in a vault behind the Thirteenth Magistrate's chair, burning past its term the way a prisoner serves a sentence that ended years ago while no one told him.

The brother. *Her* brother. Sold before she was born, by her own blood, to buy their freedom from a feud that had otherwise eaten them all. Oren knew the shape of the entry the way he knew the shape of his own hands. Debtor: Villalobos. Collateral: one soul, unborn, sex unknown, term: until paid or claimed.

No one had paid. No one had claimed. Two hundred years, and the flame still burned in its lantern, waiting for a family that had forgotten it existed.

Except the god hadn't forgotten. That was the other thing the wardens had not understood, or had understood too well. The mark on Marguerite's wrist was not decoration. It was a witness-mark. Her god had witnessed the original sale—had been summoned as witness, as gods were in the old days, bound to remember what men preferred to forget—and when the Edict of Silence fell and magic was outlawed, the god had not abandoned her. It had been *silenced*, the way you muzzle a witness. But a witness-mark remembers its business. It burned last night when Oren spoke. It would burn hotter. It was the key, and the court had sent him to the lock.

Or sent him as payment. He still could not make those two sentences sit together in his chest.

* * *

In the afternoon, the Inquisitor came.

Oren felt him half a street away—a wrongness in the air like the moment before lightning, a smell of scorched paper and clean linen. The man wore gray and walked as though the street owed him money. Where his boots struck the cobbles, the soul-moths scattered, and the lamplight itself seemed to flinch.

Marguerite saw him through the window and went white in a way that had nothing to do with the light. She moved fast then—faster than Oren had seen her move, faster than a tired woman should be able to move—and her hands closed on his shoulders and steered him down, down, through a trapdoor he hadn't known was there, into the cellar among the unsold charms.

Rows of them hung from the rafters on twine: protection knots, luck-braids, fever-washes, love-tokens, all of them dead, all of them humming very faintly with the residue of what they used to be. To Oren it was like standing inside a graveyard of small promises. Above, muffled by floorboards, he heard the knock, and Marguerite's voice climb into its merchant register, bright and bored at once, and the Inquisitor's voice like a file drawn slowly across iron.

*Strange tongues in the Hollow district,* the file-voice said. *Reports of a child.*

*A child?* Marguerite laughed, and it was a masterpiece of a laugh, and Oren understood in that moment that she had survived twenty outlawed years not despite her fear but by weaponizing it. *Half the Hollow is children, serah. Half of them strange. Take your pick.*

The footsteps crossed the floor overhead. Stopped directly above him. Oren held his breath and reached out—not with his hands, with the sense the courts had grown into him—and touched the dead charms hanging around his face. *Sleep,* he told them, in the old tongue, the way you'd hush a room of restless children. *Be only charms. Be only twine and thread and nothing.*

And the charms, grateful for any attention after twenty years of neglect, settled. When the Inquisitor passed his detection-prayer across the ceiling of the cellar, he found nothing but a merchant's stock of worthless goods, and the file-voice grew bored, and the boots left, and Marguerite came down the ladder with her knees shaking so hard Oren could hear them knock.

She knelt in front of him. Checked him over with rough, quick hands—arms, face, the back of his head—as if the Inquisitor might have gotten to him through the floorboards. Then she stopped, holding his shoulders, and looked at him, really looked, and asked him a question in Common he couldn't follow, but whose shape he knew from every world and every court:

*What are you?*

Oren reached up and pressed his palm flat against her wrist, against the god-mark, and let it answer for him. The mark warmed under his hand. Not blazing—not yet—but warm, the way a hearth is warm an hour after the fire dies, the way a dog is warm that has been waiting by a door for twenty years and has just heard, at last, footsteps it recognizes.

Marguerite made a sound low in her throat, half wonder and half grief, and pulled her wrist back and cradled it like something injured.

Above them, through the little cellar window at street level, the afternoon light was going amber, and the soul-moths were beginning their evening circling, thousands of them now, rising off the whole wounded city like sparks off a bonfire that nobody would admit was burning.

Oren watched them through the glass and made his decision the way he made all decisions: quietly, completely, without ceremony.

This stranger with the dead charms and the weaponized fear and the witness-mark on her wrist—this was his way home. Not the mountain's way, which was a fist closing. Her way, whatever it cost her, whatever it cost him. Because the mountain wanted the flame kept, and the mountain wanted *him* kept, and everything in Oren that was still his own wanted the opposite of what the mountain wanted.

He took her hand—the uninjured one, the one not cradling the mark—and pressed it flat against his own chest, over his heart, and then pointed down. Through the floor. Through the foundations. Through the salt and the stone and the long dark road under the road.

Down. Toward the mountain. Toward the lantern and the blue flame and the thirteenth turn of the moon, which was coming whether anyone willed it or not.

Marguerite stared at him. Then at the floor, as if she could see through it. Then at him again, and he watched the arithmetic behind her eyes change—watched rent and flour lose their column to something older and larger, a debt with her family's name on it that she did not yet know she had inherited.

Tomorrow, he thought, she would find the archivist. Tomorrow the ledger would open. Tonight she would stand in her shop of dead promises and look at a mute boy pointing at the floor, and she would have to choose.

Oren hoped she would choose loudly. He had been silent long enough for both of them.

Far beneath them, patient as tide, the mountain hummed its one note into the dark—and for the first time since the crossing, Oren hummed back.
