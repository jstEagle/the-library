## 4. The Archivist's Debt

The archivist lived above a tannery in the Stink Quarter, which Marguerite supposed was the point. Nobody from the Guild came to the Stink Quarter unless they were being punished.

She'd spent two days finding him. Two days of asking careful questions in careful places—a fishwife who owed her for a birthing charm that had worked by coincidence, a fence who moved banned books along with stolen silverware, a drunk former clerk who wept when she mentioned the name *Ilvane* and told her the address three times before passing out. The name still carried weight, even disgraced. Especially disgraced.

Oren rode on her hip like he'd been born there, bare feet hooked into her belt, watching the city slide past with those eyes that saw too much. He hadn't spoken since dawn. He didn't need to. When she took a wrong turn toward the river, his small hand pressed flat against her collarbone—*no*—and when she corrected course toward the tanneries, the hand relaxed. He knew where they were going better than she did. That should have frightened her more than it did.

The stair to Sefa Ilvane's rooms smelled of old urine and older paper. She climbed it with her knife loose in its sheath and knocked the way debt collectors knock: three times, hard, no apology in it.

"Closed," said a voice through the door. Dry as vellum left in sun. "Everything's closed. The archive is closed, the kingdom is closed, the door is closed. Go away."

"The door under the mountain," Marguerite said.

Silence. Long enough that she counted her own heartbeats against Oren's breathing. Then bolts—three of them, then a fourth she hadn't heard until it slid—and the door opened on a chain, revealing one eye, watery and sharp behind cracked spectacles.

"That phrase," the eye said. "Say it again."

"I haven't said anything yet."

"You said *door*. You said *under the mountain*. In twenty years, four hundred and eleven days—" he stopped himself, visibly embarrassed at having counted, "—nobody has come up those stairs saying those words together. Either you're an Inquisitor, in which case your trap is insultingly lazy, or you're something I've been waiting for." The chain rattled off. "Come in before I change my mind. Mind the floor. The floor is mostly books."

He wasn't wrong. Sefa Ilvane was a narrow man folded into a narrow room, shelves climbing every wall to a ceiling lost in shadow, stacks of volumes rising from the floorboards like coral. A single lamp burned under a tin shade. The air was dust and lamp oil and, beneath it, the ghost of ink so old it had turned sweet. Oren wriggled down from her hip the moment they were inside and stood very still, head tilted, listening to the room the way he listened to everything—as if the walls were speaking.

"They are," Sefa said, following the boy's gaze. "Most people can't hear them. Sit. No—not there, that's a first-edition cartulary, sit *there*." He cleared a chair with a sweep of his arm, sending a cascade of scrolls onto another pile. "Now. Who gave you my name?"

"A drunk clerk named Pell."

"Pell. Good. Still alive, then. That's something." Sefa lowered himself onto a stool across from her, lacing his fingers, and Marguerite got her first real look at him: sixty, maybe older, with the stooped posture of a man who'd spent decades bent over desks, ink stains to both elbows, and hands that trembled slightly until he noticed them trembling and made them stop. His robe had once been royal blue. It was now the color of surrender. "You're the charm-seller. Villalobos. Hollow district. Your work is garbage, incidentally—the salt-lattice in your protection charms is inverted, any Guild assayer could tell you in a moment, which suggests you learned from someone who learned from someone who never understood what they were copying."

"My charms sell fine."

"Because this city wants to believe in dead things. Yes." He waved this away. "None of that matters. What matters is why you're here, and you're here because of the boy."

Marguerite's hand drifted to her knife. Sefa watched the motion with clinical interest, the way a man watches weather.

"If I wanted you robbed or reported," he said, "I'd have let you freeze on the stairs while I fetched the neighbors. Ask your questions, Villalobos. I've waited twenty years for someone to ask them. I'd hate to rush it now."

So she asked. She told him about the boy at her door, barefoot in the frost, speaking a language that made no sense and perfect sense at once. She told him about the mark on her wrist burning for the first time since the Edict—burning when Oren spoke, cooling when he slept. She told him about the Inquisitor on her street, the cellar full of unsold charms, the way the boy had looked at her lamplight like it was a cage with moths in it.

And she sang back the phrase. She'd turned it over in her head for two days until the sounds sat right, the rising lilt, the click at the back of the throat, the word that fell off the tongue like a stone down a well. She sang it the way Oren had sung it, standing in her kitchen with frost melting off his hair.

Sefa Ilvane went white.

Not pale—white, the color draining out of his face like water out of a cracked bowl, and his trembling hands came up and gripped the edge of his stool as though the room had tilted.

"Say it again."

She said it again.

"*Thirteenth turn closes the door.*" He translated it flatly, without ceremony, the way a physician delivers a diagnosis. "That's not Old Court. That's not even pre-kingdom proper. That's the deep dialect—the under-tongue, the speech of the court beneath the mountain. It hasn't been spoken above ground in living memory. It hasn't been *permitted* to be spoken." He pushed his spectacles up his nose with one finger. "Where did you learn it?"

"He taught me. By repeating it until I got it right. He can't speak Common. Not won't—*can't*. There's nothing there to speak it with."

"Nothing there," Sefa repeated softly. He looked at Oren, who was crouched by a shelf, running his fingers along book spines without pulling any out, gentle as someone touching sleeping animals. Something moved across the archivist's face—recognition, or grief, or the particular horror of a scholar meeting a text he'd only ever read about. "Boy. Can you understand me?"

Oren looked up. Nodded once.

"But you can't answer."

A shake of the head. Then, in the under-tongue, a short phrase—two words, falling cadence.

"What did he say?" Marguerite asked.

"'Only below.'" Sefa's voice had gone very quiet. "'Only below.' Meaning the rest of his voice is somewhere else. Meaning—" He stopped. Took off his spectacles, polished them on his sleeve with unnecessary thoroughness, put them back on. When he spoke again, the dryness was back, but it was a wall now, and Marguerite could hear the thing pacing behind it. "Meaning I need to show you something. And once I show you, neither of us gets to unsee it. So I'm asking plainly, mercenary: do you want to know? Because knowing has a weight, and you'll carry it the rest of your life, and I've found most people would rather be light and ignorant than heavy and doomed."

"My whole trade," Marguerite said, "is selling people things they think will make them safe. I know exactly how much ignorance weighs. Show me."

Something flickered in his face—approval, maybe, or pity. He rose, joints cracking like green firewood, and went to the far wall, where a shelf stood slightly proud of the others. He pulled it open on hinges she hadn't seen. Behind it: a cavity in the plaster, and inside the cavity, wrapped in oilcloth, a ledger.

It was old. Old the way the city was old, old the way the mountain was old—bound in hide gone dark and stiff, corners worn round, a brass clasp green with age. Sefa unwrapped it on his desk with the tenderness of a man uncovering a wound, and Marguerite leaned in and read the title stamped into the cover in letters that predated the kingdom's alphabet:

"What does it say?"

"*Debts of the Descent.*" Sefa's fingers hovered over the clasp. "One of seven. This is the only one that survived the burning. I copied it before they burned the original—I copied all of them, every page, every entry, over eleven years, in my own hand, hidden in the margins of legitimate work. That's why they exiled me. That's why they didn't kill me, because killing an archivist makes martyrs and martyrs get remembered, and remembering is the one thing the Edict cannot survive." He opened the clasp. "I have been waiting twenty years for a reason to show this to someone. I confess I always imagined the reason would be braver than a charm-seller with inverted salt-lattices."

"Disappointment builds character."

"So I'm told." He turned pages. The handwriting inside shifted across centuries—different hands, different inks, some entries in the pre-kingdom script, some in old Common, one entire section in cipher that Sefa passed without slowing, clearly already broken. "The court beneath the mountain predates Vell Hollow. Predates the kingdom. Before there was law above ground, there was law below, and the below-law is contract. Absolute. Binding. Older than gods, some say—or maybe gods are just its best customers. Everything down there runs on terms. Payment. Exchange. You want something from the under-court, you sign. And the under-court *always* collects."

Villalobos.

Her own name stopped her cold. There, mid-page, in a hand from three hundred years ago: *Villalobos*, entered twice, once crossed out with a line so violent it had torn the paper, once written fresh beside it. Below it, columns of figures in a notation she didn't recognize, and a phrase in old Common that her eyes snagged on like cloth on a nail:

*Soul rendered in payment. Term: perpetual.*

"Sefa." Her voice came out strange. Flat. "What am I looking at?"

He didn't answer immediately. He was watching her instead, the way she'd seen physicians watch a patient they were about to hurt on purpose because the alternative was worse.

"Your family bought their lives," he said. "Three hundred years ago, give or take a decade—the dating is contested. There was a blood-feud. Do you know what a true blood-feud is, Villalobos? Not a quarrel. A *feud*. Two families bound to destroy each other, generation after generation, until one line ends. The old families of the Hollow were entangled in a dozen of them. Most ended the usual way—with everyone dead. Yours ended differently. Someone in your line went below and made a purchase. The under-court extinguished the feud—unmade it, unraveled the obligation out of every living member of both families, rewove them free. And in exchange—"

"In exchange," Marguerite said, because her mouth was moving ahead of her now, "they took a soul."

"They took a soul." Sefa nodded slowly. "Rendered in payment. Perpetual term. And here's the part that kept me awake for years, the part I copied four times to be sure I wasn't misreading the notation." His finger moved down the column of figures to a marginal note, small, cramped, in the same ancient hand. "The soul wasn't taken from a living member of the family. It was taken *before* life. Rendered *in utero*. An unborn child of the Villalobos line—promised before birth, collected at the threshold. The under-court doesn't distinguish between a soul that exists and a soul that will exist. To them, a debt is a debt regardless of which side of existence it sits on."

The room got very quiet. Even the dust seemed to stop moving.

Marguerite looked at the boy. Oren had come to stand at her knee without her noticing, drawn by something in the ledger's presence, or in her silence. He was looking at the page—at the name, at the notation—and his expression held none of a child's confusion. It held recognition. It held the weary patience of someone confirming what he had walked a long way to confirm.

"There was a story," she heard herself say. "My grandmother told it. My mother refused to. That the Villalobos line was cursed to have one child too many and one child too few—that every generation, a child was owed. We thought it was just a story. The kind you tell to explain why the family was unlucky."

"It isn't a curse," Sefa said gently. "It's arithmetic. The under-court holds a Villalobos soul, and the family line bends around the absence of it, generation after generation, like a river bending around a stone. Every Villalobos born since has been born in the shape of that missing child. Including, I'd wager—" he looked at her wrist, at the god-mark hidden under her sleeve, "—including you."

Her mark was burning.

Not the warm flicker it gave when Oren spoke. This was deeper—a slow coal-glow under the skin, the old oath-sign waking after twenty years of silence, and Marguerite realized with a lurch that it had been burning since Sefa opened the ledger. Since the name. Her god had gone silent the day of the Edict; every sworn mercenary in the city had felt the connection sever like a cut rope. But apparently silent was not the same as absent. Apparently something down in that mark had been waiting, all these years, for someone to say the name *Villalobos* next to the words *soul rendered in payment*.

"Sefa," she said. "The witness clause. On contracts this old—is there a witness clause?"

His eyebrows climbed. "There is. Standard in descent-contracts. The under-court requires a witnessing power—one entity that perceives the terms and is bound to remember them, so that no party can later claim ignorance. For the old family contracts, the witnesses were usually..." He paused. Recalculated. Looked at her wrist again, and this time he understood, and she watched understanding land on him like a dropped anvil. "Gods," he whispered. "The sworn gods of the old families. They witnessed the contracts. Which means when the Edict silenced them—"

"It didn't silence them," Marguerite said. "It silenced *us*. It cut the line between the sworn and the swearing. But the god remembers. The god has always remembered. It's been holding the terms of my family's contract for three hundred years, and it couldn't say a word about it, because nobody asked."

Oren touched her wrist. Small fingers, deliberate, resting exactly on the mark, and the burn steadied into something almost like comfort. He looked up at her and said one word in the under-tongue. One word, soft, and Sefa translated it before she could ask, his voice barely working:

"*Sister.*"

The floor did not move. The walls did not fall. Marguerite stood in a dusty room above a tannery in the Stink Quarter and felt absolutely nothing move anywhere in the world except inside her chest, where twenty years of mercenary pragmatism was coming apart at the seams around a hole she'd never known had edges. A brother. Sold before she was born. Carried home to her door by a mute child who had known her name before she gave it—who had *always* known her name, because it was written in the same ledger, in the same hand, three hundred years deep.

"The thirteenth turn closes the door," she said. Her voice was steady. She was almost proud of that. "What door, Sefa? What happens on the thirteenth turn?"

Sefa closed the ledger with great care, as though shutting a coffin.

"On the thirteenth turn of the moon, the under-court settles its accounts. All of them. Perpetual terms become permanent—held forever, beyond appeal, beyond witness, beyond even memory. Anything still held below after the thirteenth turn is held *eternally*, Villalobos. The moon is twelve turns from full cycle tonight." He met her eyes. "You have thirteen days to decide whether you're a woman who sells broken charms, or a woman who breaks into hell to collect a family debt."

Outside, faint through the shuttered window, a bell began to ring. Guild bells. Three strokes, pause, three strokes—the signal for an active inquiry in the district.

Oren's hand tightened on her wrist, and the mark flared like a struck match.

They were already looking for the boy. Now they'd be looking for her too.

* * *

Marguerite counted exits while Sefa blew out the lamp. Back stairs behind the shelf-wall—he'd built them himself, he admitted, the way other men built panic rooms out of gold and he'd built his out of footnotes. Window over the curing yards, drop of fifteen feet, survivable. Front stair, unusable, unless she wanted to introduce herself to whatever the Guild bell had summoned.

"Through the back," she said. "How fast can you walk?"

"I'm sixty-one years old and I haven't run since the Regency. How fast do you need?"

"Faster than that."

He wrapped the ledger in its oilcloth and pressed it into her arms—her arms, not his—and Marguerite understood that she had just become, whether she liked it or not, the courier of three hundred years of buried law. Oren took the hem of her coat in one fist. Sefa took a walking stick from beside the door and a satchel that clinked suspiciously like contraband.

"For the record," the archivist said, opening the hidden panel onto darkness and the smell of old lime, "I want it noted that I warned you knowing has a weight."

"Noted," Marguerite said, and stepped through after her brother.
