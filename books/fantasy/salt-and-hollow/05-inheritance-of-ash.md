## 5. Inheritance of Ash

Sefa Ilvane's house was not a house. It was a book that had learned to stand up.

Marguerite understood this the moment she crossed his threshold, ducking under a lintel strung with drying ink-skins, and found herself in a room where every surface—walls, ceiling, the backs of doors, the underside of a slanted table—was covered in writing. Not decoration. Not art. Text, in a dozen hands and half a dozen alphabets, some of it so old the letters had gone brown as dried blood. The air smelled of tallow, dust, and vinegar, and beneath all of it something mineral, like the inside of a cave.

"You're the charm-seller." Sefa did not look up from the desk wedged into the room's only corner. He was a narrow man, gray at the temples, with ink stains climbing both forearms to the elbow as if he'd waded into a flood of it years ago and never fully drained. "Villalobos. Hollow district. You've been on my list for six years."

"I don't want to know what kind of list."

"Neither would you enjoy it." He set down his pen and turned, and Marguerite saw that his eyes were the pale, washed-out color of paper left too long in the sun. Archivist's eyes. Eyes ruined by lamplight and small print. "You brought the child."

"He's outside. With Ysolde." She'd left them at the mouth of Sallow Row, under the broken lantern where the moon-priestess kept her vigil like a woman guarding a door no one else could see. Oren had gripped her sleeve when she moved to go alone—not pulling her back, just holding on—and she had pried his fingers loose one by one and told him, in the flat tone she used for buyers who tried to haggle, *I come back. That's the whole trick of me.*

He hadn't liked it. But he'd let go.

"Sit," Sefa said. There was exactly one chair, and it was buried under a drift of ledgers. He cleared it with a sweep of his arm that sent three of them to the floor, and Marguerite had the distinct impression he knew which three, and would find them later by sound.

She sat. The chair was hard as penance.

"You translated something for the priestess," Marguerite said. "A phrase. *The thirteenth turn closes the door.*"

"I translated more than that." He pulled a ledger toward him across the desk—an actual ledger, bound in gray leather gone soft as cloth, its corners rounded by centuries of hands. "But before I show you what, I need to ask you a question, and I need you not to lie to me, because I'll know. Lying leaves marks in a room like this."

Marguerite said nothing. Waiting was free.

"Do you know why your family name is on a debt ledger?" He laid the gray book open between them. "Do you know what your people sold?"

"My people were mercenaries." Her voice came out level. Twenty years of practice, level. "Sworn blades. Before the Edict, Villalobos contracts are all over the guild-halls of three cities. We fought for coin and kept our oaths. That's the whole inheritance. That and a mark on the wrist nobody can explain anymore."

"The mark is part of it." Sefa turned the ledger around so she could read it. His finger came down on a line of script so old it predated the kingdom's alphabet—a column of characters that looked less written than carved, each one a small act of violence against the page. Beside it, in a later hand, someone had added a gloss in Old Common: *Villalobos. Paid in full. Witnessed.*

"Witnessed by whom?" Marguerite asked.

"That's my question for you." Sefa leaned back. "Your god. The one who went silent. What was his name?"

And there it was—the thing she had not said aloud in twenty years, the name she kept the way you keep a knife you no longer trust yourself to hold. She almost didn't say it. Then she thought of Oren in the cellar among the dead charms, mouthing words older than kingdoms while her wrist burned like a brand fresh from the fire, and she said it.

"Ashen-Vow."

Something happened to Sefa's face. It was very small and very fast, and if she hadn't spent twenty years reading buyers across a market stall she'd have missed it entirely: a flinch, quickly converted into stillness. The look of a man who has just confirmed a number he'd been dreading.

"Say it again," he said quietly.

"Ashen-Vow. God of sworn blades. God of—" She stopped. Because the rest of the litany was rising in her throat unbidden, in the cadence her mother had used, kneeling, when Marguerite was small enough to kneel beside her: *God of the debt remembered. God of the account kept open. God who closes no book.* She had thought, as a child, that these were just titles, the way gods collected titles the way magpies collected tin. Now, watching Sefa's face, she felt the titles rearrange themselves into something else. A job description.

"Debt remembered," Sefa repeated. "Account kept open." He tapped the ledger. "Marguerite Villalobos. Do you understand what you've been praying to your entire life? Your family's god isn't a war god. War is just how he collects. He is a *creditor*. The oldest kind. And twenty years ago, when the Edict passed and every sworn mercenary in Vell Hollow chose survival over faith—" his washed-out eyes held hers, merciless in their mildness— "he didn't go silent. He stopped *reminding* you. There's a difference. A creditor who stops reminding you of a debt hasn't forgiven it. He's waiting for the court to reopen."

The room seemed to tilt a degree. Marguerite put her hand flat on the desk to steady herself and felt, absurdly, the grain of wood that had probably been felled before her grandmother's grandmother drew breath.

"What debt," she said. "Tell me the debt."

* * *

Sefa told it standing up, pacing the narrow aisle between his wall of text and his wall of text, hands moving as though he were conducting the story rather than telling it, and Marguerite sat in the hard chair and let it happen to her.

"Two hundred and thirty-one years ago," he began, "the Villalobos line wasn't a mercenary line. They were salt-farmers. Out past the eastern flats, where the mountain's runoff poisons the ground and nothing grows but the white crop. Poor, clannish, and cursed—which is to say, they were locked in a blood-feud with another salt-family, the Corvane, that had run four generations. Four generations of tit-for-tat killings. Every wedding a funeral waiting for its turn. You know how feuds work. They don't end. They pause."

"And then?"

"And then a girl of the Villalobos line got clever, or desperate—I've never been able to decide which, and the record doesn't say. She went where you don't go. Under the mountain. To the court beneath it, the one your child speaks the language of. And she made a contract." Sefa stopped pacing. "The Thirteenth Magistrate—that's what the under-court calls its sovereign; there were twelve courts above and this one below, the thirteenth, the one that keeps what others discard—the Magistrate agreed to end the feud. To make the Corvane forget their grievance. To buy the Villalobos line out of the killing, permanently, in exchange for payment."

"What payment? Coin?"

"Soul-crop." Sefa's voice had gone very dry. "That's the term in the original. The under-court doesn't take coin. Coin is just promises wearing armor. It takes what coin stands in for. And the contract specified the payment schedule with a precision that should tell you everything about what we're dealing with: one soul per generation, remitted on the birth of each third child, until the principal is discharged."

Marguerite felt cold start at the base of her spine and climb.

"One soul per generation," she repeated.

"Remitted on the birth of each third child." Sefa picked up the ledger again, gently now, the way you handle a thing that has already hurt someone. "The contract ran ninety years. Three payments. The first two were adults—Corvane men, actually, taken as part of the settlement; the Magistrate threw those in as a bonus, which should have been the first warning that the bargain was rotten at the core. The third payment fell due in the year the contract closed. And the record of that final payment reads—" he turned the ledger toward her one last time, and read aloud, translating as he went, his voice flattening the old tongue into Common the way you flatten a flower in a book— "*Final remittance received: one soul, unborn, of the debtor line. Taken prior to breath. Held in perpetuity or until claimed by blood.*"

The silence afterward had texture. It pressed on her eardrums.

"Unborn," Marguerite said.

"Your mother's mother was carrying her third child," Sefa said. "A boy, by the midwife's record. The contract took him before he ever drew breath. And the Villalobos line bought its freedom with him, and forgot him, because forgetting is what the under-court sells along with everything else. Except—" he raised one ink-stained finger— "one party to the contract refused to forget. One witness signed the ledger and never let the account close. Guess."

Marguerite's wrist was burning. Not aching—burning, the way it had burned the night Oren stood in her doorway speaking the deep tongue, and she looked down at the mark she'd worn for forty years without understanding it, the coiled blade-and-knot sigil that every Villalobos carried and none could explain, and she finally, finally saw it for what it was.

Not a blessing.

A signature.

"He witnessed it," she said. Her voice sounded like someone else's. "Ashen-Vow witnessed the contract. And when the Edict came and magic went silent in Vell Hollow, he couldn't remind us anymore. Couldn't collect. Couldn't even—" she laughed, and it came out wrong, cracked down the middle— "twenty years I thought he'd abandoned us. Twenty years I sold charms off his dead name and called myself faithful because I hadn't stopped *saying* the prayers, even though nothing answered. And the whole time he was holding a place in line. Keeping the seat. Waiting for someone from the blood to walk back into the court and claim what was taken."

"Claimed by blood," Sefa quoted softly. "It's in the release clause. The soul was held *in perpetuity or until claimed by blood*—and here's the part that matters, Marguerite, the part I've spent twenty years in exile making sure I could recite: the contract specifies no time limit on the claim. None. The Magistrate's own law binds it to honor a blood-claim whenever it comes. Which means your brother has been sitting in a lantern under the mountain for two hundred years, paid, delivered, receipted—*paid in full*, the ledger says it right there—and the door is about to close on him forever anyway, because the thirteenth turn ends the court's obligation period and everything held reverts to the Magistrate absolutely. After that night, no claim stands. Not blood. Not god. Nothing."

Marguerite stood up. She didn't remember deciding to. Her legs simply informed her.

"I never met him," she said. It came out accusatory, aimed at the room, at the ledger, at two hundred years of salt and silence. "I never met him and I'm grieving him, which is the stupidest thing I've ever done, and I have done professionally stupid things for coin most of my life. He's a line in a book. He's a midwife's note. He never breathed, Sefa. What am I supposed to do with grief for someone who never got to be a person?"

Sefa considered her for a long moment. Somewhere in the walls of text around them, the house settled, pages shifting like the building was turning over in its sleep.

"You're asking the wrong question," he said. "You keep asking what he was. A soul that never breathed. Ask instead what the Magistrate thinks he is—ask why an entity that deals in souls by the barrel, that takes adults and kings and whole villages' worth of names, wrote a special clause for one unborn child. *Held in perpetuity or until claimed by blood.* The Magistrate doesn't write sentimental clauses. It writes binding ones. Which means either your brother's soul is worth more than the ledger admits—or the Magistrate has been keeping him the way a miser keeps the one coin he can't spend. Either way, Marguerite, you're not going down there to retrieve a memory. You're going down to take something the oldest law in the world says is yours, from a creature that has had two hundred years to convince itself otherwise."

Marguerite looked at her wrist. The mark had stopped burning. Now it was warm—steadily, patiently warm, like a hand resting there, like a creditor who has waited two hundred and thirty-one years and can wait a little longer, but not much, and is letting you feel the weight of every day of it.

Purpose arrived in her the way weather arrives: not a decision but a pressure change. She had spent twenty years selling protection that didn't protect, faith that didn't answer, a life measured out in small dishonesties to strangers. And underneath all of it, it turned out, there had been a debt the whole time—hers, her family's, owed not to the under-court but to a boy who never got to be born into it. She thought of Oren in her cellar, sleeping among the unsold charms, speaking a language her blood had helped bury.

"I need to know one more thing," she said. "The child. Oren. Why does he speak the deep tongue? Why did he come to *my* door?"

Sefa's face did the small fast thing again. The flinch converted to stillness.

"Ah," he said. "That. Yes." He began moving through his ledgers, pulling volumes, stacking them with the speed of a man who has rehearsed this moment in his head for twenty years and hoped never to perform it. "Before I answer that, you should understand that everything I'm about to show you comes from copies I made of forbidden records, which is why I live in a book instead of a house, and why the Guild wants my head. And you should also understand that the answer involves a second ledger. A newer one. One that names your family again—"

He stopped. Looked up at her. Outside, far off across the rooftops, a Guild bell began to ring the hour, and beneath its bronze voice Marguerite heard—or imagined she heard, or perhaps her wrist heard for her—the mountain, humming.

"—names your family again," Sefa finished quietly, "as *vessel-line*."

The word landed in the room and did not leave it.

"Get Ysolde," Marguerite said. "And bring the boy inside. Whatever that word means—we're going to need all of us awake to hear it."
