## 6. The Moon-Priestess

Ysolde had counted four thousand three hundred and twelve nights from her rooftop, and she had not missed one.

She kept the tally the old way: a bowl of river stones beside her mat, one stone moved at moonrise from the full bowl to the empty one, and back again at dawn. Two bowls. One motion. The whole liturgy of a dead order compressed into something small enough to hide under a sleeping mat when the Inquisitors came through the Weaver's Stair with their lanterns and their lists. The Edict of Silence had outlawed the speaking of prayers; it had not yet found a way to outlaw arithmetic.

Tonight was the twelfth turn's waning, and she moved her stone with hands that did not quite shake.

Below her, Vell Hollow went about its business of forgetting. Lamplighters worked the lower streets. A fish-cart argued with a drunk. Somewhere a dog sang to nothing. Twenty years since the Order of the Veiled Moon had been dissolved by decree, its shrines bricked over, its vestments burned in the Saltmarket while the city stood and watched and mostly enjoyed the fire. Ysolde had been thirty-one then. She was fifty-three now, and her knees hated the rooftop stairs, and she climbed them anyway, every evening, because someone had to keep count.

That was the whole of the last priestess's office now. Counting. Watching the moon do its slow wheel overhead like a coin rolling toward the lip of a table.

And tonight — tonight the coin was nearly over the edge.

She sat up on her mat and looked at the sky for a long time before she let herself think the thought that had been circling her all day like a gull over offal.

*The thirteenth turn closes the door.*

She hadn't heard those words in twenty years either. She'd heard them last from Mother Casilde, on the night before the Edict passed, when the old woman had gripped Ysolde's wrist hard enough to bruise and said: *When the moon makes her thirteenth turn, girl, everything we owe comes due at once. Count the turns. Promise me you'll count.*

Ysolde had promised. She had assumed, in the arrogance of the young, that the promise would matter for a season, maybe a year. She had not understood she was signing on for a vigil without end, a sentry post at the top of a dying world, watching for an army that never came.

Until, perhaps, tonight.

Because tonight there were footsteps on her stairs.

* * *

No one climbed to Ysolde's roof. That was the point of it. The tenement below had been condemned twice and re-condemned a third time for good measure; the stairwell smelled of mildew and rat leavings, and the door at the bottom had been nailed shut by the Guild wardens eight years ago. Ysolde came and went by the drainpipe and the neighboring roofs, a thin gray woman of no interest to anyone, and in twenty years exactly three people had made the climb: a Guild assessor who took one look at the rot and left, a cat, and now — judging by the careful, uneven rhythm of the steps — a man carrying too much weight in a satchel, a boy moving light as spilled water, and a woman whose boots hit the treads like she resented each one personally.

Ysolde rose and put her back against the little shrine wall, where the moon-sigil was carved deep enough to feel under her palm. She had no weapon worth the name. She had never needed one. The moon had always been her weapon, and the moon, these days, was only light.

They came up out of the stairwell hatch in single file: first the mercenary — Ysolde knew her by sight, everyone in the Hollow district knew Marguerite Villalobos by sight, the charm-seller with the dead eyes and the god-mark she pretended wasn't there — then the archivist Sefa Ilvane, looking worse than rumor said, his coat pockets bulging with papers he clutched like a drowning man clutches driftwood, and last the boy.

The boy stopped at the edge of the hatch and looked up.

Not at the roof. Not at the mercenary or the archivist or the crone standing against the wall. At the sky. At the waning moon hanging over the mountain's shoulder like a nail pulled halfway out of a board.

And the boy smiled.

Ysolde felt it go through her like cold water down a dry well: recognition, sudden and total, the way you know your own name in the dark. The child was looking at the moon the way she looked at the moon. The way the whole Order had once looked at the moon — not as a lamp, not as a calendar, but as a face. A face turned toward you, patient, waiting to see what you would do about what you owed.

"Close the hatch," Ysolde said. Her voice came out rusty. She cleared her throat. "There's a bar behind you. Use it."

Marguerite Villalobos didn't move. She stood with her hand resting on the boy's shoulder — lightly, Ysolde noted, the way you touch something you're afraid of breaking or afraid will break *you* — and she looked around the rooftop with a soldier's inventory: the mat, the two stone bowls, the low shrine wall with its worn sigil, the wind-bent little tree growing out of a cracked cistern, the bundles of dried herbs hanging under the eave.

"You're her," Marguerite said. "The moon-priestess. Sefa said—"

"Sefa Ilvane," Ysolde said, "was expelled from the Royal Archive for theft, falsification, and consorting with proscribed orders, and if he told you I was up here, then he has decided to spend whatever shreds of his reputation remain. Which tells me how desperate you are." She looked at the archivist. "Hello, thief."

"Lady Ysolde." He bowed, actually bowed, paper rustling. "I'm sorry to bring trouble to your door."

"I don't have a door. I have a drainpipe and a view." She let that sit, then jerked her chin at the boy. "Who is he?"

"That's rather the question," Sefa began.

"The question," Marguerite said flatly, "is whether you can help us. We're told you read the moon-road. We're told you've been counting."

Something in Ysolde's chest turned over. *We're told you've been counting.* Twenty years of counting, alone, in the dark, with nobody in the world aware the count mattered — and here was a stranger saying it aloud as if it were a known thing, a fact of the city, a service rendered.

"How many turns?" Ysolde asked.

Marguerite blinked. "What?"

"How many turns until the thirteenth? Don't answer. You don't know. Nobody knows. That's why they come to me." She held up one finger. "One. The thirteenth turn begins tomorrow at dusk and completes in fourteen days. After that—" she tapped the shrine wall, the worn sigil, "—the door beneath the mountain shuts, and whatever is owed stays owed forever. So. Now you know. Was the climb worth it?"

For the first time, the mercenary looked uncertain. It made her younger, Ysolde thought. It made her almost pitiable. Almost.

"We need to go down," Marguerite said. "Into the court. Before the turn completes. And we need a road."

* * *

They sat around the shrine wall because there was nowhere else to sit, and Ysolde brewed tea on her little brazier — nettle and yarrow, no sugar, the tea of people who had stopped expecting comfort — and listened to the whole shape of it laid out in pieces. The boy and his impossible tongue. The ledger and the Villalobos debt. The soul in the lantern. The deadline.

Sefa talked most of it, in the breathless manner of a man who had spent twenty years rehearsing a confession to an empty room. Ysolde watched him more than she watched the others. She remembered him, distantly — a young archivist with ink-stained cuffs who had attended two of the Order's last secret rites before the Edict, taking notes he should not have been taking, asking questions no one else thought to ask. *Why does the moon make thirteen turns? Why does every other calendar stop at twelve?* She had liked him then. She had assumed him dead or broken long since. Apparently merely exiled.

"And so we came to you," Sefa finished, "because the moon-road is the only approach the Guild doesn't watch, and you are the only person left who can read it."

"The moon-road isn't a road," Ysolde said.

"It's a road," the boy said.

Everyone stopped. The boy had not spoken Common before — this much Ysolde gathered from the way Marguerite's head snapped around, from the archivist's open mouth. But the word came out clear, accented strangely, round at the edges, like a coin from a foreign mint.

"It's a road," Oren said again, pointing up, past the eave, past the mountain, at the moon. Then he pointed down, between his own bare feet, at the roof boards. And then he drew one line in the air, straight through the middle of everything, from sky to stone.

Ysolde set down her cup very carefully.

"He's right," she said. "It's both. That's the part the Order understood and the Inquisitors never did. The moon-road isn't a path you walk. It's a path the moon walks — and everything under its light is walking it too. There are places where the two roads touch. Thresholds. Doorways. The court beneath the mountain sits on the deepest one." She looked at the boy. "Where did you learn that?"

Oren considered her with those still, unchildlike eyes, and answered in the old tongue — a short phrase, liquid and formal.

"He says," Sefa translated slowly, "that the moon taught him. Or—" he frowned, listening to something after the fact, "—or that he learned it *from* the moon. The grammar's ambiguous. It might also mean he learned it *as* the moon."

"Wonderful," Marguerite said. "A child who may or may not be the moon. Anything else?"

But Ysolde wasn't laughing. She was looking at the boy's shadow, thrown long and thin across her rooftop by the brazier-light, and noticing — the way she had been trained to notice, in another life, in a living order — that his shadow fell a half-inch wrong. As if the light source were slightly elsewhere than the fire. As if something above were also casting him.

She said nothing about it. Some knowledge was a burden you checked for weight before handing it to strangers.

Instead she said: "Before I agree to anything, you should understand what you're asking me to join. And you should understand what the under-court is."

"We know what it is," Marguerite said. "It's a debtors' prison with a crown."

"It's a *court*," Ysolde said. "Which is worse. Prisons take your body. Courts take your case — and they always, always find a way to be right." She fed a twist of dried herb to the brazier and watched the smoke rise straight up in the windless dark. "I served the lunar order for nineteen years before the Edict. In that time I officiated at forty-one contracts between mortals and the under-court. Marriage settlements. Boundary pacts. Three soul-pledges, though those were supposed to be legend even then. Do you know what every single contract had in common?"

"No one reads the fine print," Marguerite said.

"No one *can*," Ysolde corrected. "The fine print is written in the pre-kingdom tongue, and the pre-kingdom tongue doesn't work like ours. Our language says things. Theirs *does* things. When the court writes a price into a contract, the price isn't a clause. It's a hook. It waits. And it never takes what you expect, because what you expect is the one thing the contract cannot take — expectation is yours, free and clear, guaranteed by older law than theirs. So it takes the thing beside the thing. The thing you didn't guard because you didn't know it could be guarded." She looked around the circle: the mercenary, the archivist, the child. "You want to steal a soul out of that place. Fine. Souls get stolen out of it — rarely, but it happens; the court respects audacity, in its way. But I am telling you now, as the last priestess of an order that spent nine hundred years studying these bargains: the court will demand a price, and the price will not be what any of you expect, and you will not see it coming until it is already paid."

Silence, except the brazier ticking.

"Then why help us?" Marguerite asked. It wasn't hostile. It was genuinely curious, which was somehow worse.

Ysolde looked at her for a moment. Then she got up, stiff-kneed, and crossed to the corner where her spare robe hung under oilcloth, and brought it back, and unfolded it in her lap: gray wool, silver-threaded at the hem, the veiled-moon sigil embroidered at the breast by her own hand twenty years ago, the night before the burning.

"Because I have counted four thousand three hundred and twelve nights," she said, "and every night I asked myself the same question: counted for whom? An order needs someone to serve. A vigil needs something to watch for. I kept the count because Mother Casilde made me promise, and because stopping would mean admitting it was all for nothing." Her thumb moved over the embroidery. "And then tonight a barefoot boy climbs my stairs, looks at my moon like an old friend, and tells me the thirteenth turn is real and the door is closing and there is something to be done about it. Forgive me if I don't ask twice."

* * *

They stayed till the small hours. Ysolde spread out her charts — actual charts, drawn on treated hide, hidden inside a false bottom of her stone chest, the last cartographic work of the lunar order — and showed them what she meant by the moon-road.

"Here." Her finger traced the mountain's western flank. "The Guild watches the mine-mouths. They've watched them for twenty years, badly, because watching is boring and Inquisitors are promoted for zeal, not patience. But the road the court itself uses doesn't come out of any mine. It follows the moon's reflection. On the night of a turn's completion, the moonlight strikes the reservoir above the old aqueduct and runs downhill along the waterline — a road of light lying on top of a road of water, lying on top of the true road, which is underneath both. We called it the Silver Stair. You walk it in the hour when the moon is directly overhead and the water is still. Step off the lightline and you step off the road, and the mountain decides where you end up."

"And the next turn completes in fourteen days," Sefa said, writing fast. "So we descend on the night of the thirteenth. The same night the door shuts."

"The same night the door shuts," Ysolde agreed. "Which is either the worst possible timing or the only possible timing. The court keeps its appointments. On the night of a closing, every gate stands open for the traffic going *in*. Traffic going out—" she shrugged, "—that's what contracts are for."

Marguerite stared at the chart. Ysolde watched her look at it — really look, the way soldiers look at terrain, measuring slopes and sightlines and places to die — and saw the exact moment the mercenary stopped evaluating and started committing. It happened in the shoulders first. Then the jaw.

"All right," Marguerite said. "Fourteen days. We'll need supplies, a decoy plan for the Guild patrols, and—" 

"Rest," Ysolde said. "All of you. You can't fight a court on no sleep. There's room under the eave, and the roof has held for twenty years; it'll hold one more night."

The archivist was already asleep, sitting up, papers fanned across his chest like fallen leaves. The boy curled against the shrine wall without being told, in the exact posture — Ysolde's breath caught — the exact posture novices used for moon-watching, spine straight, palms open, face turned up. Asleep, he looked like a child. Almost.

Marguerite didn't sleep. She stood at the roof's edge with her arms folded, watching the dark bulk of the mountain, and Ysolde came and stood beside her because someone had once stood beside her, on her first vigil, and said nothing, and it had been the kindest thing anyone ever did.

"You've been alone up here a long time," Marguerite said finally.

"Twenty years."

"Why?"

Ysolde considered the question honestly, because the woman had earned honesty. "Because someone had to remember how," she said. "Not the rites — those are just words, and words survive in books, and books survive in thieves' satchels. How to *wait*. Waiting is a discipline. Everyone thinks faith is the loud part — the prayer, the rite, the miracle. Faith is the quiet part. Getting up. Climbing the stairs. Moving the stone. Doing it again." She nodded at the mountain. "Whatever's under there has been waiting longer than I have. I intend to arrive better rested."

Marguerite huffed — almost a laugh. "My god went silent on me twenty years ago too. Different reasons. Same math." She was quiet a moment. "Does it get easier? The waiting?"

"No," Ysolde said. "It gets lighter. There's a difference."

Below them, the city slept its lawbound sleep. Above them, the moon slid westward, one night closer to the edge of the table.

And it was then, in the dead middle of that shared silence, that Ysolde felt it — faint, unmistakable, a vibration in the soles of her feet and the roots of her teeth, the same feeling she'd had once as a novice with her palm pressed to the great bell of the drowned chapel:

The mountain hummed.

Not wind. Not settling stone. A hum with *pitch*, low and patient, rising and falling like breathing, like a choir holding one note for centuries. She glanced sideways and saw that the boy had opened his eyes. He was looking at her across the rooftop, awake, calm, entirely unsurprised.

He pressed one finger to his lips.

Then he pointed — not at the mountain, not at the moon, but at Marguerite's wrist, where the god-mark lay under its wrappings, and where, Ysolde realized with a chill that had nothing to do with the night air, a faint light was beginning to show through the cloth.
