## 7. Thirteen Turns

The bounty notice went up on the Guild board at dawn, and by the time the fish carts rolled through Hollow district, every charm-seller in Vell Hollow had read it twice.

Marguerite read it three times.

*By order of the Inquisitors' Guild: information leading to the capture of any person speaking forbidden tongues within city walls. Reward: forty crowns. Any person harboring such persons will be tried as a speaker themselves.*

Forty crowns was a year of honest charm-selling, if charms still worked. It was two years of selling the ones that didn't.

"You should turn me in," Oren said. He'd learned enough Common in six days to say that much, delivered with the flat calm of a child reporting weather. He sat on her floor amid the unsold charms, sorting them into piles she hadn't asked him to sort—bone beads here, salt knots there—with a precision that unsettled her more than his language did. He knew what each one had been for. She could see it in how he handled them: gently, like things that had once been alive.

"Shut up," Marguerite said.

"That is not gratitude."

"It's survival advice. Nobody says *turn me in* out loud in this city. You think it, you keep it behind your teeth, and you definitely don't say it in front of the window." She crossed the room and pulled the shutter closed, though the street outside was empty except for a cat with opinions about the fish carts. "And I'm not turning you in because forty crowns doesn't buy back the part of me that would have to live with it."

Oren considered this. His Common was coming fast—too fast, Sefa said, faster than any living learner—and when he spoke it still sounded like he was translating from something older and larger, choosing small words to carry big freight.

"Your god-mark burned again last night," he said.

It wasn't a question. He always knew. She'd woken at midnight with her wrist blazing under the blanket, the old scar of her oath glowing like a coal raked from a fire, and Oren had been sitting up in the dark watching her with those eyes that saw too much.

"It does that now," she said. "Twenty years of nothing, and now it burns whenever you're near. I haven't decided if that's a miracle or a symptom."

"What is a symptom?"

"A sign something's wrong with you."

He nodded slowly. "Then we are both symptomatic."

Marguerite laughed before she could stop herself—a short, rusty sound, like a door opening in a house long shut. Oren smiled at having caused it, pleased with himself in a way that made her chest ache. Six days. Six days since the boy appeared barefoot at her door speaking the tongue of the deep courts, and already she was laughing at his jokes and hiding him from Inquisitors and lying awake doing arithmetic on forty crowns versus her soul.

She was in trouble. Not the Guild kind. The other kind, the kind where a child becomes yours without paperwork or oath, just by being in your house long enough.

A knock at the door. Three sharp raps—the rhythm Sefa used, which was to say, the rhythm of a man who'd spent twenty years flinching and had developed a knock accordingly.

Marguerite opened it a hand's width. Sefa Ilvane stood in the alley doorway looking like a man who'd run most of the way from the archive district, which he had; his coat was buttoned wrong and his satchel hung open, papers bristling out of it like feathers.

"They posted it early," he said, pushing past her. "The bounty. It was supposed to go up after the moon's twelfth turn—they always wait for the turn, it's ritual, it's *procedure*—but someone reported you ahead of schedule." He dropped onto her one chair, caught his breath, and looked up at her with an expression she'd only seen on men reading their own death warrants. "It was a charm-seller. Someone in Hollow district. Someone who's seen the boy."

The list of candidates was short and ugly. Maren Ostwick, who sold luck-threads two stalls down and had been eyeing Marguerite's corner for years. The tallow-man. Anyone, really. A city full of people scraping by on charms that didn't work would sell their mothers for forty crowns and call it enterprise.

"Did they give a name?" Marguerite asked.

"Informants are anonymous. That's the whole architecture of it." Sefa rubbed his face. "Marguerite, listen to me. This isn't a patrol matter anymore. A named bounty means a retrieval team. Four Inquisitors minimum, a reader, and a hound-handler. They'll come at night. They'll come quietly. And when they find the boy, they won't kill him—they'll do worse, because a child speaking pre-kingdom tongues isn't evidence, he's a *specimen*."

From the floor, Oren said, "I understand some of that."

"I know," Sefa said. "I'm sorry."

"Don't be sorry where he can hear it," Marguerite said. "Be useful instead. What do we do?"

Sefa blinked. "*We?*"

"You've been waiting twenty years for someone with a reason to go under the mountain. I've got the reason. The boy's got the door-key in his mouth. What we don't have is time, and what we especially don't have is a way to survive the next three nights in this district with a bounty on us." She held up her wrist, where the god-mark had faded back to its dull scar-gray. "So. We?"

Something moved through Sefa's face—fear, yes, but underneath it, for the first time since she'd known him, a kind of relief. The relief of a man finally allowed to stop pretending he wasn't waiting.

"We," he said. "We need Ysolde."

* * *

Ysolde's rooftop shrine sat above the tannery row, up four ladders and one ladder that wasn't a ladder so much as a dare. Marguerite climbed it with Oren on her back—he weighed almost nothing, another thing about him that was wrong—and came up into a garden of hanging lamps, each one holding a sliver of moonlight that had no business existing indoors, or outdoors, or anywhere in a city where lunar orders had been outlawed for twenty years.

Ysolde stood among them like a woman tending bees. She was older than Marguerite had guessed from below—silver-haired, broad-handed, wearing a priestess's gray gone soft as dishcloth. She didn't turn around.

"The twelfth turn is tomorrow night," she said. "You've brought me the key and the archivist both. Sit. There's tea, if you don't mind that it's mostly chicory and regret."

"How did you know we were coming?"

"The moon told me." She said it without irony, poured four cups, and handed one to Oren, who took it in both hands and stared into it like it might contain instructions. "Also, your archivist has been asking after me for a week through three intermediaries, all of whom talk too much. Sit down, mercenary. You look like a woman doing sums she doesn't like."

Marguerite sat. "I'm doing the same sum everyone in this city does. What it costs to do the right thing."

"And?"

"So far the right thing is expensive."

Ysolde snorted. She lowered herself onto a cushion across from Oren and studied him with the unhurried attention of someone who had spent decades reading light off rock. Then she said something in the old tongue—three syllables, low and round—and Oren went very still.

He answered. Four syllables. His voice cracked on the last one.

Ysolde closed her eyes. When she opened them they were wet. "That's a greeting," she said, to Marguerite, though she kept looking at the boy. "The greeting priestesses used before the Edict. *Moon over the water, water over the stone.* No one has said it to me in twenty years. I'd forgotten my own ears could hurt like that." She wiped her eyes briskly, businesslike, and turned to Marguerite. "Right. Business. Tell me everything, starting with why a mute child from nowhere speaks court-tongue and why your mark lights up like festival glass when he's near."

Marguerite told her. All of it—the failed charm, the boy at the door, Sefa's ledger, the Villalobos debt, the brother sold beneath the mountain before she was born, the thirteenth turn closing the door forever. Ysolde listened without interrupting, which was its own kind of skill, and when Marguerite finished she was quiet for a long moment while the hanging lamps swayed in a wind Marguerite couldn't feel.

"You understand what you're proposing," Ysolde said finally. "Not the theft. Anyone can propose a theft. I mean the rest of it. If you take that flame out of the under-court, you'll be carrying a soul across a threshold in a body marked by a silent god. The old texts say the carrier pays passage. And passage, for a thing like that, is never money."

"I know."

"Do you? Because I've read the fine print, girl, and the fine print is written in a grammar that eats people. The under-court will demand a price no one expects. That's not a warning, it's a description of how they operate. They are *contractual*. They are *precise*. They will let you win, and then they will show you what winning cost." She leaned forward. "I'm not saying don't go. I'm saying go knowing."

"I've fought for pay my whole life," Marguerite said. "This is the first job I'd do free. Maybe that makes me stupid."

"It makes you dangerous," Ysolde said. "Paid soldiers calculate. People with nothing to gain don't, and the under-court has no procedure for them." She rose, went to the shrine's far wall, and took down something wrapped in oilcloth—a staff of pale wood banded with silver, worn smooth by a century of hands. "I counted the turns alone for twenty years. Every month, up here, marking the moon's road, keeping the count alive so that someday someone would need the count. Do you know what that's like? Being the only one who remembers a calendar nobody uses?"

"No," Marguerite said honestly. "But I know what it's like to pray to something that stopped answering."

"Yes," Ysolde said softly. "I suppose you do. That's kinship, of a kind. Ugly kinship, but real." She slung the staff across her back. "I'll read the moon-road tonight. Tomorrow we move. And Marguerite—" she paused at the shrine's edge, silhouetted against her impossible lamps, "—the boy stays within arm's reach of one of us at all times. Not because he's fragile. Because he's *bait*, and none of us has agreed to spend him yet. Make sure it stays that way."

* * *

They came on the second night, and they came quiet, exactly as Sefa had promised.

Marguerite woke to the sound of her own name spoken in a voice like wet slate, and knew before her eyes opened that it wasn't a neighbor. An Inquisitor's calling-word—it dragged the true name out of a sleeping mind and made the body answer whether it wanted to or not. She'd seen recruits jerk upright in barracks, eyes white, walking toward the sound. She bit through her own lip to stay down, tasting copper, and put her hand over Oren's mouth before he could make a sound.

Through the gap under the door: lantern-light, moving. Two shadows, then four. Boots on her stairs, careful ones.

*Retrieval team,* Sefa had said. *Four Inquisitors, a reader, a hound-handler.*

There was no back exit to her rooms. There never had been. But there was the cellar, and the cellar had been built by smugglers two hundred years dead, and Sefa—who had spent twenty years memorizing the city's bones because the living city wouldn't have him—had shown her the hatch behind the salt racks on the first night he'd translated Oren's speech. *Everyone forgets the salt-cellars,* he'd said. *They connect half of Hollow district. The Guild maps end at the trapdoors because the trapdoors were illegal before the Edict was.*

She gathered Oren, the satchel, the lantern with its hooded shutter, and went down through the floor.

The cellar was cold and smelled of old brine. Sefa was already there—he slept badly and heard everything—and Ysolde came down the ladder last, moving with a silence that shouldn't have been possible for a woman her age. Above them, the door splintered. Voices, clipped and procedural. The reader's voice, reciting detection verses, each line landing in Marguerite's skull like a fingernail on slate.

Oren pressed against her side. His heart was going fast but his breathing was even, controlled, and she realized with a jolt that he'd done this before—hidden from hunters, somewhere else, somewhere older. She put her arm around him and felt him lean into it, deliberately, trusting the weight of her.

*Good,* she thought, fierce and sudden. *Lean. I've got you.*

Sefa lifted the trapdoor. Beyond it lay a corridor of packed earth and ancient brick, ceiling low enough that Sefa had to stoop, walls crusted with two centuries of salt bloom, glittering faintly in the hooded lantern-light like a tunnel lined with frost. Cold air moved through it, smelling of the river far away.

Behind them, above, the reader's verse found the empty room and faltered.

"They'll know about the cellars eventually," Sefa whispered as they slipped through. "But eventually is a word I've made a career out of."

They moved. Ysolde took point with her staff, counting turns in a whisper—left at the collapsed stair, right where the bricks changed color—and Marguerite carried Oren when the boy tired and set him down when pride flickered across his face, and somewhere in the third hour of walking through the salted dark, she noticed that the fear had changed shape. It wasn't gone. It had simply stopped being hers alone. Four people breathing in rhythm, moving as one body through the bones of the city—that was a phalanx, that was a shield-line, that was the oldest thing she knew. She'd been a company of one for twenty years and had called it freedom.

It hadn't been freedom. It had just been quiet.

They surfaced near dawn in a tannery cellar Ysolde owned beneath her rooftop, and Oren fell asleep against Marguerite's shoulder mid-sentence, still trying to tell her something in Common about moths. She sat very still so as not to wake him, and watched Ysolde bank the lamps, and felt Sefa watching her with an expression she pretended not to see.

"You'd die for him already," Sefa said quietly. "Six days."

"Shut up, archivist."

"For the record, I'd have said eight days for you. You're slower than the boy."

Above them, through the roof boards, the last stars were going out, and the moon—fat, nearly full, one turn from done—hung over Vell Hollow like a coin about to be spent. Tomorrow night, the twelfth turn. The night after, the thirteenth, and the door beneath the mountain closed for good.

Marguerite looked down at the sleeping boy, at the blue-white glow beginning to seep from between his lips with every breath, brightening as the deadline approached, and understood suddenly what he'd been trying to tell her.

The moths weren't a story. They were souls. And they were running out of time.

"We leave at dusk," she said. "All of us. Tonight we go down."
