## 8. The Road Under the Road

Sefa Ilvane had spent twenty years learning the exact weight of a door he could not open.

Not a literal door—though there was one of those too, at the bottom of the Hollow district, rusted shut behind the old salt-works, sealed by Guild writ and three feet of poured concrete poured by men who thought concrete could stop what lived under stone. No. The door Sefa meant was the one in his own chest, the one marked *forbidden*, the one the Inquisitors had welded shut the day they burned his office and struck his name from the rolls.

He had copied ledgers for eleven years before they caught him. Eleven years of ink-stained fingers and candle-grease and the slow, ecstatic theft of a kingdom's memory. And in all that time, he had never once found anyone who needed to know what he knew. That was the cruelest part—not the exile, not the poverty, not the way former colleagues crossed the street when they saw him coming. The cruelty was that the knowledge sat in him like water in a closed jar, going stale, going strange, going sour with disuse.

Then a charm-seller named Marguerite Villalobos had walked into his cellar with a mute child who spoke pre-kingdom court-tongue, and the jar had cracked open so fast it nearly took his hands off.

*The thirteenth turn closes the door.*

Four days left. Maybe five, depending on how Ysolde counted the waning. Which was why Sefa now stood at the mouth of the abandoned Vetch mine with a lantern, a coil of rope, a satchel of chalk and paper, and four people who were about to trust him with their lives on the strength of twenty-year-old memories.

"You're certain this is the way down," Marguerite said. Not a question. A mercenary's way of giving him one last chance to lie.

"No," Sefa said honestly. "I'm certain it's *a* way down. I'm certain it's the way the contracts describe. Whether the mountain has kept it open—" He shrugged. "Mountains are not obligated to keep their promises. Neither are archivists, but we pretend otherwise."

Behind Marguerite, Oren stood very still, his bare feet pale against the scree. The boy had not spoken since the cellar—not in Common, which he couldn't, and barely in the old tongue, which he could but seemed to ration like a man rationing water in a desert. But his eyes moved constantly: over the mine-mouth's timber frame, over the warning sigils chiseled into the lintel, over Sefa himself, with an attention that made the archivist feel transcribed.

Ysolde came last, her moon-priestess grays dusty from the walk, a small silver bell hanging silent at her belt. She looked up at the sky—the twelfth-turn moon, a fat paring of itself—and then at the dark opening in the hillside, and she smiled the way people smile at funerals they've decided to enjoy.

"Twenty years I've watched that moon from my roof," she said. "First time I've ever walked toward something instead of waiting for it."

"Sentiment later," Marguerite said. "Down now."

Sefa almost laughed. Twenty years of exile, and the first person to take him seriously was a woman who sold broken charms and treated hope like a supply to be inventoried. He lifted his lantern and stepped into the dark.

* * *

The Vetch mine had been dead for sixty years, but stone remembers longer than timber rots, and the first gallery still bore its history in cuts and scars. Sefa kept them moving at a pace just short of reckless, counting side-shafts by the numbers some long-dead foreman had painted at the junctions. Seven. Nine. Fourteen, where the air changed—cooler, mineral, faintly sweet, like the breath of something sleeping with its mouth open.

It was at nineteen that Oren stopped walking.

The boy stood before the gallery wall where the lamplight fell strongest, head tilted, lips parted. Sefa followed his gaze and felt his stomach drop with a joy so violent it was nearly indistinguishable from terror.

"There," he breathed. "You see it too."

Carved into the rock face, in lines fine as spiderwork, ran text. Not the crude tally-marks of miners, not Guild script, not any alphabet taught in any school in Vell Hollow. This was contract-hand—the formal incision-script of the under-court, each character cut with a precision no human tool could match, because no human tool *had*. The wall was covered in it, floor to ceiling, gallery after gallery of it, receding into the dark like scripture in a cathedral built by something that worshipped obligation.

Marguerite ran her fingers near the carvings without touching. "Contracts."

"The oldest ones." Sefa set down his satchel and pulled out paper and charcoal, hands already moving, already working, and God—it felt *good*, it felt like a stiff limb remembering how to bend. "The deep courts don't write their bargains on vellum. Vellum burns. Stone is their signature and their seal both. Every deal ever struck beneath the mountain is carved somewhere in these galleries, and the mines were dug through them like a knife through a ledger."

Oren touched the wall. Just once, two fingertips, light as a moth landing. His whole body shivered.

"What did he feel?" Ysolde asked quietly.

"I don't know. But I can tell you what I felt, the first time I found these." Sefa unrolled a fresh sheet and began copying a section of the wall, block by block, his charcoal whispering. "I was thirty-one years old. I'd been in the royal archive nine years, cataloging tax rolls, trade manifests, the thrilling paperwork of grain. And then a surveyor's report crossed my desk—a routine thing about mine subsidence in the Vetch shafts—with a rubbing attached. Someone had pressed paper against this wall and taken an impression, thinking it was decorative masonry." He tapped the page. "I recognized the hand within a minute. I'd seen it once before, in a fragment in the restricted vault. A debt instrument. Pre-kingdom. The oldest document in the collection, and nobody alive could read it."

"And you could," Marguerite said.

"I could *learn* to. There's a difference, and the difference took me six years." He kept copying as he talked, because if he stopped working his hands would start shaking. "Six years of stolen evenings in the restricted stacks. Six years teaching myself a dead language from a single fragment and a wall full of examples. And do you know what I discovered, down here, with my lamp and my rubbings? The under-court doesn't lie. It's the one honest institution in the world. Every term, every price, every trap—it's all written down, plain as daylight, in a language no one reads anymore. They hide nothing. We simply stopped looking."

"That's not honesty," Ysolde said. "That's a lock that only opens for the patient."

"It's both. Most terrible things are both." Sefa finished the section, rolled the sheet, started another. "That's why they exiled me, incidentally. Not for trespassing—I never trespassed; I copied documents that were technically in my care. I was exiled for *understanding*. An archivist who can read the old contracts is a threat to every bargain the crown has ever struck below. My patron—the Lord Chamberlain himself, a man whose family name appears in these galleries more often than any other—stood up at my tribunal and called me 'a man who knows too much to be permitted to know anything.'" He smiled without warmth. "They let me keep my life. They burned my copies. All except the ones I'd hidden inside bindings of tax law, because no Inquisitor in history has ever voluntarily opened a book about tithes."

Marguerite huffed—almost a laugh. "So the kingdom's secrets survived inside its most boring records."

"Boredom is the best vault ever built." He finished the second sheet and finally looked up at her. "I want to say something before we go deeper, and I want you to hear all of it, because you deserve to make your decisions with your eyes open."

She waited. Oren turned from the wall. Even Ysolde went still.

"I have been waiting twenty years for someone with a reason to come down here," Sefa said. "Not curiosity—curiosity I've had in abundance, and it's worthless, it's a coin that spends nowhere. I mean *need*. Someone with a debt, or a grief, or a deadline, someone the mountain would actually take seriously. Because the under-court respects need the way banks respect collateral. And I have spent two decades knowing exactly how to navigate these galleries, exactly which clauses bind which powers, exactly where the law beneath the law bends—and having no one to use it *for*. Do you understand what that's been like? To be a key in a country with no locks?"

"Yes," Marguerite said quietly. "I sold charms that didn't work for the same number of years."

Something passed between them then—not friendship, not yet, but the recognition of two people who had kept faith with useless things until the things stopped being useless. Sefa nodded once and shouldered his satchel.

"Gallery twenty-two is where the Villalobos entries begin," he said. "If we're quick, we'll reach it before the next shift of air comes through. After that—" he pointed down the black throat of the tunnel, where his lantern-light died without finding a floor— "after that, we go where the maps stop pretending."

* * *

Gallery twenty-two took two hours, and Sefa talked the entire way, partly to teach and mostly because silence in the deep mines had a texture he didn't trust—a listening quality, as though the stone were taking notes.

He showed them the toll-clauses carved at every junction, the little gatekeeper-writs that demanded names or memories or years. He showed them the salvage rights, the sanctuary clauses, the ancient indemnities that had protected mining families for generations and expired, one by one, as the families forgot they existed. He showed them a stretch of wall where an entire village's worth of names had been sold in columns—hundreds of entries, each one a person reduced to a line of incised script—and watched Ysolde press her palm flat against the stone and murmur something that might have been a prayer or an apology.

And at the far end of gallery twenty-two, exactly where his memory said it would be, he found the Villalobos ledger.

His lamp shook. He hated that it shook. He held it steadier.

"Here." His voice came out wrong, so he cleared it and tried again. "Marguerite. Here."

The entry was older than the others around it—older cutting-style, deeper grooves, the work of a court taking its business seriously. Sefa translated aloud, slowly, because some sentences deserved to be heard at the speed of understanding:

"*Let it be entered that the house of Villalobos, pressed by feud and fearing extinction, does sell unto the Thirteenth Magistrate one soul, unborn and unnamed, in satisfaction of blood-debt and purchase of peace. Let the term be forever, save that the debt be proven paid, in which case the soul returns to the line that sold it.*"

Silence, except for the drip of water somewhere far below.

"*Save that the debt be proven paid,*" Marguerite repeated. Her voice was flat, controlled, the voice of a woman holding a blade very carefully by the wrong end. "Paid. The feud. Was it—"

"Paid generations ago. I found the settlement record eight years ago in a provincial archive, filed under land disputes, of all things. Both feuding houses extinct. Debt discharged." Sefa met her eyes. "The under-court has been holding your brother past term for a century and a half. It's in the stone, Marguerite. It's *written down*. They hide nothing—we just stopped looking."

Her jaw worked. She put her hand against the carving, over the word *unborn*, and held it there for a long moment, and Sefa looked away to give her the privacy of the dark.

When she spoke again, her voice had changed. The weariness was gone from it. What remained was colder and much more dangerous.

"Then we don't steal anything," she said. "We collect."

Oren made a sound. Small, urgent. He was standing at the edge of the lamplight, staring down the tunnel ahead, and his whole thin body was trembling—not with fear, Sefa realized, but with recognition. The boy raised one hand and pointed into the black, and when he spoke, it was the longest sentence he'd offered in days, the old tongue rolling out of him like water finding a channel it had always known.

Ysolde translated, her priestess-calm cracking at the edges.

"He says the humming is louder here. He says the mountain isn't asleep anymore." She swallowed. "He says something down there has noticed us reading its walls."

Far below, through miles of stone, something answered—a sound less heard than felt, a vast slow shifting, like a ledger opening.

Sefa picked up his lantern. Twenty years of waiting ended here; whatever came next, he would meet it with ink on his fingers and a reason to go down.

"Then we'd better read faster than it counts," he said, and led them into the dark.
