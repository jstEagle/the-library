## 9. Where the God Went Silent

The shrine found them before they found it.

Sefa had been leading them by the contracts—reading the carved stone walls like a man reading street signs, muttering toll rates and forfeiture clauses from a century nobody alive remembered. Ysolde walked with her lantern hooded to a sliver of blue. Oren walked between them all, barefoot on the cold rock as if the mountain were a floor he'd known since birth.

Then the tunnel simply opened, the way a throat opens, and there it was.

A chamber no miner had cut. The walls were salt, but not the dead white salt of the upper shafts—this was salt shot through with veins of something that caught Ysolde's hooded light and held it, glowing faintly gold, like honey held up to the sun. A basin of black stone sat at the center, dry as old bone. Above it, set into the wall, was a niche—and in the niche, a shape.

Marguerite knew the shape. She had worn its likeness on her wrist for thirty-one years.

*The Hollow Blade.* Not a sword, exactly—the god of Vell Hollow's sworn mercenaries had never been a thing you could draw in full. What the carvers gave him was a blade seen edge-on, so thin it was almost nothing, a line of absence cutting the stone. His faithful had called him the Edge. The Unseen Cut. The Answer When Called. Every mercenary company in the city's history had sworn to him, because a god who lived in the space between *threat* and *strike* understood what their lives were worth.

The Edict had outlawed him by name. *No god of violence shall hold dominion within the walls of Vell Hollow.* As if naming it made it true. As if a law could reach into the dark under the world and take a knife out of a god's hand.

Marguerite stopped at the threshold and did not go in.

"Go on," Sefa said gently. He'd already unrolled his coat on the floor and was laying out dried meat like this was any campsite. "It's safe ground. Shrines were neutral even under the old courts. Even the Inquisitors' Guild honored truce-ground, back when they still pretended to have honor."

"I'm not worried about safety."

Ysolde looked at her. The moon-priestess said nothing, which was worse than anything she could have said. She had a way of looking at people that made them hear their own thoughts in someone else's voice.

Oren went in. Of course he did. He crossed the chamber without hesitation, knelt at the black basin, and pressed both palms flat against the stone. And the salt veins brightened—not gold now, but a pale silver, rippling outward from his hands like rings on water.

"He's listening," Oren said, in his own tongue, and Marguerite understood it the way she was beginning to understand everything the boy said: not translated, just *known*, arriving whole in her head like a memory she hadn't made yet. "The mountain remembers every prayer ever spoken into it. It keeps them. Like ledgers."

"Everything down here is a ledger," Sefa muttered.

Marguerite stayed at the threshold. Her wrist burned.

Not the dull ache it had carried for twenty years—a real burn, the old burn, the one that used to mean *your god is paying attention*. She pulled back her sleeve. The mark sat on the inside of her forearm where the oath-tattoo had been inked over her pulse: a single line, thin as a blade seen edge-on. For two decades it had been gray, scarred-over, a tattoo and nothing more. Now it glowed faintly, and the glow leaned. There was no other word for it. The light bent toward the tunnel ahead, toward the deep dark below them, the way iron leans toward a lodestone.

*He's down there,* she thought. *Or what's left of him.*

She went in. Because she was a coward in exactly one direction only, and it had never been forward.

* * *

They ate. They slept in shifts. Marguerite took first watch, which meant she sat alone in the gold-lit dark with a god's empty face staring at her from a wall.

"You chose survival."

Ysolde's voice, low, from beside her. Marguerite hadn't heard her sit down. Moon-priestesses moved like that; it was why the Guild had outlawed them second, right after the war-gods. Hard to arrest a woman you couldn't catch.

"I don't remember asking for a confessor."

"No. You never do." Ysolde folded her hands in her lap. "I read it off you the night we met. You carry shame like a coin you keep checking is still in your pocket. I've watched a hundred faithful since the Edict. Most of them grieved. Some of them raged. You're the first one I've met who acts like she *committed a crime*."

"The Edict came down," Marguerite said, "and every sworn blade in Vell Hollow had a choice. Keep the oath and be outlawed—lose your license, your lodging, your legal standing, your right to walk the streets armed. Or swear the new loyalty oath to the city magistrates and keep eating." She turned her wrist over. The line of the mark caught the light. "Four hundred years of the Edge's faith, and it took the Guild eleven days to break. Eleven days. I counted."

"And you?"

"I lasted nine."

The words came out flat, and that was the shame of it—they came out *flat*, after twenty years of carrying them, and they should have come out bleeding. She had rehearsed this confession in a thousand sleepless hours and it always sounded the same: like a report. Like a mercenary filing paperwork on her own soul.

"Two days longer than my captain," she went on, because once the door opened it wouldn't stay shut. "Roderic Vasq. Twenty-two years sworn. He stood in the Guildhall square and told the Inquisitors they could take his license and shove it somewhere the Edict didn't reach. They took more than his license. He died in the Saltworks prison inside a year, and do you know what the worst part was?" She laughed, and it came out wrong. "The worst part was that his god never answered him. Not once. Not in the square, not in the cell. The Edict passed and the Edge went silent—*silent*, everywhere, all at once, like a door closing in a house—and Roderic died alone for a god who wouldn't say thank you."

"And you think that means the god abandoned *him*," Ysolde said quietly, "so you can't forgive yourself for not abandoning the god first."

Marguerite didn't answer.

"Tell me about the ninth day," Ysolde said.

* * *

The ninth day had been raining. Marguerite remembered that with terrible clarity—the rain, the mud in the Guildhall square, the smell of wet wool and lamp oil. The Edict had been law for eight days and the city had spent those eight days learning a new posture: shoulders drawn in, eyes down, everyone listening to see if the sky would fall.

Her company had gathered in the square because the Guild had ordered every licensed mercenary to attend the Loyalty Muster. Swear the civic oath or surrender your badge. Simple. Clean. The Inquisitors had set up a table under an awning with a ledger open on it, and behind the table stood a Guild inquisitor named Corvane, whom Marguerite would spend the next twenty years trying not to think about, because he had been kind about it. That was the unforgivable part. He had leaned across the table and said, quietly, *Villalobos. Sign. Nobody thinks less of you. The gods understand. That's what they're for.*

She had stood in the rain with her thumb on the signature line for the length of three bells.

She had prayed. That was the thing she never told anyone. Right there, in the mud, in front of the ledger, she had prayed the way she hadn't prayed since she was a girl taking her first oath—*answer me. Give me one word. One burning in the mark. Make it impossible, and I'll stand with Roderic, I'll die in the Saltworks, I'll be faithful, just make it impossible.*

And the mark had been silent. Gray. Dead leather on her arm.

So she signed. Because a god who wouldn't answer couldn't complain about how she fed herself. Because survival wasn't a sin, it was arithmetic. Because Corvane had been kind, and kindness was harder to refuse than a whip.

She signed, and collected her badge, and walked out of the square past Roderic Vasq, who was being put in irons, and he looked at her—not with contempt, that was the hell of it, he'd looked at her with *pity*—and she had gone back to work the next morning and taken a contract escorting grain wagons, and she had eaten that winter, and every winter since.

"And here's the joke," she said to Ysolde, twenty years later, in a god's abandoned shrine under the mountain. "The Edict worked. Whatever they did when they passed it—it wasn't just law. Something actually happened. The silence isn't abandonment. It's a *wound*. I've felt it for twenty years and told myself it was guilt, but it's not. Guilt doesn't lean." She held up her arm. The mark's pale light strained toward the tunnel mouth like a dog at the end of a rope. "Something cut him off. And whatever did it is down there. Below us. Deeper than we're going."

Ysolde was quiet a long moment. "You know what I think?"

"I suspect you'll tell me."

"I think you didn't fail your test on the ninth day. I think the test hasn't finished." The priestess rose, unhurried, and nodded toward the black basin. "You prayed for a word that would make faithfulness easy. He didn't give it. Maybe because the answer was never supposed to come from him." She touched Marguerite's shoulder once, briefly, and walked away to check on the boy.

Marguerite sat with the blade-shaped absence in the wall and felt the truth of it settle into her chest like cold water finding its level.

She had wanted the god to make her choice for her. He had refused. Not out of cruelty—out of respect, maybe, or despair, or the particular mercy of a war-god who knew that a faith chosen under compulsion was worth nothing. *Make it impossible,* she'd begged. And he had left it possible. Terribly, easily possible. All she'd had to do was sign her name.

She got up and walked to the basin. Knelt. Put her palm flat on the black stone the way Oren had.

Nothing. Cold stone, and beneath the cold stone, far down, a hum she could feel in her teeth.

"I'm going down there," she said aloud, to the shape in the wall, to whatever might be listening through twenty years of silence. "Not for you. For the boy. For my brother. But if you're down there—if some piece of you is down there—" Her voice cracked and she hated it and kept going. "Then hear this. I signed. I know what I signed. And I'm not asking forgiveness. I'm telling you I'm coming anyway."

The mark flared—once, hard, a white-hot line across her forearm—and went dim again.

It could have been the mountain. It could have been nothing.

But it had leaned toward the dark, and so, she realized, had she, for twenty years, without ever once admitting which direction she was walking.

* * *

She woke to Oren shaking her arm, and the boy's face was wrong—pale, eyes huge, one small hand pressed against his own chest as if holding something in.

"What is it? What's happened?"

Oren pointed down the tunnel, the way they'd been heading, deeper. Then he spoke, fast, in the old tongue, and the meaning arrived in her head with the weight of falling masonry:

*"There are people ahead. In the walls. People who are missing pieces—and they can hear us coming."*

Marguerite was on her feet with her knife out before the translation finished landing.

Behind her, the mark on her wrist pulsed once toward the dark, patient as a debt.

Ahead, from somewhere down the tunnel, faint and wrong, came the sound of humming—syllables missing, names half-spoken, a village singing itself incomplete.
