## 10. Moths in the Lamplight

The village had no name.

Oren knew this the way he knew most things now—by listening. The road under the road ran for three days through shafts where the contracts were carved, and Sefa touched each one as they passed like a man greeting old enemies. But on the third day the tunnels opened into a hollow where lamplight glowed amber through cracks in a ceiling of salt, and Oren stopped walking because he could hear it before he saw anything at all.

Humming.

Not wind. Not water. Syllables. Fragments of names, worn smooth by years of being spoken by no one, drifting up out of the walls the way heat rises off stone. He pressed his palm flat against the tunnel mouth and let the sound pour into him.

*—ara. —emuel. —issa. —orin—*

That last one made his chest tighten. *Orin.* Close to his own name. Close enough that for one terrible moment he thought the walls were calling him, that the mountain had already begun to take him apart and store the pieces.

"Oren." Marguerite's hand on his shoulder. She'd learned to read his stillness. "What is it?"

He turned and looked at her, and then, because there was no way to say it in her language, he said it in his own: *the walls are full of people.*

She didn't understand the words. But she understood his face. Her jaw did the thing it did when she was deciding whether to be afraid or angry, and mostly she chose angry, so he wasn't surprised when she drew her knife and stepped ahead of him into the light.

*

The village sat in a bowl of salt like an egg in a cup. Maybe forty houses, built from mine-timber and packed earth, their roofs furred with pale crystal that grew a finger's length every year and had to be scraped away or it would swallow the houses whole. Lamps hung on every doorpost—not oil lamps but lanterns of woven reed with candles inside, and every single flame burned the same strange color: gold shot through with veins of blue, like sunlight remembering the sea.

People came out to look at them. That was the first wrong thing, Oren realized. In the villages above, travelers were met with suspicion, doors closing, eyes narrowing. Here they were met with something worse. Hope. Forty faces turning toward four strangers with the naked, unguarded hope of people who had stopped expecting anything and couldn't stop themselves anyway.

An old woman approached first. She walked with a stick and her back curved like a drawn bow, and when she spoke her Common was slow and careful, each word set down like a coin she couldn't spare.

"You're going down." Not a question. Her eyes went to Oren and stayed there, and something moved across her face—recognition, or its ghost. "You're one of theirs."

*I'm nobody's,* Oren signed, forgetting she wouldn't understand, and then said it aloud in the old tongue, and the old woman flinched as if he'd struck her.

"Don't," she whispered. "Don't speak that here. They hear it. The walls carry it down." She looked over her shoulder at the salt-crusted houses, at the lamps, at nothing. "Everything carries down."

Marguerite stepped between them, hand still on her knife. "Who are you? What is this place?"

The old woman smiled, and it was the saddest thing Oren had ever seen, sadder than the silence of the mountain, sadder than the door grinding shut. "We're what's left," she said. "My name was Marisol Abante. I can say the first part. The second part went south forty years ago, when the fever took my daughter and I needed a cure that only the court sells." She tapped her own chest. "There's a hole here where *Abante* used to live. You get used to the shape of it. You never get used to the cold coming through."

Sefa made a sound behind them. Oren turned to see the archivist standing very still, his ledger-calloused hands hanging at his sides, his face the color of the salt around them.

"How many," Sefa said. His voice cracked on the second word.

"Here? Forty-one souls. Above us, in the deep shelves?" The old woman shrugged, a small dry movement. "Thousands. Tens of thousands. Every family that ever needed something the surface wouldn't give. Every debtor, every desperate mother, every man who signed thinking a name was a small thing to pay." She looked at Marguerite. "You have a merchant's face, child. Tell me—what would you pay for your brother's life?"

Marguerite went white. Oren watched her hand tighten on the knife until the knives of her fingers showed through the skin.

"That's not—" she started.

"No," the old woman agreed. "It never is. That's how they get you. It's never a big price. It's a syllable. A memory of a smell. The sound of your mother saying your name when you were small. Small things. And then one day you reach for yourself and find the shelf empty." She turned and beckoned with her stick. "Come. If you're going down, you should see what you're walking past."

*

They followed her through lanes where the salt grew like frost on everything living. Oren kept his ears open and wished he hadn't.

Every house hummed. He heard it clearly now, the way he'd heard the mountain humming from the moment he'd woken above ground—the low chord of stored things, syllables stacked like cordwood. A woman watering a garden of pale mushrooms paused as they passed, and Oren heard the gap in her speech, a missing piece in the middle of every third word, so that her sentences sounded like a song with notes stolen out of it. A boy of maybe ten ran past chasing a beetle, laughing, and his laugh had a hole in it too—a whole bright register gone, so that what came out was half a joy, the shadow of a laugh instead of the thing itself.

A man sat on a doorstep mending a net with no river anywhere to fish. As they passed he looked up and said, "Good evening, travelers," and Oren heard it as *good even—, tra—lers,* and understood that this man had sold the sounds *ing* and *vel* and probably didn't know what they'd been, only that other people's words were fuller than his, richer, and that when he tried to remember what he'd traded he reached into himself and found smooth walls where the memory should have been.

*This is what justice costs,* Oren thought, and the thought arrived in the old tongue because there was no Common word cruel enough. *This is what it looks like when the powerful make contracts and the powerless sign them.* The Magistrate below kept perfect books. The Magistrate below had never broken a term in its long gray life. And its perfect justice had done *this*—had peeled people like fruit and left the rinds walking around tending mushroom gardens and mending nets for rivers that weren't there.

He caught Ysolde watching him. The moon-priestess had been quiet since the village came into view, her silver-marked hands folded, her face turned up toward the salt ceiling as if she could see the real moon through it. Now she looked down at him and nodded once, slowly, the nod of someone confirming a fear.

"You hear them," she said. It wasn't a question either. Everyone in this party had stopped asking questions they knew the answers to.

Oren nodded.

"The missing pieces," she said. "Where do they go?"

He pointed down. Then, after a moment, he pointed at the lamps. At the blue-veined flames burning in every doorway.

Ysolde closed her eyes. When she opened them again they were wet, but her voice was steady. "Of course. Of course they burn them. Why waste fuel when you've harvested a thousand years of other people's names?" She laughed, one short bitter note. "I spent my life believing the moon was the great keeper of memory. That nothing given to her light was ever truly lost." She touched one of the door-lanterns, gently, the way you touch a sleeping child. "And all along, down here, they were burning the memories as kindling."

The old woman—Marisol, the part of her that remained—led them to the edge of the village where the tunnels forked, one passage continuing down toward the deep shelves and the cathedral of salt, the other climbing back toward the world of full-named people. A shrine stood at the fork. It was nothing—salt blocks stacked hip-high, a flat stone on top, a candle guttering in the wind that breathed up from below. But Oren felt it before he saw it: the hum was quieter here. Held at bay. Whatever the villagers had placed on this stone over the years, it was the one thing in the village the mountain's chord couldn't quite cover.

"People leave what they can spare," Marisol said. "Which is to say, almost nothing. We have almost nothing left to spare." She looked at Ysolde. "But you're a priestess. Even disgraced, even outlawed—I can see it in how you stand. You people always stood like the sky was watching." Her voice frayed. "Will you—"

She didn't finish. She didn't have to.

Ysolde knelt at the shrine. From her pack she took the things she'd carried since the rooftop: a vial of moonwater blessed under eleven turns of the true moon, a lock of her own hair cut at her ordination, and a small silver disc stamped with the sigil of the lunar order—her order, dead twenty years, outlawed, forgotten, its shrines pulled down and its priests scattered or worse. She had carried that disc through two decades of hiding the way Marguerite carried her god-mark: as proof she had once belonged to something.

She set all three on the stone.

Then she did something Oren had never seen anyone do. She spoke the names of the villagers. Not their whole names—she couldn't; the halves were sold—but the parts that remained. "Marisol," she said. "And the gardener, whose name begins with the sound of rain. And the boy who laughs, may he laugh whole again someday. And the net-mender, who works with his hands though the river forgot him." Each fragment she spoke aloud, carefully, completely, giving each remaining piece of each person the dignity of being said in full. "I cannot return what was taken. But I witness what remains. Let the moon keep what the mountain will not."

The candle flame steadied. Just for a moment—one breath, two—the blue veins in the nearby door-lamps flickered, and Oren heard, faint as a dying echo, something that might have been relief.

Then it passed, and the humming resumed, and the village went on being a wound in the shape of a home.

Marisol pressed her stick-hand against her mouth and wept without any sound at all, which Oren understood: she had probably sold her crying somewhere too, decades ago, and what was left was the motion of it, the shape, the grief with the voice taken out.

*

They camped that night in the fork of the tunnels, close enough to the village that its lamplight reached them, far enough that its humming faded to a thread. Sefa sat apart with his back against the salt wall, writing. He wrote constantly now, filling page after page of a ledger he'd bought in Vell Hollow for two coppers, recording everything—the contracts he'd translated, the terms he'd parsed, and now, in a hand that shook slightly, the fragments of names Ysolde had spoken aloud.

"When we come back up," he said to no one in particular, to the dark, to the ledger, "this goes in the archive. All of it. Every name she said. Every name I can recover from the ledgers below." He looked up at Oren, and his eyes were fierce behind the exhaustion. "They think a contract erases a debt. But a contract *creates* debts. That's the whole architecture of law, boy. Every agreement binds both parties. They just spent five hundred years making sure no one could afford to hold them to their side of it." He tapped the ledger. "Someone's going to hold them to it."

Oren believed him. That was the strange thing—he believed this hollow man, this exile who'd lost his own name at a tollgate and drifted like a leaf in wind until they roped him to the party each morning. Believed him the way you believe a fire is hot.

Marguerite sat sharpening her knife and not looking at the village. Oren watched her for a long time. He knew what she was carrying now—Sefa had told her about the ledger, about the Villalobos line, about the unborn brother sold beneath the mountain before she ever drew breath. He knew she was doing the arithmetic every traveler to this place must do: *my grief is real, but look at the size of everyone else's.*

He crawled over to her and put his hand flat on her wrist, on the god-mark that hadn't burned in twenty years and had started burning again the day he spoke at her door.

She looked down at him. "What?"

In the old tongue, softly, so the walls couldn't carry it down: *you are not them. Your brother is not a syllable in a wall. We are going to take him home whole.*

She didn't understand the words. But her mark flared warm under his palm, and whatever her silent god said to her through it, her shoulders came down from around her ears, and she put her knife away, and she looked at the village lights for the first time all evening.

"They're just moths," she said quietly. "All those flames. I kept thinking they looked like moths in lamplight." She shook her head. "They're not moths. They're people. Trapped people, burning."

Oren nodded. Yes. Exactly that. And tomorrow they would walk down past all of it, past the shelves and the lanterns and the perfect gray justice of the Thirteenth Magistrate, into the cathedral of salt where his own body waited to be weighed as payment.

He lay back and looked at the salt ceiling and listened to the village breathe its broken names, and he made the decision again, the one he'd made the day he knocked on Marguerite's door: *these people are worth what it costs. I am worth what it costs. Someone has to walk into the dark carrying more than they took in.*

Sleep came slowly. And in the last hour before dawn, the humming in the walls changed.

Oren woke with his heart slamming. The chord had shifted—lower, closer, threaded through with something new. Not the stored syllables of the sold. Something else. Something moving.

Footsteps, far below, climbing. Many of them. And beneath the footsteps, rising through the salt like blood through cloth, a sound he knew from the deepest dreams of the mountain:

A bell. Ringing once. Twice.

Calling the court to session.

Sefa was already awake, rope in his hands, binding his wrist to Marguerite's pack-frame with the quick practiced motions of a man who had learned not to trust his own wandering feet. "It knows we're here," he whispered. "It's known since the shrine. Judgment doesn't wait for trespassers to arrive, boy. Judgment comes up to meet them."

Above them, through three hundred feet of salt and stone, the twelfth moon began, silently, to wane.
