## 11. The Tollgate of Names

The gate was not a door. That was the first thing Marguerite understood about it, standing in the dark with her lantern raised and her breath fogging in air that had no business being cold this deep.

It was a threshold wearing the shape of an argument. Two pillars of black stone, veined with salt that caught the light and threw it back wrong, and between them a curtain of nothing—air that refused to be looked at directly, the way you can't look at the exact center of your own eye. The road they'd followed for two days ended at that curtain. There was no road on the other side. There was only the certainty that going around was impossible, because Marguerite had sent Sefa to check, and Sefa had come back pale and quiet and said, "The walls curve. We've been walking in a spiral since the shrine."

"Then we go through," Ysolde said.

She stood at the front of their little procession with her moon-sigil bare in her hand, silver catching lantern-light, and she did not look afraid. Ysolde never looked afraid. Marguerite had decided sometime in the last three days that this was either faith or a very long practice of lying with her face, and she hadn't yet worked out which frightened her more.

Oren pressed close against Marguerite's side. He'd done that more often since the village of the half-forgotten—the one where the walls hummed with sold syllables, where a woman had wept over a name she could feel missing like a tooth. The boy's hand found the hem of Marguerite's coat and gripped it, and she let him, because there was no one down here to see a mercenary softened by a child, and even if there were, she found she didn't much care.

"All right," she said. "Sefa. You've read everything ever written about these courts. What's through a gate like this?"

Sefa Ilvane had been strange for two days. Not dangerous-strange, not yet—but he kept stopping mid-sentence, staring at his own hands as if checking they were still attached to him. He answered now, though, promptly, gratefully, the way a man answers when someone throws him a rope.

"Tollgates," he said. "In the contract-law of the deep courts, every passage is a transaction. Nothing crosses without payment. And the currency—" He hesitated. His fingers went to his collar, an old habit Marguerite had catalogued: he touched his collar when the truth was expensive. "The currency is identity. A true name, freely given. Or a piece of one."

"A piece of one," Marguerite repeated. "Like what?"

"Your mother's face. Your first betrayal. The sound of your own laugh." He said it flatly, archivist-flat, but his eyes flicked to Oren and away again. "The half-forgotten weren't made whole. They were paid out in installments."

Ysolde turned her sigil over in her fingers. "And if we refuse?"

"Then the gate refuses us. Forever. It doesn't open twice." Sefa swallowed. "There are ledgers describing travelers who sat before tollgates until they died of thirst, waiting for a price they could bear."

Marguerite looked at the curtain of nothing. She thought about the thirteenth turn of the moon, which hung somewhere above all this stone, waning toward its last night. She thought about a small blue flame flinching from her hand like a startled bird—a memory now, hours old and already worn smooth by handling—and about how many turns of walking still lay between them and the cathedral of salt.

"Well," she said. "I've been called Marguerite my whole life. How bad can it be to give them the truth of it?"

* * *

Bad. It could be very bad. She learned this from the way both Sefa and Ysolde went still at her words, the particular stillness of people watching someone reach toward a blade's edge.

"You don't give your *whole* name," Ysolde said quietly. "Not here. A true name given entire is a soul handed over with the wrapping on. The gate would own you past death. You give a piece. One true thing, freely surrendered, and no more than the gate asks."

"And what does it ask?" Marguerite asked. "Does anyone know? Does your moon-reading say?"

Ysolde's jaw tightened. "The moon-road shows doors. It does not show prices. Prices are the mountain's business."

As if summoned—as if it had been listening the whole time, which Marguerite increasingly suspected it had—the curtain between the pillars moved.

It didn't open. It *condensed*. The nothing gathered itself into a shape, tall and narrow, and the shape had shoulders and a suggestion of a head and no face at all, only a smooth expanse like river ice, and when it spoke, the voice came from everywhere at once—from the salt in the walls, from Marguerite's own teeth.

**TOLL.**

One word. It landed in her chest like a dropped stone.

"We hear you," Ysolde said, steady as a litany. "What do you ask?"

The wraith's arm—it was an arm now, the nothing resolving just enough to be worse—extended one long finger and pointed.

At Sefa.

"No," Marguerite said immediately.

**THE ONE WHO KEEPS RECORDS,** the walls said. **HIS NAME IS HEAVIEST.**

"That's not how this works," Marguerite said, stepping forward, hand finding her knife out of pure reflex, knowing even as she did it that steel meant nothing here. "We decide who pays. That's the whole point of a company."

"The point of a company," Sefa said softly, behind her, "is that someone carries what others can't."

She turned. He was smiling. It was the worst thing she'd seen in days, worse than the humming village, worse than the wraith's faceless patience—because it wasn't brave. It was *relieved*. Twenty years of exile had been waiting for a door exactly like this one, and here it was, and he was grateful.

"Sefa—"

"Ilvaneth Corrin Sefa," he said, to the wraith, to the walls, to the listening salt, in the clear voice of a man reciting from a ledger he'd memorized so thoroughly it had become him. "Third of that name. Royal Archivist, by appointment revoked. I give it freely."

"Don't—" Marguerite started.

He gave it anyway.

* * *

Later, Marguerite would try to describe what it looked like when a man's name left him, and she never managed it better than this: it looked like frost leaving glass in reverse. Something shimmered up out of Sefa's chest—a thread, a ribbon of pale light stitched with tiny moving marks, writing of some kind, *his* kind, the kind he'd been exiled for copying—and the wraith drew it in with something horribly like satisfaction, and the curtain of nothing sighed open onto darkness, and Sefa Ilvane swayed on his feet and asked:

"Why are we here?"

* * *

They got him through the gate before it closed. Marguerite took his arm and half-dragged him, and Oren pressed against his other side, and Ysolde walked backward through the threshold with her sigil raised, watching the wraith the way you watch a dog that hasn't decided about biting. The nothing-curtain rippled shut behind them with a sound like a ledger closing.

They stood in a new tunnel—wider, older, the walls carved with script that made Sefa's eyes hungry even now—and Sefa looked around at all of them with polite, blank courtesy, the look of a man at a gathering where he knows no one.

"I'm sorry," he said. "Have we met? You seem to be expecting me to know things."

Marguerite's stomach dropped through the floor of the world.

"You're Sefa," she said carefully. "You're an archivist. You came with us."

"Am I?" He looked at his hands again—that habit, that damn habit—and this time she understood it. He was looking for evidence. For anything written on himself that might tell him who he was. "I suppose I must be. You all seem certain of it." A pause. His brow creased, and Marguerite watched him strain against the absence the way a tongue worries a missing tooth. "I remember... books. I remember books the way other men remember their mothers. But I don't remember why I'm underground. I don't remember what we're doing."

"He gave his name," Ysolde said. Her voice was level but her knuckles were white around the sigil. "The gate took the whole of it. Not just the word—the *weight*. Everything the name held. His office. His purpose. His reasons."

"Can we get it back?" Marguerite asked.

"From a tollgate of the deep courts?" Ysolde shook her head slowly. "Nothing comes back out of a ledger, Marguerite. You taught me that phrase yourself, three nights ago, when I wanted to turn back."

Marguerite had no memory of saying it. Three nights ago felt like a year. She looked at Sefa—this brilliant, ruined, generous man, standing in the dark holding nothing but his hunger for books—and felt something in her chest crack along a line she hadn't known was there.

He'd been waiting twenty years for someone with a reason to go down. And now he didn't have one. He'd paid his reason as the toll.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about sacrifice: at the moment it happens, it can seem almost small. A word spoken. A ribbon of light. Sefa didn't scream. He barely staggered. And for one obscene moment Marguerite had thought, *that's it? that's the price?*—the way you think a wound is shallow until you see what's underneath.

The smallness was the horror. The gate hadn't taken his pain or his fear or his life. It had taken his *why*, and a man without a why is a lamp with the flame gone out of it, and everything else—the body, the mind, the encyclopedic memory of forbidden contracts—was still burning oil with nothing to illuminate.

They couldn't leave him. That was the arithmetic of it, cold and simple. A nameless man alone in the tunnels under the mountain would wander until the dark took him, or worse, until something else found him and gave him a new name, and Marguerite had seen enough of what the under-court did with names to know that would be mercy for no one.

So they bound him.

Ysolde had rope in her pack—hemp, honest, bought above ground with honest coin—and she uncoiled it with the brisk tenderness of a woman dressing a wound. "An old rite," she said. "Older than the lunar order. When a pilgrim loses their way, the company lends them theirs. We tie ourselves to him, and the knot remembers what he cannot."

"Will it work?" Marguerite asked.

"It works for pilgrims." Ysolde glanced at Sefa, who was watching the rope with the mild interest of a stranger at a fair. "For a man who sold his name to a tollgate, I make no promises. Hold out your wrists, archivist."

Sefa held out his wrists.

That was the part Marguerite couldn't shake, afterward. He didn't argue. Didn't flinch. He trusted them—four strangers, because that's what they were to him now—with his hands open and his face calm, because they seemed kind and he had nothing else to go on. Twenty years of scholarship and exile and stubborn devotion to a dead royal archive, and what remained of him was willing to be tied up by people whose names he didn't know, on the strength of their faces alone.

Ysolde looped the rope around his wrists—not tight, a loose cradle of knots—and then she took Marguerite's hand and wrapped the rope's end around it, speaking words in a cadence Marguerite didn't recognize, low and rolling, like tide. Then Oren's small wrist. Then her own. The rope passed through all of them, a circle, a chain, a promise.

"When he drifts," Ysolde said, tying off the final knot, "he'll feel the pull of the rope. Of us. It won't give him back his why. But it will keep him walking in our direction."

Sefa flexed his bound hands and looked down at the rope and then up at Marguerite, and something moved across his face—recognition of nothing, reaching for something.

"You have kind eyes," he said. "Did I mention that? I feel like I would have mentioned that."

"You didn't," Marguerite said, and her voice came out rougher than she intended.

"Shame. It seems like the sort of thing I'd notice." He smiled again, and this time it was just a man's smile, unburdened, empty as a cleared room. "Where are we going?"

Marguerite opened her mouth to answer and stopped.

Because she realized she was about to explain the whole of it—the stolen soul, the brother, the thirteenth turn, the court beneath the mountain—to a man who had given up his memories of *why any of it mattered*, and that some part of her had been counting on him for this. On his ledgers, his law, his twenty years of forbidden reading. On the archivist. And the archivist was gone, paid out to a gatekeeper like coins across a table, and what was left was a stranger with a scholar's eyes and a rope around his wrists.

But then Sefa tilted his head, and his gaze drifted to the walls—to the carved script running along the stone in lines older than any kingdom—and Marguerite watched something happen in his face. Not memory. Not quite. Something deeper than memory, burned in below the place the gate had cut.

His lips moved. Reading.

"That's contract-hand," he murmured. "Pre-kingdom cursive. Clause structure suggests... a leasehold. Souls held in trust against payment of interest." He frowned, tracing the air above the words. "Whoever carved this was sloppy. Sloppy means old. Old means there may be terms worth contesting."

Marguerite stared at him.

"What did you say your name was?" he asked pleasantly.

* * *

They walked. The rope ran from wrist to wrist, four souls strung together like beads, and Sefa drifted at the center of it, tethered, reading the walls as they passed and narrating in a soft, wondering voice—tolls and terms, forfeits and fees, centuries of the under-court's law scratched into living stone by hands that had signed away pieces of themselves.

He remembered the law. The gate had taken the man and left the craft.

Marguerite walked at the front with Oren's hand in hers and tried to count what they had left. A priestess with a dwindling store of consecrated dark. A child who carried souls across thresholds and knew more than he'd said. A nameless archivist who could still read the enemy's fine print. And herself—her knife, her oath, her mark that ached toward the dark below like a compass needle pointing at grief.

It would have to be enough. It was going to have to be enough, because somewhere ahead the tunnel widened, and the air changed, and the salt in the walls began to glow with a faint blue luminescence that Marguerite recognized from a lantern she had seen once, in a vault, flinching from her hand.

And Sefa, drifting behind her at the end of his rope, stopped suddenly and said, in a voice gone sharp and scholarly and utterly certain:

"There will be wards ahead. Vault-wards. I can draw them from memory—I don't know *whose* memory, but I have them." He paused. "Why would I have vault-wards memorized?"

Nobody answered him.

Ahead, far down the glowing corridor, something vast shifted in its sleep, and the mountain hummed a single note, and Oren's grip on Marguerite's hand went tight as wire.
