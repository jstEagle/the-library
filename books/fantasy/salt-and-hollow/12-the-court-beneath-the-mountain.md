## 12. The Court Beneath the Mountain

The tunnel stopped pretending to be a tunnel.

One moment they were walking with their shoulders hunched beneath stone that sweated salt; the next, the ceiling simply gave up and climbed away into dark, and Marguerite's lantern-light couldn't find the top of anything. She held the flame high and it showed her walls instead—walls of white and grey and bone-pale blue, faceted like the inside of a geode the size of a cathedral. Which, she understood slowly, was exactly what this was. Salt had grown here for longer than Vell Hollow had stood above it. It had grown in columns thick as watchtowers and in curtains thin as a liar's promise, and every surface threw their light back doubled, tripled, until there seemed to be ten of them walking down the causeway instead of four.

"Mind where you step," Ysolde said quietly. "It's all floor until it isn't."

Below the causeway, on either side, the salt fell away into terraces, and the terraces were full of light.

Marguerite had imagined vaults. She had imagined cells, chains, iron racks—the Guild's own architecture turned inward, because she had never been able to picture cruelty in any other shape. This was nothing like that. This was beautiful, and the beauty was the worst thing she'd seen in twenty years of selling lies by the charm.

Lanterns. Thousands of them. They hung from hooks of black iron driven into the salt, or floated in slow orbits around one another like moths that had given up on the moon, or sat in rows along the terrace edges as neat as market stalls. Each held a flame. Not fire—she knew fire, she'd warmed her hands over it every night of her life—and not magic, which she'd known too, once. These flames were colors fire had no business being: green like pond-water, amber like old honey, one far off to the left that burned a deep bruised violet. They didn't flicker. They breathed.

"They're souls," Sefa said. His voice came out flattened, the way voices do in very large rooms when the room refuses to give them back. He was clutching his rope—the rope they'd bound him with at the tollgate, the rope he hadn't let them untie since—as if it were the last solid fact in the world. "I read about this. I thought—I assumed the ledgers exaggerated. Ledger-men exaggerate. It's how we stay employed." He swallowed. "They did not exaggerate."

"How many?" Oren asked. The boy's voice, in the old tongue, made no echo at all. The salt drank it.

Ysolde counted the way moon-priestesses count: without hurrying, without finishing. "More than the city admits losing," she said. "Fewer than it should be ashamed of." She touched the small bone whistle at her throat. "Every one of these was somebody's grandmother once."

Oren walked to the causeway's edge and looked down at the nearest lantern—a squat thing of green glass holding a flame no bigger than a thumbnail. He crouched, put his palm flat against the causeway stone, and listened. Marguerite watched his face change. Whatever he heard, it wasn't words. It was something older than words, the way a heartbeat is older than a name.

"She's singing," he said. "Wrong notes. But singing."

"Don't touch the glass," Ysolde said. "Not yet. Nothing here is free, including curiosity."

They went down.

The causeway spiraled, and with each turn the air grew warmer and thicker, until Marguerite could taste what the mountain had been humming since Oren first arrived at her door. Salt. Not the salt of the sea or the salt of sweat but something else—salt as preservation, salt as the thing you rub into a wound so it can't close. Her god-mark ached under her cuff, a low continuous burn she'd almost stopped noticing, the way you stop noticing a tooth gone bad. It had been burning since the tollgate. Since Sefa gave up his name and the mark had flared hard enough to light the dark, one pulse of the old answer, there and gone.

*You're still in there,* she thought at it—at him, at whatever was left of the thing that used to answer her blades. *You've been down here the whole time, haven't you? Witnessing.*

The mark said nothing. It just burned.

* *

They passed a gallery of lanterns arranged in perfect concentric rings, and in the center of the rings stood a salt pillar carved with writing. Sefa drifted toward it like a man sleepwalking, and Marguerite let him—let him get three steps before she caught his sleeve, because nameless men wander, and the rope only works if someone holds the other end.

"I know this script," Sefa said, eyes fixed on the pillar. His voice had gone strange, eager, the archivist surfacing through the fog where his reasons used to be. "This is the contract language. This is the—" He squinted. "This is an index. These are *categories*." He turned to her, and there was horror in his face wearing scholarship's clothing. "They're filed, Marguerite. By debt, by term, by origin. There's a section for voluntary sales. There's a section for *inherited* debts."

"Inherited," Marguerite repeated.

"Villalobos would be—" He stopped himself. He'd been stopping himself from saying the name since chapter and verse of the ledger had come due; a nameless man keeps other people's names carefully, like borrowed tools. "Section nine. Deeper in."

Oren tugged Marguerite's hand. He pointed.

Ahead, the terraces opened into the nave proper, and the nave was dominated by a single structure: a lantern the size of a chapel, suspended from chains that rose into darkness, its glass smoked grey except where thirteen seams of light ran through it like the ribs of a closed fist. Around its base, the largest terraces fanned out in tiers, and the tiers were occupied.

Not by lanterns.

By clerks.

They sat at desks of black iron, dozens of them, ranks upon ranks, quills moving in unison. They wore robes the color of wet ash, and where faces should have been they had smooth salt, featureless as unfired clay, tilted down over their work. The sound of their quills was the sound Marguerite had mistaken for the mountain humming. Scratching. Always scratching. Ledgers being kept. Debts accruing interest even now, even here, even for people who didn't know they owed anything at all.

"The Thirteenth Magistrate holds court in the great lantern," Ysolde murmured. "Or so my order taught, before teaching it became a hanging offense. The clerks are the ones who never got sold whole. Sold piecemeal, piece by piece, until only the part that keeps accounts was left." She smiled, and it was a terrible smile, priestess-calm. "That's what justice looks like when nobody's watching. Bookkeeping."

"We're being watched now," Sefa whispered.

He was right. The quills hadn't stopped—but the smooth faces had lifted, all together, all at once, the way a flock turns. Hundreds of blank salt countenances rotated toward the causeway with a sound like sand shifting, and held.

Marguerite did the thing twenty years of mercenary work had taught her to do when the odds announced themselves: she kept walking.

"Four of us," she said, low, for her people alone. "We're nobody. Charm-seller, exile, priestess, and a boy who talks funny. We're here to make a lawful inquiry about a family debt. That's all we are until we're something else." She glanced at Oren. "Where?"

The boy closed his eyes. His lips moved, tasting the air the way he tasted everything down here, and then he opened them and pointed—not deeper toward the great lantern, but sideways, up, to a side gallery where the lanterns hung in smaller, older clusters. Dusty clusters. Neglected clusters. One cluster where the iron hooks had gone brown with rust, as if whoever tended them had stopped tending long ago and hoped the debt would simply be forgotten.

"Term-expired," Oren said, in the old tongue, and then, laboriously, in Common—the words Sefa had taught him on the road, one per day, like sweets rationed out: *"Past. Due."*

* *

The gallery smelled of dust and cold iron, and the lanterns here burned low, most of them guttering down to embers of themselves. Souls past their term, left to dim. Marguerite walked the row reading the little brass plates riveted beneath each hook—names, dates, sums—and felt something in her chest compress with every plate. These weren't monsters or martyrs. *Hesta Colm, term ended 401.* *Anselm Dree, term ended 398.* People who had paid what they owed and then some, held anyway, because holding was cheaper than releasing and nobody below the mountain had ever once been audited.

Sefa copied plate after plate into the little wax notebook he'd carried since the mine-shafts, his hand steady, his breathing not. "Evidence," he kept saying. "Term, date, original sum. Evidence, evidence. If we get one hearing, if we get one *moment* before that thing in the big lantern, this is—" His quill scratched. Somewhere behind them, distantly, hundreds of other quills scratched back.

Ysolde stood watch at the gallery mouth, her back straight, her shadow long. "Something's coming," she said. "Clerks don't lift their heads for charm-sellers. Keep your hands empty and your mouth shut unless I nod."

Marguerite barely heard her. Because she had found the end of the row.

The last hook was newer than the rest. The iron was black, unworn; the brass plate was bright, recently engraved, and the engraving was not a name. It was a number and a word:

**LOT 9-V. VILALOBOS. HELD PENDING TERM.**

And beneath the plate, in a lantern of plain grey glass, no bigger than a cupped hand, burned a small blue flame.

Blue. Not the green of the pond-soul they'd passed, not the amber, not the bruised violet. Blue the color of deep ice, blue the color of a vein seen through winter skin, blue the color Marguerite had only ever seen one other place in her life: the flash at the heart of her god-mark, on the single night twenty years ago when it had answered her and then gone silent forever.

Her knees nearly went. She caught herself on the causeway rail, and the salt bit her palm, and the pain was good, the pain kept her upright.

"Hello," she said. Her voice cracked in the middle of the word and she hated it and let it crack anyway. "I'm—" And then she stopped, because what was she? She'd never met him. He'd been sold before he drew breath; she'd been born eight years later into a house that never spoke of him, a mother who lit one extra candle at midwinter and called it tradition, a father who went quiet in a way she'd mistaken, her whole childhood, for ordinary tiredness. What did you say to a brother who was a flame? *I'm your sister* meant nothing. *Our parents are dead* meant less. *I came eleven hundred feet under a mountain with a nameless man and a priestess and a stolen child to carry you home*—

She reached out.

The flame flinched.

It pulled away from the glass like a bird slamming against a window—a small, sharp, terrified recoil, pressing itself to the far curve of the lantern, burning smaller. Dimmer. Hiding.

Marguerite's hand hung in the air between them, and the cold of the gallery crawled up her arm, and she understood. Of course it flinched. Everything down here flinched. Twenty years of silence above ground, centuries of ledgers below, and why would a soul trust a reaching hand? Every hand that had ever come near this flame had belonged to a clerk, or a debtor, or a Magistrate's servant coming to check the seal. It didn't know her. It couldn't know her. It had been a *lot number* longer than it had ever been a person, and Lot 9-V had learned exactly one lesson in all its held years:

Hands take.

"It's me," she said uselessly, and her voice was rough and low and it didn't matter, and she hated the whole world for a moment—hated the Villalobos who'd signed, hated the feud that had priced a child's soul at passage-rights, hated the Edict that had silenced the only witness who might have screamed loud enough to stop it. Her god had refused to forget. That was what Sefa had told her, what the ledger implied, what the mark's twenty-year ache had whispered on every mile of road: her god had witnessed the sale and been punished for witnessing, struck dumb by the same law that outlawed magic, and it had spent two decades burning the memory into her wrist anyway so that someday, someday, someone would come down here and say *no.*

Well. She was here. She was saying it.

"Marguerite." Ysolde's voice, quiet and urgent, from the gallery mouth. "*Now.*"

She didn't turn. She leaned closer to the lantern, close enough that her breath fogged the grey glass, close enough that the small blue flame shivered at the warmth of her.

"Listen to me, brother," she said. "I don't know your name. Nobody upstairs ever said it out loud where I could hear. I think our mother knew it. I think she said it to that candle every midwinter for thirty years, and I think that's why I'm standing here instead of her." Her hand came up again, slowly, and pressed flat against the glass—not grabbing, just resting. A hand that stays. "I'm not going to grab. I'm going to steal you fair. There's a difference. Ask anyone I've ever robbed."

The flame had gone very still against the far side of the glass. Still the way prey is still. But it wasn't dimming anymore. It watched her—or did whatever a soul does instead of watching—with the whole of its small blue attention, and Marguerite felt the god-mark on her wrist shift from ache to blaze, a slow rising heat like a banked coal remembering it was ever fire, and she thought: *there you are. You do know me. Some part of you, some part older than the sale, knows exactly whose hand this is.*

"Cold resolve," she murmured, half to the flame, half to herself. "That's what they'll get. No bargaining, no begging, no tears for the Magistrate. Terms. Dates. Signatures. We're leaving through the front of the contract or we're not leaving."

Footsteps behind her—soft, many, wrong. Not boots. The whisper of ash-colored cloth over salt floors.

"Marguerite." Ysolde again, and now the priestess's calm had a blade in it. "It's time. It's *here.*"

Marguerite turned.

At the head of the gallery, where the rusted hooks gave way to clean ones, stood a figure in robes of grey so deep they drank the lantern-light, tall in the way that architecture is tall, and where its face should have been there was a disc of polished obsidian, and in the obsidian she could see herself reflected—small, dusty, hand still pressed to her brother's cage. Around it, filing silently into the gallery's mouth, the clerks arranged themselves into two ranks, quills poised over fresh pages, ready to record.

The Thirteenth Magistrate inclined its obsidian head, courteous as a banker.

"Lot Nine-Vee," it said, and its voice was the sound of a quill scratching, magnified. "A claimant approaches the asset. How *novel.*"

Marguerite took her hand off the glass. Slowly. Deliberately. Letting the flame see her do it.

"Novel," she agreed, and stepped forward to meet it.
