## 13. Terms of Trade

Ysolde had counted moons for thirty years, and she knew a bargain when she heard one the way a sailor knows weather by the ache in his knees.

The Thirteenth Magistrate did not speak in words. It spoke in offers.

They stood in the antechamber beyond the salt cathedral, a round room floored with pale stone worn smooth as river glass, and the Magistrate's presence filled it like water fills a cup—everywhere at once, pressing gently against the walls of everything. It had no face Ysolde could fix her eyes on. When she looked directly at where it stood, she saw instead the things she had lost: her order's rooftop shrine under open sky, the first moon she'd ever counted, her teacher's hands folding a paper lamp. It wore your griefs as clothing and expected you to be flattered.

"The offer," Sefa whispered beside her. He was holding the rope that bound him to Marguerite's belt, both hands wrapped in it like a man holding a rail on a pitching deck. His name was gone but his instincts were not; he still leaned toward documents the way flowers lean toward light. "It's making an offer. Can you feel it?"

She could feel it. Everyone could. The air itself had gone thick with *terms*, the way the air before lightning goes thick with charge.

Marguerite stood with her back straight and her hand resting on Oren's shoulder—not gripping, just resting, an anchor rather than a cage. The boy had gone very still. In the lantern-light from the cathedral behind them, his face was the face of a child listening to music no one else can hear: attentive, troubled, transported.

The Magistrate's offer arrived in Ysolde's mind fully formed, the way dreams arrive, and she hated how reasonable it sounded.

*The flame you came for,* it said, or something beneath saying, *for the vessel that carried it to you. A soul for a soul. A door for a door. This is fair. This has always been fair.*

Her stomach turned over slowly, deliberately, like a body turning in sleep.

Because here was the terrible thing, the thing she would carry out of this mountain if she carried nothing else: for one long breath, it made sense. The boy was a vessel. He had been built—or bred, or bought, or grown in some dark workshop of the deep courts—for exactly this purpose, to carry souls across thresholds. He had walked into Vell Hollow carrying her brother's soul—no, not her brother, Marguerite's brother, the unborn one, the sold one—and now the transaction could complete itself cleanly, symmetrically, the ledger balanced to the last grain.

One soul released after centuries of wrongful imprisonment. One soul surrendered who had only just arrived.

And Oren could not argue. That was the cruelty folded inside the cruelty. The boy opened his mouth and the old tongue poured out of him, urgent, rising, and the Magistrate received it the way a wall receives rain.

"It says," Oren managed, in his broken Common, "it says fair. It says—" He stopped. His jaw worked. Whatever the old tongue had for *this is a trap*, he either didn't know the word or couldn't make himself say it.

"Ysolde." Marguerite's voice was low and even, the voice Ysolde imagined she used on clients who tried to haggle down a protection charm. "You read moons. Read this."

"I'm trying."

"Try faster."

Ysolde closed her eyes. She let her training come up through her like water rising in a well. Thirty years of counting turns alone on a rooftop while an empire outlawed her god—there was a discipline in it, a grammar. The lunar order had never been permitted scripture. They had been permitted only the sky, and so they had learned to read terms written in light and shadow, in waxing and waning, in the fine print of eclipses. Every cycle was a contract. Every contract had clauses.

She opened her eyes and looked down.

The floor of the antechamber was carved. Not decorated—*carved*, dense with characters so shallow they were visible only when the light moved across them, which meant they were meant to be read only by someone standing exactly where Ysolde stood, at exactly this angle, while the great lamps of the cathedral breathed their slow tidal glow. The pre-kingdom tongue ran in spirals from the center outward, and at the center, where the Magistrate's presence pressed hardest, the carving was deepest.

"Sefa," she said. "The floor."

He dropped to his knees so fast the rope snapped taut. His fingers hovered over the stone without touching it, tracing the spirals, and his lips moved. Twenty years exiled for copying forbidden ledgers, and here was a forbidden ledger laid out beneath his hands like a feast.

"This is a contract," he breathed. "This is—the whole floor is a contract. We're standing inside the signature block." He laughed, a small unhinged sound. "I used to dream about documents like this. I used to have *dreams*—"

"Sefa."

"Right. Yes." He crawled two feet along the spiral, following it inward. "The outer ring is boilerplate. Jurisdiction, term of custody, the usual. The middle ring is the offer itself—soul for vessel, door for door, sealed at the thirteenth turn." He stopped crawling. Ysolde watched the color leave his face in real time, draining down through his neck and into the stone.

"What?" Marguerite said. "What does the middle ring say?"

"It says the exchange is *voluntary*." Sefa's voice had gone thin. "That's the word repeated eleven times. Voluntary. Voluntary. It needs the vessel to consent. It cannot simply take him—it needs him to walk to the center of this floor and say yes in the old tongue, and then the trade is lawful and irreversible and *fair*."

"So we don't let him say yes," Marguerite said. "We pick him up and we walk out."

"You can't." Sefa had reached the innermost spiral. He knelt there with his head bowed, and when he spoke again his voice was the voice of a man reading a eulogy. "There's a condition. A rider. If the vessel declines—if the vessel is removed from this chamber before consenting—then the original debt reverts. The Villalobos contract reactivates. The soul in the lantern returns to custody, plus penalty, plus interest, compounded across—" he did arithmetic with his lips, horribly fast, "—across four hundred years. And the debtor line is still extant." He looked up at Marguerite. "You're still extant."

The silence in the antechamber was total. Even the mountain's hum seemed to hold its breath.

Ysolde watched Marguerite absorb it. Watched the mercenary do the same cold math any soldier does when the map turns traitor: every road leads into the ambush. Consent and lose the boy. Refuse and lose the brother. Stand still and the thirteenth turn comes anyway, and the door closes on all of it forever.

"That's not a bargain," Marguerite said finally. "That's a knot."

"It's worse than a knot." Ysolde crouched beside Sefa, because something had been itching at her since he began to read, some wrongness in the rhythm of the spirals, and thirty years of reading the sky had taught her that wrongness in rhythm is where truth hides. "Look at the innermost ring. Look at what it's carved *around*."

Sefa bent close. Then closer. Then he went so still that Ysolde thought for a moment the mountain had taken him too.

"There's a clause underneath the clause," he whispered. "A sub-clause, hidden in the ligatures—they've joined the characters so they read as decoration unless you parse them separately. It's archaic. It's *very* archaic. Even I—" He stopped. He sat back on his heels. "Only someone who speaks it natively could untangle those joins. Only—"

Everyone looked at Oren.

The boy had not moved. He stood in the breathing lamplight with his bare feet on the pale stone, and Ysolde saw him looking down at the floor with an expression she had last seen on the faces of novices who had just realized their teachers were mortal: recognition, and grief, arriving together.

He crouched. He touched the innermost spiral with one finger, and began to read aloud.

His voice in the old tongue was different than it was in Common—in Common he stumbled and gestured, a child building bridges out of driftwood, but in the old tongue he was fluent as falling water. The sounds came out of him and the carvings answered, the shallow lines brightening faintly where his voice passed over them, and Ysolde felt the hair rise on her arms because the floor was not being read.

The floor was being *awakened*.

"What is it saying?" Marguerite asked. "Oren. What does it say?"

The boy read to the end of the spiral. Then he sat back on his heels, exactly mirroring Sefa, and looked up at Marguerite with eyes that held far too many years for his face.

"Not trap," he said carefully, in Common. He searched for words the way a man searches pockets. "Trap is—" he shook his head. "Worse-small-words. Old words." He touched his own chest. "This clause. It says who may consent *for* the vessel. If vessel is... young. Unsworn. Without name given by court." His finger moved to point at the center of the floor, at the deepest carving of all. "Then guardian may consent. Guardian's yes is vessel's yes. Lawful. Fair. Binding."

Ysolde felt the meaning land in the room like a dropped blade.

"And who counts as guardian?" Marguerite asked, very quietly.

Oren looked at her. Held her gaze. And did not answer, which was itself an answer, and Ysolde watched the mercenary understand that the Magistrate had not built this trap for the boy at all.

It had built it for her.

All she had to do—all the mountain required of her, all its patient law demanded—was one word of consent, spoken in love, in protectiveness, in the fierce maternal thing that had been growing in her since a barefoot child appeared at her door. The Magistrate did not need Oren's yes. It needed *hers*. It had arranged the whole architecture of the bargain so that the most honest feeling in her chest would be the instrument of her ruin.

"Oh," Ysolde said softly. "Oh, it's good. It's very good." She meant it as condemnation. It came out as something like awe.

"Can it be broken?" Marguerite asked. Her hand had found Oren's shoulder again. "The clause. The whole floor. There's always a flaw—every contract has one, that's how I make my living."

"Every *human* contract," Sefa murmured. But he was already bending back to the stone, and Ysolde crouched with him, and together the disgraced archivist and the last priestess followed the spirals inward, hunting the flaw the way you hunt a leak in a roof at night—by sound, by feel, by faith that it exists.

It was Oren who found it. Or perhaps it was the flaw that found Oren, because when he spoke again it was not in Common and not quite in the old tongue either, but in the space between, and the carvings flickered like a lamp guttering.

He pointed at a single character at the exact center of the floor, so worn it was nearly smooth, older than all the rest.

"That one," he said. "First-written. Before the rest." He looked up, and for the first time since the antechamber, since the cathedral, since the long dark road beneath the mountain, he smiled—a small, fierce, knowing smile that had no childhood left in it and all the childhood in the world. "It says the court must keep its own terms. First law. Older than Magistrate." His finger tapped the worn character twice. "And the Villalobos debt—"

He stopped. Looked at Marguerite. Looked at the lantern-glow spilling from the cathedral behind them, where a small blue flame waited, flinching at shadows.

"—ended," he finished. "Long ago. The floor knows. The floor has always known." He drew a breath. "But the floor cannot say it. Only witness can."

Ysolde rose slowly to her feet. Beneath her wrist, against her pulse, she felt the faint answering warmth of the mark she had carried since her ordination—the old lunar sigil, outlawed, faded, alive. Thirty years of counting turns, and every one of them had led here, to this floor, to this clause, to this single worn word older than the Magistrate itself.

The court demanded a witness.

And somewhere above them, silent for twenty years, there was a god who had refused to forget.

"We don't trade," Ysolde said. Her voice came out steady, and it surprised her, and then it didn't, because it was not entirely her voice; it was thirty years of vigil speaking through her. "We don't consent, and we don't refuse. We put the contract itself on trial."

Marguerite turned to look at her, and behind her the Magistrate's presence shifted in the antechamber like a tide changing its mind, and somewhere in the cathedral of salt, a small blue flame lifted toward the sound of her voice.

* * *

The Magistrate's attention settled over them like snowfall—soft, total, inescapable.

*Terms,* it offered again, gentler now, almost tender. *The turn wanes. Fairness waits.*

"Yes," Ysolde said aloud, to the air, to the floor, to the ancient worn word at the center of it. "Let's talk about fairness. Let's talk about it in front of everyone."

The lamplight in the cathedral behind them guttered once, all together, like a held breath.

And in the silence that followed, Marguerite rolled back her sleeve, and the god-mark on her wrist began—faintly, impossibly, for the first time in twenty years—to burn.
