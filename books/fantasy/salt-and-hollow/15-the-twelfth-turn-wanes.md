## 15. The Twelfth Turn Wanes

The half-forgotten village had a name once. Ysolde said it was called Merrow's Bend, back when names still stuck to things like burrs. Now the people there went by what was left of them—*the woman with the missing vowel*, *the boy who'd sold his laughter*, *old Tam who had no first name at all and answered to the shape of a hand raised in greeting*. They lived in houses built of river stone and salvaged mine timber, and the walls hummed faintly, day and night, with all the syllables that had been carved out of them.

Marguerite sat on a bench outside the largest house—a longhall, really, its roof patched with slate and hope—and watched Sefa Ilvane draw.

He drew on anything. On the dirt with a stick, on the tabletop with charcoal stubs the village children brought him like offerings, on his own forearm when nothing else was near. He didn't remember why he'd come down the mountain. He didn't remember Marguerite's name, or Oren's, or his own. The tollgate wraith had taken it all in one clean bite: *Sefa Ilvane, archivist of the third rank, exiled for copying forbidden ledgers*—gone, swallowed, and what remained was a tall man with ink-stained fingers and an expression of permanent mild puzzlement, like a scholar perpetually arriving late to his own lecture.

But he remembered ledger-craft.

It was in his hands now, the way a swordsman keeps the forms after the war ends. He'd been drawing for two hours without stopping: the vault beneath the Thirteenth Magistrate's dais, rendered from memory in lines so fine they looked like spider silk. Concentric rings of ward-script. Lantern-hooks arranged in a pattern that wasn't decorative—it was arithmetic. A door drawn twice, once as it appeared and once as it truly was, which was to say as a sentence written very large.

"That one," he said, tapping the innermost ring. His voice had gone strange since the gate—not empty, exactly, but unanchored, a voice that kept checking over its shoulder for its owner. "That ring is a counting-ward. It counts souls. Anything living passes through it, it ticks. Anything dead, it doesn't." He frowned at his own drawing as if it had been left by a stranger whose work he admired. "I think I stole this memory from myself before we crossed the gate. I don't remember doing it. But here it is."

"You wrote it on your arm," Oren said.

They'd taught him enough Common in the tunnels—Ysolde had been relentless about it, drilling him with salt-cakes as rewards—to make himself understood, though he slipped into the old tongue when he was tired or frightened, and sometimes both at once. He touched Sefa's forearm, where the ward-diagram curled around a note in the archivist's own handwriting: *FOR THE ONE WHO CAME WITH ME. YOU ARE SAFE. KEEP WALKING.*

"I did?" Sefa turned his arm over, examined it. "Clever fellow. I wonder where he learned that."

Marguerite looked away. There was something unbearable about watching a man read his own last will and testament without knowing he'd written it.

Ysolde came out of the longhall carrying a covered basket, and behind her came the woman with the missing vowel, moving slowly, one hand pressed to her side as if holding something in.

"Well?" Marguerite asked.

Ysolde set the basket down. Inside, packed in wool, lay a lantern. Not salt-glass like the court's—ordinary tin and horn, the kind farmers used—but inside it burned a flame the color of old honey, and it moved wrong, the way a trapped thing moves. It pressed against the horn panes. It watched them.

"She agrees," Ysolde said quietly. "Her name was Ansette. What's left of it. She sold—" the priestess stopped, started again. "She sold pieces of herself across forty years. Her mother's lullabies. The smell of bread. The name of the road she grew up on. And finally this—the oldest part, the part that remembers being a child before any of it. She says she can't miss what she can't remember wanting."

The flame flared against the glass, and Marguerite would have sworn, would have bet her last coin, that it was listening.

"And the price?"

"Nothing." Ysolde's mouth twisted. "That's the horror of it, isn't it? She won't take payment because she doesn't believe she's giving anything away. The under-court taught these people their own souls are small change." She put her hand flat on the basket lid, gently, the way you'd calm a horse. "I told her about your brother. About Oren. I told her the truth—that we mean to steal a soul out of the Magistrate's own vault, and that if it goes wrong, the whole village could pay for it. She said—" Ysolde closed her eyes. "She said, *let them learn my fire by heart.*"

Marguerite stood up. Walked to the edge of the bench, looked down the bend of the river toward the mountain, which even from here made itself known—a pressure on the horizon, a silence shaped like a peak. One turn left. The moon above them was already thinning, a paring of bone in the afternoon sky, and every hour it shed was an hour off the thirteenth turn, off the closing of the door, off her brother's soul going past saving forever.

"Then we plan it," she said. "All of it. Every step, every failure point. We get one try."

* * *

They planned it in the longhall, around a table scarred by generations of knives, while the villagers drifted in and out bringing food none of them quite knew how to eat—food that tasted of almost, of nearly, of words just out of reach.

Sefa drew the approach first. "The court has three mouths," he said, sketching fast, sure-handed. "The great stair we came by. The service galleries where the lantern-tenders walk. And—" he paused, and Marguerite saw him reach for something, strain, and find it waiting, pre-planted, like a coin under a cup. "And the ash-chute. Where the burnt-out contracts go. It runs from the judgment floor down to the deep furnaces, but there's a maintenance climb near the top. I know because—" he laughed, short and lost. "I don't know why I know. But I know."

"The chute," Marguerite said. "Lantern-tenders don't expect anyone coming up through the garbage."

"No one comes up through the garbage," Sefa agreed solemnly. "That is its entire virtue."

Oren leaned over the diagram. He'd gone quiet in the way he did when the old tongue was rising in him, his lips moving around shapes the rest of them couldn't hold. When he spoke, it was in Common, careful as a child crossing ice. "The counting-ring. If we carry the decoy in, it ticks. The Magistrate knows how many souls are in its vault. It counts them the way you count your own teeth."

"So we don't carry it in," Marguerite said. "We carry it *out*." She put her finger on the vault's outer wall. "The swap happens inside. We go in light. My brother's flame comes off its hook, the decoy goes on. Same weight, same burn-color, same size. The counting-ring never sees the decoy enter because it never enters—it's already home."

"It has to look right," Ysolde said. "Not just burn right. Souls in that vault are catalogued. Labeled. There'll be a contract-tag on the hook, a ledger-line naming what's held."

"Sefa," Marguerite said.

Sefa was already drawing. His pen moved like it was remembering for him. "Contract-tags are standardized. Court-hand, three registers: the debtor's line, the term of holding, and the seal of the Magistrate who accepted it." He sketched one, then another, then a third, each more precise than the last. "I can forge the tag. I can forge it better than the court can write it, because the court writes ten thousand and I have spent twenty years writing only the forbidden ones." He said it without pride, the way you'd report the weather. Then, softer: "Whoever I was, he was very good at this. I hope he'd be pleased."

"He'd be insufferable," Marguerite said, and Sefa smiled like a man given a gift he couldn't identify.

Oren's role was the one nobody wanted to say aloud, so Marguerite said it. "You sing the wards asleep. All of them, on our way in and on our way out. The counting-ring too."

The boy shook his head. "Not asleep. Asleep is a lie the ring will notice. I sing it *busy*. I give it a number to count that takes forever. A song has notes; notes can be counted; a song that circles can be counted forever." He looked up at her, and in the lamplight his eyes had that depth the old tongue gave them, wells with something moving far down. "My mother sang me one. I thought it was a lullaby. It is a lullaby. It is also a very large number."

Marguerite's chest did something complicated. She nodded instead of speaking.

"And the extraction," Ysolde said. "The moment the flame leaves the hook, the vault knows. Even busy, even fooled—the Magistrate is not a lock, Marguerite. It is a mind. Locks sleep. Minds notice."

"Then we're gone before it finishes noticing." Marguerite laid it out, step by step, and heard her own mercenary voice come back to her—flat, unhurried, the voice she'd used to brief crews on jobs where hesitation killed. "Ash-chute up. Oren sings us through the galleries. Vault door—Sefa, your forged tag gets us past the tender's checkpoint?"

"Tenders don't check tags entering," Sefa said. "Only leaving. Tags are for inventory, not admission. The court trusts its walls." His pen paused. "Which is the second-most useful arrogance in creation."

"And the first?"

"Believing no one will ever invoke the old terms." He looked at her then, straight on, and though his eyes held no memory of her, they held something else—recognition of a kind, archivist to oath-bearer. "You carry an oath-mark older than the Edict. When the time comes, the law in that room will have to listen to you. Make sure the time comes."

* * *

They drilled it until the candles drowned in their own wax.

Walk the chute-climb blindfolded, hands learning the rungs. Count the paces between gallery turns—forty-one, forty-one, always forty-one, Sefa's memory precise as scripture. Oren practiced his endless song in whispers until Ysolde, listening with her head bowed, pronounced it *large enough*. Marguerite rehearsed lifting a weighted horn-lantern off a hook and replacing it in under four seconds, over and over, until her hands stopped shaking and started simply working.

The decoy flame sat in its basket through all of it, burning steady, pressing at the glass whenever Oren came near. Once, passing the table, the boy stopped and put his palm against the horn pane, and the flame leaned into it like a cat.

"What does it feel like," Marguerite asked him quietly, "being near it? Knowing what it is."

Oren considered for a long time. "Like hearing someone hum through a wall," he said. "You don't know the song. But you know they're there."

On the third night, the woman called Ansette came to the longhall doorway while they worked. She stood with her hand on the frame, watching the basket, and Marguerite rose to meet her, ready to give the flame back, ready to find another way, ready for whatever a person asks for when they've sold everything sellable.

Ansette didn't ask for anything. She looked at the lantern a long moment, and her face moved through expressions that couldn't finish themselves—grief with a syllable missing, love with a syllable missing—and then she reached out and straightened the wool blanket over the basket, tucking it in at the corners, the way you'd settle a child.

"Tell it," she said, in a voice with a hole in the middle of it, "that the road home is the one with the bread smell. If it ever gets loose. Tell it that."

"I'll tell it," Marguerite said.

Ansette nodded once and walked back into the dark between the houses, humming something that kept stopping, like a wheel with a broken spoke.

* * *

They left on the eve of the twelfth turn's ending, walking up the river-road in the blue hour before dawn, the mountain growing ahead of them until it stopped being scenery and became a fact. Ysolde carried the basket. Sefa carried his drawings rolled in oilcloth, and his own forearm, and no name at all. Oren walked in the middle, where they'd put him, though Marguerite suspected the boy protected them more than they protected him.

At the treeline below the old mine-mouths, she called the halt and took stock the way she had taken stock before every job of her mercenary life: blades sharp, water full, plan spoken aloud one final time, each of them answering *ready* in turn.

When it came to her own answer, she found it wouldn't come out as *ready*. What came out, looking up at the black mass of the mountain, at the thin moon fading into the pale sky above it, was quieter and truer:

"One more turn," she said. "Hold on, little brother. I'm coming to carry you home."

Far above, at the summit, where no light should have been, something flickered—once, twice—like a lamp being counted.

And in the basket, against the horn, the decoy flame began, very softly, to scream.
