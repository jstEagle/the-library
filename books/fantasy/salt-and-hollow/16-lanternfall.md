## 16. Lanternfall

The salt cathedral breathed.

Marguerite felt it in her teeth before she saw it—a slow tidal rhythm in the air of the vault antechamber, as if the whole mountain inhaled and exhaled on some clock older than moons. The walls were not walls so much as compressed centuries, veins of white and amber salt running through black stone like frozen lightning. And everywhere, everywhere, light.

Lanterns hung from the ceiling by chains that vanished upward into dark. Hundreds of them. Thousands. Each one held a flame that was not fire—flames that moved wrong, that leaned toward passersby like flowers toward sun, that flickered in patterns too deliberate to be chance. Souls in their thousands, banked and shelved and catalogued, the wealth of a court that had spent twenty generations buying what people could not afford to lose.

"Ledger says the Villalobos flame is in the Ninth Row," Sefa whispered. His voice had a hollow quality now, like wind through an empty house. Since the tollgate he spoke carefully, testing each sentence for load-bearing walls. He still remembered how to read contracts. He no longer remembered why he'd wanted to. The rope around his waist ran back to Ysolde's fist, and she paid it out hand over hand like a sailor who did not trust the sea. "Ninth Row, Third Lantern, marked with a debt-seal. Blue flame, small. It will be cold to the touch."

"Cold," Marguerite repeated. Her brother's soul, and the archivist's instruction was *it will be cold*. Twenty years of silence, a family's buried sin, a child sent down the mountain as currency—and when she finally held what remained of him, it would feel like holding a stone from a riverbed.

She found she was angry about the coldness more than anything else. Anger was useful. Anger kept her hands steady.

Above them, through a seam in the vault's ceiling, the great lamps burned—the Thirteenth Magistrate's own lights, seven flames the size of bonfires that lit the entire under-court. No shadow had ever fallen in this place. That was the problem. You could not steal anything in a room where darkness had been outlawed.

Which was why Ysolde stood at the center of the antechamber with her arms raised and her face tilted up toward the seam, looking less like a priestess than a woman about to commit blasphemy on a scale the world had not seen since the Edict.

"Moon-mother," she said quietly. Not a prayer. An introduction. "I'm going to borrow your other face."

She reached into the satchel at her hip and drew out a stoppered jar of ordinary clay, unremarkable except that looking at it made Marguerite's eyes water, the way staring at a solar eclipse does even through closed lids. Consecrated dark. Ysolde had spent three days making it in the tunnels—three days of chanting over a sealed jar while the others slept, pouring the memory of every moonless night she had ever kept vigil through into something small enough to hold in one hand.

"The wards first," Ysolde said. "Oren."

The boy stepped forward. In the soul-light his scars looked silver, and his eyes had that fixed, distant quality they got when he was listening to something below the range of human ears. He had barely spoken since the half-forgotten village. He had barely slept. But when Ysolde nodded at him, he squared his small shoulders and walked to the base of the nearest ward-stone—a pillar of veined salt taller than two men, carved top to bottom with script that predated every kingdom Marguerite could name.

And he began to sing.

It was not a melody, or not only one. It was the old tongue given voice the way it was meant to be given voice—not words laid in rows but a single sustained chord that seemed to come from Oren's chest and the stone's chest at once. Marguerite's god-mark flared hot against her wrist, and she pressed her palm over it, half to smother it, half because the warmth was the closest thing to comfort she'd felt in twenty years.

The carvings on the ward-stone began to dim.

Not all at once. Line by line, the way windows go dark in a great house at nightfall, each glyph guttering out as Oren's song reached it. The stone's hum—it had been humming since they entered the antechamber, a note so low Marguerite had mistaken it for the mountain itself—slid downward, softened, thickened, became something drowsy and slow.

*Sing them asleep,* Ysolde had told him. *Wards are promises, and promises are only awake when someone is keeping them.*

Oren sang the keeping to sleep.

One pillar. Then, moving along the row with the patience of a child doing chores, a second. A third. Sefa counted under his breath, following the route he'd memorized from the vault plans carved into the mine-shafts forty years ago by an archivist who'd never dreamed anyone would use them. "Left at the fourth pillar. The Ninth Row is behind the magistrate's dais—there's a service corridor, old, pre-court, the salt-cutters used it before the Magistrate claimed the mountain."

"And the great lamps?" Marguerite asked.

"When the last ward sleeps." Ysolde turned the jar over in her hands. "Then we do the unforgivable thing."

* * *

The last ward went quiet as a snuffed candle, and Oren sagged against the stone, breathing hard, and Ysolde pulled the stopper from the jar.

There was no explosion. There was no sound at all. That was the horror of it—the consecrated dark came out of the jar like water poured from a pitcher, but upward, defying everything, streaming through the seam in the ceiling into the cathedral above. And where it touched the great lamps—

They didn't go out. They were *unmade*. Seven flames the size of bonfires, souls or things older than souls, and the darkness drank them without ceremony, the way the sea takes a dropped coin. One. Three. Five. The cathedral's eternal noon collapsed inward, shadows blooming for the first time in living memory, long and soft and utterly alien, and Marguerite heard—distantly, from somewhere deep in the court—the first sounds of alarm. Voices. Not human voices. The scrape of something vast turning over in its sleep.

"Now," Ysolde said, and her voice shook, and she did not pretend it didn't. "We have minutes. Perhaps fewer."

They ran.

Sefa led, rope trailing, counting turns in a flat archivist's drone that was somehow steadier than any battle cry. Left at the fourth pillar. Through the service corridor, low-ceilinged, rough-walled, cut by hands that had harvested salt here before there was a court to forbid them. Marguerite carried the decoy.

The decoy was a lantern of cheap tin, bought three nights ago from a woman in the half-forgotten village whose name had been sold away syllable by syllable until only "-eth" remained. Inside it burned a soul the color of weak tea—a fragment, the woman had said, the piece of her grandmother that remembered lullabies. She had sold it to them for a promise: that when this was done, someone would write her name back together again. Marguerite had made the promise because it was the price of passage, and because she was beginning to understand that in Vell Hollow, beneath every transaction, there was always another debt waiting.

The Ninth Row opened before them like a throat.

Shelves of lanterns rose into darkness on either side, and in the new-made shadow the flames were visible as they never could have been under the great lamps—leaning, straining, every soul in the vault turning toward the intruders like moths toward the one light left in the world. Marguerite's skin crawled. She had sold dead charms to desperate people for twenty years and told herself it was honest work compared to this. She had been lying to herself on an industrial scale.

"Third lantern," Sefa whispered. "Debt-seal. There—" His lamp-hand shook. "Villalobos."

The seal was a stamp in the tin, centuries old, worn nearly smooth: a coiled serpent biting its own tail, and beneath it letters in a script Marguerite recognized from her father's one precious book, the family name rendered in the spelling of people who had still believed the future belonged to them.

*Villalobos.*

She set down the decoy lantern with hands that would not quite obey her. Lifted her brother's lantern off its hook.

Cold. Sefa had been right. The tin was cold as grave-goods, and inside, the flame was small and blue and it shrank from her—actually shrank, pressing itself against the far side of the glass like a startled bird pressed against the back of a cage, like a thing that had learned over long centuries that hands reaching toward it meant nothing good.

"Oh," Marguerite said. Just that. Just a single syllable that contained everything she couldn't say. *Oh, little brother. Oh, what they did to you. Oh, how long you've been alone in the dark with your cold tin walls and your fear of hands.*

Her mark blazed. And the blue flame—hesitated. Leaned a fraction toward the warmth, the way a freezing man leans toward a hearth, then flinched back, then leaned again, caught between two ancient lessons: *hands hurt* and *warmth is life*.

"It knows the mark," Ysolde breathed. "Marguerite. It knows the blood."

"We'll have time for reunions upstairs." Marguerite's voice came out rougher than she intended. She wrapped the lantern in the wool cloth Sefa provided, and even through the wool she felt it—a faint, tentative warmth blooming against her wrist where the mark burned. "Swap it. Now. Gently."

She took up the decoy lantern. Reached for the empty hook.

The decoy screamed.

Later, Marguerite would try to describe the sound and fail every time. It was not loud, exactly. It was *total*—a scream that occupied every frequency at once, that came not from the tin lantern but from the fragment of grandmother-soul inside it, the lullaby-remembering piece of a dead woman suddenly lifted out of the hands that had promised to protect her and thrust into a debt-sealed cage in a vault of stolen souls, and whatever passed for a soul's understanding, it understood betrayal.

The scream hit the sleeping wards like a thrown stone hits a window.

Above them, through the vault's high dark, a glyph flared back to life. Then another. The wards woke in a cascade, pillar after pillar, the old tongue blazing crimson on the salt, and the hum that Oren had sung to sleep roared back up out of the floor like a beast remembering its anger. Somewhere in the cathedral, a bell that had never rung began to ring—a sound like a verdict being read aloud in a language of pure procedure.

"No no no—" Sefa grabbed the decoy lantern, hushed it, rocked it like a colicky infant, an archivist soothing a stolen soul with the only lullabies his nameless mind could still produce, fragments of songs he didn't remember learning. The screaming dulled to a keening. Too late. Far too late.

Lights were coming. Down the Ninth Row, around the dais, from corridors Marguerite hadn't known existed—lantern-light and torchlight and worse than both, a pale luminescence that moved with intention. Shapes resolving out of the dark. Clerks of the court, if clerks were made of shadow and ledger-paper. Bailiffs, if bailiffs had too many joints and moved like marionettes with the puppeteer's hand showing.

And beneath everything, rising through the salt floor, through Marguerite's boots, through the soles of her feet and up her spine and into the mark on her wrist, a voice like a mountain clearing its throat.

*"WHO DISTURBS THE LEDGER?"*

Ysolde's hand found Marguerite's shoulder. Oren pressed against her side, small and shaking, and Marguerite shifted the wrapped lantern to hold it against her heart with one arm while the other hand went to the blade at her hip—an old habit, a useless one, steel against a court of law and shadow.

Twenty years ago she had chosen survival over faith. Standing in the Ninth Row with her brother's cold soul warming against her wrist and the whole waking wrath of the under-court converging on the sound of a grandmother's grief, Marguerite Villalobos understood, with perfect clarity, that she was done choosing survival.

"Sefa," she said. "The contract. Tell me you remember the terms."

The nameless archivist looked up at her, and though he could no longer remember why he had come, his eyes were bright with the only thing the tollgate hadn't taken.

"I remember *everything else*," he said.
