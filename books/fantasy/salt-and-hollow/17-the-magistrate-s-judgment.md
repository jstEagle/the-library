## 17. The Magistrate's Judgment

The decoy soul's scream had not stopped.

It filled the salt cathedral the way water fills a lung—everywhere, in everything, vibrating in Marguerite's teeth, in the lantern-glass of a thousand caged flames that flickered and pressed against their prisons like moths against a window. Above them, along the vaulted ribs of the cavern, the great lamps guttered where Ysolde's consecrated dark still clung to them, but it was burning off, thinning, and through the gaps the Thirteenth Magistrate's light came down cold and absolute.

They were cornered. She could feel it in the geometry of the room: the doors they'd come through sealed behind them like healed skin, the wards reawakening in slow spirals across the floor, Oren on his knees with his hands over his ears because the screaming soul was screaming in a language only he could hear. Sefa stood beside him with the rope still knotted around his own wrist—he didn't know why he was holding the other end, but he was holding it, nameless and steady as furniture. Ysolde had her arms raised, feeding the last of her dark into the lamps, and her lips were moving in a prayer that had been outlawed for twenty years and was working anyway, barely.

And Marguerite Villalobos stood in the center of the court beneath the mountain with a stolen flame cupped in both hands—a small blue fire that flinched from her even now, even here, like a bird that had never once been held by anything kind—and she understood, with the flat clarity that had kept her alive through two decades of bad contracts and worse employers, that steel was finished.

There was nothing left to cut. The Magistrate had no throat. The Inquisitors were a city away. Every blade she owned was worth exactly nothing in a cathedral built out of law.

So she did the thing she had sworn never to do again, the thing she'd buried so deep she'd almost forgotten the shape of it.

She reached for the oath.

* * *

It lived under her mark. That was the thing no one told you about sworn mercenary marks—they weren't tattoos, they weren't brands, they were contracts, inked into the skin in the old way, in the days when gods witnessed such things and magic made the ink mean something. Hers had gone dark and silent with everything else after the Edict, just a gray scar on her wrist that ached when it rained. She had spent twenty years telling herself it was dead weight, a relic, a debt owed to a creditor who'd left town.

But Sefa had said it once, back in his exile-cellar full of forbidden paper, in the tone he reserved for things carved in stone: *A contract doesn't die because one party stops speaking. It dies when its terms are fulfilled.*

Her god hadn't spoken in twenty years. But the contract—

The contract had never been fulfilled. Not by her. Not by anyone.

Marguerite lifted her head and looked up at the Thirteenth Magistrate.

It was not a body. She had known that going in, but knowing and seeing were different animals. It was a shape the light made when the light was very old and very tired of being lawful—a tall absence at the far end of the cathedral, robed in the same salt that built the walls, wearing a face like a verdict waiting to be read. Around it, suspended in rings, hung the instruments of its office: scales without pans, a gavel of black stone, a ledger open to a page so old the vellum had gone the color of teeth.

"Magistrate." Her voice came out rough. She cleared her throat and started over, and this time she put twenty years of market-stall haggling and siege-line shouting into it, made it carry the way it carried across a battlefield. "I invoke the Rite of Standing Claim."

The screaming faltered. Just for a heartbeat—the whole cathedral seemed to lean toward her, the way a courtroom leans when someone says something either very stupid or very dangerous.

Ysolde's prayer stuttered. "Marguerite—" the priestess breathed, low, urgent, "*what are you doing?*"

"Buying us a door," Marguerite said, and did not look away from the Magistrate, because she remembered—gods, she remembered, from another life, from before the Edict, from the years when she'd been young and devout and stupid enough to swear herself to a dying order—that you never looked away from a judge while your claim was on the floor. "By the terms of my oath, sworn and marked and never discharged: I claim the right of audience. I claim the right of terms. I claim judgment under the original contract."

The silence that followed had texture. It had weight. It pressed down on the cathedral like snowfall.

Then the Magistrate moved—or rather, the light rearranged itself into the suggestion of movement, the way shadows suggest a figure in a doorway—and when it spoke, its voice was not a voice at all but the sound of a page turning somewhere very far below the world.

***STANDING IS CLAIMED. THE STANDING IS HEARD.***

Oren's hands came slowly down from his ears. He looked up at her, and there was something in his face she hadn't seen before—not fear, not betrayal's residue, but recognition. He knew these words. They were his words, or close cousins of them, the pre-kingdom tongue wearing legal dress.

Sefa, nameless, drifting, tugged the rope on his wrist like a man checking a mooring line, and said, faintly, in the voice of a man reading aloud from memory he no longer understood: "Rite of Standing Claim. Pre-Edict commercial law. Older than the kingdoms. Older than the Guild." His brow furrowed. "I don't know why I know that."

"You copied ledgers for thirty years," Ysolde said. "It's in your bones if nowhere else."

"It's in the bones of this place," Marguerite said. "That's what matters."

She walked forward. Every ward-spiral on the floor woke as she crossed it, threads of cold light crawling up around her ankles, testing her, tasting her intent—and then, one by one, finding the oath-mark on her wrist and *stopping*, recoiling like snakes from a brand. Because the mark was a contract too. Because whatever else had died in Vell Hollow, contract-law ran deeper than Edicts, deeper than kingdoms, deeper than the mountain itself.

The Magistrate waited for her at the foot of its dais of salt, and up close it was worse and better than from afar—worse because the facelessness went all the way down, better because she could see now that it was *bound*. Chains of script ran through its robes, through its light, clauses and subclauses and provisos written in the pre-kingdom tongue, and the Magistrate wore them the way a man wears a debt he can never pay off.

Good, Marguerite thought. Good. You're not the law. You're just the oldest clerk in the building.

"I have a claim," she said.

***STATE IT.***

"The Villalobos line entered contract with this court four generations past." Her voice held. She was almost proud of it. "The terms are a matter of record. A soul, given in payment, against a blood-feud discharged and safe passage guaranteed in perpetuity. My family paid. The feud ended. My family walked free." She drew a breath. "The debt was paid in full."

***THE DEBT WAS PAID IN FULL,*** the Magistrate agreed, and the agreement came down like a hammer, like a fact, like gravity.

Ysolde made a small sound. Sefa's rope-hand tightened. Oren had risen to his feet, and his eyes were wide and wet and fixed on her.

"Then explain to me," Marguerite said, and here it was, the whole fight, the whole war, compressed into one sentence, the sentence she'd carried up from the archive and through the tunnels and into the heart of the mountain, "why you are still holding the collateral."

* * *

The cathedral went very quiet.

Not empty-quiet. *Procedural* quiet. Marguerite had seen it before, in guild halls and magistrate courts above ground, in the frozen moment after a lawyer says the thing everyone in the room has been pretending not to know. The moment where the machinery of law, which does not care about mercy or truth or anything except its own smooth operation, encounters a gear that does not fit.

***THE SOUL IS HELD AS ASSURANCE,*** the Magistrate said.

"Against what?" Marguerite asked. "Name the contingency. Name the clause. Show me the term under which a paid debt entitles the creditor to retain the payment beyond term."

***THE CONTRACT PROVIDES—***

"The contract provides," Marguerite said, "for return of the soul upon discharge of the debtor's obligations. I've read it. Or rather—" she jerked her chin at Sefa, at the rope, at the hollowed-out man who had given his name to a wraith so she could stand here, "—he read it, before he gave away the part of himself that remembered reading it, and he transcribed every word, and those words are carved into the mine-shafts three levels above your head in stone that will outlast your cathedral. Article Nine. Term of holding: until discharge. Discharge occurred four generations ago. Four generations, Magistrate. Your own ledger will show it. Your own ledger *must* show it, because you are bound to keep accurate accounts—are you not?"

The last two words cracked like a whip, and the Magistrate's light flinched.

Flinched. The oldest clerk in the building, flinching, because there was one law older than it, one rule stitched into the bedrock of the world before the world had names: *a keeper of accounts must keep true accounts.*

***THE COURT ACKNOWLEDGES,*** the Magistrate said, and each word cost it something; she could hear the grinding, the terrible bureaucratic agony of a creature built entirely of procedure confronting an error in its own books, ***THAT THE TERM OF HOLDING HAS EXPIRED.***

"Then convene judgment," Marguerite said. "Formal. On the record. By the Rite of Standing Claim, invoked under oath-mark, witnessed." She turned her wrist outward, the gray scar facing the light. "You are required to provide a witness of standing. And I am providing one."

***THERE IS NO WITNESS HERE WITH STANDING,*** the Magistrate said. ***YOUR GOD IS SILENT. ALL GODS ARE SILENT. THE EDICT—***

"The Edict," Marguerite said, "is a law of the city above. This is not the city above."

And she held out her arm, and she did the hardest thing she had done since the boy arrived at her door: she prayed. Out loud. In front of the enemy. After twenty years of nothing, after the shame of chapter nine of her life that she would never fully scrub out, after choosing survival over faith on the night the Edict passed and every sworn blade in Vell Hollow pretended not to hear their gods go quiet—she stood in the dark under the mountain and prayed like a rookie, like a believer, like a woman with nothing left to lose, which was the purest form of prayer there was.

*I don't ask you to speak,* she thought, hard, at the gray scar, at whatever might or might not be listening. *I don't ask you to forgive me. I ask you to witness. That was always the job. That was the first job. Witness.*

The mark did not answer.

The mark *burned*.

* * *

Light poured off her wrist—not white, not gold, but the color of a promise being kept, a color she had no name for and suspected no language had ever bothered naming because it appeared so rarely. It ran up her arm, across her shoulder, down her spine, and where it touched the salt walls the salt sang, a low harmonic chord that made the thousand caged souls flare in their lanterns like a congregation catching fire with one shared thought.

Oren gasped. Ysolde dropped to her knees—not in defeat, in *worship*, the first worship she'd been able to perform in twenty years, tears cutting clean lines down her face. Even Sefa, nameless, turned toward the light with an expression of dumb animal longing, the way a man who has lost his name still knows warmth when it touches him.

And the Magistrate—bound, chained in its own clauses, older than kingdoms and servant to something older still—bowed its faceless head.

***WITNESS IS PRESENT,*** it said. ***WITNESS CONFIRMS: THE TERM HAS EXPIRED. THE SOUL KNOWN AS [the sound that followed was not a word but a feeling, brief and blue and small] IS HELD PAST TERM. JUDGMENT MUST BE CONVENED.***

"Then convene it," Marguerite said, and her voice shook now, finally, because the light was still pouring off her and it felt like being remembered by something enormous. "Full formality. I want it on the record."

***JUDGMENT IS CONVENED.***

The black gavel fell without being lifted. The scales turned without weights. The ancient ledger flipped its own pages back, back, back, four generations, to an entry in faded brown ink, and the whole cathedral read it together—the party, the Magistrate, the thousand imprisoned flames—instantaneously, the way law reads, all at once and forever:

*Villalobos. One soul, tendered. Feud discharged. Passage granted. Term of holding: until discharge. Discharged.*

***FINDING,*** said the Magistrate, and the word rang through the salt like a bell through a nave, ***THE SOUL IS RELEASED FROM HOLDING. THE CREDITOR SHALL SURRENDER THE FLAME TO THE CLAIMANT. THE ACCOUNTS ARE MADE TRUE.***

The nearest lantern unsealed itself.

The little blue flame rose out of it slowly, hesitantly, the way a bird leaves a cage whose door has been open for years—hovering, trembling, not believing. Marguerite held out her cupped hands, and the light from her mark wrapped the flame gently, and for the first time it did not flinch from her. It drifted down into her palms and sat there, warm, impossibly warm, warmer than any fire had a right to be, and she felt it feel her—felt it recognize, dimly, distantly, the way you recognize a house you lived in before you could walk.

"Hello," she whispered, in Common, in the old tongue, in every language she had. "I'm your sister. I'm taking you home."

***THE JUDGMENT IS RENDERED,*** the Magistrate said. ***BUT.***

Every candle in the cathedral guttered at once.

***LAW IS SYMMETRY,*** the Magistrate said, and its voice had changed—no longer the grind of reluctant procedure but something colder, something that had been waiting behind the procedure all along, patient as interest accruing. ***RELEASE GRANTS THE CLAIMANT THE FLAME. BUT A SOUL MAY NOT CROSS THREE THRESHOLDS UNVESSELED. IT MUST BE CARRIED. AND CARRYING HAS A PRICE, WRITTEN IN THE OLDEST CLAUSE OF ALL, WHICH NO JUDGMENT CAN WAIVE, WHICH EVEN YOUR WITNESS CANNOT VOID:***

The chains of script on its robes parted, and one clause detached itself and hung in the air between them, glowing, undeniable, and Oren read it aloud in a whisper before anyone else could, translating as he went, his voice getting smaller with every word:

*"The vessel who claims a soul shall render up... to the flame... all that the vessel holds most sacred..."*

The light on Marguerite's wrist flickered.

And somewhere far above them, through miles of salt and stone, the moon began its thirteenth turn.
