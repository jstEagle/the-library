## 18. Witness

Ysolde had counted ten thousand moons from her rooftop, and she had never seen one like this.

It hung above the salt cathedral as though the mountain had cracked open to let it in — no, not *in*, she corrected herself. The Thirteenth Magistrate's court did not admit the moon. It admitted the *idea* of the moon, a pale reflection bleeding down through stone that should have been a mile thick, and Ysolde understood that this was itself testimony. The old law ran deeper than rock. The old law reached up and pulled the sky down after it.

She stood at the edge of the judgment floor with her hands folded in her sleeves, the way she had stood at a thousand rites when the lunar order still had a name and a city and a future. Her feet remembered the stance. Her voice, when she would need it, would remember too. That was what vigilance was: not hope, exactly. Muscle.

Below her, on the floor of white salt worn smooth by centuries of verdicts, Marguerite Villalobos stood alone.

That was the first thing the trial demanded. The claimant stands alone. Sefa had told them this in the tunnels, his nameless voice flat with recitation, drawing the forms in the dust with a stick because he could no longer remember why he knew them but his hands had not forgotten. The claimant stands alone. No advocate. No blade. No friend within the circle of judgment. Ysolde had watched Marguerite unbuckle her sword and hand it back without looking at it, and had thought: *there goes a woman setting down twenty years of the wrong weapon.*

Oren stood outside the circle, small and barefoot on the salt, his hand gripping Sefa's rope-bound wrist. The archivist drifted when unattended — his eyes went soft and sideways, seeking something he'd paid away — but the boy anchored him, tugging gently whenever the current took him. A child leading a man who had sold his own name. Ysolde filed the image away. She suspected she would need it later, when the price came due. Prices always came due. That was the one scripture the under-court had never needed to write down.

The Thirteenth Magistrate filled the far end of the cathedral the way a verdict fills a silence.

It was not large. That was the horror of it. It was roughly the size of a seated man, robed in layered salt that had grown over it like coral over a drowned thing, and its face — if face it was — was a ledger page stretched across bone, columns of figures shifting beneath the skin like fish under ice. When it spoke, it did not open a mouth. The words simply arrived, already inside your skull, stamped and dated.

*The claim is heard,* it said. *The claimant will state it.*

Marguerite's voice carried in the salt acoustics the way a blade carries light. "The contract of the Villalobos line, entered beneath this court four generations past, purchased freedom from a blood-feud at the price of one soul unnamed and unborn. The payment was made. The term of holding was fixed at one hundred years." She lifted her chin. "One hundred and twelve years have passed since the soul was taken. You have held it twelve years past term."

*Term,* said the Magistrate, and the figures on its face rippled, *is a construction of the surface kingdoms.*

"Then let us use older law." Marguerite turned, slowly, addressing the whole cathedral — the lanterns of souls hanging in their thousands from the vaulted dark, each one a small captive sun, blue and gold and green, trembling at the sound of voices below. "Older than you. Older than the kingdoms. The law of witness."

The salt floor went very quiet. Even the humming in the walls — the missing syllables of the half-forgotten, which Ysolde had heard growing louder for two days until her teeth ached with them — even that seemed to lean in.

*A witness,* said the Magistrate, *must be present at the making of the contract. Four generations. Name your witness.*

And here it was. Here was the knife-edge they had walked three days underground to reach, and Ysolde felt her heart go slow and enormous in her chest, the way it did before an eclipse.

"You had one," Marguerite said. "You just couldn't hear him."

* *

They had argued about this in the dark of the mine-shaft, the night before, while Oren slept with his head on Sefa's ledger-scarred knee.

"It won't work," Ysolde had said. "Gods can't testify. Everyone knows—"

"Everyone knows what the Guild taught," Sefa had murmured. His voice had grown quieter all journey, less a man speaking than a book left open in wind. "The Edict of Silence didn't outlaw magic, priestess. It outlawed *testimony*. Think about it. Why silence the gods first? Because a god who witnessed a contract can contest it forever. Because divine witness doesn't expire." He had traced a column in the dust. "The Edict wasn't fear of fireballs. It was statute of limitations."

Marguerite had gone very still. In the lamplight Ysolde had watched her do the arithmetic she'd been refusing for twenty years — that her god's silence might not have been abandonment, might not even have been defeat, but *gag order*. That the mark on her wrist wasn't a scar of shame. It was a sealed deposition.

"He refused to forget," Marguerite had said at last, so quietly the words barely disturbed the air. "All these years I thought he'd stopped answering me. He was holding his tongue in a courtroom."

"And now," Ysolde had said, "you've dragged the courtroom into session."

Now, on the salt floor, the Magistrate's ledger-face churned through its columns, and Ysolde read the motion there the way she read cloud-motions for weather: it was searching precedent. It was finding none. Because there was no precedent — no one in four generations had been stupid enough, or faithful enough, or desperate enough to stand in the under-court and demand that a silent god speak.

*There is no god in this court,* the Magistrate said.

"No," Marguerite agreed. "There's a god in *me*."

She raised her left arm. She pushed back the sleeve.

Ysolde had seen the mark before — everyone in Vell Hollow had learned to look away from such things, the way you looked away from beggars and grave-diggers, marks being contraband now. It was a small sigil at the wrist, ink-dark, the oath-sign of a sworn mercenary of the old orders: a blade crossed with a bell. Sworn to answer. Sworn to be answered.

It began to burn.

Not glow. *Burn.* Light came off it in sheets, white-gold, the color of dawn on snow, and the whole cathedral of salt caught it and threw it back multiplied ten thousand times, every facet of every wall suddenly a window onto morning. The hanging lanterns flared in sympathy — souls crying out in colors, moths battering toward a light they had been denied for a century. Ysolde threw her sleeve up against the brilliance and heard Sefa shout, heard Oren *laugh*, one bright startled note, and beneath it all she heard — felt — *knew*, in the marrow-way that a priestess knows —

A voice that was not a voice. A presence that had been standing in the doorway of the world for twenty years, hand raised to knock, waiting for someone to open the door.

*Testimony,* it said, and the word went through Ysolde like a bell through water.

The mark blazed higher. And then — this was the part Ysolde would spend the rest of her life trying to describe, and fail — the light *shaped itself*. Not words. Images, pressed into the air the way seals are pressed into wax:

A chamber beneath this very mountain, four generations gone. A table of black salt. A frightened man and woman — Villalobos, both of them, she had the jaw, the stubborn set of the shoulders — signing while a Magistrate presided. And in the corner, unseen by the signers, unnoticed or ignored by the court: a presence. A witness standing in the wall of the world, watching, marking the date, marking the term, marking the hundred years as they passed like a jailer counting down a sentence he intended to see served to the last hour.

The images showed the term ending. Showed the hundredth year turning. Showed the soul — a small blue flame, no bigger than a sparrow's heart — held anyway, past term, past law, lanterned among ten thousand others while the witness raged silent behind the Edict's gag.

Then the light collapsed. The mark dimmed to embers. Marguerite swayed on her feet, caught herself, stayed standing.

Silence, of the kind that has weight.

*The testimony is received,* said the Thirteenth Magistrate.

Its ledger-face had stopped churning. Every figure on it had gone still, and Ysolde, staring, understood that she was watching a creature discover that it too was bound — that the older law it had invoked all evening against the surface kingdoms ran upward through itself like a root through a coffin. The contract had expired. The soul was held illegally. There was no column for this. There had never been a column for this, because no one had ever come down to collect.

*The flame designated Villalobos-unborn is released from holding,* the Magistrate said. Each word cost it something; Ysolde could hear the salt grinding. *Effective upon—*

"Upon nothing," Marguerite said. "Upon *now*."

*Effective,* the Magistrate continued, with the patience of a glacier, *upon payment of vessel's price.*

The cold that went through the cathedral then was not temperature. Ysolde had spent forty years reading omens and she knew a pivot when she stood on one.

"A price was paid," Marguerite said. "Four generations ago. In full."

*A price was paid for the taking. Vessel's price is owed for the carrying.* The Magistrate's face tilted toward her, and the columns began to move again, and Ysolde saw with sick clarity that this had been in the fine print all along, carved into the floor at their feet where only a moon-reader would think to look — she had seen it that morning, half a phrase, and hoped. *A soul cannot walk out of this court uncarried. The carrier pays. The carrier pays in the only coin a mortal holds that the court cannot mint.*

"Name it," Marguerite said, and her voice did not shake, and Ysolde loved her for it and feared for her in the same breath.

*Memory,* said the Magistrate. *The memories of the claimed. All of them. The carrier will leave this mountain knowing that she carried a flame, and nothing else of what the flame is, or whose, or why she came, or what it cost her to come. The witness who testified through her will pass out of her knowledge entirely. She will stand in the sunlight holding her brother and will not know the word brother. She will not know the word god.*

*This is the vessel's price. It is not negotiable. It is not appealable. The older law binds me as it binds you.*

Ysolde closed her eyes.

She had warned them. On the rooftop, with the twelfth turn waning, she had said: *the under-court will demand a price no one expects.* She had imagined gold. Years. A name, poor Sefa's fate repeated. She had not imagined this — that the court, unable to keep the soul, would take the next best thing: the keeping. If it could not hold the flame, it would hollow the one who carried it. Justice rendered and vengeance administered in a single clause.

She opened her eyes and looked at Marguerite Villalobos, standing alone in the circle of judgment with her dead god's mark cooling on her wrist, and waited to see what twenty years of surviving had built.

* *

What it had built was this: a woman who did not hesitate.

But before she spoke, she did something no form required. She stepped to the edge of the circle — breaking form, Sefa would have wept if he could have remembered why it mattered — and she looked up at Ysolde, and at Oren, and at the drifting archivist bound to the boy's hand.

"Ysolde," she said. "When I don't know what this is—" she touched the mark, lightly, the way you touch a scar you've stopped resenting, "—you'll tell me? Not what he was. Just that I *had*. That it mattered."

"Every day," Ysolde said. Her voice held. It was the hardest thing she had done in forty years of hard things. "I'll count it for you. Moon by moon."

"And him?" Marguerite's gaze dropped to Oren.

The boy stepped forward, right to the circle's edge where the salt smoked faintly at the boundary, and he looked up at her and said something in the old tongue — three words, soft, the language of the deep courts that had no word for goodbye because the deep courts believed nothing ever truly left. Then he said it again in Common, badly, carefully, the way you carry water in your hands:

"I'll say it to you. Every day. What you did. Who he is. Until it grows back true."

Marguerite smiled. It was terrible and bright, a mercenary's smile, a sister's smile. "Then it's a fair trade," she said, and turned back to the center of the floor, and faced the Magistrate with her empty hands open at her sides.

"The vessel's price is accepted," she said. "Bring me my brother."

The Magistrate rose. Salt cascaded from its shoulders like snow off a roof. It extended one limb — not a hand, a *column*, a ledger-line given flesh — and somewhere high in the vaulted dark, among ten thousand captive dawns, one small blue lantern began, slowly, to descend.

Ysolde watched it come down through the blazing dark, and she counted the turns of her own heart, and she began, silently, the first of the ten thousand tellings she had promised.

*Her name was Marguerite. Her god's name was—*

Above them, faint and far, the moon finished its thirteenth turn.
