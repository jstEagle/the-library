## 19. The Price of the Flame

The Magistrate's terms hung in the salt air like a blade waiting to fall.

*A vessel's price,* it had said, its voice the sound of a ledger closing. *The claimant shall surrender what she has carried longest.*

Marguerite had known before Ysolde finished translating. She had known in her bones, in the mark on her wrist that still blazed with her god's testimony, in the way Oren would not look at her.

"Memories," she said now. "You want my memories."

The Thirteenth Magistrate sat at the far end of the salt cathedral, a shape like a judge's bench grown into a mountain, its face a smooth blank where features should have been carved. Around it, ten thousand lanterns burned with stolen souls—blue flames, green flames, one gold one near the ceiling that flickered like a nervous heartbeat. Her brother's lantern sat on the bench before it, small and steady and warm.

*The law is old,* the Magistrate said. *Older than your Edict. Older than your kingdom. A soul may not pass a threshold unclaimed, and a claimant must pay passage in the coin of her own keeping. You claimed him. You will carry him. The toll is memory.*

"There has to be another way," Sefa said. He stood with his rope-bound wrist in his hand, nameless and drifting, but his eyes were sharp as awls when they fixed on the Magistrate. "There are precedents. Substitutionary payment. I've read the ledgers—"

"You've read copies," Marguerite said quietly. "It wrote the original." She looked up at the blank face. "How much?"

*All of it,* said the Magistrate. *Or none. Partial payment voids the claim, and the flame returns to the vault for another term.*

Another term. Another century of her brother burning in a jar while some other Villalobos came down the mountain to bargain.

"How much," she repeated, "is all of it?"

Ysolde's hand found her shoulder. The moon-priestess had not stopped touching her since the judgment ended, as if Marguerite might dissolve into salt if left alone. "Every prayer you ever made," Ysolde said softly. "Every answer. Every night you spent talking to something that talked back. Twenty years of faith, Marguerite. It takes the whole of it. That's what makes it a fair price—the courts don't deal in fractions of devotion."

"And after?"

"After, you'll know he existed." Ysolde nodded toward the lantern. "You'll know you paid for him. But the shape of what you gave up—" she pressed her lips together. "It'll be an empty room. You'll walk past it every day and feel the draft."

Marguerite laughed. It came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "Twenty years ago I chose survival over faith. Now faith costs me everything just to survive." She wiped her face with the back of her sword-hand. "There's a symmetry there the Magistrate probably finds very satisfying."

*The claimant speaks,* the Magistrate said, and there was something almost like patience in it. *Does she accept?*

Oren stepped forward.

He had been silent since the judgment—since his confession, since her forgiveness, since the moment she'd pulled him against her chest and felt his heart hammering out the rhythm of a language older than kingdoms. Now he planted himself between her and the bench, small and barefoot and immovable, and spoke in the old tongue. His voice filled the cathedral the way water fills a cup: completely, without effort.

The Magistrate listened. Then, for the first time in what might have been centuries, it hesitated.

"He says," Ysolde translated, her voice unsteady, "that there is a clause. An old one. If the claimant surrenders her memories freely—not under compulsion, not as a trick, but as a gift given to family—then the payment is... sanctified. It cannot be resold. It cannot be kept in a lantern. It passes through the court untouched and goes—" she stopped.

"Goes where?" Marguerite asked.

"To the god who witnessed the contract," Oren said.

In Common. In cracked, rusty, beautiful Common, the first words she had ever heard him speak.

*The child parses truly,* the Magistrate said, and its voice had gone flat, careful, the voice of a creature discovering a trap in its own floor. *The clause stands. The memories will return to the witness.*

"To my god," Marguerite said slowly. "Who has been silent for twenty years."

"To the god who refused to forget you," Ysolde whispered. "Marguerite. Do you understand what this means? Your faith won't be destroyed. It will be *returned*. Held in trust. He took silence so the contract couldn't be enforced—and the moment you pay, he can speak again, because the debt that silenced him dies with your payment."

The mark on her wrist flared. Not pain—something else. Something like a hand closing around hers from the inside.

She thought about the night the Edict passed. She was nineteen, kneeling in a mercenary chapel with forty sworn brothers and sisters, and the Guild-master had come down the aisle with shears in his hands, cutting marks off wrists like trimming wicks. Some had wept. Some had fought and been dragged out. Marguerite had held out her arm and let them cut, because rent was due and steel didn't pray back anyway—or so she'd told herself every day for twenty years, until the telling wore a groove so deep she couldn't climb out of it.

Her god had gone silent that same hour. She'd always assumed it was grief, or disgust, or simply the Edict doing what Edicts did.

Now she knew better. He hadn't abandoned her. He had *refused*—refused to be a party to a contract that held her brother's soul past its term, refused to lend his voice to any proceeding conducted under the Edict's shadow. Silence as testimony. Twenty years of it. The longest prayer anyone had ever answered.

And the price of ending it was every memory she had of him.

"I accept," she said.

* * *

They prepared her in the antechamber behind the bench, a hollow in the salt where the walls glowed faintly rose-colored, like flesh lit from within. Ysolde drew a circle of moon-salts on the floor. Sefa copied the relevant clauses onto a scrap of parchment with hands that shook only a little. Oren sat cross-legged at the circle's edge and would not leave, no matter how many times Marguerite told him he didn't have to watch.

"I watched my own soul get put in a jar," he said. "I can watch yours get taken out."

"That's not the same thing."

"It's exactly the same thing," he said, "and you know it, and that's why you're being nice to me, and I hate it."

She almost smiled. Almost.

Ysolde knelt across from her inside the circle. Up close, the priestess's eyes were pale as the moon's face, and ancient, and kind. "I need you to understand what happens," she said. "This isn't sleep. There's no gap. You'll feel each memory go. And when it's done, you'll still be you—but a you that never knelt, never prayed, never heard an answer. The mark will be a scar. That's all."

"A scar that warms when Oren laughs," Marguerite said. "Sefa told me. Apparently that survives."

"Sefa remembers ledger-craft and nothing else. Don't take his word for metaphysics."

"Fair." Marguerite rolled up her sleeve. The mark sat on her wrist where it had sat for twenty years—a coiled blade wrapped in a crescent, inked by a temple priest when she was sixteen and certain she would die gloriously before twenty-five. It had been cold for two decades. Tonight it burned like a coal.

"One question," she said to the air, to the mark, to whatever was listening. "If you're really there. If you can really hear me one last time." Her throat closed. She pushed through it. "Was it worth it? The silence. All those years. Was I worth it?"

The warmth spread up her arm, across her chest, into the hollow places she'd stopped noticing years ago. It didn't answer in words. It never had, even in the old days—it had answered in the swing of a blade arriving exactly when needed, in the improbable survival, in the dreams where someone vast and patient sat beside her fire without speaking, and the silence had felt like company instead of absence.

Yes, the silence said, in the only language it had ever used.

"Oh," Marguerite said. Her eyes were wet. "Oh, you absolute bastard. You could have written a letter."

Ysolde began the rite.

* * *

It went the way drowning goes: slowly, then all at once.

The first memory to go was small. Rain on a temple roof in Vell Hollow, the year she was seventeen, the sound of it drumming while she prayed for a dead friend's safe passage. She felt it lift out of her like a splinter drawn clean, and she gasped, and reached for it, and it was already gone—she knew there had been a memory there, knew its shape by the hole it left, and the hole hurt worse than any wound she'd ever taken.

Then the next. A dawn blessing before her first contract. Gone.

The taste of sacramental salt on her tongue. Gone.

They came faster after that, a tide going out, and Marguerite learned that a life of faith is not one large thing but ten thousand small ones—every muttered prayer before a fight, every thank-you after, every night she'd talked to the dark because the dark had once talked back. Each one tore free and flew somewhere she couldn't follow, and she clung to the circle's edge and made sounds she wasn't proud of, and Oren crawled forward and put his small hands on her arms and held on.

"I'm here," he said. "I'm here. I'm here."

Somewhere in the middle of it, she lost the memory of her god's presence itself—the vastness, the patience, the feeling of being known down to the marrow. That one nearly killed her. She screamed. The salt walls rang. Ysolde's voice cut through, steady as a rope thrown to a swimmer: *"Breathe. Breathe. It's passing. Let it pass."*

And then, near the end, one last memory surfaced on its own, rising like a body coming up from deep water, and she grabbed it with both hands because she understood it was the last:

A campfire. She was twenty-two, three years into the silence, and she had stopped praying out of bitterness. But that night, drunk on cheap wine after a bad job, she'd spoken aloud to the empty dark—*if you're still there, I'm sorry. I chose wrong.* And the fire had burned a little brighter. Not a miracle. Not proof. Just enough.

*I know,* the dark had seemed to say. *I know. Wait for me.*

Gone.

Marguerite came back to herself in a rose-lit room, kneeling in a circle of salts, holding a child's hands, with tears on her face and nothing behind her eyes where the most important thing in her life used to live.

She tested the emptiness the way you test a missing tooth. There was the mark on her wrist—cold now, ordinary, a scar. There was the knowledge that she had believed in something, once, deeply enough to bleed for it. There was the fact of the payment, bright and recent. And beyond that: draft. An open door in her chest with wind coming through it, and no room on the other side.

"Well," she said. Her voice sounded fine. That seemed obscene. "That's done, then."

Ysolde was crying silently, which Marguerite found confusing, because surely she was the one with the reason. "You're still here," the priestess said. "Do you understand? Everything else is gone and you're still *here*. I was afraid there'd be less of you left."

"Less of me?" Marguerite looked down at her hands. Callused. Steady. Hers. "No. I don't think so. I think I'm exactly the same size. There's just..." She searched for it. "More space in me now. Room to put things."

Oren threw his arms around her neck and buried his face against her collarbone, and she held him, and the mark on her wrist stayed cold and quiet, and somehow that was bearable, because the boy was warm and alive and breathing her name in a language she was only beginning to learn.

* * *

The Magistrate released the lantern without ceremony. Perhaps, Marguerite thought, even a thing like that recognized a debt honestly paid; perhaps it simply wanted them gone. It pressed the lantern into her hands with limbs of salt-smoke and said, in its ledger-closing voice: *The account of Villalobos is closed. The door closes with the turn. Go, and do not return.*

The walk out took a day and a night. They moved through the tunnels in single file—Sefa murmuring contract-clauses to himself like a rosary, Ysolde marking their path with pinches of consecrated salt, Oren walking beside her with his hand hooked in her belt because he'd decided, somewhere in the dark, that he was done letting go of her.

The lantern swung gently from her fist. Inside, the blue flame had changed. Before, it had flinched from her like a startled bird. Now it leaned toward her hand whenever she lifted it, pressing against the glass the way a cat presses against a familiar knee. Warmth bled through the salt-glass into her palm.

"Hey," she said to it, somewhere in the long dark stretch between the shrine where her shame lived and the stair that led up to the world. "I'm Marguerite. I'm your sister. I know you don't—I know none of us got a say in any of this. But I paid a lot for you, kid, so the least you can do is keep warm."

The flame pulsed. Once, twice. A rhythm. It might have been a heartbeat. It might have been laughter.

She walked on.

When they finally climbed out through the mine-mouth into grey pre-dawn air, Vell Hollow lay below them in the valley, rooftops silvered, the mountain's shadow retreating west. Above the city, the moon hung fat and white and nearly full—twelve turns spent, the thirteenth beginning tonight.

Ysolde shaded her eyes and counted, lips moving. When she finished, her face had gone strange.

"Moonrise is at dusk," she said. "The turn completes at moonset, tomorrow morning. We have one day." She turned to Marguerite, and the wind pulled at her grey robes, and far below, at the tunnel mouth they'd just left, torchlight was spreading through the dark like spilled fire—Guild lanterns, dozens of them, Inquisitors climbing.

"But Marguerite," Ysolde said, "the moon isn't rising alone. Look at the sky. Look at the color of it."

Marguerite looked. Along the eastern horizon, beneath the fading stars, the sky had begun to glow—not with dawn. Dawn was hours away. This light was green-gold and pulsing, rising off the city like heat-shimmer, like breath, like something enormous turning over in its sleep after twenty years of stillness.

The mark on her wrist—cold, scarred, empty—gave one faint, inexplicable throb.

"What is that?" Oren whispered.

Marguerite gripped the warm lantern and started down the slope toward the city, toward the torches, toward the last turn of the moon.

"That," she said, "is the bill coming due. And this time, we're the ones collecting."
