## 20. Thirteenth Turn

Oren had never seen the moon whole before.

Beneath the mountain there was no sky, only salt and lantern-light and the long hum of held things. Above it, in the tunnels, there had been cracks and seams where a sliver of white leaked through like water through a roof. But here, at the mouth of the world, the moon hung over Vell Hollow fat and full and almost finished, one thin shadow already climbing its eastern edge, and Oren stood in the cold with his bare feet on frozen mud and thought: *so that is what I am for.*

The thirteenth turn. The closing of the door.

He could feel it behind him, deep in the dark of the tunnel — the great door grinding its slow way toward shut, stone teeth meeting stone teeth, older than any kingdom's oldest law. It did not hurry. Doors that close forever do not need to hurry.

In front of him, spread across the slope between the tunnel mouth and the city walls, were the Inquisitors.

* * *

There were more than Marguerite had guessed. Oren counted them the way he counted everything now, in the old tongue, because numbers in the old tongue felt like weights: forty-one. Forty-two. Torches in two ragged lines, and between them, walking slow as a funeral, a man in grey robes with a chain of iron keys across his chest. High Inquisitor. Oren didn't know the title but he knew the shape of him — a man built entirely out of locked doors.

"Marguerite," Ysolde said quietly. She stood at Oren's left, her silver crescents catching torchlight, and her voice had the calm of someone who has counted every turn of every moon for twenty years and is not surprised by this one. "They knew we'd come up here."

"They always knew." Marguerite's voice was rough. She had been crying, Oren thought, though she would deny it until she died. She still held the lantern. She held it the way other people hold children. Inside it, the small blue flame sat warm and quiet against the glass, and when Oren looked at it sideways he could see the shape of a man no older than Marguerite, folded up small, waiting to be born into whatever came next. "We have the soul. That's what matters. Get past them, get to the city, get him somewhere safe—"

"And then what?" Sefa asked. He stood slightly apart from all of them, the way he had since the tollgate, rope still knotted around his wrist and tied to Ysolde's belt. His face was open and puzzled and brave, a man who had sold his own name and kept his purpose without knowing why. "Marguerite. The Edict stands. The Guild stands. We come out of the mountain carrying a stolen soul and outlawed magic in a jar, and they will take it and burn it and burn us after. There is no *somewhere safe*. Not yet."

"Not yet," Ysolde agreed, and smiled, and Oren understood that she had known the ending of this for a long time. Priestesses count. Counting means you see the last number coming.

The Inquisitors had stopped advancing. They stood in their lines with their torches guttering in the wind off the mountain, and the man in grey raised one hand, and every voice on the slope went silent at once — not because he commanded it, Oren realized, but because everyone, even the Inquisitors themselves, was afraid of what he might say.

He said it in Common, loud enough to reach them:

"By the Edict of Silence, in the name of the Guild and the Crown that fears its own shadow: surrender the vessel, surrender the flame, surrender the witch, the priestess, and the archivist, and you will be tried fairly."

Marguerite laughed. It was not a kind sound. "Fairly," she called back. "Like the half-forgotten village? Like the names in your ledgers?"

"The law is the law."

"The law is a door," Marguerite said, "and doors can close." And Oren heard it in her voice — she wasn't talking about the Edict anymore. She was talking about the grinding thing behind them in the dark, the thing that had nearly swallowed her brother forever, and she was daring the Inquisitor to understand her.

He didn't. Men built out of locked doors rarely do.

"You have until the moon completes its turn," the High Inquisitor said. "Then we come and take what the law says is ours."

* * *

Oren turned around.

The tunnel mouth behind them breathed cold air, and far down inside it, deeper than light, he could hear the door. Not hear with ears. Hear the way he heard everything — the mountain humming, souls moaning soft as moths against glass, the under-court's long slow heartbeat winding down. The Thirteenth Magistrate had lost. Bound by older law than itself, it had released the flame, released the vessel, closed its accounts. And now it was going home, and taking the road home with it.

When the door shut, the old tongue would have nowhere left to live.

That was the part nobody else understood. Sefa had translated the phrase weeks ago — *the thirteenth turn closes the door* — and they had all assumed it meant the court, the vaults, the lanterns. It did mean those things. But words are doors too. The pre-kingdom tongue was the tongue of the deep courts, spoken by salt and stone and the things that hold souls in jars, and when the last door beneath the mountain sealed, the language itself would seal with it. Every word Oren carried would become a dead thing in a living mouth. He would keep his voice and lose his speech, the way Marguerite had kept her mark and lost her god.

Unless.

Ysolde had told him, on the last stretch of tunnel, in her patient rooftop-shrine voice: *Magic doesn't die, little one. It goes silent. There is a difference, and the difference is a door left open somewhere.* She had looked at him then the way she looked at the moon. *You were made to carry souls across thresholds. What do you think a language is?*

So Oren turned to face the dark, and Marguerite put her hand on his shoulder — careful, always careful, as if he might break, as if he hadn't already broken and mended himself twice over — and said, "What are you doing? We have to move."

He looked up at her. He had known her name before she gave it; he had chosen her door out of every door in Vell Hollow; he had let her believe he was sent to find help, and then told her the truth in the worst room under the world, and she had forgiven him with her hands shaking. He owed her more than a stolen soul. He owed her the silence ending.

He opened his mouth and spoke the old tongue aloud, in the open air, above ground, where it had not been spoken since before kingdoms.

* * *

The first word was *open.*

It did not sound like much. That was the terrible and wonderful thing about the old tongue — it sounded like almost nothing, like breath, like a child humming, like wind finding a crack in a wall. The Inquisitors on the slope leaned forward, straining to hear, and some of them laughed, because a mute boy making noises at a hole in a mountain is a funny thing if your heart is made of ledgers.

Then the second word, which was *remember,* and the laughter stopped.

Because the mountain answered.

Oren felt it come up through his feet first — the hum changing pitch, the long held note beneath Vell Hollow bending upward, waking, widening. The salt in the ground began to shine. Not torchlight-shine; a pale inner glow, rising out of the earth itself, running up the slope in veins like dawn moving through frost. The old contracts carved in the mine-shafts lit one after another, miles of carved law flaring gold in the dark, and the air filled with a sound like ten thousand pages turning.

"What is he doing?" an Inquisitor shouted, and his voice cracked in the middle, and Oren pitied him a little. The man had spent his whole life enforcing a silence. He had no practice at all with what came after.

Oren spoke the third word, which was *return,* and the Edict of Silence broke.

Not gradually. Not like a wall coming down, brick by brick, argument by argument, edict by edict through some slow parliament of fear. It broke the way a chord breaks — all at once, every note together, one ringing sound that was not a sound at all but a *permission*, rolling out of the tunnel mouth and across the slope and down into the city of Vell Hollow like a tide.

Oren saw it hit the city. He saw the lamplight change color. He saw charms wake in shop windows — dead protection-knots twitching like sleeping hands, love-charms glowing pink and embarrassed, a whole street of worthless trinkets suddenly worth something, and somewhere below, he knew, charm-sellers were waking up to shelves full of miracles. He saw the salt-cellars glow. He saw the shrine-roofs light up silver, every hidden crescent and buried altar and whispered prayer of twenty years igniting at once, because the god-marks had never stopped existing — they had only stopped being allowed to answer, and now the answering came back through them all at once, a chord struck on an instrument the size of a city.

And he saw the Inquisitors' torches go out.

All of them. Every flame on the slope snuffed in the same instant, not by wind — there was no wind — but by something older deciding that fire obeyed different rules tonight. Forty-two men and women stood in sudden moonlight, blinking, holding sticks of smoke, and the High Inquisitor's chain of keys began, very softly, to sing. Iron remembers what it locks. Magic was back, and the locks knew it, and the keys sang like frightened birds.

"No," the High Inquisitor said. It came out very small. Then louder, gathering the law around him like a cloak: "No. The Edict is law. The Edict is *law*—" and Ysolde stepped forward, silver and calm, and said in a voice that carried to the city walls:

"The Edict was a door. The door is closed. Go home."

* * *

Behind them, deep in the mountain, the great door finished closing.

Oren felt it the way you feel a heart finish a beat. A vast final grind, a settling of stone, a silence where the humming used to be — and then nothing. The under-court was gone, or asleep, or simply elsewhere, beyond the reach of anyone who had not already paid its prices. The half-forgotten village was empty now, its people free to be forgotten gently instead of by contract. The lantern-vaults were dark. Somewhere in the new silence, a Magistrate who had ruled for longer than kingdoms sat alone with its empty shelves, and Oren found, to his surprise, that he pitied it too. It had only ever known how to hold things. Nobody had ever taught it how to let go until a mercenary with a scarred wrist stood in its hall and read its own law back to it.

But the old tongue did not die.

That was the miracle Oren had gambled everything on, standing in the dark with his borrowed voice. Because a language does not live in courts. It lives in mouths. And when he spoke it aloud under the open moon, above ground, in front of forty-two witnesses and one whole listening city, he had done the one thing the deep courts never intended: he had taken it across the threshold. Into the air. Into the common world. Into the ears of Inquisitors and charm-sellers and children leaning out of windows in the Hollow district, hearing sounds older than any kingdom pour out of the smallest person on the slope.

The magic flooding Vell Hollow came home through him. Through the vessel. Through the body made to carry souls across thresholds — and a language, Ysolde had said, what do you think a language is?

The blue flame in Marguerite's lantern flickered, and Oren felt the man inside it stir, and press close to the glass, and look out at the moonlit world with eyes made of light. *Brother,* Oren said to him, in the old tongue, silently, the way family talks. *This is the sky. This is the city your sister bled for. You are late by twenty years and a hundred, but you are here.*

The flame burned a little bluer. Which Oren chose to understand as *thank you.*

* * *

Afterward — after the Inquisitors had dropped their smoke-sticks and drifted down the slope like people leaving a funeral that turned out to be a wedding, after the first distant cheer rose from the city and then another and then a hundred, after Sefa stood staring at the glowing contracts in the tunnel mouth with tears running freely down his face and no memory of why except that he was sure, absolutely sure, they were happy ones — Marguerite crouched down in front of Oren.

She looked tired in a way that went all the way through, the way people look when something has been taken out of them and something else put in its place. Her wrist, where the god-mark lived, was still faintly glowing. Everyone's marks were, tonight. But hers glowed steady, like a lamp left burning in a window.

"That word you said," she said. "*Return.*" Her voice caught. "I felt it. In the mark. Like — like someone I don't remember putting a hand on my shoulder." She frowned, chasing a memory that wasn't there anymore, and Oren's chest hurt, because he knew exactly what she had paid in the dark of the judgment hall, and she would never know, and that was the price working correctly. "Was that my god?"

Oren considered lying. He was good at it; he'd been built for it, more or less. But she had fought a Magistrate with its own law for him. She had given up twenty years of faith so a stranger's brother could come home.

He reached out and touched her glowing wrist, and spoke one more word of the old tongue — the word for *witness* — and let the mark tell her what his voice could not: *yes. yes. yes.*

Marguerite closed her eyes. When she opened them, she was smiling, mostly.

"All right," she said, standing, turning toward the city, where the lights were wrong in all the best ways and bells that hadn't rung in twenty years were beginning, hesitantly, to remember how. "Then we'd better go down there. Somebody's going to have to explain to a whole city full of newly legal witches why they shouldn't burn it all down again, and I know an archivist with a ledger, and a priestess with a rooftop, and—" she looked down at Oren, and her voice went soft and fierce at once, "—and the kid who ended the longest silence in history. They're going to want to hear from you, Oren. Everyone's going to want to hear from you."

Oren looked at the city. At the wound in the world, already knitting. At the thousands of windows lighting up, and the tiny figures in the streets looking up at the moon, and somewhere among them, he was certain, other children like him — vessels, carriers, mute kids with old words stuck in their throats, waiting for someone to prove the words could live in daylight.

He took a breath, and opened his mouth, and began to teach the city its oldest language — starting, naturally, with the word for *home.*
