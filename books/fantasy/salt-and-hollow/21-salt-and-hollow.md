## 21. Salt and Hollow

The rain over Vell Hollow fell gold for three days after the Edict broke.

Marguerite stood in her doorway with a chipped cup of chicory and watched it come down—actual light, actual warmth, dripping off the eaves like the city was weeping something other than salt. In twenty years she had never seen weather do that. In twenty years weather had done nothing at all but fall and freeze and be weather. Now the gutters ran bright, and children were out in it barefoot, catching sparks on their tongues like they'd been doing it all their lives.

Oren was one of them.

He stood in the middle of Saltrow with his arms out and his face turned up, laughing at whatever the sky said to him in that old tongue of his, and every time he laughed Marguerite's wrist warmed like a hand had closed around it.

She looked down at the mark—the god-mark, the mercenary's brand, the thing that had been dead leather on her skin since the Edict passed—and felt nothing she could name. That was the trouble. The mark worked. She did not know why.

*You paid for him,* Ysolde had told her, the night they came out of the mountain, when Marguerite had asked what exactly she'd lost. *The vessel's price. You gave up your memories of your god. Every prayer. Every answered blade.*

And Marguerite had said, *I don't feel any different,* because it was true. There was no hole in her. No wound where faith used to live. Just a smooth blank stretch inside, like a room swept clean before she ever walked into it, and the terrible part was how easy it was. She kept reaching for grief the way you reach for a stair that isn't there, and her foot kept finding floor.

The mark warmed again. Oren had slipped in the gutter-water and was sitting in a puddle, delighted about it.

"Charm-seller," called a voice from the street, and Marguerite tensed out of pure habit before she remembered there was no reason left to tense.

It was the baker's wife from two doors down, holding out a protection knot Marguerite had sold her back when charms were lies. "It lit," the woman said, wonder-struck, holding the little braided cord up like evidence in a trial. "It lit last night, blue as a gas-flame. My husband swears it turned a fever off our boy."

"It does that now," Marguerite said.

"But I only paid four coppers—"

"Then you got a bargain." She drank her chicory. "Everything's a bargain this week. Come back when the panic wears off and we'll talk real prices."

The woman laughed and went home through the golden rain, and Marguerite stood in her doorway and took inventory of her life, which was a thing she hadn't done in years because inventory was for people who expected to keep things.

Her stock of unsold charms—two hundred-odd pieces of knotted string, carved bone, pressed salt—had gone from dead inventory to working inventory overnight, and half the Hollow district knew it. She'd sold out three times. She'd raised prices twice and people still queued. A charm that actually worked was worth more than a sword in Vell Hollow now, and everyone who'd spent two decades pretending otherwise was suddenly an expert.

The Inquisitors' Guild hall had hung a sign: *CLOSED PENDING REVIEW.* Three of them had resigned publicly, one of them in tears on the courthouse steps, renouncing the Edict-era warrants like a man shedding wet clothes. Two hadn't resigned. Those two still made Marguerite's hand drift toward her knife when she saw gray coats at the end of a street. Old habits. The kind that outlive their reasons.

Sefa Ilvane would say that was the whole city's problem, dressed small. Habits outliving reasons. Laws outliving their makers. Debts outliving debtors. Sefa said a lot of things lately, most of them from behind a desk in the ruined archive, most of them true, none of them comfortable.

Marguerite finished her chicory and went inside to work.

* * *

The shop—if you could call a front room with a counter a shop—smelled of salt and lamp oil and, faintly, of the blue flame sleeping in its lantern on the shelf behind her head.

She'd gotten in the habit of talking to it. She wasn't sure when. Somewhere in the second week, probably, when the silence of the flat got heavy and she caught herself narrating her day aloud just to hear a voice, and then realized there *was* another voice in the room, if a quiet one.

"Morning," she said now, hanging her wet cloak on its hook.

The flame leaned east. It always leaned toward her when she came in. She'd stopped telling herself it was the draft.

"You missed a show. Oren found a puddle deep enough to drown a cat and decided to swim in it. Ysolde fished him out by the collar like a kitten. He bit her. She blessed him for it, which I don't think is how blessing works, but she says everything's how blessing works now that the moon can answer back."

The flame pulsed once, slow, the way a chest rises when someone settles deeper into sleep. Her brother. Her brother's soul, or what remained of him after generations under the mountain—a small blue fire that flinched from strangers and leaned toward her and, on certain nights when the moon was high, flickered in patterns too regular to be random. Ysolde said it was trying to speak. Ysolde said give it time; souls that had been held past term came back slow, like limbs waking from numbness.

Marguerite had never met her brother. Hadn't known she'd had one until Sefa put the ledger in front of her and her family name sat there in faded ink next to a transaction older than the kingdom. Grief for a stranger—that had been the shape of the whole descent, really. She'd gone under the mountain for a person who was a word in a book, and somewhere between the tollgate of names and the Magistrate's judgment floor, the word had become a small blue flame that flinched like a bird, and by the time she carried him out she'd have burned the city down rather than put him back.

Now he lived on a shelf and leaned toward her in the mornings, and it was the closest thing to family she'd had since her mother died, and she talked to him like a person because he was one, and because talking to him was easier than thinking about what she'd paid.

She set the kettle on and laid out the morning's commissions. Six knots to braid, two wards to press in salt, a birth-charm for the ferryman's wife—new business, that. Before the Edict broke, nobody had come to her for anything that mattered.

A knock at the door. Not Oren's knock—he didn't knock, he arrived, the way weather arrives. Not Sefa's; Sefa knocked like the door owed him money.

Three knocks, even, official. Gray-coat rhythm.

Marguerite opened the door with her knife hand loose at her side.

The man on her step wore civilian wool, but he stood like a Guild man, and he had the particular pallor of someone who had recently stopped believing his own sermons. Inquisitor, ex- flavor. He held his hat in both hands like an offering.

"Mistress Villalobos. Forgive the hour. I—" He swallowed. "I served the Guild eleven years. I signed warrants. I burned stock." His eyes flicked past her to the shelves of charms, and Marguerite watched him look at them the way a starving man looks through a window. "I'm told you're taking commissions."

"I am."

"My daughter's sick. Fever, six days, the physicians are out of—there aren't any answers anymore, there's nothing but *this* now, everyone says—" He stopped himself, gathered the hat tighter. "Everyone says you're fair. Even the ones who used to hunt you."

Marguerite looked at him for a long moment. Twenty years of gray coats. Twenty years of whips and warrants and the specific cruelty of men enforcing a law they didn't understand against a crime that wasn't real. And here one was on her step, hat in hands, asking.

The mark on her wrist warmed—not Oren this time, not anyone. Just warm. Like something approving.

"Fever ward runs eight coppers," she said. "Ten if you want it keyed to bloodline so it holds past childhood. Pay me when she's well. If she's not well, don't pay me, and I'll redo it free until it takes." She stepped back from the door. "Come in. And take off that coat. The gray stays outside."

He blinked. Then he shrugged out of the coat like it had been burning him for years, and came in, and sat where she told him, and answered honestly when she asked how long the fever had run and whether it broke in the mornings, and when she pressed the salt ward into his palm and told him how to hang it, his hands shook.

"Thank you," he said at the door, and then, wrecked and quiet: "We told ourselves it was order. Eleven years, we told ourselves—"

"I know what you told yourselves," Marguerite said. "I heard it while you tied me to posts. Go home. Hang the ward above her bed, north side. Come back Thursday and tell me how she is."

He went. Marguerite shut the door and stood with her back against it and realized her heart was going hard.

Not fear. Something adjacent to fear. Something like standing at the edge of a drop and finding, instead of a long fall, a staircase going down into the dark, and knowing you were going to have to walk it.

Ysolde had a word for the feeling. Ysolde had a word for everything, being the last priestess of an outlawed order and therefore professionally full of words. She'd said it three nights ago on the rooftop shrine, both of them drinking tea while Oren slept in a nest of blankets and the moon did something overhead that made Ysolde's eyes go silver:

"You're rebuilding," she'd said. "That's what this is. Everyone thinks restoration means putting things back the way they were. It doesn't. The city can't go back—twenty years of silence changed what's under the foundations. Neither can you. You're not getting your old life back, Marguerite. You're building a new one on top of the hole, and the trick is to build honest, so it doesn't collapse the first hard winter."

"And what goes in the hole?" Marguerite had asked. "The place where my god used to be?"

Ysolde had looked at her wrist, at the mark that glowed faintly whenever Oren laughed, and smiled the way priestesses smile—sad and certain at once.

"Something new," she'd said. "Something that doesn't need remembering. Watch and see."

* * *

Thursday brought the Inquisitor back with news of his daughter—fever broken, first full night's sleep in a week—and a purse he refused to leave without emptying, and a question he asked carefully, hat once again in hand:

"The child. The one who speaks the old tongue. Is it true he ended the Edict? That the magic came back *through* him?"

"He opened the door," Marguerite said. "What came through belongs to everybody."

"They're saying he's the new court. That the under-mountain sent him up as a herald, that he'll sit judgment on the city for what it did." The man's voice dropped. "They're saying the half-forgotten are coming out of the tunnels. Asking for their names back."

Marguerite set down the knot she was braiding.

That much was true. She'd seen the first of them herself, three days ago—a woman at the market with a gap in her speech, a missing syllable that made her own name unpronounceable, standing in front of the courthouse with a written petition and a patience that was worse than anger. More came every day. Out of the cellars, out of the mine-shafts, out of villages on the mountain's skirt where people had sold themselves piecemeal across generations just to eat. They came out blinking into the golden rain, holding scraps of contracts, asking a city that had profited for two decades to make them whole.

Some in the council wanted to turn them away. *The contracts were legal,* the councilors said. *Signed and sealed.* As if legality were the same as right—as if Marguerite hadn't stood in a cathedral of salt and watched a magistrate hide murder inside fine print and call it law until somebody read the bones of it back to itself.

"They're not coming for judgment," Marguerite said. "They're coming for their names. There's a difference, and the council had better learn it fast."

"Will you help them?" the man asked. "People say you went down there. People say you argued contract-law to the Magistrate itself and won."

"People say a lot of things."

"Is it true?"

Marguerite looked at him—this gray-coat man with his healed daughter and his shaking hands—and thought about the staircase going down into the dark, and how far down it might go, and who else was already walking it.

"It's true I owe some people," she said. "Down there. Above it too. Ask me again when I know what I'm doing about it."

He left. She went back to her knots, and the flame on the shelf leaned toward her, and outside Oren's laugh came ringing up the street with the sound of the whole district in it, and her mark glowed like a coal, and she thought: *watch and see.* Something new, growing in the swept-clean room. Not memory. Direction.

* * *

Sefa Ilvane arrived at dusk, which meant trouble or paperwork, and with Sefa it was usually both.

He came in shaking rain off his coat, accepted tea without being offered it, and set a ledger on her counter with the care of a man laying down a card he'd been holding a long time. New binding. New paper. But the hand that had labeled it was steady and precise, archivist's script, and it read: *RESTITUTIONS — VOL. I.*

"You've heard about the petitions," he said.

"I've seen the woman at the courthouse."

"There are four hundred and eleven of them so far, and that's only the ones who can walk. Some of the half-forgotten sold so much of themselves they've forgotten there's anything to ask for. Somebody has to find those. Somebody has to go down the lists, match syllables to contracts, argue terms." He looked at her over the rim of his cup with eyes that had lost their name at the tollgate and gained something else instead—a steadiness, a settledness, like a man who has finally been given work worthy of his guilt. "The council will appoint a commission. Bureaucrats. Men who think a soul is a line item."

"And you want me on it."

"I want you to *lead* it." He slid the ledger across the counter. "You're the only living person who's beaten the under-court at its own game. You're the only advocate they'd trust who isn't afraid of them, and the only one the surface trusts who isn't lying to them. Also—" the ghost of his dry smile— "you owe them. You said so yourself. Debts want paying, Mistress Villalobos. I've built a career on knowing that."

Marguerite put her hand on the ledger. The cover was cold and honest under her palm.

Below the counter, on the shelf, the blue flame leaned toward her. On her wrist, the mark warmed—no god she could remember, no prayer she could recall, just warmth, just direction, just the sense—unprovable, undeniable—of something patient waiting to see what she'd build on the hole.

Outside, the golden rain kept falling, and somewhere in it a child was laughing in a language older than kingdoms, teaching it back to the city one word at a time.

"All right," Marguerite said, and opened the ledger to the first blank page. "Who's first?"
