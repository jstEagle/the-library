## 22. The Ledger Left Open

The rebuilt archive smelled of cut pine and new glue, and Sefa Ilvane hated it.

Not the building itself. The building was a miracle—three floors of the old Guildhall's bones given over to shelves, with tall windows that let in the honest gray light of Vell Hollow mornings, and a reading room where anyone could walk in off the street and ask for anything. No seals. No oaths of office. No Inquisitors at the door weighing what you were permitted to know. He had fought for that for six weeks, arguing with the provisional council until his voice went hoarse, and he had won, and the doors were open, and he still stood in the doorway some mornings just to watch a fishwife carry out a book on tides like it was bread she'd bought with her own coin.

What he hated was the smell. New paper had no memory in it. The old archive—the one that had burned the night the Edict fell, or been burned, the distinction mattered less every year—had smelled of salt and lamp oil and two hundred years of hands. This place smelled of nothing yet. It was an archive waiting to become one.

He climbed to his desk on the third floor, the one by the window where he could see the mountain, and set down his morning tea beside a ledger that was not new at all.

It was the oldest object in the collection now. Salt-stained, water-swollen at one corner, its spine held together with cord and stubbornness: the Villalobos debt ledger, the one he had copied in secret twenty years ago and paid for with his name and career. The original had come up out of the tunnels with them, tucked under Marguerite's arm like contraband, which in a sense it always had been. The Magistrate's court below the mountain had released it as part of the judgment—a courtesy between courts now that the door was shut, or perhaps a taunt, or perhaps simply the habit of a bureaucracy older than kingdoms. Ledgers wanted to be read. Even dead ones.

He opened it to the last written page and looked, as he did most mornings, at the final entry. His own hand had transcribed it long ago; the original script beneath was older, cramped, the ink brown with age:

*Received: one soul, unbegun, of the line Villalobos. Term: until the debt of blood is discharged in full. Witness: the god who does not forget.*

The debt had been discharged generations ago. The soul had been held past term. He had known both facts for twenty years and done nothing with them but copy them into hiding, and a child had walked across half the Hollow country carrying the proof in his chest because no one else would go down and get it.

Sefa dipped his pen. Then he turned past the last entry, past everything, to the blank pages at the back, and hesitated there the way you hesitate at any threshold you've been told your whole life is forbidden.

There was no rule against this anymore. That was the strange part. The Edict of Silence was broken—shattered in a single ringing chord the night Oren spoke the old tongue at the tunnel mouth, when magic came flooding back into Vell Hollow through the smallest voice in the city. The Guild had resigned itself in droves; three Inquisitors had shown up at the archive's opening asking, sheepishly, if they could volunteer. There was no law against writing down what was owed. There never had been. There had only ever been fear, wearing law's clothes.

He wrote the heading first, in careful capitals, because headings were how archives remembered what they were for:

**LEDGER OF DEBTS OWED**

Then, beneath it, the first entry:

*Owed by the city of Vell Hollow, being the beneficiary of contracts made with the court beneath the mountain: restitution to the village of the half-forgotten, whose names were sold piecemeal and whose persons remain incomplete. Number of claimants unknown. Syllables missing: uncounted.*

His pen stopped. He sat with it a while, listening to the building settle around him, the way buildings do in the morning when the sun finds their east face.

He had walked through that village on the way down. He had seen the people whose names had been sold—one syllable here, a whole family name there—and heard the missing pieces humming in the walls, the way Oren heard them, except that Sefa heard only silence where something should have been, which was worse. A woman had served them tea and could not tell them her own name, and had smiled about it, the way people smile when a wound has been theirs so long it has become furniture.

The city had profited from those contracts. Every charter, every guild grant, every generation of safety bought beneath the mountain had been purchased with pieces of the powerless. And the city had never once asked what the price had been, because the price was never paid by anyone who mattered enough to be asked.

That ended today. Not because a council decreed it—he had learned better than to wait for councils—but because a ledger existed, and ledgers are arguments that never sleep.

He wrote on. *Claimant: the woman called Ess, missing the syllable "eth" from her given name.* He had gotten that much from Ysolde, who remembered everything, who had gone back up the moon-road twice since the door closed to sit with the half-forgotten and write down what they could recall of themselves. *Claimant: the ferryman of the low crossing, missing his family name entire.* On and on, entry after entry, each one small, each one enormous. Debts owed. To be repaid by the city that profited.

When he finally looked up, the light had moved across his desk and his tea had gone cold, and Marguerite Villalobos was standing in the doorway of the reading room below, dripping rain, looking like a woman who had been arguing with someone important and lost on purpose.

*

She came up the stairs two at a time, which she still did, though she was forty-one and claimed her knees had opinions now. She dropped a leather satchel on his desk hard enough to rattle his inkwell.

"I need you to witness something," she said.

"You're drenched."

"It's Vell Hollow. Everything's drenched." She pulled a folded document from the satchel, damp at the corners, and spread it flat between his ledger and his tea ring. "Read it. Tell me if it's real."

He read it. It took him longer than it should have, because his eyes kept snagging on the seal at the bottom—not wax but pressed salt, the way the under-court sealed things, except that this seal bore no magistrate's mark. It bore a lantern, and around the lantern, in the pre-kingdom tongue, words that Oren had taught the council to pronounce and the council had mangled beautifully before getting right.

"It's real," Sefa said slowly. "This is a commission. From—" He looked up. "From the court? But the door is shut."

"The door is shut to souls going *down*." Marguerite pulled out the chair across from him and sat, wet and unbothered, leaving a dark bloom on the good new wood. "Turns out it was never shut to business coming *up*. Oren says the old courts always kept an advocate's office above ground. Someone bound to argue contract law on behalf of claimants who can't climb the stairs themselves. They haven't filled the post in four hundred years, because nobody above ground knew it existed." Her mouth did something that wasn't quite a smile. "Until a mercenary stood in front of the Thirteenth Magistrate and beat it with its own rulebook."

"They're offering *you* the office."

"They're offering whoever can do it. I'm the only person alive who's argued before them and walked out." She tapped the commission. "First outside advocate. It comes with no pay, no title, and a standing obligation to take cases from the half-forgotten before anyone else's."

"That last part isn't in the text."

"No. It's in the spirit. Oren made me swear it in the old tongue, and apparently that's binding in ways I'm still finding out about." She leaned back. "I don't remember my god, Sefa. You know that. Everyone knows that. I gave up twenty years of prayers to carry my brother home, and I don't miss them because you can't miss what isn't there. But I remember the shape of the work. I remember what it felt like to be sworn to something bigger than my own survival." She looked at the window, at the mountain, at the place where a door had ground shut forever with the Guild waiting outside it and lost. "I'd like to be sworn to something again. Even if the something is mostly paperwork."

Sefa laughed—a rusty sound; he was out of practice—and turned back to the commission. He read it properly this time, all the clauses, and found the trap-clauses absent, which surprised him more than their presence would have. The Magistrate had learned something too, apparently. Or Oren had read the fine print before letting it leave the mountain. Either way: clean. Binding. Real.

"There's a problem," he said.

"There's always a problem."

"This archive has a rule. I wrote it myself, first day: we serve any claimant who walks through the door, and we keep no counsel secret from another claimant whose case depends on the same records." He gestured at the ledger open before him, at the growing list of names. "If you take this office, half your caseload will come from these pages. And I'll be the one keeping the pages."

Marguerite looked at him for a long moment. Rain dripped off her braid onto the floor, patient as a metronome.

"So hire me," she said.

"What?"

"Hire me. As the archive's advocate. The commission makes me the court's officer; the salary makes me yours. Conflict resolved. Or rather—" she shrugged—"conflict *documented*, which in my experience is worth more than resolution."

He stared at her. Twenty years ago he had copied a forbidden ledger alone at night, certain that no one would ever come with a reason to use it, certain that his exile was the price of knowledge no one would spend. Now a woman who didn't remember her own god was proposing a legal structure over tea, and it was sound, and it was the kind of thing he should have thought of himself, and somehow that stung less than it warmed.

"I can't pay much," he said.

"I've slept in salt-cellars. My standards are geological."

"I'll draw the contract tonight."

"Draw it in duplicate. One for us, one for the mountain. Oren says they appreciate a copy." She stood, slinging the satchel, then paused at the edge of his desk, looking down at the ledger—the heading in his careful capitals, the entries beneath, the debts of a city laid out in ink like a map of everywhere it had failed. Her brother's soul sat in a salt lamp in her rooms above a charm shop that actually worked now; she had told Sefa once that the flame hummed when she read aloud, and that she'd started reading aloud every night, working through the archive's holdings one volume at a time, on the theory that a soul kept past term deserved to catch up on what it missed.

"First case," she said. "The woman called Ess. I want her syllable back within the season."

"The door is shut."

"Doors shut to souls, not to names. Oren thinks there's a procedure. There's always a procedure—that's the whole lesson of the last month." She grinned, and for a moment Sefa saw the mercenary she had been, and the advocate she was becoming, and the sister she had never gotten to be, all stacked in one expression like pages in a binding. "Get me the file."

He got her the file.

*

By evening the rain had cleared and the third-floor window showed the mountain going violet in the dusk, and Sefa sat alone with two documents side by side: the ancient Villalobos ledger, closed at last, its debt discharged and its soul returned; and the new one, open, eleven entries deep, waiting for tomorrow's claimants.

He thought about inheritance. He had spent twenty years believing he had inherited nothing but guilt—a stolen copy, a ruined career, a name traded away at a wraith's tollgate and never recovered. He had been wrong. What he had inherited was the *craft*: the patience to read a contract to its bones, the stubbornness to keep records no one thanked him for, the faith—because that's what it was, whatever word you dressed it in—that what is written down cannot be entirely lost.

The city owed debts it had never counted. The half-forgotten waited with their missing syllables. And somewhere below the mountain, behind a door that would never open again, an old court kept its laws, and needed a voice above ground to hold it to them.

Sefa uncapped his ink, drew the twelfth entry's ruling line, and left the rest blank.

Open, the way a good ledger should be. Waiting for whoever came through the door next.
