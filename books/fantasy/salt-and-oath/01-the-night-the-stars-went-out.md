## 1. The Night the Stars Went Out

The trouble with selling luck, Freya Almeida had learned over seven years of market mornings in Dunmark, was that everyone wanted it cheap and nobody wanted to admit they needed it.

"Three coppers for a salt-charm," she said to the fishwife who was currently turning her little wooden charm over as though she might find the luck hiding inside it like a worm in an apple. "Four if you want it blessed."

"Blessed by who?"

"By me. I'm very thorough."

The fishwife — Marta, third stall from the harbor gate, herring so fresh they still looked offended — snorted. "You're a fraud, Freya Almeida."

"I'm a *reasonably priced* fraud," Freya said, "and you bought one last month, and your nets came up full twice after."

"My nets come up full because I mend them, which is more than can be said for your charms, which are just knots and salt in a bit of cloth."

"And yet." Freya held out her palm.

Marta grumbled, paid three coppers, and tucked the charm into her apron with the furtive speed of a woman who would deny under oath that she'd ever done such a thing. That was the whole business, really. Nobody in Dunmark believed in magic — believing in magic had been against the law for twelve years, ever since the Purge, since the ward-laws, since the king's men burned the speaker-circles and put up border stones instead — but everybody bought a little insurance anyway. Salt against ill winds. Knotted thread against bad roads. A dried sprig of rowan, sold as "culinary herb, very traditional," against whatever people were afraid to name.

Freya's charms worked about as well as anyone else's, which is to say: not at all, mostly, except when they did, and she never asked too loudly why hers did more often than chance should allow.

Because asking loudly was how you ended up on a pyre, or worse, on a cart to Haldmere, and Freya Almeida had made a quiet career out of being unremarkable. She was twenty-six, brown-haired, brown-eyed, of entirely average height, with a laugh that carried just far enough to seem friendly and no farther. She lived above a chandler's shop on Tanner's Row. She overpaid her rent. She complained about the weather. She was, by every measurement that mattered, a woman made of beige.

She was also, though she'd have denied it with her last breath and possibly had planned to, a beast-speaker. The real kind. The kind the ward-laws were written about.

It wasn't dramatic. That was the thing nobody understood. It wasn't glowing eyes and whispered incantations. It was that the gulls on the breakwater had opinions about the weather, and she could hear them. It was that the chandler's cat, Bristle, told her when the widow on the corner was crying again at night, because Bristle worried. It was that horses liked her, dogs forgave her, and once — once, and never again, she'd sworn — a mule had given her a piece of advice about a man she'd been seeing that had turned out to be devastatingly correct.

Small things. Ordinary things. Things she kept behind her teeth the way other people kept coins in a loose floorboard.

And on the morning the stars went out, the first thing she knew of it was that the rats left Dunmark.

* * *

They went at midmorning, which was wrong in itself. Rats moved at dusk, at dawn, in shadow. These came down the gutters in broad daylight, past the feet of shoppers and the wheels of carts, in a long gray river of them, flowing east toward the sea gate with the grim purpose of emigrants. Not fleeing something. *Leaving.* There was a difference, and Freya felt the difference in her back teeth.

"You right, love?" asked Oskar the pie-man, following her gaze.

"Rats," Freya said. "Hate 'em."

"Good riddance, I say."

"Mm," said Freya, who was already doing arithmetic she didn't like. Because the pigeons had gone quiet. Market Square's pigeons were never quiet; they were the city's argumentative background hum, a permanent committee meeting about crumbs. Now they sat along the eaves of the wool-merchant's hall in a silent gray row, every head turned east, watching the rat-river go.

And old Bramble, the dray-horse who had pulled the same cider cart for nineteen years and whose opinion of humanity was low but stable, was pressing himself against the far wall of his stable yard and trembling.

Freya set down her tray of charms, told Oskar she needed more thread — a lie so smooth it surprised even her — and walked, at a pace she carefully kept casual, toward the stables.

Bramble saw her coming and his ears went flat.

"*Out,*" he said. Horse-speech isn't words exactly; it's weight and image and fear, and what Bramble gave her was a picture of the ground opening, of a cold coming down like a lid, of *wrongwrongwrong* laid over everything like a second sky. "*Run-run-run-run-run.*"

"Easy," Freya murmured, keeping her hands loose and visible, the way you did with a frightened horse and also, she suspected, with a frightened world. "Easy, old man. What is it? What do you see?"

He couldn't say. That was the horror of it. He could only feel it, some vast pressure building somewhere north and west, beyond the hills, beyond the border, a pressure like the moment before lightning — except stretched, drawn out, hours of it, the sky holding its breath.

"*Go,*" Bramble said again, and then, because he was old and tired and had decided she was one of the few humans worth the effort: "*Take your herd and go.*"

"I don't have a herd," Freya said. "I have a tray of knotted string."

But her hands had gone cold, and she stood there in the straw-smelling dimness of the stable yard, listening with the part of herself she was supposed not to have, and beneath the horse's terror she heard the rest of the city's animals saying the same thing in a hundred voices. The dogs wouldn't stop pacing. The chickens had piled themselves into one corner of their coop like children afraid of thunder. The gulls — the gulls, who feared nothing, who would scream at a whale — had stopped screaming.

Everything was waiting. Everything was listening to something she couldn't hear yet.

Freya went back to her stall. She smiled at customers. She sold a love-knot to a boy with sweaty palms and a protection charm to a grandmother who pinched her cheek and called her "dearie," and all the while the dread sat in her stomach like a swallowed stone, growing, growing, and the afternoon light got strange — too gold, too slanted, as though the sun itself were leaning away from something.

At four o'clock, the cats of Dunmark all looked up at the same moment.

She saw it from her stall: a dozen cats along the rooftops and windowsills of the square, frozen mid-stretch, heads tilted, staring at nothing — staring at the air itself, at a point somewhere above the northern hills. And then, one by one, they flattened their ears and *yowled*, all together, a sound like grief, and Freya's blood turned to ice water.

Something had arrived. Something had crossed whatever line there was to cross.

She was still standing there, half-risen from her stool, when the bells began.

Not the chapel bells. The *ward-bells* — the great bronze bells in the towers along the old wall, the ones that hadn't rung in twelve years, the ones every child in Dunmark was taught were ceremonial, historical, decorative. They rang now with a sound like the sky cracking, deep and ragged and desperate, and the whole market square went still as a held breath, and then it did not stay still.

"THE WARDS!" someone screamed from the wall road. "THE BORDER WARDS ARE DOWN!"

* * *

Afterward, Freya could never quite order the next hour in her memory. It came back to her in pieces, the way a dropped plate comes back: here a shard, there a shard, and never quite the whole shape of it.

The light going strange again — not gold this time but thin, bleached, as though someone had diluted the evening.

Oskar abandoning his pies, actual pies, hot ones, which told her more than any bell.

The crowd in the square turning like a flock, faces tipped north, where a faint glow that might have been the ward-light of Vhalen's border stones — a glow nobody living had ever seen, because it had simply always been there, the way the ground was always there — flickered once, twice, and went out.

And then the stars.

That was the shard that cut deepest. It was full dark by then, and the sky over Dunmark had been filling with the ordinary miracle of an autumn night, and Freya had looked up — everyone looked up, thousands of faces, one motion — and watched the stars go out. Not fade. Not cloud over. *Go out*, all of them, in a single instant, from horizon to horizon, as if a hand had closed over the whole burning roof of the world.

The dark that followed was total. It was a dark with texture. It pressed.

Somewhere in it, a child began to cry, and then another, and then the square dissolved into shouting and running and the shriek of a cart-horse breaking its traces, and Freya stood in the middle of it with her heart hammering and did the only useful thing she knew how to do, which was to grab the bridle of the panicked horse as it bolted past her stall and hold on, talking to it in the low steady voice that wasn't magic, wasn't anything, just a woman and a horse in the dark, until it stopped shaking.

"It's all right," she told it, and lied, and the horse knew she was lying and forgave her, because that's what horses do.

* * *

By midnight, the refugees had started arriving.

They came up the coast road from the south and east in a straggling torchlit line — crofters, drovers, a whole village's worth of people carrying what they could, which was almost nothing. The ward-line had been two days' ride from Dunmark, which meant whatever had happened had happened fast, faster than anyone thought possible, and the stories came in ahead of the people like foam ahead of a wave.

Lights in the hills. A sound like singing, but wrong. The ward-stones split clean through, each one, every one, like eggs tapped on a bowl's edge. And — this was the story that made the hair on Freya's arms stand — the animals gone first. Every beast south of the border walking east, walking *away*, days before any human knew to be afraid.

Freya stood in the crowd at the sea gate handing out what she had — blankets, bread, the contents of her own larder — and kept her face arranged into ordinary concern, and inside she was screaming. Because she had known. Hours before. The rats had known before her, and she had known before the bells, and she had stood in a market square selling knotted string to people who were going to lose their homes tonight, and she had said nothing, because what would she have said? *The animals are frightened, please evacuate based on my illegal magical communion with rodents*?

A woman with a baby wrapped in a wet shawl took Freya's blanket without seeming to see her. An old man sat down against the gatepost and wept without sound. A boy of maybe ten clutched a hen to his chest like a doll, and the hen, remarkably, let him.

"The stars'll come back," the boy was telling the hen. "They've got to come back. Stars don't just — they don't just *stop*."

"No," Freya heard herself say, kneeling beside him. "No, they don't."

She believed it, too, in that moment. Whatever had broken the wards was big and terrible, but it was a thing in the world, and things in the world could be fought, mended, survived. The stars were older than all of it. The stars were the one thing—

The gull hit the cobbles two feet in front of her.

It fell out of the pitch-black sky like a thrown stone, and Freya flinched, and then she was on her knees beside it without deciding to be, because it was dying — she could feel that the way she could feel weather coming, a certainty in the skin — and because some animal part of her had been waiting all day for exactly this.

It was a herring gull, silver-gray, its breast already stilling. One wing bent wrong. Its eyes were open and fixed on her face with an intensity that no bird's eyes should have.

"Don't," Freya whispered, meaning *don't die*, meaning *don't look at me like that*, meaning a dozen cowardly things.

The gull opened its beak. What came out was not a gull's cry.

"*Freya Almeida.*"

Her name. Her whole name, spoken in a voice like wind through a keyhole, like many voices braided into one, and every hair on her body rose, and every instinct she had spent seven years building screamed at her to look around, to check who had heard, to be beige, to be nothing —

But there was no time for cowardice, because the voice kept speaking, fast, spending itself like a candle burning at both ends.

"*Speaker. Little speaker, hear me. It is not a beast that broke the wards. It is not a storm. It sings and the songs are lies and it eats what it hears — it eats names, it eats remembering, it eats the past out of the world. The crown knows. The crown has known for years. Warn—*" The voice snagged, frayed. The gull's legs kicked once. "*Warn the— it will wear him like a glove, it will wear the king like a— find the keeper. The keeper of the drowned stone. Harbor. Under the— under the third—*"

"Whose voice?" Freya breathed, leaning close, heedless now of the crowd, of the law, of anything. "Whose voice is this? Who sent you?"

The gull's eye found hers. And in it, for one impossible moment, she saw reflected not the torchlight of the sea gate but a vast cold whiteness, snow falling upward, and a figure standing in it — small, human-shaped, *singing* —

"*Midwinter,*" said the gull, in the braided voice, in a hundred stolen voices at once, and Freya understood with sick certainty that she was hearing an echo of the enemy itself, a message passed mouth to mouth down a chain of dying birds. "*Before midwinter. Or there will be nothing left to remember, and no one left to remember it. Freya Almeida. You were named for this. You were* — "

The voice stopped.

The gull lay still on the cobbles, and its eyes were just a bird's eyes again, empty and bright, reflecting torchlight and nothing else.

Freya stayed kneeling. Her hands were shaking. Around her the refugee crowd flowed and murmured, nobody looking, everybody busy with their own terror, and she understood that she had just been given a message meant for one person in all the world, and that person was her, and she had no idea what to do with it except that it began at the harbor, under the third — the third what? Pier? Arch? Stone?

"Third," she whispered to the dead bird. "Third *what*?"

The gull did not answer. Dead things never did; that was the rule, the mercy, the wall between her gift and the country of the gone. But as she gently closed the bird's eyes with two fingers, Freya felt something she had never felt in her life: the sense of being *watched back*. Of the silence overhead — the starless, textured, listening dark — having noticed her.

Having taken her name.

She stood up on unsteady legs, pulled her cloak tight, and looked east toward the black water of the harbor, where somewhere beneath the piers a drowned ward-stone waited with a disgraced keeper she'd never heard of, at the start of the longest winter in the history of the world.

"Well," Freya Almeida said to no one, in the tone of a woman agreeing to a terrible idea, "bugger."

And she went to find her boots.
