## 2. What the Gulls Knew

The harbor at Dunmark smelled, on any ordinary morning, of fish guts and tar and the particular sourness of men who had been awake since before dawn arguing about the price of herring. This morning it smelled of fear.

Freya felt it before she saw it — a pressure against her gift like a hand pressed over a screaming mouth. The gulls were not wheeling and crying the way gulls did over a working harbor. They were packed onto the rooftops of the fish-houses in dense white rows, silent, every head turned toward the water. Hundreds of them. Thousands. A congregation that had forgotten how to sing.

She stopped at the top of Harborside Stair with her boots half-laced and her breath fogging, and she listened the way she was not supposed to listen.

*Down,* said the gulls, all together, without words. *Down. Down. It is down.*

"Right," Freya muttered. "Thank you. Very helpful. Down where? The sea is quite famously down."

A gull nearest her — an old one, gray-winged, missing a toe — turned its yellow eye on her and gave a single croak that landed in her chest like a dropped coin.

*Stone.*

Then it went back to staring at the water with the others, and Freya stood there feeling the way she always felt when the animals spoke plainly to her: as though someone had opened a door she'd spent thirty years nailing shut, and cold air was pouring through the gaps.

Her mother's voice, or the memory of a voice — thin now, worn smooth by years of telling herself she'd imagined it — murmured up from somewhere deep: *Never let them see, little wren. What they can't see, they can't burn.*

Freya pulled her hood up and went down the stairs into the crowd.

* * *

The harbor was wrong in smaller ways too. The fishermen were hauling their boats up onto the shingle rather than putting them out, which made no sense at all, because whatever had happened to the sky last night, the herring were still presumably herring. But no man would look at the water. They worked with their backs to it, quick and jerky, like children pretending not to be afraid of a dark doorway.

And the dogs. There were always dogs at Dunmark harbor, weaving between legs, stealing fish, conducting their small economies of theft and affection. Today there was one dog, a brindled lurcher, sitting at the very edge of the pier with its ears flat, and when Freya passed close it looked up at her and whined.

*Bad under,* it said. *Bad under the water. Old bad. Waking bad.*

"Yes," Freya said under her breath, "I'd gathered."

"You'd gathered what?"

She hadn't seen him. He was sitting against the bollard at the pier's end, half in the shadow of a stacked crab pot, and for a moment she took him for a heap of old sailcloth until he moved. Big man. Big the way sea-worn things are big — not fat, just vast, weathered down to essentials. Gray stubble. Eyes the color of dishwater with something underneath them that had once been sharper. A bottle of something sat in the sand beside him, cradled in one enormous hand like a doll.

"I talk to myself," Freya said. "It's cheaper than conversation."

"It is that." He looked at her properly then, and she watched his gaze do the thing gazes did — flick from her face to her hood to her hands, cataloguing, dismissing. Charm-seller. Market woman. Nobody. She let him think it. "You came down here on purpose, though. Everyone else is leaving the water. You're walking toward it."

"Someone told me to come."

"Who?"

"A bird," Freya said, because lying badly was sometimes better than lying well; people believed the truth shaped like a joke.

The man laughed. It was a terrible sound, rusty, like a hinge on a door nobody wanted to open. "A bird." He drank. "Well. Birds have been saying a lot lately. Nobody listens." He patted the stone beside him — an invitation, or maybe just a habit. "Sit if you like. I'm not good company, but I'm stationary."

Freya should have walked away. Every instinct bred into her by twenty years of hiding said walk away, say nothing, sell your charms, keep your head below the fence line. Instead she sat down on the cold stone at the end of the pier, because the gulls had said her name last night, and names meant something, and this man sat closer to the water than anyone else in Dunmark, close enough that whatever was under it might speak to him too.

"What happened to the wards?" she asked.

The bottle stopped halfway to his mouth.

For a long moment the only sound was the water slapping the pilings and, above, the vast soft rustle of ten thousand silent birds.

"Nobody asks that," he said finally. "They ask what happened to the stars. They ask if it's plague, if it's war, if it's the Vhaleni come over the border. Nobody asks about the wards." His voice went flat. "Because nobody remembers there were wards. Twelve years since the purge, and the whole kingdom decided wards were a story mothers tell. And I—" He stopped. Started again. "I kept them. That was my work. Ward-keeper, third circle, coast district. Twenty-two years. And three nights ago I felt them go, all along the border, like teeth going out of a jaw one by one, and I was—" he gestured at himself, at the bottle, at the crab pot, at the general ruin, "—here."

"Drunk."

"Drunk," he agreed. "Torv Askeland. Formerly of the King's Wardens, currently of this bollard." He didn't offer his hand. "You know the funny part? I reported it. Three years ago. Wrote it up proper, seals and everything. The stones were going quiet. Not failing — *quieting*. Like something was drinking them slow. And the reply came back from Haldmere that my reports were 'not consistent with observed conditions' and my commission was 'under review,' and six months later I was reviewed right out onto this pier." He smiled, and it was the worst smile Freya had ever seen, because there was nothing behind it but a man chewing on his own guilt like gristle. "So when they fell, the only man in Dunmark who knew they could fall was sitting here getting blind, and everyone in this city thinks the stars going out is some act of God."

"Maybe it is."

"Gods don't drink ward-stones." He said it simply, without drama, and Freya felt the truth of it settle into her bones like cold. "Stones are fed. That's what keeping is — you feed them, you sing to them, you give them a little of your own remembering so they've got something to hold. Mine haven't been fed in twelve years except by me, alone, and I'm one man, and I got old, and I got tired, and I got—" he lifted the bottle slightly, "—thirsty. And something out there knew exactly which nights I'd be weakest."

He looked at her sideways. "Why am I telling you this? I don't tell anyone this."

"Because I asked," Freya said, "and because you've been wanting to say it to someone for three years, and drunk men on piers will tell anything to a woman who sits down."

"That's fair," Torv said solemnly. "That's entirely fair."

* * *

It happened an hour later, and afterward Freya would understand that it had been arranged — that the thing beneath the water, or the gulls, or whatever moved pieces on this board had arranged it — but in the moment it was simply chaos.

The cart came down Harbor Road too fast, a dray horse in full panicked flight, eyes rolling white, foam flying off its bit. Behind it the carter ran and screamed, and ahead of it the market crowd scattered, and the animal came straight down the pier road toward the tangle of nets and crates and people where nothing living was going to survive the meeting.

Freya was moving before she decided to move.

Later she couldn't have said how it worked — she never could, any more than a singer can explain singing. She stepped into the horse's path, which was insane, and she opened herself, which was worse, and she reached out with everything she had spent a lifetime folding small and hiding, and she *spoke*.

Not words. Never words. A feeling, shaped and offered: *easy, easy, slow now, nothing is chasing you, I have you, easy.* The smell of crushed grass. The memory of a stable door opening onto morning. Safety, poured out like water.

The horse screamed — a high, breaking sound — and its forelegs locked, and it skidded through the shingle in a spray of stones and stopped with its nose eight inches from her chest, trembling all over, sides heaving. And Freya stood there in the middle of the silent street, hands out, holding a thousand pounds of terrified animal with nothing but her open self, and she knew — she knew with sick certainty — that she had just done it in front of forty witnesses.

The horse breathed into her palms. *Safe,* it said, wondering. *Safe?*

"Safe," she whispered. "Go home now. Go on."

It went, docile as a lamb, back to the carter, who stood clutching his hat and babbling thanks at her while the crowd murmured and pressed in, and Freya backed away with her heart hammering, already composing the lie — trick of standing still, horses respect calm, anyone could—

"No," said a voice behind her. Deep. Sober-sounding, suddenly, in the way a man sounds when he has just become very interested. "No, that wasn't standing still."

Torv Askeland had come up off the bollard like a ship coming off its moorings, and he was looking at her the way a starving man looks at bread. "I saw you," he said. "From the pier. I saw what you did. That wasn't horsemanship. That was—"

"Don't." Her voice came out low and hard, and the crowd noise seemed to dim around it. "Don't say it. Not here. Not anywhere."

"*Speaker,*" he said anyway, barely above a whisper, and the word went through her like a hook through the cheek. "You're a speaker. By the oath and the salt, you're a *speaker*, and you're standing in a market square twelve years after the purge pretending to sell charms."

"Keep. Your. Voice. Down."

He did. He actually did — closed his mouth, glanced at the crowd, and jerked his head toward the shadow of the net-sheds, and Freya followed him because the alternative was staying in the open with her skin crawling, and because — she admitted it to herself with real anger — because the gulls had sent her to him, hadn't they. *Stone,* they'd said. And here was the keeper of stones, and here she was, walking right into it.

In the shadow between the sheds, smelling of tar and old rope, Torv turned and faced her, and the drunkard was gone. Underneath the ruin of him there was something else, something that had kept twenty-two years of watch, and it was looking at her now with fierce, desperate attention.

"How long?" he demanded. "How long have you had it?"

"Since I was a child."

"And you hid it. Through the purge. Through twelve years of—" He shook his head slowly. "Do you know what I would have given, three years ago, for one speaker? One? To feed the stones properly? I wrote reports until my fingers bled and they shelved me, and the whole time the answer was walking around selling love-charms in the—"

"The answer," Freya cut in, "is a crime. The answer got people burned, Torv Askeland. My mother among—" She stopped. The sentence had gone further than she'd meant it to. Further than she'd ever let it go.

Torv went very still. "Your mother," he said carefully, "was a speaker."

"I didn't say that."

"You started to."

"I misspoke."

"You did not." He wasn't accusing her. That was the horrible part. He was looking at her with something like grief, like recognition, one keeper finding another kind of keeper. "Twelve years ago. The purge. They took people, and afterward—" he rubbed a hand over his face, "—afterward nobody could even say who'd been taken. Whole families with holes in them. I remember thinking at the time it was strange, that we mourned names we couldn't recall. I put it down to grief. Grief does strange things."

Freya's mouth had gone dry. "What do you mean, holes?"

"I mean—" He frowned, groping. "I mean I remember there was a woman. In Dunmark. Who spoke to animals, they said, before the purge. Everyone knew her. And I cannot, for the life of me, remember her name, or her face, or a single thing she ever said to me, and I *knew* her, girl. I bought eels from her husband every week. I think. Did she have a husband? Why can't I—" His hands had started to shake. "Why can't I remember?"

The gulls overhead, ten thousand strong, were absolutely silent.

Freya thought of her mother's voice worn smooth by years of telling herself she'd imagined it. Thought of the gap where a name should be, the hole she'd papered over with *she died, it was fever, it was fever, it was fever* until the paper wore through. Thought of a dying gull saying *Freya Almeida* in the dark.

"Something is eating us," she said quietly. "Isn't it. Something has been eating us for twelve years, and last night it finished the wards and came through."

Torv sat down. Not gracefully — he just folded, down onto a coil of rope, the big hands hanging between his knees, and for a moment he looked older than the sea itself.

"Three years," he said. "I've been sitting on this pier for three years telling myself I failed. That if I'd been sober, if I'd been stronger, the wards would have held." He laughed his terrible hinge laugh, softly this time. "And it turns out the walls were being eaten from inside the whole time, and I was guarding crumbs."

"I didn't come here to make you feel better."

"No," he agreed. "You came here because birds told you to. Which, incidentally, is the most ward-keeper thing I've ever heard. The old texts say the coast speakers used to run messages through the gull-lines before there were roads." He looked up at her. "They spoke to you by name, didn't they. That's why you came."

Freya said nothing, which was an answer.

"They don't do that for nothing," Torv said. "Birds aren't generous. If the gulls named you, something wants you at the center of this, and I'll tell you now, freely, as a man with nothing left to lose: I want no part of being at the center of anything. I want to sit on this bollard and drink until the winter takes me, and I'd have earned it." He pushed himself upright, swaying, and stood over her, huge and wrecked and stubborn. "But I also spent twenty-two years keeping a wall, and I know what happens tomorrow if nobody stands in the gap tonight. So."

"So," Freya said.

"So I'm going to help you whether you want it or not, and you're going to hate it, and I'll probably be useless and drunk through half of it." He stuck out one enormous hand. "Torv Askeland. Formerly of the King's Wardens. Currently at your disposal, gods help us both."

Freya looked at the hand. Everything in her that had survived twelve years by trusting no one screamed at her to turn and walk back up the stairs and sell her charms and keep her head down until the world ended politely elsewhere.

But the gulls had said her name. And the horse had said *safe* in her own voice's echo. And somewhere under the black water a drowned ward-stone waited, and she was suddenly, terribly sure that she was not going to be allowed to leave this harbor without touching it.

She shook his hand. His grip swallowed hers whole.

"One condition," she said. "We go look at that drowned stone, and then we decide together what's true. No oaths, no orders, no Keeper-and-speaker nonsense. I've had enough of kings and their arrangements."

"Agreed," Torv said. Then he paused, and something crossed his weathered face — surprise sharpening into alarm. "Although if you're planning to swear no oaths ever again, you may want to hear what the stone has to say first. Because I've been thinking, these past three years, about why the wards failed *now*, and there's one thing I never wrote in any report."

"Which is?"

"The stones weren't just going quiet," Torv said. "They were going quiet in *order*. From the oldest first. Like something reading a book from page one." He looked past her, out at the black water, and lowered his voice as though the harbor might repeat it. "And last spring, the last stone to go quiet before the border fell — the one I could still feel, faint, right up until the end — it wasn't singing anymore, girl. It was *reciting*. Over and over, same words, all night long. And I'm no speaker, but I'd swear on my commission that what it was reciting was a list of names."

The gulls rose off the rooftops all at once — ten thousand wings, one motion, no sound — wheeled once over the harbor, and settled again into silence.

Freya found she was holding her breath.

"Names," she said.

"Names," Torv agreed grimly. "And I've a horrible feeling, Miss Almeida, that yours was on it."

Behind them, at the mouth of the harbor, the tide began to go out — fast, far faster than any tide had a right to, dragging the water back from the pilings like a sheet being pulled off a body, and revealing, wet and black and ancient, the top of a stone that had no business being visible at all.
