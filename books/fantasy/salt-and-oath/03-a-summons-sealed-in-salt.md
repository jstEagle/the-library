## 3. A Summons Sealed in Salt

Torv Askeland had learned to measure his mornings by the state of the bottle. A full one meant a bad day ahead; an empty one meant he'd already had the bad day and could get on with things. The morning the courier came, the bottle was half full, which was somehow the worst of all possible arrangements — enough left to be tempted by, not enough to justify opening.

He was sitting in the back room of the Saltcellar, the dockside tavern where the landlord had stopped charging him for the corner bench on the understanding that Torv's presence discouraged fights rather than encouraged them. Twelve years ago this would have been unthinkable. Twelve years ago, Torv Askeland had been Keeper of the Western Marches, third rank of the ward-keepers' order, a man whose signature carried weight in four provinces. Now his signature carried weight only insofar as it got him credit at the Saltcellar, and even that was more charity than contract.

The courier arrived at midmorning, which was itself strange. Royal couriers did not come to dockside taverns at midmorning. Royal couriers came to what remained of official residences at dawn, all brass buttons and disapproval, and they certainly did not come looking for men who smelled of last night's ale.

This one stopped in the doorway, took in the dim room, the sleeping fisherman slumped over a table, the landlord polishing a mug that had given up hope long ago, and asked, in a voice pitched carefully between courtesy and command:

"I'm looking for Askeland. Torv Askeland."

The whole room looked at Torv. This was another thing that didn't happen. In twelve years, the room had never once looked at him for any reason except to check whether he'd heard the joke.

"That's me," Torv said, and was surprised at how steady his voice came out. "Unless there's another one, in which case I hope he owes you less money."

The courier did not smile. Couriers of the crown were selected partly for their inability to smile, Torv suspected; it kept the wax seals intact. He crossed the room, produced a leather case from inside his coat, and laid it on the table before Torv with both hands, the way you'd set down something alive.

"From the Lord Protector's office, by authority of the crown," the courier recited. "You're to read it privately. You're to answer within the day."

Then he stood there. Waiting. Which meant he wanted to see Torv's face when it was opened, or he wanted to make sure Torv didn't simply tuck it under his arm and wander off into the harbor. Both were fair concerns. Torv had done less responsible things with more important objects.

The case was good leather, oiled and supple, the kind that cost more than Torv's current wardrobe entire. Inside was a fold of vellum.

And that was when Torv's hands began to shake.

Not because of the writing. The writing was ordinary court script, black ink, a formal summons commanding one *Askeland, T., formerly of the Ward-Keepers' Order* to present himself at the capital of Haldmere within fourteen days, there to render account of matters touching the security of the realm. He'd received a dozen such documents in his working life. They were boilerplate with your name pasted in.

It was the vellum itself.

Salt-preserved. He knew it the way you know your mother's voice — instantly, bodily, before thought could interfere. The old magic. Vellum cured in brine drawn at the dark of the moon, worked with ash and prayer until the page itself became a small ward against forgetting. Anything written on salt-vellum stayed written. Not just legible — *true*. It resisted alteration, erosion, and, if you believed the oldest accounts, it resisted the slow dishonesty of memory as well. What was sealed in salt remained.

The ward-keepers had written their most dangerous records on it. And the ward-keepers' order had been dissolved twelve years ago, its members scattered, its practices outlawed, its archives burned in the courtyard of the Citadel while the king watched from a balcony with his new advisors smiling around him.

Torv looked up at the courier. The courier looked back with the flat patience of a man paid by the mile.

"Who wrote this?" Torv asked.

"I'm not told."

"Who told you to deliver it?"

"The office of the Lord Protector Serane."

"And did anyone in that office mention," Torv said, keeping his voice very level, very bored, the way you talk past a guard dog, "that the material this document is made of has been illegal to produce, possess, or distribute for twelve years? Carrying penalties I remember quite well, since I was among those who enforced them?"

Something flickered behind the courier's eyes. Not guilt. Recognition. The look of a man who had carried stranger things for stranger masters and had decided some questions were above his pay.

"I'm told to say," the courier offered, after a silence, "that the person who wrote it hopes you'll remember how to read it properly."

*Properly.* Not just the words on the surface. Salt-vellum carried a second text, written in the curing, readable only by touch — by running a thumb along the grain and feeling where the salt had been worked thick and where thin. It was a code older than the kingdom, older than the crown. Torv hadn't used it in twelve years. His thumbs remembered anyway.

"I'll answer within the day," Torv said.

The courier nodded once, turned, and left. At the door he paused, glanced back at Torv with an expression that might, in a man capable of smiling, have been sympathy, and then was gone into the grey morning.

* * *

The landlord brought him tea without being asked, which was the landlord's way of saying he'd noticed something. Torv sat with the vellum flat on the table and his hands flat on either side of it, like a man steadying a bird.

*Askeland, T., formerly of the Ward-Keepers' Order.*

*Formerly.* Someone at court knew exactly who he was. Not the drunk at the Saltcellar, not the cautionary tale other keepers traded in whispers — but the man himself, his rank, his record. And whoever it was had chosen salt-vellum deliberately, knowing what it would mean to him. Knowing it would cut through twelve years of deliberate forgetting the way a whistle cuts through noise.

He touched the grain.

The second text rose under his thumb in bumps and hollows: *The wards are broken. We know why. We know you know why. You are not summoned to be punished. You are summoned because you are the last keeper who heard them sing.*

Torv read it three times.

Then he put his head in his hands and laughed, because the alternative was to weep in front of the landlord, and he had a reputation — a poor one, but a reputation — to maintain.

Because here was the thing about the night the stars went out. Here was the thing Torv had told no one, not the inquiry board, not the magistrates who stripped his rank, not the sympathetic priest who'd tried to talk him out of the bottle: the wards hadn't failed. He had been at the western anchor-stone when it happened. He had felt them go, and they hadn't shattered the way a wall shatters, all at once and from outside.

They had been *opened*. From the inside. By someone with the right words, the old words, the words only keepers and speakers knew.

And Torv had spent twelve years letting everyone believe it was his negligence, because the alternative explanation involved things the crown had outlawed so thoroughly that even saying them aloud felt like picking up a hot coal. Speakers. The beast-speakers, the word-speakers, the men and women born with voices that things answered. Purged, proscribed, erased — and not merely banished, but *forgotten*, which was a cruelty Torv still didn't fully understand. People spoke of the purge and could not name a single speaker they'd known. Whole friendships had holes in them shaped like people. Torv remembered because the wards had sung through him every day of his working life, and the songs still held the shape of the ones who'd helped raise them.

Someone at court knew. Someone at court was using the outlawed medium to call in the outlawed man. *Gathering*, said a cold little voice in the back of his skull that sounded unpleasantly like his old commander. *That's what this is. A gathering. Question is, gathering for what, and gathering whom else?*

Because if they had him, they might have others. And if they had others—

Torv's mind went, unwillingly, to the market square two days past. To the woman selling charms with sea-glass and thread, the one who had walked into a stampeding cart-horse's path without a flicker of fear and spoken to it — not loudly, not dramatically, just spoken, the way you speak to a frightened child — and the horse had stopped as though it had run into a wall of quiet. Freya Almeida. He'd known what she was before she'd finished her first sentence. You didn't spend fifteen years listening to wards sing without learning what a true voice sounded like, even one wrapped up small and hidden under market patter.

She'd lied to his face afterward, cheerfully and badly, and Torv had liked her immediately and trusted her not at all, and both feelings had grown since.

*You are not summoned to be punished.*

Fine. But summoned to be *used*? That he believed completely. Court did not do anything without wanting something, and a disgraced keeper plus a hidden speaker was not a rescue party. It was inventory. Tools, collected.

So don't go, said the sensible voice, the bottle-adjacent voice, the voice that had governed him for twelve years. Stay here. Drink your tea. Let the kingdom sort out its own broken sky.

Except the kingdom wasn't sorting anything out. Refugees were flooding down the coast road with stories of villages gone silent, elders who couldn't recall their grandchildren's faces, birds that wouldn't land. He'd seen a gull yesterday sitting motionless on a mooring post for six hours, staring inland, and gulls did not stare inland; gulls had the attention span of weather.

The wards had been opened from inside. Something was coming through, or something was already here, and the only people who might understand it were precisely the people the crown had spent twelve years erasing. Whoever wrote that summons knew it too. That was either the most hopeful thing Torv had encountered in a decade or the most frightening.

Possibly both. Possibly, he thought, wryly, that was what hope was made of now.

* * *

He found her where he'd expected to — the market square, the charm stall, the sea-glass catching the flat grey light. Business was slow. Business everywhere was slow, unless you sold candles or padlocks, both of which had tripled in price since the stars went out.

Freya saw him coming and her face did something complicated — a flicker of welcome quickly folded away under professional neutrality, like a shopkeeper hiding the good stock when the wrong customer walks in.

"Master Askeland." She said it like a title, which was funny, since the only thing Torv was master of these days was his corner bench. "Back for more charms? I can recommend the anti-nightmare bundle. Very popular lately. Slightly more nightmares than usual to protect against, mind."

"I need to talk to you somewhere with fewer ears," Torv said.

Her eyes went, quick and light, to the gulls on the rooftops. Then to the fishmonger's cart. Then back to him. "Fewer ears than a market square is a short list this week," she said. "But there's a stretch of seawall past the net-drying racks where even the crabs pretend not to listen."

They walked. She moved like someone perpetually about to change direction, and twice he caught her tilting her head at nothing — listening to something he couldn't hear, some conversation between sparrow and sparrow, or worse, between sparrow and silence.

At the seawall she stopped, folded her arms, and waited with the patience of a woman who had sold charms to liars for years and could out-wait any of them.

Torv took out the vellum. Unfolded it. Held it up.

She went still.

Not shocked, exactly. Recognizing. Her fingers actually twitched toward it, an instinctive reach, before she caught herself and tucked the hand under her arm again.

"Where did you get that?" she said quietly.

"A royal courier handed it to me this morning in a dockside tavern, which tells you either the crown's intelligence has collapsed entirely or this is exactly as strange as it looks. It's a summons. To Haldmere. Fourteen days." He turned it so she could read the court script. "And the vellum itself is the part that matters. Salt-cured. Old work. Outlawed same as everything else we're not supposed to talk about."

"You talk about it fine," Freya observed.

"I talk about it *badly*, in empty rooms, to bottles. It's different." He ran his thumb along the grain. "There's a second message in it. Written in the curing. It says the writer knows the wards didn't fail — knows they were opened — and wants me at court because I'm the last keeper who heard them sing."

Freya looked out at the harbor. A fishing boat was coming in low in the water, and the gulls wheeled around it screaming their usual grievances, and Torv noticed she flinched at the sound, very slightly, as if the gulls had said something rude.

"They want you to fix it," she said.

"They want to *use* me to fix it, or use me to find whatever else they think will fix it, or use me as bait, or blame me for all of it in a nicely lit courtroom. Court doesn't send for people out of gratitude, especially not people it spent twelve years pretending didn't exist." He paused. "Which brings us to the awkward part."

"There's an awkward part? I thought the outlawed vellum was the awkward part."

"The awkward part," Torv said, "is that I'm going. And I think you should come with me."

Silence. A crab clattered somewhere below the seawall, honoring its promise not to listen.

"No," Freya said.

"I haven't made my argument yet."

"You don't need to. The answer's no. I sell charms. I have a stall. I have—" She gestured vaguely at Dunmark, at the market, at the whole warm shabby apparatus of her ordinary life. "Whatever you think I am, going to the capital and standing in front of the people who outlawed— who did what they did— is not on my list of things to survive."

"Nothing's on anyone's list of things to survive anymore," Torv said. "The stars are out. The birds have opinions. There are villages up the coast where grandmothers are asking visitors their own names. Whatever this is, it isn't going to respect the borders of your stall." He gentled his voice, which took effort; gentleness had rusted in him. "I'm not asking you to declare yourself. I'm asking you to be nearby. Because if they're gathering speakers — and I'd stake my nonexistent pension on it — then they'll find you eventually, Freya. Better they find you standing next to me, with a summons in hand, than find you on their own schedule, in their own way."

"And what do you get out of it?" she asked sharply. "Don't dress it up. Everyone dresses it up. What does Torv Askeland get for delivering a hidden speaker to the same court that burned the order he belonged to?"

The question landed harder than she could know, mostly because it was exactly the question he'd been failing to ask himself all morning.

What did he get? Protection — hers, wrapped around his own guilt like a coat. Access. Relevance. A chance to stand in front of whoever had opened the wards and finally stop being the man who let it happen. And yes, use — the cold arithmetic of it, one hidden speaker, one disgraced keeper, each making the other valuable enough to keep alive.

"I get to stop drinking myself toward an answer I already know," he said finally. "I get to stand in front of whoever did this and ask them one question with my whole chest. And I get—" He stopped. Started again, honestly, which hurt more. "I get a shield that talks back. If they're going to gather speakers, then God help me, I'd rather the speaker they gather is one I've seen calm a runaway horse, one who's clever and stubborn and mean with sarcasm, than some poor soul they drag out of hiding terrified. I'd rather you were *armed*. Even if the arming is me, and even if part of why I want you armed is that I need you. Both things are true. I won't pretend otherwise. You deserve better than pretending."

Freya stared at him for a long moment. The wind came off the water and pulled at her hair, and somewhere above them a gull cried out — and this time Torv would have sworn the cry had a shape to it, almost a word, almost *go*, though he was a keeper, not a speaker, and maybe he was only hearing what he wanted.

"You realize," Freya said slowly, "that if I come with you, and this is a trap, I will never forgive you."

"Yes."

"And if it's not a trap, and we actually manage to help, you'll still have dragged me to the one place in the world designed to destroy people like me."

"Yes," Torv said. "I've been honest with myself about that since roughly nine o'clock this morning. It hasn't helped."

Another silence. Longer. When she spoke again, her voice had changed — quieter, stripped of the market patter, young underneath in a way that startled him.

"My mother told me once," she said, "never to go where the crown can count you." Then she frowned, faintly, like a hand brushing over a smudge on glass. "Or — someone told me that. Someone..." She shook her head, the frown dissolving into the practiced brightness he was beginning to recognize as armor. "Anyway. Fourteen days, you said?"

"Fifteen, if we argue about it for a day first."

"Don't tempt me." She looked at the summons in his hands, at the salt-white vellum gleaming dully in the grey light, and Torv watched her make a decision the way you watch someone step off a ledge — hoping for wings, fearing the ground. "All right, Master Askeland. But we travel as charm-seller and hired escort. Nobody at that court hears so much as a whisper out of me that isn't about sea-glass and thread. And if I tell you to run, you run, and you don't ask questions until we've stopped running. Agreed?"

"Agreed," Torv said, and meant it, and understood even as he said it that he was agreeing to a great many things he had no intention of honoring if it came to keeping her safe. Betrayal dressed as duty. Duty dressed as protection. He wondered if she saw through him. He suspected she did. He suspected, further, that she was dressing her own betrayals just as finely, and that this was simply what trust looked like now — two people holding knives, showing each other the handles first.

They turned back toward the market together.

And that was when the gull came down.

It dropped out of the grey sky onto the seawall not three feet from Freya — an old bird, storm-feathered, one eye clouded white — and it did not scream, and it did not beg for bread. It looked at her with its good eye, and it spoke, and though Torv had spent his life among singing wards and talking beasts by proxy, hearing a wild thing form actual words still raised the hair on his arms.

"*Haldmere listens,*" the gull said, in a voice like wind through rigging. "*Go careful. The crown has ears where the head should be.*"

Then it ruffled, shook itself into ordinariness, and flew off toward the fish carts, screaming for scraps like any other beggar.

Freya and Torv looked at each other.

"Well," Torv said at last. "That's ominous."

"It's worse than ominous," Freya said quietly, watching the bird go. "That warning wasn't for me. Gulls don't waste words." She turned to him, and her face had gone pale and certain all at once. "Somebody *sent* that message, Torv. Somebody who knew we'd be standing right here. Which means somebody already knows we're coming — and they wanted us to hear it before we ever set foot on the north road."

Above the rooftops of Dunmark, where the stars should have been waiting for evening, the sky stayed stubbornly, terribly blank.

Fourteen days to Haldmere. Torv found he was no longer sure whether he was walking into a summons or a snare — and the worst of it was, the two were starting to look remarkably alike.
