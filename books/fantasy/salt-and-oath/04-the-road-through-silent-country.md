## 4. The Road Through Silent Country

The mule was named Duchess, and she lied about everything.

She lied with her ears first — pinning them flat to suggest she would absolutely bolt if Freya came one step closer with that saddle. She lied with her teeth, baring them at Torv in a way that had him convinced she'd bite, when in truth she hadn't bitten anyone since the reign of the old king's grandfather. And she lied most of all with her eyes, which held the deep, wounded patience of a creature who had never once been fed on time.

"She's saying you smell like herring," Freya told Torv, tightening the girth strap while Duchess stood with the saintly stillness of a statue. "Also that your boots are an insult."

"My boots are good leather."

"She's very traditional. She prefers felt."

Torv looked at the mule. The mule looked at Torv. Something passed between them that Freya chose not to translate, because it involved words she'd have blushed to say aloud in daylight.

They left Dunmark on the north road two days after the summons came, and three days after the stars went out. The sky stayed empty. Not cloudy — *empty*, a clean black bowl over the world by night and a flat pale nothing by day, as though someone had scrubbed the heavens down to the primer coat. People along the road had begun calling it the Blank, which Freya thought was too tidy a name for something that made her chest hurt every time she looked up.

Duchess carried their packs. Torv walked, because he said he didn't trust anything with four legs that hadn't sworn him an oath, and Freya walked beside him because walking gave her hands something to do besides shake.

*North,* the summons had said. *To Haldmere, by the king's need.* Written on vellum preserved in salt, the old way, the outlawed way. Someone at court knew what Freya was. Someone at court wanted her there anyway.

She tried not to think about what happened to speakers who were wanted rather than hunted. The last purge had been twelve years ago, and it had been thorough, and everyone agreed it had been necessary, and no one could say why.

The first village without birds was called Marrow's End, which even before everything went wrong had been a name chosen by someone with a grudge against hope.

Freya noticed it from half a mile out. The road climbed through hedgerows loud with sparrows, and then the hedge ran out and the fields began, and the sound simply — stopped. Not faded. Stopped, like a door closing.

"Huh," said Torv.

"That's not 'huh,'" Freya said. "That's a whole lot worse than 'huh.'"

She reached out, the way she always did, the way she'd done since she was small enough to fit inside a flour barrel — not a spell, nothing so grand, just a listening. A letting-down of the gate in her chest that let the voices of living things wash through. In Dunmark it was a roar: dogs gossiping, rats complaining, gulls reciting their endless liturgy of grievance. On the road it was sheep and crows and the low muttering contentment of well-fed worms.

At Marrow's End, the listening came back to her like a shout into a well that never echoed.

There were animals. She could see them — a dog chained by a smithy, geese in a pen, a cat asleep on a wall in a patch of sun that couldn't possibly be warm under that blank sky. They moved. They breathed. But when she listened, where their voices should have been there was only a smooth, polished nothing, like river stones worn blank.

Except the cat. The cat had a voice, thin and frayed as a rope about to part, and what it was saying, over and over, was:

*gone gone gone gone gone*

"Torv." Her mouth had gone dry. "Something's wrong here. Wrong wrong. Not bandits-wrong. Wrong-wrong."

He'd already stopped walking. He was looking at the village the way he looked at ward-stones — squinting, head tilted, reading something in the air that ordinary eyes weren't built for. Twelve years a ward-keeper before the disgrace; some habits don't wash out.

"The wards," he said slowly. "Every settlement this far north sits inside a ward-net. Old work, older than the kingdom. You can't see it, but you can feel it, like a roof over your head. It's supposed to keep things out." He paused. "It's not keeping anything out anymore. It's not doing anything at all. These are dead wards. Every one of them, dead as the stones they're carved on."

"Can they be fixed?"

"Everything can be fixed." He started forward again, jaw set. "Question is whether fixing them matters, when whatever broke them is still standing right there."

* * *

Marrow's End had people, and the people were the worst part.

They weren't sick. They weren't starving, or fleeing, or rioting, or any of the things Freya knew how to brace herself against. They were going about their business — splitting wood, drawing water, arguing cheerfully about a fence post — in a village where the air itself had been scooped hollow.

An old woman sat outside the tavern shelling peas into a bowl, and Freya heard her talking to herself in the aimless way of the old, and so at first she didn't understand what she was hearing.

"—and then he'll want the brown bread, not the white, he always wants the brown, my Tam, sweet tooth like his father—" A pause. The peas kept clicking into the bowl. "Tam. That's — Tam. Tamsin? No. No, Tam is the boy. My boy. He'll be wanting..." The clicking slowed. "I have a boy. I know I have a boy. Brown bread. Why do I know about the bread?"

Freya stopped in the road so suddenly that Duchess walked into the back of her and complained at length.

The old woman looked up, saw strangers, and smiled with perfect, unbroken warmth. "Travelers! You'll stay for soup. There's soup. I made it for — for someone. For the family. I have a family." The smile flickered, just at the edges, like a candle in a draft. "Don't I?"

"You do," Freya said gently. She crouched by the woman's chair. "You have a son. His name is—"

"Tam," the woman said instantly, relieved. "Yes. Tam. Thank you, dear. Isn't that funny, it goes right out of your head, like water through a sieve. You go to fetch it and it's just — gone. Everyone's the same, these past weeks. Old Marta called her own husband 'sir' for two days before it came back to her. We laugh about it." She laughed a little, to demonstrate. It was the loneliest sound Freya had ever heard. "We laugh about it so we don't do the other thing."

"What other thing?" Torv asked quietly.

The old woman looked at him. Her eyes were clear and brown and terribly sane.

"Crying," she said. "We don't do the crying thing. There wouldn't be any stopping."

Inside the tavern, the innkeeper had nailed a list above the hearth. Names, dozens of them, in different hands, some fresh, some weathered. Under each name, smaller writing. *My daughter. Likes eels, hates thunder. Gap in her teeth.* *My brother. Whistles when he's nervous.* *Mother. Sang off-key on purpose.*

"Memory ledger," the innkeeper said, following their gaze. He was a big man with flour on his forearms and fear worn smooth into his face. "Whole village keeps one now. Write everything down while you've got it. When it slips, someone reads it back to you." He wiped his hands on his apron. "Works, mostly. Except—" He hesitated.

"Except?" Freya prompted.

"Except sometimes you read it back and it doesn't take. You read it and read it and it's just words. Like telling someone else's story." He looked at the list, and his voice dropped. "My wife's entry is starting to feel like that. *Loves rosemary. Scared of deep water. Laughed through her nose.* I know it's true. I wrote it. But it's getting — thin. Like a shirt washed too many times."

Freya's skin crawled. Because she understood, then, what she was hearing — or rather, what she wasn't. She let her listening open all the way, wider than she'd dared since Dunmark, wide enough to hurt.

And beneath the village — beneath the dead wards, beneath the birdless fields, beneath the whole silent country stretching north — she heard it.

Not a voice. A *chewing.*

Slow. Patient. Vast. The sound of something working through the world the way a moth works through wool, fiber by fiber, thread by thread. It wasn't eating sound. Sound was just what fell away when it finished with a place. It was eating what the sounds were *made of*. The warm weight of a name. The shape of a face behind closed eyes. The reason a woman shells peas for a son whose name keeps slipping.

It was eating memory. All of it. Everywhere, faintly, like frost creeping across a window — and here, in Marrow's End, it had already eaten down to the glass.

"Freya." Torv's hand was on her arm. "You've gone white as salt. What is it?"

"It's not the wards failing," she whispered. "Wards are just — a lid. A lid on a pot. Something took the lid off, and what's underneath is hungry, and it's been under there a long, long time, and now it's *eating*." She swallowed. "It's eating remembering, Torv. Out of people's heads. That's what the silence is. That's what the Blank is. It ate the stars' names and the sky forgot how to hold them."

Torv stared at her. She watched him decide — watched the drunk's reflexive *she's mad* rise up in him and get shoved back down by twelve years of guilt and three days of a starless sky. Ward-keepers, she was learning, believed in hunger the way farmers believed in weather.

"And the summons," he said hoarsely. "Salt-preserved vellum. Old magic. Somebody at court knows exactly what's happening. Somebody's known for a while."

"Then we'd better hope they're the helpful kind of somebody."

"Yes," Torv said. "We'd better."

* * *

They stayed the night at Marrow's End because Duchess refused to travel after dark, and because the old woman with the peas pressed soup on them with the fierce hospitality of someone holding onto the last warm thing she owned.

Freya slept badly, in a room above the tavern, with the window shut against a cold that had no business existing in harvest season. Sometime past midnight she woke to a sound and lay rigid in the dark, listening.

The chewing was closer. Or louder. Or perhaps it had simply gotten around to noticing *her* — the small bright knot of remembered things walking north through its larder. She felt it pause, the way a diner pauses over an unexpected spice. Felt something vast and attentionless turn its regard toward the little room, the little bed, the little woman in it who could hear.

*You,* it seemed to say, in no words at all. *You are full of names.*

She pulled the blanket to her chin and did the bravest thing she knew how to do: she listened back, deliberately, and filled her head to bursting with everything she had. Her mother's hands kneading dough. The smell of Dunmark harbor at dawn. Torv's face the moment he'd realized what she was — the hope and horror fighting openly across it. Duchess's lies. The gull's dying warning, spoken in her own name.

*I'm not on the menu,* she told the dark, with more conviction than she felt. *I'm the cook.*

The attention withdrew, unhurried, unoffended. Somewhere far off, a thread of the world went quiet forever.

In the morning, the old woman met them on the road with a bundle of bread and a request: "When you find whoever can fix this — and you will, dear, you have the look of fixers — tell them to hurry. I've still got Tam. Most days I've still got Tam. But this morning it took me a full minute, and a minute is a long time to look at your own child's name and see nothing."

Freya promised. It was the only currency she had.

* * *

The second silent village was worse, because by then she knew what to listen for.

Hollowmere — another cheerful name, another lie — had lost more than Marrow's End. Here the ledgers covered entire walls of the meetinghouse, floor to rafter, generations of names and details written and rewritten as the ink of memory faded. An elder named Brand met them at the well, a straight-backed man of seventy who greeted them courteously and then asked, with careful politeness, whether they might know his children.

"I have four," he said. "Or I had four. The ledger says four. Two went south for trade, one married a cooper, one died young of fever." He recited it like scripture, because that's what it had become. "But when I try to see their faces, I get — weather. Just weather. Am I a man who never had children, walking around in a man's life?"

Freya took his hands in hers. "You had four children," she said. "And one of them whistled when he was nervous, and one of them is alive and married to a cooper, and you loved them so much you wrote it all down so you'd never lose them. That's not nothing, Brand. That's love with the details scraped off. The details come back. Things like this — they come back."

She believed it when she said it. Mostly. The chewing under the earth made belief hard work.

Torv spent the day examining Hollowmere's ward-stone, a moss-eaten pillar at the village edge, and came back grim-faced and dust-covered.

"Same as the others. Dead. But look at this." He showed her the stone's face, where old carvings spiraled inward — knots and interlacings, and at the center, worn nearly smooth, a single word in the old script. "Every ward-stone in the north country has this word at its heart. It's not a spell-word. Spells are instructions. This is — a name. The wards aren't walls, Freya. They're *held shut*. By someone. Was held, anyway." He brushed moss from the word, and even touching it made his fingers tremble. "Twelve years ago they outlawed the old craft. Purged every speaker they could find. And I always thought the wards kept working because wards are wards. But what if they kept working because the ones who held them were — elsewhere. Bound somewhere. Still holding, even after."

"After what?"

"After the crown decided they were criminals." He looked north, toward Haldmere, invisible beyond the hills. "What if the purge didn't break the wards? What if the purge *was* the breaking? What if they took the lock off the pot themselves?"

Freya thought of the chewing. Of the patient, bottomless appetite beneath the silent country. Of a lid lifted twelve years ago, and something underneath that had waited, politely, all this time — and then, three weeks ago, when the stars went out, had finally, *finally* sat down to eat.

"Duchess," she said, because someone had to say something light or she was going to sit down in the road and cry, "is giving you a look. She says you talk too much and she agrees with everything you're saying, which should worry you."

Torv glanced at the mule. The mule gazed back with vast, innocent insincerity.

"One of these days," he muttered, "you're going to tell me what she actually says, and I'm going to be flattered, and then I'm going to be deeply sorry."

"Not today," Freya said, and swung up onto the cart seat, and pointed Duchess north.

* * *

They made nine miles that afternoon through country growing quieter with every league. Not just birdless now — the crickets had stopped, and the wind moved through the pines without sound, and once they passed a farmstead where a family stood in their yard staring at their own hands as though trying to remember what they were for.

Toward dusk, Freya heard something new in the chewing. A change in its rhythm. Satisfaction, maybe. Or anticipation.

Because ahead of them, past the hills, past the silent villages, past the whole unraveling north — Haldmere sat on its river with ten thousand lamps burning, and in Haldmere a king wore a crown that something spoke through, and somewhere in that city a stranger had written a summons on salt-preserved vellum and known exactly whom to send it to.

The Choir — she was calling it that now, privately, because *the chewing* was undignified and the truth deserved a name — the Choir had noticed her. Had turned its regard on her in the dark and found her full of names.

Which meant that when she walked through Haldmere's gates, she wouldn't be sneaking past it.

She'd be introducing herself.

"Torv," she said, as the first cold flakes of impossible midwinter snow began to fall through the windless dark, "when we get there, remind me to teach you the word Duchess uses for 'strategically unwise.'"

"Why? What does she call this plan?"

"'Dinner bell,'" said Freya, and clicked her tongue, and drove on into the snow.
