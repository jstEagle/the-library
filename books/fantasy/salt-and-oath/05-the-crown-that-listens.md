## 5. The Crown That Listens

King Aldric of Vhalen had learned, in the past half-year, to measure his mornings by what was missing.

There was a time — he could not say how long ago, and that was the trouble — when he woke and knew the day entire: the petitions, the harvest ledgers, the name of the man who held the eastern mills. Now he woke and lay still beneath the furs, running his hands through the dark of his own mind like a man patting his pockets for keys, checking what remained.

His name. He had that. Aldric. Good.

His daughter. Isolde. He had that too, though lately the name felt less like a person and more like a word he was keeping safe for someone.

His wife—

He sat up too fast. The room tilted. And there it was again, that soft rasp at the edge of everything, like a file drawn gently across a lock.

*Good morning, Aldric,* said the crown.

It did not speak aloud. It never spoke aloud. It spoke in the space behind his eyes, in a voice made of many voices braided together — a choir, though he had no memory of ever hearing a choir, which should have told him something right there. It was patient. It was kind, in the way that deep water is kind to a stone. And every morning it offered him the same bargain with the same terrible gentleness.

*You are tired,* it said. *You have carried so much. Give us the hard years, and we will give you rest.*

"I'm not giving you anything," Aldric said to the empty bedchamber, because saying it out loud helped, the way shouting at a storm helps, which is to say not at all but honestly.

*The winter your father died. The plague year. The face you keep trying to find and cannot.* A pause, delicate as a knife laid on silk. *You reach for her every morning. We can make the reaching stop.*

And that was the cruelty of it, Aldric thought as he dressed himself — refusing help from a servant who had once been able to do it without instruction, another thing the crown had quietly taken, along with the names of three of his councilors and, he suspected, an entire summer. The crown did not threaten. It consoled. It offered to sand down the splinters of him one by one, and called each shaving a mercy.

He had already given it more than he knew. He understood this the way you understand a leak in a roof — not by seeing the hole, but by counting the stains.

His wife. Maren. He had her name; he clutched it like the last plank of a wrecked ship. But her face — when he closed his eyes and reached for it, he found a warm shape where a face should be, a presence, the feeling of being loved by someone specific, and above it all a smooth blankness like fog over glass. He remembered that she had laughed at his solemnity. He could not remember her laugh.

Some mornings he stood before her portrait in the long gallery and searched the painted woman's face for confirmation, the way a man checks a mirror against his memory. *Yes,* the portrait seemed to say. *This is she.* But portraits lie by their nature, flattering and freezing, and he no longer trusted his own agreement with them. Perhaps the painter had flattered her. Perhaps the woman in the frame was a stranger Maren would have laughed at.

Perhaps, whispered the choir, sweet as rot, *you would like to stop grieving? We can take the grief too. You would feel so much lighter.*

"No," Aldric said, and meant it with his whole remaining self. "The grief is hers. It's the only part of her I have left, and you will not have it."

*As you wish,* said the crown, unbothered, unhurried. It had all the time in the world. It was, in fact, making the world have less time in it, though Aldric did not yet know that either.

* * *

The council met at midmorning in the map room, where the windows were tall enough to show the sky, and Aldric had taken to sitting where he could see it. The stars had gone out two nights past — every star, all at once, from horizon to horizon, while the border wards of Vhalen shattered like dropped crockery. His astronomers were beside themselves. His priests were worse. His people were already telling each other it was an omen, because people always were, and usually about the king.

"Reports from the east," said Chancellor Bramwell, a round, decent man whose name Aldric had written on the back of his hand that morning in small careful letters, and who mercifully had not noticed. "Three villages silent. Not empty — silent. No birds. The elders in Corren's Ford have stopped speaking altogether. They sit in the square and look north."

"Look north at what?"

"That's the difficulty, sire. They won't say. Or—" Bramwell hesitated. "Or they've forgotten how."

Aldric looked at the map, at the little painted towns marching toward the northern border, and felt the crown shift almost imperceptibly against his brow, the way a cat shifts its weight when it is comfortable and intends to remain.

*They are coming home to us,* the choir murmured. *Do not trouble yourself with borders. Borders are such anxious things.*

"The wards failed," said Lord Commander Hesse flatly. She was a grey iron woman who had once pulled Aldric out of a river during a flood and had never let him forget the debt. "Twelve years of maintenance budgets cut, twelve years of my requests refused, and now the whole line is down. I want garrisons reinforced. I want ward-keepers recalled to service. And I want to know why the old magic was outlawed in the first place, because whoever decided that left us defenseless."

"The old magic was outlawed," Aldric began, and then stopped, because he had been about to say *for good reason*, and discovered, reaching for the reason, that there was nothing there. A hole. A smooth-walled absence, worn comfortable, like a socket for a tooth long gone.

"Sire?"

"There was a purge," he said slowly. "Twelve years ago. Under my regency." He could remember signing something. He could remember the weight of the seal. He could not remember why, or on whose counsel, or what — or who — had been purged. "I want the records. All of them. From before the outlawry."

Hesse's eyebrows rose. Bramwell's face went carefully blank, which was its way of going pale.

*Careful,* sang the crown, and for the first time there was something beneath the sweetness, something with edges. *You agreed, Aldric. You wept when you signed it. Do you want to remember why?*

"Yes," Aldric said aloud, into the middle of Lord Commander Hesse's sentence about garrison rotations, causing her to stop and stare at him. "I mean — yes. To the records. Proceed with the garrisons. Forgive me. Long night."

The council moved on. The crown settled against his temples like a returning bird.

*We are trying to protect you,* it said, wounded. *You were so unhappy then.*

* * *

In the afternoon came the hour Aldric both dreaded and treasured, which was the hour his daughter came to bully him into eating lunch.

Isolde arrived the way weather arrives — announced by a change in pressure, preceded by a rustle of pages. She was nineteen and had her mother's stubbornness, which was to say all of it, and she had lately taken to carrying a small ledger everywhere, bound in green leather, in which she wrote constantly with the fierce concentration of a general mapping enemy country.

"What's in the book?" he asked, as she swept in and began moving the decanters off the table to make room for soup.

"Accounts," said Isolde.

"You're nineteen. What accounts can you possibly have?"

"Yours," she said, without looking up.

He laughed. It surprised him, the laugh; it came out rusty. "Should I be worried?"

She looked at him then — a long, level look, her mother's grey eyes doing arithmetic behind them — and something in his chest clenched, because she looked at him the way one looks at a beloved house with a crack in the foundation. Searching for movement. Listening for settling.

"You eat standing up," she said. "You've started doing it since autumn. You stand at the window and eat and watch the road south, and you don't know you're doing it, and when I ask what you're watching for, you say 'nothing,' and then you look sad." She set the ledger down. "That's an account, Father. I keep them."

For a moment he nearly told her. Nearly said: *there is a voice in my head that eats me like a meal, course by course, and it has your mother's face already, and I am afraid that one morning you will come in and I will smile at you the way I smiled at Chancellor Bramwell this morning, warmly, at a stranger.* The words climbed his throat like survivors from a cellar.

But the crown stirred, soft as snowfall. *She is young. It would frighten her. And children, bless them, tell things — to servants, to suitors, to anyone. You know how she worries. Let her have her peace. Let her have her father, whole and calm, a little longer.*

And that was the trap within the trap, Aldric thought, watching his daughter ladle soup with unnecessary precision. The crown did not merely take memories. It took the sharing of them. Every secret it counseled him to keep was another wall between him and the people who might have noticed the erosion in time. Isolation, served lovingly, spoonful by spoonful.

"Father?" Isolde said. "Where did you put Mother's ring?"

The question landed in the room like a dropped glass.

"Her ring," Aldric repeated.

"The garnet one. She wore it every day. After the funeral it went in the treasury under seal, and last month I asked to see it, and the keeper said the seal order bore your signature." Her voice was very steady, which was how he knew she was not. "But there's no record of it ever arriving. The treasury has no garnet ring. There's no record anywhere. It's as if—" She stopped herself, jaw tightening. "As if it never existed."

Aldric reached for the memory and found the same smooth blankness, the fogged glass, the socket without the tooth. Had he sold it? Given it away? Had there ever been a ring? Yes — yes, there had been a ring, he would swear it on anything, her hands had never been without it, she'd turned it when she was thinking—

*You're upsetting yourself,* the crown soothed. *Rings are such small things. Let the small things go. Keep what matters.*

"It will turn up," he heard himself say, in a voice that sounded like a man reading someone else's letter. "These things always turn up."

Isolde stared at him. Something flickered across her face — not anger, worse than anger. Recognition. The look of someone who has just realized the crack in the foundation runs under her own room.

"You didn't even check," she said quietly. "You didn't say 'let me ask the keeper.' You said 'it will turn up.' Father, you don't remember the ring either, do you. You remember that you should."

The candle flames leaned in the draft from the window. Somewhere below, a dog began to bark — one of the palace hounds, barking at the throne room doors again, as they had done for weeks now, hurling themselves against the wood until handlers dragged them away. Nobody could explain it. The dogs simply would not go near that room, and lately they would not go near the king.

*The animals have always been stupid,* said the crown, with the first true emotion Aldric had ever heard from it. Fear, quickly smoothed over. *Pay them no mind.*

"Isolde," Aldric said carefully, "if you ever notice — if you ever think that I am not quite myself—"

"You mean more than usual," she said. "You haven't been quite yourself since the stars went out. Since before, really. Since autumn." She picked up her ledger and hugged it to her chest, and for one moment she looked six years old and asking why Mama wasn't coming back. Then the moment passed, and the general returned. "I'll be keeping accounts, Father. Of everything. Someone in this palace has to remember."

She left. The soup went cold. Aldric stood at the window afterward, as he apparently always did, watching the road south, and could not have said what he expected to come up it.

*Peace,* the crown offered, gentle as evening. *That is what you're waiting for. We could give it to you tonight. One memory more — the hardest one, the grief, the reaching, the ache that wakes you — and you would sleep like a child. You would be happy, Aldric. Truly. Isn't that what a king owes his people? A king who suffers makes poor decisions. A king at peace—*

"A king at peace," Aldric said to the glass, "is a king who has stopped noticing what's being done to him."

*Or,* said the choir, with infinite patience, *a king who has finally stopped fighting.*

Below in the courtyard, the hounds had begun to howl — all of them at once, heads thrown toward the north, toward the silent villages and the starless sky, singing the only warning they were permitted to give. Aldric pressed his hand flat against the cold window and listened, and for the first time in months, he did not know whether the tightness in his chest was dread or hope.

Because far off, at the edge of hearing — at the edge of everything — something else had begun to answer the dogs. Something small, and wild, and awake.

And the crown, for the first time, had gone quiet to listen.
