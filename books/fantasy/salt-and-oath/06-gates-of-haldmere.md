## 6. Gates of Haldmere

The city announced itself long before we saw it — first as a smudge of woodsmoke on the northern horizon, then as a sound. Dunmark had been loud in the way of harbors: gulls, rigging, fishwives arguing about the price of herring. Haldmere was loud in a different way entirely. It was the sound of ten thousand people all pretending very hard that nothing was wrong.

"They've doubled the gate guard," Torv said, squinting from the cart bench beside me. "And tripled the ones on the walls. You don't do that for a festival."

"You would know," I said. "You used to be one of them."

"I used to be *good* at being one of them," he corrected, with the particular dignity of a man who had been drunk for most of the journey and was now aggressively sober. "There's a difference."

We had been three days on the road since the courier's summons, and I still hadn't decided how I felt about Torv Askeland. He was a large man gone soft at the edges, with a beard that looked like it had lost an argument with a hedge and eyes that flinched away from mine whenever I caught him studying me too closely. He knew what I was. He'd known since the horse. And he hadn't told anyone — not yet, not so far — but knowing a secret is its own kind of leash, and I could feel the slack going out of it mile by mile.

Beside us, the cart-horse — Bramble, who had appointed herself my responsibility somewhere outside Fenlow and refused to hear otherwise — flicked her ears back toward my knee.

*Walls smell wrong,* she said. *Like the silent villages.*

I kept my hand where she could feel it and said nothing aloud. In the quiet country we'd crossed, where no bird sang and old men had forgotten their own sons' names, Bramble had been the only voice I trusted. She was not a subtle thinker, my mare. But subtlety is overrated when the world is ending.

"Freya." Torv's voice had dropped. "Look at the gate."

The gates of Haldmere were old iron over old stone, tall enough to admit a war-elephant and ugly enough to make you wish they wouldn't. What was wrong with them took a moment to see, because it was a matter of absence rather than presence.

No birds.

Every other city I'd ever entered had its attendants of sparrow and pigeon, its gulls squatting on the battlements like small feathered tax collectors. The walls of Haldmere were bare. The sky above them was bare. It was as if someone had drawn a line around the city and everything with wings had read it and turned around.

"The palace dogs," Torv murmured, almost to himself. "That's the third thing."

"What are the first two?"

"The bells stopped ringing true two winters ago — everyone blamed the bellfounder. And the king stopped walking the gardens last spring." He rubbed his jaw. "Animals first, then bells, then gardens. Things that don't lie to people."

"That's a ward-keeper's list," I said quietly.

"It's a drunkard's list," he said, "which is better, because nobody checks a drunkard's list against anything." He flicked the reins. "Smile, charm-seller. We're about to be looked at by people whose whole trade is looking."

* * *

They looked at us for a long time.

The queue at the gate moved with the slow, suspicious rhythm of a city at war with something it couldn't name. Guards in the king's blue checked papers twice. A clerk with ink-stained fingers asked our business, our birthplaces, our mothers' names — and here I felt the first cold thread of real fear, because my mother's name was a hole in every record I'd ever seen, including my own memory, and I'd learned to fill it with a smile and a shrug.

"Maren," I said, using the name of a woman in Dunmark who had once fed me soup and claimed me loosely, the way harbor folk claim strays. "Maren Almeida. She's dead."

The clerk wrote it down without interest. Lies travel best when they're boring.

Inside the walls, Haldmere was worse than the queue suggested. Not ruined — nothing so honest as ruin. It was *polished*. Streets swept clean, shopfronts freshly painted, guards at every corner standing straight as fence posts. And underneath all of it, a silence with teeth. People spoke in the normal way, bought bread in the normal way, laughed in the normal way — but the laughter came half a beat late, like an echo arriving before it should.

A dog trotted past us dragging a string of sausages it had clearly earned through honest effort, and I nearly wept with relief at the sight of it until I noticed it was running *away* from the palace hill, and running with purpose.

"Bramble says the stables are bad," I told Torv as we handed the cart to a stable-boy with hollow cheeks. "She says the horses stand facing the doors all night."

"Horses do that anyway."

"Not all night. Not all facing the same door." I stroked the mare's neck. "She wants to sleep with one eye open. I told her that's just good sense in this city."

Torv almost smiled. It was the first time in three days his face had done anything but rehearse guilt. "You talk to her like she's clever."

"She *is* clever. She thinks I'm the dim one for needing words." I glanced up at the palace, white and many-windowed against a gray sky, crouched on its hill like a cat watching a mousehole. "Torv. Before we go in there. If they test me — if anyone asks me directly —"

"I don't know what you mean," he said promptly.

"No. I mean if they ask me *directly*. What do I say?"

He was quiet for a moment. Somewhere behind us, Bramble snorted at the stable-boy, who apologized to her, which I approved of.

"You say you sell charms," Torv said finally. "Luck-knots, salt-wraps, weather-bones. Little things. Harmless things. You say it the way you said it to me, like it's slightly embarrassing." He looked at me then, straight on, and his eyes weren't flinching. "And Freya — whatever they put in front of you, whatever they ask you to touch or hold or swear — you let *me* go first. That's not chivalry. That's tactics. I'm expendable. I've been expendable for twelve years; it's the one thing I'm qualified for."

"That's the saddest thing anyone's ever said to me," I told him, "and I once heard a dying eel give a speech."

"Was it a good speech?"

"It was mostly bubbles. But the sentiment was there." I shouldered my pack. "Come on. Let's go be looked at."

* * *

They received us in a hall rather than a cell, which Torv muttered was either a good sign or a very efficient one.

The Lord Protector Serane met us beneath a window of colored glass that threw red and gold light across the floor like spilled wine. I had prepared myself for a soldier, or a schemer, or some combination — the broad-shouldered, hard-eyed sort that courts breed in the winter of their discontent. What I got instead was a woman of perhaps fifty, silver-haired, dressed in gray so fine it made the king's blue look like burlap, with a face built for kindness and hands folded in front of her like a woman waiting patiently for tea.

"Keeper Askeland." Her voice was warm. Warmth poured off her the way heat pours off a forge — you believed in it without examining the fuel. "Twelve years, and the crown remembers its servants. I hope that means something to you."

"It means something," Torv said carefully.

"And this is the charm-seller." Her eyes came to me, and I felt them the way you feel a hand on the back of your neck. Gray eyes. Very steady. Very empty, in the way a well is empty — deep, and dark, and waiting for you to drop something in. "Freya Almeida. Of Dunmark. You've come a long way on short notice."

"The summons was persuasive," I said, and bobbed the little curtsy I'd practiced on the road, the one that said *harmless tradeswoman, please don't look at me*.

"Mm." She smiled. It reached every part of her face except the part that mattered. "The kingdom is in difficulty, as I'm sure the road has shown you. Villages gone quiet. Old folk confused. The king—" a pause, delicate as a surgeon's knife pausing over skin, "—the king has been unwell. We are gathering people with useful skills. Charm-sellers among them."

"Charms are small things," I said. "I don't see what use—"

"Indulge me." She gestured, and a servant brought forward a small table, and on the table, on a cushion of velvet, lay objects arranged like a fortune-teller's spread. A knife. A ring of keys. A dried sprig of rowan. A child's wooden horse, paint worn away at the ears. A lump of raw salt the size of a fist. "A little game. An old custom, from before the outlawry. We ask visitors to hold each item and tell us what they feel. Nothing more. Most people feel nothing at all, of course." Her smile deepened by a degree. "Most people."

My heart performed a maneuver I can only describe as falling downstairs politely.

I looked at the objects. The knife sang faintly of the smith who'd forged it — ordinary, human, safe. The keys jangled with the memory of doors. The rowan was just a weed that had died with dignity. The wooden horse—

The wooden horse had been loved. Loved hard, for years, by a child with sticky hands, and it held that love the way amber holds a fly, and my gift rose up in my chest like a tide answering the moon, and I wanted, *wanted*, to pick it up and tell Serane about the boy who'd slept with it under his pillow and grown into a man who still kept it in a drawer —

I thought about the gull. I thought about the drowned ward-stone. I thought about villages where grandmothers stared at their grandchildren like strangers.

I picked up the wooden horse and turned it over in my hands like a woman appraising secondhand goods.

"Bit of a chewer, whoever had this," I said. "You can tell from the ears. My nephew was the same — ate the tail off a rocking horse when he was four. Mother said he'd grow out of it. He's twenty-six now and bites his nails, so." I set it down. "It's a nice toy. Is there a point to the game?"

Silence. The fire crackled. Somewhere far off, a door closed.

Then Serane laughed — a real laugh, brief and surprised out of her, and for one unguarded instant the hollow went out of her face and there was a person underneath, tired and sharp and frightened. Then it was gone, sealed over like ice on a pond.

"A chewer," she repeated. "Yes. Well." She waved the table away. "You'll be lodged in the east wing. Keeper Askeland will report to the marshal's office at dawn — yes, Keeper, the crown does remember its servants, and the crown has questions only a ward-keeper can answer." Her gaze slid back to me, and the warmth returned, and with it the cold underneath, the way a river holds its current under its shine. "Rest well, Mistress Almeida. Court can be... hungry, for those who aren't paying attention."

*I bet it can,* I thought. *I bet it eats.*

* * *

The east wing was luxurious in the way of places designed to keep you comfortable and exactly where you were. Our rooms had thick carpets, deep beds, windows overlooking a courtyard garden gone strangely brown though it was barely autumn — and a connecting door between mine and Torv's, which neither of us remarked upon and both of us noticed.

I unpacked slowly. My charms — the honest ones, knots and salt-wraps and painted bones that worked through suggestion and good materials and nothing else — went into their display tray. Under the false bottom of my pack, wrapped in wool, went the other things: my mother's thimble, which hummed when I held it, though I could not have said whose thumb had worn it smooth; the gull's feather, stiff and salt-stiffened; and the shard of drowned ward-stone Torv had pried loose from the harbor mud, which I had not been able to stop touching since, because it whispered.

It whispered the same thing over and over, in a voice like wind through a keyhole:

*It listens through the crown. It listens through the crown. Do not let it hear your name.*

I was still holding it when the scratching started at the door.

Not knocking. Scratching. Low down, at the gap beneath the wood, patient and deliberate. I crossed the room and opened the door onto an empty corridor — and onto a dog.

It was a big brute, some kind of deerhound cross, gray as the sky, with ribs showing and eyes full of an intelligence that made the hair rise on my arms. It did not bark. It looked at me the way Bramble looked at broken fences: assessing, deciding whether I was worth the trouble.

*You're the one,* it said. Its voice was rough, a stray's voice, unused to being answered. *The one who talks back.*

"Where did you come from?" I whispered, glancing down the corridor. Empty. "Dogs aren't allowed in the guest wing."

*Dogs aren't allowed anywhere near the throne room,* the dog said. *That's why I'm here.* It sat, heavily, ribs flexing. *You're going to be taken to see the king. Everyone useful gets taken to see the king. Don't go in.*

Cold walked down my spine wearing small boots. "Why not?"

The dog's ears flattened. For a moment it looked less like an animal and more like a soldier describing a battle it had lost.

*The room is wrong. The room is a mouth. We can smell it from the corridor — old meat and nothing, meat-and-nothing, a hunger with furniture in it. The two-legs walk in and walk out and some of them come out lighter. Not thinner. Lighter. Like something's been ladled out of them and they don't know enough to miss it.* It scratched at the carpet with one paw, agitated. *The pack leader went in last winter. He came out and didn't know us. Didn't know his own name for two days. They sent him to the kennels and called it illness.*

"The king goes in there," I said. "The king *lives* in there."

*Yes,* the dog said simply. *Poor thing.*

I crouched down, level with its eyes, and felt the ward-stone whispering in my pack like a conscience. "What's your name?"

The dog blinked. Something moved behind its eyes — surprise, and under the surprise, a grief so old it had gone smooth, like sea glass.

*Nobody's asked that in a long time,* it said. *I think I had one. There was a girl. There was a name. It's — * it shook its great head, frustrated, chasing its own tail through the dark. *It's like a word on the tip of the tongue. Like a room in a house I can't find the door to anymore.*

And I understood, suddenly and completely, what Serane's little game had been. Not a test of what I could feel. A test of what I could *hide*. She hadn't cared whether the wooden horse meant anything to me. She'd watched my hands, my breath, the pulse in my throat — watched for the moment the mask slipped and something looked out through my eyes that wasn't a charm-seller.

I had passed. Barely. By the width of a chewed ear.

But the dog was still sitting in front of me, and the corridor was still empty, and the palace around us was quiet in the way of a throat clearing itself to speak.

"Listen to me," I told the dog. "Can you warn the others? The animals? Keep them away from the throne room, away from the king, away from anyone who comes out of that place smelling wrong?"

*We already do,* the dog said. *We've been doing it since the bells went strange. But two-legs don't listen to us. Two-legs never listen.* Its tail moved, once, a small flag of hope in the gray light. *Except you.*

Down the corridor, a door opened. Footsteps — measured, unhurried, the footsteps of a woman who had never once needed to hurry in her life. The dog flowed backward into the shadows beneath a sideboard and was gone, and I stood up and smoothed my skirt and arranged my face into harmlessness just as Serane rounded the corner, silver-haired and warm-eyed, with two guards behind her and that smile that reached everywhere except the part that mattered.

"Mistress Almeida." Her voice was honey over a well. "Forgive the interruption. His Majesty has heard a speaker has come to court, and wishes to see you at once."

The word landed in the silence between us like a dropped glass.

"*Speaker*," I repeated, lightly, blankly, while my heart fell down another flight of stairs, less politely this time. "I'm sorry, my lady, I sell charms. Knots and bones and salt. Unless the king has trouble sleeping, I'm afraid I'm not sure what use I—"

"Of course," Serane said smoothly, and her smile widened, and behind it, deep in the gray wells of her eyes, something waited with the patience of a thing that had already eaten a kingdom's past and knew precisely how to wait for its future. "A figure of speech. This way, please. His Majesty dislikes to be kept waiting — and lately, Mistress Almeida, he dislikes it *so very much*."

Behind me, in the shadow under the sideboard, the dog began, very softly, to whine.
