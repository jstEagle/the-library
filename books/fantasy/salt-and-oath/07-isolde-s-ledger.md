## 7. Isolde's Ledger

The ledger lived behind a loose stone in the third-floor garderobe, which was the least dignified hiding place in Haldmere Palace and therefore the best. Nobody visited the third-floor garderobe. The plumbing had been declared cursed by two successive Master of Pipeses, and Isolde had done nothing to correct this impression. She had, in fact, encouraged it, once leaving a dead rat and a chalk sigil near the door for verisimilitude.

She knelt now on cold flagstone, worked the stone free, and drew out the oilcloth bundle. Inside was a book bound in undyed calfskin — deliberately plain, the sort of book a clerk might use to track grain shipments — and a stub of graphite pencil that she sharpened with her belt knife because carrying a whetstone *and* a pencil sharpener would have been excessive even for her.

*Entry one hundred and forty-one,* she wrote. *Father forgot the name of the river Vesse. He has fished it every autumn of his life.*

Her hand did not shake. She had trained it not to. That was the whole point of the ledger: grief was a flood, and floods drowned people, but grief written down in columns became data, and data could be fought.

She had started the ledger eleven months ago, after the night her father called her by her mother's name at dinner. Not a slip of the tongue — everyone called everyone the wrong name sometimes; Isolde herself had addressed the Master of Horse as "you enormous walnut" with complete sincerity. No, this had been different. Aldric had looked at her across the roast boar with an expression of warm, distant fondness, the kind reserved for portraits, and said, "Maren, you've grown so tall," and when Isolde said, "Papa, I'm Isolde," he had laughed and agreed too quickly, the way a man agrees when he is concealing that he has no idea what you are talking about.

That night she had made a list. Just three items. By midsummer it was forty. Now the pages were filling faster than she could rule lines for them.

*Entry one hundred and thirty-eight: Father forgot the Third Accord of the Salt Coast, which he himself negotiated.*
*Entry one hundred and thirty-nine: Father forgot the face of Captain Brenna, who saved his life at Redford Bridge. He thanked her warmly as "the new fellow."*
*Entry one hundred and forty: Father forgot how to whistle. He stood at his window for ten minutes moving his lips like a man chewing a secret.*

The whistling one had hurt worse than the rest, somehow. Kings were supposed to forget treaties. Fathers were not supposed to forget whistling.

Isolde closed the ledger, replaced the stone, and went down to do battle with the court.

* * *

The problem with being a princess whom nobody took seriously was that it bought her extraordinary freedom. Serane, the Lord Protector, smiled her hollow smile whenever Isolde passed and dismissed her from thought before the smile finished. The chamberlain assigned her embroidery hours. The Master of Records gave her whatever documents she requested because requesting documents was considered a harmless eccentricity, like collecting snuffboxes or believing in the sea-god.

So Isolde collected documents. She had spent four months assembling the shape of the thing without knowing its name. The pattern was simple once you saw it: everything her father forgot, something else grew heavier. The crown itself. It sat in the treasury most days under guard, and the guards rotated weekly because longer rotations made them strange — quiet, agreeable, prone to standing too long in doorways looking at nothing. One of them, a good man named Pell, had told her over wine that guarding the crown felt "like being listened to by a very patient aunt." Then he'd laughed, then he'd stopped laughing, then he'd asked to be transferred to the stables, where nothing listened to anybody except horses, and horses only judged.

And there were the charm-sellers. Three had arrived in the past month, summoned from the provinces by sealed writs bearing the king's seal — writs Isolde was nearly certain her father had not signed, because entry ninety-seven established that he had forgotten what a seal *was* for the better part of an afternoon. Someone at court was gathering folk-magicians the way a cook gathers onions: quietly, in quantity, and ahead of a meal nobody wanted to think about.

Which was why, on a grey morning smelling of snow, Isolde found herself walking down into Dunmark Quarter toward the lodgings where the charm-women were housed, with her ledger hidden in a bread loaf under her arm, because the best hiding places were always slightly ridiculous.

* * *

The woman they'd taken in from Dunmark — Freya Almeida, per the intake roll, occupation "purveyor of minor charms, licensed" — was in the courtyard teaching a palace cat to sit.

This was notable for two reasons. First, the cat was Duchess, a creature of such concentrated contempt that the Master of Hounds had formally petitioned to have her exiled. Second, the cat was sitting. Sitting with the expression of someone who had been blackmailed, but sitting.

"Sit," Freya said again, gently, and Duchess sat, glaring, and Isolde felt the hair rise on her arms in a way she had learned to trust.

"You're the charm-seller," Isolde said.

Freya turned. She had a wind-burnt face and the wary posture of someone accustomed to being underestimated, and she dropped into a curtsy exactly deep enough to be unimpeachable. "Your Highness."

"Stop that. Walls have ears and most of them work for my Lord Protector." Isolde glanced at the cat. "How did you do that?"

"Patience, Highness. And sardines."

"Mm. And nothing else?"

A pause. A very small pause, but Isolde had spent a year measuring pauses against her father's face, and she knew what concealment looked like at every scale. "Nothing else," said Freya Almeida, lying.

"Good," said Isolde. "I'd be disappointed if you were only a charm-seller. Charm-sellers can't help me. Come inside. Bring the cat; she's earned sardines and I need witnesses who can't testify."

* * *

Her sitting room overlooked the inner garden, which meant it also overlooked the throne room's garden entrance, which meant Isolde had chosen it deliberately, because the dogs refused the throne room and she liked to watch them refuse it. Sure enough, as they settled — Freya in the chair, Duchess on the windowsill radiating grievance — the kennel-master led six hounds along the path below, and all six pulled up short at the garden door, hackles up, and would not pass it no matter how he coaxed. He shrugged and took them around the long way, as everyone did now, as everyone had stopped remarking upon.

Freya watched the dogs too. Isolde watched Freya watch the dogs.

"I'm going to show you something," Isolde said, "and then I'm going to ask you a question, and I want you to notice that I'm not asking the question you're expecting."

She retrieved the bread loaf, excavated the ledger, and set it on the table between them.

"What is it?" Freya asked.

"A record. My father, King Aldric, is forgetting things. Not the ordinary way. I've documented it." She opened to the marked page. "One hundred and forty-one entries in eleven months. Here — dates, laws, faces, songs. The first twenty entries took five months. The last twenty took nine days. Whatever is taking his memory is accelerating, and everyone at court either hasn't noticed or has been instructed not to, and I don't know which possibility frightens me more."

Freya read. Isolde let her read, watching her fingers move down the columns, watching them stop at entry sixty-two — *Father forgot Mother's face. Asked me what she looked like. I told him and watched him fail to hold it. Three times.* — and seeing the charm-seller's throat move.

"This is..." Freya began, and stopped, and tried again. "You wrote all this yourself?"

"Every entry. In this room, mostly, after dinners. It's the only useful thing I do here, so I do it very well." Isolde kept her voice level. Methodical. Grief was a flood; method was the levee. "Now. My question. You felt it just now, reading about the dogs. Something in you recognized the shape of this. So here is what I want to know, Freya Almeida, purveyor of minor charms: when you calm an animal, what exactly are you doing?"

The silence stretched long enough for Duchess to yawn.

"Because I'll tell you what I think," Isolde continued, before the silence could harden into refusal. "I think you talk to them. Not with words. I think things answer you. I think that's why the writ brought you here, though whoever wrote it may not know precisely what they've caught. And I think that a kingdom whose king is being eaten by something invisible needs, more than anything else in the world, one person who can hear invisible things."

"That's treasonous," Freya said quietly. "What you're describing. Speakers were outlawed twelve years ago. People died."

"People are dying now. Slower, and politely, and one memory at a time, and nobody's calling it anything." Isolde leaned forward. "I'm not asking you to confess to anything. Confession is for priests and fools. I'm asking you to help me diagnose my father. Consider it medicine. If you find nothing wrong with him, I'll apologize and give you sardines for the cat. But if you find something — if you hear something — then you and I are going to be the only two people in this palace who know what we're fighting, and I will burn through every favor, every document, and if necessary every dignity I possess to keep you alive while we fight it."

Freya looked at the ledger again. At entry sixty-two. Isolde saw her lips move faintly, reading it a second time, and something in the woman's face cracked open — not pity exactly, recognition, the look of someone finding their own private grief catalogued in a stranger's handwriting.

"My mother used to sing to gulls," Freya said, apropos of nothing, voice rough. "Sorry. That's not — you asked me a question."

"You don't have to answer it today."

"No. I—" Freya pressed her palms flat on the table, steadying herself, and when she looked up her eyes were wet and furious and resolved, all three at once, and Isolde thought: *there she is. There's the ally.* "I'll tell you what I am. But not here. The walls in this palace listen, you said so yourself, and I don't think you know how right you are. Tonight. Somewhere the stones are older than the politics."

"The old chapel crypt," Isolde said immediately. "Twelfth century, abandoned, and the acoustics are terrible, which means nothing carries. Midnight. I'll bring the ledger."

"And I'll bring the truth," said Freya. Then, glancing at the windowsill: "Does the cat come too? She's the only one here I trust."

"Duchess trusts no one. It's her single virtue." Isolde rose, extended her hand, and found it clasped in a grip calloused from market-work and honest as a handshake could be. "One more thing, Freya Almeida. Thank you. For sitting with the ugly arithmetic. Most people, when I show them the ledger, start comforting me within a minute. Comfort is useless. Columns are useful."

"Columns," Freya said, with the ghost of a smile, "and sardines."

* * *

After she left, Isolde sat alone with the ledger open to a fresh page, pencil poised.

She should write entry one hundred and forty-two. There was always another entry; that was the horror of it, the ledger never ran dry, the well of forgetting was apparently bottomless. This morning's offering had been small and awful in the usual way: her father had greeted the Lord Protector at breakfast as "my lady wife," and Serane had smiled her hollow smile and played along, and Aldric had seemed happy, briefly, in the way of men dreaming.

But instead Isolde found herself writing something else. A heading, at the top of a clean page, above a fresh column:

*Things We Will Take Back.*

Underneath, the first item: *Mother's face. (Mine to describe. His to relearn.)*

It wasn't data. She knew that. It was hope wearing a column's clothing, and a proper archivist would strike it out.

She left it in.

Outside, the first snow of the season came down soft and silent over Haldmere, and somewhere below, the dogs were taking the long way around, and in the treasury under guard the crown sat listening, patient as an aunt, to everything.
