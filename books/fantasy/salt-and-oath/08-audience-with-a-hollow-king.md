## 8. Audience with a Hollow King

They came for her at dawn, which Freya thought was rude. Whatever else you could say about royal summons — and she had been saying quite a lot of it to Torv since the salt-vellum letter arrived — the least they could do was let a girl finish her breakfast.

Instead there were four guards at her door in the guest wing of Haldmere Palace, and a steward with a voice like oiled hinges informing her that His Majesty would receive her within the hour.

"Receive me," Freya repeated, when Torv had been fetched and the door shut behind them.

"That's what I said."

"I heard what you said. I'm questioning whether you said it to the right person. I sell charms, Torv. Dried lavender and knucklebones. The king does not *receive* charm-sellers."

"The king receives whoever Serane tells him to receive." Torv was dressed in his old ward-keeper's coat, brushed and buttoned, and he kept tugging the hem down as though it might still fit the man he'd been twelve years ago. "Which means someone told Serane about you. Which means we're past the point where breakfast matters."

Freya looked at the tray the steward had left anyway — white bread, honey, a pear sliced into fanned crescents that no one in Dunmark would ever waste time on — and ate the pear out of spite.

* * *

The throne room of Haldmere was larger than any building Freya had ever stood inside, and it was empty in a way that made her teeth ache. Not merely unoccupied — *hollow*. Her footsteps went up into the vaulted ceiling and did not come back down. Even her breathing seemed to be taken somewhere and not returned.

The dogs had known, she thought. Back in the courtyard, the palace hounds had planted their paws at the threshold and refused, absolutely refused, to cross it. One of them had looked at her with an expression she recognized from animals watching a storm front roll in. She'd pretended not to understand it. She was getting very good at pretending not to understand things, and she was tired of it.

"Walk slowly," Torv murmured beside her. "Bow when the herald says. Don't speak unless spoken to."

"And if I'm spoken to?"

"Then lie beautifully."

The doors at the far end opened, and King Aldric of Vhalen came down the long floor alone.

She had expected more ceremony — a canopy, courtiers, the Lord Protector gliding somewhere in the shadows. Instead it was just a man in a plain grey tunic with a circlet of dark metal on his brow, walking with the careful steps of someone crossing ice. He was younger than his portraits, or perhaps the portraits had been painted by flatterers. There was grey coming into his beard early, and his hands hung at his sides like tools someone had forgotten to put away.

And on his head, the crown.

It didn't look like much. A band of blackened silver, worked with runes so old and worn they were barely suggestions. But Freya felt it before she saw it clearly — felt it the way you feel a change in pressure before weather breaks. Something in the air around the king was *leaning toward him*. Listening.

The herald announced her — "Freya Almeida, of Dunmark" — and she bowed the way Torv had drilled into her that morning, low enough to be respectful, shallow enough to keep her eyes up.

"Almeida." The king's voice was quiet, and rough, as if it hadn't been used for anything but formalities in a long while. "You come from the coast."

"Yes, Your Majesty."

"You were in Dunmark when the stars went out."

"I was, Your Majesty."

He studied her. And here is what Freya would remember afterward, what she would carry like a stone in her chest: he studied her the way a drowning man studies a rope. Hope and shame in the same face, fighting for space.

"Do you know," he said, "why you were summoned?"

"No, Your Majesty."

"A lie," said the crown.

Freya's whole body went cold.

It wasn't a voice, exactly. It was many voices, layered thin as leaf over leaf, speaking in unison with the softness of snowfall. It came from everywhere and nowhere — from the walls, from the rafters, from the space just behind her own ears. And underneath the words there was something worse: a texture. A taste. Like standing in a room where a meal had been eaten long ago, and knowing, without being told, what had been eaten.

*Memories.* The air itself tasted of memories, chewed fine and swallowed.

"Your Majesty asked you a question," said the crown, through the king's mouth now, and Freya realized with a lurch that Aldric's lips hadn't moved.

Torv's hand found her elbow. Steady. *Careful,* the hand said.

"I — don't know why I was summoned, Your Majesty," Freya said. "I only came because I was asked."

"Asked." The chorus turned the word over, tasting it. Somewhere in that layered whisper she heard a woman's voice, and a child's, and an old man's, all saying *asked* together, and she understood with sick certainty that those voices had once belonged to people. People who had been *eaten.*

The king swayed slightly on his feet. For half a heartbeat his face changed — the blank courtesy cracking like river ice — and then the smoothness poured back over him.

"She has it," the crown murmured. "The listening. We smell it on her. Little speaker. Little bird. Come closer."

Every instinct Freya owned screamed at her to run. She took one step forward instead, because Torv's hand was still on her arm, and because some stubborn coastal part of her refused to be chased out of a room by a *hat.*

"Your Majesty," she said carefully, "are you well?"

The question landed in the hollow of the throne room and vanished. And then —

The king blinked. Once, twice, like a man surfacing from deep water. His eyes found hers, and this time there was nothing borrowed in them. This time it was only Aldric: a tired, decent, drowning man.

"*Run,*" he mouthed.

Freya's heart slammed against her ribs.

"*Don't listen. Don't answer it. It learns your —*" His jaw clenched, wrestling his own mouth back under his own command. Sweat stood out on his forehead. "*— your name. Never give it your —*"

His eyes glazed mid-word. Just — glazed, like frost creeping across a windowpane, and the warmth went out of them, and the smooth courteous mask slid home, and the thing wearing him smiled with his mouth.

"You are dismissed, little bird," said the crown, in a chorus of stolen voices, gentle as a lullaby. "But we will hear you again. You cannot help it. Listeners always answer, in the end."

* * *

Torv didn't speak until they were three corridors away, and even then he spoke in the flat, careful tone of a man walking on a frozen lake.

"Well," he said. "That went better than I expected."

Freya stopped walking. "Better?"

"He didn't have you arrested."

"He *begged*, Torv." Her voice cracked on the word, and she hated it, and kept going anyway. "He begged me. He's in there. He's *alive* in there, and that thing is wearing him like a coat, and everyone in this palace walks past him every day and bows and calls him Majesty and doesn't see —"

"I see it."

"— and the voices, Torv, the *voices* in it, there were children in there, there were —" She pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes. "How can nobody know?"

"Because knowing is dangerous, and because the ones who'd believe you are the ones already gone." Torv leaned against the wall, arms crossed, and for once there was no drink-slouch in him, no deflection. Just the old ward-keeper, looking at the problem straight on. "Twelve years ago I helped build the border wards. Everyone swore they were protection. Nobody asked what they were protecting us *from*, or why the crown paid for them out of the privy purse instead of the army's. I've had a decade to wonder about that. Today I stopped wondering."

"What do you mean?"

"I mean the wards weren't keeping something out." He pushed off the wall. "They were keeping something *in*. Or — no. Keeping something *asleep.* And the night the stars went out, whatever lid we built came off."

Freya lowered her hand. In the grey light of the corridor window, she watched dust drift, ordinary and slow, and tried to make her mind hold still.

"It called me little bird," she said. "It knew what I am. Torv — Serane tested me yesterday. If Serane serves that thing, why am I not in a cell?"

"Maybe because it wants you close." Torv's jaw worked. "Listeners always answer, in the end. That's what it said. Maybe it wasn't a threat, Freya. Maybe it was an invitation."

The word sat between them, cold and patient.

Somewhere below them, muffled through stone, a bell began to ring — not the hour bell, but the smaller one from the chapel tower, rung only for deaths and coronations. They looked at each other.

Neither of them moved toward it. Neither of them could have said why.

But as they stood there, Freya became aware of a small sound at the end of the corridor: the scratch of claws on flagstone. A palace dog — the same brindled hound that had refused the throne room — stood at the far corner, watching her. It did not bark. It did not approach.

It simply looked at her, then turned its head deliberately toward a narrow stair she hadn't noticed, tucked behind a tapestry, leading down.

Then back at her. Then toward the stair.

Animals, Freya had learned long ago, never wasted words. When one spoke, it meant it.

"Torv," she said quietly, "I think we've just been invited somewhere too."
