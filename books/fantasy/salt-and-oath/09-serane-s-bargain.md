## 9. Serane's Bargain

The trouble with ruling a kingdom through its grief, Serane Voss had learned long ago, was that grief made people so terribly literal.

They wanted villains. They wanted a hooded figure in a tower, a poisoner, a traitor with a name you could shout in a tavern. What they got instead was a woman of fifty-three who kept meticulous accounts, remembered every servant's child's name, and had once wept for three days over a dead cat named Admiral. It was very difficult to shout *Serane Voss* in a tavern with any conviction. The name sounded like someone who would help you with your taxes.

She thought about this while dressing that morning, because Maren — her maid of nineteen years, the only person left alive who had known her before — was brushing out her hair and humming, and the humming had gone slightly wrong.

"Maren," Serane said. "What did you hum yesterday?"

Maren's hands paused at her crown. "I don't... I couldn't say, my lady."

"It was the mill song. Your mother's mill song. You've hummed it every morning since I've known you." Serane met her eyes in the glass. "Yesterday it was different. Slower. Like something wearing the shape of it."

"I expect I'm tired, my lady."

"You are not tired. You slept nine hours; I checked." She said it without apology, because Maren knew her well enough not to be offended by being checked on. That was the arrangement between them: Serane watched, Maren pretended not to notice she was being watched, and both of them called it loyalty, which was easier than calling it fear.

"The mill song," Maren said slowly, testing the words like ice on a pond. "I can't hear it anymore, my lady. When I reach for it there's just — a smooth place. Like a stone where a tooth used to be."

"Say nothing to anyone."

"Yes, my lady."

"And if it happens again — if another smooth place opens up — come to me first. Before anyone else. Before you tell yourself it's nothing."

Maren nodded, and resumed brushing, and did not hum, and the silence of the unhummed song sat between them all the way through the rest of the toilette like an unpaid debt.

* * *

The audience chamber received petitioners until noon, and Serane presided over it with the king's seal on her hand and the king's absence in his eyes. Aldric had not held open court in eleven days. The official word was a cold. The unofficial word, among the servants, was that His Majesty had taken to standing at windows for hours, watching snow fall in a season when no snow should fall.

The petitions were ordinary and therefore precious. A dispute over a mill race in the eastern valleys. A widow asking relief from a tax levied on a husband now dead. A merchant guild complaining, at exhausting length, about harbor fees.

Serane resolved each one fairly, quickly, and with genuine attention, because this was the whole architecture of her life and always had been: do the small good things flawlessly, and no one looks up at the large terrible thing standing behind them.

It was during the merchant guild's complaint that she noticed the dogs again.

Haldmere palace kept six wolfhounds, descendants of the king's hunting line, and they were permitted to lounge through audiences as they pleased — a tradition older than anyone could remember, which meant it predated the purge, which meant it probably predated the crown itself. Today three of them lay by the doors. Not one of them had crossed the centerline of the chamber. And when a page walked past the throne dais carrying a tray, the largest hound — Brawn, a stupid sweet animal with hips like a hay cart — rose to his feet, hackles lifted, and growled at the empty throne.

Not at the king. At the throne. At the crown resting on its cushion beside the seat, waiting for an evening ceremony Aldric was increasingly unlikely to attend.

"Remove the animals," Serane said quietly, and the guards did, and the merchants went on talking about harbor fees as though the world were a place where harbor fees mattered.

She found she envied them. Genuinely, warmly envied them, the way one envies sleepers.

* * *

Freya Almeida was lodged in the Falcon Wing, third floor, in a room chosen specifically because its window overlooked the kennels and the mews and nothing else of interest. Serane had read the reports twice. The charm-seller from Dunmark who calmed a stampeding cart-horse with two words and a touch. Who spoke to gulls. Who had arrived in Torv Askeland's disreputable company and been vouched for, of all people, by Princess Isolde, whose ledger Serane had known about for four months and allowed to continue existing for reasons she examined every night before sleep and found, every night, still sufficient.

A speaker. An actual speaker, grown in the wild like a mushroom after rain, twelve years after the last of them had been burned or scattered or forgotten.

Serane dressed for the meeting carefully. Not in state — never in state; power displayed was power halved — but in dove-grey wool with no jewelry except a plain chain, the kind of outfit that said *I am here as a person*. She brought tea. This was not a whim. In twenty years of difficult conversations she had learned that it is nearly impossible to threaten someone while pouring for them, and nearly impossible for them to believe they're threatened while holding a warm cup. Tea was a treaty you could drink.

She dismissed the guards at the corridor's end, knocked once, and entered without waiting, because asking permission gave the other party time to arrange their face.

Freya Almeida was sitting on the window ledge with her boots off, which told Serane several useful things. She was reading — actually reading, a battered book of coastal birds — and she looked up with the unhurried assessment of someone who had spent her life being underestimated in markets.

"Lady Protector." Freya set the book down but did not rise. Good. Rising was deference, and deference was a currency Serane wanted her to keep spending elsewhere.

"Miss Almeida. I hope the room suits."

"It has a window that doesn't face anything trying to kill me. In my experience that's luxury."

Serane smiled — a real one, small, because false smiles were wasted on this one — and poured the tea. "Chamomile and something the kitchen insists is honey. I'd verify the honey's provenance myself but I've stopped asking questions the kitchens answer too smoothly."

That earned her a flicker of amusement. Good. They sat, and for a moment Serane simply let the silence work, because silences were instruments and she had studied hers the way other women studied lutes.

"You spoke to the gulls," Serane said at last. "In Dunmark. Before the wards fell."

Freya's cup paused halfway to her mouth. "Torv talks too much."

"Torv talks exactly enough, when he's paid correctly. He didn't tell me. The harbormaster's wife did — her son saw a woman standing in the rain arguing with a dying bird, and the bird going quiet afterward like a witness released from oath." Serane sipped her own tea. "I'm not here to arrest you, Miss Almeida. If I wanted that, I'd have sent soldiers, and you'd be in a cellar, and we'd both have saved a great deal of tea."

"What are you here for?"

"Honestly? To make you an offer. But offers are cheap, and you strike me as a woman who prices things properly. So first I'm going to give you something free." Serane set down her cup. "I'm going to tell you what happened to me, and you may believe none of it, and that changes nothing."

* * *

"The Grey Fever came to Haldmere the winter I was thirty-one," Serane said. "You'd have been a child. Perhaps you heard of it. The histories call it the Weeping Winter now — they renamed it afterward, the way people rename things that frightened them."

"I've heard the name," Freya said. "My mother—" She stopped. Something moved across her face, quick as a fish under ice. "Go on."

"My husband took it first. Then my daughter, Liss, who was seven and could catch anything, measles, chickenpox, the mood of a room. Then my son, Tomas, who was four and afraid of geese." Serane said the facts plainly, because she had said them so many times to herself that they had worn smooth, like sea glass. "The fever didn't kill cleanly. It burned. Three weeks per person, and you sat with them the whole time because the alternative was worse than the fever, and then one morning there was a body and a hole in the world shaped like them."

Freya had gone very still. Stillness, Serane noted, was this woman's armor of choice. Interesting. Most speakers flinched; this one froze and listened.

"By spring I had buried three people and half the city had buried more, and the king then — Aldric's father, a decent dull man — did the thing kings do. He convened a commission. Reports. Committees. And one report came back that wasn't like the others. It came from the ward-keepers' college, before the college was purged, and it said: the fever is not natural. It is *hungry*. It eats along lines of connection — households, families, congregations. And it can be fed something else instead."

"Memories," Freya said quietly.

"You know."

"I've seen villages on the road north where the elders forgot their children's names. I've heard the silence chewing." Her hands had tightened around the cup. "So you fed it."

"I negotiated with it." Serane let herself smile at the distinction, because the distinction was the entire architecture of her soul and she might as well display it. "There is a difference, Miss Almeida, and everyone who calls me monster will skip over it, so I'll say it once. Feeding implies it takes and you resist. Negotiating means you sit across from it and ask what it wants and what it will give. It wanted memory — old memory, deep memory, the kind that sits in a kingdom's bones. Charters. Bloodlines. The names of dead kings. The location of the old shrines. Things that hurt no living person to lose, I told myself. Things gathering dust anyway."

"And what did it give?"

"A boundary. The fever stopped at the river in the spring of that year. It has never crossed since. Twenty-two years, and not one case west of the Vell." Serane looked at her own hands, which were steady, which were always steady, which had signed away a country's past and never once trembled. "Every child born since, every grandchild, every wedding in the western provinces — none of them carry ash. I bought that. I am the only person alive who knows the price, and I paid it alone, and I have governed this kingdom competently for two decades while knowing that underneath everything there is a mouth."

The fire ticked. Somewhere below, a wolfhound barked once at nothing and was shushed.

"And now it's eating faster," Freya said. "And closer. And it's in the crown."

"It was always in the crown. That was the door I opened — or rather, the door I found already ajar and, God forgive me, leaned against." Serane set her cup down with a small precise click. "I thought I was managing it. A leash, not a lord. For twenty years the payments were small and distant and I told myself the story governors tell: better me than chaos. And then the stars went out, and the wards fell, and the payments started coming due in the capital, in daylight, in the king's own head. So now you understand my position, and I'll make the offer."

* * *

"Here is what I know that you don't," Serane said. "I know the Choir — yes, I've heard it called that, the crown whispers and I listen at doors, it's a hobby — cannot be fought the way your new friends intend to fight it. Torv Askeland wants wards rebuilt. Isolde wants ledgers and evidence and a reckoning. Sweet children, both. But you cannot cage a thing that eats cages. You cannot out-oath a thing that drinks oaths. Every soldier who swears loyalty to Aldric is a straw in its mouth — did you know that? Loyalty itself is the vector. The more devoted the kingdom, the deeper it feeds. Your princess's rebellion, your keeper's wards, your charming rites of release — delays. Beautiful delays. I respect a beautiful delay. But midwinter comes regardless."

Freya hadn't touched her tea in some minutes. "And your offer?"

"Leave." Serane spread her hands, palms up, the posture of a merchant showing empty scales. "Take Askeland, take the princess if she'll come, take a purse that will embarrass you, and go south past the Salt Coast, onto a ship, to somewhere the Choir's winter doesn't reach. I'll write you letters of passage under a name of your choosing. In five years you can sell charms openly on some shore where no one has heard of Vhalen. You will live. They will live. The kingdom will forget — that is what it does best now — and forgetting, Miss Almeida, is not the worst fate. I have examined the alternatives closely, and I promise you forgetting is merciful. Ask anyone who remembers."

"You're asking me to abandon the king."

"I'm asking you to abandon a corpse that hasn't fallen over yet. I loved Aldric once, in the way one loves a colleague — he was kind to my children, he cried at Liss's funeral harder than I did, I've never forgiven him for making me watch that. Whatever is wearing him now is not him. You stood in the audience chamber. You heard it. Don't pretend you didn't; your face did something complicated and then went careful."

Freya was quiet for a long moment. When she spoke, her voice was level, but Serane heard the current under it. "You said the Choir took the locations of the old shrines. Deep memory. Charters and bloodlines."

"Yes."

"Then it knows where the first binding was done. Where the drowned shrine is. Where the speaker in the crown was—"

"Careful," Serane said, softly, and meant it as a kindness.

"—was put *in*." Freya set down the cup and stood, and she was smaller than Serane and somehow filled the room. "You're not offering me escape, Lady Protector. You're offering me a comfortable seat outside the walls while it finishes eating the only witnesses who could ever name what it is. Because if anyone reaches that shrine — if anyone learns what's actually bound in that crown and *speaks to it* — then your twenty-year bargain becomes what it always was. Not governance. Collaboration."

The word landed. Serane let it land. She had been called worse by better-informed people.

"You may believe that," she said. "But consider: I could have had you killed twice today. Once at breakfast, once by the kennels — the dogs dislike you almost as much as they dislike the throne, which is a fascinating detail I encourage you to think about. Instead I brought chamomile. Ask yourself why a collaborator bothers with chamomile."

"Because monsters also get lonely," Freya said, pulling on her boots. "Thank you for the tea. Thank you even for the offer — I mean that. Someone should know what you told me. Someone should carry it." She paused at the door, and gentled, unexpectedly. "Your daughter. Liss. What was she like?"

And there it was — the knife Serane had not seen coming, because it wore the face of courtesy. She found her throat had closed. Twenty-two years of smooth places, and this stranger with her market-stall eyes had reached into the one pocket Serane kept locked and touched the thing inside.

"She laughed at everything," Serane said finally. "Everything. Funerals, thunderstorms, my coronation-day nerves. The priests said it was irreverence. I said it was grace." She straightened her cuffs. "Get out of my office, Miss Almeida."

Freya went. She did not run, which Serane respected, and she did not look back, which Serane noted.

Alone, Serane stood at the window and watched the girl cross the courtyard below, head bare in the falling snow that whispered as it fell, and she thought: *she'll go to the shrine. Of course she'll go. They always choose the doomed noble thing; it's why I prefer negotiating with monsters, monsters at least accept payment.*

She rang for her secretary. "Find me everything the archive holds on the drowned shrine on the salt coast. Everything, including the sealed folios. And send someone to follow the Almeida woman — at a distance. If she finds what I think she'll find, I need to know before the crown does."

Outside, the snow kept whispering, and somewhere beneath the whisper, patient as winter, something turned a page.
