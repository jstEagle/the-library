## 10. The Archive Beneath the Chapel

Torv Askeland had spent twelve years learning how to be small. It was a skill, like any other — you practiced it the way a ward-keeper practices knotwork, over and over until it sits in the hands without thinking. You learned to pour wine for men who'd once saluted you. You learned to laugh at jokes about the night the wards failed, because laughing along was safer than correcting the record. You learned to walk through Haldmere with your shoulders folded in, taking up no more space than a man who had done nothing worth remembering.

He was very good at being small now. He hated it.

And so there was something almost medicinal about following Princess Isolde Vhalen down a servants' stair at midnight, carrying a crowbar and a lantern, on her way to commit what was very likely treason.

"You're walking too loudly," she said, not turning around.

"I'm walking like a man with knees."

"Walk like a man with *quiet* knees."

The stair wound down behind the royal chapel, past walls that sweated cold even in autumn. Isolde moved ahead of him with the certainty of someone who had memorized every step long before she ever intended to take them. She carried her ledger under one arm — that grim little book she consulted the way other people consulted scripture — and she wore a man's coat two sizes too big, which should have made her look ridiculous and instead made her look like exactly what she was: trouble.

Torv had known her for four days. In those four days she had interrogated him twice, forgiven him nothing, and once handed him bread with the expression of someone settling a debt. He liked her enormously. It was like liking a drawn blade.

"Here." She stopped at a door he would have walked past without a glance: plain oak, unremarkable, tucked between a linen store and a room full of broken chairs. Except the lock was wrong. Everything else in this corridor was iron and age; the lock was bronze, bright-edged, and stamped with the seal of the old ward-keepers' order.

Torv's stomach dropped somewhere below his boots. "That's a warden's seal."

"Yes."

"That order was dissolved twelve years ago. Every member sworn to silence or exiled. I was—" He stopped. Swallowed. "I was the last apprentice they took."

"I know," Isolde said. "I read your file. All nine pages. The last three are just the word *insubordinate* written different ways."

"It was a fair assessment."

She knelt before the lock and produced a key from inside her coat — an ugly, functional thing, no ornament to it. "My father kept a ring of keys from every office he ever abolished. He couldn't bear to throw them away. Sentimental," she said, and the word came out with an edge, because sentiment was precisely what was eating him alive these days. She fit the key, turned it, and the bolt gave with a sound like a knuckle cracking.

Beyond the door: darkness, and dust, and air that hadn't been breathed in twelve years.

Isolde lifted her chin. "After you, Keeper Askeland."

"Why me?"

"Because if there's a trap, you're more expendable than the heir to the throne." A beat. "That was a joke."

"Was it?"

"Fifty percent," she said, and went in first anyway.

* * *

The lantern found a room that made Torv's chest ache with recognition. Shelves. Hundreds of them, floor to ceiling, stone-carved and numbered in the old wardens' cipher. Ledgers, folios, sealed tubes of salt-preserved vellum stacked like cordwood. This wasn't a forgotten closet. This was an *archive* — a real one, maintained by the old order, holding everything the crown had spent twelve years pretending had never existed.

"They didn't burn it," Torv said stupidly.

"No," Isolde said. "They locked it. There's a difference, and I've been trying to decide all week whether it means anything."

"It means whoever ordered the purge wanted it *forgotten*, not destroyed. Burning makes martyrs. Locking makes rumors die." He ran his fingers along a shelf spine and came away with a century of dust. "My masters told me the old records were ash. That we were better off. That ward-keeping was superstition and the wards themselves were..." He trailed off.

"Were what?"

"A cage lid," Torv said slowly. The words came out of some part of him older than his disgrace. "That's what Master Orlin called them, the last week before they took him away. Everyone thought he'd gone senile. He said the wards weren't built to keep things out of Vhalen." He turned to look at Isolde. "He said they were built to keep something *in*."

Isolde opened her ledger to a marked page. "Then let's find out what."

* * *

They worked for two hours, and it was the best two hours Torv had spent in twelve years, which said something depressing about his life.

Isolde read with terrifying speed, her lips moving, her pen scratching notes. Torv did the heavy lifting — literally, hauling folios down, but also the lifting that mattered, translating the old wardens' shorthand. The cipher was still in his hands. Twelve years of pouring wine and it sat there waiting, patient as a knot.

"The dating is wrong," he said at last, frowning at a shelf. "These aren't sorted by year. They're sorted by *reign*. Look — Aldric's grandfather, Aldric's great-grandmother — but this whole section here is older. No reign mark. Just a symbol." He drew it in the dust with one finger: a circle, crossed by three lines.

Isolde stared at it. "I've seen that. On the oldest coins. Before Vhalen had kings, supposedly."

"Supposedly." Torv pulled down the nearest tube, cracked its salt seal — the smell hit him like a wave, brine and time, the smell of his apprenticeship, of Master Orlin's study, of a life — and unrolled the vellum across a reading table.

The hand was old and cramped and beautiful. And it was not a chronicle. It was a *warding log*.

"*Third month of the long watch,*" Torv read aloud, haltingly. "*The singing continues beneath the chapel. The brothers have requested earwax and prayer in equal measure.*"

Isolde looked up. "The singing continues beneath the— we're standing above the chapel."

"I noticed," Torv said, and his voice came out thinner than he wanted. He kept reading. "*The king's masons have completed the third seal. His Grace asks again whether the binding can be loosened for festival days, that the girl might hear the bells. Answered: it cannot. Answered again: it must not. She is not a prisoner. She is a wound that talks.*"

Silence. Somewhere far above them, faint as breath, the palace settled on its foundations.

"*A wound that talks*," Isolde repeated. "What girl? What binding?" She was already pulling tubes, tearing seals, spreading vellum beside vellum until the table looked like the wing of a very anxious bird. "Askeland. Find me the beginning. Archives have beginnings. Someone wrote down the first day of this."

Torv went to the back wall, where the oldest shelves stood, and there — wedged behind a false panel that his fingers remembered before his mind did — he found a single folio, thick as a brick, its cover stamped with the crossed-circle and one word in the old tongue:

*Sefa.*

* * *

"She has a name," Isolde said, much later, when the candles had burned down to stubs and neither of them had any business still being awake.

They had read the folio twice. The second time neither of them spoke at all.

It was all there, in the dry administrative hand of clerks who had no idea they were writing horror. Three hundred years ago, before Vhalen had kings, there had been speakers — people who could hear and be heard by the world's living things. And the greatest of them, the one whose voice could reach through stone and salt and silence itself, had been a girl named Sefa.

The kings who came after — the first kings, the ones on the old coins — had feared her. The record didn't dress it up. There were petitions. There were councils. There was a phrase that appeared four times across the folio in four different hands, always with the same terrible calm: *the crown requires her voice.*

So they had taken it. Not killed her — killing a speaker, the clerks noted, only releases the gift to wander. They had *bound* her. Bound her voice into gold and iron and oath-work, forged it into an object that could carry her hearing forever, and set that object on the head of every king since, so that the crown could listen to the kingdom always, and the kingdom could never stop being loyal, because loyalty poured into the crown and the crown poured it into—

Into the thing Sefa had become, down there beneath the chapel, three hundred years of being listened *through* instead of *to*, until whatever was left of the girl had curdled into hunger. Into the Hollow Choir. Into the voice now whispering through King Aldric's crown, eating the kingdom's memories one loyal heart at a time.

"And the wards," Torv said. His mouth tasted of salt and old shame. "The border wards. Every generation of ward-keepers believed we were protecting Vhalen from the wilds. From beasts, from raiders, from the cold." He laughed, and it came out wrong. "We were the lock on the cage. Twelve hundred years of oaths, and the whole order was just... maintenance staff for a prison."

"Don't," Isolde said.

"Don't what?"

"Do that thing where you make it about your guilt. I've watched my father do it for months. It's useless. It feeds it, actually — guilt is a memory with hooks in it, I think. I think it likes the taste." She tapped the folio with two fingers, precise as a surgeon. "What matters is this: the wards aren't failing, Askeland. They're being *opened*. From underneath. Whatever is happening at the border, it isn't the Choir escaping. It's the Choir finally strong enough to lift the lid its own jailers built."

Torv sat down heavily on a crate of three-hundred-year-old paperwork. "Then everything I swore—"

"Was a lie given to you by liars, yes. Welcome to my entire childhood." But she said it without cruelty; she said it the way you'd tell someone the tide was coming in. Then, quieter: "There's one more thing. Look at the last entry."

He looked. The final page of the folio was in a different hand — shakier, hurried, ink blotted as though written by candlelight in a draft. Dated three days after the binding.

*The rite cannot be undone while her name endures in any living memory. We have struck it from every record, every bell-song, every grave. Let the archives keep only what is safe. If any soul ever speaks her true name again, the binding weakens. God forgive us. We have made certain no one will remember enough to try.*

Torv read it three times. "They erased her name. From everything. Even this archive keeps only the letter of it, not the sound."

"So the Choir eats memories," Isolde said slowly, "and the crown was born from an erased memory. Father's scholars would call that coincidence."

"Your father's scholars are currently forgetting their own children's names."

"Mm." She closed the ledger. For a moment she looked very young, and very tired, and very much her father's daughter in all the ways that hurt. "Freya needs to see this. Tonight. Before Serane notices the archive door is open, before—" 

She stopped.

Torv heard it too. Faint, rising up through the stone floor, through three hundred years of seals and masonry: a sound like many voices humming one note. Not menacing. Almost gentle. Almost like relief.

Like something that had waited a very long time, hearing footsteps overhead, beginning to hope.

Isolde blew out the candles. "Get Freya," she whispered. "Now. And Askeland — tell her to bring anything her mother ever left her."

Torv froze halfway to the door. "Why would you say that? Freya doesn't have—"

"I know what the ledger says," Isolde said. "Everyone's ledger says it. That's rather the point."
