## 11. What Was Taken from Me

The archive beneath the chapel smelled of salt and old paper and, faintly, of candle smoke that had gone out a hundred years ago and never quite admitted it.

Freya had been down there for three days.

Torv brought her food twice a day — bread, hard cheese, tea brewed from needles because the palace stores had run strange since midwinter crept early down the mountains — and each time he set the tray beside her he said something like, "You know, most people visit Haldmere for the fountains," or "There's a very nice window on the third floor. It looks at other windows." And each time she looked up at him with ink on her cheekbone and something wild behind her eyes, and each time he retreated up the stairs with the expression of a man who had poked a sleeping dog on purpose and was only now considering the consequences.

Isolde came too, in the evenings, with her ledger. She would sit cross-legged on the cold flagstones in her plain gray riding dress — she owned court gowns, Freya had seen them hanging like dead moths in her chambers, and refused them — and read aloud the entries she'd copied from the pre-outlawry records while Freya dug deeper into the shelves.

It was Isolde who found the first mention of the purge.

"Here." Her finger stopped on a line of cramped clerk's hand. "*In the twelfth year of Aldric's reign, by decree of the Lord Protector Serane, the practice of beast-speech and all kindred arts is declared outlawed throughout the realm.* That's it. One sentence. Twelve years ago."

"That can't be all of it," Freya said. "A purge is never one sentence. A purge has lists."

"There are no lists." Isolde turned pages, quick and precise, the way she did everything. "I've looked. There should be arrest records, trial transcripts, execution rolls. The archive keeps everything — I found a complaint from 200 years ago about a baker who shorted the guard on their festival loaves. There is *nothing* about the purge except the decree itself."

Freya sat back on her heels. The candle between them guttered in a draft that shouldn't have existed underground.

"No records," she said slowly, "or no *memories*?"

Isolde looked at her. In the three weeks Freya had known her, the princess had developed a whole vocabulary of silences, and this one meant: *I hadn't thought of that, and I hate how obviously true it is.*

"The Choir eats memory," Freya said. "It's been eating for years. Slowly at first — through the crown, through the wards, through every oath sworn in this kingdom. What if the purge wasn't just a law? What if it was a *meal*?"

* * *

She went looking for her mother that night, though she didn't yet know that was what she was doing.

It started small, the way these things do. She was cataloguing — Isolde's word, and Freya had adopted it because it made her feel less like a woman rummaging and more like a woman with a method — the personal effects confiscated during the outlawry years. They were kept in iron-bound chests at the far end of the archive, sealed with wax stamps that had cracked like river ice. Charms mostly. Amulets. A child's rattle carved in the shape of a wren. Dozens of small illegal tendernesses, gathered up by soldiers who had been told they were collecting weapons.

And then her hands stopped moving.

At the bottom of the third chest, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff as bark, was a charm she knew. Not one like it. *This one.* A knuckle of driftwood on a leather cord, worn smooth, with a single gull feather bound to it in red thread. She had sold hundreds of charms in Dunmark market. She had made thousands. But she knew this one the way you know your own front door in the dark — not with your eyes but with your feet.

Her mother had made it.

The thought arrived whole, unbidden, and Freya sat there holding it the way you'd hold a hot coal, waiting for the pain.

Because here was the thing. Here was the thing she had never once said out loud, in twenty-six years, not even to herself in the dark hours before dawn: she could not remember her mother's face.

She remembered a voice. Low, amused, always half a laugh away from saying something irreverent about the harbor merchants. She remembered hands — brown, strong, smelling of tallow and sea-salt — tying knots, mending nets, braiding Freya's hair so tight it made her eyes water. She remembered being carried on those hips along the Dunmark seawall, age four or five, watching the gulls wheel and hearing her mother say—

Say what?

Nothing. The words were gone. There was a hole where the words should be, smooth-edged, like a stone worn round in a streambed. And around the hole, everything else was intact: the seawall, the gulls, the weight of her own small body, the smell of low tide. Everything except the woman carrying her.

Freya had assumed, all her life, that grief had simply done what grief does. That she'd been young when her mother died — she couldn't even remember *that*, the dying, only that somewhere between one year and the next her childhood lost its center of gravity and she'd been raised loosely, kindly, by an aunt whose face she *could* remember perfectly, thank you very much, and wasn't that strange? Wasn't that the strangest thing?

She had assumed she was broken in the ordinary way people are broken.

Now, sitting on the floor of a sealed archive with her mother's charm in her hands, she understood that she was not broken. She had been *edited*.

* * *

"You're doing the thing again," Torv said.

It was near midnight. He had come down without a tray this time, which meant he'd given up on the pretense of caretaking and moved straight to worrying. He lowered himself onto a crate across from her with the creak of a man whose knees had opinions, and he waited. He was good at waiting. Ward-keepers, she supposed, had to be — standing on cold walls for years, watching for things that mostly never came.

"She was taken," Freya said. "Twelve years ago. During the purge."

Torv didn't ask who. That was the thing about Torv Askeland that still surprised her, after everything: he never asked the obvious question when the real question was sitting right underneath it, breathing.

"The purge took speakers," he said carefully. "You weren't—"

"I was fifteen. I hid it. I'd been hiding it my whole life; hiding it was as natural as breathing." She turned the driftwood charm over in her fingers. "But my mother — everyone in Dunmark knew my mother talked to gulls. Openly. Thought it was charming. The harbormaster used to pay her a copper a week to tell him which days the herring would run. Nobody thought of it as magic. It was just Maren being Maren."

"Maren," Torv repeated softly.

"Yes." And then Freya heard herself, and went still. "Wait. You know that name?"

"Do I?" Torv frowned. His brow did a complicated thing, like a man reaching for a tool that used to hang on that hook. "It sounds... yes. No. It sounds like a name I should know. Like a word on the tip of—" He shook his head. "Why do I know your mother's name, Freya? You've never spoken of her."

"Because the purge missed a thread." Her voice came out low and fierce and shaking. "When they erased her, they erased her from minds and records both. But I'm a speaker. My gift doesn't live in the same place memories live — it lives in *listening*. And somewhere in me, in whatever part of me hears, a trace survived. Enough that I kept her charm without knowing why. Enough that I said her name to you just now and it snagged on something in you — some secondhand scrap, some rumor that got swallowed wrong. The erasure isn't perfect. It's just thorough."

Torv was quiet for a long moment. When he spoke, his voice was rough. "My grandmother," he said. "I've been trying to remember my grandmother all evening. Since you said that name. I remember a kitchen. I remember her hands in flour. I cannot remember her face or her name or whether she ever said a single word to me." He looked up, and his eyes were wet and furious. "Was she taken too? Was half this kingdom taken, and we've been walking around inside the gap our whole lives calling it normal?"

"I don't know." Freya closed her hand around the charm until the cord bit her palm. "But I'm going to find out."

* * *

Isolde arrived at dawn, which meant she hadn't slept, which meant she'd found something.

She came down the archive stairs two at a time, ledger under her arm, hair escaping its braid in silver-gilt wisps, and she didn't say good morning. She dropped onto the floor beside Freya, opened the ledger to a page near the back — a page Freya had never seen, torn edges, older paper than the rest — and laid it flat between them.

"This was tucked inside my father's copy of the accession laws," Isolde said. "I found it six months ago and couldn't make sense of it. I thought it was a cipher. Look at it now."

It was a list. Names — perhaps forty of them — written in a hurried hand, some crossed out, some annotated. And beside nearly every name, in the same handwriting, a single word:

*Forgotten.*

"What is it?" Freya whispered.

"A prisoner manifest. From the purge year. Someone — a clerk, a guard, someone with access and a conscience — wrote down every speaker arrested in the southern provinces." Isolde's finger traced the column. "And then whoever kept this list watched each name vanish. Watched the trials dissolve. Watched the families stop asking questions. They wrote *forgotten* next to each one like a doctor marking symptoms." Her finger stopped. "Third from the bottom."

Freya looked.

*Maren Almeida. Dunmark. Gulls. Forgotten.*

The archive was very quiet. Somewhere above them, the chapel bell rang matins, and the sound came down through the stones like light through water.

"They took her," Freya said. Each word came out flat and separate, like coins counted onto a table. "They took her in the night, and they tried her — or they didn't — and then the Choir ate her. Out of the world. Out of the *records*. Out of me." She was on her feet without remembering standing. "Out of *me*, Isolde. I am her daughter. I am the one person alive with a right to carry her, and it reached in and scooped her out of me and left a hole and told me the hole was grief. Told me I was *lucky* to have gotten over it so cleanly."

"Freya—"

"And nobody mourned her!" The words were climbing now, loud in the stone room, echoing off shelves full of everything the kingdom had decided to keep. "Do you understand? Mourning needs witnesses. A funeral, a grave, someone to say *she was here, she mattered, she tied terrible knots and laughed at her own jokes* — and there was none of it. She died twice. Once when they took her, and once when they took her from everyone who loved her, so that no one was left to love her. Even my aunt — even the woman who raised me — she thinks she took me in out of charity. She doesn't know why. She doesn't know *who*. My mother has been dead twelve years and no one in the world is grieving her except a girl who can't remember her face!"

Her voice broke on the last word. And then, horribly, absurdly, she was crying — great ugly sobs, the kind she hadn't let herself have since she was fifteen and woke one morning with a smooth-edged hole where her childhood used to be — and Isolde did the last thing Freya expected.

The princess of Vhalen got off the floor, put her arms around her, and held on.

"It's not fair," Isolde said into her hair, fiercely, like she was arguing with the universe and expected to win. "You're right, it's not fair, and I'm going to write her down. Right now. In the ledger. Name, city, gift, daughter. And I'm going to keep writing it until this kingdom remembers how to be ashamed of itself."

* * *

Later — much later, when the candles had burned to puddles and Torv had silently appeared with tea and then silently disappeared again, because he was a man who understood that sometimes the kindest thing a person could offer was an exit — Freya sat alone with the ledger open in front of her.

Isolde's entry stood at the top of a fresh page in her precise, upright hand: *Maren Almeida, of Dunmark. Speaker to gulls. Mother of Freya. Remembered against all instruction.*

Below it, Freya added a line of her own, in her market-quick scrawl:

*She smelled of tallow and salt. She braided too tight. She was funny. I don't remember her face, but I remember that she was funny, and I will build the rest from that if it takes me the rest of my life.*

Then she closed the book, and the rage settled in her chest and cooled into something harder and more useful. All winter she had thought she was fighting for strangers — for villages without birdsong, for elders mouthing empty shapes where their children's names used to be. Good causes. Distant ones.

Not anymore. The Hollow Choir had eaten its way into her own history, into the space between her own ribs, and it had done it so smoothly she'd thanked it for the tidiness.

She climbed the stairs into the grey morning and found Torv waiting at the top, leaning against the chapel doorframe, arms crossed, watching the sky with an expression she'd learned to dread.

"Snow," he said. "In autumn. Heavy. And Freya—" He paused, and she saw him choose honesty over comfort, the way he'd been choosing it more and more lately. "The flakes are whispering. I heard them from the wall. Little voices, telling people it's time to come inside and rest. Time to forget what they were doing."

Freya looked past him, at the fat white silence already swallowing the rooftops of Haldmere, and felt her mother's charm warm against her sternum like a small held breath.

Midwinter wasn't coming.

It was here.
