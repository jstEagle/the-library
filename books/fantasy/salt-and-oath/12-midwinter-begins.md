## 12. Midwinter Begins

### Freya

The snow began at noon on the day before midwinter's eve, and it was wrong in a way Freya could not have explained to anyone who didn't speak sparrow.

She was in the palace kitchens — she had taken to haunting them lately, because the kitchen cats were honest and the cooks were generous and both things were scarce in Haldmere that winter. She sat on a flour barrel with a cup of barley tea while a one-eared mouser named Duchess conducted her morning inspection of Freya's boots.

Then Duchess stopped mid-stride, tail lifting like a raised flag, and looked at the window.

Every cat in the kitchen did it at once. Eleven of them, frozen between tasks, all facing north.

"What is it?" Freya asked softly, because asking cats questions out loud had stopped embarrassing her somewhere around the third day of the journey from Dunmark.

*Cold,* said Duchess, in the way cats say things — not words exactly, but a shape of meaning, cold with an edge on it, cold that *wanted*. *Cold coming down the mountain like it knows the way.*

Freya went to the window. The sky over the northern peaks had gone the color of pewter, and the first flakes were falling through it — fat, unhurried flakes, drifting down as if they had all the time in the world, which, she thought with a chill that had nothing to do with the weather, they might.

A sparrow landed on the sill outside, feathers puffed, and looked at her with one bright eye.

*Don't listen to it,* the sparrow said. *The white. Don't listen.*

"Listen to what? It hasn't said anything."

*It will,* said the sparrow, and flew off, and Freya stood there holding her cooling tea and understanding, with the particular dread of her gift, that the snowfall outside was not weather.

It was a voice, waiting for someone to answer.

### Torv

Torv Askeland had spent twelve years perfecting the art of being useless, and he resented how quickly the skill was abandoning him.

He was in the ward-keepers' barracks — his old barracks, though no one had ordered him back and half the men still looked at him like something scraped off a boot. The wards were dead; he had helped kill them, or failed to save them, depending on which confession you preferred, and there was nothing left to keep. But the young keepers kept coming to him anyway, because he was the only one among them who had seen the wards alive long enough to remember how they'd breathed.

"They're saying it's just an early winter," said a boy named Pell, barely nineteen, wringing his mittens. "Cook says we've had hard winters before."

"Cook is right," Torv said. "We have." He looked out at the snow, falling straight and silent and thick as wool. "This isn't one of them."

"How can you tell?"

Because the snow was falling *upward* along the north wall, half an inch above the stones, curling against the grain of the wind like it was reading the wall for weaknesses. Because the horses in the stable yard had gone quiet in a way horses never did. Because every dog in Haldmere was howling at noon, and then, worse, all at once, they stopped.

"Pell," Torv said, "when I tell you to do something today, don't ask why. Just do it."

"Yes, sir." A pause. "Sorry — I mean—"

"Don't apologize either. It wastes time." He pulled his coat off its peg. Twelve years of practice at not caring, undone in three weeks by a charm-seller with gull-scars on her palms and a princess with a ledger full of everything the kingdom was losing. He was almost grateful. Almost.

He found Freya in the courtyard, standing very still in the falling snow with her face tipped up, and his heart dropped through the cobbles.

"Freya." He crossed to her, took her shoulder. "What are you doing?"

"Listening." Her eyes were unfocused. "Torv, it's talking. The snow. It's — oh, that's clever, that's horrible—" She blinked, came back to herself, grabbed his sleeve. "It's whispering. Every flake. Little lullabies. *Rest now, rest now, you've carried it long enough.* And people are listening without knowing they're listening."

"Listening to what?"

"To forgetting." She was already moving toward the gates. "Torv, the city's been drinking this for an hour. We need Isolde. We need everyone in the great hall, away from windows, away from the open sky—"

Somewhere across the courtyard, a guard who had been pacing his post for twenty years stopped pacing, looked at his spear with mild confusion, and set it down against the wall like a man setting down a heavy basket he no longer remembered picking up.

### Isolde

Isolde had learned to measure catastrophe in ink.

Her ledger — a plain merchant's book she'd bought under a false name from a stationer who assumed she was keeping accounts for her father's wine cellar, which in a way she was — had grown fat over the autumn. In September, her father forgot one thing a week: a minor envoy's name, the year of a harvest festival. By November it was daily. Now she wrote entries in batches, because the forgetting came faster than she could walk to her rooms for the book.

*Midwinter's eve, morning:* Father called Chamberlain Vess "Vess" but could not recall Vess had a daughter. *Midday:* Father signed a pardon using his dead brother's middle name. *Afternoon:* —

She was writing *afternoon* when the screaming started in the lower city.

She went to the window of her study — against every instinct, because Freya had told her that morning about the whispers in the snow, and Freya's instincts about such things had proven better than any general's — and looked down into the square below the palace walls.

At first it looked like a festival crowd. People in the streets, milling, calling to one another. Then she saw that they weren't celebrating. They were *searching*. A woman stood in the center of the square turning in slow circles, opening her mouth, closing it, opening it again, like a fish. A man knelt beside a boy of perhaps six, gripping the child's shoulders and shaking him gently, and even from the window Isolde could read the shape of the question on his lips, because she had written that question forty times in her own hand:

*What is your name? What is your name?*

The boy was crying. The man wasn't shaking him anymore. He was just kneeling there, and Isolde watched his face go slack with a horror too large for him, watched him look up at the falling snow as if it might hand back what it had taken.

Then the crowd changed. Fear curdled into fury, the way it does, needing somewhere to go. Someone shouted that the king had done it, that the crown had cursed them, that the palace had eaten their children's names — and Isolde, who knew precisely how true and how false that was, felt the unfairness of it like a physical blow, because her father was in his chambers right now losing himself flake by flake same as any fishwife in the square, and the mob would burn the palace down around him before they'd ever know it.

"Guards!" She was moving before she finished the word, ledger clutched to her chest like a shield. "Seal the gates! Get the household inside, away from the windows — *now!*"

A captain started toward her, then stopped. Frowned. Looked at his own hands.

"Captain," Isolde said, sharp as a whip-crack. "Your name. Say your name."

"Bren—" He frowned harder. "Brennan. Captain Brennan, my lady." Relief flooded his face, then shame. "Forgive me, it slipped, it just — slipped—"

"It's slipping everywhere," Isolde said. "Say your name every hour. Write it on your hand if you must. That's an order." She had never given an order in her life. It came out so naturally that Brennan saluted, and she thought, distantly, *so that's what Father always felt like, God help him.*

In the corridor, she collided with Torv and Freya, both breathless, snow melting on their shoulders.

"The lower city's rioting," Isolde said. "They're blaming the crown."

"They're not wrong," Freya said quietly.

"No," Isolde agreed. "That's the worst part. Seal the palace. And Freya — the dogs. Have you checked the dogs?"

Freya's face answered for her. The palace dogs had refused the throne room since the day they'd arrived. This afternoon, Isolde had been informed, they had begun refusing the entire east wing.

Where the king's chambers were.

### Aldric

King Aldric Vhalen was losing a game of cards with a memory, and losing gracefully, which was the only way he knew how to lose anything anymore.

He sat by the fire in his chambers with a small wooden box on his knees. Inside it were things he had collected against the dark, on the advice of a charm-seller with kind eyes and a scarred hands — his daughter's idea, or the charm-seller's, or his own; the provenance of good ideas had become uncertain lately. A lock of hair tied with blue ribbon. A letter in his wife Maren's hand. A painted miniature of Isolde at seven, gap-toothed and furious about it.

He held the miniature and repeated, like a prayer, like a rope thrown to a drowning man: *Isolde. My daughter is Isolde. She keeps lists. She bites her thumbnail when she reads. She has her mother's chin and my stubbornness and neither of our patience.*

The fire crackled. Outside the window, the snow whispered.

*You've carried it long enough,* said the snow, in a voice like his mother's, like Maren's, like his own. *Put it down. Rest. Kings carry so much. You could be light.*

"I know what you are," Aldric said to the window. His voice sounded old in the empty room. "I've known since autumn. You're not a comfort. You're a mouth."

*And yet,* the snow said pleasantly, *you cannot remember her face.*

And that was the cruelty of it, the precision. Because it was true. He had Maren's handwriting, her ring, her grave, her name — but her face was gone, scooped out clean, and where it had been there was a smooth white blank like fresh snowfall, and the snow offered, gently, to fill it. Give me a little more, it murmured. Give me the year of your coronation, give me your brother's laugh, and I'll give her back to you. Her smile. Her frown. The way she looked at you across a crowded hall. Fair trade. Fair trade.

"That's not a trade," Aldric said. "That's a loan shark wearing her face."

But his hands were shaking, because some nights — most nights — he wanted it. God forgive him, he wanted his wife's face more than he wanted his kingdom, and the thing in the crown knew it, and waited, patient as winter, patient as snow.

There was a knock. Isolde's voice: "Father? It's me. May I come in?"

He closed the box. He put on his face — the one he had left, the kingly one, assembled each morning from habit and love and spite.

"Always," he said.

She entered with snow-melt on her shoulders and terror carefully folded away behind her eyes, and she sat beside him, and took his hand, and said, "Tell me about Mother. Tell me anything. Start anywhere."

And King Aldric, who was losing everything, found that he could still do this one thing: he could talk. So he talked, fast, hoarding aloud — Maren's laugh, Maren's terrible singing, the rain on their wedding day, Isolde's birth in the blue room — pouring it out while the snow whispered at the glass, spending his treasure while he still owned it, and neither of them said what both of them knew, which was that he was making a will of words, and that his daughter was memorizing every line.

### Serane

Lord Protector Serane watched the riot from the high window of the armory tower, and felt nothing she would have called guilt.

Below, the lower city burned in patches — a customs house, a chandler's shop, the gatehouse of the granary, because hungry frightened people always went for the granary first; she had read enough histories to know the choreography. The palace guards were sealing the gates. Some of them were crying. Loyalty, she thought, watching a sergeant drag a sobbing recruit bodily indoors, loyalty was such a useful solvent. It dissolved everything except itself.

The Choir had promised her this would be gentle. And in fairness — she had always prided herself on fairness — it mostly was. No plague-carts this time. No mass graves steaming in the summer heat, no little names chalked on doors. Just a soft white forgetting, a kingdom laid down to sleep like an old dog by a fire. She had lost her husband and both daughters to the grey fever in a single season, and she had sworn, kneeling in the ash of that summer, that she would never watch a city die screaming again. If the price was that no one remembered her daughters' names — well. The fever hadn't cared about names either. At least the Choir kept its bargains.

Mostly.

She turned from the window. On her desk lay a report she had read four times: the charm-seller Almeida had been seen in the courtyard at noon, standing in the open snow, listening. Not resisting. *Listening.* Which meant the girl could hear it, which meant the girl was exactly the threat the Choir feared and exactly the tool Serane needed — and tools, Serane had learned long ago, were best picked up before anyone else thought to reach for them.

There was also the matter of the archive beneath the chapel. Someone had broken the seal. Someone had been reading about the crown's origins, the bound speaker, the truth underneath three hundred years of tidy lies. Serane had her suspicions. Suspicions with a princess's handwriting on them.

She rang for her steward. "Find the Almeida woman. Politely. Tell her the Lord Protector requests a private audience tonight, after the curfew bell." She hesitated, then added, because fairness demanded it: "Tell her she may bring the ward-keeper, if it makes her feel safer. Tell her—" and here Serane smiled her hollow smile at the burning city below, "—tell her I have something to confess."

### Freya

They gathered in the great hall as the light died — Isolde with her ledger, Torv with his sword and his old keeper's badges polished for the first time in years, Freya with Duchess the cat riding her shoulder like a furry accusation, because the cats had refused to be left anywhere alone that day and Freya had stopped arguing with cats years ago.

Beyond the high windows, the snow fell and fell, whispering, patient. Below in the city, fires flickered and bells rang raggedly, and every hour the guards came to report another name lost, another face blank, another soldier found sitting quietly in the snow with his helmet off, smiling, letting it take him.

"We can't fight snow," Torv said flatly. "I've been thinking about it all afternoon and that's my conclusion. You can't fight snow."

"We're not fighting snow," Freya said. "We're fighting what's *in* it. And it's in the crown first, the city second, and us last. Which means—" she looked at Isolde, "—we still have time. A little."

"How much?" Isolde asked.

Freya closed her eyes and listened, really listened, past the lullaby-hiss of the flakes, down to the vast slow hunger underneath, the choir of stolen voices singing their endless white round. It was closer than it had been in autumn. It was *inside the walls* now, inside the oaths of every sworn soldier, sipping loyalty through a hundred thousand straws.

"Three days," she said. "Maybe four. Then midwinter stops being a date and becomes a door, and it walks through it."

Silence in the great hall. Duchess put one paw deliberately on Freya's hand, claws sheathed, which from Duchess was roughly equivalent to a battlefield promotion of courage.

Then the doors opened, and a servant in Serane's colors entered, bowed precisely, and delivered the message.

Freya read it twice. *The Lord Protector requests a private audience. Tonight. Bring whom you trust. I have something to confess.*

"Well," said Torv, after a long moment. "Either it's a trap, or it's the first honest sentence anyone's spoken in this palace since we arrived."

"With Serane," Isolde said grimly, snapping her ledger shut, "it has historically been both."

Outside, the snow went on whispering its lullabies to a dying kingdom, and Freya pulled her coat tight, and they went to hear what the Lord Protector had to confess.
