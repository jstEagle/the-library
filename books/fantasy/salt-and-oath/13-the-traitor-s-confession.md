## 13. The Traitor's Confession

Serane had always believed that a person could be measured by how they took their tea.

The frightened ones gulped it. The proud ones let it cool while they talked. The dangerous ones watched the steam and said nothing at all.

Princess Isolde did none of these things. She sat across Serane's desk in the Lord Protector's study, laid a leather-bound ledger on the blotter with both hands — the way one lays down a verdict — and waited for her tea to reach exactly the temperature she liked. Which was, Serane noted with something like professional admiration, precisely the temperature at which it had been poured.

"You've been in my archive," Isolde said.

"I don't have an archive."

"The royal archive. Beneath the chapel. Sealed by writ twelve years ago." Isolde opened the ledger to a page marked with a ribbon. "Torv Askeland's name is still on the old access rolls. He never signed out his key. Sloppy of him. Convenient for me."

Serane folded her hands and gave the girl her best smile — the hollow one, the court called it, though they only thought it, never said it. "Your Highness, if you've come about your father's health—"

"Don't." The word came out flat as a dropped plate. "Please don't do the voice. I've watched you do the voice at ambassadors and grain merchants and my father, and I know exactly what it costs you to produce it, which is nothing. Sit there and be quiet for once. I have read your ledgers too."

Ah, thought Serane. There it is.

She had known this day would come. She had planned for it, actually — three versions of it, each with its own exit, its own contingency, its own carefully worded confession calibrated to whatever evidence the child managed to dig up. Serane was very good at confessions. She'd rehearsed them the way other women rehearsed lullabies.

But she hadn't planned for the ledger.

It sat there between them, fat with years of small, meticulous grief. *Father forgot the name of the eastern harvest festival. Father forgot Mother's favorite chair. Father asked me twice who taught him to ride.* Page after page in a hand that had started round and childish and ended narrow and hard, like a road walked too many times.

"You were eight when you started it," Serane said quietly.

"Seven." Isolde's chin lifted. "I didn't understand what I was writing. I just knew things kept going missing from him and everyone pretended they hadn't."

The fire ticked in the grate. Somewhere below, in the courtyard, the changing of the guard went through its ritual — boots, steel, the oath spoken in unison, *by crown and winter, by salt and blood* — and Serane felt the words pass through the floor and up through the legs of her chair like a cold draft. She always felt them now. That was the price of knowing.

"Ask me," she said.

Isolde blinked. "What?"

"You came here with accusations loaded like crossbows. Ask me instead. One question. The right one." Serane leaned back. "I find I'm tired of the voice, as you put it. It's been a long twelve years."

Isolde studied her the way she studied everything — as though taking inventory. Then: "Why does the Choir need the king?"

And there it was. The right question, first try. Serane closed her eyes briefly. *Aldric,* she thought, *your daughter is better at this than either of us ever was.*

"Not the king," she said. "The crown needs the kingdom. The king is just where it keeps its mouth."

* * *

She told it plainly, because plainness was all that was left to her, and because — though she would deny it under torture, and had arranged to be tortured by no one who could survive the attempt — some small starved part of her had wanted to say it aloud for twelve years.

"When the plague came," Serane began, "the grey sweating, they called it — you won't remember it, they took that too, eventually — it killed one household in four. My husband died on a Tuesday. My daughters followed within the month. I held them. I buried them in the chapel yard with my own hands because the gravediggers were dying faster than the diggers could be replaced." Her voice stayed level. She was rather proud of that. "And then a man came to me. Not a man. Something wearing a man's courtesy. It said: *Grief is memory with nowhere to go. Give us the memories, and the grief goes too. Give us the kingdom's remembering, and the plague will have nothing left to feed on but what people are willing to forget.*"

"That's not how plagues work," Isolde said.

"No," Serane agreed. "But it is how the Choir works. It doesn't eat flesh. It eats the past. And a kingdom drowning in death has more past than it can carry. So we bargained. The wards were raised — no. The wards were *opened*, properly opened, tuned the way the old ward-keepers used to tune them before your grandfather outlawed speakers and burned their books. And the plague slowed. And then stopped. And everyone celebrated, and I stood at the victory feast holding wine I couldn't taste, knowing what we'd paid."

"What did you pay?" Isolde asked, though her eyes had gone to the ledger, as if she already suspected the currency.

"Memories. At first, mine alone. Small ones. The smell of my mother's kitchen. My wedding morning. The color of my daughters' eyes — I know they had eyes, Your Highness, I know it the way you know arithmetic, but I cannot see them anymore." She said it without drama; drama was a luxury. "But mine weren't enough. The Choir has an appetite like winter has cold. It needed more. And this—" here she paused, because this was the part that mattered, the part Freya Almeida and Torv Askeland and every fool who thought the answer was a sword would need to understand before midwinter finished falling— "this is the mechanism. Listen closely, because I will only say it once, and then I expect you to hate me efficiently so we can move on to saving what's left."

Isolde's jaw tightened. "Say it."

"The Choir feeds through loyalty." Serane spread her hands. "Every oath sworn to the crown is a straw in its mouth. Every soldier who says *by crown and winter* opens a little channel. Every servant who curtsies. Every subject who pays taxes gladly, who sings the anthem, who tells their children the king is good and means it. Devotion is the vector. Love of country, love of king, love of law — all of it flows uphill to the throne, and the crown drinks. The more loyal the kingdom, the fatter the Choir grows, and the faster the past runs out of it. That's why the forgetting accelerates. That's why your father worsens every year he reigns well. His goodness is killing him. Their faithfulness is eating them."

The silence that followed had weight. In the courtyard below, the relief watch took up the oath again, and Isolde flinched — actually flinched — hearing it now, hearing the straws sucking.

"That's obscene," she whispered.

"Yes."

"It's — every song, every ceremony, every time someone says *long live the king*—"

"They're setting the table," Serane said. "You begin to see why I stopped attending chapel."

Isolde pushed back from the desk and stood, pacing — three steps, turn, three steps, a habit Serane recognized from her father, which must have stung the girl if she noticed it. "Then the answer is simple. We depose him. A dead or abdicated king breaks the oaths—"

"Does it?" Serane let the question hang. "An oath sworn *by crown and winter* isn't sworn to the man, child. It's sworn to the crown. To the idea. The Choir lives in the idea. You could put Aldric in a boat and row him out to sea and half the kingdom would still be pouring itself into that thing's mouth every time they felt patriotic. Loyalty outlives its object. That's what makes it loyalty." She smiled, and this time it wasn't the hollow one; it was the tired one underneath, which fewer people had seen. "No. The oaths have to be *released*. Unbound. Freely relinquished, one by one, by the people who swore them — and there is exactly one kind of person alive who can perform such a rite, and your grandfather's purge nearly made certain there would be none."

Understanding moved across Isolde's face like weather. "The charm-seller. Almeida."

"She heard the gulls, didn't she? Of course she did." Serane sighed and reached for her own tea, gone cold, and drank it anyway, because she had long ago stopped being particular. "Twelve years ago I helped burn the speakers' college. I told myself it was necessary — that loose voices were a danger, that the wards worked fine without them. And then I learned what the wards were actually for. Not protection. A lid. And I had helped weld it shut over the only people who might have lifted it." She set the cup down with a small precise click. "So yes, Your Highness. I am the traitor. I sold my kingdom's yesterdays to stop its tomorrows from ending. I have fed it my family, my history, and finally my face — do you know I found a portrait of myself in the west gallery last spring and did not recognize the woman in it? I assumed she was some dead cousin. She had my laugh. I checked the plaque twice."

For the first time, something in Isolde's armor cracked — not forgiveness, nothing so generous, but recognition. Grief knows grief the way dogs know dogs.

"My mother," Isolde said. "There's no portrait of my mother anywhere in the palace. No letters. I asked once and Father looked at me like I'd asked about the weather on the moon. Was that—"

"The purge takes whole people, sometimes, when they're bound tightly enough to the crown. Queen Maren loved Aldric enormously. She was — nourishing." Serane's stomach turned saying it. Good. Let it turn. Numbness was the Choir's gift, and she refused every scrap of it. "I'm sorry. I was not the architect of that particular theft, but I held the door."

Isolde stood very still. When she spoke again, her voice had gone quiet and terrible, the voice Serane imagined she'd use someday on councils and battlefields. "Then here is what happens now. You will come with me. You will tell Almeida everything you told me — the mechanism, the rite, everything in the archive. And when she stands in that courtyard and unbinds the oaths, you will stand beside her wearing your treason openly, so that every soul in Haldmere knows the cost of what was done and who did it."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then I publish the ledger and the archive both, and Haldmere decides what traitors get in midwinter."

Serane considered it honestly — the escape routes, the contingencies, the third version of this day she'd rehearsed. All of them ended with her safe and the kingdom eaten, and somewhere in the last few minutes, without noticing the exact moment, she had stopped wanting that.

"You know what will happen to her," Serane said instead. "Almeida. If she unbinds the army's oaths publicly, the crown's party will call it treason before she finishes the first line. The hunters will come. White-eyed ones, by then. Sworn men who can't be reasoned with because their reasoning has been drunk like soup." She rose, stiff-backed, and took her cloak from its peg — a heavy thing, fur-lined, absurdly warm for a woman who had been cold for twelve years. "They'll call her traitor too. They'll be wrong, but they'll shout it louder than the truth ever manages to."

"Then she'll need witnesses," Isolde said. "People who saw the truth first."

Serane paused at the door, hand on the latch, and allowed herself one last look at the study where she had committed her slow treason in neat columns and polite correspondence. On the desk lay the princess's ledger, open to the final page, where a new entry sat in fresh ink:

*Father forgot my birthday. He apologized, which means some of him still knows there is something to forget. Hurry.*

"Hurry indeed," Serane murmured, and opened the door onto the corridor — where, far below in the courtyard, the evening watch was swearing its oath to the crown, hundreds of voices rising together like water finding a drain, and the first snow of the early winter was coming down soft and white and whispering.
