## 14. Breaking Oaths

The rite had no name anymore. That was the first thing Freya learned, digging through Torv's battered memory of ward-keeping and Isolde's ledger of forbidden things: the old speakers had never needed to name it, because you did not name a thing as common as breathing. Releasing an oath had once been as ordinary as a midwife's work. Kings had made it treason in a single generation, and like everything else the crown touched, the forgetting had been thorough.

"So we'll invent a new word for it," Freya said, on the third night of preparation, when her hands would not stop shaking and she'd snapped at Torv twice for breathing too loudly near her notes.

"Traitor," Torv offered from the doorway. "I believe that word is taken."

"Something kinder."

"Heretic?"

"You're terrible at this."

"I'm excellent at this," he said, coming in and setting down two cups of tea that neither of them would drink while it was warm. "Naming things honestly is half of ward-keeping. You can't ward against what you're too polite to call by its name." He looked at the circle she'd chalked on the floor of the old granary — salt at the four quarters, a fifth line left deliberately open, facing the door. "That gap is wrong."

"It's meant to be wrong. An oath freely released has to be able to leave. If I close the circle, I'm just building a smaller cage." She rubbed her eyes. "Everything about this is backwards, Torv. Every instinct I have says seal it, hold it, protect the people inside. And the whole rite only works if I open the door."

He was quiet for a moment. Then: "My grandmother used to say the same about larks. You can keep one alive in a cage, but it won't sing unless the latch is loose enough that it *could* go."

"Did yours sing?"

"Never. But it stayed anyway." He said it lightly, and then seemed to hear what he'd said, and went slightly pink, and became very interested in his tea.

Freya decided, with great discipline, not to say anything about that at all.

* * *

They chose the courtyard of the Haldmere barracks because it could not be mistaken for anything secret. No chapel, no cellar, no midnight. Noon, in the open, with the snow falling in its whispering way — though the whispers had grown thinner since Serane's confession, since Isolde had begun telling soldiers the truth of what their loyalty fed. Knowledge didn't stop the Choir from drinking. But it made the drinking taste different, Isolde said, and Freya believed her, because the animals had taught her long ago that a creature which knows it is being hunted fights differently than one that doesn't.

Two hundred soldiers stood in the snow. Some had come out of curiosity. Some because Princess Isolde had ordered nothing and asked everything, which turned out to be more persuasive than orders ever were. And some, Freya suspected, had come hoping to see the traitor burned on the spot, and were disappointed to find only a small woman with chapped hands and a chalk-stained sleeve.

Serane was under guard in her own tower, having confessed everything and then refused, with maddening serenity, to help further. "You cannot unmake a bargain," she'd told Freya through the bars, "only stop renewing it. Go and stop renewing it." It was the closest thing to a blessing the Lord Protector would ever give.

Isolde stood at the courtyard's edge in plain wool, no circlet, her ledger tucked under her arm like a shield. Torv stood beside Freya inside the chalk, which was not part of any rite and which he refused to apologize for.

"Last chance to run," Freya murmured.

"I don't run. I limp with dignity."

She almost laughed. Her heart was hammering so hard she could hear it in her ears, and the laughter helped, and she suspected he knew that too.

She raised her hands, and spoke the words she had rebuilt from fragments — from the archive, from the drowned shrine's warmth still lingering in her bones, from Sefa's grief, which had become, strangely, a kind of instruction manual. The old tongue came off her tongue easier than it should have, as if the language had been waiting three hundred years for someone to use it for mercy instead of binding.

"Hear me," she said, and her voice carried farther than a voice should across snow. The dogs at the barracks gate, who had refused the throne room, lay down all at once, ears forward. "You were sworn. Your oaths were given in good faith to men and women who did not tell you what they fed. A promise made to a lie is not broken when you set it down. It is finished. I do not command you to leave your oaths. I only unlock the door. What you do after is yours."

She spoke the releasing. It felt like opening a window in a house shut for winter — cold rushing in, stale air rushing out, and underneath it, faintly, a sound like a vast throat swallowing, and then, for the first time, choking.

Across the courtyard, men and women staggered. One sergeant dropped his sword and sat down in the snow and began to laugh, or weep, or both. A young archer tore at her collar as if something had been resting on her neck for years and had suddenly stepped off. Freya felt each release like a small bell ringing somewhere behind her ribs — forty, sixty, ninety of them, a carillon of doors opening.

And some did not open.

Eleven soldiers stood rigid in the snow, white-eyed, and then — worse — eleven soldiers shook their heads, eyes clearing, and planted their feet, and one of them, a grey-bearded captain named Halvard whom Isolde had trusted for twenty years, said in a voice like grinding stone:

"No."

Freya's stomach dropped. "Captain—"

"You unlocked the door, speaker. Fine. I heard it open." He set his hand on his sword hilt, not drawing, just holding, the way a man holds a railing in a storm. "I swore to King Aldric when he was a man worth swearing to. Before the crown ate him. I remember that man. Somebody in this kingdom has to remember him, and if my remembering feeds the thing wearing his face, then let it choke on him a little longer. I choose it. Do you hear me? I *choose* it, and you can't take that from me, because I'm not giving it back — I'm giving it *forward*."

The courtyard was very quiet. Snow whispered. Somewhere behind Freya, Torv exhaled slowly.

"That's not how it works," Freya said gently. "Loyalty given to the Choir isn't loyalty anymore. It's a straw in its mouth."

"Then it's a straw that knows what it is," Halvard said. "There's a difference between a fool and a martyr, girl, and I've decided which one I am today. Ask me again at midwinter."

Three others stepped to stand beside him. Seven more wavered, and then followed. Eleven oaths, held open-eyed, in full knowledge, and Freya felt them like eleven stones in her chest — but stones that burned instead of sank, because there was something in them the Choir could not digest. Choice. It had eaten every memory in Vhalen, every name, every face, and it could not eat a decision made after the truth was known.

"They're feeding it poison," Isolde said quietly, at her shoulder. "Look at the snow."

Freya looked. Where the eleven stood, the falling flakes did not whisper. They fell silent, ordinary, dead as weather ought to be.

* * *

It was Torv who did the thing that Freya would remember — later, when remembering was the whole of her life's work — for the rest of whatever she had left.

The crowd was dispersing. The released soldiers wandered the barracks in a daze, touching their own necks, asking each other their names like men waking from fever. And Torv walked to the center of the courtyard, into the trampled snow where the chalk circle still showed, and cleared his throat, and said, in a voice pitched to carry:

"If anyone's still listening, I have an announcement."

"Torv," Freya hissed. "What are you doing."

"Being honest. It's new for me; bear with me." He found her eyes and held them, and his ears had gone pink again, and he plowed on regardless. "Twelve years ago I kept the border wards, and they failed, and I drank about it for a long time, and drinking about a thing is not the same as answering for it. So here is my answer." He turned to face the courtyard, the walls, the windows full of watching faces, the sky where stars should have been waiting for nightfall. "I, Torv Askeland, swear an oath — freely given, easily broken. I swear to stand beside Freya Almeida for as long as she'll have me, and if she tells me tomorrow to go, I'll go, and it will still have counted. No chain in it. No cage. A latch loose enough that the lark could leave." His voice cracked, just slightly, on the last part. "It stays because it stays. That's the whole oath. That's all of it."

Silence. Then Halvard, of all people, barked a laugh. "Worst oath I ever heard. No terms, no penalty clause, no term of service. Any barrack-lawyer would tear it apart in a minute."

"Aye," Torv said. "That's rather the point."

And Freya felt it land — felt the new oath settle into the world like a seed into thawing ground, felt the Choir recoil from it the way a tongue recoils from a bitter herb. Because there was nothing to drink. An oath freely given and freely breakable had no purchase, no hook, no straw. It was not food. It was only love, wearing the grammar of loyalty, and the Hollow Choir had forgotten, in three hundred years of eating, that such a thing existed.

She crossed the snow to him. Two hundred people watched, and Isolde was smiling in a way that suggested she would be entering this in the ledger, and Freya did not care even slightly.

"That was the most reckless thing I've ever seen," she told him.

"I've seen you talk to gulls. I had competition."

"Torv." She took his cold hands in hers. "Ask me again at midwinter."

His face did something complicated and wonderful. "That's my line."

"Borrowed. Everything good is borrowed." She rose on her toes and kissed his cheek, in front of God and the garrison and the whispering snow, and somewhere far to the north, beneath the ice, something ancient screamed — not in rage, Freya thought, but in hunger, the hunger of a thing that has just watched a meal walk away on its own legs.

* * *

They came for her at dusk.

Not hunters — not yet, not with white eyes. Worse: heralds, in royal livery, bearing a decree read aloud at the palace gates by torchlight while the snow listened. *Freya Almeida, called speaker, traitor to the crown, has unlawfully dissolved the sacred bonds of fealty. She is to be taken alive and brought before the king.*

Brought before the king. Freya read the true message underneath it the way she read animal tracks: the Choir wanted her close now. Wanted to look at the thing that had learned to open doors. Perhaps to eat her gift entire. Perhaps — and this thought arrived cold and unwelcome — perhaps it had finally understood what she was planning, and wanted her within reach of the crown before she could reach the shrine.

Isolde met them in the corridor outside Freya's rooms, hood up, ledger clutched to her chest, and behind her the calm of the palace had curdled into something running on rails. Servants moved without speaking. Guards stared through walls. And down the great stair, slow as a funeral, came a procession that made Freya's blood turn to salt water: the king's own banner, and under it, wrapped in furs against a cold he could no longer feel, King Aldric himself — walking, upright, awake-eyed, descending toward the gates as if to lead the hunt personally.

Except Aldric could not walk. Freya had seen him carried to his audience chamber not ten days past.

"He's going south," Isolde whispered. Her knuckles were white on the ledger. "Freya — he's going to the coast. He's going to the shrine. It's taking him to meet you halfway."

Torv's hand found Freya's shoulder, steady and warm. Outside, the first true dark of midwinter was gathering, starless, patient, and the snow had begun to whisper again — but thinner now, hoarser, like a choir that had lost voices and knew it.

"We ride tonight," Freya said. "Ahead of him. Whatever it costs."

As if in answer, far off toward the salt coast, something beneath the ice began — very softly, very sweetly, in a voice three hundred years out of practice — to sing.
