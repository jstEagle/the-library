## 15. Flight to the Salt Coast

They left Haldmere through the corpse of a garden.

It had been the palace's pride once, Freya gathered from Isolde's clipped commentary — rose terraces, a fountain shaped like a leaping trout, hedges trimmed by a man who had been paid more than a soldier and had, in Torv's opinion, deserved it. Now frost had taken everything in a single night. The roses stood black and brittle, each bud frozen mid-bloom, as if the garden had been caught holding its breath.

"The trout fountain's frozen solid," Isolde said, picking her way over the cracked flagstones. "My father used to throw coins in it when I was small. One for every wish." She paused at the edge of the terrace. "I checked yesterday. The ice is full of coins. Nobody remembers wishing them."

"Cheerful," said Torv. "Anyone else want to say something hopeful before we climb the wall? I had one prepared about the weather."

"You had one prepared about the weather?"

"It was short."

Freya almost smiled. Almost. Behind them, above the frozen garden, the palace windows glowed with that particular light she had come to dread — not firelight, but the pale, patient shine of something watching through its host. Somewhere in there, King Aldric sat on his throne with snow gathering on his shoulders indoors, and somewhere beneath the crown, a man was still screaming in a room too small for anyone to hear.

She put her hand against the stable wall and listened. The horses were gone — taken by the guard, or fled, or simply forgotten where they stood until they starved. But the wall itself remembered pigeons, hundreds of seasons of them, and under her palm the old stone hummed with their ghost-warmth.

"There," she said. "The north postern. The dogs won't go near it."

"The dogs won't go near anything anymore," Isolde said. "Half of them have stopped answering to their names."

"That's how we know which way to run," Torv said grimly. "Follow the absence of dogs."

* * *

The postern gate opened onto the road north, and the road north was wrong.

Freya had traveled out of Dunmark often enough to know what winter roads sounded like: creak of cart, complaint of mule, the small industry of sparrows working the hedgerows. This road made no sound at all. The snow lay smooth as poured milk, unbroken from hedge to hedge, and the hedges themselves stood silent — no sparrows, no wrens, not even the fat gray crows that would squabble over a dead fox at a funeral.

"They've gone quiet like the villages," she murmured.

"Worse," Torv said. He walked with his ward-keeper's staff, the iron-shod end leaving the only honest prints in the snow besides theirs. "In the villages, the silence was hungry. This is..." He searched for the word. "Obedient. The silence out here has been told to wait."

Isolde pulled her cloak tighter. "Then let's be rude and keep talking."

So they talked. It was Torv's idea, though he disguised it as complaining. He complained about the state of the roads, the price of salt, the quality of royal wine he'd drunk twelve years ago at a wedding nobody could now name. Isolde contributed dry observations about the courtiers who would discover her missing by morning and spend an entire council session pretending not to panic. Freya found herself laughing twice — actual laughter, startled out of her like birds off a fence — and hated how guilty it felt, and laughed anyway.

Because the alternative was listening. And if you listened, really listened, the way she couldn't stop doing, you could hear the whispers riding the wind off the fields. Not words yet. Words came later, in the deep snows. For now it was only a sound like pages turning in a very large book, somewhere past the horizon, and every few minutes a page tore out.

"What does it feel like?" Isolde asked her, on the second evening, when they'd sheltered in a shepherd's hut whose flock had wandered off mid-grazing weeks ago and never been sought. "When it takes someone. Can you tell?"

Freya fed sticks into the little stove and watched the fire take them. "You know the moment after you wake from a dream, when you can feel it going? You grab at it and your hands close on nothing?"

"Yes."

"It's like that. Except the dream is your mother's voice. Or your own name. And your hands don't work right ever again."

The hut went quiet. In the quiet, Freya heard Isolde open her ledger — the small oilskin-wrapped book she carried everywhere now, the record of everything her father had forgotten — and write. The scratch of the nib was the loudest thing in the room.

"My father used to sing," Isolde said at last. "Badly. He sang to my mother when she was ill, and after she died he kept singing, worse every year, because he said grief needed somewhere to live and his lived in his throat." A pause. "I wrote that down three days ago because I realized I was starting to doubt it. That he ever sang. That's how it works, isn't it? First it takes the thing, then it takes your belief in the thing."

"Yes," Freya said. "That's exactly how it works."

"Then I'll write faster."

* * *

On the fourth day they reached the crossroads shrine at Marrow Ford, and that was where Freya first saw the hunters.

There were three of them, standing in the falling snow at the meeting of the roads, and at first glance they looked like soldiers — royal blue cloaks, swords at their hips, boots planted in the stance of men who had stood many watches. But they did not stamp their feet against the cold. They did not shift their weight. Snow accumulated on their shoulders and hats and they let it lie, and their eyes —

Their eyes were white. Not blind-white, not scarred-white. White the way milk is white, the way the snow was white, white all the way through with nothing looking out, and yet Freya knew with absolute certainty that they saw her. All three heads turned together, smooth as weathervanes finding wind.

"Down," Torv breathed, and pulled her behind the ford's stone marker.

The hunters did not advance. They simply stood, and turned, and stood, and after a long moment they spoke — all three in unison, one voice wearing three throats, a voice like a choir rehearsing in an empty church:

**"SHE WHO HEARS. THE CROWN SEES THROUGH ITS SWORN. COME HOME. ALL OATHS RETURN TO THE CROWN. ALL VOICES RETURN TO THE CHOIR."**

Then they turned, all three at once, back toward the capital, and walked away into the snow without leaving footprints.

Freya discovered she was shaking. Not from cold. From the voice — because she had heard underneath it, the way you hear the sea inside a shell, dozens of other voices pressed flat beneath the announcement, and some of them were still fighting. One of them, faint as a candle seen through a shut door, was singing. Badly.

"Oh no," she whispered. "Oh, Torv. The king's in there. Some of him is still in there."

"I know." Torv's face was gray. "I heard it too."

"No, you heard a monster. I heard a man singing to his dead wife while something wears his mouth." She pressed her fists against her eyes. "We have four days to the coast. Maybe five. How many sworn soldiers does Haldmere have?"

"Three thousand," Isolde said quietly. "Give or take the ones who've already forgotten why they're marching."

* * *

They cut west after that, off the road, through country so empty it felt pre-human. Torv led them along drovers' paths he swore he remembered from his ward-keeping years, though twice he took a turning, stood frowning at a landscape that didn't match his memory, and chose another. On the fifth day the land began to smell of salt, faint and far, like a letter from someone you'd thought dead.

That night, camped in the lee of a stone sheepfold, Isolde asked the question none of them had wanted to ask.

"When we reach the shrine. When you do whatever it is you're going to do." She was cleaning her sword, a princess's habit done with a soldier's competence. "What happens to the oaths? All the soldiers, the servants, everyone bound to the crown?"

"They come loose," Freya said. "That's the point. The Choir feeds through loyalty — Serane told us that much truth. Break the binding, starve it."

"And the people? The ones who've been... ridden. Like those three at the ford. Do they wake up?"

Torv answered, because Freya couldn't. "Some do. Some won't remember the last weeks. Some—" He stopped.

"Some won't wake up at all," Isolde finished. She set down her sword. "I need to know these things, Freya. I'm the heir. Whatever's left of this kingdom afterward will be mine to carry, and I refuse to carry it believing pleasant lies."

Freya met her eyes across the fire. "Some won't wake up. And some will wake and wish they hadn't. And I can't tell you the number, and I hate that I can't, and I'm going to do it anyway."

Isolde nodded slowly, and then, unexpectedly, she smiled — tired and crooked and real. "Good. You're the first person in months who's lied to me less than my father did, and he only lied by forgetting."

* * *

The hunters found them on the seventh day, at the cliffs above Gullwrack Bay, and the cost came due all at once.

There were six of them this time, coming up the cliff path in that terrible synchronized silence, snow not daring to settle on their shoulders. Freya heard them first — or rather heard the absence of them, a hole in the world's noise moving uphill — and shouted the warning, and then everything happened very fast.

Torv planted his staff and did the last thing a disgraced ward-keeper should be able to do: he raised a ward. Not a good one. A ragged, guttering half-circle of force, the kind of thing that would have gotten him laughed out of his order in the old days, thrown up from twelve years of rust and guilt and sheer bloody-minded refusal. It caught the first hunter's blade an arm's length from Freya's ribs and held, groaning, the iron shoe of his staff glowing cherry-red.

"It won't hold!" he roared. "It won't hold, get to the sh—"

Isolde moved.

Later, Freya would try to reconstruct those thirty seconds and fail. She remembered Isolde stepping past her, calm as a woman crossing a ballroom, sword drawn. She remembered the first hunter going down with the princess's blade through the gap beneath his helm — no cry, no blood-cry, just the awful mechanical collapse of something that had stopped being operated. She remembered the second one's sword taking Isolde high in the side, below the ribs, a red bloom spreading on blue-and-white silk, and Isolde *staying in the fight*, twisting with the blow instead of away from it, dragging her blade free of the first hunter and putting it through the second's throat.

And she remembered the rest of the hunters stopping. All at once. Six white faces turning toward the sea, toward the bay below the cliffs, where the tide had begun to fall off the black rocks at the shrine's drowned mouth — and walking off the cliff edge, one after another, unhurried, like men stepping into a doorway.

The ward failed. Torv caught Freya before her knees did.

Below, the sea swallowed the hunters without a ripple. Above, Isolde swayed, put one hand against the cliff face, and sat down with great care, the way you sit when sitting is the only reasonable response to being alive.

"Well," she said. "That's inconvenient."

* * *

They got her down to the fisherman's bothy above the bay — Torv carrying her the last stretch with Freya breaking trail, the princess insisting the entire way that she could walk, in the tone of someone who absolutely could not. The wound was bad. Not killing-bad, the old woman who patched it said — a widow named Maren who asked no questions and charged no coin and smelled permanently of tar — but deep, and angry, and in a body that had lost blood it could not easily spare.

Isolde bore the stitching with her jaw set and her hand crushing Freya's, and afterward, feverish, she made Torv hold the ledger open while she dictated.

"Page forty-one. Seventh day from Haldmere. Wounded at Gullwrack. Two hunters destroyed — note: *destroyed*, not killed; write it properly, Askeland, this is a record, not a saga. Tide behavior at the shrine confirms the drowned approach opens at low water. Freya Almeida's gift responds to—" Her breath hitched. "Write that I said she sings to the fire when she thinks no one's listening, and it helps."

"I am not writing that."

"You are writing that. History needs to know you're fond of her, Torv. Someone has to say it out loud; you never will."

Torv's ears went red. Freya looked very hard at the fire, which was doing something interesting, probably.

"Also write," Isolde continued, softer now, her eyes drifting closed, "that my father sang badly, and I believe it, and no crown gets to take it."

Maren's tar-smoke drifted. Outside, the tide groaned against the rocks, going out and out and out, uncovering things that had waited three hundred years under the water.

Freya stepped to the door and looked down at the bay. Far out, where the retreating sea had peeled back from the headland, she could see it: black stones in a ring, older than any kingdom, slick and gleaming, with a darkness at the center that was not shadow.

It was warm. Even from here, even through the wind, she could feel it — the binding circle, still holding its prisoner after all this time, patient as a scar.

And rising up from it, threading through the crash of the waves, she heard singing. Faint, cracked, wordless. A girl's voice, worn down to almost nothing, singing to herself in the dark the way prisoners do, the way grieving kings do, the way anyone does who refuses to let the silence win.

Freya gripped the doorframe and listened, tears freezing on her lashes, and understood with total clarity that the tide would be fully out at dawn.

At dawn, they would walk down to the drowned shrine.

And behind her, from the pallet by the fire, Isolde's ledger scratched on — the princess dictating through her pain, recording everything, forgetting nothing — while far to the south, along the snow-choked roads, three thousand pairs of white eyes turned north, and began to march.
