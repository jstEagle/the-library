## 16. The Drowned Shrine

The tide gave them one hour.

Torv had worked it out from a fisherman's almanac he'd stolen—borrowed, he insisted—from a village two days back, and now he stood at the lip of the cove consulting it like scripture, holding the little book at arm's length because his eyes had gone soft around the edges from too much road and not enough sleep.

"One hour," he said. "Give or take."

"Give or take what?" Isolde asked. Her arm was bound tight against her chest where the hunter's blade had found her outside Haldmere's walls, and she'd refused to let anyone look at it properly since, which was its own kind of wound.

"The tide, princess. The sea doesn't negotiate."

"No," Freya said quietly. "But it remembers. That's rather worse."

They came down to the salt coast in the last grey hour before dawn, three figures limping along a shore that seemed to have been abandoned by everything except gulls—and even the gulls were strange here, wheeling in perfect silence, their cries swallowed by whatever hung over the water like a held breath.

Freya felt it before they reached the cove: a hum beneath her skin, the way she always felt magic, except this was old. Old the way bedrock is old. The wards of Vhalen had sung when they lived, a bright lattice-work song she'd only ever heard secondhand through birds and beasts who nested near the border stones. This was different. This was the note underneath all songs, the one the world was tuned to, and it was still ringing after three hundred years.

"It's warm," she said aloud, without meaning to.

"What is?" Torv asked.

"Whatever we're walking toward. It never went cold."

Isolde looked at her the way she often did lately—with the expression of a woman doing arithmetic she didn't enjoy. "You say things like that as if they're normal."

"I'm told I used to say normal things. I don't remember which ones."

That earned the ghost of a smile from Torv, which Freya counted as a small victory. He'd been carrying guilt the way other men carried coin-purses, heavy and close and constantly checked, ever since the wards fell on the night the stars went out. She'd stopped trying to convince him it wasn't his fault somewhere on the third day of travel and settled instead for making sure he ate.

* * *

The cove itself was a crescent of black stone, and at its center, revealed by the retreating tide like a secret told by the sea, lay the shrine.

It was smaller than Freya expected. She had imagined something vast—a cathedral sunk in the sand, pillars and vaulted ceilings, the grandeur of kings. Instead there was a circle of standing stones no taller than a man, half-buried, furred with weed and barnacle, arranged around a sunken floor of pale flagstones that gleamed wetly in the pre-dawn light. Carved into each stone were words in an alphabet older than any kingdom Freya knew, worn shallow by centuries of salt.

And the stones were warm.

She knelt and pressed her palm against the nearest one before Torv could stop her. Not sun-warm—the sky was still grey and cold, and the stones had been underwater an hour ago. Warm the way a sleeping animal is warm. Warm from the inside.

"Three hundred years," she murmured. "Someone has been holding a candle lit this whole time."

"Or something has been burning this whole time," Torv said. "There's a difference."

"There usually is." Freya stood, wiping salt from her hand onto her coat. "The difference is whether you run toward it or away from it, and I've noticed we keep running toward."

"That's not a strategy, Almeida. That's a personality flaw."

"We've established mine. Shall we discuss yours?"

Isolde, descending the slick steps into the shrine basin ahead of them, cut through the banter with the flat practicality she wore like armor. "If the tide returns in an hour, then whatever we're going to do, we do quickly. Father taught me that much about sieges—you never fight on ground the enemy can flood."

Freya's stomach tightened. "Your father isn't choosing this ground."

"No," Isolde said softly. "I know. But whatever wears him is. And it knows exactly how long we have."

* * *

The center of the shrine held a single flagstone different from the rest—not pale but dark, polished smooth as glass, and carved with a spiral that wound inward toward nothing. When Freya stepped within ten feet of it, the humming in her bones resolved into something almost like language.

Almost.

She had spent her whole life hearing animals—a gift hidden since childhood, smuggled through the purge years like contraband, useful only in whispers. Dogs complaining about thunder. Horses narrating their grievances with great dignity. Gulls, who were the gossips of the coast and had once told her an entire fishing fleet's worth of secrets in exchange for bread crusts. Animal speech was simple, direct, honest. It didn't do metaphor.

This was not animal speech. This was human speech, or had been once, folded so many times and pressed under so much weight that it came through flattened, compressed, a voice speaking through three centuries of stone.

*hello*

The word arrived not in her ears but in the hollow behind her sternum, and Freya sat down hard on a barnacled step.

"Freya?" Torv was beside her instantly, hand hovering uselessly near her shoulder. "What is it? What did you hear?"

"Not hear. That's the trouble. It's—" She breathed. "There's someone here. Someone *in* here. In the circle. She's been in the circle the whole time."

Isolde went very still. "The first speaker. The one the archive spoke of. The one the crown was forged from."

"Yes."

"And it speaks?" Torv's voice dropped, the ward-keeper in him surfacing—the old reflexes of a man who had spent his life cataloguing threats. "What does it want?"

"I don't know. It said hello." Freya heard how it sounded and grimaced. "I realize that's not reassuring. But in my experience, things that want to devour you generally lead with something more elaborate than hello."

"Perhaps it's being polite."

"Perhaps it's been alone for three hundred years," Freya said, "and has forgotten how to begin."

The words left her mouth and she understood them all at once, fully, the way you understand ice is slippery only after falling. Something in the circle stirred—a pressure change, a shift in the warmth, like a sleeper turning over.

*alone,* the voice agreed, and the word was a wound. *yes.*

* * *

She should have been afraid. Some part of her noted, distantly, that a responsible person would be afraid. This was the thing that had eaten the stars. This was the Hollow Choir, the memory-eater, the long winter wearing a crown. It had unmade villages. It had taken King Aldric's wife's face out of his own head. Somewhere north, snow was falling that whispered people into forgetting their children's names mid-lullaby.

But kneeling on the cold stone with her palm flat against the spiral, Freya did not feel the terror she had braced for.

She felt someone small.

*they called me sefa,* the voice said. And then, with the terrible simplicity of a child stating a fact adults spend lifetimes avoiding: *but that was before. i don't remember if it's my name anymore. names are the first thing to go. i've been so hungry for so long.*

"Oh," Freya whispered. "Oh, you poor creature."

"Freya." Torv crouched beside her, his face drawn. "Talk to me. What's happening?"

"She's telling me her name." Freya looked up at him, and her eyes stung with salt that wasn't from the sea. "Her real name. Or what's left of it. Torv—the Choir, the thing in the crown—it started as a girl. A girl who could speak, like me. They bound her into this circle because kings were afraid of her voice, and she's been down here ever since, starving, and everything she's eaten, every memory, every name—it wasn't cruelty. It was hunger. She's been hungry for three hundred years and nobody came."

The silence that followed had weight. Even the silent gulls above the cove seemed to lean in.

Isolde lowered herself carefully onto a stone, cradling her bound arm. "The archive said the wards weren't protection. They were a cage lid. My family built the cage." She said it without drama, the way she recited entries from her ledger—dates, faces, laws—but Freya had learned to hear what lived underneath the princess's precision. Grief, converted into method. "We owe her more than pity."

"Pity's a start," Freya said. "Pity keeps you kneeling instead of running."

*i can hear you talking about me,* Sefa said, and there was something in the flattened voice that might, in another life, another century, have been wry humor. *it's rude to discuss a person in front of her. even a person made mostly of winter.*

Freya laughed—a startled, wet-edged sound. "I'm sorry. You're right. It's rude."

*you laugh like my sister did.* A pause, long enough that Freya thought the contact might have broken. *i think i had a sister. i have a shape where a sister was. the kings took the rest.*

"They took your memories," Freya said gently. "When they bound you."

*they took them slowly. that was the cruelty of it. not all at once—that would have been a mercy. they took them as payment for the binding. a memory for every year of my imprisonment, they said. a fair trade, they said. the kingdom gets my voice; i get to keep my Sundays. and then they took my Sundays. and then they took my mother's face. and then—* the voice frayed, static and sea-sound, *—and then i was so hungry, and there was so much memory just lying around the kingdom, loose, unguarded, in ten thousand sworn heads, and oath is a door, oath has always been a door—*

"You fed yourself," Freya finished quietly. "After three hundred years of starving."

*i did. and i would tell you i regret it, but i don't remember most of what i took, so i can't even regret it properly. that's what i am now. a hunger that has forgotten its own meals.* The warmth in the stones flickered, guttering. *you came here to kill me. i can feel your intent the way you feel weather. i don't blame you. but before you do—there's a thing i need to ask. and it will cost you. i want you to know the cost before you answer. i was never told the cost of anything. i want to be better than the men who built my cage.*

Freya's mouth had gone dry. "Tell me."

*my name. sefa. if a speaker carries my true name—speaks it, holds it, makes it hers again—then i can be unbound. the circle lets go. the winter ends. the crown cracks.* A tremor ran through the whole basin; loose pebbles chattered against the flagstones. *but a name must be carried by equal weight. that is the oldest law, older than your kings, older than the cage. you cannot hold my name without giving up a piece of your own past to make room. equal measure. the law does not bargain.*

"Equal measure," Freya repeated. Her heart was beating strangely, slow and loud. "How much is a girl's whole life weighed against?"

*a life,* Sefa said simply. *some part of yours, as large as mine was. i cannot choose which memories go. neither can you. the law chooses, and the law has never once been gentle.*

Torv had gone pale. Freya realized distantly that he understood before she did—or understood differently, in the direction she couldn't yet bear to look. "Freya. Don't. Whatever it takes, there has to be another way. We'll find another way. We'll—"

"Torv." She looked at him—at the drunk-soft eyes gone sober, the guilt-carrier, the disgraced ward-keeper who had followed her across a silent country because hiding had stopped being possible for both of them at the same moment. Something in her chest clenched around a thought she hadn't yet let herself finish, and the law, patient as tide, waited beyond it like deep water.

Before anyone could speak again, the warmth in the stones died.

Not faded—died, all at once, snuffed like a pinched candle, and the silence that rushed in was the same silence Freya had heard on the road north, the chewing silence, the sound of something vast attending closely.

Above the cove, the gulls rose as one body and fled inland.

Isolde stood, her good hand going to the knife at her belt. "What is it? What happened?"

Freya got to her feet, her whole skin prickling. Far off across the water, at the head of the coastal road, she saw them: a line of figures moving down toward the shore through the pre-dawn grey, moving with the wrongness of puppets, and even at this distance she could see the moon-pale shine of their eyes.

"Hunters," she said. "Oath-sworn. They've found us." She turned back to the dead-warm circle, to the presence huddled inside it like a child listening at a door. "Sefa. How long does the unbinding take?"

*one hour,* said the girl who had been starving for three hundred years. *the tide will give you exactly that.*

* * *

Behind them, the first white eyes reached the rim of the cove.
