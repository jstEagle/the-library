## 17. Sefa's Name

The tide was going out, and the shrine was coming up for air.

Freya felt it before she saw it — a long, slow exhalation through the stone beneath her boots, as if the whole drowned temple had been holding its breath for three hundred years and had finally decided to let it go. The water slid back from the black rocks in sheets, uncovering arches, stairways, a doorway crowned with carvings of gulls whose eyes had been chiseled out by someone who feared being watched.

"Charming," Torv said behind her. "Someone went to a lot of trouble to make this place blind."

"Someone went to a lot of trouble to make it deaf, too." Freya pressed her palm against the wet stone of the archway. Even through her gloves she could feel it: warmth. Faint, stubborn, wrong — like a hearthstone in an abandoned house. "It's still singing. Under everything. It never stopped."

Isolde leaned against the rocks, one arm bound tight across her ribs where the hunter's blade had caught her on the cliffs above Haldmere. She had refused to stay behind with a face that dared anyone to suggest it. Her color was bad and her voice was steady, which Freya was learning was simply how Isolde did things.

"The archive said the binding circle is below," Isolde said. "The records used the word *warm*. I assumed that was poetry."

"The archive is rarely poetic. That's how you know when it's frightened."

They went down into the shrine together, three small figures in a throat of ancient stone, while behind them the sea kept withdrawing, and withdrawing, farther than any honest tide should go.

* * *

Three hundred years earlier, a girl sat on a dock in a fishing village that no longer existed, and taught a crab to count.

"Not four," Sefa told it sternly. The crab waved its claw at her, unrepentant. "You have six legs. Six. We have been over this."

The crab clicked its claw twice, which in crab meant *I am thinking about it*, and Sefa laughed so hard she fell off the dock into the cold green water, and the fish gathered around her in the shallows to share the joke, because fish loved jokes about crabs, they were a simple people with simple pleasures.

Her mother found her dripping on the sand later and didn't scold her. Her mother never scolded her for the speaking. That came later, from other mouths, from men in gray robes who arrived with ledgers and questions and a king's seal, and asked, very politely, whether it was true that the girl could put words into animals' mouths.

"It isn't putting words in," Sefa had said, twelve years old and honest. "The words are already there. I just listen properly."

She would remember, much later, in the dark under the mountain, that this was the last true thing she ever said freely.

* * *

Inside the shrine, the air tasted of salt and old iron. Freya's gift rose in her like water finding its level, and the silence that had pursued them down the coast — the chewing, patient silence — fell away all at once, and what was underneath it began to speak.

Not the Choir. Not yet. Something older and smaller, folded up inside the great devouring thing like a seed inside a fruit.

"*Hello,*" it said. "*Hello. Hello.*"

Torv heard nothing — she could tell by his face, by the way he watched hers instead of the walls. But Isolde had gone pale. "I heard that," the princess whispered. "In my mother's voice. My mother has been dead nine years and I just heard her say hello."

"It borrows voices it finds in you." Freya kept walking. "Don't answer it. Answering is how it learns your doors."

The stairway spiraled down past niches carved into the rock, and in each niche lay offerings gone to dust: shells, beads, a child's wooden bird worn smooth by centuries of hands. People had come here once, before the outlawry, before the wards. People had come to *listen*.

At the bottom, the passage opened into a round chamber, and Freya stopped so suddenly that Torv walked into her back.

The binding circle covered the entire floor. Three hundred years submerged twice a day by the sea, and still it glowed — faintly, stubbornly, the color of embers under ash. And in its center, coiled like a knot the size of a wagon, was the Choir.

Or rather, the part of the Choir that had been here all along. The rest of it wore a crown in Haldmere and drank a kingdom through ten thousand loyal straws. But this was the root. This was the wound. And wrapped around the root, holding it, woven through every line of the circle like thread through cloth, was a name.

Freya didn't know how she knew that. She simply knew it, the way she knew a dog was in pain or a horse was lying. The name was there, in the geometry, in the warmth, and it was screaming without sound.

"Oh," she said softly. "Oh, you poor thing."

"Freya." Torv's hand closed on her shoulder. His voice had the careful flatness he used when he was frightened and refusing to admit it. "What do you see?"

"A girl," Freya said. "I see a girl."

* * *

They brought her to the mountain when she was fourteen, and it was not called a prison. It was called a *warding*, and the men in gray explained that her gift was a danger to the realm unless it was bound into service, and that service was an honor, and that honor was forever.

"They keep saying forever," Sefa wrote to her mother, in letters that were never delivered. "I don't think they know what the word costs."

The work was simple at first. She stood in circles and spoke to the land, and the land spoke back, and the ward-stones drank her words and grew strong, and the borders held against raiders and beasts and the slow malice of winter. The king sent gifts. The priests wrote hymns. A young captain named Aldric's grandfather's grandfather — the ledger-keepers were vague — once stood guard outside her door all night because she'd cried, and pretended in the morning that he hadn't.

But binding is a hungry craft. Every year the circles wanted more of her. Every year the stones drank deeper. And the men in gray, consulting their ledgers, discovered something wonderful and terrible: that a speaker's memory was the richest fuel of all. That if you fed the wards a memory instead of a word, the wards sang for a decade.

"We'll start with small things," the chief warden promised her gently, the way you promise a child that the physician's knife will only hurt a little. "Your seventh birthday. The taste of your grandmother's bread. Nothing that matters."

Nothing that matters. They took the smell of rain first, because she loved it most, and love made memories burn brighter. Then her father's laugh. Then the name of her village. Then the faces, one by one, of everyone she had ever spoken to or for, until the animals in the courtyard looked at her with kind, strange eyes, and she realized she no longer knew their names either, only that she had known them, once, and that the knowing had been taken and burned to hold back winter.

That was the joke of it. That was the filthy, eternal joke. The wards held back the long dark, and the fuel was every bright thing she'd ever carried, and the kingdom slept warm at night because a girl in a mountain was forgetting her mother on purpose, forever, in her place.

The last thing they took was her name. Names, the wardens had discovered, were the deepest well of all. A nameless speaker could be bound indefinitely; a nameless speaker couldn't even be mourned properly, because mourning requires a name to carve.

Sefa. Her name had been Sefa. She knew this the way you know a scar — by its shape, not its story.

And what was left of her, alone in the dark with nothing left to burn, did the only thing left to do.

It listened.

* * *

"There's a way to unbind her." Freya's voice cracked on the second word, and she had to start again. They had been in the chamber for an hour, Torv pacing the circle's edge with a ward-keeper's old instincts reading the geometry, Isolde sitting propped against the wall with her wounded side and her notebook open, because Isolde recorded everything, it was her weapon and her prayer.

"You're certain?" Torv asked.

"I'm certain the circle wants to let her go. It's been trying for three hundred years. Look—" Freya knelt at the rim, where the ember-light thinned to almost nothing. "These lines aren't a cage. They're a tourniquet. Whoever built this knew the binding was monstrous. They built it to fail, slowly, safely. It just needed someone to finish the sentence."

"And the sentence is?"

Freya looked at the coiled darkness in the center, and the darkness — the small, buried, original heart of it — looked back, and said hello hello hello in a dead woman's voice, in a stolen child's voice, in voices Freya was beginning to recognize as pieces of herself.

"The name," she said. "Her true name is wound through the whole binding. You can't cut it out; it *is* the binding. You have to give it back. Speak it home. Return it to her."

"Then speak it," Isolde said. "We came all this way—"

"I can't just *say* it." Freya pressed her fists against her knees. "A name isn't a word, Isolde. It's a whole person compressed into a single sound. To carry it — truly carry it, hold it in yourself long enough to give it away — a speaker has to trade equal weight. Name for name. Memory for memory. That's the law underneath all the laws; even the wardens obeyed it, in their rotten way. Nothing given for free. Nothing returned without payment."

The chamber was quiet except for the sea, far above, moving strangely.

"Equal weight," Torv repeated. He had gone very still. He was a careful man; Freya had watched him ration his words like a miser rationing coin for two thousand miles. "Meaning what, exactly? In plain speech. I've had enough of prophecy-speak to last me."

"In plain speech." Freya made herself look at him. Made herself say it aloud, because the price should be named aloud, in front of witnesses, so it couldn't slink away later and pretend it hadn't been agreed to. "To carry Sefa's name, I have to fill myself with something of equal mass. Memories. Real ones. Whole ones. And when I give her name back, the payment goes with it. Permanently. I won't lose everything. I'll lose—" she swallowed— "enough. Whatever weighs the same as a person's entire self."

Silence.

"How much is that?" Isolde asked quietly. She wasn't being cruel. She was being a ledger-keeper. Someone had to ask.

"I don't know yet. The circle will weigh me. It might take my childhood. It might take my mother — what's left of her." Freya's voice steadied itself with an effort she could feel in her teeth. "It might take Torv."

"Don't," Torv said.

"It might. I'd rather say it now than discover it mid-sentence."

"No." He crouched in front of her, and his eyes were fierce and red-rimmed and absolutely furious, and Freya thought, absurdly, that anger had never looked so much like tenderness. "You don't get to spend yourself like loose change. There has to be another way. There's always another way; I spent twenty years keeping wards alive on scraps and prayer, I *know* there's always another way—"

"Torv."

"—and if you think I'm going to stand here and watch you trade away—" He stopped. His jaw worked. "Whatever this is between us. However you'd weigh it. It isn't yours alone to pay."

"I know." Freya reached out and took his rough, scarred hand in both of hers. "That's why I'm telling you now. Both of you. Because whatever I forget — I want you to remember it *for* me. That's the other half of the rite. Someone has to witness. Someone has to keep the account." She turned to Isolde. "You're good at accounts."

Isolde closed her notebook. Opened it again. Tore out a blank page, and licked her pencil like a schoolgirl, and said, "Dictate."

* * *

*My name is Freya Almeida,* she said, while the tide crept back in above them and the hunters' white eyes gathered somewhere in the dark of the coast road. *I was born in Dunmark, in a room that smelled of tar and bread. My mother sold charms and lied about it cheerfully. I learned to speak from a gull with a torn wing who forgave me for the bandage. Torv Askeland drinks his tea bitter enough to strip paint and pretends it's a virtue. Isolde keeps a ledger because grief with nowhere to go becomes method. The first time Torv smiled at me, I dropped a jar of salve. He picked it up. Neither of us mentioned it. We never have.*

*If I forget these things, they happened anyway. Write them down. Read them to me. Make me learn them again, even if learning them again hurts, even if I flinch, even if I don't believe you.*

*Especially if I don't believe you.*

* * *

Three hundred years earlier, in the dark under the mountain, the thing that had been a girl felt the tide turn.

It had eaten so much. It had grown so vast and cold and clever, seeping out through the cracks of its own prison, learning to wear crowns, learning that loyalty was a straw and kingdoms were full of willing mouths. It had forgotten almost everything, on purpose, because remembering hurt, and there was no one left to make it stop hurting.

But tonight — tonight something was coming down the stairs. Something warm. Something that smelled of salt and bread and spoke to the dark without fear.

And deep in the frozen core of it, in the last cell that had never burned, a shape stirred that was not hunger.

A shape that was waiting to be called by its name.

Above them, far above, the first white-eyed hunters reached the shore, and the sea, obedient to older oaths, began to rise ahead of schedule — and on the coast road, dust rising behind outriders, came a palanquin of black iron, and inside it, wearing a king like armor, the Choir came south.
