## 18. The Crown Comes South

Aldric Vhalen had once believed that a king's worst day would be the day he buried someone he loved. He had been wrong about many things in his fifty-one years, but he had been wrong about that one most thoroughly.

His worst day was this one: waking on a horse he did not remember mounting, three hundred miles south of his own capital, with snow in his beard and no memory of leaving Haldmere.

He knew it was three hundred miles because a small, cold voice at the base of his skull told him so — told him everything, always, in that patient chorus of voices that was not one voice but many, layered like sediment, like the rings of a felled tree. *South,* the voices said. *The shrine. The girl who hears. We are going to see her, Aldric. You are going to carry us there.*

"Carry," he said aloud. His lips were cracked. The word came out in pieces.

*You wanted to be useful,* said the voice of his old tutor, Maester Corwin, dead eleven years and still somehow in his head, still correcting his grammar. *You are being useful.*

That was the cruelty of it, Aldric had learned. The Choir never lied to him. It simply rearranged the truth until lying became unnecessary. He *had* wanted to be useful. He had been a decent man trying to govern a hard country, and when the crown whispered its first offer — *give us your griefs, Aldric, and we will give you peace* — peace had sounded like the most kingly gift imaginable. A quiet mind. A steady hand. No more nights staring at the ceiling where Maren's face used to be.

He had not understood that peace and emptiness wore the same coat.

* * *

The column moved south through falling snow, and Aldric rode at its center like a jewel in a setting. Two hundred soldiers flanked him, and every one of them had eyes gone white as boiled eggs. They did not speak. They did not eat, though he watched one man ride a full day with his mouth open, catching snowflakes, as though the snow itself were nourishment. Perhaps it was. It certainly whispered.

Aldric had tried, in the first hundred miles, to signal for help. At a waystation near Greyford he had scrawled *HELP* in the frost of a window with his fingernail before the Choir smoothed his arm down like a parent calming a child mid-tantrum. At a river crossing he had screamed — actually screamed, a full-throated royal bellow that would have carried across the water — but the sound had come out as a low, contented hum, and the white-eyed soldiers had hummed along, and for one horrible moment they had been a choir indeed, forty men harmonizing on nothing while the ferryman stared.

After that he stopped trying. Not from despair. From strategy. That was the other thing the Choir had taught him, without meaning to: how much of a man survives even when nearly everything is taken. What remained of Aldric Vhalen was not much. But it was stubborn, and it could count, and it could wait.

He counted the days by the bells he remembered. Morning bell, noon bell, vespers — he rang them silently in his head, an invisible sexton keeping time inside a stolen church. On the ninth day of riding, somewhere past the salt marshes, he felt something he had not felt in two years.

Quiet.

Not the Choir's quiet, which was the quiet of a room after all the furniture has been removed. This was different. For perhaps ten minutes — he counted, because counting was what he had — the chorus at the base of his skull went silent, and Aldric surfaced into his own body like a drowning man breaking water.

He gasped. Actually gasped, out loud, and nobody stopped him, because the soldiers rode on with their egg-white eyes fixed southward and the snow kept falling and for ten glorious minutes Aldric Vhalen was alone in his own skull for the first time since midwinter began.

He did what any sensible man would do with ten minutes of freedom.

He wept, briefly. Then he took inventory.

Hands: present, chapped, gripping reins. Teeth: all accounted for, which surprised him. Mind: full of holes. He probed them gently, the way one probes a sore tooth. His mother's face — gone; he knew he'd had a mother the way one knows a country exists on a map one has never visited. The name of his capital's chief cook — gone, though he could taste her honeycakes. Maren.

Maren.

He reached for her the way he reached for her every night, and found — not nothing. Worse than nothing. A shape where she should be. He knew he had loved a woman named Maren; the name survived like a stone surviving a flood. But her face was a smear, her voice a rumor, and the only thing left whole, the single intact artifact of twenty-two years of marriage, was this: she had laughed at his jokes even when they were bad, and she had smelled of rosemary, and she had once told him — he clung to this, he polished it daily like a relic — *"Aldric, you worry like a man bailing a boat with a spoon. Sometimes you must simply trust the hull."*

Trust the hull.

The quiet ended. The chorus returned like tide over footprints, and Aldric settled back behind his own eyes and let it wear him, and inside, in the small stubborn room that was still his, he began to plan.

* * *

They made camp on the twelfth night within sight of the sea. Aldric knew it was the sea because he could smell it — salt and weed and cold iron — and because the whispering snow grew thicker here, almost eager, swirling around the camp in patterns that looked deliberate. The Choir was excited. He could feel its excitement the way one feels a dog straining at a leash.

*Tomorrow,* the voices murmured, overlapping, hundreds of them, thousands. *Tomorrow we will be whole again. Tomorrow we will meet the girl who hears, and we will show her what we have become, and she will understand, and then—*

"Then what?" Aldric asked aloud, into the fire. It was a risk. Talking to them fed them attention, and attention was food. But he was tired, and lonely, and a man can only keep his own counsel for so long before his own counsel starts answering back.

*Then winter ends,* said a voice he did not recognize — young, female, cracked at the edges like old paint. *Then we are finished, and everyone rests.*

"You've been saying that for three hundred years," Aldric said. "According to your own archive. Which my daughter found. She's clever, my Isolde. Cleverer than me."

The silence that followed had texture. He had surprised it. There was very little satisfaction in surprising a parasite, but Aldric took what victories came.

*The princess,* the young voice said at last, and something in it — hunger? fear? both braided together? — made the snow swirl faster. *She carries the ledger.*

"She does."

*Names. Dates. Faces. Everything we have taken, written down.* A pause. *Writing is a kind of remembering we cannot reach. We have tried.*

"I know," Aldric said. "I've read her ledger. Well — I read the first page. Before." He fed another branch to the fire, or rather his hands did, while he watched from inside. "You should know something about my daughter, since you're going to meet her. She keeps accounts. All of them. And she has never once, in nineteen years, forgiven a debt."

The chorus said nothing more that night. But the snow fell harder, and the white-eyed soldiers stood their watches facing the sea, and Aldric sat by the fire planning, because tomorrow he might get ten minutes again, and if he got ten minutes, and if his daughter was anywhere near that drowned shrine — and she would be; Isolde had never in her life let a mystery go uninvestigated, a trait she had inherited from Maren, whom he could no longer picture but whose stubbornness lived on in the girl like a lamp in a window — then he intended to spend those ten minutes well.

* * *

The shrine revealed itself at low tide, and Aldric saw it first through his own eyes rather than the Choir's, which startled him. The entity had grown careless in its eagerness, or perhaps it wanted him to see — wanted him to witness, to approve, some final obscenity of consent. The stones rose from the retreating water black and ancient, ringed in carvings worn smooth, and the air above them shimmered faintly, like heat in reverse.

And there, on the shingle beach, small against the vast grey morning: three figures. A broad man with a ward-keeper's broken staff. A dark-haired girl cradling her bandaged side. And Isolde.

Isolde, with her ledger strapped across her chest like armor, standing between her father and the sea with her chin up, exactly the way her mother used to stand when the tax collectors came and Maren informed them, politely and devastatingly, that they had made an arithmetic error.

*Go,* the Choir commanded the soldiers, and the column surged forward down the shingle, and Aldric felt himself urged forward too, the crown hot on his brow, and he thought: *ten minutes. Give me ten minutes, you thieving chorus. You owe me far more than that.*

Whether it was carelessness, or contempt, or some flicker of the girl Sefa still buried in that ancient tangle of stolen memory — he would never know. But as the soldiers closed the distance, as the dark-haired girl raised her hands and the air began to sing, the chorus faltered. Stuttered. Went, for one impossible moment, silent.

Ten minutes. He counted them as they started.

Aldric slid from his horse. His legs nearly failed him — three hundred miles of riding a body he hadn't been allowed to feel — but he caught himself on the animal's flank and walked, stumbled, half-fell down the shingle toward his daughter, and the look on her face when she saw his eyes clear was a thing he would carry out of this world and into whatever came after.

"Papa?" Her voice cracked on the word. Nineteen years old, keeper of ledgers, archivist of a kingdom's forgetting, and she sounded six years old calling across a market square.

"Isolde." He reached her and gripped her shoulders, and her arms came around him, and for a moment father and daughter simply held on, in the wind, in the snow, with battle gathering around them like weather. "Listen to me. We haven't long. When it comes back — and it will come back — you must not hesitate. Do you hear me? Whatever needs doing. Whatever it costs."

"Papa, no—"

"Isolde." He took her face in his hands. Her cheeks were cold and wet and alive, and he memorized them, deliberately, furiously, carving each detail deep enough that maybe, maybe, whatever the Choir had already taken of him, this would survive in the stone beneath. "I have forgotten your mother's face. I have forgotten my own coronation. I have forgotten the taste of bread, Isolde — bread. But I remember that you were born on a Tuesday in a thunderstorm, and the midwife dropped her instruments twice from the lightning, and you screamed at her, actually screamed, as though the noise were a personal affront." He laughed; it came out ragged. "You have been affronted by imperfection ever since. It is your finest quality."

"Papa, please—"

"The ledger." He tapped the book at her chest. "Keep writing. Whatever happens here today, however it ends — write it down. Names, dates, faces. Everything. Because forgetting is only theft if no one writes down what was stolen, and you—" his voice broke, and he let it, because there was no dignity left worth protecting, "—you are the best thief-catcher this kingdom has ever produced."

Behind them, the dark-haired girl — Almeida, the beast-speaker, the one the gulls had named — cried out something in a language Aldric didn't know, and the air above the shrine began to glow, and the white-eyed soldiers drew steel with a sound like ice cracking.

"Isolde." He gripped her hands one last time. "When I go under again — and I will go under — know that I am in here. However small. However buried. I am in here, watching, and I am proud of you, and I trust the hull."

"What hull?"

"Your mother would have explained it better." He smiled at her, and it cost him everything he had left, and it was worth it. "Now do whatever is needed. That is a royal command. The last one I will ever give, so make it count."

The chorus came back like a wave closing over a rock.

Aldric felt it take him — felt his knees straighten, his face empty, his eyes film white — and the last thing he saw from inside was his daughter's face, fierce and tear-streaked and utterly unafraid, turning toward the glowing shrine with her ledger open and her pen already moving, writing him down, writing all of it down, so that no power in heaven or under the sea could ever say it had not happened.

*Hold the line, little storm,* he thought, from the small stubborn room at the bottom of the drowning dark. *Papa's coming too.*
