## 19. Unbinding

The tide had turned against them, and the sea knew it.

Freya stood in the drowned shrine with water to her knees and the binding circle glowing beneath her like a hearthstone that had never gone cold. Three hundred years, Torv had said, and the salt-etched stones still held their warmth as if the girl bound inside them had been pressed there only yesterday — which, in the way of such things, she had been.

Outside, the hunters were coming down the cliff path.

She could hear them the way she heard everything now: not with her ears but with that other sense, the one her mother's ghost of a memory had taught her to stop flinching from. The hunters' minds were quiet in the way a swept floor is quiet. No fear. No doubt. Just the long white patience of things that had given away every part of themselves worth keeping.

"Freya." Isolde's voice came from the shrine entrance, tight with pain. The princess was propped against the doorpost, one hand pressed to the bandage on her side where a hunter's blade had found her on the cliffs. She had refused to stay behind at the inn, refused twice, and then refused a third time with such royal finality that Torv had simply stopped arguing and started carrying extra bandages. "How long?"

"Minutes." Freya looked down at the circle. At its center, faint as breath on glass, a shape was forming — a girl made of tide-light and old grief, no taller than Freya herself, watching her with eyes the color of deep water over sand.

Sefa.

*They are coming,* Sefa said, and her voice arrived not through Freya's ears but directly into the place where Freya kept her own voice, which was a strange sensation, like someone reading over your shoulder from inside your skull. *You should run. I have watched three hundred years of people who should have run.*

"I'm done running," Freya said. "I've been done since Dunmark."

*That is what they all say before—*

"Sefa." Freya crouched, bringing her eyes level with the fading shape. Water sloshed against her shins, cold as honesty. "Tell me again. All of it. I need to hear it one more time before I do this."

The girl's form flickered. For three hundred years she had been the Hollow Choir — a thousand stolen voices braided into one hunger, wearing kings like coats, eating a kingdom's past one name at a time. But here, in the circle where kings had first bound her, the braid came loose. Here she was only Sefa: a speaker, a girl whose gift had been feared by men who wore crowns they hadn't earned, betrayed by an oath of protection that had been a leash all along.

*My name,* Sefa said. *You know what it costs.*

"I know."

*A memory of equal weight. The Choir will take its payment through you, and it will not be gentle about choosing. It will reach for the thing you hold warmest.* The girl's tide-light hands curled into fists. *I have eaten ten thousand memories, Freya Almeida. I know how people treasure them. I know which ones shine brightest. It will go for the brightest.*

Freya thought of Torv — of his crooked smile and his terrible jokes and the way he'd stood beside her in the square at Haldmere while she broke a kingdom's oaths, knowing it would brand them both traitor, choosing it anyway. *A new kind of oath,* he'd said. *Freely given, easily broken, and I'll keep renewing it daily if you'll let me.*

Her chest tightened around the thought like a fist around something small and alive.

"Then we'd best be quick," she said, "before I lose my nerve."

* * *

Torv Askeland had fought in two skirmishes, one tavern brawl, and a memorable dispute over a fishing net, and none of it had prepared him for defending a drowned shrine against men whose eyes had gone white as unmarked paper.

He'd wedged himself into the narrow entrance with Isolde behind him and a ward-stone shard — the last of the drowned ward-stones from Dunmark harbor — gripped in both hands like a very blunt sword. The first hunter came down the slick steps at a dead run, and Torv did the only sensible thing: he stepped aside and let momentum do the work. The man went past him into the shallows with a tremendous splash.

"That," Torv announced to no one, "is why they pay me nothing."

"Torv." Isolde's voice, strained. "There are more."

There were always more. They came down the steps in ones and twos and threes, silent, tireless, faces smooth as eggs. Torv swung the ward-stone shard and felt it hum against the first man's chest — the old magic waking up after twelve years of outlawry, remembering what it was for. The hunter staggered, and for just a moment his white eyes flickered, and underneath the blankness Torv saw something human and horrified, a man trapped in his own loyalty, screaming behind glass.

"I'm sorry," Torv told him, and meant it, and hit him again.

From deeper in the shrine, he could hear Freya's voice rising — not loud, but *deep*, the way a bell is deep, the sound arriving through his bones rather than his ears. Words in no language he knew, and yet he understood every one, because they weren't words at all. They were names.

* * *

Inside the circle, Freya opened her hands.

*Speak it,* Sefa whispered. *And when it leaves me, do not let go. A name wants to fly home. You must hold it until the unbinding is finished, or everything I am goes back to being a cage.*

"What happens to you? After?"

The girl smiled, and it was the saddest and most beautiful thing Freya had ever seen. *I stop being a chorus. I become a girl. And then, shortly after, I become a story, and then, if the world is kind, I become rest.*

"And me?"

*You become lighter,* Sefa said. *And poorer. That is the trade. It has always been the trade. Kings never understood that — they wanted to take without paying, and so they made me.* Her flickering form steadied, gathered itself. *Are you ready?*

"No," Freya said honestly. "But my mother taught me that ready is a luxury. Go."

Sefa spoke her own name.

It was not a word. It was a *sounding* — the crash of a wave and the cry of a gull and a child laughing on a salt-flat all folded into one syllable that had been folded apart for three hundred years. It poured out of the binding circle like light out of a cracked jar, and Freya caught it the way you catch anything precious: clumsily, desperately, with her whole self.

And the Choir noticed.

It came up through the water and the stone and her own blood, vast and patient and *starving*, and it did exactly what Sefa had warned. It reached past her mother's face — her mother, whom no one else remembered, whose grave existed only in Freya's head — it reached past the market at Dunmark and the dying gull and the first time she'd calmed a frightened animal and known she could never tell anyone —

It reached for Torv.

Not a grand memory. Not the oath-breaking at Haldmere or the flight to the coast. Something small. Terribly, specifically small: the morning outside Haldmere's walls when he'd bought her tea from a stall that sold it too weak, and complained about it at length, and then quietly given her his own cup because hers had gotten cold, and she'd laughed, and he'd looked at her with an expression she'd spent weeks pretending not to understand.

The Choir took it the way winter takes a candle — no violence, just absence spreading.

"No," Freya gasped. "No — give it back — *give it back*—"

*Hold the name,* Sefa said, urgent now, her form blazing. *Hold it! Grief later. Unbinding now. HOLD IT.*

And Freya, weeping, holding a dead girl's name in one hand and reaching with the other for a memory already dissolving like sugar in rain, spoke the unbinding.

* * *

Torv felt it happen from the doorway.

The sound — if it was a sound — began under the water and rose through the shrine like a bubble the size of the sky. Every stolen voice in the Choir came apart at once. He heard them: farmers and soldiers and queens and children, thousands of names and faces and half-forgotten songs, all of them suddenly *themselves* instead of fuel, streaming upward through the water like fish released from a net. The white-eyed hunters in the shallows dropped their weapons and collapsed to their knees, weeping, clutching their heads, whole again and drowning in everything they'd done.

Above the cliffs, the sky — which had been the color of dirty wool for days — split open along a seam no one else could see, and the long winter shuddered.

In the center of the shrine, the crown came south.

King Aldric walked into the water alone. Whatever had marched him down the coast had used the last of itself getting him here; now the Choir, unraveling, had nothing left to wear him with. He moved like a puppet with most of its strings cut, and as Freya's unbinding reached him, the crown on his head — iron and salt-dark silver, older than any record — *cracked*.

The sound of it was small. A teacup breaking. That was somehow the worst part.

Aldric fell forward into the shallows, and Torv was closest, and so it was Torv who caught the King of Vhalen by the shoulders and dragged him clear of the water, and it was Torv who saw the king's eyes come back — clouded, confused, diminished, a man waking from a fever that had taken years instead of weeks — and it was Torv who heard him say, in a voice like dry leaves:

"Isolde? Where is — I was going to — there was something I was supposed to remember—"

"I'm here, Father." Isolde knelt in the shallows, pale, one hand still pressed to her wound, and took his hand. "I kept the list. Everything you forgot. We can start putting it back."

Aldric looked at her for a long moment. Then he began, very quietly, to weep — not like a king, just like a tired man in cold water who had lost years of his life to a thing he couldn't even name anymore.

* * *

Freya came out of the shrine slowly, wading, one hand trailing along the wall.

She felt *lighter*. Sefa had promised that, and Sefa had kept her word. The grief of ten thousand voices was gone from her shoulders; the Choir's long hunger no longer pressed against the inside of her skull. The stars were coming back overhead — she could see them appearing one by one through the torn clouds, tentative, like guests checking whether the party was really happening.

She also felt poorer.

She stood in the shallows and searched for the memory of tea outside Haldmere's walls, and found a hole shaped exactly like it. She knew there had been something there. She knew its outline the way you know the outline of a missing tooth. Around the hole were other memories, intact: the oath-breaking, the flight, the night Torv had fallen asleep sitting up in the archive and she'd covered him with her cloak—

Wait.

Had that happened? Yes. Yes, she was sure. That one was real. That one was—

That one was fine.

She frowned. There was a wrongness, a small cold draft blowing through a room she'd thought was sealed. Somewhere in the house of her memory, a window stood open onto snow.

"Freya!" Torv came splashing toward her, Aldric left in Isolde's care, his face lit with a joy so naked it hurt to look at. "The crown — it cracked — the hunters are themselves again, and the *stars*, Freya, look at the *stars*—" He caught her hands. "We did it. We actually — you did it. You impossible, reckless, magnificent woman, you *did* it."

She looked at him.

He was soaked to the waist, mud to the elbows, grinning like a boy, and he was — she knew this the way she knew her own name — he was important to her. Vital. The knowledge sat in her chest, solid and load-bearing.

But when she reached for *why* — for the tea, for the laugh, for whatever small warm moment had once lived between those facts — her hand closed on snow.

"Freya?" His grin faltered. "What's wrong?"

"Nothing," she said, and then, because lying to him had apparently gotten harder rather than easier: "I don't know. Something's — there's a gap. Something's missing and I can't find the edges of it."

His face went carefully, terribly still. "What kind of something?"

"I don't know. That's the problem." She laughed, and it came out wrong. "Isn't that absurd? We just saved the kingdom and I'm standing here missing a thing I can't even name—"

"Freya." His voice was very gentle. "What do you remember about the road north? About the tea stall outside Haldmere?"

"The tea stall," she repeated. The words tasted like someone else's. "Was there... was there a tea stall?"

Something in Torv Askeland's face broke, quietly, without any sound at all — the way crowns break, the way hearts break, small and complete. But his hands didn't move from hers. If anything, they tightened.

"Yes," he said. "There was a tea stall. The tea was terrible. I'll tell you about it sometime." He swallowed. "Or — well. Perhaps I'll tell you about it many times."

Behind him, the stars kept returning, filling the sky over the salt coast like a debt finally being repaid. Sefa's last echo drifted across the water, so faint only a speaker could have caught it:

*Lighter. And poorer. I am sorry. I am so sorry.*

Freya looked at the man holding her hands — this stranger with kind eyes and mud on his sleeves who was looking at her as though she were the last warm thing in the world — and felt the shape of the hole where something bright had been, and understood, with a clarity that felt like falling:

The payment wasn't finished.

Somewhere in the dark water of her past, more windows were standing open onto snow, and the winter was still going out of her, one warm room at a time.

"Torv," she said slowly, testing the name, finding it familiar and foreign at once. "Why do I know your name?"

He smiled, and it was the bravest thing she had ever seen anyone do.

"Because," he said, "I'm going to keep telling you."
