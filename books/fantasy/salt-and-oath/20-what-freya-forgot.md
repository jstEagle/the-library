## 20. What Freya Forgot

The stars came back at dawn, which was wrong — everyone knew stars belonged to the dark — but Torv Askeland had stopped trusting his knowledge of how the world ought to behave somewhere around the time a dead king's crown cracked open on a drowned altar and screamed in three hundred voices.

He was sitting on the shingle above the tide line with Isolde's bloodied cloak folded under him like a pillow he hadn't earned, watching the sky do its impossible trick. One moment the heavens were a smooth grey slate, as they had been for eleven days. The next, they were stitched. Thousands of tiny punctures of light, shy at first, then bold, as if the whole firmament had been holding its breath and finally let it out.

Torv did not cheer. He wanted to. His chest actually hurt with wanting to. But he was looking instead at the woman lying ten feet away on a pallet of fishing nets and gull-down, breathing slow and even, her face slack with the deepest sleep he'd ever seen on anyone who wasn't dead.

Freya Almeida had been asleep for two days.

The healers of the salt-coast villages — what remained of them, the ones whose names hadn't been eaten out from under them — said she would wake or she wouldn't, and that either way there was nothing to be done, which Torv felt was the sort of thing healers should keep to themselves.

Isolde limped up the shingle to stand beside him, her bandaged thigh stiff, her face grey with exhaustion and something else. She followed his gaze to the sky.

"They're back," she said.

"I can see that."

"Say something more useful than that."

Torv considered. "They're back, Your Highness."

"That's worse." But the corner of her mouth twitched, and after a moment she sat down beside him with a grunt, arranging her wounded leg with the care of a woman handling someone else's heirloom. For a while neither of them spoke. The tide came in and went out, doing its honest work. Somewhere behind them, in the village, Aldric was sleeping too — truly sleeping, for the first time since anyone could remember, his crown reduced to a ring of cracked black metal that the blacksmith had offered to melt down twice already.

"She spoke it," Isolde said quietly. "At the end. I heard it. A name that wasn't hers, going into her like water into dry ground. And then Sefa — the girl, whatever was left of her — she looked at Freya and she *wept*, Torv. The Choir wept. I didn't know a thing like that could weep."

"It knew what it was losing," Torv said. "That's all grief is. Knowing what you're losing."

He said it lightly. He did not feel light. Because he had done the arithmetic on the shrine floor, kneeling in seawater beside a dying god and a collapsing king, and the arithmetic had come out ugly: *equal memory for equal name*. A speaker must carry it. He had known before Freya finished speaking the unbinding exactly what it would cost her, because she had told him once, weeks ago, around a fire in silent country, in the offhand way she talked about frightening things.

*Everything has a price. Usually it's the thing you least want to pay.*

He just hadn't known he was the thing.

* * *

She woke near sunset.

Torv knew it before anyone told him, because the gulls started up — the real ones, the ordinary rude shrieking fish-thieves of the coast, not the messengers of the last month — and their noise had a different quality when Freya was awake to hear it. The whole village seemed to lean toward her door.

He made himself walk slowly. He made himself stop at the well and draw water he didn't need, because a man who arrives breathless at a sickbed announces his fear, and Torv Askeland had spent twelve years learning to hide things, and old habits were the last possessions he had left.

Then he went in, and forgot every one of them anyway.

She was sitting up against the wall, wrapped in a blanket, hair loose and salt-stiff, looking out the window at the returning stars — visible now in the last violet of dusk, the early ones. She looked thin. She looked tired. She looked, God help him, exactly the same, and that was the worst part, because he had braced himself for some visible wound, some mark where the payment had been taken, and there was nothing. Just Freya, whole and unhurt, as if the last four months had happened to someone else.

Because they had.

"Hello," she said, when she saw him. Polite. Warm, even. The voice you give a stranger who has brought you soup.

"Hello," Torv said.

"You're the ward-keeper." She said it with the small satisfaction of someone retrieving a fact from a shelf. "Torv. Torv — Askeland. Isolde told me. She's been — she's been filling me in. There's rather a lot." She laughed, a little helplessly, and rubbed her eyes. "I remember my mother."

The words landed strangely, out of order, and he understood she wasn't changing the subject; she was telling him the shape of what she'd woken to. Her voice cracked on *mother*.

"I remember my mother," she said again. "Her name was Maren. She smelled of beeswax and river mud. She used to sing to the goats so they'd stand still for milking, and I thought every child's mother could do that, and I never told anyone, and then one day she was gone and nobody remembered her, not even the goats, and I thought I'd invented her. Twelve years I thought I'd invented her." She was crying now, without drama, the way people cry when they're too exhausted for drama. "And I remember the shrine. I remember Sefa. I remember the unbinding, every word of it, I remember choosing it. I remember everything." She looked up at him, red-eyed, bewildered, honest. "So why don't I remember you?"

There it was. The question he had rehearsed answers to for two days, standing on this exact stretch of shingle, pacing out the length of it like a man measuring a grave.

He had prepared explanations. He had prepared the whole story, ordered and annotated: the harbor, the horse, the road north, the fire in silent country, the oath he'd sworn freely at the breaking, the flight south, the way she'd laughed when he'd complained about gulls, the way she'd held his hand through the night Isolde's fever peaked, the way — the hundred small ways — that four months of almost and nearly and not-yet had braided itself between them until he couldn't find the seam where friendship ended.

He had prepared all of it. And standing in the doorway, looking at her wet, waiting face, he found he couldn't do it. Explaining felt like showing someone a map of a country they'd never visited and expecting them to be homesick.

So instead, Torv Askeland — disgraced ward-keeper, drunk, coward by his own long accounting, survivor of the fall of Vhalen's wards — did the bravest thing of his life, which looked, from the outside, like almost nothing.

He stuck out his hand.

"Torv," he said. "Torv Askeland. Formerly keeper of the western border wards, currently unemployed, chronically under-slept, and — full disclosure — the man who drank through the worst year of this kingdom's history. You calmed a cart-horse in Dunmark harbor and I decided you were either a saint or a criminal, and I've spent the intervening months failing to determine which."

Freya stared at his hand. Then at his face. Then, slowly, she laughed — a real laugh, surprised out of her, wet and cracked at the edges but real — and took his hand and shook it, solemn as a treaty.

"Freya Almeida," she said. "Charm-seller. Beast-speaker. Apparently also the woman who unmade a god, though I have to take everyone's word for that, because honestly I don't remember doing anything more dramatic than being extremely cold in the sea."

"It was very dramatic," Torv assured her. "There was screaming. Mostly mine."

"There usually is, with you, I'd imagine."

"Oh, you have no idea." He did not let go of her hand immediately, and she did not pull hers away, and both of them noticed, and both of them pretended not to, and the pretending had a warmth to it that startled him — because this was new, this was genuinely new, and new was not what he had expected to feel. He had expected to feel robbed. Instead he felt — introduced.

* * *

The days that followed settled into a rhythm that Torv learned to live inside the way you learn to live inside a house with a room you don't open.

Freya recovered fast. Too fast, the healer grumbled, as if speed were a symptom. She walked the tideline. She sat with Aldric for hours, speaking to him in the low even tones she used on frightened animals, and slowly the king began to answer — first with his eyes, then single words, then, one grey morning, a complete sentence about the state of the granaries that made Isolde leave the room so no one would see the princess of Vhalen sobbing over grain reports.

Freya remembered all of it. The purge, her mother Maren, the gull that had spoken her name, the drowned ward-stone, the archive beneath the chapel, Serane's confession, the breaking of oaths in the palace square while white-eyed hunters closed in. She remembered Torv's role in each event with the clean clarity of a historian. *You carried me when my leg gave out on the salt marsh. You stood beside me when I freed the soldiers. Isolde says you swore a new kind of oath.* She recounted these things to him at supper, grateful and curious, interviewing him about himself the way scholars interview survivors.

And every evening, without fail, she would reach the edge of what she knew and stop, like a cart reaching the end of a plowed road. *And then?* she'd ask. *What happened then?* And he would tell her — carefully, lightly, leaving out the hand-holding and the laughing and the night on the marsh when she'd pressed her forehead to his shoulder and said, *when this is over, I'm going to learn what your laugh sounds like when you're not hiding it* — and she would nod, and thank him, and look at him with those clear brown eyes that held no recognition at all, only interest, only openness, only the terrible neutral kindness people extend to strangers who have been good to them.

It should have destroyed him. Some nights it came close. He would lie awake in the loft of the fisherman's house they'd been given and catalog what was gone: she no longer saved him the heel of the loaf, because she didn't know he liked it. She no longer hummed the Dunmark market song when she worked, because — no, wait, she still did that, that had survived, and he'd had to leave the room the first time he heard it, and stand outside in the cold with his hand over his mouth like a man holding in a wound.

But here was the thing he discovered, the thing that kept ambushing him: she was still *her*. The payment had taken the memories, not the person. She still talked to the gulls when she thought no one was listening (and they answered, rudely, affectionately). She still had the habit of standing with her weight on one hip when she was thinking. She still found his grumbling funny — new-found-funny, fresh-funny, but funny nonetheless, and she sought it out, asked him questions designed to make him complain, and Torv realized with a jolt one evening that she was flirting with him, clumsily, experimentally, the way you flirt with someone you've just met and already like, and that he was blushing like a boy of fifteen.

"You're staring," she said, catching him at it across the supper table.

"I'm introducing myself," he said. "Internally. It takes a while. I'm a complicated fellow."

"Mm." She passed him the heel of the loaf. She had started doing it without knowing why, days ago, and when he'd pointed it out she'd frowned and said, *habit, apparently,* and he had carried that heel-of-the-loaf moment around inside him ever since like a coal in a pocket.

* * *

On the ninth evening after she woke, they walked out past the village to the point where the shrine's rocks broke the surf, because she asked to see it. The binding circle was gone — scoured clean, the stone pale as bone, warm no longer. Three hundred years of a prisoner's heat, released in an afternoon.

"She thanked me," Freya said. They stood side by side, boots in the wet sand, the tide breathing at their feet. "Sefa. At the end. I have it — the memory. She put her hand on my face and she said, *now someone will remember me kindly.*" Freya's jaw tightened. "I intend to. I'm writing it all down. Everything. Names, dates, what was taken, what it cost. Isolde's keeping the kingdom's ledger; I'll keep this one. No one gets erased unwitnessed again. Not her. Not my mother. Not—" she paused, "—whatever else there is to lose."

"No," Torv agreed. "Not unwitnessed."

She turned to look at him. The stars were coming out behind her, all of them, the whole repaired sky, and her face was half-lit and half-dark and entirely unreadable, which was unusual for her, and therefore alarming.

"Can I ask you something, Torv Askeland?" she said. "And I want you to answer honestly, because I've noticed you're a man who answers honestly at precisely the moments it costs you most, and I've decided I admire that."

"That's an accurate portrait," he managed.

"The version of me you knew. Before." She hugged her elbows against the sea wind. "Was she — were we —"

She stopped. Started again. Stopped. And Torv watched her wrestle with the question, watched her decide whether to ask it, and he understood suddenly that he had a choice, the same choice he'd had in the doorway nine days ago, and that he would be making it every day for the rest of his life, and that this was not a tragedy. This was, in fact, the entire point. An oath sworn once and forgotten is a chain. An oath chosen daily is a gift.

"We were friends," he said. "Good ones. The kind who were probably headed somewhere else eventually, if either of us had ever worked up the nerve."

Freya exhaled. Something in her shoulders loosened. "Were you working up the nerve?"

"Aggressively. For months. I had a whole speech prepared. Very moving. Isolde helped with the structure."

That startled a laugh out of her — and then she went quiet, and looked at him for a long moment, waves crashing, stars wheeling, the whole indifferent gorgeous machinery of the returned universe grinding overhead.

"Well," she said at last. "I can't speak for her. The other me. But I'll tell you what I know." She stepped closer, close enough that he could see the salt drying in her lashes. "I've known you eleven days. In that time you've carried my water, defended my reputation to a skeptical fisherman, listened to every one of my stories twice without yawning, and looked at me — and I may be a charm-seller, Torv, but I know what longing looks like on a man, I sold love-charms for six years — you've looked at me like I was something you lost and found again. So." She took a breath. "Introduce yourself again. Properly. Not the joke version. The true one. And then let's see if we can't get somewhere faster the second time, since apparently I'm a quick study."

Torv Askeland had faced the Hollow Choir. He had stood in a square full of white-eyed hunters and spoken a rite that branded him traitor. None of it had made his heart hammer like this.

"Torv Askeland," he said, and his voice cracked, and he let it. "Keeper of no wards. Sworn to one oath, freely given, easily broken, renewed daily. Which starts now." He held out his hand — and then, because the second time should be braver than the first, he didn't shake hers. He simply held it.

Behind them, far up the beach, a door opened in the fisherman's cottage, and Isolde's voice called something urgent — a rider on the coast road, a banner, words that didn't belong to any kingdom Torv knew. The world, being the world, was already asking things of them again.

But for one moment longer, Freya Almeida held his hand under the returned stars, and smiled at him like a beginning.

"All right, Torv Askeland," she said. "Tell me everything."
