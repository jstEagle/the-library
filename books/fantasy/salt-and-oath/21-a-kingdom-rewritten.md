## 21. A Kingdom Rewritten

The regency council had been arguing about bread for three hours.

Isolde sat at the head of the long table in the Council Hall of Haldmere — a seat she had not asked for and could not, by any law she respected, refuse — and watched the Lord of the Granaries and the Master of Coin conduct their slow ritual of mutual destruction. The question was whether to open the southern granaries early. The winter had broken late, the stores were thin, and both men were so committed to being right that neither had noticed they were saying the same thing.

"Lord Vess," Isolde said, when the Master of Coin paused for breath, "you propose opening the granaries in six weeks. Lord Harn proposes opening them in five. May I suggest we open them now, while everyone still has the strength to carry the grain home?"

A silence. Then Lord Harn, the Granaries man, went faintly pink. "Your Highness, the protocols—"

"The protocols," Isolde said, "were written by men who assumed the kingdom's records would always be intact. The kingdom's records are ash, my lord. We wrote new ones last month. I signed them." She smiled, because her father had taught her that a smile cost nothing and bought much, and because she had learned from Serane's example that a smile could also be a wall. "Unless anyone wishes to tell me why we should let people starve for six weeks out of respect for documents that no longer exist?"

No one did. The vote took four minutes.

Afterward, as the council filed out muttering — some of them, she suspected, already composing letters of complaint to her, which was at least better than composing them behind her back — Torv Askeland lingered by the door. He looked better than he had on the salt coast, where he'd been gray-faced and shaking and carrying Freya like something breakable. Better, but not well. Ward-keepers, Isolde had learned, did not recover from watching their life's work fail. They only learned to stand next to the failure without flinching.

"Princess," he said. "She's asking for you."

Freya Almeida had taken a room in the east tower, against all advice. The physicians wanted her abed; the council wanted her under observation; the more superstitious clerks wanted her anywhere but inside the walls. Freya wanted tea, sunlight, and a window overlooking the harbor, and she had gotten two of the three, which she said was a fair average for a Tuesday.

* * *

The east tower room smelled of salt and ink. Freya sat wrapped in a blanket despite the mild spring air, her journal open on her knees, writing in that quick, careful hand of hers — the hand of someone who had decided, once and forever, that nothing would go unwitnessed again.

"You look terrible," Isolde said.

"I look rested," Freya said. "You should have seen me in midwinter."

"I did see you in midwinter."

"Then you know I'm exaggerating upward." She set down the pen and studied Isolde with those clear gray eyes that missed nothing, that had heard the crown whispering when no one else could. "How is your father today?"

That was the question Isolde dreaded and needed, every visit, like a bone she had to set each morning.

"He remembered my mother's name yesterday," she said. "Not her face. But the name. He said it out loud while feeding the gulls and then cried for an hour, and then forgot he'd been crying, but not what he'd been crying about. It's — uneven. Like frost melting off a window. Some patches clear, some not."

"And the other thing?"

"He asked me if I was angry with him." Isolde sat down across from her. "I told him no. I told him the truth, which is worse: that I'm not angry at all. That I spent twelve years keeping a ledger of everything he forgot, and now I understand he wasn't choosing to forget, and I don't know what to do with all that grief I built. It has nowhere to go. Grief wants an enemy, and mine turned out to be a girl who died three hundred years ago and a crown that's currently sitting in a locked box in the archive, cracked down the middle, humming very quietly when it rains."

Freya nodded slowly. "Torv says it hums less every month."

"Torv says a lot of things. He also says the wards can be rebuilt properly this time — not as a cage lid, as he puts it, but as an actual door. With hinges. And a bell." Isolde allowed herself a small smile. "He's very insistent about the bell."

"Of course he is. He's a ward-keeper. A ward without a bell is just a wall." Freya pulled the blanket tighter. "Isolde. Why do you think I asked you here?"

"Because you're bored and I'm entertaining."

"Because the council is going to come for me soon," Freya said, gently, "and when they do, I want to know which side of the table you'll be sitting on."

There it was. Isolde had known it was coming since the day the stars returned and the first clerk, blinking in unfamiliar starlight, had asked the obvious question: *what happened to us?* And the second, more dangerous question: *who did this?*

The answer to the second question was sitting in front of her, wrapped in a blanket, drinking tea.

"They won't come for you," Isolde said. "They'll come for *speakers*. You happen to be the one they can find."

"That's what I said."

"My father outlawed magic twelve years ago because he was afraid," Isolde said. "And the purge that followed erased my mother from every record and every memory except mine and Freya Almeida's, and none of us knew it until it was almost too late. If the council tries to rebuild that fear, I will stand in front of it. Not because I love you particularly—" Freya snorted— "all right, partially because I love you particularly, but mostly because I've read the archive. I know what fear costs. I've seen the invoice."

Freya was quiet for a moment. Then she turned her journal around and pushed it across the table.

"Then help me with this," she said.

* * *

The journal was not one book anymore. It was eleven books, bound in sailcloth and string, and it was growing. Page after page in Freya's hand, and interleaved with it, in other hands — Torv's blunt block letters, Isolde's own careful script, even a few pages in her father's shakier post-illness scrawl. Names. Dates. Accounts.

"It's not enough to remember," Freya said. "Remembering is private. One person remembers, and when that person dies, the remembering dies too. That's how the Choir worked, in the end. It didn't need to eat everyone's memories. It just needed everyone to assume someone else was keeping track." She tapped the page. "This needs to be public. Copied. Distributed. A record that lives in a thousand houses, so that no king, no council, no *crown* can ever make it disappear again."

"You want me to found an archive."

"I want you to found the opposite of the old archive. The old one was sealed and secret, and secrets get eaten. This one should be loud. Boring, even. Copies in every parish. Children learning it in school alongside their letters. The most tedious document in Vhalen, so dull that no one would ever think to burn it, because burning it would be more interesting than reading it."

Despite everything — the exhaustion, the grief with nowhere to go, the weight of a regency she hadn't asked for — Isolde laughed. "You're proposing to defeat the forces of eternal darkness with bureaucracy."

"I'm proposing to defeat them with *witnesses*. Bureaucracy is just witnesses with good filing." Freya's grin faded into something more serious. "It worked, you know. In the end. What broke the Choir wasn't power. It was a name, given back, in front of people who saw it happen. Sefa got her name back because I carried it, and I carried it because someone wrote it down — her name survived three hundred years in a binding record nobody was supposed to find. Writing things down is how we win. Slowly. Unheroically. Forever."

Isolde looked at the journals. Eleven books. She thought of her ledger — the secret list she'd kept of everything her father forgot, dates and faces and laws, written in the dark years when writing it down felt like the only rebellion available to a princess with no army. She had thought that ledger was a wound. Maybe it had been a foundation.

"All right," she said. "But if we're doing this, we do it properly. Which means the council hears it from me before they hear it from a rumor. And it means the first entry is the whole truth — including the part where loyalty itself was the vector. Every sworn soldier, every oath-bound subject, a straw in its mouth." She paused. "That part will be... unpopular."

"Unpopular," Freya said, "is survivable. Forgotten isn't."

* * *

She gave the speech to the full council nine days later, standing rather than seated, because she wanted them to remember that she had stood.

She told them all of it. The Choir. The crown forged from a bound speaker. The wards that were never protection. The oaths that fed it. Her father's erosion, described plainly, without shame — his, or hers. The purge twelve years ago that had eaten a woman named Maren Almeida out of the world so thoroughly that her own daughter had only recently learned her name. And at the end, she held up the first copy of the new archive — plain paper, plain ink, deliberately unadorned — and said:

"This will be copied ten thousand times. It will be in every parish, every schoolhouse, every garrison. No one will be required to believe it. Everyone will be required to have access to it. That is the difference between a kingdom and a cage."

Lord Vess, the Master of Coin, cleared his throat. "Your Highness. With respect. The last time magic was spoken of openly, we had purges. Mob violence. Families torn apart. How do you propose to prevent—"

"By doing it differently than my father did," Isolde said. "He answered fear with silence, and silence with law, and law with erasure. We answer it with daylight. Yes, there will be speakers among us. There always were — hiding, the way Freya hid, selling charms in a market and hoping no one noticed. Would you rather they hid, or would you rather they were licensed, trained, accountable, and *bored*?" A ripple of uncomfortable laughter moved down the table. Good. Laughter opened doors that arguments couldn't. "I propose a registry. Voluntary. Protected. And I propose that the first name on it belong to the woman who saved us, who is currently upstairs drinking tea and complaining about our harbor view."

Silence. Then, from the far end of the table, old Lady Merren — ninety if she was a day, sharp as a fishhook — said in a carrying voice: "Seconded. And I'll remind the council that I remember the purge. I remember women being dragged from their homes over rumors. Anyone who votes against this registry had best hope I die before they do, because otherwise I intend to be extremely tiresome about it."

The vote carried. Not unanimously — Isolde noted the four dissenters' names in her own ledger, because noting names was simply what she did now — but carried.

* * *

Her father came to the archive himself, in the autumn.

He walked slowly these days, leaning on a cane Isolde had bought him from a Dunmark carpenter, and he wore plain clothes, having formally set aside the crown — the real one, the cracked one, which lived in a lead-lined box in the deepest vault, humming less every season. The regency governed in his name. Everyone understood, without anyone saying it, that it would govern for years, perhaps forever, and Aldric seemed — Isolde watched him carefully for this — relieved.

He stood in the long gallery of the new archive, where the copies hung in open racks, free for any citizen to take, and read the account of his own unraveling. His face did something complicated.

"I don't remember most of this," he said quietly.

"I know."

"No — I mean I don't remember it happening. Reading it is like reading about a stranger. A stranger who wore my face and loved my daughter and lost her, page by page." He touched the edge of a rack, lightly, the way he used to touch the heads of his soldiers. "Do you hate me for it? For the outlawry? For what the purge took from you?"

Isolde considered the question honestly, because he deserved honesty and had rarely gotten it from anyone in this palace.

"For a long time I hated the version of you that signed the edicts," she said. "Then I learned there wasn't really a version of you that chose freely. And now I think — Father, you were the Choir's first hostage and its last. I don't hate hostages." She hesitated. "But I am going to spend the rest of my life making sure it never happens again. That's not forgiveness. It's work. I've decided they can look similar."

Aldric laughed — a rusty sound, but real. "Your mother would have said exactly that."

"You remember her?"

"Bits. More every week, the physicians say. Her name. Her laugh. The way she hummed when she cooked." He looked out the high window, toward the harbor, where the gulls wheeled under a sky full of returned stars' daytime absence — ordinary sky, blessedly ordinary. "I'm relearning how to be a man, Isolde. It turns out I was never very good at being a king. Perhaps the second lesson will improve the first."

* * *

On the first anniversary of the night the stars went out, the kingdom held no grand ceremony. Isolde had insisted on that. Instead, in every parish, the new archive copies were read aloud — the whole tedious truth of it, all four hours — and afterward people simply stood outside together and looked up until the stars came out, and went home.

In Haldmere, Isolde stood on the palace steps with Freya and Torv. Somewhere below in the city, thousands of people were looking up. Above, the stars burned in their ancient, indifferent constellations, and Isolde found she could look at them now without counting the gaps.

"Do you ever miss it?" Freya asked. "The gift? Hearing them?"

"Sometimes," Isolde said. "Mostly I miss being able to ask the dogs what they think of the council. They had opinions."

Freya laughed, and leaned her head briefly against Isolde's shoulder — a small gesture, freely given, easily withdrawn, worth more than any oath. Below them, Torv was explaining the principle of bells to a cluster of children who had clearly cornered him and were showing no mercy.

"Isolde." Freya's voice had gone quiet. "There's something I haven't told you. About the unbinding. About what I heard, at the end, when the Choir came apart."

Isolde turned. Freya was watching the sky, her gray eyes reflecting starlight.

"When it unraveled, for half a heartbeat, every voice it had ever stolen spoke at once — everyone it ate, everyone whose memories fed the winter. Thousands of voices. And underneath all of them, one voice I didn't expect." She paused. "It said thank you. Not to me. To the kingdom. To everyone who kept records, who wrote things down, who refused to forget on purpose. It said: *now I was never alone.*"

The wind came off the harbor, smelling of salt. Down in the city, the reading had ended, and the crowd noise rose like a tide — ordinary, warm, alive.

"So that's why the archive matters," Isolde said slowly. "Not just so we don't forget."

"So that whatever comes next," Freya said, "knows it isn't alone either."

Isolde looked out at her kingdom — battered, thinner, grieving, and stubbornly, magnificently still here — and reached into her coat for her notebook, because there was, as always, something that needed writing down.

She had just uncapped the pen when a runner came pounding up the palace steps, salt-spray soaked, from the direction of the coast road.

"Your Highness," the boy gasped. "Message from Dunmark. From the shore watch." He fumbled the folded paper out. "They say the tide went out past the old shrine rocks tonight — farther than anyone's ever seen — and the drowned shrine is *dry*. And there's light coming from inside it."

Freya went very still beside her.

Isolde read the message twice, then folded it carefully and put it in her notebook, between the pages of things witnessed and things owed.

"Well," she said. "Fetch your boots."
