## 22. Salt and Oath

The morning market at Dunmark smelled of kelp and frying onions and, for the first time in a year, of nothing hidden.

Freya Almeida set out her stall the way she had always done — a strip of canvas over two barrels, a plank laid across trestles, her wares arranged in careful rows. But where once she had sold knotted thread and river-stones with vague promises stitched into them, *for luck, for safe travel, for a good night's sleep*, she now sold charms that worked, and said so, loudly, to anyone who passed.

"Bone-knot against toothache," she called to a fishwife hauling baskets up from the quay. "Gull-feather for fair winds. Salt-glass if you want to dream true. All of it honest, all of it mine, and I'll tell you exactly what each one does or your coin back."

The fishwife paused, squinting. "You're the one who ended the winter."

"I'm the one who sells charms," Freya said. "The winter had help."

This was, by now, a familiar exchange. It happened four or five times a morning. Some people wanted to touch her sleeve. One old man had wept onto her counter, which she had found harder to bear than any battle. A child had asked her if she could make his dead dog speak again, and she had knelt down on the cobbles and told him the truth: no, but dogs remembered him, and that was its own kind of magic, and he had looked at her with such fierce solemnity that she'd had to turn away and pretend to rearrange her feathers until her eyes stopped stinging.

She kept a jar on the corner of the stall now, half-filled with coins and one dried starfish, and beside it a sign she had painted herself, in letters slightly crooked:

**CHARMS, HONESTLY MADE. SPEAKING DONE BY APPOINTMENT.**

The speaking was the part people were still shy about. A year ago it would have meant the purge-wagons. Now it meant a queue on Thursdays, mostly farmers wanting to know why their cows were off their feed, and once a widow who paid three coppers just to have someone confirm that her husband's last words really had been about the roof. They had been. He'd loved that roof like a rival. The widow had laughed and cried at the same time, which Freya was learning was simply what grief did when you gave it room.

And every day, without fail, she wrote it all down.

The journal lived under the counter in an oilcloth wrap. It was her fourth. The first three sat in a chest at home, and copies of those went into the new archive Isolde was building in Haldmere — three sets, inked by three different hands, because Freya had learned what forgetting cost and had no intention of ever letting it happen unwitnessed again. She wrote the weather. She wrote the names of everyone who bought a charm. She wrote what the gulls told her, which was mostly complaints about herring quotas, but you never knew when a complaint might matter. She wrote down the taste of the tea Torv brought her each morning, because she had decided that if she was going to lose things, she would at least keep excellent records of them first.

She was writing exactly that when his shadow fell across the stall.

"Good morning," said Torv Askeland. "I don't believe we've met. Torv Askeland. Formerly a ward-keeper, currently unemployed, chronically early." He set down two cups of tea on her counter, steam curling into the cold bright air. "I brought you tea. I hope that's not presumptuous. I can leave if it's presumptuous."

"It's a little presumptuous," Freya said, and took the cup.

It was the same every morning, and it was never the same at all. That was the arrangement they had made, or rather the arrangement he had offered and she had, after a long terrible week of crying into her pillow, accepted. Every day he came. Every day he introduced himself as though they were strangers. And every day she chose — actively, deliberately, with witnesses whenever possible — to learn him again.

Because here was the shape of what she had lost: she remembered everything else. Her mother's face, restored to her like a lamp relit — thin, sharp-eyed, humming while she worked, smelling of juniper and woodsmoke. Her gift, whole. Sefa's name, which lived in her now like a second heartbeat, quiet and warm. The winter, the shrine, the crown cracking, Aldric collapsing free. She remembered Torv's role in all of it. She could recite his history: disgraced ward-keeper, drunk on guilt, loyal past all sense, who had stood beside her in the surf while white-eyed hunters closed in and sworn an oath that was freely given and easily broken, and then kept it anyway.

What she did not remember was *him*. Not the particular him. The way his laugh started in his chest before it reached his mouth. The exact weight of his hand. Whether he took honey in his tea (he did; she had written it down; the knowing still slid off her like water off oiled cloth). The memories had been the price, equal weight for equal weight, and the Choir's ledger had been precise. It had taken the man himself and left her only the story of him.

So every morning he introduced himself, and every morning she learned him, and every evening she wrote down what she had learned so that tomorrow's version of her could pick up the thread.

"You're staring at me," Torv said. He had settled onto the crate behind the stall that was, by ancient custom, his. "Is this the part where you decide I'm untrustworthy? Because yesterday you decided I was trustworthy around lunchtime, so you've got a few hours yet."

"I'm deciding whether you take honey."

"One spoon," he said gravely. "Two if the morning is bad. This morning I saw a gull steal a whole mackerel from Old Bram's cart, so I'd call it a two-spoon morning, but I drank it before I got here. You'll have to trust my account."

Freya laughed — and caught herself laughing, and felt the strange doubled joy of it, the laugh arriving fresh even though some deeper part of her suspected she had laughed this exact laugh a hundred times before. There was a ghost of familiarity in him that never quite resolved. Like a song heard through a wall. She had stopped resenting it. Mostly.

"Tell me something about yourself," she said, because that was the ritual too. "Something true. Yesterday you told me about the time you fell asleep in a ward-shrine and woke up covered in cats."

"That's a true story. There were nine cats. I counted the bites." He turned his cup slowly on the counter. "Today's true thing. Hmm." He looked out at the harbor, where the water lay flat and silver under a sky full of returned stars' daytime absence — no, that wasn't right, there were no stars in daytime, but Freya still checked sometimes, a quick involuntary glance upward, confirming. The sky stayed full. It stayed full every day, and every day she checked anyway. "Today's true thing is that I'm nervous," Torv said. "Which is ridiculous, because I've faced down oath-sworn hunters with white eyes, and this makes me more nervous than that."

"Why nervous?"

"Because today I'm going to ask you something, and I want you to know — before you answer, before you even hear it — that the answer changes nothing about whether I come back tomorrow. That's the whole point of what we're doing. The oath I swore at the shrine was easy to break. That was the point of it. Nobody holds me to it but me. So whatever you say next, I'll be here tomorrow with tea, introducing myself, starting over, for as long as you'll have me. Even if the answer is no. *Especially* if the answer is no, actually, because I suspect you'll be embarrassed, and embarrassment goes better with company."

Freya's heart was doing something complicated against her ribs. "Torv—"

"Let me finish, I rehearsed this on the walk over and if I stop I'll lose my nerve and start talking about cats again." He took a breath. "You keep records. You write everything down so nothing gets taken unwitnessed. And I thought — well. I thought maybe I could be witnessed too. Not remembered. I understand the difference better than anyone alive, probably. But witnessed. I'd like to be in the journal. By name. As yours. If you want. Courtship, the books call it, though I notice the books are very vague on the actual mechanics."

The market went on around them — the fishwife shouting, a cart rattling past, gulls arguing overhead about the mackerel incident — and Freya sat inside all of it, absolutely still.

"I already write about you," she said finally. "Every day. What you told me. What you brought. What I figured out about you before the tea went cold." She pulled the journal out from under the counter and opened it to yesterday's page and turned it toward him. "*He takes one spoon of honey, two on bad mornings. He counts bites from cats. When he laughs, he looks surprised about it, like the laugh got there ahead of him.*"

Torv read it. His eyes went bright. He blinked hard and pretended to study the crooked sign instead.

"That's me," he said, voice rough. "That's — yes. That's me exactly."

"I know," Freya said. "You told me. And yesterday's me believed you, and tomorrow's me will believe you, and I will spend the rest of my life choosing to believe you, every single morning, fresh, on purpose." She closed the journal and put her hand flat on its cover. "That's the only kind of loyalty worth anything. I learned that from a girl who was bound by kings for the crime of being trusted wrongly. So yes, Torv Askeland. Witnessed. Write it down yourself if you like — I want it in your hand as well as mine."

He laughed — that surprised laugh, starting in his chest — and dug a stub of pencil from his coat, and beneath her entry he wrote, in a ward-keeper's blunt engineer's hand:

*She said yes. Witnessed. — T.A.*

Then he leaned across the counter, slow enough that she could have stopped him, and kissed her, tasting of honey and salt, and the gulls screamed approval or possibly outrage, and somewhere out on the water the tide turned, gentle and ordinary, carrying nothing but fish.

* * *

They married in spring, small and loud, on the Dunmark shore at low tide, because Freya wanted the drowned shrine visible in the distance when she made her vows — wanted the sea that had held the binding to hold the promise too. Isolde came down from Haldmere with an escort she kept trying to disband and a gift of a blank journal bound in blue leather, "for volume five." King Aldric sent a letter in his own careful, relearned handwriting, each letter formed like a child's, each word chosen like a treasure: *I am told I met you once. I am told you saved us. I believe both, because I am told them by my daughter, who does not lie, and because the kingdom is still here, which is proof of something. Be happy. I am practicing being happy myself. It is harder than kingship.*

Serane did not come. She lived now in a house by a different sea, tending graves, and once a year she sent a letter containing nothing but the date and the words *still counting*, and Freya understood it was penance and let it be.

Torv's vow was six words long. Freya had expected poetry. Instead he took her hands on the wet sand, with the shrine a dark shape far out in the bright water, and said: "I choose this. Every day. Witnessed." And the assembled guests — fishwives, farmers, one extremely smug gull perched on Old Bram's cart — made the response that had become customary in Dunmark since the winter, the response that was spreading along the coast road into the hills, town by town, the way good ideas travel:

"Witnessed."

Freya's own vows ran longer. She was a speaker; words were her craft. But the heart of hers was also short: "I cannot hold every memory. No one can. But I can witness everything, and I choose you daily, and choice is stronger than remembering. Ask me tomorrow. I'll say it again."

* * *

Years later — though not so many years, only enough for the crooked sign to need repainting twice — a traveler came through Dunmark market on a Thursday, when the speaking-queue was longest.

She was young, maybe seventeen, with a scarf wrapped high over her ears despite the mild weather, and she did not join the queue. She hovered at the edge of the stalls, watching Freya work with an intensity that made the horses in the nearby carts shift and stare at her. Freya noticed. Freya had always noticed. The animals were looking at the girl the way animals look at a door that shouldn't be open.

When the queue thinned, the girl approached the stall. Up close there was something odd about her — a faint doubled quality, like a voice heard in harmony with itself.

"Are you the one who unbound the Choir?" the girl asked.

"I'm the one who sells charms," Freya said, in the old familiar rhythm. "The winter had help."

"My name is Wren." The girl hesitated. "Except it isn't. That's the name I made. I don't remember the one I was born with, and neither does anyone else, because—" she swallowed— "because something ate it. Before your winter. Something older, out past the border, in the deep country where the wards used to run. My grandmother says there was a hollow place in the world there, and children born near it came out missing pieces." She put both hands on Freya's counter, and her eyes were the color of the sea at low tide, and somewhere far out past the harbor, past the drowned shrine, the water went suddenly, perfectly still. "You gave a stolen name back once. I'm told you're the person who'd know how. I want mine."

Freya looked at this girl — this hungry, hopeful, broken-open girl — and then at Torv, who had gone quiet beside her, and then at the journal lying open on the counter, its pages waiting, its whole purpose standing up inside her chest like a second spine.

"Well," Freya said, and picked up her pencil. "Then let's start at the beginning. Tell me everything you remember. We'll witness the rest."

Out past the shrine, the tide began, very slowly, to come in.
