## 1. The Ledger of Impossible Things

The Salt Guild's counting room smelled of tallow, brine, and old money, and Astrid Brightwater had long ago decided it was the most honest smell in Greyharbor — because everything else in the building lied.

She sat at the third desk from the window, the one with the warped leg she'd shimmed herself with a wedge of driftwood, and worked her way down the column of figures for the autumn quarter. Quill, ink, blotter, abacus of whalebone beads. Around her, the other junior clerks scratched and muttered and dreamed of the dinner hour. Astrid did not dream of the dinner hour. She dreamed of page four hundred eleven.

But first, the ordinary work. A shipment of grey salt out of the Cindering Mines, forty barrels, sold to the temple kitchens at a price that would have made a beggar blush. Three casks of pickled eel. A tithe of lamp-oil to the Warden's House. All of it dull as dishwater, all of it balanced to the copper by the time the bells rang noon.

It was the other ledger that kept her at her desk past dark.

* * *

They kept it in the inner archive, behind a door of black oak banded in iron, and only the senior masters held keys. But Astrid had been balancing the guild's books since she was nine years old — first as a debt-child sweeping shavings in this very room, later as a clerk too useful to dismiss and too poor to promote — and usefulness opened doors that keys could not. Master Ondrell liked his numbers immaculate. Master Ondrell also liked his evenings warm, his wine mulled, and his ledgers reconciled before he returned from supper. So Astrid reconciled them. All of them.

The second ledger had no title on its spine. It was bound in hide so pale it might have been skin, and its pages were ruled not in ink but in something faintly silver that caught the lamplight like fish-scale. She had learned, over six years of quiet theft-by-candlelight, to read it the way one reads a tide chart: patiently, respectfully, and with a constant awareness that it could drown you.

Tonight's entries, in Master Corvane's narrow hand:

*Bottled tide, spring-high, harvested off the Teeth. Sold to Lady Vess of the Hill Houses. Payment: three favors, unspecified, redeemable at will.*

*A borrowed year. Debtor: unnamed fisherman's widow. Term: twelve months of health and vigor, transferred to the buyer. Interest: compounding.*

*A memory, thirty years' standing, sold whole and intact. Buyer's name redacted even here.*

Astrid copied each line into her own hidden book — a slim thing of cheap paper sewn into the lining of her satchel — because that was what she did now, what she had done for years, the way some people prayed or picked locks. She transcribed the impossible trades of the Salt Guild in her small, exact hand, and told herself it was prudence. Evidence. Insurance. Anything but what it actually was, which was hunger. A life too small for her body, fed drop by drop on stolen pages.

Because here was the truth she carried like a stone in her boot: Astrid Brightwater was very good at exactly the things the guild would never let her do. She could read contracts in four scripts. She could find an error in a ledger the way a gull finds a dropped fish. And she understood — truly understood, in her wrists and her teeth — that the Salt Guild's power did not come from salt at all.

Salt was the cover story. The wards along the border were real; everyone in Greyharbor owed their safety to the pale shimmer that ran along the mountain passes and the harbor mouth, keeping out whatever lived in the wild dark beyond. The guild claimed the wards were fed by tithed salt and pious maintenance. The second ledger said otherwise. The second ledger said the wards were fed the way any fire is fed — with fuel — and that the guild had found a fuel no one would miss until it was gone.

Years. Memories. Names.

Souls, though the ledger never used the word. It used *principal*. It used *long-term holdings*. It used language the way a butcher uses an apron.

Astrid turned a page, and her breath went thin.

There, in the middle of a fresh leaf, an entry dated three days past:

*Received into holding: one (1) soul, mature, willingly tendered. Seller: T— [name struck through in silver]. Consideration: extension of city ward-term, one season.*

The struck-through name was Tam's. She knew his handwriting anywhere, even half-erased — the little hook he put on his T's, the way he crossed nothing twice. Tam Vael, who had taught her her letters on the counting-room floor with a stick in spilled salt. Tam, who still brought her honey-bread on the first cold morning of every year, and who had looked tired lately in a way she'd told herself was age.

Willingly tendered.

She read the entry four times. Her quill hovered over her copy-book and did not move. Outside the shuttered window, the harbor gulls argued over something dead, and the lamps of the lower city came on one by one, honest little flames, and above it all the evening sky was doing what it always did — filling up with stars, indifferent and bright.

*One season,* the ledger said. As if a season were a fair price. As if a man were a barrel.

Astrid closed the second ledger with hands that were almost steady. Almost. She had learned steadiness the way she'd learned everything: by needing it.

* * *

Before she left, she did what she always did last. She went to the deep shelf in the archive's coldest corner, where the oldest contracts lay in their lead-lined drawers, and she drew out the one with her own name on it.

Contract of Indenture and Debt-Service, Brightwater, Astrid, aged seven years. Signed in a child's wobbling hand — her mother had guided her fingers around the pen, she remembered that much, remembered the smell of her mother's fear-sweat and the clerk's patient voice saying *just there, little one, just make your mark.* Below her name, in the guild's formal script, the terms: her mother's debts of 340 crowns to be discharged by twenty years of service to the Salt Guild, said service to commence immediately, said contract binding upon the signer "unto the full satisfaction of the Guild, its heirs and assigns."

Twenty years. She was twenty-six. Six years served, fourteen remaining, and every year the arithmetic of her life narrowed around her like a closing fist. Clerk, third desk, warped leg. Copyist of other people's wonders. Keeper of secrets she could tell no one, because who would believe the debt-child? Who would believe that the respectable guild that lit the city's wards was trading in bottled tides and borrowed years?

Her thumb moved across her own signature, the loops of it, the childish certainty of it. Seven-year-olds should not be allowed to sign away two decades. Somewhere in the guild's founding law there was probably a clause about that. There was probably a clause about everything, which was the problem. The guild wrote its clauses the way the sea wrote shorelines — endlessly, patiently, taking a little more each tide.

She slid the drawer shut. She checked that her copy-book sat snug against her ribs where the satchel's lining hid it. She banked the archive lamp, locked what she was permitted to lock, and let herself out through the clerks' door into the blue cold of evening.

Greyharbor at dusk was her favorite lie. Lamplighters working the terraces. Fish-wives haggling under strings of drying kelp. The mountain rearing up behind everything, black-shouldered, wearing its crown of ward-light like a circlet of frozen stars — brighter tonight than she'd ever seen it, she thought, though perhaps that was imagination. Perhaps the wards always burned brightest just after they'd been paid.

She bought a skewer of grilled squid from Old Maren at the harbor stair and ate it walking, letting the smoke and vinegar settle her. Tam's name sat in her chest like a swallowed coal. *Willingly tendered.* Tomorrow, she told herself. Tomorrow I'll go to him. Tomorrow I'll ask him what he's done, and he'll laugh at me, and pour tea, and tell me I read too many forbidden pages.

Except she didn't believe it. She had spent her life learning to read what people wrote without meaning to — the tremor in a master's signature, the hesitation in a sum, the way a ledger's margins grew crowded when someone was hiding something. And Tam had been writing *forgive me* in his posture for a month.

At the mouth of Scribe's Alley, where she rented a room above a chandler's shop, she stopped. Above the rooftops, the sky was doing something wrong.

The stars were going out.

Not clouding — going out, one by one, in orderly succession, west to east, like windows being shuttered in a great dark house. Astrid stood with the squid-skewer forgotten in her hand and watched the constellation the sailors called the Fisherman's Net unravel star by star, and felt the hair rise on her arms, and heard, distantly, the first bell of alarm begin to ring from the Warden's tower.

Behind her, up the hill, the guild's black oak doors boomed shut — bar after bar after bar.

The wards were failing. And somewhere in a pale-bound ledger, she suddenly understood, the night's accounts were already open.
