## 2. The Night the Stars Went Out

The trouble began, as all the Guild's troubles did, with a column of numbers that refused to add.

Astrid Brightwater sat in the counting room on the third floor of the Salt House, where the lamps burned whale-oil and the windows were glazed with glass thick enough to turn the harbor into a smear of green and gold. Around her rose the shelves of the living archive: ten thousand ledgers bound in salt-cured leather, each one a record of what the Salt Guild had bought, sold, or merely borrowed from a world that had never agreed to lend it.

Tonight's ledger was worse than most. She ran her pen down the column for the third time, lips moving, and arrived at the same impossible sum.

*Forty tides, bottled, delivered to the Undercourt. Forty received. One missing.*

She checked the manifest again. The entry was in Master Corvel's hand — she knew it by the way his *f*s hooked like fish-guts — and it recorded forty-one vessels leaving the vaults below, though only forty appeared in the return inventory. A discrepancy of one tide, which was not like losing a coin. A bottled tide was a season of the sea folded into glass. Losing one was like losing a moon.

She should have flagged it. She should have written *DISCREPANCY — REFER TO MASTER CORVEL* in the margin in her small, exact script, as she'd done eleven times before, and let the machinery of the Guild grind over it while she went home to her cold room above the ropewalk.

Instead she turned back two pages and began copying.

This was her secret, the one thing in Greyharbor she owned outright: a thin notebook sewn from sailcloth, hidden inside the false binding of a hymnal she'd bought at a dead priest's estate sale. Into it went the entries no auditor was meant to keep — the trades that made no sense, the payments routed through accounts that didn't exist, the recurring line item that appeared every seventh month like a fever:

*To the Undercourt, beneath: maintenance of the border wards. Consideration: see sealed schedule.*

*See sealed schedule.* The Guild's favorite phrase, its little locked door in every ledger, the place where the arithmetic stopped being arithmetic and became something else. Eleven times she had copied those words. Eleven times she had told herself she was only being thorough.

She knew better now. You did not copy forbidden pages unless some part of you was already planning to use them.

Outside the window, the moon was rising over the breakwater, fat and copper-colored, dragging the evening tide up the harbor stairs. Astrid glanced at it without thinking, the way sailors glance at weather, and something in her chest tightened — an old instinct, animal and wordless, the feeling of a floor about to give way.

The moon cleared the headland.

And the first star went out.

Not dimmed. Not veiled by cloud. It simply ceased, the way a candle ceases when pinched, and the darkness where it had been was somehow blacker than the sky around it. Astrid stood up so fast her chair skidded. She pressed her palm flat against the cold glass and watched.

A second star died. Then three together, a little constellation off the southern masthead of heaven, gone as if someone had blotted them with a thumb. Then more, faster, a whole bright river of them snuffed in a single breath, and now she could see it happening in waves, a tide of dark rolling across the sky from the mountains inland toward the sea, swallowing stars the way the sea swallowed lights dropped from a ship.

"No," she said aloud, to no one. "No, no —"

Below her window, Greyharbor noticed. It began with the fishing fleet coming back up the harbor road, boats still dripping, crews pointing upward and shouting. Then the temple bells began — not the measured hour-bells but the alarm pattern, three-and-one, three-and-one, the sound that meant *wards down, get inside, bar your doors*. Doors slammed down the length of Saltrow like dominoes falling. Somewhere a woman began screaming the names of her children, over and over, a litany rather than a call. Dogs howled from every yard in the district at once, a chorus with no beginning and no end.

Astrid had grown up on stories of the night the wards failed. Every child in Greyharbor had. *Before the Salt Guild*, the grandmothers said, *the things behind the mountains came down to the sea whenever they pleased, and what they took, they kept.* Two hundred years of safety rested on the border wards in the passes — and the Guild's ledgers said the Guild paid for them.

*Consideration: see sealed schedule.*

Her hands were shaking. She made them stop, because shaking hands could not write, and whatever happened tonight, someone in this building was going to have to know exactly what the numbers said.

* * *

The Salt House sealed itself around her like a fist closing.

She heard it happen floor by floor: the bronze shutters dropping over the ground-floor windows with a boom that shivered the ink in her well; the chains going across the main doors; the ward-lamps in the stairwells flaring white as the building's own protections woke. Masters' voices in the corridor outside, quick and clipped. Master Vess saying, "Get the vault keys. Get the *keys*, man." Master Corvel's voice answering, low and even, and there was something in it Astrid had never heard in nine years of auditing his figures: fear.

They passed her door without looking in. In nine years, they had never once looked in. The counting room was where numbers lived, and numbers were beneath the masters' notice — which meant that when the masters sealed themselves into their sanctums, they left the ledgers alone with her.

Astrid took the lamp off her desk and went down into the archive.

The soul-ledger was kept on its own stand in the deepest alcove, chained to the shelf like a dangerous animal, and it was the only book in the Guild that was not written in the Guild's hand. The entries appeared on their own pages — ink surfacing out of the vellum like drowned things rising, in a spidery script no scribe would claim. Every apprentice learned what the book was for and learned, in the same breath, not to look at it. *Debts of the last resort,* old Master Quill had called it, tapping his nose, and had never said whose debts, or against whom.

The chain opened to her key. That was the first wrong thing — her key, a counting-room key, should not have fit the soul-ledger's lock, and the fact that it did told her the Guild had decided, somewhere above her head and years ago, that she would need access someday.

The second wrong thing was waiting on the open page.

New ink was surfacing even as she watched, spidering up through the vellum in that unclaimed hand, and Astrid read it upside down first, the way she read everything, before her mind allowed her to turn the book around.

**Received this night, at moonrise: one (1) soul, freely given, in settlement of the ward-schedule, tranche fourteen (14). Name: TAMMAS VELL. Trade: keeper of the eastern light. Term: perpetual.**

The lamp trembled in her grip and threw wild shadows across the alcove.

Tam. Tammas Vell, who had taught her her letters with a stick in harbor sand because her mother couldn't afford slate. Tam, who had signed her first indenture papers when she was nine years old and wept after, believing she hadn't seen. Tam, who walked the seawall every evening of his life checking the eastern light because he said somebody had to count what the sea took, and nobody else would do it.

*Freely given.*

She read the entry four times. The ink did not change. The date was today's date, in the calendar the temples used and the Guild pretended not to. At moonrise — twenty minutes ago, while she had been balancing columns upstairs, while the sky had been dying star by star — Tammas Vell's name had been entered into the book of sold souls, and the transaction had settled clean.

The wards had failed anyway.

That was the thought that broke her paralysis, that moved her from horror into something colder and more useful. Fourteen tranches. If each tranche had bought a season of protection, the arithmetic was simple and obscene: thirteen souls already spent, Tam the fourteenth, and the sky going black regardless. Either the trade had stopped working — or it had never worked the way the masters believed, and the *sealed schedule* was buying nothing but time for someone.

She looked at the ledger, and then at the shelf beside it, where the Guild seal sat in its cradle of lead — the great stamp of the crossed anchor and wave, the mark that made any paper it touched legally, magically true. Unattended. Because who would steal it? Who but a counting girl would even understand what it was worth?

Above her, boots thundered across the third floor. Someone shouted Corvel's name. The ward-lamps flickered — the Salt House's own protections guttering like the stars had guttered — and steadied.

Astrid Brightwater closed the soul-ledger, took it under her arm, lifted the seal from its cradle, and blew out her lamp.

In the dark of the archive, with the city screaming beyond the shutters and her mentor's name still wet on the page against her ribs, she felt the dread settle in her chest and harden, grain by grain, into something that was not fear at all. It felt almost like the moment a column finally adds. The terrible relief of knowing exactly how bad the numbers are.

"Freely given," she whispered to the dark. "We'll see."

Then she went to find the stairs that led down, past the vaults, to the drowned places beneath the harbor — because the ledgers had shown her where the Guild's money truly went, and Astrid Brightwater had never yet let a debt go unreconciled.
