## 3. What the Guild Owes

Tam Vess had spent forty years teaching apprentices that a ledger never lies, and he intended to spend his last free night proving it.

He sat in the counting room of the Salt Guild with the door barred and the lamps turned low, and he read the entry that bore his name. *Tammas Vess, ward-keeper of the third rank. One soul, whole and unencumbered. Received in payment for: one season's maintenance of the Greyharbor border wards.* The ink was still wet enough to shine. His own signature sat beneath it, steady as a mooring line, because he had written it that afternoon in the guild master's office while the man watched him with something that was almost pity.

Almost. Guild masters did not pity. They *accounted*.

Outside the shuttered windows, Greyharbor was screaming. He could hear it even through salt-thick glass — the bells of the temple district clanging out of rhythm, the raw animal sound of ten thousand people who had looked up at moonrise and seen the sky going dark star by star, like lamps being snuffed down a long corridor. The wards along the northern border had failed at dusk. Everyone knew what came next. Everyone remembered the last time, sixty years ago, when the things beyond the wards had come down to the coast like a tide that hated.

The guild masters had sealed their doors at the first bell. Tam had not been asked to leave. That, more than anything, told him what his life was worth now: exactly what was written in the ledger, no more.

He heard her boots on the archive stairs before he heard her voice. Of course he did. Astrid Brightwater climbed stairs the way she wrote figures — fast, precise, and louder than she thought she was.

"The doors are sealed," Tam called out, not turning from the ledger. "Guild order."

"Guild orders are for guild members." She came around the corner of the shelving with a lamp in one hand and her satchel over her shoulder, and her face stopped him cold. He had expected terror. Every face in Greyharbor tonight wore terror. But Astrid's fear had already burned down past panic into something harder, something with edges, and it was aimed — he realized with a lurch — at *him*.

"You signed," she said.

"Astrid—"

"I balance these books, Tam. I have balanced them since I was nine years old. I would have *seen* it." Her voice cracked on the last word, and he understood that the anger was grief wearing armor. "You didn't tell me. You let me shelve this very ledger yesterday and say goodnight to you and go home to my little room above the chandlery, and you knew that tomorrow your name would be in it."

"There wasn't time for a better goodbye." He closed the ledger gently, as if it were sleeping. "Sit down, child. If you're going to shout at me, do it sitting down. I'm an old man and I've had a long day of selling myself."

She didn't sit. She stood there trembling, lamp held high, and he thought: *I taught her that stance. Feet planted, chin level, ready to argue arithmetic with a god.* Pride and sorrow arrived together, twins, inseparable.

* * *

They talked until the small hours, because that was what they knew how to do. Words across a table, ink between them.

"It buys one season," Tam said. "One full season of wards, maintained at full strength, all borders. The Undercourt honors its terms — you know that better than anyone. It's the only thing about them that can be trusted absolutely."

"One season." Astrid's laugh had no humor in it anywhere. "And then?"

"And then the city finds another way. Or doesn't." He spread his hands. "I'm sixty-one years old, Astrid. My eyes are going. My hands shake when the weather turns. What is my soul worth to me, honestly? Another twenty years of squinting at ledgers? Whereas a season of wards is worth—" he gestured upward, toward the black sky, toward the screaming streets — "everything. Every child in the harbor district. Every fisherman. Every apprentice who will ever complain about my handwriting. It is the best trade I will ever make, and I have made trades my whole life, so believe me when I tell you I know a good price."

"That's not how souls work and you know it."

"How do they work, then? You're the archivist. Cite your sources."

"They don't get sold back, Tam! That's the whole point of the ledger. Nothing in it has ever come *out* of it. I've copied every page — don't look at me like that, yes, I copy the forbidden ones, someone in this building should know what we actually do — and in two hundred years of entries, the Undercourt has returned nothing. Not once. Not a borrowed year, not a bottled tide, nothing. You are not buying the city a season. You are paying yourself into a hole, and the city gets one season, and then the next old man sells himself, and then the next, and the guild calls it maintenance."

Tam was quiet for a long moment. The lamps guttered. Somewhere below them, the harbor water slapped against pilings in a rhythm that suddenly sounded like counting.

"You've been reading deeper than I thought," he said finally.

"I was reading deeper than you thought when I was twelve."

"Yes." He smiled, and it hurt. "Yes, you were. That was always the trouble with you, Astrid Brightwater. I gave you a life too small and you kept growing anyway."

"So don't do this. Come with me. There are other ways — Brannoc still knows the old ward-smithing, the kind that doesn't run on contracts. We could—"

"Brannoc is disgraced and drunk and lives under a wharf." Tam said it kindly. "And I am already signed. Child, listen to me now, because this is the important part, the part I need you to carry." He reached across the table and took her hand, and her fingers were cold as coin. "Do not follow me. Whatever happens at moonrise tomorrow, whatever ceremony they perform, however it ends — you stay above ground. You burn your copies, or better, you keep them and use them to tear this guild apart from inside, legally, with paper, the way you do everything. But you do not go down the Salt Stair. Promise me."

"Why?"

"Because I know you." His voice went rough. "Because you'll find some impossible clever thing to try, because you always do, and the Undercourt eats clever things. Because I did not sell my soul to save this city only to have it cost the city *you*. That would make my trade a bad one, and I refuse — I *refuse*, Astrid — to end my career with a loss on the books."

For a moment he thought she would promise. Her face did the thing it always did before surrender, the slight fold at the brow, the breath drawn through the nose.

Then she said, "I promise I won't follow you *tonight*."

"Astrid."

"That's the promise you get. Take it or leave it, because it's the truest one I can make, and you taught me never to sign what isn't true."

He left it. What else could he do? He had one night left, and he would not spend it watching her cry.

* * *

They spent it in the archive, of all places, because neither of them could bear the quiet of anywhere else. Tam pulled the old ledgers and read her the strange entries aloud — the bottled tides, the borrowed years, the winter the guild purchased twice — and Astrid corrected his pronunciation of the Old Court terms and argued with him about depreciation schedules for intangible assets, and for a few hours it was almost like any other night in forty years of nights.

Near dawn, she fell asleep at the table with her cheek on a volume of tide-contracts, and Tam sat looking at her and doing sums he could not put down.

When the first grey light came, he woke her, and she gathered her satchel, and at the door she stopped.

"Last chance to tell me where you'll be at moonrise," she said.

"Guild chapel. Don't come."

"Then I'll see you after." And she was gone down the stairs, quick and precise and loud, and Tam stood in the doorway listening until he couldn't hear her anymore, and then he went and barred the door behind her, because he was a fool, but he was not careless.

* * *

Moonrise found Tam Vess in the guild chapel wearing ceremonial white, and Astrid Brightwater was not there, which was the only mercy the day offered.

The guild masters performed the rite with brisk efficiency, the way they did everything. There was a vessel of grey glass. There was a sigil cut into the floor that hummed like a struck bell. There was a moment — he would remember this, he was almost certain he would remember this — when the master of ledgers hesitated over the final line and glanced up at Tam as though waiting for him to object.

Tam did not object. He had made his trade. A ledger never lies, and neither, he told himself, would he.

The vessel filled with light the color of deep water, and the world went quiet and far away, and the last thing his remaining senses registered was the sound of the great doors of the guild hall being unbarred from the outside — quickly, urgently, by someone who climbed stairs fast and precise and much too loud.

* * *

Astrid reached the chapel ninety seconds too late.

She stood in the doorway with the stolen ledger pressed against her ribs and a guild seal warm in her pocket, taken from the master of ledgers' own desk while he knelt at the ritual, and she watched the grey glass vessel being carried out on a cushion of black cloth like a relic, like a treasure, like a thing that had never had a name or a mother or a habit of humming while it worked.

The guild masters filed past her without seeing her. Servants, in their eyes. Furniture that could read.

Astrid Brightwater stood alone in the empty chapel, in the sigil-scarred circle where her mentor had stood, and she opened the ledger to the newest page and read the entry again, and then she did the thing she had been doing since she was nine years old whenever the numbers refused to make sense.

She looked for the error.

There. At the bottom of Tam's contract, in the clause describing what the payment purchased — *one season's maintenance of the Greyharbor border wards* — the term of enforcement was wrong. She had memorized the standard form years ago; she had copied it a hundred times. This contract ran the season *from the date of signing*, not from the date of delivery. A technicality. A rounding error. The kind of discrepancy no magistrate would ever notice and no guild master would ever bother to fix.

Unless, she thought slowly, heart beginning to hammer, unless someone brought it before judges who *could not lie* and could not abide a false term.

She looked down through the chapel floor, through stone and clay and drowned stair, toward the court beneath the mountain, and she felt the terror arrive on schedule and set itself aside like a coat she would need later.

"Hold on, Tam," she whispered to the vessel disappearing down the corridor. "I'm coming to audit."

She turned and went to find Brannoc, and the ledger rode against her ribs like a second heartbeat, and above the harbor the stars stayed dark.
