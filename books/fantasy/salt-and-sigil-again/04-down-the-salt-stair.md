## 4. Down the Salt Stair

Brannoc came for her at the turn of the tide, when Greyharbor's bells had stopped ringing and even the gulls had gone quiet, as if the whole city were holding its breath beneath a sky with no stars in it.

He was not what she expected. She had imagined a ward-smith — even a disgraced one — as some grand figure, robed and rune-scarred, trailing consequence behind him like smoke. Instead he was a broad, stooped man in an oilskin coat crusted with old salt, his hands wrapped in strips of leather stained black with whatever ink smiths used to draw wards into living stone. His beard was grey and cropped short. His eyes were the color of deep water, and they did not soften when they looked at her.

"You're the Brightwater girl," he said. Not a question.

"I'm the Brightwater auditor," Astrid said. "The ledger is mine to reconcile."

Something flickered across his face — not quite a smile. "That's what I heard. That you walked out of the guildhall with the soul-ledger under your arm and no one dared stop you." He turned without waiting for her answer. "Come on, then. The stair won't stay dry past slack water."

She followed him down through streets she barely recognized. Greyharbor by starless night was a city of shuttered lanterns; folk had learned in a single terrible evening that light drawn upward seemed to *call* to whatever had eaten the sky, and so the whole harbor district glowed only from below — from fish-oil lamps set low in doorways, from embers raked into hearth-pits, from the phosphorescent wrack the tide left along the quay like a warning written in someone else's handwriting.

"The wards went out at moonrise," Brannoc said as they walked. It wasn't small talk. He said it the way a surgeon describes a wound. "All of them. Border stones, harbor chains, the temple spire — everything fed from the guild's great sigil. Forty years I kept those stones alive. Forty years, and I never once asked where the power came from." His jaw worked. "I asked last month. That's why they struck my name from the rolls."

"What did you find?"

He didn't answer. He stopped instead at a squat customs house near the waterline, its door swollen shut with damp, and pressed his palm against a carved lintel that looked, to her eye, like nothing but wave-scroll decoration. The stone sighed. A seam opened in the floor, breathing out cold air that smelled of brine and something older — iron, maybe, or blood long dried into rock.

"The drowned stair," he said. "The guild calls it the Deep Receiving Room. They think that's funny."

* * *

The stair went down farther than any stair had a right to go.

It was cut from black basalt, each step worn concave by centuries of feet, and it descended through the harbor's foundations in a slow spiral, passing through strata of the city's history like a core sample: granite laid by the first masons, then older worked stone no guild record mentioned, then stone that had never been worked at all but simply *hollowed*, its walls rippled as if the mountain itself had once been soft and something had pressed through it.

Astrid counted steps out of habit. At three hundred she lost count because the dark changed.

It wasn't that lanterns appeared. It was that the darkness began to hold light the way deep water does — a dim, sourceless glow rising from below, blue-green and patient, until she could see Brannoc's shoulders ahead of her and the wet gleam of the walls and, far beneath them, the reason for the light.

Souls.

There was no other word for them, though the ledger used others: *assets*, *holdings*, *long-term deposits*. They hung in the flooded chamber at the stair's foot like lanterns hung out for a festival — hundreds of them, thousands, drifting in slow constellations through water that should have drowned them and did not. Each one was a soft flame the color of dawn seen through fog, and each one moved. Not randomly. With *gesture*. One flared and dimmed in a rhythm Astrid's ear caught as speech. Another drifted close to a neighbor and both brightened, the way two people lean together to share warmth.

"Oh," she said. The sound came out of her small and involuntary. "Oh, they're *alive*."

"They're aware," Brannoc corrected quietly. "Alive is a different thing. Alive is what they'd be if they were still in their bodies." He stood at the chamber's edge, hands hanging at his sides, and did not look at the lights. He looked at the floor. "I warded this room for eleven years. I thought I was keeping the sea out." His voice roughened. "I was keeping them in."

Astrid stepped forward before she could make herself afraid of it.

Up close, the nearest soul resolved into something almost like a face — not a face, but the *idea* of one, features suggested rather than fixed, the way a name suggests a person. It turned toward her. It had no eyes, and yet she felt seen, felt known down to the childhood signature drying at the bottom of a contract she'd been too young to read.

"Hello," she whispered, absurdly, and the soul brightened — a flare, quick and glad, like a candle catching a draft of good news.

Then she saw the vessels.

They lined the chamber walls in niches cut with terrible precision: glass-and-bronze reliquaries, each one holding a single soul pressed flat against the curve of the glass like a moth pinned to a board. The free-floating lights drifted and spoke and leaned together. The ones in the vessels could only press. Their light beat against the glass in slow, arrhythmic pulses — not speech, Astrid realized with a lurch. *Knocking.*

"How many?" Her voice came out steady. She was proud of that, later, and ashamed of it too.

Brannoc finally raised his eyes to the chamber. "When I warded it? Six hundred. Maybe seven hundred." He swallowed. "Count the niches now."

She counted. She couldn't help it; numbers were the one language she had never been afraid in. Niches ran the full circumference of the chamber, tier upon tier, and every niche held a vessel, and there were empty niches waiting beyond the filled ones, rows and rows of them, freshly cut, their edges still sharp —

— freshly cut. Someone had been expecting more.

"Four thousand," she said. "At least. And room for twice that."

"The border wards failed because the Undercourt called in arrears," Brannoc said. "Every year the guild pays, and every year the price goes up. Souls are the only coin they take." He gestured at the drifting lights, the knocking vessels, the hungry empty shelves. "This isn't a vault, Brightwater. It's a *harvest*. And the guild has been planting for two hundred years."

* * *

They went deeper.

Below the chamber the stair narrowed, and the water rose around their ankles — warm water, which was worse than cold, warm like something living. The souls' light fell away behind them, and new light rose ahead: a pale gold radiance seeping up from below, the color of lamplight through vellum, the color of a magistrate's seal.

"The antechambers," Brannoc murmured. "Court business happens above us, in the mortal world — contracts signed, debts recorded. But the *reckoning* happens down here. When the magistrates want to look at a debtor, they bring them down the salt stair." He glanced back at her, and in the gold light his deep-water eyes looked almost kind. "You're carrying the ledger?"

Astrid touched the satchel at her hip. Tam's name sat inside it, inked fresh, sold that very night. Her own name sat beside it, next due. "Every page."

"Good. Because they'll ask to see it. And listen to me—" He caught her arm, and his grip was strong as a mooring line. "The magistrates cannot lie. Whatever else they are, whatever they do to you, they cannot speak a falsehood. Use that. Make them say things. Make them say things *in front of witnesses*." He hesitated. "And if it goes wrong — if they take you — the tide-channel on the east wall runs back up to the harbor. My old workshop sits above it. Knock twice on the stone and I'll hear."

"Why?" she asked. "Why help me? They struck your name off the rolls. You owe the guild nothing."

Brannoc let go of her arm. For a moment he only looked at her, and then past her, back up the stair toward the chamber of drifting lights and knocking glass.

"Because I drew the wards that keep those vessels sealed," he said. "Eleven years, I drew them. Every line of my hand is holding one of those souls down." His voice dropped to almost nothing. "I'm not helping you steal them back, Brightwater. I'm trying to unwrite myself."

* * *

The stair ended at a doorway of white coral grown over black stone, and beyond it the antechamber opened like the inside of a shell — vast, ribbed, lit from nowhere, floored in sand so fine it took their footprints and erased them behind them as they walked, as if the court preferred that no one leave evidence of having arrived.

Astrid stood just inside the threshold and made herself look at all of it.

Columns of nacre. Ceilings lost in golden haze. And everywhere, everywhere, the *sound* — a vast, layered murmur like a reading room, like ten thousand voices reciting terms and conditions in their sleep. She understood, with a cold settling in her chest, that it was exactly that. Somewhere in these halls, every contract ever signed with the Undercourt was being read aloud, forever, by someone.

Somewhere in these halls, a child's unsteady signature was being recited right now. *Astrid Brightwater, aged nine years, in payment of her mother's debts—*

"Steady," Brannoc murmured.

"I'm steady."

"You're shaking."

"That's just the cold," she lied, and squared her satchel on her hip, and lifted her chin the way she did across a guild table when a master tried to bluff her about figures she'd already checked twice.

Ahead, through an archway of woven bone-white branches, she could see the first of them: a pale figure seated at a desk of driftwood and glass, its face smooth as an egg, its hands — its many-jointed, elegant hands — already turning toward her as if it had been waiting since before she was born.

Its mouth opened. What came out was her own voice, pitched perfectly, reciting her own words back to her:

*"I'm steady."*

A pause. Then, in a voice like paper turning:

*"Auditor Brightwater. Your appointment was made nine years ago. We are pleased you could finally attend."*

Behind her, Brannoc melted back into the dark of the stair. Ahead, the sand erased her footprints. Astrid Brightwater, who had spent her whole life balancing other people's impossible books, walked forward to be audited herself.
