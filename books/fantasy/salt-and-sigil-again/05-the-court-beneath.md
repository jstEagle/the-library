## 5. The Court Beneath

The antechambers ended where the light began.

Astrid had expected darkness at the bottom of the world — the drowned stair had prepared her for that much, mile after mile of black water and blacker stone with Brannoc's ward-lantern guttering ahead of her like a dying moth. But the Court Beneath did not need fire. It glowed from within, a vast cavern of pale coral architecture grown rather than built, every spire and archway lit by a soft, sourceless luminescence the color of moonlight through milk. It was beautiful. That was the first horror of it.

The second was the sound.

Souls flickered everywhere — she could see them now, not just in the antechamber niches but set into the walls themselves, thousands upon thousands of glass vessels no larger than her fist, each one holding a small restless light that pulsed in rhythms too human to be anything else. And each one whispered. Not words, exactly. More like the memory of words, the way a shell remembers the sea. The whole cavern murmured with the dead-and-not-dead, a tide of voices that never quite resolved into language.

Astrid pressed her palm flat against her satchel, feeling the ledger's spine through the leather, and made herself breathe.

*You are an auditor,* she told herself. *You are Guild of Salt, third rank, come to reconcile accounts before the quarterly reckoning.* She wore the guild seal on a chain at her throat — stolen from Master Corvel's own desk while he stood at his window watching the starless sky, so entranced by the dark that he never heard the drawer slide open. The seal was warm against her skin, warmer than metal should be, as if it knew it was being worn by a liar.

"Third rank auditors don't get an audience," Brannoc had warned her, back on the stair, in the last pocket of air where whispering couldn't reach them. "You'll have to claim one. Claim something they can't refuse."

"What can't the Undercourt refuse?"

"A debt," he'd said. "It's all they are. All they understand. Walk in owing nothing and asking about everything, and they'll eat you alive just to taste what you're hiding. But tell them you've come to *settle*, and they must hear you. It's in their nature. Maybe in their law. I never learned which, and I never dared ask."

Now Astrid walked alone down the avenue of vessels, boots silent on stone that felt faintly, horribly soft, like walking on the inside of something living. She counted doors out of habit — the ledger-keeper's reflex that had kept her sane through twenty years of other people's numbers — and stopped counting when she passed two hundred.

The magistrates were waiting in a chamber shaped like the inside of a nautilus, its walls spiraling up and up into gloom, and they were not what the guild's forbidden pages had promised. No horns. No teeth. No grandeur at all. They sat behind a crescent of white stone that might have been coral or might have been bone, and they looked like people who had been left in water too long: pale, smooth, their features softened as though someone had rubbed at them with a wet thumb until only the suggestion of faces remained. Their eyes were the darkest thing in the room. Dark as the sky over Greyharbor now.

Three of them. The center one spoke first, and its voice came from everywhere, from the walls, from the vessels, from Astrid's own chest.

"You are not third rank."

Her heart slammed once, hard, against her ribs. She had rehearsed this. She had rehearsed everything except being seen through in the first breath.

"I am whatever my seal says I am," she said, and lifted the chain so the guild sigil caught the pale light. "The Guild of Salt sends me to reconcile accounts before the reckoning. There are discrepancies in the ledger. Discrepancies are owed attention. Attention is owed payment. I am here to pay it."

Silence. The kind of silence that has weight, that presses on the eardrums like deep water.

"The guild sends no one," said the magistrate on the left, and its voice was different — older, slower, like something spoken through fathoms. "The guild sends *terms*. Terms written in ink we taught them to make. You are not ink."

"I am the hand that holds the pen." Astrid kept her voice level by an act of will she would remember, afterward, as the hardest thing she had ever done — harder than the stair, harder than the dark. "You cannot lie. Neither can I. Ask me what you wish."

She saw then, with a clarity that felt like falling, why the guild's founders had feared these creatures and coveted them in equal measure. They could not lie. But they did not need to. The center magistrate tilted its smoothed face, and when it spoke again, every word was true and every truth was a knife turned just enough to wound.

"You stole the seal."

"Yes."

"You stole the ledger."

"Yes."

"You intend to steal what the ledger records."

"No," said Astrid, and let the word hang there, trembling, until she could finish it honestly: "I intend to *reconcile*. What is reconciled is neither stolen nor kept. It is made correct."

The three magistrates regarded her. In the walls around the chamber, soul-vessels pulsed a little faster, as if the whispers had sharpened into listening.

"An auditor," said the right-hand one, and its voice was almost gentle, which was worst of all. "Come to make us correct. Come down the salt stair with salt-water still drying in the seams of her coat, carrying a book of names she has already read twice on the way, looking — " it paused, tasting the truth like wine — "looking for one name above all others."

"Tamsin Vael," Astrid said. "Journeyman ward-smith. Sold this night past. Show me his account."

*

They led her through the archive, because of course the Undercourt had an archive; of course it looked exactly like hers above, if hers had been grown instead of built, if its shelves had been ribs and its aisles had been gullet-dark corridors where the light came from the merchandise itself. Row upon row of vessels, sorted by some system she could not parse — not alphabet, not date, not price. Perhaps by regret. Perhaps by weight.

She found Tam on the four hundredth shelf of the ninth row, and she knew him instantly, the way she would have known his handwriting anywhere.

His vessel sat among hundreds of identical ones, and yet it was not identical at all. The light inside it burned amber-gold, warm as lamplight in a cold office, warm as the evening he'd found her at nine years old crying over her mother's debts and taught her double-entry bookkeeping with charcoal on the back of a fish-market flyer. The light moved the way Tam moved — quick, wry, leaning toward things. As she watched, it flared and dimmed, flared and dimmed, and Astrid understood with sick certainty that it was trying to speak.

"Tam," she whispered, and pressed two fingers to the glass.

The light leapt toward her touch like a dog hearing its name.

"He cannot hear you," said the left-hand magistrate, drifting at her shoulder without footsteps. "The vessels hold what was taken. Hearing went with the body. Wanting stayed. He wants very loudly. It is tiresome."

"What are the terms of his contract?" Astrid made herself ask it in auditor's language, flat and clean, because if she asked it any other way her voice would break. "Read them to me. All of them."

The magistrate extended one long pale hand, and the vessel rose from its shelf and floated to the space between them, turning slowly. Lines of script swam across its surface — the same impossible ink the guild used, silver-black, moving like something alive.

"'One soul, freely given,'" the magistrate recited, "'in exchange for the maintenance of the Greyharbor border wards for one season, such maintenance to be performed by the Guild of Salt through its lawful instruments—'"

"Stop." Astrid's stomach dropped through the floor of the world. "Say that again. *Through its lawful instruments.*"

"The guild wrote the terms," the magistrate said. "We do not write terms. We only keep accounts. The guild maintains the wards. We maintain the souls. Each keeps its side." A pause, delicate as a scalpel. "You believe this is a lie. It is not a lie. But you have found the shape of something. Auditors do. It is why we permit them."

*The guild lied about the terms.* Brannoc's voice, from the stair, from another lifetime: *the whole trade is theft repaid with theft.* Her mind raced forward faster than she could control it, down corridors of implication, and she forced it shut, forced her face still, because the magistrates read truth off people the way she read handwriting, and what she was planning could not be read yet. Not even by her.

"His term is one season," she said carefully. "When does it end?"

"When the season ends."

"When does the season end?"

The magistrate's smoothed face did something that was almost a smile. "Ask your ledger."

Her hands were steady. She noted that distantly, gratefully, the way she noted balanced columns. She opened the satchel, unbound the soul-ledger, and turned to the newest page — Tam's entry, the ink still fresh enough to gleam. And there, beneath his name, in the column reserved for *next due*, the guild's clerks had entered the following account. Of course they had. Ledgers always showed what came next. That was the entire point of ledgers.

**Brightwater, Astrid. One soul, freely given. Due at season's end.**

She had known. Some part of her had known since the night the stars went out, since she'd found her childhood contract in the archive and understood that her signature, small and careful in its nine-year-old loops, had been waiting in the guild's files for thirty years like a blade kept oiled. Knowing was different from reading. Reading made it real in the specific way that only ink makes things real.

"They signed me when I was nine," she heard herself say. "My mother's debts. Is a child's signature a freely given thing?"

"It is a signature," said the magistrate. "We keep accounts, not judgments."

"But you honor the terms as written." Her heart was pounding so hard she could feel it in her throat, in her teeth, and beneath the terror something else was rising, something bright and reckless and familiar — the same exhilaration she'd felt at fifteen, balancing a ledger everyone said couldn't be balanced, finding the error no one else could see. Terror sharpened by audacity. Audacity sharpened by terror. "Terms as written. All of them. Every clause. Every instrument. Every party bound to every line."

"All of them," the magistrate agreed. "That is what keeping means."

"Then I have a discrepancy to report," Astrid said, and closed the ledger, and smiled at the pale thing with the dark eyes, and marveled somewhere far away at how steady her voice sounded coming out of a body this frightened. "But not to you. Not yet. I'll need the full court. Every magistrate. Every witness-vessel in the chamber. Because a correction this large — " she tapped the ledger once, lightly, like knocking on a door — "this large, you'll want to watch me make."

The three of them looked at her for a long moment. In the walls, ten thousand small lights flickered, and flickered, and listened.

"The court convenes," said the center magistrate, "at the turn of the tide."

"How long is that?"

"For you," it said, with terrible gentleness, "one night. Your name comes due at season's end, auditor. But seasons end quickly, beneath the mountain."

It turned, and the others turned with it, and their voices trailed back to her down the ribbed corridor like water draining away:

"Rest, if you can. The vessels will keep you company. They are very good at wanting."

Astrid stood alone in the archive with Tam's amber light burning beside her cheek, close enough to warm her skin, and took out her pen, and her ink, and the little book of her own sigils that no one in the world had ever seen.

One night. One court. One chance to write the error that would unmake everything.

She uncapped the ink.
