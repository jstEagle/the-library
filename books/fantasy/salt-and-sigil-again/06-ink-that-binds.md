## 6. Ink That Binds

The workshop smelled of copper and old rain, and Brannoc had not opened its shutters in three days.

He had been a ward-smith once. The word still fit him like a coat cut for a broader man — he could wear it, but everyone could see the seams. Eleven years since the Guild had struck his name from the rolls, eleven years since he'd stood before the masters and told them the truth about the harbor wards: that they were eating something. That no lattice of salt and silver drew power from nothing, and whatever fed them had a price, and the price was being paid by someone who hadn't agreed to pay it.

They had called it heresy then. They had called it ingratitude. They had taken his tools and his seal and given him a stipend to keep quiet, which was the most insulting part — as if his conscience had a rate, like fish.

Now the stars were out, all right. Out of the sky entirely, snuffed like candles pinched between wet fingers, and the whole city knew what he'd known for eleven years: the wards were failing because the food had run short.

Brannoc sat at his bench with the founding charter of the Salt Guild unrolled before him, weighted at the corners with four ingots he'd never been able to sell. He had stolen it from the archive two nights ago, walking through streets gone mad with lantern-light and prayer, past the guildhall where the masters had sealed themselves behind doors thick enough to stop a siege. Nobody stopped a bent old man with a satchel. Fear made people blind to anything but their own doors.

The charter was beautiful. That was the obscenity of it. Vellum the color of cream, ink mixed with ground pearl, the founding sigil drawn at the head of the page in lines so fine they seemed less written than grown. He had copied this sigil a hundred times in his working life, into lintels and keel-boards and the great anchor-stones beneath the harbor wall. Every apprentice learned it before they learned their letters. *The Sigil of Surety*, the manuals called it. The mark that held.

He had spent three days doing what he'd been too proud or too frightened to do in thirty years of craft: reading it. Not using it. Reading it.

Ward-sigils were grammar. Any smith worth his salts could parse one the way a priest parsed scripture — subject, binding, object, term. And the Sigil of Surety, read honestly, line by line, said something no manual had ever admitted aloud.

It had an owner's mark.

Not a maker's mark — those were humble things, initials and dates, pride scratched small. This was different. It sat inside the first curve of the sigil like a hook set in flesh: a spiral coiled around a vertical stroke, and off the spiral, seven teeth. Brannoc knew every heraldic device on the coast, every merchant flag from here to the ice-straits, and he did not know this one. But he knew its *style*. It was not human work. Human hands hesitated. Human hands corrected. This line ran without a single tremor from beginning to end, the way water runs, the way nothing drawn by a living wrist ever quite does.

And beneath the owner's mark, in the sigil's deep structure — the part apprentices never saw because they were taught to copy, not to read — was a clause. Brannoc had translated it twice, once in the old trade-tongue and once in plain Greyharbor speech, and both times it came out the same:

*Rendered unto the mark's true holder: all that is bound thereby, in perpetuity, plus interest.*

Plus interest.

He sat back and looked at the ceiling beams for a long time.

The guild had not invented the Sigil of Surety. The guild had *taken* it. Somewhere in the founding years, some clever, desperate man — Aldous Brightwater's name was on the charter, first among the nine — had gotten hold of a mark that belonged to something else, something below, and used it to bind the tides and the borders and the luck of Greyharbor to the city's service. And the thing below had let them. Had waited, patient as tide, while the interest compounded. And when the debt came due, the guild had discovered what every debtor discovers: that you do not haggle with your creditor. You pay.

Bottled tides. Borrowed years. And now souls, entered neat as spice shipments in a ledger Astrid Brightwater kept balanced to the copper.

*Theft repaid with theft.* That was the whole architecture of it. The guild stole the sigil; the Undercourt collected the guild's debts in the only coin the sigil could legally render — whatever was bound by it. And what was bound by it, if you read the founding terms honestly, was not the sea. It was everything the sigil touched. Every warded door. Every blessed keel. Every person born inside the border-lattice who had never signed a thing in their life and never been asked.

Brannoc thought of the harbor children playing on the breakwater, and his hands went cold.

* * *

He worked through the second night because grief was useless and purpose was not.

If the sigil was stolen goods, then the contracts built upon it were built on sand — no. Worse than sand. Built on another man's foundation, and the other man had come to collect. But there was a crack in it. There was always a crack; that was the first law of ward-craft, the law they taught you before they taught you anything else: *every binding has a flaw, and the flaw is always honesty.* A ward holds only what it truthfully claims to hold. Lie in the terms and the whole lattice goes hollow.

The Undercourt's magistrates, if half the drowned-men's stories were true, could not lie. Which meant their law could not abide a lie either. Which meant a contract containing a false term was not merely broken in their eyes — it was *void*, root and branch, and every contract of its kind with it.

But proving a false term required more than argument. It required a mark the court would recognize as legitimate — a counter-sigil, an auditor's stamp, something that could stand inside their law and say *this line is false* in a language the deep things respected. You could not shout truth into the dark in trade-tongue and expect the dark to care.

So Brannoc did the thing he had sworn never to do again. He took up his tools.

His hands remembered. That was the terrible mercy of it — eleven years of mending nets and soldering lamp-brackets, and still his fingers found the graver's grip like a dog finding home. He worked on a scrap of the same vellum, grinding pearl into ink by candlelight, and he did not copy the Sigil of Surety. He inverted it. He took the owner's mark — the spiral, the seven teeth — and he turned it inside out, so that instead of claiming ownership it declared *inspection*: a mark that existed only to look, to weigh, to find the false line. An auditor's seal, forged in the grammar of the court itself.

Around dawn, gray light seeping under the shutters, he finished it and held it up, and the little spiral seemed to turn slowly even though the paper was still, and he felt watched, and he made himself not flinch.

Then came the harder problem: getting it down.

* * *

There was a tide-channel beneath his workshop. Everyone in the low district had one; the old builders had run the city's cellars into the harbor's veins like roots into soil. Brannoc's had been bricked over since the year he lost his name, but brick was brick and he was a smith, and by midmorning he had a hole in it big enough to kneel beside.

The channel breathed cold salt air up at him. Far down in the dark, water moved with a sound like slow applause.

He wrote the message small, because the messenger was small. He had caught it that morning in the eel-traps off the pier — a channel-wisp, one of the pale blind fish-things that swam the drowned ways between the harbor and whatever lay beneath it, the ones the old wives said carried gossip between the crabs. Superstition, the guild had always said. But the guild also said the wards fed on nothing, and the stars had believed them right up until they went out.

On a sliver of oiled silk, in letters finer than thread, Brannoc wrote:

*To the auditor below, if there is one. The founding sigil is stolen property. Its true holder's mark is the spiral-and-seven-teeth. I have made a counter-seal that reads as inspection, not ownership. False term voids all. Find the girl with the ledger. Her name is Astrid Brightwater. Tell her: the flaw is honesty. — B., who owes.*

He wrapped the silk around a sliver of cork, bound it with wax, and held it a moment over the black water.

Eleven years of silence pressed on him — every petition unfiled, every warning swallowed, every night he'd taken the guild's stipend and told himself a man had to eat. He had watched them feed the wards and said nothing loud enough. Now a girl who balanced their books was somewhere under the mountain, alone, armed with nothing but arithmetic and nerve, and the only help he could give her was a fish and a prayer.

*The flaw is honesty,* he thought. *Gods, let her be honest enough to use it.*

He dropped the message into the channel. The water took it without a ripple, the way deep water takes everything, and the wisp followed it down, pale as a fingernail, until the dark closed over it like a ledger closing.

Brannoc knelt there a long time, listening to the slow applause of the tide.

Then he got up, banked his fire, and began grinding more pearl. If the counter-seal worked — if she could get word back up — she would need more than one. She would need enough to void a ledger.

Above the workshop, through the shutter-cracks, the starless sky hung over Greyharbor like a lid. Below, the water carried his name downward, and for the first time in eleven years, Brannoc slept without dreaming of doors.
