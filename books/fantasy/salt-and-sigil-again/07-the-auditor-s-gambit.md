## 7. The Auditor's Gambit

The antechamber of the Undercourt smelled of cold iron and old rain — the smell of a cellar that had never seen sun and never wanted to. Astrid stood before the pale magistrates with the stolen ledger pressed against her ribs like a second heartbeat, and she made herself think in columns.

Debits. Credits. Terms. That was the language they spoke down here. That was the only language they were permitted to speak.

There were nine of them, seated in a crescent of basalt thrones that seemed grown rather than carved, their bodies long and grey as drowned rope. They had no faces in the way mortals understood faces — only smooth expanses where features should have been, and yet she felt their attention settle on her like wet cloth. They could not lie. Brannoc's message, sent down through the tide-channel in a fisherman's cipher, had been explicit on that point: *they cannot lie, but they will let you lie to yourself, and they will charge you for every word.*

"State your purpose," said the magistrate at the crescent's center. Its voice was the sound a ledger makes when a page turns.

Astrid bowed — not deeply. An auditor did not grovel; an auditor *arrived*. "I am Astrid Brightwater, reconciliation clerk to the Salt Guild of Greyharbor. The guild has dispatched me to review its accounts with this court." Every word was true, which mattered, because the magistrates would taste falsehood the way she tasted brine. She had learned, in three days of preparation in the dark of the salt stair, to build sentences that were technically honest and wholly misleading. It was, she realized, the guild's native tongue. She had simply never been permitted to speak it before.

"The guild," she continued, "suspects irregularities."

That was even true. The guild suspected nothing, but *she* suspected everything, and she was the guild's hand extended.

The magistrates did not confer. Perhaps they could not; perhaps conferring implied doubt, and doubt implied a gap between what was known and what was said, and gaps were lies waiting to be born. The center magistrate spoke again.

"The Salt Guild's contracts are sound. Each term was spoken aloud, witnessed, and sealed. No clause is false."

*No clause is false.* Astrid turned the words over like coins bitten for gold. They believed it. That was the miracle and the trap both — the magistrates had accepted the guild's papers for two hundred years because the guild's founding sigil had been *theirs*, stolen or traded away long ago by some ancestor with expensive tastes and cheap morals, and a contract sealed with a court's own mark carried the court's own certainty. The papers were clean because the seal said they were clean. The seal said so because the seal was the seal.

It was the most elegant circular fraud she had ever seen, and she had spent her childhood balancing books full of bottled tides.

"Then," Astrid said, and here was the abyss, opening beneath her feet like a well with no bottom, "there can be no objection to a formal audit."

Silence. The kind of silence that had weight, that pressed on her shoulders like deep water. Somewhere behind the thrones, in the dark beyond the candlelight, souls flickered — hundreds of them, thousands, vessels of glass and bone and stranger materials, each one burning with the small stubborn light of a person who had been sold. Tam was out there somewhere. She had found his vessel yesterday: a squat jar of green sea-glass, his light inside it the color of lamplight through whiskey, steady and warm and horribly patient. He had always been patient. It was the worst thing about him.

"What manner of audit?" asked the center magistrate.

"A correction," Astrid said. "In the mortal world, when ledgers disagree, we do not burn them. We reconcile them. I propose a covenant of verification: I will examine each contract between the guild and this court, term by term. If every term proves true, the contracts stand, and the guild's obligations are confirmed until the last star gutters." She paused, letting the concession land. Magistrates loved concessions. Concessions made a bargain feel won.

"And if a term proves false?"

"Then the fault is systemic, not singular. A ledger with one false entry cannot be trusted in any entry. If any single contract contains a false term — any term, in any contract, however small — then all contracts are void. All debts discharged. All vessels returned."

She heard her own heart. She was astonished the magistrates could not hear it too, that it didn't echo off the basalt like a drum.

"You ask us to wager two centuries of holdings against the guild's good faith," said a magistrate at the crescent's end, its voice like water finding a crack in stone.

"I ask you to wager them against your own seal," Astrid corrected. "You told me no clause is false. If that is true, you risk nothing. If it is false—" she spread her hands, "—then you have already been robbed, and I am offering you the thief's estate."

Another silence, longer. In it, Astrid thought about her mother, who had signed nothing and been signed away regardless; about the debt-contract in the Greyharbor archive with her own childish handwriting at the bottom, the letters wobbling where her hand had shaken, *Astrid Brightwater, aged nine years*, promising a soul she hadn't understood she owned to pay debts she hadn't understood her mother owed. She thought about how the guild's clerks had filed it under *M* for *minor instruments*, as if she were a flute.

"We accept," said the center magistrate.

Astrid kept her face still. Inside, something screamed — half terror, half the wild singing joy of a gambler watching the dice leave her fingers, tumbling end over end above an abyss, knowing she had weighted them.

"One condition," the magistrate added, and her blood went cold. "The audit proceeds under our observation. You will read each contract aloud, term by term, in this chamber. Nothing leaves the court."

*Nothing leaves the court.* Which meant she could not carry the contracts back to the stair and work in secret. Which meant every forgery, every planted discrepancy, would have to happen here, under the eyeless gaze of creatures who could smell a lie like blood in water.

"Agreed," said Astrid, and discovered she was smiling. It felt strange on her face, like wearing someone else's hat.

* * *

They gave her a cell of a room off the antechamber: a slab of stone for a desk, a stool, a lamp that burned without oil and cast shadows that moved slightly wrong. The contracts came to her in armfuls, borne by things that might once have been clerks — grey, stooped shapes with ink-stained fingers and no eyes at all, only smooth skin where eyes belonged. Two hundred years of bargains. Bottled tides. Borrowed years. First snows. Last words. Names. Souls. So many souls.

She worked through the first night reading terms aloud while a magistrate observed from the doorway, motionless as a reef. *"One season of ward-light, renewed annually, in consideration of the vessel designated Tammas Vael, lamp-keeper, age fifty-one."* Her voice did not shake. She was proud of that, later, in the way one is proud of surviving a storm one has sailed directly into.

And in the margins of her mind, in the space between the spoken terms, she forged.

Her sigil — the one she had invented, alone, in the archive at sixteen, a private shorthand for *this number does not reconcile* — was a small thing. Three strokes and a hook, like a gull's footprint in sand. But Brannoc's message had taught her what it could become. The guild's founding sigil was the Undercourt's own mark, turned inside out, and inside-out marks were mirrors, and mirrors showed whatever stood before them. If she wrote her little gull-track into the witness line of a contract — not over the original ink, but *under* it, in the ghost-layer where intent lived — the mirror-seal would reflect it. Would read it as part of itself. Would certify a discrepancy as though the court itself had written it.

She could not lie to the magistrates. But she could make the paper lie, and the paper was the court's own voice.

The first one took four hours. A contract from 1741, trading a fisherman's luck for thirty years of calm waters — luck being the vaguest commodity on any ledger, and vague commodities had vague boundaries. She wrote the gull-track into the margin of the boundary clause, small as a flea, and read the term aloud with her pulse hammering in her ears: *"Calm waters within the harbor's mouth."*

The observing magistrate tilted its smooth head. For one endless moment Astrid was certain it would say *false*, certain the whole gambit would collapse here, in a cell lit by a lamp that burned wrong.

"The term is recorded as spoken," the magistrate said at last, and moved on.

Astrid exhaled very slowly, the way Tam had taught her to bleed air when diving the kelp-rows. *Slow out, girl. Panic spends breath faster than work does.*

By the third night she had planted seven discrepancies. By the fifth, nineteen. False boundaries. False durations. A contract that promised "the firstborn's blessing" where the original negotiation, she found in the guild's duplicate files smuggled down in her coat lining, had said "the firstborn's *labor*" — a difference worth a soul, if the magistrates ever looked. She was learning the shape of the guild's sloppiness, two centuries of clerks assuming no one would ever audit the auditors. There was rot everywhere. She hardly needed to forge anything; she mostly needed to *point*.

But pointing wasn't enough. The covenant required a false term to void *all* contracts, and the magistrates would judge each term as it stood in the final instrument — and the final instruments were sealed with the stolen sigil, which certified everything clean. Unless the mirror reflected something else. Unless her gull-tracks, threaded through forty contracts now, wove together into a pattern the seal could not ignore.

She was stitching a net, she realized, on the sixth night, her fingers cramped and ink-black to the wrist. Stitch by stitch, discrepancy by discrepancy, a web of small falsities that would catch the great one. The guild had built its empire on the assumption that no one would count. She was counting.

On the seventh night, the eyeless clerk brought her the last folder, and her hands went still.

It was thin. One page. Her own name at the top in the guild-master's upright hand: *Brightwater, Astrid — final payment due, ward-renewal, autumn tide.* And below it, the terms, already drafted, already witnessed, awaiting only her signature and the court's seal.

She read them twice. Then a third time, because the third time she noticed the clause tucked at the bottom, in letters barely larger than dust:

*"Vessel to be surrendered alive."*

Not after death. Not at the natural end of a long life, as the guild had told its signers for two hundred years. Alive. The wards did not run on departed souls. They ran on living ones, fed slowly, season by season, the way a lamp feeds on oil.

Tam's green-glass jar flickered in her memory, steady and warm and patient. How many seasons had he been burning? How much of him was left?

The magistrate in the doorway shifted, the first movement it had made in seven nights.

"The final account," it said. "Do you find the terms in order, auditor?"

Astrid looked down at the page with her name on it, at the clause that proved the guild had lied to everyone, about everything, since the beginning — proof she hadn't forged, proof the guild had written itself, proof sitting right here in the court's own archive.

She picked up her pen.

"The terms," she said, dipping the nib, "require closer examination."

And beneath the witness line, in ink so small it might have been a shadow, she drew the first stroke of her gull-track — just as the lamp guttered, and every soul-vessel in the dark beyond the door began, all at once, to sing.
