## 8. Tam's Bargain

There was no pain in the vessel. That was the first lie he had to unlearn.

Tam Vess had expected fire, or cold, or the long dark of drowning. Instead there was light — a warm, amber, honeyed light that pressed against him like a mother's hand, and a silence so complete it felt like being held. The guild's brokers had described the trade in careful terms: *your soul, surrendered; your body, yours until it fails; the city, warded one season more.* They had not mentioned that it would feel like this. Like being kept. Like being *wanted*.

That was how he knew something was wrong. Nothing in Greyharbor wanted anything without a price.

He drifted in the amber and tried to count the days. There was no sun here, but his soul remembered rhythm — the tide of his own heartbeat, slowed now to something tidal, patient. He counted heartbeats into hours, hours into what might have been days. Around him, far off in the glow, other lights hung suspended: hundreds of them, thousands, each one a soft gold seed in the dark. Souls. He could feel them faintly, the way you feel other sleepers breathing in a shared room. Some pulsed quick and frightened. Some had gone dim and slow, worn down by whatever the Undercourt did with what it bought.

He thought of Astrid and his chest — or whatever a soul used for a chest — ached.

*Don't follow me,* he had told her. *Promise me, girl.* She had looked at him with those ledger-gray eyes of hers, the ones that could find an error in ten thousand entries, and she had said, "I promise," in the exact tone she used when she intended to break her word. He'd known her since she was nine years old, copying columns in the guild hall with ink on her knuckles. He should have demanded a better promise. He should have known better than to ask for one at all.

He spent his counted hours doing what he had always done: auditing. If he was going to be a lamp in some pale magistrate's collection, he would at least understand the accounts. He reached out along the edges of himself, testing the vessel's walls the way he'd once tested a suspect contract — gently, thoroughly, looking for the flaw.

The walls were flawless. That was the second thing that frightened him. Guild work was never flawless. Guild work was shortcuts dressed in ceremony. A vessel this perfect hadn't been made by the Salt Guild at all. It had been made *for* them. Which meant the guild wasn't the seller here. It was the middleman. And middlemen, Tam knew from forty years of ledgers, always took their cut from both ends.

*One season of wards,* they had promised him. One season, and then his body would be released to rest, and the city would have time to raise new wards honestly. He had signed because he was old and tired and because the alternative was watching Greyharbor's border-fires gutter out while the things beyond the dark came down to the water. He had signed because Astrid's name was already in their books and he would not let them take her too.

But if the vessels were Undercourt work, then the terms were Undercourt terms. And the Undercourt did not sell seasons. The Undercourt sold *forever*, and let the buyer believe otherwise.

He turned the thought over in the amber light, and the light seemed to press closer, soothing, warm, saying without words: *rest, you've earned rest, the city is safe, you are safe.* And that was the third lie, the sweetest one, and he clung to his suspicion the way a drowning man clings to a spar — not because it will save him, but because letting go is worse.

Then the message came.

It arrived as light does through water — bent, broken, arriving all at once. A thread of silver slid through the amber dark and coiled around his vessel like a tendril of frost, and where it touched, the warmth *stung*. Tam flinched from it, and then, because he was Tam Vess, forty years a guild clerk, he read it.

It was written in no script he knew. The characters were angular, salt-white, wrong-angled — sigils stacked on sigils, a grammar invented rather than inherited. But beneath them, in a hand he would know anywhere, in the small fierce letters she'd used since childhood, ran words in plain Common:

*Tam. The wards failed the night you signed. All of them. Moonrise, every star out. Whatever they told you your soul was buying — it was already spent. The guild has been feeding souls to the court for years to keep its own sigil lit. Yours didn't buy a season. It bought nothing. I'm coming. Don't argue. — A.*

He read it eleven times.

The amber light surged against him, gentle, insistent, wrapping the message's edges in warmth, whispering its old sweet arithmetic: *the city stands, doesn't it? The stars came back, didn't they? Your sacrifice mattered. Rest.*

And Tam Vess, who had taught a nine-year-old girl to check every figure twice, finally did the sum he had been avoiding.

If the wards had failed the night he signed — if the sky had gone black over Greyharbor while his soul crossed the counter — then his purchase had never held anything. Either the guild had lied about the timing, or the guild had lied about the mechanism, or the guild had never controlled the wards at all. Three possibilities, and every one of them meant the same thing: he had given up everything real about himself for a fiction written in a broker's careful hand.

No. Not quite the same thing. There was a fourth possibility, and it sat in his gut like a swallowed stone: that the wards had failed *because* the guild's power was failing, because the stolen thing at the heart of it was starving, and his soul — and Astrid's name waiting behind his — was food. Not payment. Food.

The warmth pressed against him. *Rest,* it said. *You are safe. You are home.*

"This isn't home," Tam said aloud, and his voice made a sound in the amber, a small crack like ice on a pond. "This is a larder."

The light shuddered. Just slightly. Just enough.

He laughed — a dry, rusted sound, the laugh of a man who has found the error at last, on the final page, after everyone told him the book was clean. Forty years of balancing impossible trades, and the greatest fraud in the guild's history had him filed under *assets*.

Around him, in the deep glow, the other souls stirred. Faintly. The way sleepers stir when one voice in the house says something true.

* * *

They came for him after that. He felt them before he saw them — a change in the pressure of the dark, a attention turning toward him like a lighthouse beam. The magistrates' servants, if servants was the word: tall shapes of gray glass and tide-water, faces smooth as sea-worn stone. They gathered outside his vessel and regarded him, and though they had no mouths he heard them the way he heard the message, directly, without air:

*The vessel is disturbed. The asset is distressed. Distress spoils the contents.*

"I'm not an asset," Tam said. His voice was stronger now. Anger will do that. "I'm a witness. There's a difference, and the difference is the whole point of me."

*Assets do not speak.*

"Then your bookkeeping is already wrong." He pressed his awareness against the inner wall, and where the silver thread had touched it, he found the scar — hairline-thin, a seam of foreign script burned into the vessel's perfect surface. Her sigil. Her invented, impossible, unauthorized sigil. The guild had taught her that contracts were fixed things, eternal things, things written once in blood and binding. And she had gone and written something *new*, which meant the law itself could be written anew, which meant —

Which meant nothing was fixed. Which meant even here, even now, the terms could be contested.

"You put me in glass," Tam told the gray shapes, "but you filed me under a contract. And a contract, gentlemen, is a story two parties agree to tell. I am done agreeing."

*The contract was signed willingly,* they said, and their voices had the flat certainty of entries in a ledger. *Willing signature is binding.*

"Willing, yes." He smiled, and if souls can smile, his did — thin and sharp as a paper knife. "Signed willingly, in good faith, on stated terms. And the terms were false. So ask yourselves, and ask your magistrates, who learned forgery from whom?"

The gray shapes went still. Tide-water has no expression, but he felt it anyway — the flicker of a machine encountering an entry it cannot reconcile. They withdrew, slowly, the way clerks withdraw to consult a senior partner, and Tam was left alone in the amber with the scar of her sigil glowing faintly on the wall beside him like a lit window.

He put his awareness against it and kept it there. Holding the door, in his way. Standing watch at the one small breach in the vault, the way he had stood watch over her desk when she was small and fell asleep among the ledgers, covering her with his coat so the masters wouldn't see and mark her down as idle.

She was coming. Of course she was coming. She had promised not to, which in Astrid Brightwater's private grammar had always meant *yes*.

* * *

Time passed, or the amber passed, which amounted to the same thing. Tam counted heartbeats and waited, and in the waiting he did the hardest work of all: he grieved.

Because the truth he had fought so hard not to add up was not only about the guild. It was about him. He had wanted the bargain to be clean. He had wanted to be the kind of man whose sacrifice actually purchased something — one season, one wall, one winter of safety for the city that raised him. The lie had been comfortable. The lie had made him noble. Strip it away, and what remained was an old clerk who had thrown away his one life on a fraud, and the shame of that was colder than any vessel.

But then — and this came slower, the way dawn comes in winter — another thought followed the shame down.

If the terms were false, then the contract was voidable. And if the contract was voidable, then he was not spent. He was *recoverable*. Somewhere above him, in the cold honest wind, his body still stood or lay or waited, and somewhere below him a girl with ink on her knuckles was walking into the pale magistrates' hall with a forged discrepancy in her sleeve and forty years of his teaching in her spine.

*I taught her that no entry is final,* he thought. *Let her teach the court.*

And when the summons came — and it came as he knew it must, a tolling through the amber like a bell through deep water, all the vessels trembling, all the souls rising on a tide not their own toward the great hall where the pale magistrates sat in judgment of their own accounts — Tam Vess did not resist. He rose with the others, a dim gold seed among thousands, carried up through the dark toward the light of the court.

But as he rose, he felt the scar on his vessel, her sigil, her handwriting, and he pressed himself against it one last time and spoke into it, not knowing if words traveled down a thread of frost, but saying them anyway, the way you say things at gravesides:

"Astrid. When they ask me, I'll tell them everything. Every term they broke, every lie they wrote, every soul they fed into the dark and called it warding. You get them into the hall, girl. I'll burn the books from the inside."

The amber parted above him. The court opened like a wound full of candlelight, and the pale magistrates turned their faceless heads toward the rising tide of souls — and toward one vessel among thousands that glowed at its seam with a small, stubborn, salt-white light that did not belong to any of them.

The leading magistrate extended a hand of gray glass toward Tam's vessel, to receive it, to file it, to close the account.

And inside the glass, calm as a clerk presenting evidence, Tam Vess began to speak.
