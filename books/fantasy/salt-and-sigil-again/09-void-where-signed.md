## 9. Void Where Signed

The Undercourt did not have a ceiling. It had a sky — a vault of black stone so high that the lantern-souls drifting through it looked like a drowned firmament, each one a small green-gold star burning in its glass. Astrid stood at the center of the Court of Accounts with the guild's ledger open on her arms and her heart beating somewhere up around her throat.

The magistrates had come in their full number for this: nine pale figures robed in grey that moved like water, seated on thrones of fused salt, their faces smooth as unmarked wax. They could not lie. That was the one mercy of them. Everything they said was true, and everything true they said like a knife laid flat on a table, waiting to be picked up.

"The auditor of the Salt Guild," said the first magistrate, whose voice arrived without passing through air. "You have presented yourself for final reconciliation."

"I have." Astrid's voice held. She was almost proud of it. "Before this court convenes its closing session, I invoke the Rite of Correction. If any contract under audit contains a false term, all contracts sharing the founding sigil are void."

The silence that followed had weight. The lantern-souls overhead dimmed, as if they too were holding their breath.

"You understand what you offer," said the second magistrate. "If your audit fails — if the guild's papers are clean — the correction falls upon *you*. Your name stands next on the ledger. It would be entered tonight, and no rite would ever be invoked again."

"I understand the terms," Astrid said. "I wrote them myself. In your own law-language. You accepted them at moonrise; you cannot unaccept them now."

"We cannot lie," the first magistrate agreed, and there was something in the flatness of it that might have been, in another creature, amusement. "Proceed."

She opened the ledger.

* * *

She had spent three days in the antechambers forging discrepancies, working by the light of traded souls she could not yet free, Brannoc's tide-carried instructions spread beside her like a surgeon's chart. The guild's founding sigil — the great interlocked knot that sealed every contract, every ward, every trade — had been stolen from the Undercourt itself, centuries ago, by the guild's founders. And because it was stolen, it carried a flaw the thieves had never noticed: the knot's binding stroke crossed itself once, in the lower left quadrant, where no honest sigil ever crosses itself. A lie built into the geometry of power.

Astrid's own invention — the small closed loop she'd drafted in the margins of ledgers since she was nineteen, the one she'd called, privately, *the honest mark* — fit into that flaw like a key into a lock. Wherever she drew it, the false crossing became visible. Became *provable*. Became, in the language of the court, a discrepancy.

Now she turned the pages one by one before the nine thrones, and where her sigil sat hidden in the margin flourishes, the black stone of the court began to weep lines of white light.

"A contract for bottled tides," she read. "Term the third states the tide was freely given by the sea. The sea does not give. The sea was bound. Discrepancy."

"Discrepancy," the magistrates answered as one, and the page in her hands went soft as wet paper.

"A contract for borrowed years. Term the seventh states the years were surplus to the seller's life. No life has surplus years. Every year was taken from a living span. Discrepancy."

"Discrepancy." Another page dissolved into salt-smoke.

She worked down the ledger the way she had once worked through the guild's books in the archive above — patient, exact, relentless — except now every line she struck was a soul set loose. She could feel them going out of the vessels in the antechambers, feel the light change in the vault above as lantern after lantern guttered and rose, rose and vanished upward through stone toward the world where bodies waited. Somewhere in the dark behind her, Tam stood watching from his vessel, and she did not let herself look at him yet. Not yet. If she looked at him now she would rush, and rushing was how auditors died.

The magistrates watched her strike their accounts and did not stop her. That was the terrible thing about them, she thought. They wanted truth more than they wanted profit. Truth was the only currency they had never learned to counterfeit.

At last only two pages remained. The master contract — the founding trade between the Salt Guild and the Undercourt, signed four hundred years ago in ink mixed with blood — and beneath it, fresh as a wound, the page bearing her own name.

Astrid Brightwater. Final payment due.

She set the master contract on the reading-stone and drew her sigil across the founding knot, large, unhidden, deliberate.

The court went white.

* * *

Above Greyharbor, in the guildhall where the masters had sealed themselves behind triple wards, the great sigil burned into the foundation stone screamed.

They felt it as cold first — the wards going grey, then transparent, then simply *gone*, four hundred years of purchased protection unraveling in the space of nine heartbeats. Master Corvane, who had signed Tam's sale with a steady hand, stood up from his chair and found he could not remember why he had ever been afraid of the dark, and then remembered everything, all at once, every soul the ledger held, and sat back down again very slowly.

By morning the guildhall doors stood open. By noon the masters were walking the harbor district barefoot, as the old penance required, while the wardless poor they had spent generations pricing watched from doorways and said nothing at all. The Salt Guild's seal cracked in its setting above the customs house and fell into the sea, and the sea, which had never been asked, took it without comment.

But Astrid saw none of this. Astrid was standing in a court of salt and shadow, holding the last page, watching nine faces of unmarked wax turn toward her like moons.

"The master contract is void," said the first magistrate. "All contracts sharing its sigil are void. The trade is ended. The debt—" and here the voice paused, and Astrid understood with a lurch that even these creatures could hesitate over a truth, "—the debt named on the final page is not discharged. It is merely *unowned*. There is no longer a guild to collect it. There is no longer a court empowered to hold it. The name Astrid Brightwater belongs to no ledger anywhere in creation."

"Then I'll take my name back," she said, and tore the page in half.

It came apart easily, the way paper comes apart when nothing is holding it together anymore. The pieces dissolved before they reached the floor. Something in her chest that had been clenched since she was seven years old — some small cold fist of obligation signed in a child's careful hand — opened, and did not close again.

The magistrates rose. The thrones of salt crumbled to powder. "The court is concluded," they said, all nine voices fraying into one. "We were owed honesty. We were paid in theft. We are... finished." And they went out like candles, not one after another but all together, and the black vault overhead softened, and the lantern-souls still rising through it were suddenly just lights in a large dark room, going home.

* * *

Tam's vessel stood in the third antechamber, on a shelf of drowned granite, glass fogged from within. Astrid lifted it with both hands. It was lighter than she expected. She had spent her whole life believing souls must weigh something — that was what the guild's whole arithmetic rested on, weight and payment and balance — and here was proof they weighed nothing at all. All that mass had been the *keeping*, not the kept.

The seal broke at her thumb.

He came back into himself on the stair, halfway between the Undercourt and the harbor, where Brannoc had laid his body with a ward-smith's care and sat waiting beside it through the long dark, refusing to say aloud what they were both afraid of. When Tam breathed, Brannoc made a sound Astrid had never heard him make before, low and cracked, and gripped the old man's shoulder hard enough to bruise.

Astrid knelt in the seawater on the steps and put her hands on either side of Tam's face, and his eyes opened, and they were his eyes, tired and wry and entirely his own.

"You weren't supposed to follow me," he rasped.

"You knew I would."

"I counted on it," he said, and coughed, and laughed, and the laugh cost him something and he paid it gladly. "The wards, Astrid. The city—"

"The stars are coming back." She looked up through the shaft of the stair, where far above, past the harbor water, the first honest light was beginning to show. "Dim ones. Cheap ones. Ours."

His face did something complicated then — relief and grief in the same motion, because he had given up everything to buy one season, and the season had been bought instead with truth, and truth had cost the guild its power and cost the city its certainty and cost him, he would learn in time, forty years of memory he'd sold along with the rest. He knew already. She could see him counting what was missing.

"It was worth it," he said, testing the words. "Even now. Knowing what it cost. It was worth it to have bought you all a season."

"No," Astrid said gently. "It wasn't worth it. Nothing was worth it. That's the whole lesson, Tam. That's what I came down here to learn." She helped him sit up against the stair. "We don't buy seasons anymore. We build them."

He looked at her for a long moment — at the ink stains on her fingers, the salt in her hair, the ledger-less emptiness of her arms — and then he nodded, slow, like a man agreeing to terms he finally understood.

* * *

They climbed out of the Salt Stair an hour before dawn, into a Greyharbor gone strange and quiet. The wards were down. Everyone could feel it — the nakedness of the streets, the openness of the sky — and instead of panic there was something else abroad in the harbor district, something Astrid had no ledger-entry for. People were standing in their doorways looking up. Old women were pointing. Children who had never been allowed outside after dark were running down the lanes shouting names of stars they couldn't possibly know, guessing, inventing, getting them wrong and not caring.

The stars came back the way Brannoc had promised they would: dimmer, fewer, honest. Not the guild's purchased firmament with its guaranteed constellations, but the real sky, ragged and imperfect and free. Somewhere up there, she thought, were the souls that had risen ahead of them, finding whatever sky souls find when no one owns them.

Brannoc stood at the top of the stair with the tide dripping off him, staring east. "Wards can be rebuilt," he said. "Volunteer work. Slow. Ugly. Nobody paid, nobody bound." He glanced at her sideways. "I'll need scribes. Honest ones. Ones who can draft a sigil that doesn't lie."

Astrid looked down at her hands — the hands that had balanced impossible books, copied forbidden pages, forged a court into ruin with a mark of her own invention — and felt, for the first time in her life, that they were empty in the good way. Ready.

"I know where to find one," she said.

Behind them, deep beneath the harbor, the last light of the Undercourt went out, and the sea closed over the Salt Stair like a page being turned.
