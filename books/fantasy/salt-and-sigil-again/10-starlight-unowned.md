## 10. Starlight, Unowned

The first star came back on a Tuesday.

Astrid knew it was a Tuesday because she was in the archive — what remained of it, the guild's great vaulted hall now stripped of its seals and its soul-ledger and its terrible tidy order — cataloguing the last of the impossible trades. Bottled tides. Borrowed years. A jar of someone's grandmother's laughter, purchased in 1141 and never once opened. She had the ledger open across her knees, her pen moving in the small precise hand that had once signed away her future, when the light through the high windows changed.

Not brightened. *Changed.* It went from the flat grey of a wardless sky to something with grain in it, something with texture, and she looked up and through the broken glass of the clerestory she saw it: a single point of silver, low over the eastern water, trembling like a needle finding north.

She stood so fast the ledger slid to the floor.

By the time she reached the harbor steps, there were hundreds of people already there — fishwives and rope-makers and the temple orphans who had nowhere else to go, all of them craning their necks at a sky that had been black as spilled ink for nineteen days. The star did not blaze. It was not the fierce jewel-throwing star of the old songs. It was small, and it flickered, and half the time you thought you'd imagined it.

Nobody cared. A woman near Astrid began to laugh and then to weep and then to do both at once, and an old man took off his hat, and down the length of the quay people were lighting lamps anyway, pointlessly, gloriously, because a sky with one honest star in it deserved candles.

"They're coming back," Brannoc said beside her. He had a splint on two fingers and ash still worked into the creases of his knuckles from the night the guild's sigil shattered above them all. "Slow. Like embers catching. The Undercourt released what it held, but the sky's been starved a long time. It'll take seasons before they burn properly again."

"Dimmer," Astrid said.

"Honest." He shrugged. "I'd take honest over brilliant. Brilliant was bought."

That was the phrase that spread through Greyharbor in the weeks that followed, scrawled on walls and stitched into banners and eventually painted, in Astrid's own hand at the request of no one and everyone, over the bricked-up doors of the Salt Guildhall: *DIMMER BUT HONEST.*

* * *

The wards came back too, but not the way they'd been.

The old wards had been contracts — lines of power drawn in stolen sigils, fed by souls on a ledger, maintained by a guild that had spent three hundred years quietly selling its city's people to the pale magistrates beneath the mountain. When the magistrates voided every contract, the wards died with them, and for nineteen days Greyharbor had lain open to the sea-things and the dark-things and whatever else lived past the borders where the stars had gone out.

What replaced them was uglier and better. Volunteers. That was all. Fishermen who knew the old shore-songs their grandmothers sang without knowing why. Temple novices who'd fled the temple when they learned what the temple had known and kept quiet about. Children who chalked protective marks on doorsteps because it made them feel brave, and whose chalk marks turned out, when Brannoc tested them, to hold — weakly, imperfectly, but *honestly*, because they were given freely by people who expected nothing back.

"It shouldn't work," Brannoc said, the fourth week, standing in the rain on the western wall while a twelve-year-old girl redrew a boundary line with a bucket of lime-wash and total confidence. "Wards need power. Power needs price. That's the whole architecture of the art."

"Maybe that was only ever the architecture of theft," Astrid said. "You said it yourself. The founding sigil was stolen. Everything built on it was built crooked. Maybe straight-built things hold differently."

He looked at her for a long moment, rain running off his chin. "You know what the masters would say."

"The masters are ruined." Three of them had fled the city. One had drowned himself in the harbor rather than face the crowds. The rest sat in the temple under guard, writing out confessions in their own hands, which Astrid considered a fitting punishment for men who had spent their lives making other people sign things. "Let them say it to each other."

* * *

She burned her debt-page on a windless evening in the archive, alone, because some things you should not have witnesses for.

It was a small page. That was the thing that struck her, holding it up to the lamp after all these years. Small paper, cheap rag-stock, the kind the guild used for receipts. On it, in a child's careful hand — hers, aged seven, guided by a guild clerk who had told her she was signing for a doll — the contract that bound her mother's debts to Astrid's soul. Her mother had died believing the debts were paid. They had been paid. Just not with coin.

Astrid read it through one last time. Every clause. Every term. She had memorized it long ago, but she read it anyway, the way you visit a grave.

Then she touched the flame of the lamp to the corner and held it until the fire reached her fingers, and dropped it into the brazier, and watched seven years old curl and blacken and go up as smoke.

She had expected to feel free. What she felt instead was stranger: a kind of spaciousness, like a room in her chest she hadn't known was furnished with fear. She sat down on the floor of the archive, in the dust of the guild that had owned her, and laughed until she cried, and cried until she laughed, and when it was done she wiped her face, picked up her pen, and got back to work.

Because there was so much work. That was the other thing nobody warned you about freedom. It was not an ending. It was a beginning with no instructions attached.

* * *

The school opened on the first morning the sky showed more than ten stars.

It was not much to look at. A warehouse on Cordwainer Row in the harbor district, rented with money Astrid had — to the lasting scandal of what remained of polite society — claimed from the guild's liquidated assets, on the grounds that the guild had stolen it from the harbor district in the first place and she was merely reconciling the account. The floor was warped. The windows were the ones Tam broke escaping the guild seals the night he sold himself, and she refused to have them fixed, because Tam said the cracks looked like lightning and lightning was lucky.

Forty-one students came the first day. Then sixty. Then a hundred and twenty, and Astrid stopped counting.

They were not the sort of students any academy in Greyharbor would have taken. Dockhands learning letters so they could read their own wage-contracts and know when they were being cheated. Fishwives studying arithmetic. A one-eyed smuggler with a beautiful hand who wanted, he said, to go legitimate, and whom Astrid hired on the spot to teach penmanship because his loops were gorgeous and she chose to believe him. Orphans from the temple, sent by the novices. And always, always, children — children taught to write their own names first, before anything else, because Astrid remembered what it was to be seven years old and handed a pen by someone who meant to use your name against you.

"Rule one," she told every new class, standing on a crate because the lectern had collapsed in week two and she'd decided she liked the crate. "Your signature is yours. Not your master's. Not your guild's. Not your mother's creditor's. If anyone asks you to sign something, you read every word, and if they won't let you read every word, you don't sign, and if they insist—" she would hold up the little iron stylus she wore on a cord around her neck now, the one with her own invented sigil cut into it, the one that had unmade the Undercourt's contracts and shattered a three-hundred-year theft, "—you come find me, and we will discuss it at length."

Tam taught on Thursdays. His soul had come back to him on the salt stair, cold and confused and furious with her for disobeying him, and he had gotten over the fury in roughly the time it took to climb back up to daylight. He was thinner than he'd been. There were nights, Astrid knew, when he woke reaching for the vessel, dreaming he was still a lantern in a pale magistrate's antechamber. But he taught Thursdays, contracts and their dissection, and he was merciless, and his students adored him, and once — only once, late, grading papers by lamplight — he said to her, "I meant it, you know. When I begged you not to follow me."

"I know."

"I was wrong."

"I know that too," Astrid said, and did not look up from her ledger, because some truths are kinder delivered sideways.

* * *

Winter came, and the stars kept returning, a few more each clear night, dim as banked coals but steady. The volunteers' wards held through the first storm of the season — barely, badly, patched with chalk and song and stubbornness — and held through the second, and by the third the whole city had stopped speaking of them as temporary measures and started speaking of them as simply *the wards*, ours, the ones we make.

On the last evening of the year, Astrid climbed to the roof of the school with a cup of hot cider and found Brannoc already there, sitting against the chimney with his splinted fingers finally healed crooked.

"Any regrets?" he asked. "Temple offered you a position again. I heard. Master of Archives. Your name restored, all sins forgiven, provided you stop teaching dockhands."

"They want the ledgers back," Astrid said. "All that knowledge, locked up where only the right people can be harmed by it."

"And?"

"And I told them the archive is open to anyone who can write their name." She sipped her cider. Below them, through the school's cracked windows, lamplight moved — the evening class, the smuggler's beautiful penmanship, a child sounding out her own signature syllable by syllable like a spell, which it was. "They called me a radical."

"What did you say?"

"I said yes."

Brannoc laughed, a short bark of steam into the cold air. Above the harbor the stars were coming out one by one, and Astrid counted them out of habit — eleven, fourteen, nineteen — and then stopped counting, because the number didn't matter. What mattered was that they were there, unowned, unbought, burning on no one's ledger.

Then, far out past the breakwater, where the drowned stair ran down into the dark, the sea lit green.

Not storm-light. Not the phosphor of fish. A column of pale radiance rising from deep water, structured, deliberate, and within it — unmistakable even at this distance — shapes like figures, tall and thin, arranged in ranks.

Brannoc went very still. "Tell me that's a reflection."

Astrid set down her cup. Around her neck, the iron stylus had begun, very softly, to hum.

"No," she said. "That's the court. And they're coming up."
