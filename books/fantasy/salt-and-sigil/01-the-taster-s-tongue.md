## 1. The Taster's Tongue

The soup arrived at half past nine, as it always did, borne on a silver salver by a boy whose hands shook only slightly less than the lid.

Marguerite Sterling stood at his station beside the king's high table with the posture of a man who had long ago stopped expecting to die and had not yet decided what to do about it. He wore the crown's colors — white and gold, the livery of trust — though nothing about him had been trusted in years except his tongue. That was the arrangement. The crown owned his life; he had sold it at nineteen for his mother's debts and a title no one used. In exchange, he tasted everything that passed the king's lips, and if it killed him, well. The crown would simply buy another.

He lifted the spoon.

The soup was a pheasant broth, saffron-gold, threaded with leeks cut fine as secrets. It steamed against his face, and beneath the steam he caught it — faint, green, the smell of a meadow after rain, of parsley gone to seed, of something sweeter underneath that did not belong in any kitchen in Vellamare. Hemlock wore its perfume like an apology. *I am only a flower,* it said. *I grew by the roadside. I meant no harm.*

He let the broth touch his tongue and held it there, three seconds, four, the way he'd trained himself to do — long enough for the poison to speak, short enough that it could not finish the sentence. The hemlock announced itself the way it always did: a creeping numbness at the root of the tongue, a softness spreading toward the throat like frost climbing a windowpane. In another man it would be terror. In Marguerite it was arithmetic. *Dose sufficient to stop a heart of one hundred sixty pounds within the hour,* he noted, somewhere behind his eyes where he kept such figures the way a miser keeps coins. *Prepared by someone who knows their craft. Not the kitchen boy. Someone with access to the herb closet and a reason.*

He spat into the silver basin — a gesture so practiced it had become almost courteous — and said, in a voice pitched to carry exactly as far as the king's ear and no farther:

"Hemlock, Your Majesty. In the broth."

The hall went still the way a pond goes still when a heron lands. King Aldous the Third, seventy-one years old and determined to be immortal through sheer stubbornness, set down his fork and regarded his soup with the expression of a man betrayed by a mistress.

"Again," the king said.

"The third time this season, sire." Marguerite set down the spoon with a small precise click. "Someone in your household is either very persistent or very bad at learning."

It was meant as dry humor. It landed as accusation, which was also intended, though Marguerite would have denied it under torture — and given his profession, torture remained a live possibility on most days.

The king waved a hand, and the guards moved, and the kitchen boy was dragged out weeping innocence, and the morning proceeded in the manner of all court mornings: a petition from the wool merchants' guild, a dispute over fishing rights on the Serpent's Tooth river, a visiting duke who laughed too loudly at jokes that were not funny. Marguerite tasted the king's wine, his bread, the honeyed figs he took at midmorning, the small cup of melted snow-water he believed — against all evidence — kept his humors balanced. Nothing else was poisoned. The hemlock had been a single note played in an otherwise quiet composition, which told Marguerite more than a symphony would have. A single note means a specific target, a specific day, a specific reason.

At noon the Duke of Cairnwell arrived to present his accounts of the eastern provinces, and the king, in a fit of generosity or performance, invited him to share the royal table. A second taster — a nervous young man named Pell who had held the post for eleven weeks and prayed every night to be promoted anywhere else — stepped forward for the duke's portion.

Marguerite moved first.

There was no rule that said he must taste for anyone but the king. There was no reward for what he did next. But he had watched the duke eat, and he had seen the way the man's serving-woman's eyes flicked to the gravy boat and away again, quick as a fish turning, and some part of Marguerite — the part that kept accounts even when no ledger was open — had already done the sum.

He took the duke's plate, dipped the tip of his smallest finger into the gravy, and touched it to his tongue.

Aconite. Monkshood. The wolf's-bane, the little blue flower that grew in the castle gardens because the head gardener thought it pretty and no one had ever corrected him. Faster than hemlock. Less forgiving. It bloomed on the tongue like a bell tolling — cold, then hot, then a strange metallic singing that climbed toward the jaw.

In another man, that dose would begin killing him now. His heart would stumble, stagger, forget its work. His limbs would go to pins and needles and then to nothing. He would die badly, in front of two hundred courtiers, and the last thing he'd hear would be someone shouting for a physician who would arrive too late and bill the estate anyway.

Marguerite swallowed.

Nothing happened.

That was the horror of it, if he let himself look at it directly, which he made a discipline of not doing before sundown. Nothing happened. The aconite rang its bell inside him and no one answered. His body had been dosed so long, so patiently, so methodically — seven years of measured poisons taken nightly in his own rooms, each one a little stronger than the last, until his blood became a fortress and his organs a garrison that had forgotten how to fall — that death itself seemed to have struck his name from its rolls. He was, by every law of nature, a dead man walking around in his own skin, and the skin declined to notice.

He set down the duke's plate. "Your Grace," he said mildly, "you may wish to dine elsewhere today."

The duke went pale, then gray, then the color of old candle wax, and was escorted from the hall under guard, and the serving-woman vanished before anyone thought to look for her, and by evening she would be three counties away or floating in the harbor, and Marguerite would never learn which, because that was how these stories always ended: not with justice but with subtraction.

What Marguerite felt, standing in the great hall while the court murmured around him like wind through wheat, was nothing at all.

That was the part he did not tell anyone. Not the physicians who studied him with hungry eyes. Not the spymaster who paid him a private stipend for reports on the kitchens. Not the priest who heard his confession twice a year and had long since run out of sins to assign penance for. None of them knew that the poison had done its work too thoroughly — that it had numbed not only his tongue but everything downstream of it. He hadn't felt warmth in four years. Cold reached him as information, not sensation. When his father died, he had wept once, briefly, mechanically, the way a clock strikes the hour whether or not anyone is listening, and then the grief had drained away like water through sand, leaving him standing in a room that smelled of lilies and feeling precisely nothing.

His body was a ledger, and every entry had been paid in feeling.

* * *

The Gilded Ledger kept its counting-house off Cordwainer's Row, behind a chandler's shop that never seemed to sell candles and a door that required you to knock in the rhythm of your own name.

Marguerite arrived at midnight, as he did six nights in seven, still wearing the smell of the palace on his clothes — tallow, saffron, the ghost of hemlock — and let himself down the narrow stair into the counting-room, where the lamps burned low and green and the air tasted of salt and old paper.

The Gilded Ledger was not a guild of thieves. Thieves were honest by comparison. The Ledger traded in things that had no business being traded: borrowed years, smuggled names, doors between realms, debts owed by the living to the drowned. Its brokers dealt in contracts written in ink that dried black and stayed wet. Its vaults held collateral that could not be weighed — a childhood, a shadow, the last words of a dying woman, forty years of rain promised to a kingdom that had since turned desert. And someone had to keep the books.

That someone was Marguerite.

By day he was the crown's taster, a man whose body belonged to the throne. By night he was the Ledger's accountant, a man whose mind belonged to the books, and between the two of them they had left very little of Marguerite Sterling for Marguerite Sterling. He had come to the guild the same way he came to everything — through debt, through need, through the particular desperation of a man who has discovered that his one talent is surviving things that should kill him. The Ledger had found that talent fascinating. The Ledger found many things fascinating. Fascination, in the Ledger's grammar, was a synonym for appetite.

He lit the lamp over the master ledger and opened it to the current folio, and began, as he did every night, to balance the impossible.

*One year, borrowed by the widow Anselm against her son's majority. Interest accruing. Due at his wedding.*

*Three names, smuggled across the salt marsh border for the family Verreault, fleeing a debt of honor. Names to be returned when the debt is paid or the bearers die, whichever comes first.*

*A door, leased quarterly, between the cellar of the House of Coins and a market street in the realm beneath the marshes. Rent payable in —*

He stopped.

Rent payable in years. Forty years, drawn annually, remitted to the deep folk of the sunken realm as payment under a covenant older than the guild itself — the treaty that bound the human court above to the drowned realm below, brokered by the Ledger's founders when both kingdoms stood on the edge of ruin. He knew the clause by heart. He had copied it a hundred times. *And in each year of the covenant's life, the upper realm shall render unto the lower forty years of stored life, taken gladly from those who hoard it, that the waters may stay sweet and the drowned may keep their names.*

He turned back one page. Then another. Then another, faster, his finger running down columns that had been steady as a heartbeat for as long as he'd kept them.

The entries stopped.

Not faded. Not smudged. *Stopped* — cleanly, deliberately, the way a throat is cut. Eleven years ago, the annual payments ceased, and no entry recorded why. No default was noted. No contract amended. No new terms negotiated. Someone had simply reached into the oldest book in the guild and closed an account that was still open, and the balance — he ran the calculation twice, three times, his stylus scratching in the lamplight — the outstanding balance stood at four hundred and forty years, plus interest compounded in the currency of the deep, which was memory.

Below the final entry, in a hand he did not recognize, someone had written a single line:

*The account is not closed. It is hidden.*

Marguerite sat back in the dark and listened to the building settle around him, beams ticking as they cooled, and felt — distantly, through layers of numbness thick as wool — something that might once have been fear.

He was the Ledger's accountant. He knew what happened to people who noticed things in these books. They were corrected, the way errors were corrected: quietly, completely, without remainder.

And yet the line remained. Whoever wrote it had wanted it found. Wanted it found by whoever balanced these books at midnight, alone, six nights in seven.

Wanted it found by him.

He looked at the entry again, and the lamp guttered, and somewhere above him — through the floorboards, through the chandler's shop that never sold candles — came the sound of the street door opening, though the guild received no visitors at this hour, and footsteps crossing the floor overhead, slow and deliberate, and stopping directly above the counting-room, directly above his chair, and waiting.

Marguerite closed the ledger, and blew out the lamp, and sat in the dark with his hand on the book, doing the only arithmetic that mattered: the distance to the stair, the weight of the door, and the number of seconds it would take a man who could not feel fear to decide, anyway, to be afraid.
