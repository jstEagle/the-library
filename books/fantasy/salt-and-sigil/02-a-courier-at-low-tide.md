## 2. A Courier at Low Tide

The footsteps above him did not come down.

That was the first strange mercy of the night: the weight on the floorboards shifted once, twice, like a man settling in to wait out a vigil, and then went still. Marguerite sat in the dark of the counting-room with his palm flat against the ledger's cover and counted his own pulse the way he counted everything — patiently, without alarm, because alarm had been dosed out of him years ago along with the hemlock and the arsenic and the seven other poisons whose names he kept the way other men kept saints.

One hundred and forty seconds. Then the footsteps withdrew, unhurried, and the street door opened and shut, and the chandler's shop above was silent again.

He did not decide to be relieved. Relief required a body that agreed with itself about things. He simply noted that his hands were steady, as they always were, and that this steadiness had begun to feel less like composure and more like a kind of death he hadn't yet gotten around to grieving.

He relit the lamp and reopened the ledger.

* * *

The Gilded Ledger kept its accounts in three columns, though only two were ever written down. The first column held what was owed. The second held what was paid. The third column existed only in the mind of whoever balanced the books, and it held the difference — the residue, the interest, the small cruelties that accumulated in any honest arithmetic between what people wanted and what they could bear to give.

Marguerite had balanced the guild's books for six years. He had entered borrowed years and their repayment schedules; he had recorded the smuggling of names across borders that did not officially exist; he had priced doors between realms by the inch of their thresholds. He had never once asked where the years came from, or who had owned the names before the guild sold them onward, because asking questions was a form of tasting, and he had learned at court that a taster who flinched at the first flavor would never survive to the second course.

But tonight, working by lamplight through the accounts of the salt-marsh trade — the old contracts, the ones so settled no one touched them — he found an entry that made even his unflinching hand pause above the page.

*To the Realm Beneath the Brine, per covenant: forty measures of years, rendered quarterly.*

The entry was dated sixty-three years back. It ran for twenty-two years, quarter after quarter, ink brown with age, each payment countersigned in a hand that started firm and grew shakier and then stopped altogether.

After that, nothing. Twenty-two years of payments, and then a blank stretch of four decades in which the drowned realm had apparently received nothing at all — and yet the ledger showed no breach declared, no penalty assessed, no contract voided. The account simply went quiet, the way an account goes quiet when someone very powerful has asked it to.

Marguerite read the entry three times. His body reported nothing: no chill, no quickening, none of the animal weather that ordinary men felt in their chests. But his mind, which was the one part of him still permitted to work, turned the page over as a tongue turns over a suspect morsel, and found beneath the blandness of the ink something that tasted wrong.

Someone had erased forty years of debt. And the ledger had let them.

He was still staring at the blank space when the smell reached him.

Salt first — real salt, sea-salt, not the marsh-rot salt of the streets outside — and under it blood, and under *that* something older, like weed left to dry on a hull, like the inside of a shell. Marguerite's nose had been trained past the limits of ordinary noses; it was the one sense his masters had never thought to poison dull. He knew before he turned around that someone was dying in the doorway behind him.

* * *

The man came apart at the threshold, the way wet paper comes apart.

He had been tall once. Now he folded, knee then shoulder then hand, catching himself against the doorframe hard enough to crack the plaster, and slid down it into a heap on the counting-room floor. He was dressed as a courier — oilskin coat, satchel strapped crosswise — but the coat was crusted white to the shoulders, stiff with dried brine, as if he had swum here from somewhere very far away, or been carried by water that wanted him delivered.

Marguerite rose without hurrying. Hurrying was for men whose bodies told them to hurry.

"Who sent you?" he asked, and heard how flat his own voice was, how much it sounded like the ledger.

The courier looked up. His eyes were the worst thing about him — not wild, not afraid, but *sanded*, as if whatever had lived behind them had been scoured away and something else had written its name there instead. Salt water ran from his hairline into his collar. When he spoke, his voice came out in pieces, like a tide going out over shingle.

"You keep... the accounts."

"I keep the accounts."

"Then you'll know." The man dragged his hand off the floor and caught Marguerite's wrist with a grip that should have been weak and was not, that burned cold all the way up Marguerite's arm like a bracelet of winter. "You'll know what it means. What it *costs*. Tell me you know."

"I know the ledgers," Marguerite said carefully. "All of them."

"The old one." The grip tightened. Blood was coming up through the man's lips now, black in the lamplight, and where it struck the floorboards it hissed faintly, the way spittle hisses on a hot iron. "The covenant. Between the crown and—" A cough took him, whole-body, wracking. "It broke. Do you hear me? It didn't lapse. It didn't fade. Someone *broke* it, and they've been feeding on the gap ever since, and now the gap is widening, and it will take both realms down, the dry one too, the dry one last—"

"Slowly," Marguerite said. Not out of kindness. Out of the taster's habit: get the whole mouthful before you judge it.

"There's no slowly left." The courier fumbled at his satchel with fingers gone gray as drowned flesh. He pulled out a roll of oilcloth, tied with tarred string, and pressed it into Marguerite's free hand, folding Marguerite's fingers closed over it with his own. The map — Marguerite knew it was a map before he saw it, knew it by the weight and the stiffness and the way the salt crust crackled — was warm. That was the detail his mind snagged on later, in the terrible quiet afterward: warm, like something living had been wrapped around it for a long time. "Take it to the water. Take it to the reeds at Grayharbor, at low tide, and give it to whoever comes for it. They'll know you're coming. They've known for forty years."

"Forty years," Marguerite repeated. The number sat in his mouth beside the blank space in the ledger, and the two fit together with a click he felt in his teeth.

"They've been waiting for the debt to come due." The courier smiled, and it was the worst smile Marguerite had ever seen, because it was peaceful. "You balance the books, yes? Then balance this one. Tell them we paid. Tell them we paid *everything*."

And then the light went out of him — not gradually, not like a lamp guttering, but all at once, the way a wave withdraws, leaving the sand bare. The grip on Marguerite's wrist loosened finger by finger. The sanded eyes fixed on the middle distance, on some door only he could see, and stopped seeing it.

Marguerite knelt beside the body for a long moment, holding the oilcloth roll, listening to the blood hiss itself cool on the boards.

His heart beat at its usual rate. His breathing did not change. This was the horror of him, the quiet horror he had made his peace with long ago or told himself he had: that a man had died gripping his arm with a message that smelled of the end of worlds, and Marguerite's body received the news the way a ledger receives an entry — accurately, and without trembling.

But his mind. His mind was doing something new.

His mind was afraid.

* * *

He heard the enforcers before he saw them, which was the only reason he survived the next five minutes.

They came down the stair without bothering to soften their steps — three sets of boots, and the particular creak of oiled leather that meant the guild's grey-coats, the ones who collected debts that could not be collected in coin. Marguerite had time to do exactly three things, and he did them in the order his new fear dictated rather than the order his training would have chosen.

He rolled the courier onto his side, arranged the satchel strap, and stepped back. He set the lamp so its light fell away from the corner where he stood. And he slipped the oilcloth roll inside his shirt, against his skin, where it lay warm as a second heartbeat.

Then the door opened, and Vess Corane came in first.

She was the guild's senior collector, a narrow woman with a face like a folded letter, and she had never once spoken to Marguerite except about figures. Her eyes went to the body, then to Marguerite, then to the body again, and her hand rested on the hilt at her belt with the ease of long ownership.

"Well," she said. "Low tide brings everything in."

"He was dead when he reached the bottom of the stair," Marguerite said.

It was the first lie of his life. He noted it the way he noted flavors: precisely, completely, with a part of his mind already cataloguing the aftertaste. It tasted of copper and of something sweeter underneath, something almost like freedom, and he understood in that instant why men became liars, and why kingdoms fell to them, and why the guild's third column existed at all.

"Was he." Vess crossed the room and crouched by the courier. She did not touch him. She looked at the salt crust, at the black blood, at the sanded eyes, and something moved behind her face that Marguerite had never seen there before, and would spend the rest of his life identifying as fear. "Did he say anything?"

"He was past words."

Another lie. The second one came easier. That frightened him more than the first — no. Nothing frightened him, not in the body, not in the place where fright was supposed to live. But it *registered*, the way poison registers on a tongue trained past feeling: a wrongness noted, filed, awaiting consequences.

Vess stood. She looked at him for a long moment, and Marguerite met her eyes with the perfect blankness of a man who has tasted worse than suspicion and swallowed it without comment.

"Burn the coat," she said finally. "Scour the floor. And Marguerite—" She paused at the door, half-turned, lamplight cutting her folded-letter face in two. "If anything came ashore with him, the guild will want it logged. Everything gets logged. You know that better than anyone."

"Everything," Marguerite agreed, "gets logged."

She went. The boots went. The street door went.

Marguerite stood alone in the counting-room with a dead man's salt drying on the floor and a warm roll of oilcloth hidden against his chest, and he did the only arithmetic that mattered now, the sum he had been dreading without knowing its name: the weight of the lie in one hand, the weight of the map in the other, and between them the growing certainty that the blank space in the ledger — those forty silent years — was not an absence at all.

It was a grave. And someone had buried the truth of the drowned realm in it, and signed the plot with the guild's own seal, and the courier had died to dig it up.

Marguerite untied the tarred string with steady fingers, unrolled the oilcloth on the table, and looked down at the salt-crusted map of the marshes — and at the single line of writing across its face, in a hand that shook the way the old covenant entries had shaken:

*Bring the taster. The debt is due.*
