## 4. The Scribe in the Reeds

The marsh did not want him.

Marguerite understood this the way he understood poison — not through study but through the body's quiet, unanimous vote. Three days out from the burning counting-house, his boots had surrendered to the mud, and now he walked in ruined leather while the reeds closed behind him like a ledger being shut. The salt smell was wrong here. Not the clean bite of the sea but something older, something that had been steeping under the world for centuries: brine and rot and beneath it, faintly, a sweetness like honey left too long in a dead man's mouth.

The map guided him by means he did not like to examine. It was not ink on vellum so much as blood dried into a promise; when he held it toward the low sun, the channels of the marsh appeared on it before his eyes found them, dark lines blooming like bruises. It wanted a direction. It wanted, he suspected, a bearer. He tried not to think about what a bearer might be *for*.

He had not slept properly since the fire. When he did sleep, he dreamed of the courier's hand pressing the map into his own — the grip of a man spending his last coin — and of the enforcers' faces lit orange by the counting-house windows, patient as arithmetic. He dreamed of the king's soup, of the hemlock he had swallowed without tasting anything at all, and woke with his tongue pressed flat against the roof of his mouth, waiting for a flavor that never came.

That was the horror he could not say aloud, even to himself: he had walked into these killing marshes carrying nothing but a dead man's map, and some traitorous part of him had been *relieved*. A body that could not be hurt. A life that cost nothing to risk. He was beginning to understand that this was not courage. It was the freedom of a man who has already been spent.

The sun was going down in strips of copper when the reeds began to whisper.

Not wind. Wind moves in waves; this moved in words. Marguerite stopped, water rising cold around his ankles, and listened to the marsh say his name — or something shaped like it, worn smooth as a pebble, *Marg-er-ite, Marg-er-ite*, chanted by throats that had forgotten how throats worked.

They came up out of the water without disturbing it.

They had been men once — he could see that in the architecture of them, the suggestion of shoulders, the hollow where a face should hold its argument. Now they were salt given ambition, white crusts over shapes of gray water, and their eyes were two holes apiece through which the tide looked out. The reeds they touched went gray and crumbled. Where their feet dragged, the mud bleached bone-white.

Marguerite stood very still and took inventory, because inventory was what he did. Six of them. Closing in a slow crescent, herding rather than hunting — no, correcting himself, herding first, *hunting later*. His heart performed its small professional duty and quickened, and he noted, with the detachment of long practice, that the fear arrived in his mind but not his body, that his hands stayed steady, that his pulse was a report filed by someone else.

*This is what I am,* he thought, watching death walk toward him across the water. *A man who watches.*

The nearest wraith reached for him, and its touch was going to be — he knew this suddenly, the way he knew hemlock — a taking. Not of flesh. Of name. He felt the map heat against his chest like a brand coming awake.

Then the marsh caught fire with light.

It came from the west, a line of white script burning across the surface of the black water, letters taller than a man, written in a hand so fierce the reeds bowed away from it. The wraiths recoiled as if scalded. And through the gap came a woman walking on the water's skin as though it were a floor she had personally paid for.

She was small and spare and moved like a blade being drawn. Her clothes were the colors of deep water, and her skin — he saw this even at distance, even in dying light — was *written on*. Sigils curled along her forearms and throat and the sharp line of her jaw, fine as needlework, and they moved. They crawled and rearranged themselves like ants doing urgent work, and where they gathered brightest, the air smelled of rain about to fall.

She raised one hand and spoke a word that was less sound than pressure, and the lead wraith simply came apart — dissolved into a stain of white on the water, its salt settling like snow that had lost its argument.

The others fled, folding back into the marsh with a sound like a sigh let out of a drowned chest.

Silence. The real kind, thick enough to lean on.

The woman stopped an arm's length from Marguerite, standing on water he was sinking into, and looked him over with the frank appraisal of someone reading a bill of lading. Up close, her sigils had stilled, lying against her brown skin like a tattooed alphabet waiting for orders. Her eyes were the green-gray of a wave just before it decides.

"You're bleeding," she said.

He looked down. A cut on his palm from a reed-blade, shallow, an hour old. "It's nothing."

"It's everything." She said it with a strange intensity, almost anger. "Blood is a signature. You've been signing yourself into this marsh all day. Every wraith within ten miles knows your name by now." She crouched, touched two fingers to the water, and drew something — a sigil that flared and sank, and the water around them went still and *uninterested*, the way a room goes when a door is properly shut. "There. Deaf now. They can't hear you."

"Thank you," Marguerite said, and meant it, and hated how thin it sounded.

"Don't thank me yet." She straightened. This close, he could see exhaustion carved under her eyes, the kind that comes from holding a door shut for years. "You have the map."

It was not a question. His hand went to his chest anyway, traitorously. "How do you know what I carry?"

"Because I was sent to find it." Her chin lifted. "Or its bearer. Whichever survived longer."

"And if only one of us survives?"

"Then I hope it's the map," she said, "because the bearer is replaceable and the covenant is not." She paused, and something flickered across her face — regret, maybe, or its well-worn costume. "That was supposed to sound like a threat. I'm told I'm bad at threats."

Despite everything — the burned counting-house, the dead courier, the marsh chanting his name — Marguerite felt the corner of his mouth move. "You're terrible at threats."

"I know." She exhaled. "Wren," she said, as if offering a weapon hilt-first. "Scribe of the Tidewrought Court. Fourth voice of the Ledger of Salt."

"Marguerite Sterling." He hesitated over the next part, the part that had defined him for eleven years, and heard himself say it differently than he ever had: "I taste things for the crown."

Something changed in her face. Recognition, but not of his name — of the shape of the sentence, the way it was built like a cell. "Ah," she said softly. "Owned."

"*Excuse me?*"

"The crown owns your tongue. My court owns my skin." She turned her forearm toward him, and he saw, in the moving script, a line of sigils bound at either end by characters he somehow understood were chains — he read them the way he read poison, in his body before his mind. "Every mark on me is a debt I signed to be allowed to exist. Every flavor on you is a death you swallowed so a king could keep eating. We are both," she said, "entries in other people's books."

The marsh breathed around them. Somewhere far off, the wraiths whispered to each other in their ruined voices, patient as interest accruing.

"I need answers," Marguerite said.

"I need you ambulatory and uncaught until moonrise." Wren glanced at the bruising sky. "We can trade. Answers for distance. But not here — the deaf-sigil won't hold past full dark, and the things that live deeper in don't care about names. They take the whole person." She extended her hand, palm up, sigils rippling. "Well, taster?"

He looked at her hand. He had taken a hundred hands in his life — dukes', ministers', the king's own — and every handshake had been a transaction wearing gloves. This one offered nothing except passage and a question he wasn't ready to answer.

He took it anyway.

Her grip was warm and salt-stung, and where their palms met, one of her sigils flared briefly gold — a reaction, he would learn later, that living script gave only to living blood.

* * *

They walked the drowned paths while the last light failed, and Wren talked the way people talk when silence has become more dangerous than speech.

"The covenant was a door," she said, stepping from hummock to hummock with the ease of long ownership. "Not a wall between your realm and mine — a *door*. Doors swing both ways. Your court gave ours years — lifespans, harvests of time, freely rendered each season. In exchange, we kept the salt off your fields, kept the marsh from remembering it used to be seabed, kept the old things fed and named so they'd stay asleep." She glanced back at him. "Your guild brokered it. The Gilded Ledger keeps accounts for both crowns. Or it did."

"It still keeps accounts," Marguerite said grimly. "I balance them. There are entries I've never been able to reconcile. Payments that stop forty years ago. Lines erased so cleanly I thought it was my error."

"It wasn't your error." Wren's voice went flat and careful, the way voices go around wounds. "Forty years ago, the payments stopped. Someone on your side broke the covenant and hid the breach inside the ledger itself. And everything your realm owed mine has been *taken* instead — ripped out, unrecorded, stolen." She stopped on a hummock and turned to face him fully, and in the moonrise her sigils had begun to glow faintly, a constellation waking. "Do you know what happens to my people when their names go unpaid?"

"No."

"They dissolve." She said it simply, which made it worse. "A name is a thing that holds a person together — yours holds you, even now, even owned. Ours are written in salt and contract, and when the payments stopped, the writing started to fade. My mother is a memory of my mother. My teacher is a smell of ink and an empty chair. The youngest children were born half-written and the tide takes a little more of them every year." Her sigils dimmed, then steadied, disciplined. "I was sent through what's left of the door to find the breach. To find whoever carries the proof of it. To retrieve them." A pause, precise as a knife laid down. "Or end them, if retrieval would spread the breach further. Those were my orders. Both of them."

Marguerite stood in the cold water and considered, with the detachment he had once brought to hemlock, the fact that this small fierce stranger had been sent to kill him, and that he felt — not fear. Curiosity. And beneath it, unwelcome as a flavor he couldn't spit out, a kind of kinship so sharp it was nearly grief.

"You're telling me this," he said slowly, "because you want me to trust you. That's tradecraft."

"I'm telling you this," Wren said, "because I've decided not to follow my orders, and saying it out loud makes it true, and making it true makes it harder to un-decide." She smiled, and it was crooked and tired and utterly without armor. "My people dissolve, taster. I will not add a drowning man to the ledger to save my own ink. Whatever else I am, I am a scribe. I do not falsify accounts."

Ahead of them, the marsh opened into a wide black lagoon, and in the middle of it — impossible, inevitable — stood a door. Free-standing, frame without wall, ancient wood grown over with barnacle and glowing script, its threshold brimming with water that did not spill.

"That leads to my realm," Wren said. "What's left of it." Then she stiffened, head tilting, sigils flaring bright along her arms like a struck match. "No. No, no — get down—"

Light burst through the reeds behind them. Lantern-light, many lanterns, and with them a sound Marguerite knew better than his own heartbeat: the measured cadence of boots that belonged to the crown. Riders in royal colors fanning out along the marsh edge, and at their center a voice he recognized calling his name — his full name, title and all, the way it was spoken when a possession was recalled.

"Marguerite Sterling," the voice rang across the water. "By order of the king. Surrender the map, and your debts are forgiven."

Wren grabbed his wrist, sigils blazing. "Can they cross the water?"

"Not yet," Marguerite said, and heard himself smiling, wild and light, a man tasting fear for the second time in his life and finding it *alive*. "But they'll learn. They always learn."

The door stood waiting. Behind them, the lanterns multiplied. Wren's hand tightened on his wrist, and her sigils wrote themselves faster, urgent as a heartbeat.

"Choose quickly," she said.
