## 5. Doors Between Realms

The door was a drowned thing, and Wren had always loved it the way one loves a scar.

It stood in the deepest fold of the marsh, where the reeds grew black and the water went still as poured glass — an archway of green stone furred with salt-bloom, half-sunk in the peat. To human eyes it would have been nothing: a ruined gate, a farmer's folly swallowed by the fen. But Wren could feel it humming against her skin, the sigils along her forearms warming like coals raked over. The door remembered her. Doors between realms always remembered their keepers.

"Wait here," she said.

Marguerite stood ankle-deep in the shallows, his fine court clothes ruined past redemption, mud to the knee and salt crusted white along his sleeves. He looked less like a crown's food-taster now and more like something the marsh had decided to keep. His eyes moved over the archway with the particular attention of a man reading figures in a ledger — cataloguing, weighing, refusing to be impressed.

"It's locked," he said. Not a question.

"It's *asleep*." Wren waded forward until the water kissed her thighs. "There's a difference. A lock wants a key. Sleep wants a name."

She pressed her palm flat against the stone. The sigils on her skin woke in answer, rising from their resting brown to a faint luminous blue, and she spoke her own name into the grain of the door — not aloud, never aloud, names spoken near doors had consequences — but in the old way, through the blood beneath her fingernails, through the salt in her spit.

The archway shuddered. Water began to move *upward*, sheeting along the stone in silver ropes, climbing instead of falling, and where it climbed the air changed. Marguerite made a small sound behind her — surprise, quickly mastered — and Wren found herself absurdly pleased by it. Let the man who felt nothing feel *that*.

Beyond the arch, where there should have been more marsh, there was instead a slow descent of steps going down into green-gold light. The water hung at the threshold like a curtain drawn aside, held there by covenant-law older than either realm.

"After you," she said, and then, because honesty had been gnawing at her since the chapel-reeds, since the wraiths, since the moment she'd first seen him standing in the ruin of his own careful life: "*After me.* Stay close. And whatever you see down there — don't say anyone's name. Not mine. Not yours. Especially not yours."

"Why?"

"Because down there, a name is a rope," she said. "And someone has been cutting ropes."

* * *

The sunken realm did not look drowned. That was the first cruelty of it.

They descended through light that came from everywhere and nowhere — a green-gold suffusion, like sunlight filtered through cathedral glass and then through forty feet of sea. Towers rose around them, slender as needles, their spires crowned with gardens that bloomed underwater in colors Marguerite had no words for: violet that burned, gold that wept. Fish moved through open windows as though windows were simply another kind of door. Streets of pearl-ash curved away below, and people walked them — or things that had been people —

Wren heard him stop breathing.

She had warned herself for this. She had rehearsed it on the long walk from the reed-flats, composing sentences, arranging the truth like a merchant arranges goods. None of the rehearsal helped. Seeing her home through a stranger's eyes was its own kind of drowning.

One of the walkers turned toward them. It had been a woman once; you could see the architecture of her face beneath the change. But her edges were wrong — softening, sloughing, dissolving at the fingertips into threads of brine that drifted upward and did not come back. Where her mouth should have been there was smoothness, the way an unfinished sculpture is smooth. No lips. No voice. She looked at Wren with eyes that were still terribly, patiently human, and raised a hand that was mostly gone, and Wren understood the gesture anyway because she had been understanding it all her life:

*Remember me.*

"She can't speak?" Marguerite's voice was very quiet.

"The nameless can't." Wren kept walking. If she stopped she would not start again. "A name is what holds a body together here. Shape is just a story the name tells, and when the story goes—" she gestured, helpless, at the drifting brine, at the woman's patient eyes, "—the body forgets itself. From the edges inward. First the hands, then the face, then the voice. Last to go is the part that watches."

"How long does the watching last?"

"We don't know. No one who's finished watching has ever come back to say."

They walked on through streets where every third doorway bore a mark she knew too well: a circle struck through with a line, chalked in white salt. The sign of the unmade. Whole households of it. Children's marks, small and low on the doorposts, and Wren had to look at the middle distance and breathe through the tightness in her chest.

"You said the payments stopped," Marguerite said. He was doing it again — turning horror into arithmetic, grief into columns. She was beginning to recognize it as his way of surviving the world. "Forty years of them. What exactly did my side owe yours?"

"Not coin." She led him up a stair of nacre toward the high quarter, where the scribes kept their halls. "Names. Living ones. Once a year, the crown sent down a tithe of true names — freely given, that was the law of it, freely given or the covenant rejected them — and our scribes bound those names into the deep archive, and the archive fed the realm. Every person here exists because somewhere above, someone gave their name away and lived on nameless but whole. In exchange, we kept the tides gentle, the plagues few, the king's line unbroken. Borrowed years, your guild called it. We called it breath."

"And when the payments stopped—"

"We didn't starve. Starving would have been clean." Her laugh came out bitter and tasting of salt. "We started eating ourselves. The archive thinned, so the elders began unbinding names — spending the old tithes to keep the present alive. And when the old tithes ran out, they started taking names from the living. Quietly. From the margins first. The poor, the sick, the ones nobody would miss." She gestured at the struck circles. "Forty years of that. My grandmother's generation hid it. My mother's generation managed it. Mine is dissolving."

Marguerite said nothing for a long moment. When he spoke, his voice had a strange texture, as if the words had passed through something sharp on the way out. "I've tasted hemlock and felt nothing. I've watched men die at tables I was sitting at. None of it—" He stopped. Started again. "This is the first time I've wanted to be sick."

"Now you know how I feel about your court."

* * *

The scribes' hall was a vault of whalebone and glass, and it was nearly empty.

That was the thing Wren hadn't let herself think about on the road: how empty. In her childhood the hall had rung with a hundred voices reciting the archive, a hundred pens whispering. Now eleven scribes remained, and three of those were half-dissolved, working with hands that left brine-trails on the page, transcribing names they could no longer remember owning.

At the far end, under the great seal of the covenant — two interlocked rings, one of pearl, one of iron — the Master of Names sat motionless. Elder Sef. Her teacher. What remained of her.

"Wren." Sef's voice was a dry wash of tide over shingle. "You found him."

"I found him." Wren stepped aside so the elder could see Marguerite fully, and watched the ancient eyes take his measure — the way a buyer takes the measure of goods.

"He carries the map," Sef said. "He carries the bearer's mark. Good. Then the last clause can be honored." Her gaze slid to Wren, and there was no mercy in it, only duty worn smooth as sea-glass. "You know your instruction, child. Retrieve the bearer. And if retrieval fails—"

"I know my instruction."

Marguerite had gone very still. Wren felt the stillness like a change in pressure, the way the water felt before a storm-surge. "Retrieve," he repeated. "Or."

Sef's ruined hand lifted, graceful despite everything, and sketched the old sign in the air: the circle, the line through it. *Unmake.*

"The covenant cannot be reforged by any name in this realm," the elder said. "It must be sealed in the blood of the breach itself. You are the breach, taster. Your immunity was bought with our stolen years — do you understand? Every poison you ever survived, you survived on borrowed breath. Yours is the blood that owes us. Bring it willingly, and we write a new covenant. Refuse—" the dry voice paused, tide withdrawing, "—and the instruction stands. One way or another, the debt comes home."

The silence stretched. Somewhere above them, faint through forty feet of sea, thunder rolled — or something that would pretend to be thunder when the king's agents told their masters what it had really been.

Wren looked at Marguerite and saw him looking back, and hated how much she wanted him to say yes, and hated more the small cold truth underneath everything else, which was that if he refused, she had been trained since childhood to make sure the debt came home anyway.

"You should have told me on the road," he said quietly. Only to her.

"I'm telling you now."

"You waited until there was no road back."

"There was never a road back," Wren said. "For either of us. There was only the door, and the door only opens one way until the covenant is sealed." She drew a breath that tasted of brine and confession. "I was sent to retrieve you. Or kill you. Those were my instructions, word for word, and I have obeyed instructions my whole life the way you've tasted poisons your whole life — perfectly, and feeling nothing." Her voice cracked on the last word, and she let it. "But I'm telling you now, before the elders ask, before the soldiers come, before any of it: I don't want to feel nothing. Not about this. Not about you."

Above them, the thunder came again — closer now, and wrong, and rhythmic. Boots, maybe. Or oars. Or the beginning of the end.

Marguerite rolled up his sleeve, baring the forearm where thirty years of survived doses slept in his veins like a ledger waiting to be read.

"Then show me," he said, "where the ink goes."
