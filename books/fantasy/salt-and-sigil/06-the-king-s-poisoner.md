## 6. The King's Poisoner

They took him at the tollbridge over the Brack, where the road narrowed to a throat of stone and the river beneath ran the color of old pewter. Marguerite had known they were there for half a mile — he had learned to read ambush the way other men read weather, in the stillness of birds, in the wrongness of a beggar's shadow that fell against the light instead of with it — but knowing changed nothing. There was nowhere left to run that the map did not also run through, and the map was the one thing he could not abandon.

So he walked into it. He had spent his whole life swallowing what was given to him.

The agents came out of the reeds and the alder shade without hurry, six of them in the undyed wool of crown couriers, which fooled no one. Their leader was a woman with a face like a closed ledger and a scar bisecting her left eyebrow, and she did not draw a weapon. She simply held out her hand, palm up, as though he were a merchant arriving at a gate he already owed passage through.

"Master Sterling," she said. "His Majesty requests your palate."

"I'm on leave from court," Marguerite said. "Indefinite."

"His Majesty disagrees." Her eyes went to the satchel at his hip, then back to his face, and something almost like courtesy crossed her features. "You may carry your own bag. It will be searched regardless. It's kinder this way."

It was kinder. That was the horror of the crown's machinery — it ran on kindness the way a mill ran on water, grinding everything soft that passed between its stones. Marguerite put his hand in his pocket instead, found the vial of nightshade tincture he carried for exactly this occasion, and considered, briefly, the arithmetic of it. Six agents. One dose. A body that no longer registered poison as anything but flavor.

He swallowed the thought instead of the vial, and let them bind his wrists with silk cord — silk, because rope chafed and His Majesty disliked marks on useful property — and lead him east, away from the marshes, away from Wren, away from the drowned door he had been three days from reaching.

He did not look back at the water. Looking back was a tell, and tells got people killed, and he had not yet decided whether he wanted to live badly or die usefully.

* * *

They traveled two days to a hunting lodge Marguerite knew from court gossip: Ashenmere, the king's private retreat, all black timber and smoked glass, built on pilings above a fen that smelled of iron and rot. The servants would not meet his eye. The guards did not speak. He was fed well — absurdly well, a pheasant glazed in quince, wine older than the treaty wars — and he tasted every dish before he touched it out of habit so deep it had become breathing, and found nothing in any of it but excellence.

That frightened him more than hemlock would have. Poison said *someone wants you dead*. Excellence said *someone wants you comfortable*, and comfort, in Marguerite's long experience of ledgers both culinary and occult, was always the more expensive purchase.

On the third evening they brought him down a stair he had never seen, below the lodge's foundations, into a room that should not have existed. The fen's water table sat higher than the cellar floor; he could hear it pressing against the walls, a sound like a crowd murmuring behind stone. And along those walls, set into niches like relics in a saint's crypt, stood jars.

Dozens of them. Hundreds. Glass blown thick and greenish, sealed with wax and wire, and inside each one swam a pale luminescence, like moonlight bottled by someone who had caught it drowning. They were not lamps. Marguerite had balanced the Gilded Ledger's accounts for nine years; he knew contraband when it glowed at him.

Years. Borrowed years, the guild's most guarded trade, the commodity that appeared in the books only as entries in a hand so old the ink had gone brown: *received of the salt court, forty per annum, per covenant.* He had assumed the entries were historical. He had assumed the payments had stopped when the covenant broke.

They had not stopped. They had been redirected.

"You count things," said a voice behind him. "I'm told you're very good at it."

The king stood at the foot of the stairs, alone, unguarded, dressed not in state but in the plain wool of a man walking his own grounds. He looked older than his portraits and younger than his years — sixty-two by every record, and yet his hands were a young man's hands, smooth as a clerk's, and his eyes had the wet brightness of someone recently wept over or recently reborn.

"Your Majesty honors me," Marguerite said, and bowed the precise degree owed.

"Flattery is a currency too. I know its exchange rates better than most." The king drifted along the wall of jars, trailing one fingertip across the glass, and where he touched, the light inside leaned toward him like a plant toward sun. "Forty years ago I was dying. Do you know what kills kings, Master Sterling? Not assassins. Assassins are honest. What kills kings is *time* — time and the men who keep accounts of it. My physicians gave me a winter. Perhaps two."

Marguerite said nothing. In his experience, silence drew out confessions the way sugar drew out moisture.

"The Gilded Ledger brokered a covenant for my grandfather, you know. Crown and salt court. We pay tribute; they hold back the marshes from our fields and lend us their craft. A tidy arrangement, honored for two hundred years." The king smiled, and it was the smile of a man remembering a very good meal. "Then I learned what else the covenant contained. A clause. A reserve. The drowned realm keeps its people's *years* — pooled, communal, held in trust. Immortality by committee. Every citizen contributes their span to the common hoard and draws it back as needed. No death unless the realm itself consents to spend one."

"And Your Majesty," Marguerite said quietly, "broke the seal on the hoard."

"I *renegotiated*." For the first time the king's voice sharpened, and beneath it Marguerite heard something raw, something that had been screaming for forty years behind perfect manners. "Do you understand what I was offered? A winter. I have ruled thirty-nine winters since. Every year in these jars is a year some drowned creature agreed to lend and the ledger failed to return. Their clerks made an error in my favor. I simply declined to correct it."

"Someone erased forty years of payments," Marguerite said. "From the guild's oldest book. I've seen the gap. Ink doesn't fade evenly enough to be age. Someone scraped it."

"Scraping is such an ugly word." The king turned from the jars and looked at him fully, and Marguerite felt the look the way he once felt hemlock — a cold bloom spreading from the center of him outward. "Here is what I admire about you, Sterling. You drank everything they ever gave you and stayed standing. You are the only man in my kingdom whose body keeps no debts. You are, in point of fact, the only clean thing I own." He gestured at the jars, at the green swimming light. "And now you've gone and picked up a map that says otherwise. That says the debt has come due, and come due *through you*."

"They sent a courier," Marguerite said. "He died on my floor."

"Yes. Mine, technically. Everything here is mine." The king sighed, and it was almost gentle. "So. Terms. You surrender the map. You resume your post — I'll double your stipend, give you a house in the Palace Ward, a title if titles amuse you. In exchange, you taste for me until one of us dies, and you never again balance a book you weren't given. Freedom, wealth, and the particular peace of a man who owes nothing because he refuses to count."

"And if I refuse?"

The king spread his hands, and the jarlight gilded him, and for a moment he looked like every benevolent portrait in every government hall Marguerite had ever walked past. "Then I take the map, and I take your tongue — professionally speaking — and I find another taster. There are always other tasters. But you should understand what refusal costs beyond yourself." He nodded at the jars. "When the covenant reforges, the hoard returns to the salt court. All of it. Every year I borrowed comes out of my reign, all at once, with interest. I will die, Sterling. Not gracefully. Not in bed. I will come apart the way a dam comes apart, and everything I've held back will go through me. So you see, this isn't greed anymore. It's arithmetic. I cannot afford to let you reach the water."

There it was. Marguerite stood in the cold cellar with the fen pressing at the walls and the stolen years glowing in their hundreds of jars, and he understood, finally and completely, the shape of the thing he had been carrying since a bleeding man died whispering on his floor.

The king was not a monster. That was the terrible part. The king was a *debtor*, and debt made monsters of anyone, and Marguerite had spent nine years keeping books for people exactly like this — people who had once borrowed small and could not stop borrowing large.

"I refuse," Marguerite said.

The words came out steady, and that surprised him, because underneath them, for the first time in twenty years, he was afraid.

Not the flat, managed caution of a man tasting for arsenic. Not the professional vigilance he wore like armor. This was the real thing — animal, total, rising up his spine like cold water — fear of pain, fear of the dark, fear of ending, all the fears his poisoned blood had burned out of him decades ago, returning now in a single flood because for the first time there was something he wanted that survival alone could not protect.

He wanted to reach the marshes. He wanted to see Wren's sigils move when she laughed. He wanted the door between realms to open and stay open, and himself alive on the threshold of it.

Fear, he discovered, was just love with the safety removed.

"Give me the map," the king said, no longer gentle.

"It isn't in the satchel," Marguerite said, and watched the first true expression cross the king's face in forty borrowed years.

It was rage. It was relief. It was, unmistakably, fear — the king's own, ancient and well-practiced, flaring up like a match in a flooded room.

"Where is it?"

"Count," Marguerite said. "You're good at arithmetic. Work out what I'd have done with it."

The king's hand closed around the nearest jar, and the light inside shrank from him, and somewhere far above them, muffled by floors and fen-water, a horn began to blow — three notes, repeated, the signal Marguerite had heard only once before, years ago, in the guild's counting-house on the night the enforcers came.

But this time it wasn't coming from the lodge.

It was coming from the water.
