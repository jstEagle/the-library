## 7. Salt in the Blood

The chapel had drowned once, long before the covenant broke — Wren could taste it in the stones, that old patient salt that lived in things the water had loved and abandoned. Rain came through the collapsed roof in silver threads, pooling in the font, running down the ribs of a saint whose face had worn away to something kinder than a face. She lit no fire. Fire drew eyes, and eyes were what they fled.

Marguerite sat against the altar step with his coat spread over his knees, wringing rain from its hem with the same precise economy he brought to everything — accounts, poisons, lies. He had lied for her twice now. She kept turning that fact over like a coin of uncertain mint.

"You're staring," he said, without looking up.

"I'm reading." It was true enough. His skin was a ledger too, though he didn't know it yet — or refused to. Every poison he'd ever swallowed had left its faint signature beneath his skin, a script only her kind could see: arsenic's gray filigree at the wrists, hemlock's branching green along the throat, nightshade blooming dark as kelp at the hollow of his collarbone. He was beautiful the way a drowned ship is beautiful, she thought, and hated herself for the thought. *You were sent to retrieve him. Retrieve. The word has two meanings and you know both.*

"Reading," he repeated. "What does my body say tonight?"

"That you're tired. That you haven't eaten since the counting-house burned." She crouched beside him, close enough to feel the warmth coming off him like a small defiance. "That you're afraid."

"I'm not—"

"Wren." He said her name carefully, as if it might dissolve. Her name was one of the last true ones left below; she had given it to him without thinking, three nights ago in the reeds, and had been regretting the generosity ever since. "I have spent fifteen years learning to know exactly what I feel. Nothing. I am the most qualified man alive to be told when I'm lying about it."

* * *

She taught him sigil-craft because she could not sleep, and because teaching was the only prayer her people still had left.

"Blood remembers," she told him, kneeling on the chapel floor where the mosaic tiles still showed a tide-chart of heaven — stars arranged not in constellations but in currents. "Ink forgets. Ink is dead water and dead soot. But blood carries the name of the one who bleeds, and names are the only currency my realm has ever honored." She drew a knife from her boot — scribe's steel, thin as a reed stem. "Give me your hand."

He gave it. That was the thing about Marguerite Sterling that unsettled her more than any wraith or enforcer: he handed over pieces of himself without flinching, because nothing had ever hurt him enough to teach him caution. She turned his palm up. In the moonlight through the broken roof, the poison-script under his skin shifted faintly, like fish under ice.

"The first sigil every child below learns," she said, and cut — a shallow line across the pad of his thumb, no deeper than a paper's kiss.

He watched his own blood well up with an expression she couldn't parse. Wonder, maybe. Or grief. "It stings," he said, and then laughed, low and startled. "It actually stings. Do you know how long it's been since anything stung?"

"How long?"

"I don't remember. That's rather the point." He looked at the bead of red on his thumb as though it were a message from a stranger. "My whole life I've been tasting death for other men and feeling nothing. And now a knife the width of a hair and I—" He stopped. Swallowed. "Go on. Show me."

She guided his bleeding thumb to the tile floor. "A warding line. Simple. You think the shape, you mean the shape, and the blood does the rest." Together they drew it: a spiral closing inward, the sign for *shelter*, the first word of every covenant ever written below. Where his blood touched stone, it did not smear. It sank, and the line came up faintly luminous, blue-white as foxfire, humming against her senses like a struck bell.

Marguerite went very still.

"It worked," he said. "I've never — I'm not a scribe. I'm a taster. I count things."

"You're a ledger," she said before she could stop herself. "That's all a ledger is. A record of what was given and what was taken." She heard the weight in her own voice and saw him hear it too. Below, they said the breach in the covenant had a shape, a body, a pulse — forty years of stolen names poured into one vessel until it became a door that walked. She had known since the reeds. She had known when she put her knife to his palm, and done it anyway.

He looked up at her, and the warding-light made his eyes strange. "Wren. What am I?"

"Rain," she said. "Listen. It's raining. Let me have this hour before the truth comes back for us."

* * *

They slept in shifts, or pretended to. In the false dawn — that gray hour when the marsh exhaled and the wraiths went down to the deep channels — she woke to find him awake, drawing sigils in the air with a finger that no longer bled, practicing shapes he'd only seen once.

"You learn fast," she said.

"I memorize fast. It's different." He glanced over. "My mother taught me letters on a slate. Before the guild bought my tongue. I used to practice the alphabet in the air at night, in the dark, so no one would take that from me too." A pause. "This is the first time since then that knowing something has felt like mine."

Something in her chest turned over, slow and painful, like a keel finding water. She crossed the chapel and sat beside him on the cold step. The saint above them kept his faceless kindness over them both.

"When we reach the drowned throne," she said, "and the covenant is reforged — if it can be reforged — my duty ends. I was sent to bring back the bearer of the map. Alive, if the signs allowed. Not alive, if they didn't." She said it plainly; he deserved plainness, having been lied to by everyone else who owned him. "And when the covenant stands again, the doors between realms will lock. Sealed. What passes between worlds will pass no longer."

"And you'll go home."

"And I'll go home." The words tasted of brine and iron. "And you'll stay here, in a world where you'll be mortal for the first time since you were a boy. Every poison you ever survived will come back to collect. Every dose. All at once, or slowly — the covenant will decide which. You understand what you're offering? You're offering them your immunity. Your whole life, you've been untouchable, and you're going to hand it back like—"

"Like returning a borrowed book," he said quietly. "I never wanted to be untouchable, Wren. I wanted to be *unclaimed*. There's a difference. Untouchable just means nothing gets in. Unclaimed means someone chose to keep you." He turned his hand over, the healing cut pale on his thumb. "No one has ever chosen to keep me. The crown owns my service. The guild owns my nights. Even my body belongs to their poisons — I'm just the shelf they're stored on."

The rain thinned. Somewhere out in the marsh a heron cried, one raw note, and the sound seemed to open a door in her that she had spent her whole life keeping shut.

"I was sent to retrieve you," she said. "Retrieve. My elders chose the word carefully. It means *bring back*. It also means—" 

"—recover something lost," he finished. "Or rescue something doomed." He smiled, and it was the saddest and most honest expression she had ever seen on him. "Say the third meaning. I'd rather hear it from you than spend the rest of my life guessing."

"*Take for yourself,*" she whispered. "It also means take for yourself. And I have been telling myself for six days that I don't want to."

The silence between them filled with rain-sound and heart-sound and the hum of the warding line he had written in his own blood, still glowing faintly at the edge of the dark, still saying *shelter, shelter, shelter.*

"Duty forbids it," he said. Not a question. He knew ledgers; he knew when a debt was already entered against them.

"My people are dissolving into brine while their names unmake themselves. If I let myself love you and then lose you behind a sealed door, I will carry that the way the sea carries everything — forever, and in every current." Her voice cracked on the last word and she let it. "And yours forbids it too. The crown will hunt you until the covenant kills the king or the king kills you. Loving me makes you slower. Slower gets you dead, and dead, you can't save anyone."

"Yes," he said. "All of that is true." He reached out and took her hand — the scribe's hand, the knife-hand, ink-stained and salt-cracked — and pressed his thumb, the small healed wound, against her palm. "And none of it changes anything. Write that in your ledger. *Both entries true. Balance impossible.*"

She laughed, or wept, or did whatever it is that people do when tenderness and inevitability hold hands. Then she took his face in her hands and kissed him, and it was not gentle, because gentleness would have been a lie; it was the kiss of two people standing on a ledge with the sea rising on either side. His mouth tasted of iron and rain. Under her fingers, the poison-script beneath his skin stirred and brightened, responding to her touch the way sigils respond to their maker — and for one terrible, luminous moment she understood that she could read him entirely, every entry, every dose, every year of numbness purchased with another world's stolen names.

She pulled back, breath ragged. "We shouldn't—"

"No," he agreed, and did not move away.

Outside, the marsh went suddenly, utterly silent. No heron. No rain. Even the drip from the broken roof seemed to hold itself mid-fall.

Wren was on her feet before her mind caught up, knife out, every sigil on her skin waking cold. At the chapel's shattered doorway, framed against the graying sky, stood figures in the crown's gray-and-gold — and behind them, borne on a litter like a relic, a man whose shadow fell wrong, whose presence made the salt in the old stones weep.

Marguerite rose slowly beside her. "I know that shadow," he said, and his voice had gone flat and court-quiet, the voice of a man who had tasted a hundred poisons and finally met the one meant for him. "That's the king's own poisoner. Which means the king isn't sending soldiers anymore."

The figure at the doorway raised one white-gloved hand, and every warding sigil Marguerite had written guttered out at once, like candles pinched between wet fingers.

"He's come himself," Wren breathed. "Marguerite — the king has come for his taster."
