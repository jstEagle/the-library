## 8. The Price of Immunity

The guild's master kept his office above the salt line, in a tower that smelled of old paper and older money, and Marguerite climbed toward it the way a man climbs a gallows — slowly, counting each step as though numbering them might make them last.

He had come willingly. That was the strange part. After the chapel, after Wren's sigils drying on his skin like scars made of light, after the king's agents had torn through the marshes behind them and the drowned realm's door had groaned shut at their backs — after all of it, he had walked into the Gilded Ledger's new counting-house with his hands empty and his chin level, because there was nowhere else left to go. The map was gone. The covenant's truth was loose in the world like blood in water. And the guild's master had sent for him not with enforcers but with an invitation written in copperplate: *Come and be accounted for.*

The office was round, ringed with windows, and every window held a different weather. Rain fell past one pane; sunlight gilded another; through a third drifted snow that melted before it touched the sill. Doors between realms, Marguerite thought. The guild traded in everything, even the sky.

Vess Corvane sat behind a desk of black marsh-oak, and she was smaller than her reputation. A woman of sixty-odd years with ink-stained fingers and eyes the grey-green of sea glass, she looked less like a spymaster than a schoolmistress who had buried too many students. She did not rise when he entered. She only gestured to the chair across from her, and beside the chair, on a small silver tray, sat a single cup of tea.

"Drink," she said. "It's chamomile. I promise you'll taste nothing but flowers."

"That," Marguerite said, "is rather the point of me."

Something moved behind her eyes — pity, or its well-dressed cousin. "Sit down, taster."

He sat. He did not touch the tea. Outside the rain-window, lightning stitched itself into the clouds and unpicked again.

"You've read the hidden entries," Vess said. It wasn't a question. "Forty years of payments, erased. The covenant between the crown and the drowned realm, brokered by this guild three generations before either of us drew breath. You know what was owed. You know someone stopped paying." She folded her hands. "What you don't know is *how* the payments were made. And you've earned the truth, I think. You've carried it since a courier bled out on your floor."

"Then tell me," Marguerite said. His voice came out steady. He had spent twenty years making his voice steady over poison; it would hold over this.

Vess opened a drawer and drew out a ledger — not one of the guild's great bound volumes but something older, its cover crusted with salt, its pages soft as cloth. She turned it toward him.

"The drowned realm does not trade in coin," she said. "Coin means nothing to people who are dissolving. They trade in names. In years. In *self*. When the covenant was struck, the crown agreed to pay annually: a tithe of memory, a portion of the realm's abundance, sent down through the doors so the drowned could keep their shapes, their histories, their children's faces. And in exchange—" she tapped the page— "the crown received protection. The marshes stayed passable. The storms turned aside. And the guild received its commission, as guilds do."

Marguerite looked at the entries. Columns of names, thousands of them, flowing down the page like water finding its level. And then, forty years ago, the flow simply stopped — not crossed out, not corrected. *Erased*, as though the ink had been drunk back into the pen.

"I know who erased them," he said quietly. "I stood in front of him while he offered me a dukedom to forget I'd ever seen the map."

"Yes. The king." Vess said the word without reverence, the way one says *weather* or *debt*. "But here is what your duke's treason doesn't explain, taster. Here is what no one has ever explained to you, because you were never meant to ask." She leaned forward. "A covenant broken must go somewhere. A debt that size doesn't evaporate. It *pools*. Forty years of stolen names, stolen years, stolen selves — all of it, gathering. Looking for a vessel."

The fire crackled. Somewhere below, the counting-house bells rang the hour, and Marguerite realized distantly that he had stopped hearing bells years ago, the way he had stopped tasting bitterness, the way he had stopped feeling anything at all.

"What vessel?" he asked.

Vess turned the ledger around fully, so the last entry faced him. It was dated forty years past. It was written in a hand he almost recognized — and beside it, in the margin, in fresher ink, someone had added a single line:

*Paid in full: one vessel, born the winter of the breach.*

"Your mother came to this guild the year you were conceived," Vess said gently. "She was dying. Fever, famine — the records say she'd have lost you both. She bargained. She asked for a son who would survive anything." Her sea-glass eyes held his. "And the guild, which brokers impossible things, found the price. The pooled debt needed a shape to live in. A body to hold it. Your immunity, Marguerite — every dose of hemlock you've swallowed without flinching, every poison that slid through you like rain through a net — that isn't training. That isn't discipline. That is forty years of a drowned nation's stolen names, sitting in your blood, keeping you alive."

The room tilted. Or perhaps it was only Marguerite who tilted, some essential axis of him giving way.

"No," he said.

"You were dosed from infancy," Vess went on, merciless as tide. "Your mother consented — she thought she was buying your life. Perhaps she was. But understand what you are, taster. You are not a man who survived the poisons. You are the poisons' *accountant*. Every toxin that should have killed you was paid for — out of an account that was never yours, with coin that was never given. You have spent your whole life balancing other people's ledgers." She closed the old book softly. "You *are* one."

Marguerite stood. His legs held him — of course they did; they always held him; that was the whole cursed architecture of him — and he walked to the rain-window because he could not look at her face while the ground came up beneath him.

He thought of the first dose he could remember: bitter broth at age four, his mother weeping in the doorway, telling him it was medicine. He thought of the court physicians marveling at him. The king's delighted laughter when Marguerite sipped from the poisoned chalice and smiled. *My unkillable taster.* He had worn it like armor. Like an identity. *Nothing touches me. Nothing has ever touched me.*

And underneath, all along, a drowned realm going grey. Names unraveling into brine. Wren's people forgetting their own children's faces while he slept soundly, immune, untouched, *paid for*.

"Wren knows," he said. It wasn't a question either. He could see it now — the way she had looked at him in the chapel, teaching him sigil-craft with his own blood, tenderness and grief braided together in her expression. "That's why she was sent. Not to retrieve me. Not even to kill me." He laughed, and the laugh sounded wrong in his own ears, hollow as a dropped cup. "I'm the breach. I'm the missing payment walking around in a doublet. She was sent to collect the debt."

"She was sent to retrieve the bearer of the map," Vess said. "What she does with the truth is her own account to balance. But yes. She knows."

Marguerite pressed his palm flat against the cold glass. Beyond it, rain fell upward past the frame, into a sky that belonged to some other world. *Doors between realms.* He had spent his life as the crown's instrument, then the guild's, and now it seemed he had been an instrument longer than either — a vessel, a vault, a walking column of figures in a ledger he'd never signed.

"How much?" he asked.

Vess frowned. "How much what?"

"To reforge the covenant. To pay it back." He turned. His hands were steady. His hands were always steady, and he hated them for it. "Every name. Every year. If it's all in my blood, then it can be taken back out. Written where it belongs. So tell me the price, Master Corvane, and don't dress it up. I'm a taster. I know how bitter medicines are measured."

For the first time, the guild's master looked away from him.

"The covenant requires a signature in the blood of the debtor," she said. "Not a drop. Not a token. The whole account, settled. To write the new covenant, you would give back every dose you ever survived — every poison your body ever swallowed and paid for with stolen coin. Hemlock. Arsenic. Nightshade. All of it, returned at once, converted into ink." Her voice dropped. "No living man has survived that, Marguerite. No *mortal* man. Because the moment the debt leaves you, you become what you always should have been: a human being, in a human body, with no armor at all. The doses won't just be repaid. They'll be *felt*. All of them. At once. As the covenant takes your blood for ink."

Silence, except for the rain falling in three directions outside the windows.

"And if I refuse?"

"Then the drowned realm finishes dissolving within the year, and the breach comes looking for a new vessel. Debts pool, taster. If you will not hold them, they'll find someone who can — some child born unlucky, some mother desperate enough to bargain. The ledger always balances. The only question is who pays."

Marguerite thought of Wren's skin, alive with sigils, each one a name she refused to let be forgotten. He thought of her voice in the ruined chapel: *We are not dissolving because we are weak. We are dissolving because someone else is strong.* He had kissed her then, and tasted salt, and told himself the guilt belonged to kings and guilds.

It belonged to him. It had always belonged to him. He was the strongest thing in either realm, and he had never lifted a finger, because he had never felt a thing.

"When?" he said.

Vess studied him for a long moment. Then she rose, went to the sunlit window, and opened it — and beyond it Marguerite saw not sky but black water, a drowned throne hall lit by lamps that burned without flame, and standing on the threshold between worlds, her sigils flickering like a heartbeat, Wren.

She was looking straight at him. Her expression was not hope. Hope required belief in good outcomes. It was something rawer than that — the look of a person watching a scale tip.

"He's already gathering his fleet," Vess said quietly. "The king means to take the drowned throne by force before the solstice, and end the question of the covenant forever. Three days, Marguerite. Perhaps four." She stepped aside from the window, leaving the way open. "The reforging must happen at the throne, in the old water, with the debtor's blood. Which means you'll write it under arrows, with the man whose poisons you've been swallowing for twenty years trying to kill you before the ink dries."

Marguerite walked to the window. Wren's hand rose and pressed against the inside of the glass, and his rose to meet it, and between their palms the air hummed like a struck wire.

Twenty years untouchable. One chance to finally be touched by something true.

"Then I'd better learn to write quickly," he said, and stepped through.
