## 1. The Last Toll House

The storm came down the pass like something with intent.

Ines Oyelaran felt it before she saw it—a pressure behind the ears, a taste of iron on the wind—and she did what her mentor had taught her twelve years ago at this exact bend of the road: she stopped, spat into the wind to check its angle, and started shouting.

"Rope teams! Cloaks off the mules, oilcloth over the salt! We shelter at the toll house or we bury these animals where they stand!"

The caravan master, a fat Kellish trader named Odo with rings on every finger, sputtered something about schedules. Ines ignored him. She'd been ignoring him for six days, ever since he'd hired her at the Salt Road Guild's rate and then spent the whole journey trying to haggle it down. Guides were cheap now. Everyone knew it. That was the problem.

"Move," she said, and moved herself, up the line of eleven mules, checking knots with fingers gone numb and competent. The salt sacks rode high and dry under their oilcloth. The mules' ears were flat with misery but their feet were sound. Good animals. Better than most of the people she guided, if she was honest, which she tried not to be.

Behind them, maybe two miles back through the whiteout that was about to become their world, a Warden patrol was doing exactly what Warden patrols did in weather like this: nothing. Wardens didn't climb in storms. They sat in their stone waystations with coal fires and Crown-issued tea and wrote reports about how the old paths were closing nicely, thank you, another season of progress. By morning the drifts would erase the caravan's tracks, which was why Ines had timed the crossing for the front edge of the storm instead of waiting it out like a sensible person.

Sensible people didn't eat in winter.

* * *

The Last Toll House crouched at the shoulder of the pass like an old dog too stubborn to die. It had been built three hundred years ago by the first Guides—before the Guild had a name, before the roads had numbers—back when Thornfell was crossed by pilgrims and kings' messengers and salt caravans from the coast that everyone pretended still went somewhere important. The roof was slate, the walls were mountain granite, and the great hearth had burned without fully going out since before anyone's grandmother's grandmother, because there was a seam of peat under the floor that no one had ever managed to exhaust.

Ines got the fire talking properly within ten minutes of arrival, which was ten minutes faster than Odo deserved.

"Storm's a two-day blow," she told him, unwinding her scarf. Ice cracked off it and hit the stones like thrown gravel. "Maybe three. Your salt keeps. Your schedule doesn't. Write your wife."

Odo looked like he wanted to argue, then looked at the window, where the world had been replaced by a gray screaming nothing, and decided to write his wife.

There were eight drovers besides Odo. Two of them knew how to play cards without cheating, which made the evenings bearable. The rest filled the long hours the way travelers always did: complaining about the price of everything, asking Ines questions about the mountains, and not listening to her answers.

"The pass ahead," one of them said on the second night, a young one with a burn scar on his forearm and the particular restlessness of a man who'd signed onto caravan work to escape something. "They say the Wardens are shutting it. The high road. Is that true?"

Ines fed a split log to the fire and considered how much truth a stranger could carry.

"They're trying," she said.

"But can they? Shut a mountain?"

"You can shut a door." She watched the flame take hold. "Mountains aren't doors. They're just places people stopped being willing to go."

He frowned at that like it was a riddle, which it wasn't; it was just the truth, and truths often sounded like riddles to people who hadn't stood alone on a ridge at dawn and understood that the only thing keeping the road alive was someone willing to walk it.

The Guild had walked it for three hundred years. When she'd apprenticed at fourteen, there had been forty-one sworn guides on the rolls, toll houses staffed from Greywater to the Spire approach, a ledger of routes so dense that Master Corvin used to say you could cross Thornfell blindfolded if you could read. Now the ledger fit in a satchel. Now there were four of them. Now the Crown's Wardens called the old paths "anomalies" and the guides "unlicensed traffickers," as if the mountain were a warehouse and they were thieves in it.

Corvin would have hated the word *trafficker*. He'd died hating gentler words than that.

"Ines." Odo, from across the room, holding out a cup of the terrible wine he traveled with. "You look like a woman counting graves."

"I'm counting toll houses," she said, and took the wine anyway.

* * *

They moved out on the third morning, into that shocking blue clarity that follows a mountain storm, the whole world scoured and ringing. The snow lay four feet deep on the leeward stretches, but the wind had done half Ines's work for her, packing the trail firm along the windward wall of the pass. She led them up through the narrows at a pace that kept the mules honest and the men quiet, and near midday, cresting the last shoulder before the descent, she saw what she'd been hoping not to see.

Smoke. Thin, disciplined, one column—waystation smoke, not campfire smoke. Down in the fold of the valley where the old road met the river, exactly where her route crossed theirs.

She raised a fist. The caravan halted, mules blowing steam, and she left Odo holding the lead rope while she bellied up the last few yards of ridge and glassed the valley with the battered brass spyglass that had been Corvin's and, before that, his mentor's, and so on back until the brass itself felt like inheritance.

Six riders. Gray cloaks, the cut of them unmistakable even at this distance. Wardens. They weren't moving—they were *waiting*, drawn up at the ford in that patient way that meant someone had told them to watch a specific stretch of road. Which meant a report. Which meant someone in one of the villages below had sold information, or a Warden scout had read tracks in the fresh snow better than Ines would have liked.

Her route crossed the valley road for half a mile. There was no way around it that a loaded mule could manage. There was a way under it—an old smuggler's cut through the limestone, dry in winter, wide enough for stock—but it wasn't on any map the Guild admitted to owning, and taking eight strangers and their cargo into it meant trusting them with something worth more than salt.

She thought about Corvin again, the way she always did at moments like this. *A guide's job isn't the road,* he used to say. *Anyone can walk a road. A guide's job is deciding who walks it with you.*

She slid back down to the caravan and put on her best boring face.

"Change of plan," she announced. "We're not taking the valley road."

Odo's face performed several emotions in quick succession. "That's the road I'm paying for."

"You're paying me to get your salt to Greywater. The road is my business." She was already uncoiling the lead rope, turning the mules toward a shallow ravine that looked, to anyone who didn't know, like a dead end. "Warden patrol at the ford. Six riders. If they stop us, they'll 'inspect' the cargo, find nothing wrong, and impound it anyway pending paperwork that takes four months to process, by which time your salt is Crown salt and you're a poorer man."

"And the alternative?"

"A tunnel."

The drovers exchanged looks. The young one with the burn scar grinned like she'd offered him a festival.

"It's safe," she said, before Odo could start. "Dry, straight, half a mile, and I've walked it since I was fifteen. Single file, hands on the mule ahead, nobody talks. And—" she looked at each of them in turn, letting the weariness show through just enough to make it land, "—nobody speaks of it after. Not to other traders, not in taverns, not to pretty women or priests. The Guild has kept that cut for sixty years. You'll keep it too, or the next guide you hire will know your name, and we have long memories and longer winters."

Odo opened his mouth. Closed it. Nodded, the way rich men nod when they realize the person they're haggling with is holding the only lantern.

* * *

The cut was exactly as she'd promised, which was to say dark, dripping, and smelling of cold stone and bat dung, and it delivered them to the far side of the valley with their cargo, their necks, and their secrets intact. By dusk they were on the low road proper, and by the following noon, Greywater's chimneys were smoking on the horizon, and Odo paid her full rate plus a bonus he pretended was spontaneous and she pretended to be surprised by.

Then the caravan rolled on south, and Ines stood alone in the road watching them go, and the particular silence that follows a job's end settled over her shoulders like wet wool.

This was the part no one warned apprentices about. Not the storms, not the Wardens, not the frostbite or the falls or the years when the passes ate someone you loved. Those you could train for. It was this—the standing empty in a road after delivery, with coin in your pocket and nowhere you were needed—that thinned the guild year by year. People didn't leave the Salt Road because it killed them. They left because one day the quiet after the work got louder than the work.

Ines turned north and walked home.

* * *

The guildhouse sat above the village of Hollowmere, if you could call three roofs and a shrine a village, and it had once been a place of consequence: a long hall, stables for forty, a bell tower whose toll had governed caravan departures for centuries. Now the stable held two mules and a goat with opinions. Now the bell rang twice a day, meals and alarms, and mostly it rang for meals.

Sefa was in the kitchen garden when Ines came up the path, wrapped in three shawls against the wind, pulling the last blackened leeks from frozen ground with the grim efficiency of a woman who had buried a husband, two sons, and most of her guild and intended to outlive the ground itself out of spite.

"You're late," Sefa said, without looking up.

"Storm in the pass. Two days, maybe three."

"I know. I felt it Tuesday." Sefa finally glanced at her, and her eyes did the thing they always did—the quick inventory, boots, face, hands, the search for damage. Finding none, she lost interest. "Brann's back. Got in yesterday. He'll want to talk to you."

"Bad talk or good talk?"

"With Brann? He doesn't know yet. Ask him at supper."

Tomas was in the map room, which was what they called the small cold chamber where the guild's remaining charts hung, because calling it what it really was—the archive, the memory, the reason any of them were still here—felt too heavy for everyday use. He was drunk in the careful, functional way he managed most afternoons, copying a coastal chart by candlelight for a merchant who paid him a tenth of what his skill was worth, because his skill had a name attached to it that made respectable people flinch.

"Oyelaran," he said, not looking up. His handwriting stayed beautiful no matter what he'd drunk. It was the one thing about himself he hadn't managed to ruin.

"Tomas. Anyone come through?"

"No one comes through." He set down the pen and finally regarded her with bloodshot eyes that were, underneath everything, still the sharpest pair she knew. "That's rather the point of us, isn't it?"

She let that sit. Then: "Brann says he has news."

"Brann always has news. Half of it's true." Tomas turned back to his chart, then paused, quill hovering. "His half this time involves the high pass. Wardens surveying. Stakes and chain, Ines. Not patrol routes—survey lines. Someone in the capital has decided the old roads aren't just closed. They're being *erased*. Blasted, bridged over, struck from the new maps." He smiled, and it was the worst smile she knew, the one that had nothing behind it. "I'd know. Erasing things from maps is the family trade."

Ines stood in the doorway a moment longer, listening to the wind worry the eaves, thinking of the young drover asking whether anyone could truly shut a mountain.

Supper was barley soup, goat cheese, and the good bread Sefa made on Sundays and defended like a fortress. Brann arrived last, snow in his beard, a man built like the doorframes he had to duck through, with the careful movements of someone carrying a wound that had healed wrong. He'd been a Warden once. He didn't talk about the leaving, and Ines had never asked, because some debts you honor by not naming them.

"Well?" Sefa said, when the bowls were empty.

Brann turned his cup slowly on the table. "Survey crews on the high pass, like Tomas heard. But that's not the news." He looked up, and Ines saw something in his face she'd never seen there before—not fear exactly, but the memory of fear, worn smooth like a stone carried too long in a pocket. "There's a report circulating through the waystations. Marked urgent. An anomaly on the northern roads." He hesitated. "They're looking for a child, Ines. Alone, on foot, in country no child could survive. Seen three times now by different patrols, always at dusk, always moving north. Toward the Spire." He turned the cup again. "And here's the strange part. Every Warden who's gotten close enough to hear the child speak has filed the same note. Word for word, all three reports. *It spoke in no tongue known to the Crown.*"

The fire popped. Outside, the wind found the gap under the door and moaned through it, low and rising, almost like a voice.

Sefa had gone very still. Ines had known the old woman for nine years, through blizzards and funerals and one bad winter when they'd eaten the seed potatoes, and she had never once seen Sefa look afraid.

"Sefa?" Ines said quietly.

The matriarch stared into the middle distance, at something two hundred years away, and her hands, those indestructible hands that had pulled leeks from frozen earth and stitched wounds and buried the dead, were trembling against the table.

"That's impossible," Sefa said, in a voice Ines had never heard her use. "That dialect died with the kingdom."

"What dialect?" Ines asked. "Whose kingdom?"

But Sefa wasn't answering her. She was looking past all of them, toward the north window and the dark mass of the mountains beyond it, where somewhere in the gathering snow a barefoot child was walking toward a door that Ines Oyelaran did not yet know existed.

"Get your pack ready," Sefa said at last, and her voice had gone hard again, but underneath the hardness something had broken open. "All of you. Before the next storm closes everything."
