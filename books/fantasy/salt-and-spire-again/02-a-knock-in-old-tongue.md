## 2. A Knock in Old Tongue

The first snow came at dusk, the way it always did, as if it had been waiting all day for the light to fail before it dared to fall.

Ines stood in the doorway of the toll house and watched it come down over Thornfell—fat, unhurried flakes that turned the world soft and silent and lied about everything. Snow made the mountains look gentle. Snow made the passes look passable. Every winter she buried someone who had believed that.

Behind her, the guildhouse smelled of woodsmoke and barley stew and lamp oil going rancid because nobody could afford fresh. Four of them now. Sefa at the hearth, mending a rope with hands that had been splicing lines since before Ines was born. Brann in the corner, whittling a piece of pine into nothing in particular, his deserter's stillness so complete you could forget he was there until you needed him. Tomas asleep at the table with his cheek on a chart he'd never finish redrawing, an empty bottle standing sentry beside his elbow.

Four members. The Salt Road Guides' Guild, once two hundred strong, once the reason anything moved through Thornfell at all, now four people in a drafty stone house pretending to be an institution.

Ines pulled the door shut against the cold and let herself feel it for exactly three breaths—the grief, the smallness, the weight of being last—and then she put it away where she kept everything, in the room behind her ribs that had no door.

* * *

The knock came near midnight.

She knew it was strange before she was fully awake. Not the hour—travelers knocked at all hours, and the desperate knocked loudest—but the sound itself. Three knocks, spaced evenly, soft as a hand laid on wood rather than struck against it. No shouting. No *open in the Crown's name*. Just those three patient knocks, and then silence, as if whoever stood outside was content to wait until the mountain ended.

Ines took the short sword off its pegs out of habit more than fear and went down the stairs without lighting a lamp. She knew every step by the sound it made. Third from the bottom groaned; she skipped it.

Through the peephole: nothing but snow, falling thick and blue-white in the moonlight, drifting against the threshold like the mountain itself trying to come inside.

Then she looked down.

A child stood on their doorstep. Barefoot. In this. A girl of perhaps seven or eight, snow to her ankles, wearing a shift of some pale fabric that Ines's eyes kept sliding off—a weave she didn't recognize, fine as moth-wing, wrong for any loom she knew. The child wasn't shivering. That was the first wrong thing. The second was that the snow around her feet hadn't melted, hadn't even been disturbed, as though she'd been set down rather than arrived.

The girl looked up directly at the peephole, as if she knew precisely where Ines's eye was.

And knocked again. Those same three soft knocks.

Every instinct Ines owned said *don't open the door*. She'd grown up on stories about what knocked politely in the high passes. But she'd also grown up on her mentor's voice, and Kessin had taught her one rule above all others: *the mountain sends you what the mountain sends, and your job is not to ask why first. Your job is to open the door.*

Kessin was two years dead and had left her nothing but that rule and a journal full of cipher, and Ines hated how often she still obeyed him.

She opened the door.

Cold poured in. The child tilted her head back to look up at Ines, and her eyes were dark and calm and old—not old like a tired elder, old like something geological, like water that had been sitting in a deep place for a very long time. Snow settled on her lashes and did not melt.

"Guildhouse?" the girl said. And then a stream of words followed, quick and liquid and utterly foreign, syllables that seemed to move differently than common speech—rising where speech should fall, circling back on themselves, words that sounded less like they were being spoken than remembered aloud.

Ines's skin prickled from scalp to sole.

She knew that language. Not the words—she didn't know the words. But she knew the *sound* of it, because she'd heard it once, years ago, from a man who had wept while speaking it.

Kessin, drunk on plum brandy one midwinter night, sitting by this very hearth, had murmured something under his breath in a tongue Ines had never heard before or since. When she'd asked what it was, he'd gone quiet for a long time, staring into the fire, and finally said:

*"That's the language under all languages, little wren. Everything else is built on top of it. Don't go looking for it. Things that are sealed away dream in it."*

He'd laughed when he said it. It hadn't sounded like a joke.

Now a barefoot child stood in the snow speaking it fluently, and Ines felt the floor of the world tilt a degree, just enough to notice.

"It's all right," Ines said slowly, kneeling, keeping her voice low and even the way she would with a spooked mule on a ledge. "You're safe. Come inside."

The girl understood none of it and all of it. She stepped over the threshold with the careful precision of someone entering a place she'd been told about, and Ines closed the door behind her, and the wind rose immediately outside as if it had only been holding its breath.

* * *

Sefa was already awake. Of course she was. The matriarch slept like a cat that had learned to distrust naps.

She came down the stairs with a candle and stopped dead on the fourth step, and Ines watched sixty years of composure crack like river ice.

The child stood dripping in the entry hall, and Sefa looked at her the way people look at a ghost—not with fear, exactly, but with recognition so violent it resembled grief.

"Where did she come from?" Sefa asked. Her voice was steady. Her knuckles, white around the candlestick, were not.

"The step. Barefoot. No tracks leading up to the house—I checked twice."

"No tracks."

"Not one. Snow untouched in both directions, all the way to the tree line."

Sefa descended the rest of the stairs very slowly, the way you approach a wounded animal or an unexploded shell. She crouched in front of the girl, and the child regarded her with that deep-water calm, and then the girl spoke again—that flowing, circling language, a question this time by its cadence, ending on a word that sounded like *sel-ah-VES*.

Sefa went pale.

Not startled-pale. Not frightened-pale. Ines had seen Sefa face down Warden patrols, avalanches, and a tax assessor with delusions of authority, and the woman's color had never shifted. This was different. This was the pallor of a person hearing a name they'd spent a lifetime making sure no one would say.

"Sefa?"

"Get me the brandy," Sefa said. Her voice came out sanded flat. "The good bottle. The one we're saving for my funeral."

Ines fetched it. By the time she returned, Sefa had wrapped the child in her own shawl and seated her by the fire, and the girl was drinking warmed milk with the grave deliberation of someone performing a ritual rather than eating a meal. Brann had appeared from somewhere, silent as ever, and stood against the wall with his arms crossed, watching the door. He hadn't asked a single question. He'd simply positioned himself between the child and the outside world, which was Brann's entire vocabulary of care.

Tomas still snored at the table. Some miracles spared everyone.

"Sefa," Ines said quietly, setting the brandy down. "What did she say?"

Sefa took a long pull straight from the bottle. Then another. Then she looked at the fire for a while, and Ines realized the matriarch was deciding something—weighing something—had perhaps been weighing it for decades, waiting for a scale to tip.

"She asked," Sefa said at last, "if this was still the guildhouse. The Guides' Guild. By name. Our name." She took a third drink. "In our founding tongue."

"I don't understand."

"No," Sefa said. "You don't. And I have spent forty years making sure you wouldn't have to." She set the bottle down with a click that sounded final, like a lock turning. "Wake Tomas. We're going to need the drunk."

* * *

It took Tomas three tries to stand, and even then he leaned on the table like a ship on its moorings, blinking at the child with bloodshot disbelief while Sefa told them—all of it, or the shape of it—in a low voice, in the kitchen, while the girl watched them from across the room with her ancient dark eyes and pretended, convincingly, not to understand every word.

"The guild was never founded to guide caravans," Sefa said. "That's what we became. What we were founded as—" she exhaled through her nose, long and slow, "—was wardens. Not the Crown's kind. Ours. Two hundred years ago, when Vessar was erased—when the maps were burned and the roads renamed and the king's line struck from every record—the Guides' Guild was the ones who made sure it stayed erased."

Ines felt the word land somewhere beneath her sternum. *Vessar.* Even saying it felt transgressive, like stepping off a marked path.

"There was a kingdom in these mountains," Sefa continued. "Older than the Crown. Older than the salt trade. And it didn't fall—it was *sealed*. Closed behind a door, and the door closed behind a wall of forgetting, and the forgetting was maintained. By us. Generation after generation, the Guild kept the old routes unmarked, kept the old names unsaid, kept travelers away from certain places without ever telling them why. We weren't smugglers, Ines. We were the seal's caretakers. The salt was always just the cover."

"And her language—"

"Royal Vessarine." Sefa's mouth shaped the words like they hurt. "Court dialect. Not the merchant tongue, not the valley speech. The dialect only the royal household spoke. I heard it once, as a girl, from my grandmother, who heard it from hers." She looked toward the fireside, where the child had finished her milk and was watching the flames with an expression of profound, unsettling familiarity—as if she recognized fire itself. "No one has spoken royal Vessarine in two hundred years. It should be a dead language. It should be dust. And that child speaks it like it's her mother's milk."

Brann spoke for the first time, his voice a low rumble. "Where are her parents? Who leaves a child barefoot in a storm?"

"No one," Ines said. "That's the point. There are no tracks, Brann. She didn't walk here. She *arrived*."

Tomas had gone very still. He was staring at the girl with the fixed intensity he usually reserved for a chart with an error in it, and Ines saw the drunkard recede and something older surface underneath—the cartographer, the man who'd once held the finest mapmaker's commission in the realm before his family's name became synonymous with erasure.

"Tomas," Sefa said. "Say it."

He wet his lips. "There's a story in my family," he said hoarsely. "A debt, my grandfather called it. The Thornburys weren't just mapmakers. We were the ones who drew the erasure. Every chart, every atlas, every survey—we removed Vessar. Scrubbed it clean. My family's been paying for it for six generations, and I've been drowning the debt in brandy because there's no other way to pay a thing like that." His eyes flicked to the child, then away, as if she were too bright to look at directly. "But there was one thing we were never able to erase. One mark that appears on the trueborn line, generation after generation, no matter how far the blood scattered. My grandfather called it the keyhole mark. Said if we ever saw it, we'd know the kingdom had found a way to knock."

The fire popped. Outside, the wind pressed against the shutters like a hand.

"What mark?" Ines asked.

Tomas crossed the room slowly. He crouched beside the girl the way Sefa had, and when he spoke, his voice had gone gentle in a way Ines had never heard from him. "Little one. May I see your wrist?"

The child considered him for a long moment with those deep-water eyes. Then, with the solemnity of a queen granting an audience, she extended her arm.

On the inside of her wrist, pale against her brown skin, was a birthmark: a small spiral, coiled like a sleeping serpent, ringed by seven dots.

Tomas made a sound Ines had never heard a grown man make—somewhere between a sob and a laugh, strangled at birth. He sat back hard on the floor.

"That's it," he whispered. "Two hundred years. That's the mark."

The girl withdrew her arm and looked at each of them in turn—Sefa with her cracked composure, Brann with his silent vigil, Tomas on the floor, Ines with her hand unconsciously resting on her sword hilt—and then she said something in that liquid, circling tongue. One sentence. Soft. Almost tender.

Sefa translated, though Ines suspected translation was unnecessary, because some things cross every language there is, including the ones underneath them.

"She says," Sefa whispered, "*I knocked, and you opened. Now the Door knows your names.*"

Outside, the snow fell thicker, and somewhere high on the mountain—too high for any living throat, too steady for any wind—something began, very faintly, to sing.
