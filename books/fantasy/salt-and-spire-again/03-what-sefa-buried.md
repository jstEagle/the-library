## 3. What Sefa Buried

The child slept in the loft above the common room, wrapped in Ines's spare blanket, and Sefa sat beneath her with a lamp turned low and a ledger open to a page she had not looked at in forty years.

She had told the others to sleep. Ines had argued—Ines always argued—and then gone up the stairs anyway, because the girl had reached for her hand when Sefa tried to settle her alone. That was the thing about Ines Oyelaran that both comforted and frightened her: the girl collected strays the way the mountain collected snow, silently and without any sense of the weight accumulating. And now the snowfall had a voice, and the voice was Vessarine.

Royal Vessarine. The high dialect, the court tongue, the one with the soft aspirated vowels that common folk had flattened out generations before the Erasure. Sefa had heard it exactly once in her life, spoken by a dying woman in a snowdrift, and she had spent every year since pretending she hadn't.

She opened the ledger.

It wasn't really a ledger. It was bound like one—salt-stained leather, brass corners gone green—and if a Warden ever searched the guildhouse they would find columns of tolls and caravan weights and nothing else, because the true pages were sewn into the false ones, thin as onion skin, written in the old guild cipher that only three people alive could read and two of them were dead.

Sefa read by lamplight, her lips moving though no sound came.

*The Guild of Salt Road Guides was founded in the Year of Ash, its charter granted by the Crown of Torvald for the safe conduct of commerce through the Thornfell passes.* That was the version taught to apprentices. It was even true, in the way a mask is true of a face.

*The Guild of Salt Road Guides was founded in the Year of Ash,* said the hidden pages, *to ensure that no road, chart, or song should lead again to the Hollow Door.*

Sefa closed her eyes.

Forty years she had kept this. Forty years of teaching children knots and weather-reading and the hundred small mercies of the high passes, and never once telling them what the first Guides had actually sworn: that the guild existed not to keep the roads open but to keep one road closed. That every map the guild drew was drawn slightly wrong on purpose. That every route they guarded was guarded so that no traveler, no pilgrim, no curious fool would ever stumble down the wrong valley and find the door that did not open onto anywhere at all.

And now a barefoot child had walked out of a snowstorm speaking the language of the kingdom behind that door, and Sefa's secrets had come due like a debt called in by a creditor who had finally found her address.

* * *

She heard the floorboard creak and knew it was Ines before she looked up. The girl moved quietly for someone so loud-mouthed; it was one of the many contradictions Sefa had stopped trying to resolve decades ago.

"You should be sleeping," Sefa said, not looking up from the ledger.

"So should you." Ines came down the last steps and stood at the edge of the lamplight, arms crossed, hair still damp from melting snow. "You went white as a salt flat when she spoke. I've seen you face down an avalanche with better color."

"An avalanche doesn't know your name."

"The child doesn't know yours either."

"No," Sefa said. "But what she speaks does."

She turned the ledger around and pushed it across the table. Ines hesitated—she was not a girl who hesitated often, which made it worth noting when she did—and then sat and pulled the book toward her.

"This is a toll ledger," Ines said.

"Look closer."

Ines tilted the page toward the lamp. Sefa watched her eyes move, watched the moment the hidden stitching caught the light, watched understanding arrive the way weather arrives over a ridge—slowly, then all at once.

"There's writing under the writing."

"There always has been. Since the founding." Sefa poured herself a finger of plum brandy, thought about it, and poured a second finger. She slid one across the table. Ines took it without protest, which told Sefa more about the state of the girl than anything else. "Read the heading. Aloud, if you can manage the cipher."

Ines worked at it, frowning, her lips shaping the old characters. She'd learned the cipher as an apprentice, as all of them did—or thought they had, learning it for tallying smuggled goods—and she decoded slowly but surely. "The Guild... was founded... to guard—" She stopped. Looked up. "Guard what?"

"Keep reading."

"*To guard knowledge of the Hollow Door,*" Ines read. Her voice had gone flat, careful, the voice she used on ice she didn't trust. "What is the Hollow Door?"

Sefa drank. The brandy burned all the way down and settled in her chest like a coal, and still it wasn't enough warmth for what she had to say next.

"Sit properly," she said. "This is a long story, and I've only got the strength to tell it once."

* * *

"Two hundred and twenty-two years ago," Sefa began, "there was a kingdom in these mountains called Vessar. Not a small one. Its capital sat where the Spire still stands—the tower you can see from the high pass on clear days, the one the caravans use to navigate by. Vessar held eleven valleys, four rivers, and more wealth than Torvald's crown could count. They were famous for three things: their salt, their songs, and their doors."

"Doors?"

"Vessarine masons were the finest in the world. Every great house in Torvald wanted a Vessarine door, carved with their knotwork, hung on hinges that never squeaked. There's a saying in the oldest building records: *a Vessar door opens only for those it loves.* Superstition, everyone assumed." Sefa turned the ledger to a new page, one of the hidden ones, where a drawing had been inked so long ago the lines had gone brown. A doorway. Standing alone, in no wall, framed by stone that seemed to grow out of the mountainside like a tooth out of a jaw. "Except it wasn't superstition. It was craft. Real craft, of a kind we've lost. And the greatest of all their works was the Hollow Door."

"What does it do?"

"It stands in a valley that isn't on any map, at the end of a road that doesn't exist anymore, and it opens onto Vessar." Sefa let that sit. "Not onto where Vessar *was*. Onto Vessar. The whole of it. As it was."

Ines set down her brandy. "That's not possible."

"Child, I have been alive sixty-eight years and I have buried two husbands, outlived every guide I trained before you, and watched the Crown send soldiers to close roads that my grandmother's grandmother helped cut. I have no patience left for 'not possible.' The world is full of things that are not possible. Most of them are simply waiting for someone foolish enough to knock."

Ines was quiet for a moment. Then, because she was Ines: "So why is the kingdom gone? If the Door opens onto it, why do the maps say Vessar fell?"

"Because it did fall. Or rather—it was put away." Sefa traced the drawing of the door with one blunt finger. "In the last year of Vessar, something happened in the capital. The records are maddeningly vague—deliberately vague, I think, by people who were terrified of being understood. Ambassadors stopped answering letters. Trade caravans went in and didn't come out, or came out wrong, with men who smiled too much and couldn't remember their mothers' names. The neighboring kingdoms sent envoys. One envoy came back. He wrote a report of nine words and then hanged himself, and the report was sealed by the crown and lost, and the only reason anyone knows about it is that a guild archivist copied it into this book a hundred years later." She tapped the page. "*Do not open. Do not answer. Do not let them finish the sentence.*"

"That's five words."

"That's what terrifies me about it. Either he miscounted or something was taken from him." Sefa closed her hand into a fist on the table. "Whatever it was, the other kingdoms didn't negotiate. They didn't besiege. They did something worse. They paid the last Vessarine masons—there were seven, and the records name six of them—to build a sealing. To turn the kingdom's own craft against itself. The Hollow Door was the way in. So they sealed the Door, and the Door sealed everything behind it. The valleys folded. The roads unmade themselves. The maps went blank and then the mapmakers went to work making sure they stayed blank. And Vessar—" she spread her hands, "—Vessar went *away*. Not destroyed. Withdrawn. Like a word swallowed before it's finished."

"And the guild?"

"The guild was founded eleven years after the sealing, by descendants of the masons and by Torvald's spymasters, who between them decided that the best lock in the world is a secret nobody knows exists. The Salt Road Guides would be respectable. Honest traders' protectors. Beloved on the passes. And every generation, a handful of us—the guildmother or -father, and whoever came after—would learn the truth and carry it, and make sure the roads stayed just crooked enough, the charts just wrong enough, that no one ever found the valley again." She met Ines's eyes. "Your mentor knew. Yara knew. I swore her in the winter she turned twenty, same as I'm swearing you in tonight, except I'm doing it badly, over brandy, with a child asleep overhead instead of a quiet ceremony in the deep cellar."

Ines stared at her. Sefa had raised the girl since she was nine, plucked half-frozen off a refugee wagon with dead parents and a temper like flint, and in fifteen years she had never seen her look quite like this—not angry, not hurt, but recalculating everything, the way she did when a pass she'd trusted turned out to have a cornice.

"All those routes you made me memorize," Ines said slowly. "The ones that don't go anywhere. The dead ends with the cairns. I thought they were just... old trade paths that dried up."

"They're fences. Every one of them. You've been walking the perimeter of a prison your whole career, and so was I, and so was my teacher, back to the Year of Ash." Sefa rubbed her eyes. "We are not guides, Ines. We are gaolers. We just mostly guard an empty cell."

* * *

Above them, faintly, the child cried out in her sleep.

Both of them were moving before the second sound. Sefa took the stairs slower than her heart wanted to; Ines took them faster than was sensible, and by the time Sefa reached the loft the girl was already sitting beside the child, one hand on her shoulder, murmuring the low nonsense syllables that pass for comfort in every language.

The child—Nia, they had taken to calling her, because she had given no name and Ines had pointed at the night sky through the frost-furred window and said *nia*, star, in the trade pidgin, and the child had repeated it like she was tasting it—Nia was thrashing. Sweat on her brow despite the cold room. And she was talking. Streaming words in that liquid, vowel-heavy speech, too fast and too frightened for sleep-talk.

Sefa stood in the doorway and felt forty years of careful silence crack down the middle.

Because she understood her.

Not all of it—the royal dialect was formal, ornate, a court's language, and the child was speaking it the way a homesick noblewoman might, all honorifics and old courtesies—but enough. Enough to freeze the blood.

*—the corridor is folding, please, the corridor is folding, I was told to wait, I have been waiting, who remembers the count, who keeps the count—*

"She's dreaming about it," Sefa heard herself say. Her voice sounded like someone else's, old and scraped. "She's dreaming about inside."

Ines looked up sharply. "You understand her."

"A little." More than a little. Far more than a little, and every word was a stone dropping into a well whose bottom Sefa had believed was deeper. "My teacher made me learn it. All guildmothers learn it. It's part of the burden—you can't guard a door if you can't read what's scratched on it." She came forward and knelt, joints protesting, and took the child's other hand. It was warm. Far too warm for the room, warm as a hearthstone, and Sefa remembered the old records' phrase for the Door's threshold—*warm as a held breath*—and made herself stop remembering.

"Nia," she said, in Vessarine. High dialect, court form, the aspirated vowels coming back to her tongue like a bird returning to an abandoned nest. "*Peace. You are outside. You are kept. Sleep.*"

The child stilled.

Her eyes opened—dark, enormous, older than dark ought to be—and fixed on Sefa, and she said something in a whisper. Four words. Formal. The kind of thing a subject says to a queen, or a ward to a guardian.

Sefa translated it before she could stop herself, because Ines was watching and lying to Ines had never once worked.

"She said: *You speak the keeper's tongue. Then the keeping is not ended.*"

The lamp flame leaned sideways, though there was no draft.

* * *

They went back downstairs because neither of them could bear the loft after that. Ines carried the lamp; Sefa carried the ledger and the brandy bottle and the particular exhaustion of a woman watching her life's architecture collapse.

"Say the rest of it," Ines said. "All of it. Whatever you've been circling since you went pale at the door."

Sefa laughed, short and bitter. "You always did know when I was managing you."

"I grew up on your managing. It has a smell."

So Sefa told her the rest. About the dialects—that Vessarine had split, in its last century, into the common speech of the valleys and the high speech of the court, and that the child spoke the high speech not as a scholar learns it but as a native, with the rhythms of someone raised hearing it daily. About the birthmark she had glimpsed when they'd bathed the frost from the girl's feet—a mark below the ankle bone, a spiral of seven turns, which appeared in exactly one place in the entire hidden archive: pressed into the wax seal of the last royal house of Vessar. About the fact that the sealing was supposed to hold for as long as the royal line endured, and that the royal line had been inside when the Door closed, and that no heir should exist outside it.

"Unless," Sefa said, and her voice failed her, and she started again. "Unless someone was sent out. Before the end. Through the Door, past the seal, by people who knew what was coming. A seed saved from a burning field." She looked at the ledger, at the brown-inked door. "Or unless the seal is failing, and the kingdom is... leaking. Pushing its last piece out through the cracks like water through a dam."

"Which one?"

"I don't know. That's the truth, Ines. I have guarded this secret my whole life and I don't know the single most important thing about it." She pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes. "I should have burned this book thirty years ago. I told myself the guild was dying anyway, that the roads were closing, that the Wardens would take the passes and it wouldn't matter. I told myself keeping the secret was mercy. But it wasn't mercy. It was cowardice wearing mercy's coat. And now it's walked up to our door barefoot in the snow, and it knows the keeper's tongue, and I'm an old woman who has forgotten how to be brave in time."

Ines was quiet for a long moment. When Sefa lowered her hands, the girl was looking at the ledger with an expression Sefa recognized from mirrors: grief and fury holding each other upright.

"Yara," Ines said. "Her last route. The one she never came back from. Did she know about the Door?"

Sefa's chest tightened. "She asked me about it. Three days before she left. I lied to her. I told her it was smuggling business, crown business, nothing for a guide on a contract." The lie sat in the room between them, forty years old and three months dead, and it had killed Yara as surely as the mountain had—or so Sefa feared, in the small hours, more nights than she admitted. "If she found what I wouldn't show her—"

"Then she died guarding a secret she wasn't allowed to know she was guarding." Ines stood. Took the ledger. Held it against her chest like something rescued from a fire. "No more. Whatever comes through that door tomorrow—Wardens, winter, the ghost of Vessar itself—we do it with the truth. All of it. Starting with finding someone who can read whatever else is in this book, because your cipher is older than both of us and I want a second opinion."

"There is one man," Sefa said reluctantly. "Tomas Ferren. Royal cartographer, before he drank his commission away. His family drew the Erasure charts—they're the ones who scrubbed Vessar off every map in Torvald. If anyone alive knows what was removed, and where, it's him."

"Where do I find him?"

"The Guttered Lantern, in Millbrook, most nights. Losing arguments with a bottle." Sefa sighed, and it came out as almost a smile. "Tell him Sefa of the Salt Guild sent you. And tell him—" she searched for the words, and found the ones she'd been refusing to say for forty years, "—tell him the debt is called in. He'll understand. His family owed Vessar a map. Maybe it's time somebody paid it."

Outside, the wind shifted, and for a moment the snow against the shutters sounded less like weather and more like knocking—soft, patient, from a very long way away.

Sefa poured the last of the brandy and did not drink it.
