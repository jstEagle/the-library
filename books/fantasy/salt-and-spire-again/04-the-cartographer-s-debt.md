## 4. The Cartographer's Debt

Tomas Veyra woke with his cheek on the table and the taste of last night's wine gone sour in his mouth, which was how he woke most days now, and which was still better than the way he'd woken in the capital, in a house with a real bed and servants and his father's voice coming up the stairs like weather.

The guildhouse kitchen smelled of woodsmoke and porridge. Someone had covered him with a blanket. That was the thing about the Salt Road Guides' Guild—half its members were liars, smugglers, and drunks, and they still covered you with a blanket.

He sat up carefully, so the room wouldn't tilt, and found Sefa at the hearth stirring a pot with the grim economy of a woman who had been stirring pots since before he was born.

"You snore," she said.

"I grieve," he corrected. "It sounds similar."

"Your grief kept Brann awake half the night. He's chopping wood about it." She ladled porridge into a bowl and set it in front of him with a clack that said *eat or don't, but stop talking*. "Where's your map case?"

Tomas's hand went to his side before his brain caught up. The leather case was there, as it always was, buckled to his belt even drunk, even asleep, even now when there was nothing left in it worth stealing. Old habit. Older shame.

"Under me," he said. "Why?"

"Because Ines wants you in the reading room. Now."

"Ines wants a lot of things. Ines wants the Crown to un-invent Wardens and wants winter to be polite about itself. I want my father to have loved me. We don't always get—"

"Tomas." Sefa turned from the hearth. Her face did something he'd never seen it do in six years of drinking her wine and dodging her chores. It looked *afraid*. "There's a child at our door who speaks Vessarine."

The name went through him like a nail through wet paper. He put both hands flat on the table because otherwise they'd shake where people could see them.

"That's not funny," he said.

"No," Sefa agreed. "It isn't."

* * *

The reading room of the Salt Road Guildhouse had once held the finest collection of route-maps in the Thornfells. Now it held a table, two chairs, a stove that smoked if you looked at it wrong, and Ines Oyelaran standing over a child like a shepherd over something the wolves hadn't found yet.

The child sat on a stool with her bare feet tucked under her, wrapped in one of Brann's shirts, watching Tomas with eyes too old for her face. Dark hair, maybe seven years old, maybe eight. A smudge of ash on one cheek. And when she saw him come through the door she said something—a short phrase, liquid and strange—and Ines went very still.

"What did she say?" Tomas asked.

"I don't know. She's said it to everyone. Sefa won't translate. Sefa says—" Ines stopped. Started again. "Sefa says she can't. Not yet. She says you have to see first."

"See what? I'm a drunk with a dead trade, Ines, not a priest."

"You're a Veyra." Ines said the surname the way other people said *plague*. "You're the only person within three hundred miles whose family ever had anything to do with Vessar. So look at her. Please. Look properly."

Tomas looked.

It was stupid, what happened next. There was no magic in it, no omen, no thunder. He just looked at the child the way he'd been trained to look at things since he could hold a stylus—the way his father had beaten into him across twenty years of *observe, boy, observe, a map is a lie unless the eye behind it tells the truth*—and his eye told him the truth whether he wanted it or not.

Her proportions were wrong for a mountain child. Too fine-boned. The arch of the foot, even tucked away—he'd seen it when she shifted—too high. The hair grew in a whorl at the crown, double-crowned, which any Veyra would recognize because every dynastic portrait of the Vessarine royal line showed it, because his family had *copied* those portraits for four generations before they burned them.

And below her left ear, half-hidden by the collar of Brann's shirt: a birthmark. A small dark shape, like a flame seen through smoke.

Tomas knew the shape. He had spent one entire miserable year of his apprenticeship engraving it onto a plate that was then destroyed, along with every other trace of the kingdom it belonged to, on his grandfather's orders, while his grandfather wept.

"The seal-mark," he heard himself say. His voice came out sanded down to almost nothing. "That's the royal seal-mark of House Aurevan. It's on the coronation regalia. It was on—" He stopped. Swallowed. "It was on everything. Once."

Ines exhaled slowly, like someone setting down a weight she'd carried up a mountain. "So it's true. She's—"

"I don't know what she is." Tomas backed up a step. His hip hit the doorframe. "I know what she *isn't*, which is possible. Vessar has been gone for two hundred years. Not conquered—*gone*. Erased. My family erased it. Every chart, every atlas, every harbor record, every gravestone inscription in three kingdoms. Do you understand what that means? It means no one alive knows where it *was*. It means this child is speaking a language that has not existed since before the Thornfell passes were mapped, and she is wearing the mark of a royal house that officially never—"

The child spoke again. The same phrase. But this time she said it looking directly at him, and this time, God help him, he caught the cadence of it, because his grandfather had made him memorize the sound of it off a wax cylinder recording—one of the last surviving artifacts, played exactly once in a locked room, then melted down—and the child's accent was not the accent on the cylinder. Hers was older. Rougher. Like the difference between a courtier's verse and a fisherman's prayer.

"She said, roughly," Tomas translated, his mouth dry, "*You are the one who unmade the maps.*"

Silence. The stove ticked. Somewhere outside, Brann's axe fell and fell again.

"And then," Tomas went on, because the words were coming now and would not stop, "she said, *I have been waiting a long time for someone to make them again.*"

* * *

He drank. Of course he drank. He took the bottle down from behind the loose stone in his room—the good bottle, the one he saved for anniversaries, and every day was an anniversary of something—and he drank sitting on the floor with his back against the bed, and he let the memories come up like floodwater because fighting them had never once worked.

His grandfather, Edran Veyra, Royal Cartographer, kneeling in the archive vault at midnight surrounded by plates and plates and plates of the most beautiful work the family had ever produced—the Vessar folio, forty sheets, coastlines and cities and the great Spire drawn with such loving precision you could count its windows—and feeding them one by one into the acid bath while a Crown censor stood over him with a list.

"Why?" young Tomas had asked. He'd been eleven. He'd followed his grandfather down because he'd wanted to see the famous folio, the masterpiece, the thing whispered about in the guilds.

Edran hadn't stopped working. His hands had been steady—that was the terrible part, the hands never shook—but tears ran off his jaw and dripped onto the copper plates and hissed.

"Because we were ordered," he said. "And because it's kinder this way."

"Kinder to who?"

"To everyone who remembers." The acid took another sheet. The Spire went first, always; the censors made sure of that. "A country isn't land, boy. A country is a wound the land remembers. Some wounds are better cauterized than cleaned."

Tomas had asked what was in Vessar that needed cauterizing. His grandfather had looked at him then—looked at him the way Sefa had looked at Ines this morning, afraid—and said, "Nothing. Nothing at all. That's what makes it worse."

Then the last plate went in, and the folio was gone, and within five years Vessar was gone from every document in the civilized world, and within ten years people had begun to insist it had never existed at all, and within twenty the word itself sounded like nonsense, like a sneeze, like a mishearing of *vessel* or *vesper*.

And Tomas Veyra had grown up to become the finest cartographer of his generation precisely because his family had destroyed the finest cartography of theirs, and he had spent his whole career drawing coastlines that were missing a piece, rivers that ended in nothing, a hole in the world shaped exactly like a kingdom, and every map he made was an apology he didn't know how to write.

Until the Crown decided his talents were better used making military surveys of the Thornfell passes for the new Warden corps. Until he'd seen what the surveys were *for*—closing roads, strangling villages, sealing the mountains like his grandfather had sealed the folio—and he'd refused, and been disgraced, and crawled up here to die slowly among people kind enough to let him.

He finished the bottle. It didn't help. It never helped. But it made the shaking quieter, and quiet was all he'd ever really asked of anything.

A knock at his door. Ines's voice: "She drew something. On the slate. You should see it."

"Not tonight."

"It's a map, Tomas."

He closed his eyes. "Everything's a map. That's the disease."

"It's a map of a place none of us have ever seen," Ines said through the door, "and Nia drew it without hesitating, and there's a tower in the middle of it with windows you can count."

* * *

He went down. He hated himself for going down. He went anyway, because his grandfather's hands had been steady and his own were not, and because somewhere under sixty feet of guilt and gin there was still a boy who had wanted to see the folio.

Nia stood at the big table in the reading room, on a chair, slate in hand. She'd filled it edge to edge. Coastlines, confident and clean. Rivers with names in that liquid script. Mountain ranges rendered in the old Veyra convention—hachures, the technique his family had invented, the technique nobody else used anymore because everybody else had moved on to contour lines a century ago.

She'd drawn it in his family's style. Or rather—Tomas felt the floor tilt under him, gin or truth, hard to say—she'd drawn it in the style his family had learned *from* Vessarine masters, back when the Veyras were students and Vessar was the light of the world.

"There," Ines said quietly, pointing. "The tower."

The Spire. Drawn twice, actually—once in plan, once in elevation, side by side, the way a proper surveyor would do it. And beneath the elevation, in careful block letters in the old script, a label. Nia tapped it and looked at Tomas and waited, patient as stone, the way you wait for someone slow to catch up.

"*Hollow Door,*" Tomas read. Then he read the smaller annotation beneath it, and his legs genuinely did buckle, and he sat down hard in a chair because the alternative was the floor. "Beneath it says—she's written *the anchor sleeps, the anchor wakes, the anchor chooses.*"

"What does that mean?" Brann asked from the doorway, arms crossed, woodsmoke still on him.

"I don't know." Tomas laughed, and it came out wrong, high and cracked. "I don't know what any of it means. I know that this coastline is accurate. I know that because I've spent thirty years staring at the gap it used to fill, and she's just filled it, and it fits, it fits *exactly*, like a key in a lock that's been rusting shut for two hundred years." He pressed the heels of his hands into his eyes. "Do you know what my family did? Do either of you actually know? We didn't just erase a country. We erased the *warning*. Whatever's written under that tower—whatever 'anchor' means—my grandfather burned the instructions and called it mercy, and I spent my life redrawing his empty spaces, and now a seven-year-old child walks out of a snowstorm speaking the language I was taught to forget, and she's asking me—"

He stopped. Because Nia had climbed down from the chair. Because she had come around the table, barefoot and solemn, and taken the slate, and wiped it clean with her sleeve, and was now writing something new, slowly, in the old script, holding it up for him alone.

Three words. He translated them aloud because his throat wouldn't do anything else:

"*Help me remember.*"

The stove ticked. Snow ticked against the window. Brann shifted his weight and said nothing, which from Brann was a speech.

"I can't," Tomas said to her. His voice broke on the second word. "Child, listen to me. I'm not—I'm a drunk. I'm the last of a family of destroyers. Everything I touch gets erased or surveyed for people who burn things. You don't want my help. You want anyone's help but mine."

Nia tilted her head. Then she wrote one more line, and this time she didn't hold it up—she set the slate gently on the table in front of him, turned it to face him, and stepped back, and folded her hands, and waited.

*My grandfather also burned things,* it said. *He said the ones who burned must be the ones who rebuild.*

Tomas stared at it for a long time.

"That's not—" he started. Stopped. Started again. "How do you know about my grandfather?"

But of course she didn't answer. She was mute except in that tongue, and the tongue was two centuries dead, and she was standing in a guildhouse kitchen-light looking at him with the patience of someone who had, by her own account, been waiting a very long time already and could evidently wait a while more.

He thought of his grandfather's steady hands. He thought of the acid hissing over the Spire. He thought of thirty years of maps with a hole in them, and the hole now filled, sitting on the table in a child's chalk.

"If I do this," he said hoarsely, "we do it properly. Survey discipline. Every route recorded, every landmark fixed, every word she speaks written down in both scripts. No more drunken mornings. No more—" he gestured vaguely at himself, at the whole ruin of his life. "No more hiding. If the Crown finds out what she is, they'll do to her what they did to the folio. So we work fast, and we work clean, and we don't tell a soul beyond these walls. Agreed?"

Ines said, "Agreed," before he'd finished, quick and fierce, and Brann nodded once.

Nia picked up the slate. Wiped it. Wrote.

*First lesson tomorrow,* it said. And then, after a pause in which the chalk hovered and her small face did something complicated—grief and hope fighting for the same ground—she added a fourth word.

*Bring your steadiness.*

Tomas laughed. It hurt, and it helped, the way truth does.

* * *

He didn't sleep. He sat at his desk instead, sober for the first time in months, and unbuckled the map case he'd carried for six years like a shackle, and opened it, and took out the one thing inside it he'd never shown anyone: a rubbing he'd made at nineteen, secretly, from the last surviving fragment of the Vessar folio—a corner scrap his grandfather had missed, hidden inside the binding of an atlas of somewhere safe and boring.

A fragment of coastline. A few words of label. And the base of a tower, drawn in hachures, with windows you could count.

He laid it on the desk beside Nia's slate drawing, and held a lamp over both, and compared them line by line, heart hammering, until there was no doubt left in him at all.

They matched. Perfectly. To the stroke.

Which meant the child wasn't remembering a story she'd been told. She was remembering the *original*—the source his family had copied, the master from which all Veyra maps descended—and she had drawn it from memory in a snowbound guildhouse with chalk, better than his family had ever managed with copper and acid.

Tomas Veyra sat back in the dark and said, out loud, to the empty room, to his grandfather's ghost, to the hole in every map he'd ever made:

"All right. All right. I'm coming."

Outside, far off in the black pass, a horn sounded—long, low, wrong, a note no caravan or patrol ever blew—and did not sound again.
