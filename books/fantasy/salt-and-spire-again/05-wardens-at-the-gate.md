## 5. Wardens at the Gate

The knock came at dawn, and it was wrong.

Ines knew every kind of knock the guildhouse door had ever given her. Caravan masters knocked twice, sharp, wanting their tolls settled before the light got good. Smugglers knocked three times with a pause between the second and third—guild code older than she was. Peddlers knocked soft and apologetic. Sefa never knocked; she owned the building and said so.

This was four knocks, evenly spaced, patient as a man counting coins. Official.

She was in the kitchen already, coaxing the stove awake, when Brann appeared in the doorway without a sound. For a man his size—a head taller than her, shoulders like a load-bearing beam—he moved quiet as snowfall. He'd been a Warden once. She could see it in how he stood now: weight on the balls of his feet, eyes already tracking to the window, to the road below the switchback, to the treeline.

"Four," he said.

"I counted."

"Riders?" His voice was low. Nia was still asleep in the loft above the common room, and they had all learned in three days to speak around her sleeping like people speak around an open wound.

"Two horses, maybe three." Ines set down the poker. Her hands were steady. She made them steady. "You hear them come up?"

"No one comes up that road quietly in frost. They wanted us to hear." Brann's jaw worked. "That's a message. *We're here. We know you know.*"

Through the frosted window, down where the salt road bent around the shoulder of Thornfell, Ines could see them: dark cloaks, grey-and-white livery beneath, the Crown's mountain badge stitched at the shoulder—a peak impaled by a sword. Wardens. Three of them, and a fourth figure walking slightly ahead, unhurried, the way men walk when they've never once needed to hurry.

Even at this distance she knew him. Everyone on the roads knew Aldric Vane by reputation if not by face: the Warden-Captain who'd closed the Kestrel Pass in a single season, who burned smugglers' caches from here to the coast, who had personally hanged the Marrow brothers from a bridge over their own contraband. The Crown's answer to the old freedom of the mountains.

And he was standing at the foot of her road.

"Sefa," Ines called softly.

The matriarch was already coming down the hall, wrapped in her shawl, hair unbound and iron-grey. Behind her, half-hidden on the stairs, Tomas hovered with a bottle in one hand and no memory of picking it up. And above them all, small feet padded across the loft boards—Nia, awake, looking down through the railing gap with those too-old eyes.

"Nobody panics," Sefa said, in the voice she used for avalanches and tax collectors. "Brann, you were never a Warden. You're a hired hand, deaf in one ear, slow-witted, and devoted to your pay. Ines handles the talking. I'm your guildmother, infirm and irritated. Tomas—" she glanced up at him, "—go be drunk somewhere out of sight."

"I am actually drunk."

"Then be it invisibly."

* * *

They let the Wardens climb the last stretch themselves. That mattered, Ines knew. A guide's first law: never meet trouble halfway, make it do the climbing. By the time Vane reached the guildhouse door his breath was smoking and his two escorts looked less certain of their captain's leisurely pace.

Up close, Aldric Vane was not what the stories promised. She'd imagined a brute, all scar and swagger. Instead he was lean, forty-some years, with a scholar's pale eyes and a voice pitched low enough that you leaned in to hear it—which she suspected was the whole trick. Frost had gotten into the seams of his cloak. His boots were good but not new. A man who spent his money on information rather than tailoring.

"Warden-Captain Vane." Sefa filled the doorway like weather. "If you've come about the toll dispute, the Crown owes us eleven years of back payments and I have the ledgers to prove it."

"Not here for ledgers." He inclined his head—courteous, precise. "May we come in? It's cold enough to crack stone."

"It's cold enough to discourage trespassing."

"Sefa." Ines stepped forward, putting on the easy smile she wore for customs men and suspicious caravan clerks. "Captain, forgive her. She hasn't slept; we had a delivery come in late. Please—warm yourselves. We've nothing to hide and a fire worth sharing."

A beat. Vane studied her, and she felt the study like a hand pressed flat against her sternum, taking her measure. Then he nodded. "Thank you."

They came into the common room. The fire Ines had started was burning well now, and the room smelled of pine smoke and yesterday's stew. One of the escorts lingered by the door; the other drifted, casual as a cat, toward the stairs. Brann materialized beside the stairwell with an armload of split logs he had absolutely no reason to be carrying, and stood there, blocking the way, breathing through his mouth like a man with nothing behind his eyes.

"Wood for the fire," Brann announced, unnecessarily, to no one.

Vane's pale eyes touched him once and moved on. But Ines saw the flicker—the half-second where something behind them went very still and calculating. *Recognition?* No. Not recognition. Something else. The look of a man cataloguing.

"You'll forgive the intrusion," Vane said, settling onto the bench Sefa grudgingly indicated. He did not remove his gloves. "We're asking after an anomaly."

"Anomaly," Sefa repeated, flat as slate.

"A report came down from the high stations. Lights on the mountain roads where no lights should be. A traveler seen near the Salt Gate at night, alone, on foot." He produced a folded paper, didn't open it, just held it. "Barefoot, the report says. In the first snows."

The fire popped. Ines kept her smile fixed and her heartbeat disciplined. Barefoot. Of course. The child had come to their door barefoot through the snow, leaving prints any patrol with eyes would find—and someone had found them.

"Barefoot travelers are common enough in the passes," Ines said lightly. "Pilgrims do penance walks all through autumn. Half-mad, most of them, but harmless."

"Pilgrims walk to shrines. This one walked *from* somewhere. The tracks began at the tree line below Crag's Tooth and went nowhere—ended at your road." His gaze came up, mild and terrible. "Your road, Mistress...?"

"Oyelaran. Ines Oyelaran. Guide, third rank, Guild of the Salt Road." She gave the full recitation, bored as a litany. "And yes, our road passes below Crag's Tooth. It's the only road. If someone walked to nowhere, likely they walked until they dropped. We find frozen pilgrims every winter. It's grim work, hauling them out."

"And did you? Find anyone?"

"We found a dead man in October. Fell into a crevasse off the Kestrel trail. Took us two days to bring the body down." True, all of it, and true things were the best mortar for lies. "No children. No barefoot girls, if that's what's written there."

Something moved in Vane's expression—not disappointment exactly. Recalibration. "I didn't say it was a girl."

Ines let the silence sit exactly one breath too long, then laughed, warm and rueful. "Captain, everyone knows what the Wardens hunt these days. Old-blood families, vanished bloodlines, folk who remember things they shouldn't. My grandmother used to say the Crown stopped looking for criminals and started looking for *memories*. So yes—I assumed a girl. The reports always say girl. Girls unsettle people more."

For the first time, something almost human crossed his face. Almost. "Your grandmother sounds like she'd have been a problem for my predecessors."

"She was a problem for everyone. She's buried under the cairn behind the house, still complaining, I expect."

One of the escorts snorted despite himself. Vane didn't. He unfolded the paper at last, glanced at it as though he needed to, though Ines would have bet her guild pin he knew it by heart. "There's more. Livestock gone strange on the north slopes—cattle refusing to graze within a mile of the Spire approach. Birds. Two shepherds swear the wind spoke to them in a language they couldn't name. One of them has since stopped speaking entirely." He folded the paper away. "Superstition, most likely. Mountain winters breed superstition like rot breeds maggots. But my orders are to run every report to ground, and I am thorough to the point of tedium."

"Lucky for the Crown," Sefa said dryly.

"Unlucky for everyone else." He rose. "I'll want to look at your guest ledger. Standard procedure when a station reports activity on a guild road."

"Of course." Ines fetched it herself, unhurried, and handed it over open to the current page. This was the part she'd rehearsed in her head a hundred times over the years, because someday was always coming: the ledger was honest except where it mattered. Three caravans logged and paid. A peddler. A letter-carrier. No gaps large enough to notice, because she'd filled the gaps weeks ago with entries for traffic that had never existed. Guides who survived learned to write fiction in advance.

Vane read slowly, running a gloved finger down the columns. The second escort had drifted to the window and was looking out at the yard—at the woodpile, the drying racks, the salt store. At the small pair of boots sitting by the back door, waiting to be patched.

Ines's stomach dropped through the floor.

They were child's boots. Small, worn, unmistakably child's, sitting in plain view of a Warden whose entire purpose in life was finding exactly one child.

"Those need resoling," she said, before her fear could reach her voice. "Guild keeps spare pairs for porters' kids. Half the valley sends their little ones up to us to learn knots and manners. You'll see a dozen of them at midsummer fair, Captain, loud enough to curdle milk."

The escort looked at her. Looked at the boots. Looked at Vane.

Vane looked at nothing at all. He closed the ledger. "Your records are in order."

"We try."

He moved toward the door, and Ines allowed herself one full breath of relief—and then he stopped, hand on the latch, and turned back with the mildness of a man asking about the weather.

"The mentor," he said. "Oyelaran—you trained under Corvess, didn't you? The one who died in the spring?"

The room went very quiet. Even the fire seemed to hold its crackle.

"He fell on the Spire approach," Ines said. Each word placed like a foot on ice. "Rockfall. We brought down what we could."

"Mm." Vane's pale eyes rested on her, and there was something in them she couldn't name—something that wasn't suspicion, or wasn't only suspicion. Grief, maybe. Or its fossil. "Corvess guided me once, years ago. Before my commission. Showed me a pass through the Thornfells that isn't on any chart. Told me the mountains keep doors, and that a wise man learns which ones to leave shut." He opened the latch. Cold poured in. "I've thought about that often since. About doors, and who wants them open, and why."

"Mountains are full of metaphors," Sefa said. "It's the thin air."

"It is." And Aldric Vane smiled—a small, correct, chilling thing—and stepped out into the frost. "Thank you for your hospitality. We'll be camped at the lower toll house through the week. Running reports to ground is tedious work, as I said. If anything... anomalous... occurs to you, you know where we are."

* * *

They watched from the window until the riders dropped below the switchback and out of sight. Then another hour, because Brann insisted, and Brann had been on the other side of that patience once.

"They'll stay at the toll house," he said finally. "Not because they believe anything's here. Because staying costs them nothing and shakes something loose. People panic when the wolf doesn't leave. They talk, they run, they make mistakes." He rubbed the scar along his jaw—the one he'd never explained and Ines had never asked about. "He suspects. Not the child specifically. He suspects *something*, and he's fishing."

"He mentioned Corvess." Ines's voice came out flatter than she intended. "He knew my mentor. Guided him."

"That's what he does." Brann turned from the window. "Vane doesn't collect taxes or even prisoners, not really. He collects *connections*. Every person he meets, he files away—who they loved, who they lost, what they'd burn down to protect it. Then one day he needs a lever, and he reaches into the file." His eyes met hers, and for a moment the dull hired-hand mask slipped and she saw the soldier underneath, tired and certain. "He mentioned your mentor to see where it landed. He mentioned doors to see if you'd flinch. You didn't flinch. That'll bother him. Men like Vane hate a wall they can't find the seam of."

From the loft, a sound. Small feet on the boards again, and then Nia came down the ladder—she'd learned the ladder in a day, fearless as a squirrel—and stood in the middle of the common room looking at each of them in turn. Reading the room the way she read everything: completely, silently, with those eyes that had watched two centuries go by from wherever she'd been kept.

Then she pointed at the door. At the space where Vane had stood.

"*Man,*" she said.

Her first word. Her first word in Common, spoken clear as a bell, and Ines felt it land in her chest like a hook setting.

"Yes," Ines said gently, kneeling to the child's level. "A man came. A dangerous man. But he's gone now."

Nia considered this. Then she shook her head, slow and grave, and pointed again—not at the door this time, but east, through the wall, through the mountain, toward the lower toll house where the fires of the Wardens would be burning small and bright against the dark.

"*Man,*" she said again. "*Coming back.*"

Sefa's hand found Ines's shoulder and gripped hard. Outside, the first real snow of the season was beginning to fall, thick and silent, erasing the road, the tracks, the world—everything except the fact that they had six days, maybe fewer, before a patient man finished fishing.

"We leave tomorrow," Ines heard herself say. "Before first light. Get the packs ready."
