## 6. The Mentor's Route

The journal was bound in salt-stiff leather, the kind that crackled when you opened it, and it had spent three days on Ines's table while she pretended not to be afraid of it.

Maren's journal. Her mentor's hand, cramped and slanting, filling page after page with what looked like a merchant's ledger—weights, tolls, grain prices, weather. Except Maren hadn't kept ledgers. Maren had kept routes, and she'd kept them the way the Guild had always kept them: folded into ordinary words so that even a Warden who seized the book would find nothing but a boring old woman's accounts.

Ines had learned the code at fourteen, sitting cross-legged on this same table while Maren tapped her pipe against the margins. *Tolls are what you pay,* she'd said. *So when I write tolls, I mean cost. What it costs you. Blood, breath, fear.* She'd blown smoke at the ceiling beam. *Grain is food for the road. Weather is the sky's mood. And when I write of a buyer—* the pipe had stopped tapping— *that's someone waiting at the far end. Someone you don't want to disappoint.*

There were no buyers in the last third of the journal. There was weather, and weather, and weather. And then, on the final written page, a single line in a different ink, pressed hard enough to tear the paper:

*If the child comes, take the low road. The one I never wrote down.*

Below it, nothing. Maren had died eleven days later, alone on the high pass, and they'd brought her back wrapped in her own rope.

*The one I never wrote down.* Which meant it wasn't in the journal. Which meant Ines had to read the journal harder than she'd ever read anything, hunting the negative space—the route described by everything Maren had carefully *not* said.

She found it on the fourth night, in an entry from nine years back. A complaint about grain prices in Thornfell village, followed by: *Refused to carry the miller's flour again. Told him the road under his mill doesn't exist and he should thank every god it stays that way.*

Under his mill. The mill at Hollowford, where the river dropped through a slot canyon no wider than a cart. Ines had walked past that mill a hundred times. There was no road under it. There was barely room for the millrace.

But Maren hadn't said *there is no road.* She'd said *the road doesn't exist*—and in the Guild code, *doesn't exist* meant something specific. It meant *off the maps.* Off the Crown's maps, off the Guild's own charts, known only to the person who'd walked it and whoever they chose to tell. A smuggler's road in the oldest sense: a road that survived precisely because nobody believed in it.

Ines closed the journal and sat in the dark of the guildhouse kitchen, listening to the wind work at the shutters, and felt hope arrive in her chest like a swallowed coal—hot, dangerous, impossible to spit out.

Because if the low road existed, it might go where the high roads couldn't. Past the Wardens' new checkpoints. Past the passes they'd sealed with iron gates and oaths. All the way up into the mountains where, according to a dead woman's silence, something waited that a barefoot child had crossed two hundred years to reach.

* * *

They left before dawn, five of them and a mule.

Sefa had argued until midnight, and then, characteristically, lost the argument to herself. "I'm too old for the Spire road," she'd said, which everyone knew was a lie, because Sefa had guided the Spire approach twice before the Wardens existed. What she meant was: *someone has to stay, and if the house burns or the Wardens come, better it burns empty around me than around all of us.* So she stayed, standing in the doorway with her arms folded, watching them load the mule with the particular stillness of a woman memorizing faces.

"Feed the child first," she'd said. "Always. Whatever else happens on that road, she eats first." Then, quieter, only to Ines: "Maren knew. That last year, she knew something was coming. Ask the journal why she stopped writing buyers."

Ines hadn't asked yet. She was afraid of the answer.

Tomas came because he had nowhere else to be and said so with the flat honesty of a man three months sober who didn't trust the sobriety to hold without something to hold onto instead. He carried his father's field kit—the brass instruments, the rolled vellum—and complained about the weight of it the entire first morning, which was how Tomas complained about everything he loved.

Brann came because Brann went where Ines went. He'd been a Warden once; now he moved like a man perpetually listening for the order he'd refused, walking rearguard without being asked, checking their backtrail with a patience that made Ines understand why deserters either broke or became the best survivors she'd ever met. He'd broken once. He was determined not to again.

And Nia came because there was nowhere else for Nia to be either, though no one said it that way. They said she was safer with them. They said the Wardens were asking about anomalies on the mountain roads. What they meant, and what the child seemed to understand anyway, in the wordless way children understand exile, was: *you are the anomaly, little one, and we are the people foolish enough to love you.*

She walked well, that was the surprising thing. Barefoot for the first day until Ines cut down a pair of spare guide's boots and stuffed the toes with wool, and after that she walked like the boots had been hers all along. She watched everything—the way Ines read the sky, the way Brann doubled their trail through scree, the way Tomas cursed his sextant—and she watched the way water watches stone. Patiently. As if she'd seen it all before.

On the second morning, crossing the shingle flats below Raven's Tooth, she stopped, pointed at the sun coming up red behind the peaks, and said:

"Cold."

Not Vessarine. Common speech. One word, accented strangely, vowels rounded in a way Ines had never heard from mountain mouths—but common speech, unmistakably, her first.

Ines stopped walking. Brann's hand went to his knife out of pure reflex. Tomas said, "Well," in the voice of a cartographer confronting a river that had changed course overnight.

Nia looked at their faces, and something flickered across her own—not triumph, not fear. Recognition. As if she'd just remembered where she'd put something down a very long time ago.

"Cold," she said again, testing it, and then pointed at the red sun. "Cold." And then, with the ghost of what would become, years later, a devastating dry humor: she pointed at Tomas's grumbling mouth and said, "Cold."

Tomas laughed—a real laugh, startled out of him—and Ines felt the coal in her chest flare. Because a child who could learn a dead language's sister tongue word by word was a child whose mind worked like a key works in a lock. And a key, Ines thought, turning toward the mountains, can open things.

She wasn't sure yet whether that thought was hope or dread. She suspected it was both, braided together the way the Guild braided its ropes—one strand pulling against the other so the whole held fast.

* * *

Hollowford smelled of rotting grain and cold water.

The mill hunched over the slot canyon where the Vessarine fork dropped twenty feet into black stone, its wheel frozen mid-turn in the early ice. The miller himself was a wide, suspicious man named Odo who charged the Guild's tolls with both hands and gave nothing back but gossip.

"You'll not cross here," he told them, counting Ines's coin. "Wardens took the bridge tolls themselves now. Asking names. Asking business." He leaned close, breath sour. "Asking about a girl, some say. Small girl, dark-haired, come down off the ice without boots. There's a price on that talk, if you've seen her."

"We haven't," Ines said, smooth as poured oil. Beside her, Nia stood with her hood up and her chin tucked, small and forgettable as a bundle of kindling. Brann had taught her that much in two days: *be furniture.* She was very good at being furniture. It unnerved Ines more than she let show.

Odo shrugged and turned back to his ledger. "Mind the ford, then. Ice is lying about its thickness this year."

They waited until full dark. Then Ines led them around the back of the mill, down the goat track to the millrace, and stood at the lip of the slot canyon with Maren's journal open in her memory.

*The road under his mill doesn't exist.*

The canyon wall on the far side was black basalt, sheer as a cut gem. But at water level, half-hidden by frost-killed ferns, there was a seam—a vertical crack, barely shoulder-wide, running up into the dark. Ines had looked at that seam a hundred times in her life and seen exactly what Odo saw: nothing. A crack. Stone being stone.

Now she saw what Maren had wanted her to see. At knee height, inside the seam, the rock was worn smooth. Rounded. Polished by centuries of shoulders passing through. Somebody had squeezed through that crack so many times, over so many generations, that they'd rubbed the mountain soft.

"It's a door," Tomas whispered. He had his lantern up, and his cartographer's eyes had gone wide and hungry. "It's a *door*, and it's been here the whole time, and my family mapped these valleys down to the springs and we never—" His voice cracked. "We erased it without knowing we were erasing it. Or worse. We erased it *because*—"

"Later," Brann said quietly.

Ines went first. The seam was tight and wet and the dark inside it was total, and for ten body-lengths she moved by feel alone, breath scraping, thinking of Maren's shoulders wearing this stone smooth, Maren coming through here alone year after year carrying whatever secret had finally killed her. Then the crack opened, turned, opened again, and she stepped out into a gallery the width of a wagon road, floored with ancient flagstones, running level into the mountain's heart.

Old lantern hooks studded the walls at intervals. Someone had maintained this road once. Someone had walked it often, recently, within Ines's lifetime.

Behind her, one by one, came the others. Last came Nia, and when the child emerged from the crack into the lantern-light of the hidden road, she did not look around in wonder.

She looked *home.*

She touched the flagstones with her palm, flat, the way you'd touch a sleeping animal. She looked up at the vaulted ceiling, at the old soot-marks above the lantern hooks, and her lips moved, shaping sounds Ines couldn't hear. Then she turned and pointed deeper into the mountain, up-road, toward the spine of the Thornfells, and said—clearly, deliberately, in her strange rounded accents:

"Up."

One word. But she said it the way Maren used to say *north*: like a compass needle settling.

"She knows this road," Brann said. Not a question.

"Nia knows *something*," Ines said. "Let's find out what."

* * *

They walked the road that didn't exist for two days.

It climbed in long switchbacks through the mountain's bones, surfacing occasionally in defiles choked with fallen stone, diving under ridges that no map showed any reason to dive under. Tomas mapped as they went, feverishly, muttering corrections to charts his grandfather had drawn, striking lines, adding others. Twice he stopped dead and stared at a junction and said, "This shouldn't be here. This *can't* be here. The geology—" and then knelt and touched the flagstones and said nothing more for an hour.

Brann found the first sign on the afternoon of the second day: a niche carved into the wall, holding a stub of candle wax and, beneath it, scratched into the stone in a spidery hand, a tally. Dozens of marks. Groups of five.

"Maren's," Ines said. She knew the hand. She'd know it in her sleep. "She counted her trips."

"How many trips does a person make," Brann asked slowly, "to need sixty tallies?"

Ines didn't answer. Sixty trips, alone, on a road that didn't exist, in the last years of her life. Sixty trips carrying what? To whom? *Ask the journal why she stopped writing buyers,* Sefa had said. Ines had finally asked, the night before they left, and found the answer in the negative space one more time: Maren's last recorded buyer entry was dated eight years back. After that, nothing bought, nothing sold, nothing paid. Only weather. Only tolls. Only cost.

Whatever Maren had been doing on this road, she hadn't been doing it for money.

That night, camped in a wide gallery where an underground stream sang somewhere below the floor, Nia spoke her third word.

They were sitting by the fire—small, shielded, the kind of fire Brann built, that a hawk overhead would take for a trick of the light—and Ines was showing the child the journal, letting her turn the stiff pages. Nia handled it with a care that made Ines's throat tight, like the book was an egg. When she reached the last written page, the line pressed hard enough to tear the paper, she stopped.

Her finger came down on the words *if the child comes.*

She looked up at Ines. Ines looked back. The fire popped. Somewhere in the deep of the mountain, water kept its time.

"Me," Nia said.

Two letters. One syllable. And Ines felt it land in her chest like a hammer falls on a bell—not loud, but everything after it ringing. Because the child hadn't said *me* the way a toddler claims a sweet. She'd said it the way you confirm a delivery. The way you answer a question that's been waiting for you for a very long time.

"Yes," Ines managed. "You. If the child comes. You came."

Nia studied her face for a long moment, and then did something that undid Ines completely: she reached out and pressed her small palm flat against Ines's sternum, over the heart, and left it there. Warm. Steady. Patient as water on stone.

Then she withdrew her hand, pointed at the journal, pointed up the road, and said her fourth word:

"Soon."

* * *

They surfaced on the third day into a world gone white and silent.

The road let out through a cleft in a ridge Ines had known all her life as the Shepherd's Backbone—unclimbable, unbroken, marked on every chart as solid rock for twelve miles. They stepped out of the mountain through a gap no wider than the mill-seam, and Ines turned and looked back and understood, with a cold sliding feeling, that she could stand at the foot of the Backbone for the rest of her life and never find that gap again. It wasn't hidden. It simply declined, politely and permanently, to be perceived by anyone not already inside it.

Ahead, the high country opened up: the plateau below the Spire, treeless, windswept, the snow lying blue in the hollows. And beyond it, half-dissolved in cloud, the Spire itself—Vessar's last tower, a gray finger pointing at a sky the color of dishwater.

They were above the Wardens' checkpoints now. Above the sealed passes. Above, Ines realized, almost everything.

Tomas stood beside her with his sketchbook forgotten in his hands. "My family drew this ridge," he said faintly. "Four generations of Thornburys drew this ridge, and every single one of us drew it *solid*." He laughed, a small unhinged sound. "Do you know what that means? It means the erasure wasn't ignorance. You don't map a road as solid rock by accident. Someone told my great-grandfather what to draw. Someone with the authority to make four generations of cartographers lie."

"The Crown," Brann said.

"The Crown," Tomas agreed. "Or something older that the Crown inherited."

Ines barely heard them. She was watching Nia.

The child had walked out onto the plateau ahead of them, into the wind, and stood facing the Spire with her hood thrown back and her dark hair whipping. And she was singing.

It was the first time they'd heard her voice do anything but single words, and it wasn't speech—it was melody, a thin thread of sound rising and falling in a pattern that belonged to no lullaby Ines had ever heard, in a language that predated every kingdom on the current maps. The wind took it and shredded it and carried pieces of it away toward the tower.

And the tower—

Ines would spend years deciding whether she'd actually seen it. But in that moment, with the song threading through the wind, she would have sworn on Maren's grave that the clouds around the Spire *moved.* Pulled inward, slowly, like breath drawn through teeth. Like something very large, very far away, and very patient, hearing a voice it recognized, and leaning closer to listen.

Beside her, Brann's hand closed on her shoulder. Hard. "Tell me you see that."

"I see it."

"And are we going to talk about the fact that the child is singing to it?"

"No," Ines said. "We're going to keep moving, and we're going to get below the ridgeline before full dark, and tomorrow we're going to talk about it." She raised her voice. "Nia! Come back. Time to eat."

The singing stopped. The child turned, and for just a moment, before the wind wiped it away, Ines saw her expression—and it was the expression that would haunt her all the way up the plateau and into the chapters of her life she couldn't yet imagine.

Not wonder. Not joy.

Relief. The bone-deep relief of a prisoner who has heard, at last, footsteps approaching her cell door—without knowing yet whether the person coming holds a key or a knife.

Nia trotted back to them through the snow, took the dried apple Ines offered, and ate it in small careful bites. Then she pointed at the Spire, half-veiled in its breathing cloud, and said her fifth word.

"Hurry."

Far below and behind them, faint as a struck match, a signal mirror flashed from the ridge they'd left at dawn—three quick flashes, then two. Brann read it, and Ines watched the blood leave his face.

"That's a Warden pursuit pattern," he said. "They're not following our trail. They're *ahead* of us."
