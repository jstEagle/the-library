## 7. Brann's Desertion

The order came at dusk, written in Captain Vane's own hand, and Brann read it three times before the words stopped rearranging themselves into something he could live with.

*Burn it.*

That was all. Two words beneath the village's name: Hollowmere. Population, per the last census taken before the Crown decided censuses were a waste of ink on people who'd be ash by spring: forty-one souls, nine of them children.

He stood in the command tent with the paper in his fist and the oil lamps guttering, and around him the other Wardens were cleaning their gear the way soldiers do when they already know what tomorrow looks like—flint and steel checked, fire-oil casks rolled into line, crossbows strung not for fighting but for keeping anyone from running out of the flames.

"You've read it, then." Sergeant Corwin didn't look up from the whetstone he was dragging along his blade. "Captain wants it done clean. No looting. He says looting is how rumors start."

"Rumors," Brann said.

"That a village full of old-blood families got murdered instead of lawfully purged." Corwin said *purged* the way other men said *harvested*, flat and administrative, a word that had been sanded of everything sharp. "There's a difference, Warden. You'll learn it."

Brann had been a Warden for six years. He knew the catechism by heart—the Crown keeps the roads, the roads keep the realm, what burns behind us cannot follow—and he had recited it every morning with the rest of them, standing in ranks in the frost, and he had believed it the way you believe a floor will hold your weight: without thinking, until the one moment you think about it, and then never comfortably again.

Hollowmere was a farming village tucked into a fold of the Thornfell foothills, three days' march from anywhere that mattered. The old-blood families there kept to themselves, paid their taxes late, buried their dead with coins on the eyes facing east, toward mountains that no longer appeared on any map. Someone in the provincial capital had decided that coins facing east constituted sedition. Someone above that someone had signed the order. And someone above *that* someone had decided, some years back, that the Thornfell roads needed closing, the guild houses emptied, the guides scattered or hanged, because a kingdom could not be made safe while its people remembered there had ever been another kingdom.

Brann folded the order and put it in his breast pocket, which was the closest thing to disobedience he had managed in six years.

"We move at first light," Corwin said. "You're on the eastern cordon. Nobody runs out that side."

Nobody runs out that side. Nine children. He did the arithmetic without wanting to and hated himself for being able to do it at all.

* * *

He didn't sleep. He sat outside the tent with his cloak around his shoulders and watched the fires burn down, and he thought about his brother Tam, who had joined the Wardens two years before him because their father's farm was failing and the Wardens fed you whether the harvest came in or not. Tam was stationed on the far side of the range now, near the Spire road, where they said the cold came down wrong sometimes, where men talked about the Door the way sailors talk about a stretch of water that eats ships. Tam wrote letters full of jokes and complaints about the food. Brann wrote back the same. Neither of them ever wrote the true thing, which was that they had both become the kind of men their mother had warned them about, gently, over bread, when they were small: *Boys, the Crown is a cart. It goes where it's pulled, and it doesn't look back at what the wheels have done.*

At some hour past midnight, a woman came out of the dark toward the picket line.

She walked like she owned the ground, which was the first strange thing, because everyone in the Thornfells walked like the ground might be confiscated. She was broad-shouldered, gray-streaked, wrapped in a guide's oiled coat, and she stopped ten paces from the fires and looked at the tents and the oil casks and the sleeping machinery of the burning, and then she looked at Brann, because he was the only one awake.

"You're the eastern cordon," she said. Not a question. "I asked which of you had the worst posting. The loneliest. Loneliest men think clearest."

"I'll call the guard," Brann said, and didn't move to do it.

"No, you won't." She came closer. Up close her face was weathered into something like kindness and something like iron, and neither seemed to cancel the other. "My name is Sefa Oyelaran. I keep a guildhouse up the Salt Road. I'm told you're going to Hollowmere in the morning."

"Told by whom?"

"A shepherd. A bird. Does it matter?" She crouched down across the fire from him, close enough that he could see the scars on her knuckles, rope-burn and frostbite layered over decades. "Forty-one people, Warden. Nine children. For coins facing east."

"The order is lawful."

"The order is *lawful*." She turned the word over like a stone she'd found in her bread. "My husband used to say that. He was a Warden too, before me, before—" She stopped, and something crossed her face and was gone. "I'm not here to argue law with you. I'm here because I've stood where you're standing, on the edge of a night you know is wrong, and I know what it costs to step back from it, and I know what it costs not to. The second cost is higher. It just takes longer to arrive."

"They'd hang me."

"They'd hang you either way, eventually. That's the arrangement. They just let you choose the timing." She reached into her coat and set something on the ground between them, wrapped in waxed cloth. "A route through the hills that isn't on any chart. If you use it, you'll come out above a valley the maps forgot. There are people there who remember more than is safe. They'll take in a man with no country left."

"I haven't decided anything."

"You decided the moment you put that order in your pocket instead of throwing it in the fire." She rose, joints cracking, unbothered. "Throwing it away would've meant nothing. Keeping it means you wanted to look at it again. Men only look twice at things they mean to refuse." She walked back into the dark the way she'd come, soundless, and the last he saw of her was the pale slash of her smile. "If you come, ask for the valley that sings. We'll leave a lamp lit."

* * *

First light came gray and windless, the worst kind of morning for what mornings were for.

They marched on Hollowmere in column, forty Wardens with casks on carts, and Brann took his place at the head of the eastern cordon as ordered, and the village came into view below them—smoke from breakfast chimneys, a dog barking at nothing, a woman beating a rug on a fence rail. Ordinary. That was the unbearable part. It was always ordinary. In six years he had burned a smugglers' cache, a bridge, a print shop, a shrine, and half a terraced hillside of vineyards, and every single one had been minding its own business when the Wardens arrived.

Corwin rode up beside him. "Cordon set?"

"It's set."

"Good. Captain wants the church fired first. Symbolic value." He grinned. "Then the granary, so nobody eats while they decide whether to run."

Brann watched the woman beat her rug. Thwack. Thwack. Dust rising gold in the low sun. Somewhere in that village a child was doing whatever children did at this hour, arguing about porridge, feeding the dog that barked at nothing.

"Warden." Corwin's voice changed. "You're a long way from your post."

"I'm exactly at my post."

"Your post is the eastern treeline. You're staring at the village like it owes you money." Corwin's hand drifted to his sword, casual, conversational. "Is there a problem?"

And here was the moment, Brann thought later, that his whole life had been walking toward without knowing it. Six years of floors holding his weight, and now the floor spoke.

"There's a problem," he said.

"Name it."

"I'm not going to help you burn children."

The word *children* landed between them like a dropped torch. Around them, the nearest Wardens had gone still, the way animals go still, listening.

Corwin sighed, genuinely weary, a man inconvenienced by weather. "It's always the quiet ones. Six years, Brann. Six years of spotless service, and this is what cracks you? Coins on eyes? A few farmwives with old blood in them?" He shook his head. "You think they're people. That's the rot right there. The Crown can't afford for them to be people. People get sympathies, and sympathies get roads opened, and open roads get kingdoms killed. You want to know why we close the paths? Because the last time the paths were open, something came down out of those mountains that the histories aren't allowed to name. Everything we burn, we burn so that never happens again. That's the whole job. That's the mercy of it."

"That's the lie of it," Brann said. "And even if it weren't—even if every word were true—it wouldn't make nine children ash."

Below them, faintly, the church bell began to ring. Not the alarm pattern. The morning pattern, the one that called farmers in from fields. The village didn't know yet. It had maybe twenty minutes of not knowing left.

Corwin drew his sword. "Sergeant's privilege. I'll report you died resisting arrest. Your family gets a pension. It's more than you deserve, and less than I'd give a friend, but we were never friends, were we."

"No," Brann said, and hit him.

Not cleanly. Not bravely. He fought the way frightened men fight, all elbows and desperation, and Corwin was better trained and nearly took his eye out with a pommel before Brann got a thumb into the man's wound-scarred knee joint and felt something give. Corwin went down screaming a word that wasn't a word, and Brann ran—not toward the trees, because they'd hunt the trees, but down the slope, straight at the village, shouting the alarm pattern himself, the real one, his voice tearing on it: *Wardens! Wardens coming! Run east, run EAST—*

Doors opened. The dog lost its mind. The woman with the rug dropped it and started screaming names, and names became people, and people became motion, and Brann stood in the lane of a village he'd been sent to erase and pointed them east with both arms like a signpost, like a scarecrow, like the only honest thing he'd ever been.

Behind him, horns. Ahead of him, forty-one lives funneling into the treeline. He counted them out—he couldn't stop counting—thirty, thirty-five, the children carried, the old ones dragged, a boy going back for a goat and Brann grabbing his collar and hauling him bodily into the green.

Thirty-nine. Forty. Forty-one.

He turned to run after them and the first crossbow bolt took the doorframe where his head had been.

* * *

He caught up with them at dusk, or they caught up with him—a dozen survivors of Hollowmere moving through the high forest with the shepherd who'd warned Sefa leading them, and when they saw his Warden's coat they raised everything they had, which was a hay hook and a kitchen knife and a great deal of hatred.

"He rang the bell," the shepherd said. "Let him pass or don't, but hear it first: he rang the bell."

An old woman with a coin still sewn into her shawl looked at him a long time. Whatever she saw, she nodded once, and the hay hook lowered, and Brann followed the people he had saved into the dark, feeling not saved himself but merely postponed.

They traveled three days on routes that shouldn't have existed, along streambeds and shelf-paths and one tunnel under a ridge that the shepherd called the Old King's Cellar, and on the third evening they came down into a valley that sang.

That was the only way to describe it. Wind through hollow stones the villagers had set on the slopes, tuned, generations ago, so that the valley breathed music when the weather moved. Below, lamplit: fields, a mill, houses built of timber older than any kingdom's claim, and people turning toward the newcomers with lanterns raised, and somewhere a voice lifting a lullaby in a language Brann didn't know but recognized anyway, deep in the spine, the way you recognize a door you dreamed once.

The valley that sings. The lamp was lit.

He gave up his Warden's coat that night, folding it onto a fire the villagers built—not a ceremony, just practicality, wool burns well—and watching the crown sigil curl and blacken. He felt nothing he'd expected to feel. No freedom. No absolution. What he felt was arithmetic: nine children alive, and one sergeant's knee ruined, and a debt whose size he couldn't calculate because the currency was wrong. Sefa found him at the fire's edge with a bowl of stew he didn't taste.

"Well?" she said.

"I don't deserve this valley."

"Good," she said. "Deserving men relax. Guilty ones work." She settled beside him, shoulder to shoulder, an old soldier's comfort. "The guildhouse needs hands. Strong ones, ones who know how Wardens think. There's a girl there—you'll meet her—guides caravans through passes that would kill you twice. Ines. She thinks she's invisible." Something in Sefa's voice, then, that Brann would spend years learning to read: pride wearing grief like a coat. "The world's going to notice her soon, whether she likes it or not. When it does, she'll need someone standing between her and it. Someone who knows exactly what the Crown sends up these mountains, and exactly how far it's willing to go."

"You're asking me to be a wall."

"I'm asking you to be a door that opens the right way for once." She stood, groaning, and paused. "One more thing. The captain who wrote your order. Aldric Vane. Remember the name. He's climbing, and he doesn't climb for rank. He climbs because he lost someone to the mountains and he means to make the mountains pay. Men like that don't stop at villages."

* * *

Four years later, in the guildhouse kitchen, flour on his hands because Ines had conned him into bread duty again, Brann heard the knock at the door and the small bare feet and the word that came through the wood in a language he'd heard only once before, sung by a valley of ghosts:

*"Selama."*

Home.

He set down the dough. Across the kitchen, Sefa had gone white as salt, and Ines was already moving for the door, and Brann wiped his hands slowly, thoroughly, the way he did before anything that mattered, because four years of guilt had taught him at least one thing for certain:

Nothing that knocked on this door was ever going to let them stay invisible—and whatever was coming for the child behind that knock, he had stood in its path once already, and he knew, better than any of them, exactly how much it would burn.
