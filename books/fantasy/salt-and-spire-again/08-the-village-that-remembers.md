## 8. The Village That Remembers

Tomas had spent thirty years making maps, and in all that time he had never drawn the thing he was now looking at.

The valley opened beneath them like a held breath—a bowl of green cradled between two ridgelines that shouldn't have permitted anything green at all. It was late autumn on every chart he'd ever inked. The passes above were white with early snow, the wind carrying the smell of iron and coming frost. But down there, smoke rose from a dozen chimneys in straight, unhurried columns. Terraced fields climbed the southern slope, dark with winter wheat. A stream ran silver through the center of it all, and along its banks stood houses built of stone and timber in a style Tomas recognized from the oldest plates in his grandfather's collection—the ones labeled *pre-Erasure, provenance disputed*.

"Stop staring," Ines said beside him. "Staring is how people get followed."

"I'm not staring. I'm cataloguing." He pulled out his journal, then stopped himself, hand hovering over the page. Thirty years of habit, and the habit was exactly what had ruined his family. Every map he made became evidence. Every line he drew could be read by the wrong eyes. "No. You're right. No record."

Ines glanced at him, something almost like approval crossing her weathered face. "Growth."

Behind them, Brann shifted Nia's weight on his back. The girl rode in a sling Brann had improvised from a salt tarp, her bare feet—Sefa had wrapped them now, in wool and oiled leather—dangling against his spine. She hadn't spoken since dawn. She rarely spoke more than a word or two a day, but when she did, the words landed like coins dropped down a well: *cold*, *road*, *Tomas*. She'd started saying his name three days ago, and he still hadn't recovered from it.

"She's watching too," Brann said quietly.

Tomas turned. Nia's chin rested on Brann's shoulder, and she was looking down into the valley with an expression no child should own—not wonder, exactly. Recognition. The look of someone returning to a house they'd left long ago and finding the furniture rearranged but the walls familiar.

"You've been here before," Tomas murmured. "Haven't you. Long ago."

Nia's eyes slid to him. She didn't answer. She never answered the questions that mattered.

* * *

The descent took two hours through pine forest that grew denser and older as they dropped. Tomas noticed things he couldn't help noticing: the trail markers were wrong. Not Warden cairns, not Guild blazes—these were carved wooden posts, lichen-eaten, shaped like narrow standing figures with their hands folded. Waystones. He'd seen their likeness once, in an illuminated manuscript from before the Erasure, and he'd assumed the artist had invented them.

"They're Vessarine," he said. His voice came out strange. "These are Vessarine waystones. They should not exist. I would *know* if they existed. My family made certain nothing Vessarine existed anywhere a cartographer might trip over it."

"Then maybe your family missed one valley," Ines said.

"My family didn't miss valleys. My family erased kingdoms. There's a difference, and the difference is that we were thorough." He heard the bitterness in his own voice and didn't try to soften it. Three generations of Ondrej men had sat at the Crown's cartography table, taking royal coin to redraw the eastern ranges and leave the space where Vessar had been clean and empty and officially nonexistent. *Here be mountains only.* His grandfather used to say it as a joke. Nobody in the family laughed anymore after the third generation.

Brann fell into step beside him. The deserter moved quietly for such a big man, always positioning himself between Nia and the open slopes. "If this place is what you think it is," Brann said, "how has it survived? Wardens burn old-blood villages. I know because—" He stopped. He always stopped there.

"Because you were handed the torch," Tomas finished. "I remember your story, Brann."

"Then you know the question stands."

"The question stands," Tomas admitted. "Either these people are very good at hiding—and they must be, to hide from the Wardens' road surveys—or somebody very powerful decided long ago that this valley wasn't worth finding." He looked back at the waystone they'd just passed. Its folded hands were worn nearly smooth. Generations of travelers had touched them. "Or both."

They came around a shoulder of rock and the village was suddenly below them, close enough to hear: a dog barking, the ring of a smith, someone singing—a work song, low and rhythmic, in a language Tomas had studied in forbidden texts for twenty years and never once heard spoken aloud.

Vessarine. Living, breathing, sung over a washbasin by a woman hanging laundry.

He stopped walking. Ines looked back, frowning, then followed his gaze and understood. Even she went still.

"That's—" she started.

"Don't say it," Tomas whispered. "Don't say the name out loud. Names are how things get found."

* * *

They were seen before they reached the first house, of course. A valley like this survives by seeing first.

An old man stepped into the path ahead of them, holding a scythe not as a weapon but as a reminder that he owned one. Behind him, doors opened along the lane, quiet and unhurried, and people came out—not running, not shouting, just gathering the way water gathers. Within a minute there were thirty of them. Within two, fifty. Tomas counted automatically, uselessly, the cartographer's curse: able to measure everything, control nothing.

The old man spoke. The words were Vessarine, and Tomas caught perhaps one in five.

"He's asking who we are," Nia said.

Everyone turned. The girl had slid down from Brann's back without anyone noticing and was standing at the front of their small group, facing fifty strangers with her wool-wrapped feet planted on the frozen mud of the lane. Her voice was clear and small and absolutely certain.

"And he's asking it in the old formal style," she added. "The kind you use with royalty."

Silence. Then the old man's scythe hit the ground—not thrown, just released, fingers simply letting go. He went down onto one knee, and the motion traveled through the crowd like wind through wheat. Fifty people, kneeling in the mud of a hidden valley, heads bowed toward a barefoot child in borrowed clothes.

"No," Brann said under his breath. "No, no—get up. All of you, get up, you'll bring every Warden in the mountains down on—"

"Let them," said a woman rising from the kneel. She was elderly, straight-backed, with a face like a river stone—worn smooth, impossible to move. She wore a shawl embroidered with a pattern Tomas knew from a single surviving tapestry fragment: interlocking spires. "Two hundred years we've kept the songs. Two hundred years we've taught the children words they don't understand so the words wouldn't die. And now the words walk up our road wearing a child's face." Her eyes found Nia, and something in them cracked open—joy and grief fused into a single unbearable expression. "*Vel komara, ashai.* Welcome home, little queen."

"I'm not a queen," Nia said. Then, quieter, with that terrible old weight behind it: "Not yet. Maybe never. That's why I came."

The woman studied her, and whatever she saw in the child's face made her nod slowly. "I am Maren Ashvale. Keeper of songs. Come inside, all of you. You're freezing, and the snow is coming, and the snow isn't the only thing coming." Her gaze flicked up the trail behind them, sharp as a blade being drawn. "You were followed. Not closely. But followed."

* * *

The house Maren led them to was larger than the others, built into the hillside itself, with a great hearth and walls hung with dried herbs and bundles of feathers. Inside, Tomas counted eleven instruments he couldn't name and one he could—a bowed lyre, pre-Erasure design, lovingly maintained. His hands actually shook. Twenty years of studying dead things from books, and here was a room full of living ones.

Maren fed them barley soup with mountain honey while the villagers drifted in and out, each one pausing near Nia as if warming themselves at a fire. They touched her hair. They pressed small objects into her hands—carved spires, folded paper stars, a button that had clearly been kept for generations waiting to be given to someone. Nia accepted each offering with grave courtesy, and Tomas watched her store each giver's face away somewhere behind her eyes, the way she seemed to store everything: patiently, across distances no human should be able to see across.

"You knew she was coming," Ines said to Maren. Not a question.

"We hoped. We prayed. Those aren't the same thing, and we did the second far longer than the first." Maren settled onto a bench across the hearth. "Every keeper teaches the succession song to one child in each generation. Just one. The rest learn the lullabies, the work songs, enough to keep the tongue alive. But the succession song—the song that tells what was sealed, and where, and who must wake when the door grows thin—that song passes mouth to mouth, one keeper to the next, and never written." She smiled thinly. "Your Wardens burn libraries, Master Cartographer. They can't burn a song."

"You know what I am, then."

"We know what your family was. We also know you came up our road half-starved, guarding a child that isn't yours, when you could have sold her to the Crown and bought back your name." Maren shrugged. "Songs survive. So do judgments. Mine's already made."

Tomas looked down at his soup. He'd been called many things in his life—disgraced, drunk, traitor to his craft—but never trusted, and certainly not by the descendants of the people his family had unmade.

"There's something you should show me," he said finally. "If you have any maps. Any records of the approaches to the Spire. My charts are Crown charts—they end where they were ordered to end. If we're going to move, we need truth under our feet, and my family spent three generations burying it."

Maren exchanged a long look with a younger man at the edge of the firelight—her son, perhaps, or grandson—with a surveyor's squint and ink-stained fingers. "We have maps," she said. "Older than your family's shame, Master Ondrej. And newer. We watch the roads. We count patrols." Her jaw tightened. "Which brings me to the part of this evening I wish I could spare you."

She nodded to the young man, who spread a fresh-drawn chart on the table. It was excellent work—better than Tomas's own, if he was honest, because it was honest work, unmuzzled. Red ink marked movement on the high trails. There was a lot of red.

"Wardens?" Ines asked.

"Scouts. Six days now, working the ridgelines above the north pass. Careful ones—camping cold, no fires. But careful isn't invisible to people who've watched these mountains for two centuries." The young man tapped a red cluster west of the valley mouth. "This group is circling. Closing. They haven't found us because there's nothing to find from above—we roofed our lanes with growing turf a hundred years ago for exactly this reason. But they will find your back-trail. And your back-trail ends at our door."

"How long?" Brann asked.

The young man looked at the fire, doing arithmetic he clearly hated. "Three days. Four if the snow slows them."

The room went quiet. Nia, sitting cross-legged near the hearth with a paper star in her palm, looked up at the adults around her, reading their faces the way Tomas read contour lines.

"Then we sing tonight," Maren said briskly, rising, "because tomorrow there may be no time. Ashai—" she held out her hand to Nia, and her stern river-stone face softened entirely, "—two hundred years, and we owe you one lullaby. Will you let us pay it?"

Nia put her small hand in hers.

* * *

They gathered in the meeting hall as full dark fell, the whole village, every age, packed onto benches with lamps burning low. Maren stood before them and began, alone, in a voice that had gone thin but had lost none of its certainty:

*Ninna vora, ninna sei,*
*spire of ash and salt and clay...*

And the village answered. Tomas didn't understand most of the words—he knew their shapes from manuscripts, knew the grammar like a corpse's anatomy, but hearing it sung was another country altogether. The melody wound downward and downward, a cradle song built for a kingdom falling asleep, and around him grown men and women wept openly without shame, singing to a child the song their ancestors had sung to their children on the last night before the sealing, the night the maps went blank.

Nia sat in the center of it, very still. Halfway through, her lips began to move. She knew the song. Of course she knew it—she was older than it, probably; she might have been there the first time it was sung. But she sang it anyway, softly, letting the valley carry her, and when the final verse came around—

*Tsumi vel, tsumi vel, doru kane,*
*salt remembers, salt remains—*

—every voice in the hall stopped except hers, and a child's voice sang alone in the language of a dead nation, in a hidden valley, under the noses of an empire that believed it had deleted every word of it.

When she finished, nobody applauded. They just breathed, together, a hundred lungs in the dark.

Tomas sat with tears running into his beard and did nothing whatsoever to stop them. Beside him, Ines stared at the floor, jaw tight, eyes bright. Brann held Nia against his side afterward like a man holding something the whole world wanted to take, which, Tomas thought, was precisely what he was.

* * *

It was past midnight when the knock came—not at the door, but at the shutter. Three taps, pause, two taps. The young surveyor slipped out and returned minutes later, gray-faced, and leaned to speak to Maren and Ines in the corner by the dying fire.

Tomas caught only fragments, but fragments were his trade. *Skylight on the western ridge. Torch signal, repeated twice. Answered from the south pass.*

He crossed the room and joined them. "How many?"

"At least two parties, signaling each other," the surveyor said. "That means they've coordinated. That means they're not guessing anymore." He looked at Ines. "Whoever leads them knows what they're hunting."

Ines was silent a moment. Then she said, "Three days, you said."

"Maybe less, now."

"Then we leave at first light. Before the snow lifts, while their scouts are blind." She looked around the circle of faces—Maren, the surveyor, Brann, Tomas—and last at the sleeping child bundled by the fire, one hand curled around a paper star. "There's a route from here to the Spire approach that isn't on any chart. It's in my mentor's journal, and it starts at a river ford half a day east. We move tomorrow. All of us."

Maren shook her head slowly. "The valley doesn't run, Guide. The valley hides. It has for two hundred years, and it will for two hundred more, with or without us." Her eyes went soft only when they touched Nia. "But the little queen goes with our blessing, our supplies, and our fastest guide to the ford. And ashai—" she knelt, briefly, to tuck the blanket closer around the sleeping child, and murmured something in Vessarine that Tomas translated in his head and immediately wished he hadn't, because it was a blessing for the dead given to the living, a farewell worn smooth by two centuries of use:

*Go safely past the reach of wolves. If you cannot, then bite.*

Outside, the snow had begun again, fat and silent, filling in footprints, erasing the day. And far off on the western ridge, faint as a starfall, a torch flared—once, twice—asking its patient question of the darkness.

Nobody in the valley answered it.

But everyone knew: by tomorrow night, the asking would be done, and the finding would begin.
