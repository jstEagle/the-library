## 9. Betrayal at the Ford

The village of Lowmere gave us three nights of kindness and one morning of treachery, and I have spent every day since trying to decide which of those weighed more.

They called it the valley that remembers, and they remembered well. They fed us barley soup and dark bread. They mended Brann's bandages with linen boiled in willow bark. An old woman named Maren braided Nia's hair in a pattern I didn't recognize until Tomas went white over his maps and said, quietly, *that's how Vessarine queens wore it*. The elders sang to Nia at dusk—a lullaby in a tongue none of us spoke but all of us felt, low and round as river stones—and the child sang back, and the whole room wept without knowing why.

I should have known better than to trust a room full of tears.

* * *

It was Corren Hale who did it. I learned his name afterward, from Maren, spoken like something she'd swallowed and couldn't get down.

He was a broad, easygoing man with flour on his hands most hours of the day, the miller's son, thirty or so, with a wife and a new baby. He'd carried Nia on his shoulders through the barley rows and let her pull at his ears while he laughed. He'd told me, the second night, sitting on his own doorstep: "The Wardens came through two winters back. Took our tithe and burned the shrine stones because somebody's grandmother had old blood. We don't forget things here, Guide. That's the whole trouble with us."

I thought he meant it as a promise.

What he meant was a warning. The Crown had posted amnesty proclamations in every market town for a hundred miles: food for winter, pardon for any offense, land in the southern valleys—for information leading to the recovery of "an anomalous child" and the apprehension of her abductors. Abductors. As if we'd stolen her instead of sheltering her. The Wardens were good at words like that. Words that turned rescue into robbery.

Corren walked out before dawn on the fourth morning, telling his wife he was checking the millrace. He walked eleven miles downvalley to the Warden waystation at Harrow Ford and told them everything: how many of us there were, what we carried, which road we'd take when we left. Because there was only one road. The valley opens onto the Greywater at a single crossing—the old ford below the falls—and after that, the high passes. He knew because he'd asked me himself, friendly as you please, over that same doorstep. *Which way will you go when you go, Guide?*

I told him. Because he had flour on his hands and a baby asleep against his shoulder, and I had spent my whole life believing that people on the Salt Road kept faith the way stone keeps heat.

That belief died at the ford, along with other things.

* * *

We left Lowmere mid-morning under a sky the color of dishwater. Sefa rode the mule with Nia tucked inside her cloak—I'd argued for it and lost; Sefa wanted the child where she could feel her breathing. Brann walked point despite the wound in his side, moving stiff but steady, refusing help with a look that ended conversations. Tomas brought up the rear with the maps wrapped in oilcloth against the drizzle. I ranged the flanks, the way my mentor Ovid taught me: *trust the ground, Ines. Ground doesn't lie. People lie beautifully.*

The Greywater ran high with snowmelt, brown-green and loud, and the ford was exactly as the valley folk described it: a shelf of flat stone just under the surface, knee-deep at the center, maybe forty feet across. Above it the falls thundered down a granite lip, throwing spray and noise. Below it the river quickened toward rapids you wouldn't survive.

I smelled the ambush before I saw anything, and I don't mean that as poetry. Woodsmoke where there should have been no smoke. Wet wool. The particular animal stink of men who've been standing in one place too long, waiting.

I put up my fist and the column stopped. Brann saw my face and read it—he'd been a Warden; he knew what waiting men smelled like too.

"How many?" he murmured.

"Can't tell. Smoke says a cook fire. Cook fires say they're settled in, not chasing." I looked at the water, the spray, the roar of the falls. "They'll want us mid-crossing. Water slows us, noise covers their movement."

"They could just wait till we're committed," Brann said. "Halfway across, nowhere to run."

"They could. But Vane wants the child unharmed, and he knows I know this country. He'll expect me to find another crossing rather than risk the obvious one." I hated how reasonable it sounded. "So the obvious one is what he guards."

"And?"

"And he's right that there isn't another crossing. Not for twenty miles, and the long way adds three days we may not have." I looked back at Nia, a small dark shape inside Sefa's cloak, watching me with those eyes that were older than the mountains around us. "So we cross the obvious one. But not the way he expects."

Tomas unrolled his charts under an alder, shielding them from the drizzle with his body. His hands shook—not fear, or not only fear; it was the shaking of a man doing the thing he'd sworn off, like a drunk's first poured cup. "Pre-Erasure survey," he said, tapping a faded line. "There's a ledge above the north bank. Old salt-traders' path, cut into the cliff. It runs level for half a mile and comes down behind the falls themselves. You could drop from it onto the far bank past the ford—or you could stay on the ledge and be out of bowshot entirely."

"Could," Brann said. "Or the ledge could be rotted through, or blocked, or guarded."

"It's not on any Warden chart," Tomas said. "I know, because my family made the Warden charts. We erased this road along with everything else." Something moved through his face then, brief and bitter—shame turning itself inside out into purpose. "This is the debt I owe. Let me pay some of it."

* * *

The ledge held.

It was narrow—shoulder-width in places, slick with spray near the falls—and it demanded everything Brann had left. He went first, testing each footing, and twice the rock crumbled under his boot and he hung by his fingers over a drop that would have finished what the Wardens started. Both times Sefa, behind him, caught his pack strap and hauled, swearing at him in guild-cant with a fluency that would have scandalized the founders.

Nia crossed strapped to my back, her arms around my neck, her breath warm and fast against my ear. Halfway along the ledge, right where the falls' roar swallowed every other sound, she leaned close and said a word in Vessarine. Then, carefully, in the common speech she was learning word by word:

"Water. Remembers."

I didn't ask what she meant. There wasn't time, and I suspected I wouldn't have liked the answer.

We came down behind the falls into a curtain of cold mist and wet stone, and the sound was enormous, a heartbeat made of the whole river. And there, on the far bank, we saw the ford—and saw what we would have ridden into.

Eight men. No—nine, one already moving up the north slope to cut the ledge approach we hadn't taken. Warden grey, but not uniformed; Vane's scouts traveled plain, and these were plain as smugglers. Two had crossbows. The rest had short swords and the loose readiness of men who'd done this work before. They were positioned to take a column mid-ford from both banks, and they were good—but they were watching the ford, all of them, every eye on that flat shelf of stone.

Because Corren Hale had told them the truth. He simply hadn't known we'd stop being predictable.

For one long moment I let myself believe we'd slip away clean. Mist, noise, distance—we were ghosts and they were looking elsewhere. Brann was already signaling the line forward along the bank, toward the trees, toward the passes.

Then the scout on the slope turned, and saw us, and shouted.

* * *

Afterward, I pieced it together the way you assemble a wreck: here the first bolt, there the shout, here the moment everything came apart.

The crossbowman loosed blind into the mist and was lucky—his bolt took the mule high in the shoulder. The mule screamed and went down, and Sefa, riding it, went down with it, and Nia spilled out of her cloak onto the wet stones like a dropped doll. I heard Sefa cry out—not in pain, though her wrist was broken; I learned that later—in pure animal terror, reaching for the child.

And Nia, six years old and barefoot in a war, did the worst possible thing. She ran. Not from the fight—from the noise, the falling mule, the screaming, all of it—and she ran downstream, along the bank, toward the rapids, small and fast and utterly exposed.

The nearest scout broke cover for her. I understood why, even then. Vane's orders, certainly—but more than orders. A child alone is the easiest capture in the world, and men are men. He was fast. He was closer to her than I was.

I have gone over that next minute ten thousand times, and it never changes.

I was faster.

Not because I'm swift—I'm a guide, not a soldier—but because I knew the ground and he didn't. I cut the angle through the alders, took the bank at a slide, and hit him at the waterline just as his hand closed on Nia's hair. We went down together into the shallows, and the world became very simple: his forearm across my throat, his weight, the brown water churning, Nia's voice somewhere above us crying a word in Vessarine that sounded like a bell struck hard.

His knife came out. I caught his wrist. We strained, and the river pulled, and I remember thinking with strange clarity: *he's stronger, he's heavier, he has a knife, and I am going to die at a ford in sight of the falls, and Nia will watch.*

Then my hand found the guide's pick at my belt—the short salt-road tool, hooked like a gull's beak, for testing ice and cracking crust. Ovid's voice, two years dead: *the ground gives you weapons, girl, if you're humble enough to look.*

I drove it into the soft place below his ear.

I had never killed anyone. I had been shot at, chased, robbed, and once held for ransom for nine days, and I had always found the third way, the clever way, the way that kept everyone's blood where it belonged. My mentor used to say the Guild's whole art was moving through the world without leaving wounds.

The scout shuddered once against me, heavy as a felled tree, and then he was only weight, and the river took the decision out of my hands by pulling him free and turning him over and carrying him toward the rapids with his eyes open to the grey sky.

I got up out of the shallows and picked up Nia, and she wrapped herself around me, shaking, and I stood there in the spray with blood thinning off my hand into the Greywater and felt absolutely nothing, and that nothing frightened me more than the killing had.

* * *

The rest was short. Brann came down the bank like a landslide, wounded side and all, and broke the second scout's sword arm and his nerve in the same motion. The crossbowmen, seeing their prize vanish into a guide's arms and their colleague vanish into the river, withdrew upslope, blowing a signal whistle—three notes, rising. Calling the Captain.

"We move now," Brann gasped, blood sheeting fresh down his side where he'd torn the wound open. "Vane was close enough to have scouts pre-positioned. He'll be here within the hour."

Sefa cradled her broken wrist and refused to leave the mule, who lay breathing fast and shallow, one dark eye rolling. She put her forehead against its nose and said something to it I didn't catch, and then she nodded to Brann, and Brann did what had to be done, quickly, and I turned away and made myself watch anyway, because I decided in that moment that I was done looking away from costs. Someone had to carry them. That was the job now. That had apparently always been the job.

We stripped the camp supplies we could reach—flour, salt pork, the scouts' own dried fish, a coil of good rope—and we climbed.

* * *

We gained the treeline as the first Warden riders came down to the ford below, tiny in the grey distance, dismounting around a body laid out respectful on the stones. I watched through Tomas's spyglass and saw a tall figure in a captain's coat kneel beside the dead scout, and stay kneeling a long time.

"He knew him," Tomas said quietly beside me. "Look at the shoulders. That's grief, not procedure."

"Good," I said.

Tomas looked at me. "Ines—"

"I said *good*." My voice came out flat and cold, a stranger's voice, and part of me stood back from it in alarm. "Let him grieve. Grief slows a man down. And if it doesn't slow him down—" I handed back the spyglass. "Then he and I understand each other, and understanding your enemy is worth more than maps."

Nia tugged my sleeve. I crouched. Her face was blotchy from crying, her hair full of burrs, and she looked at me with those ancient eyes and said, slowly, arranging the common words like stones across a stream:

"You. Fell. For me."

"Yes," I said.
