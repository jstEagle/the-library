## 10. Vane's Offer

The tent smelled of lamp oil and wet wool, and Aldric Vane hated both.

He sat across the folding table from the girl—the guide, the smuggler, the last apprentice of a dying guild—and watched her take stock of the canvas walls, the two sentries at the flap, the manacles on her wrists, in the order of a professional. Not panicked. Not pleading. Cataloguing.

They had taken her at dawn, half a mile below the ford, when she doubled back for the mule they'd left bleeding in the reeds. Brannock Hale had not been with her—Vane's scouts confirmed that much, though the deserter's trail was easy enough to read even without confirmation: big man, bad hip, favoring the left side since the caverns. The child was gone too. Gone deeper, which meant the mapmaker was still alive, which meant the old charts were still in play.

Which meant everything Vane feared was still true.

"You're comfortable," Ines Oyelaran said. It wasn't a question, and it wasn't small talk. Her eyes had found the campaign desk, the sealed orders, the silver Warden's badge he'd set down beside the lamp because it dug into his chest when he leaned over maps. "Comfortable men don't chase children through frozen passes."

"No," Vane said. "They don't."

He poured two cups of the black barley tea his quartermaster brewed and pushed one across the table. She looked at it the way she'd looked at the walls. He understood—he'd have done the same—but eventually she picked it up one-handed, awkward with the irons, and drank. Good. A prisoner who refused everything was a prisoner you learned nothing from.

"Your wrist is broken," she said.

He glanced down. He'd forgotten to wrap the left one again. The splint of bark and linen under his glove was visible now, the knuckles swollen purple above it. "Cracked. It heals."

"It doesn't. Not properly, if you keep using it." She set the cup down. "You write with this hand?"

"I sign things."

"Then let me look at it after we talk, and I'll strap it right." She said it flatly, no warmth in it, an offer of trade rather than kindness. Guild training. Guides bartered everything; nothing given free on the Salt Road, because free things made debt, and debt made dead men.

Vane almost smiled. Almost.

"Do you know why I'm here, Oyelaran? Truly here, not the commission version."

"The Crown wants the old paths closed. You want a promotion. Someone reported lights on the mountain and a child speaking gibberish, and you drew the short straw."

"That's what my superiors believe I'm doing." He opened the campaign desk—not the drawer with the orders, the other one, the false bottom—and took out a square of oilcloth worn soft as cloth can get. He unwrapped it on the table between them.

Inside was a boy's boot. Small, cracked leather, laces rotted to string. Twenty years of oilcloth had kept it whole; nothing could have kept it new.

Ines went very still. He watched the guide's competence crack, just at one corner, like river ice in thaw.

"My brother Tam," Vane said. "Eleven years old. This came back off the mountain without him."

* * *

He had rehearsed this. That was the humiliating part. For twenty years he'd carried the boot and the story together, wrapped in the same oilcloth, and somewhere in those twenty years the telling had hardened into a speech, polished smooth as a river stone, and he despised speeches. Despised the way they turned grief into performance.

But there was no other way to say it, so he said it.

"We grew up in Harrowmere. You'll know it—last real town before the Thornfell wall. Our father cut timber for the salt road forts. Tam followed me everywhere. When I took the Warden trials at sixteen, he counted the days until he could take them too. He used to practice saluting at the pigs."

Ines didn't laugh. He hadn't expected her to. But something in her face shifted, some small loosening around the jaw, and he realized he'd been braced for mockery and gotten instead the thing worse: attention. Full, unguarded attention, the kind guides gave to weather reports and dying men.

"When I made full Warden, he was nine. I was stationed out of Harrowmere then, before the reorganization moved us east. There was a pass behind our house—I've told you the official name is closed. It was never officially anything. Just a crack in the mountain where the cold came out wrong. Local shepherds wouldn't graze sheep above the tree line there. They had names for it. The Seam. The Thin Place. Old-blood families left offerings." His mouth twisted. "We burned the offerings, of course. Superstition, disorder, unsanctioned worship. Standard work."

"And Tam?"

"Tam thought it was beautiful." Vane turned the boot over with his good hand, carefully, the way you turn over something that might still wake. "He'd sneak up there. I caught him twice, brought him home by the ear. The third time I didn't catch him, because the third time he didn't come home."

Outside, wind worked at the tent pegs. One of the sentries shifted, leather creaking. Ines had not touched her tea again. Her manacled hands were folded on the table like a woman in prayer who no longer believed in anyone to pray to.

"There were six of us searching," Vane went on. "Four days. On the fifth day I found where he'd gone—the crack in the rock, the place where the air tasted like standing water. And at the edge of it—" He stopped. Started again, quieter, because the polished speech had failed him exactly where he'd known it would. "There was a line. On the ground. Like frost, but moving. Slowly. Toward the mountain. And Tam's boot was sitting just on the near side of it, upright, neat, like he'd stepped out of it. And past the line, the snow was—" 

He had never told anyone this part. Not the tribunal that reviewed Tam's death. Not his wife, who had stopped asking years ago and then stopped staying.

"—the snow past the line was summer. Green grass, Oyelaran. Wildflowers. In the middle of a Thornfell January. A meadow going up into fog, and the fog was warm, and I stood at that line with my hand out and I could feel heat coming off it like an open oven door. And the frost-line kept moving toward me, slow, patient, and where it crossed the grass, the grass went gray. Not dead. Gray. Un-happened."

"What did you do?" Ines asked. Her voice was low. Not gentle—he could hear that she was choosing not to be gentle, because gentleness would have been an insult to both of them.

"I reached across." He flexed the cracked wrist absently, and felt the old ache answer, the ache that lived in that hand in every cold snap and had nothing to do with any recent fall. "I got his sleeve. Two fingers' worth of wool. And the line reached my hand, and it was the strangest pain I've ever felt—not burning, not freezing. *Subtracting.* Like the feeling was being removed from my fingers faster than I could feel it. I pulled back. I still had the wool." His voice went dry as ash. "The sleeve ended mid-air, Oyelaran. Cut clean. Whatever was past that line didn't kill my brother. It *kept* him. And the line closed up behind him like water closing over a stone, and there was grass and fog and warmth, and then there was just rock. Cold rock. January rock. As if none of it had ever been there at all."

Silence. The lamp hissed. Somewhere beyond the canvas a horse complained.

"They wrote it as a fall," Vane said. "Lost climber, winter conditions. My father signed the report because the alternative was a report that would have ended his contracts and started questions neither of us could answer. I joined the Wardens properly that spring. I told myself I was doing it for Tam. To guard the passes so no other family—" He shook his head. "That lasted about ten years. Then I read the archives. The real ones, the pre-Erasure material the Crown keeps locked under three seals. And I learned what that line in the snow was called, and I learned that it has been eating people since before Harrowmere had a name, and I learned that two hundred years ago someone had the good sense to seal the whole rotten country behind it away forever. And I learned that the seal is not stone, Oyelaran. Stone holds. The seal is a *door*, and doors are only ever as good as what's willing to stay shut."

Ines was quiet for a long moment. Then, carefully: "You think the child opens it."

"I think the child is the key. The old texts are specific—a royal vessel, born of the erased line, carried outside the seal. Your guild spent two centuries making sure such a vessel would be found if it appeared. Don't bother denying it. We've had watchers on your guildhouse since autumn. Sefa Oduya buried her secrets deep, but not deeper than the Crown's memory."

"She's a child," Ines said. Flat. Final. The first truly emotional thing she'd said all morning, and Vane noted it, filed it, aimed at it.

"Yes. She's a child. So was Tam." He leaned forward, and let twenty years come up through his voice, all of it, the whole weight, because he needed her to understand that this was not ambition and not cruelty and not the tidy zealotry she'd invented for him at the start of their conversation. "I am not asking you to betray her because I hate her, or hate Vessar, or love the Crown. I'm telling you I have seen the edge of that Door eat an eleven-year-old boy, and I know what waits behind it is not a kingdom waiting to come home. Kingdoms don't make frost that moves. Kingdoms don't leave sleeves cut clean in the air. Whatever your mentor's journal promised you on that final route of hers—it promised it to her too, and she's dead, and the journal led her exactly where these things always lead."

Something flickered across Ines's face at the mention of the journal—quick, quickly mastered, but he saw it. Confirmation. The route was real; they were following it; he was not too late yet.

"So here is my offer." He laid it out plainly, because plainness was all he had left. "Full amnesty. You, the deserter, the mapmaker, Sefa Oduya—all charges dissolved. The guildhouse stands. I'll see the guild chartered anew under Crown protection, legitimate, funded, whatever Sefa needs to rebuild what the Wardens have spent ten years tearing down. In exchange: the child comes to me. Alive and unharmed—that condition is yours to enforce, and I give you my word on it, and my word is the only thing I've never sold. She will be raised in comfort, studied by people far gentler than the thing inside that Door, and she will never know how close she came to being its key. And you walk away, Oyelaran. You go back to the salt roads with papers that make you untouchable. You get your life. All of it."

He watched her receive it. Watched her do the arithmetic—she was good at arithmetic, he could tell, the kind done fast in the blood—and he knew exactly what numbers she was running. Four lives weighed against one. An institution saved against a mystery preserved. The sensible trade. Every rational weight on his side of the scale.

And still.

"No," she said.

Not angry. Not dramatic. Just placed, like a foot finding the one stone in a scree slope that holds.

"Ines." He used her given name deliberately, and saw it land. "I watched the frost take my brother while I held his sleeve. I have spent twenty years learning everything the Crown knows about what's behind that Door, and I am telling you it is not worth saving. Nothing in Vessar is worth saving. Vessar is a mouth, and the kingdom was the bait, and the sealing was the trap, and the trap is *failing*, and when it fails it will not distinguish between the child and the mapmaker and your precious guildhouse and every shepherd on the Thornfell slopes. It will simply subtract. Everything, everywhere the cold reaches, unhappened. Is that the future you're guiding them toward?"

"The future I'm guiding them toward," Ines said quietly, "is the one my mentor died marking the way to. And she knew more about that Door than your locked archives do, Captain, because she'd stood at it. Because she came *back* from it, which is more than your brother's line ever let anyone do." She rose—or tried to; the chains caught her, and she settled for leaning forward, close enough that he could see the salt-scars on her knuckles, the exhaustion under her eyes, and beneath both of them a steadiness that frightened him more than any weapon. "You want to save children. So do I. The difference is you think the way to save them is to burn the door down while one of them is standing in front of it."

"If she opens it—"

"*If.*" Ines's voice cracked like ice shifting. "Everything you've told me is *if*. You don't know what she is. You don't know what she remembers. You haven't heard her speak. Have you, Captain? Have you actually listened to her?"

Vane opened his mouth. Closed it.

Because the truth was no. The reports described her. The watchers had sketches. He had never stood in a room with Nia—Sefa's name for her, the reports said; the guild had already named her, already claimed her—and heard what came out of her mouth. He had built his entire war on a dead boy's boot and a locked archive, and here was this exhausted, manacled smuggler looking at him like he was the one who didn't understand the terrain.

"The offer stands," he said, and heard how much weaker it sounded now. "Until the Spire. After that, it's fire-oil and collapse, and I will not be able to protect anyone inside the blast radius. Including her."

"I know." Ines sat back. "Strap my wrists loose enough to sleep in, and I'll show you how to bind that hand so it heals straight. Call it payment for the tea."

He should have refused. Protocol, dignity, a dozen regulations. Instead he unlatched the manacles himself—two sentries in the tent, a camp of forty around it, nowhere for her to run in the snow without boots—and let her take his ruined wrist in her scarred fingers and wrap it with a guide's quick economy, tight where it must be tight, loose where swelling needed room. Her hands were warmer than he expected. Everything about her was colder than he expected, except her hands.

At the tent flap, paused between the guards, she looked back once.

"Your brother's boot," she said. "Keep it wrapped. But open the cloth sometimes. Things kept too long in the dark forget they existed. Trust me. I know what my guild does with things we won't let die."

Then she was gone, escorted to the prisoner tent, and Vane sat alone with the lamp and the cooling tea and the small cracked boot in its shroud of oilcloth, and did not touch either one for a long time.

* * *

An hour before dusk, Corporal Reyes found him still sitting there.

"Captain. The prisoner."

"Gone?" He was on his feet before the word finished, the cracked hand screaming, reaching for a sword that hung on the tent pole.

"No, sir. Sleeping, guarded, accounted for." Reyes hesitated. "It's the deserter, sir. Brannock Hale. He walked into camp at the north picket an hour ago. Under a white rag. Says—" the corporal consulted a scrap of paper, though Vane suspected he had it memorized, everyone would have it memorized within the hour— "says he's come to trade himself for the guide's release. Says he's the one you really want. Says—" Reyes's throat moved. "Says he knows where the child and the mapmaker crossed the river, and he'll draw you the route to the Undermap entrance. Sir. The actual entrance. The one that isn't on any chart."

The lamp hissed. Outside, the wind had turned, and it came down off the high passes carrying a cold that had nothing to do with the season—Vane felt it against his face and knew it, knew it the way a scarred hand knows rain coming, the cold from behind the line, the subtracting cold, and it was stronger than it had been yesterday, and yesterday it had been stronger than the day before.

The seal was failing. Right on schedule. Right along the route the journal marked.

And Brannock Hale—the man whose desertion file Vane had personally signed—was standing at his picket with a white flag and a map to the Door.

"Bring him," Vane said.

He looked down at the oilcloth bundle, and for the first time in twenty years, he unwrapped it all the way, and set his brother's boot upright on the campaign table facing the entrance of the tent, facing the mountains, facing the Spire above the clouds.

"Watch this," he told Tam. "One way or another, tonight it ends."
