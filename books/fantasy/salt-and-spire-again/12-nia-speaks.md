## 12. Nia Speaks

The shrine found them the way shrines always did in the Thornfell—by accident, and only when they needed it.

Tomas spotted it first, a seam of pale stone in the cliffside where the pines thinned out. He'd been navigating by the pre-Erasure charts for two days, muttering coordinates to himself like prayers, and when he saw the carved lintel half-swallowed by ivy he made a sound Ines had never heard from him before. Not relief. Recognition.

"Waystation," he said. "They built these along the pilgrim roads. Before." He said *before* the way everyone in the mountains did now—a word with a floor that dropped away beneath it.

The shrine was small: one round chamber, a dome of fitted stone, an altar worn smooth by centuries of hands that had stopped coming. Someone long ago had stocked it against emergencies. The oil in the lamps had gone to amber jelly, but it burned. The blankets in the cedar chest smelled of dust and lavender and something older underneath, something mineral, like the mountain's own breath.

Brann was the worst of them. The wound from the choke point had gone hot, and he'd stopped pretending otherwise somewhere past the second river crossing. They laid him near the altar where the stone held warmth, and Sefa sat with him, grinding willow bark and the last of her feverfew into a paste with the patience of a woman who had buried too many people to panic at one more.

Ines carried Nia inside.

The girl hadn't spoken since the ford. Since the man Ines had killed, since the blood on the stones, since Ines herself had come up out of the river soaked and shaking and unable to look at her own hands for an hour. Nia had watched all of it with those eyes that were too old for her face, and she had said nothing, and the silence had grown between them like ice on still water until Ines couldn't tell anymore whether the child was frightened of the world or of her.

She set Nia down by the fire Tomas coaxed from the emergency wood. The girl knelt there, small and barefoot even now—they'd found her boots two rivers back, but she wouldn't wear them—and stared into the flames.

Then she spoke.

Not a word. A sentence.

"The fire is smaller than the first fire."

Ines went still. Across the chamber, Sefa's mortar stopped mid-grind.

Nia didn't look at them. She kept watching the flames, and she kept talking, in Common now, unaccented, as if she'd been speaking it all her life instead of for three weeks. "The first fire was in a hall with pillars. There were a hundred braziers and they burned blue at the edges because of what was in the oil. I remember the sound they made. Like the sea, but wrong."

"Nia," Ines said carefully, kneeling beside her. "Can you understand me?"

"I could always understand you." The girl turned then, and her face did something complicated—not quite a smile, not quite grief. "Understanding was never the problem. Speaking was. It's hard to push words through so much time. Time gets thick, like honey left in the cold. But this place—" she touched the floor with both palms, flat, reverent, "—this place remembers how to carry voices. It was built for carrying voices."

"What do you mean, *so much time*?" Tomas had drifted closer, chart still clutched in one hand, forgotten.

Nia looked at him. Looked at Sefa. Looked last at Ines, and something in the looking made Ines's chest tighten, because it wasn't the look of a child asking an adult for help. It was the look of someone checking whether the truth would break them before deciding to hand it over anyway.

"You should sit down," Nia said. "All of you. What I have to say takes longer than standing."

* * *

They sat. Even Brann, propped against the altar with Sefa's arm behind his back, gray-faced and burning, insisted on sitting up to listen. Later Ines would understand why. Later she would understand that some part of him had already known he might not get another chance to hear anything true.

"My name isn't Nia," the girl began. "But you can keep calling me that. Names are coats. You gave me this one and it fits well enough and I've worn worse."

Sefa made a small sound. Ines reached over and took her hand without thinking about it, the way you'd steady yourself on a rail in a storm.

"I was born in Vessar," Nia said. "In the capital, which your maps don't name because your maps don't name anything. My father was a salt merchant of no particular importance. I had two brothers. I remember—" she paused, and for a moment the too-old eyes went young, went wet, "—I remember my youngest brother used to hide salt crystals under his tongue so he could make faces when they dissolved. He thought no one knew. Everyone knew."

The fire cracked. Somewhere above them, wind moved through the pines, and the shrine's dome hummed faintly, like a struck bowl.

"When the devourer came, it came slowly. That's the thing no one understands about it. It doesn't arrive. It *arranges*. It got into the court first, then the temples, then the streets. By the time people understood what was happening, the king had been wearing its face for a year and no one could say when the real king had gone. Maybe there never was a real king by then. Maybe it had been arranging for a hundred years before anyone noticed the arrangement."

"The sealing," Tomas said hoarsely. "Our family records call it the Severance. We were told it was punishment. That Vessar had earned—"

"Your family was paid to believe that," Nia said, not unkindly. "Everyone who survived was paid to believe something. Fear needs a story or it eats the teller." She folded her small hands in her lap. "There were nine of us chosen. Children, all of us, because children haven't finished becoming anyone yet. The mages said we were *unwritten pages*, that the Door could hold us without tearing. They took us down beneath the Spire, to the threshold, and they asked each of us to hold one thread of the closing. Nine threads. Nine anchors. And when the Door shut, we didn't die. Dying would have been simpler. We were *kept*. Held just outside the moment of our deaths, the way you hold a door with your shoulder. For two hundred years."

Silence. The kind of silence that has weight and temperature.

"Nine," Ines said finally. Her voice came out strange. "What happened to the other eight?"

"They let go." Nia said it plainly, the way you'd report weather. "Holding hurts, once you understand what you're holding. Some of them understood quickly. One of my brothers—I think it was my brother; time tangles in there, and we couldn't see each other, only feel each other, the way you feel someone sleeping in the next room—one of them held for sixty years. I held longest. I don't know if that's strength. I think maybe I was just too afraid to stop. I was afraid of the dark on the other side of letting go, and I was afraid of what happens to a sealed country when its last anchor lets go, and mostly I was afraid that if I stopped holding, I'd find out that everything I remembered—the salt under his tongue, the blue-edged fires, my mother singing—that none of it had ever been real, that I'd dreamed it all in the dark to keep myself company."

Her voice didn't break. That was the worst part. It stayed level and small and precise, a child reporting the contents of a nightmare she'd had time to memorize.

"Then, eight months ago, the holding changed. The Door began to pull me through instead of holding me still. Something on the outside was calling. Something old, something the guild—" she glanced at Sefa, "—something your guild buried and half-remembered. The pull was gentle at first. Then it wasn't. It squeezed me out the way water comes through a crack. And I walked out of the mountain barefoot, and I didn't know any words except the ones from home, and I followed the smell of salt, because salt was the first thing I ever loved, and salt led me to your door."

Sefa was crying. Ines had never seen her cry, not at funerals, not when the guild fell to four, not when the Wardens posted their notices on the toll house doors. She cried now, silently, both hands pressed over her mouth, and Nia watched her with an expression far too gentle for a seven-year-old's face.

"I'm sorry," Nia told her. "I know what your guild lost, the winter it caused. I felt it happen. Cold travels through a seal like sound through a wall. I heard your guild dying for a whole winter, two hundred years ago, and I couldn't do anything, and neither could you, and we've both been carrying it alone ever since. You can put it down now. At least partway. At least while I'm watching."

* * *

Later, when Brann had finally been coaxed into sleep and Tomas stood watch at the entrance with his charts spread out like armor, Ines sat with Nia at the edge of the firelight.

"Two hundred years," Ines said quietly. "You're older than the Crown. Older than the Wardens. Older than every road I've ever walked."

"I'm also seven," Nia said. "Both things are true. That's the hardest part. Inside, I'm still the girl who hid behind her mother during thunderstorms. Outside—" she held up her small hands, turned them over, studied them like a stranger's, "—outside I've had two hundred years to become something else, and I never got to choose what."

Ines thought about her mentor, dead on a pass she'd walked a hundred times safe. Thought about the guildhouse with its four candles and its empty bunks. Thought about the choice waiting at the end of the mentor's route, the one the journal circled but never named.

"Nia. When we reach the Door. What happens to you?"

The girl was quiet a long moment. Firelight moved across her face, and for once she looked exactly her age—tired, frightened, wanting someone else to answer.

"I don't know," she said. "And for the first time in two hundred years, not knowing feels almost like being alive."

Outside, far down the valley, a light appeared where no light should be. Then another. Torch-fire, moving in formation, patient as winter.

Vane had found the old road after all.
