## 13. Sefa's Confession

The shrine had no name that any of them knew, which was fitting, Sefa thought. Most of the true things in the mountains had lost their names the same way the guild had lost its purpose—slowly, politely, one generation at a time, until only the shape remained and everyone agreed not to look too closely at it.

It sat in a fold of stone above the tree line, a hollowed-out chapel older than the Crown, older than the Wardens, older maybe than the maps Tomas had burned his family's honor over. Its walls were black granite veined with something that caught the firelight and held it a beat too long, like the stone was remembering. Someone had carved spirals into the lintel before anyone alive had been born. Nia had touched them on the way in and said a word in Vessarine, soft as breath, and the spirals had warmed under her small fingers.

Sefa had watched that happen from the doorway and felt sixty years of careful silence crack down the middle like river ice.

Now the others slept. Brann, feverish and gray, propped against the far wall with his bandaged side rising and falling in shallow rhythm. Tomas curled around his satchel of forbidden charts like a man sleeping beside a lover he'd wronged. Ines sat by the door with her back straight even in exhaustion, because Ines never fully slept, not since the ford, not since the blood.

And Nia lay on Sefa's own folded cloak, breathing the slow, deep breaths of a child who had been afraid for two hundred years and had finally, briefly, stopped.

Sefa built up the fire. Then she sat with her hands in her lap for a long time, doing what she had done every night for forty years: nothing. Guarding the nothing. Keeping the nothing safe.

Tonight the nothing spoke to her.

"You're going to tell her," she murmured to herself, in the old guild cant, the tongue of toll-keepers and road-witches. "Or you'll die with it in your mouth and she'll find it there, and that's worse."

Ines's voice came from the doorway, low. "Tell who what?"

Sefa hadn't heard her cross the room. The girl moved like salt wind now—quiet, inevitable, getting into everything. Since the ford something had changed in her. She carried her hands differently. She looked at people the way she used to look at weather coming over the pass.

"Come sit," Sefa said. "Or stand. You won't sit. You haven't sat properly in two weeks."

Ines didn't sit. She leaned against the wall near the door, between Sefa and the night, and waited. That was the thing about her—the patience. Everyone thought Ines Oyelaran was impatient because she walked fast and talked fast and rolled her eyes like a girl half her age. But put her in front of a closed pass or a closed mouth and she could wait out a glacier.

So Sefa waited too, until the words found their own way up, the way water finds cracks.

* * *

"The winter I'm going to tell you about happened thirty-one years ago," she began. "Before your time. Before most of the graves behind the guildhouse. They call it the White Famine in the valley villages, and they blame it on a bad jet stream off the Thornfell icefields, and the scholars in the capital wrote three papers about volcanic ash, and none of them know anything."

Ines said nothing. Her eyes flicked once to Nia, asleep, then back.

"Half the guild died," Sefa said. "Eleven of twenty-two. My mother among them. And my grandmother. And my aunt Ferren, who taught me to read snow. We buried them in one week because the ground was iron and we had to chip the graves out with picks." Her hands, knotted and brown and steady as bridge cable, turned over in her lap. "And I have let every soul since believe it was weather."

The fire popped. Somewhere above them, wind moved through the high stones of the shrine and made a sound almost like singing.

"It wasn't weather," Sefa said. "We did it. The guild. We opened the Door—or we tried to, or we cracked it, which may be worse than either—and the cold that came out of it killed eleven of us and froze the lowland orchards and starved a generation of valley children who never knew why."

She heard herself say it and was surprised at how small it sounded. Thirty-one years of carrying it, and it weighed less spoken aloud than unspoken. That felt like an obscenity. It should weigh more. Eleven graves should weigh more.

Ines had gone very still. Not shocked—Sefa had known the girl since she was nine years old, all elbows and stolen apples, and had watched her learn to hide everything behind competence the way other children hid behind tantrums. This stillness was Ines working. Ines calculating.

"The journal," Ines said quietly. "My mentor's journal. The coded route. You knew what it led to."

"I wrote half the code," Sefa said. "When I was young and certain the old ones were cowards."

* * *

She told it properly then, the whole rotten inheritance, because if she was going to dig up the bones she might as well lay them out in order.

The Salt Road Guides' Guild had not been founded to guide salt. That was the cover story, and like all good cover stories it had grown so useful the family forgot it was a costume. Salt paid the tolls. Salt kept the caravans coming and the Wardens, back when there were Wardens worth the name, tolerant. But the first Guides—the seven women and two men whose names were carved inside the guildhouse hearthstone where no visitor would ever look—had been sworn to something else entirely.

"They were Vessarine," Sefa said. "Not all of them. But enough. Servants of the royal house who fled east when the Erasure came, when the maps were burned and the borders rewritten and every chart in every chancellery was ordered scraped clean of the word Vessar. Your cartographer's family did the scraping, by the way. Ask him sometime how the ink felt coming off. He won't answer, but ask him."

Tomas stirred in his sleep and muttered something about contour lines.

"The ones who fled couldn't go back. The Door had been sealed from the outside—sealed *with* the kingdom, understand, the whole country folded away behind it, king and kitchens and graveyards and all—and the sealing was meant to be permanent. But permanence is a human word. Stone doesn't promise. So the survivors made a covenant: someone will always watch the Door. Someone will always remember the language. Someone will always be ready, if the day ever comes, to open it again and bring Vessar home."

Sefa's throat tightened around the next part. She made herself say it anyway. She was seventy-one years old; she had earned the right to say hard sentences slowly, but she had also learned that slow sentences gave you time to run from them.

"For six generations, the covenant meant watching. Tending. Learning the language so it wouldn't die, keeping the route in living memory, marking the safe approaches. Humble work. Faithful work. And then—" she laughed, and hated the sound of it, dry as old rope— "and then came my grandmother's generation. Clever women. Ambitious women. Women who read the old texts and noticed that the sealing ritual had a counterweight written in the margin, and decided that two hundred years of waiting was quite enough."

"We found the Door," Sefa said. "That part was easy; the route was always ours. What we didn't find was any way to open it gently. The counterweight required a key of royal blood speaking the opening words at the threshold. We had no royal blood. So my grandmother—brilliant, patient, damned woman that she was—decided we would do it *approximately*. A circle of twelve Guides, each carrying a fragment of the ritual, each holding a piece of the old tongue, pouring everything we had into the seam."

She looked at her hands again. They had done this. These hands, younger then, smoother, but these hands, hers, had stood in a ring of chanting women in the dark beneath the Spire and pushed against a door that God or whatever came before God had shut for a reason.

"And it worked," she whispered. "That's the horror of it. It worked a little. The Door opened—a crack. A hand's width. For perhaps ninety heartbeats."

"What came out?" Ines asked. Her voice was flat, controlled, the voice she used at toll negotiations when the caravan master tried to short them.

"Cold," Sefa said. "Not temperature. Cold like a *thing*. It came out of the crack like water under pressure, and it went through us, and it kept going. It went down the mountain and into the valleys and it did not stop for eleven days. Our fires shrank instead of growing. Our breath froze in our lungs mid-cough. Marta Oyelaran—no relation to you, though she had the same stubborn chin—died standing up, holding the rope line, frozen through before she fell. My mother lasted four days. She spent them telling me to run home and stoke the hearth, as if it were a hearth-fire problem. As if any fire in the world could touch what we'd let out."

"Ninety heartbeats," Ines repeated. "Of a crack."

"And it killed half the guild and a decade of harvests." Sefa nodded. "Now you understand why the survivors rewrote the covenant. No more opening. Ever. Not partially, not approximately, not for any reason. Watch, tend, remember—but never, never open. My grandmother lived another nine years after the famine, blind by the end, and she spent every one of those years writing warnings into the guild records in cipher, because she knew how clever women think. She knew we'd read the old ambition in the margins someday and get ideas. She wanted the last word to be *don't*."

"She got it?"

"She got it. Page after page. *Don't. Don't. Don't.*" Sefa smiled without any warmth in it. "I burned those pages myself, when I became matriarch. All except the route code."

Ines straightened off the wall. "You *burned* them?"

"I burned them because I had read them too many times," Sefa said. "Because every winter, when the passes closed and the guild sat around the fire with nothing to do but remember, I would catch myself thinking: *we were so close. Ninety heartbeats. With proper preparation—with a true key—we could do it right.*" Her voice cracked, finally, at last, and she let it. "Do you see? That's what the devourer does. It doesn't need to reach you. It waits behind a door and lets your own cleverness carry you to it. My grandmother understood that better than anyone, because it had eaten her, and she knew the only defense was to make sure no one who came after ever had the recipe."

"So you destroyed the warning to protect us from the temptation the warning described."

"I destroyed it because I didn't trust myself," Sefa said simply. "Forty years as matriarch, and I never trusted myself with that knowledge. And then a barefoot child knocked on our door speaking royal Vessarine, and I learned that the world doesn't care what you've burned."

* * *

The silence afterward was long. Sefa fed the fire and let it be long. Confession, she had learned late, was mostly learning to survive the quiet after the words.

It was Ines who broke it, and Sefa braced for anger—for the cold, precise fury the girl had learned from her, actually, which would be its own particular punishment.

"Why didn't you tell me at the guildhouse?" Ines asked instead. "The night Nia came. You went white. You sent me to bed and stayed up with Sefa's-old-secrets all night, and in the morning you handed me the mentor's journal and said *follow the route, trust the child*, and not one word of *by the way, the last time we touched that Door it murdered my mother*."

"Would it have changed what you did?"

"Yes!" Ines's voice rose, then dropped, glancing at Nia. "No. I don't know. That's not the point. The point is I've been walking toward that Door for weeks believing it was a door. A thing with two sides. Open it, close it, choose. And you knew—I was carrying your mother's death in my pack the whole way and you let me think it was just a map."

"Yes," Sefa said. "I did."

She made herself hold the girl's eyes. Ines had her mentor's stare, the one that could take a caravan master apart bolt by bolt.

"I told myself it was protection," Sefa said. "That if you knew the cost you'd hesitate at the threshold, and hesitation kills on mountain roads. That was partly true. But the truer thing, Ines—the thing I'll say plainly because you've earned plain—is that I was a coward. Again. The same cowardice, wearing different clothes. Thirty-one years ago the guild was too proud to leave the Door alone, and I have been too frightened to speak of it since, and both times I chose what was easiest for *me* and called it duty." She spread her hands. "There it is. The whole ugly ledger. Judge it as you like."

Ines was quiet for a while. Outside, the wind had changed—it had a new edge to it tonight, a thinness, and Sefa noted it with the automatic attention of sixty years on the roads and filed it away to worry about later.

"I'm angry," Ines said at last. "I want you to know that. I'm angry the way you're angry at a rope that held, you understand? It frayed, it should have snapped, it should have told you it was fraying, and it held anyway, and you can't decide whether to curse it or kiss it." She rubbed her face with both hands, suddenly looking every hour of her exhausted age. "You raised me. You taught me everything. And you lied to me for months. Both things are true and I don't get to pick one."

"No," Sefa agreed. "You don't."

"But Nia trusts you. You're the one she goes to when the nightmares come. You're the one whose cloak she sleeps on." Ines looked at the sleeping child, at the small hand curled against the black granite floor. "So here's my imperfect forgiveness, since you asked for it and I'm too tired to make it pretty: I forgive you for the secret. I don't forgive you yet for deciding alone what I could bear. Those are different debts. One's paid. The other you'll work off—by being honest from here to the Spire. Every danger, every doubt, every ugly thing you know about that Door. Starting now. Starting with the question I should have asked weeks ago."

Sefa waited.

"If the Door needs a key of royal blood," Ines said, "and the last heir of Vessar has been walking beside us eating porridge and holding your hand—then what exactly did your grandmother's ritual *need* that you didn't have?" Her eyes narrowed. "And what happens when we arrive at that threshold with the real thing?"

The firelight caught the spiral carvings above the door, and Sefa saw, with a lurch, that they were glowing faintly. Just barely. Just at the edges. As if the stone remembered the word Nia had spoken, and was passing it along, deeper into the mountain, to something that had been listening for two hundred years.

"That," Sefa said slowly, "is the question I burned thirty-one years of records hoping no one would ever ask."

And through the shrine's high window, past the stars, the first flakes of an early snow were beginning to fall—in June.
