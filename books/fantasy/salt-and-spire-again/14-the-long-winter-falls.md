## 14. The Long Winter Falls

The cold came three weeks early, and it came wrong.

Ines knew winter the way she knew her own hands—every mood of it, every trick. Thornfell winters arrived in stages: a whisper of frost, a gray week of sleet, then snow that settled in like a debt collector. This was not that. This was a wall. One morning the wind off the high passes smelled of salt and stone and something older, and by noon the puddles in the guildyard had frozen from the bottom up, which Ines had never once seen in nineteen years on the mountain.

"Bottom up," Brann said, crouched over the well trough, his bandaged side pulling at him as he leaned. He touched the ice and flinched—not from cold. "Water freezes from the top. Everyone knows that."

"Everyone knows Wardens don't burn villages either," Sefa said from the doorway, "and yet."

She didn't finish the joke. None of them were finishing jokes lately.

Inside, Nia sat by the hearth with Tomas's old atlas open across her knees, tracing coastlines that no longer existed with one small finger. She'd been speaking more each week—short sentences now, careful as a child carrying water. *The sea was warm. The king was kind. The door was made to keep.* But today she wasn't speaking at all. She was shivering, though the fire was banked high, and her breath came out white even three feet from the flames.

Ines knelt beside her. "Nia. Look at me."

The girl's eyes were strange—frosted at the edges, like a window in deep winter. When she spoke, her voice came out thin. "It knows I'm coming."

"What knows?"

"The door." She pressed her palm flat against the floorboards, and frost bloomed outward from her fingers in a perfect fan. "It's been asleep so long. It's lonely. It's reaching for me the way—" she searched for the word, and Ines watched her find it and hate it, "—the way I used to reach for my mother. Before I remembered I didn't have one yet."

Tomas set down his pen. He'd been redrawing the pre-Erasure charts all week, working by lamplight until his eyes went bloodshot, and now he looked at Nia the way he looked at a map with a contradiction in it. "The Door isn't reaching for you," he said gently. "The thing behind it is. There's a difference, and we need to remember there's a difference."

"I know the difference." Nia closed the atlas. Frost crackled across its cover. "I've known the difference for two hundred years. That's the whole problem."

* * *

They left the guildhouse two days later because Sefa said they had no choice, and Sefa was never wrong about things like that.

The plan was simple enough to say aloud without anyone laughing: take the mentor's route through the smuggler's road, reach the valley village before the deep snows sealed the high passes, and move fast enough that Vane's scouts—if they were still out there, and they were—couldn't close the net. Brann's wound had closed but not healed; he walked with a spear cut down to a walking staff and refused, flatly, to be left behind. Sefa locked the guildhouse door behind them with the ceremony of a woman closing a coffin, kissed her own palm and pressed it to the wood, and said nothing.

The first day was ordinary cold. The second day was not.

They came down into the hamlet of Lowbridge an hour past dawn and found the village frozen mid-motion. Not dead—frozen. A man stood at his gate with a bucket in both hands, icicles hanging from his beard, breathing shallowly, alive but sunk so deep in frozen sleep that Brann's shout bounced off him like weather. A dog lay curled on a doorstep, iced into its own fur. Smoke stood straight up from chimneys in gray columns that didn't drift, as if the air itself had been nailed in place.

"Nia," Ines said quietly. "Stay behind me."

"It's not me." The girl's voice shook. "I told you. It's the door. It's leaking." She looked down the frozen street with those frosted eyes, and what Ines saw in her face wasn't fear. It was recognition, and grief, and something terribly like homesickness. "This is what the last winter looked like. The one Sefa told us about. The one the guild caused. I remember it from inside."

Sefa had gone the color of old tallow. "From inside," she repeated.

"You have to understand how it felt from the other side of the door." Nia took Ines's hand. Her fingers were cold as river stones. "Every time someone tries to open it—or every time it thinks someone might—it pushes. Cold is just pushing. It doesn't know it's killing anyone. It's like a man dreaming who strikes out at whatever touches him." She looked up. "And everyone on this mountain is touching him right now, because I'm here, and I'm getting closer, and he can feel me getting closer."

Ines looked down the street at the man with the bucket. At the standing smoke. At the whole world holding its breath around them.

"How do we make it stop?" she asked.

"We don't." Nia said it simply, the way children say true things. "We get there faster."

* * *

They moved through a country going down like a lamp being shuttered room by room.

Lowbridge behind them. Then the mill at Harrow Ford, wheel locked in ice, the miller's family huddled around a stove that burned everything burnable and still couldn't push back the frost creeping through the walls like a living thing. Then the shepherd camps on the high flank of Cragmoor, empty, sheep dead in their pens with ice in their wool, shepherds gone—fled downslope toward the lowlands, toward towns, toward anywhere.

By the fourth day the roads were full of refugees.

Ines had spent her whole life being invisible. That was the guild's gift: guides moved through mountains like weather, noticed only when needed, forgotten the moment the pass was safe. She had smuggled salt past patrols, secrets past magistrates, and herself past every eye that might have lingered. Invisibility was a craft, and she had been its finest apprentice.

Now she walked a road crowded with hundreds of people, and every one of them saw her.

Not her face—they didn't know her face. They saw the child. Word had run ahead of them down the valleys the way word always runs: *a girl who walks barefoot in the snow and doesn't freeze. A girl the cold follows like a dog follows its master.* Refugees pressed against the roadside as they passed, and Ines watched their faces and read the whole ugly litany there—wonder, hope, terror, blame. An old woman reached out and touched Nia's hair as if she were a saint's relic. A man spat at the ground they walked on. A mother pulled her children close and whispered something Ines chose not to hear.

"They're freezing," Nia said that night, in the lee of a fallen pine where they'd dared a fire. Around them, scattered along the road, refugee families huddled under cart-tilts and blankets, and the frost crept toward them in slow silver filigree wherever the child's warmth failed to reach. "All of them. Because of me."

"Because of the Door," Ines corrected.

"Because of me. I'm the key. Keys don't get to say the lock isn't their fault." Nia pulled her knees up. For a moment she sounded exactly her apparent age, seven or eight, and infinitely older than that. "When I was chosen—I remember this now, I remember all of it—the priests asked me if I understood what I was agreeing to. And I said yes, because I was a good girl and good girls say yes. Nobody ever asked me again. Two hundred years, and nobody ever asked me anything."

Ines was quiet a long moment. The fire cracked. Somewhere down the road a baby cried, thin and furious against the cold.

"I'm asking you now," she said. "Do you want to go to the Spire? Knowing what it costs? You could stop. We could turn south, cross the border, lose ourselves in the Saltmarsh. The cold would follow, maybe, but slower. People would have years instead of weeks."

Nia looked at her with those frosted eyes, and Ines saw her weigh it—actually weigh it, honestly, the way no adult had ever let her weigh anything.

"If I run," Nia said, "it keeps reaching. It gets angrier. And someday it gets loose anyway, and there's no one at the door who loves it. Someone has to be there who loves it, Ines. Even the bad thing. Especially the bad thing. That's why they chose a child. Children are the only ones who can love something that eats people." She smiled, and it was the saddest smile Ines had ever seen. "So yes. I want to go. Ask me again at the door, though. Promise you'll ask me again."

"I promise."

* * *

Vane found them on the sixth day, or rather his writ did—nailed to the doorpost of the waystation at Stonebeck where they stopped to trade for flour, fluttering in air too still to move it.

BY ORDER OF THE WARDENS OF THE CROWN: The anomaly reported upon the high roads is hereby declared a matter of Crown peril. All persons harboring, concealing, or failing to report the child described herein are guilty of treason against realm and winter alike. Aid is amnesty. Obstruction is the rope.

Below it, in smaller print, someone had added the description: *a girl of seven years, dark-haired, walks unshod.*

"Winter itself," Brann read over Ines's shoulder. "He's deputized the weather."

"He's deputized fear," Sefa said. "Cleverer. Weather you can't inform on your neighbors to."

But it was worse than the writ. The waystation keeper—a woman Ines had traded with for a decade, who owed the guild three favors and her life besides—took the flour money and wouldn't meet Ines's eyes. "People are saying," she murmured, counting coins she didn't count, "that if the girl gets where she's going, the cold stops. And if she doesn't, it doesn't. And that the Wardens will pay a house's worth of silver to any guide who'll tell them which road she's taking."

"And what do you say?" Ines asked.

The woman finally looked up. Her eyes were red-rimmed, exhausted, honest. "I say I've got four grandchildren sleeping in that back room, Ines Oyelaran, and I say I'm sorry."

That night Ines sat the others down in the ruins of a shrine above Stonebeck—some old pre-Erasure waystop, walls thick, roof half-collapsed, the kind of place only a guide would know—and laid it out plain.

"They know we're on the mountain. Every guide between here and the Spire has been offered silver, and some of them will take it, and I won't blame them, because look at what's happening out there." She gestured at the wall, beyond which the frost was climbing the stones in patterns like ferns. "For nineteen years my job was to be nobody. Move quiet, take no sides, deliver the goods. That worked when the cargo was salt." She looked at Nia, asleep against Sefa's shoulder, frost feathering and melting at the hem of her cloak with each breath. "The cargo's changed. There's no invisible way to carry a living key through a starving country. Every step we take, people see us, and every person who sees us has to choose, and some of them will choose their grandchildren. I would. So we stop pretending there's a quiet route. We take the loud one. The hard one. The one where we're seen."

Tomas rubbed his eyes. "Which is?"

"The mentor's route goes through the valley," Ines said. "The village that remembers. Two hundred people who sing Vessarine lullabies and have been waiting, maybe, for a reason to stop hiding." She met their eyes one by one. "If we're going to be seen, let's be seen by people who might stand with us."

Brann grunted—the sound he made instead of arguing. Sefa nodded slowly. And Nia, waking without seeming to wake, said from the edge of sleep:

"They'll ask me to sing. I remember all the songs. I remember all of them, and I've never once gotten to sing them for anyone."

* * *

The cold broke over the valley on the eighth day like a wave over a seawall.

They heard it before they saw it—the sound of the village bells, all of them ringing at once, not alarm but something else, a pattern Ines didn't know and realized, with a chill that had nothing to do with temperature, was music. The elders were ringing the old pattern. The one from before the maps changed.

The villagers met them at the valley mouth, and it should have been beautiful and partly was—but the beauty had teeth now. Because the cold had come down the mountainside ahead of them, and the village's sheltered fields were silvered and dying, and the people who sang the welcome hymn did it through chattering teeth, and the eldest elder, a woman named Maren with a face like a walnut, took Nia's frozen hands in hers and said, in courtly, careful Vessarine:

*"Welcome home, little majesty. We hoped. God forgive us, we hoped, and look what hoping cost."*

And Nia answered in the same tongue, fluently, fully, the most words Ines had ever heard from her at once, and though Ines caught only fragments—*sorry*, and *closer*, and *soon*—she understood the shape of it entirely.

That night, in the village longhouse, with two hundred refugees from the frozen highlands packed among the villagers, with the fires burning and the frost still creeping up the window glass from outside, Maren took Ines aside.

"My grandmother kept a letter," the old woman said. "Passed down since the Erasure. It says that when the heir comes home, the door will either open or be shut forever, and that the choosing must happen at the threshold, and that no one may choose for her." She fixed Ines with eyes gone pale as skimmed milk. "It also says the devourer wakes when the key comes near. Is that true, guide?"

"Yes," Ines said.

"Then you have days, not weeks. The scouts were seen at the north ridge this morning. Red cloaks. And the cold is moving faster than any army." Maren gripped her arm with surprising strength. "My people hid for two hundred years and called it survival. Your guild hid and called it duty. The Wardens hunt and call it mercy. Everyone hiding, everyone dutiful, everyone merciful—and a child pays for all of it." She released her. "Get her up the mountain, Ines Oyelaran. Whatever it costs. The bill's already come due for the rest of us."

Outside, the first flakes of the unnatural snow began to fall—falling upward at the edges, drifting against the wind, as if drawn toward the Spire rather than down from the sky. Nia stood in the longhouse doorway watching them, and the snow bent around her like iron filings around a lodestone.

"Ines," she said softly. "It's time. He's waking up. And I think—" her voice caught, "—I think part of him is happy to see me."

Above the clouds, far up the mountain, something ancient turned over in its sleep, and every bell in the valley rang once, unprompted, and went silent.
