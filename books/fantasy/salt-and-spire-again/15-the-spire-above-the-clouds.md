## 15. The Spire Above the Clouds

Brann had fought in the Warden campaigns for eleven years, and he had never once commanded from a bed.

He hated it. He hated it with a purity that surprised him, because hatred was supposed to be simple and this was not. His leg was packed in snowmelt and yarrow paste, splinted from ankle to knee, propped on a rolled blanket in the shrine's back chamber where the cold came through the stones like a whisper he couldn't shut out. Every hour the whisper got louder. Every hour the frost on the inside of the windows climbed another finger's width up the glass, and somewhere far below them, in the world, people were freezing in their beds because a door two centuries sealed was coming loose at its hinges.

And he was lying down.

"Again," he said.

Tomas lifted the signal mirror to the gap in the shutters, caught the sun—or what passed for sun now, a pale coin behind the cloud deck—and flashed three long, one short. *Hold the ridge. Hold the ridge.* Down in the valley village, half a mile of switchbacks below, someone would be reading it on their own glass and relaying it up the line: Ines, Nia, Sefa, and six villagers who had volunteered with the particular quiet of people who had already decided they were going to die and found that decision freeing rather than frightening.

The mirror winked back. Three long, one short. *Understood.*

"They're moving," Tomas said. He'd wedged himself against the wall by the window with his charts spread across a plank laid over two stools, and he didn't look up from them, which Brann knew was how Tomas handled fear—by pretending the paper was more interesting than the world. "The scouts. They're climbing the western spur."

"How many?"

"Four. Maybe five." A pause. "They're being careful about it. Vane's careful."

*Vane's careful.* Yes. Brann had served under him for six of those eleven years, and careful was the word, though it didn't go far enough. Aldric Vane planned like a man building a bridge—he accounted for every load, every wind, every flaw in the stone—and when he'd finished planning, he crossed, and whatever was on the other side of the crossing stopped existing as a problem. Brann had watched him do it to bandit holds, to smuggler nests, to a fortified mill full of old-blood families who had done nothing worse than remember their grandmothers' songs.

The mill had been the last thing. After the mill there had been a night in the rain and a choice, and after the choice there had been running, and after the running there had been Ines Oyelaran pulling him out of a river with a rope and no questions, and everything since had been a debt he was still trying to understand how to pay.

Now he was paying it flat on his back, shouting numbers through a shutter.

"The spur's a feint," he said. "Tell them. The real push comes up the pilgrim stair—always does, always will. Vane thinks in approaches. The stair is the approach."

Tomas flashed it. Below, faintly, the answer came back: two short. *Seen.*

But the answer came back too fast, and Brann felt something cold slide into his chest that had nothing to do with the weather. Two short meant *seen*. It did not mean *believed*. And Ines—

"Ines is going to hold the spur herself," he said. It wasn't a question. He knew her. Eleven days on the undermap road, carrying a wounded man and a child older than empires, and he knew her the way you know a blade you've been sharpening: by the shape of the edge. "She'll split them. She'll keep four on the spur because she can't stand leaving it empty, and she'll put everyone else on the stair, and she'll be wrong, because Vane wants her to think the spur matters."

Tomas lowered the mirror slowly. "You could tell her that."

"She wouldn't listen. She shouldn't listen. I'm a deserter with a broken leg telling a guild guide how to defend a mountain." He laughed, and it came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "Gods. Listen to me. I'm giving orders."

"You're giving *good* orders," Tomas said. "There's a difference, and she knows it. Why do you think she asked for the mirror to come to your room?"

Because she trusted him. That was the terrible part. Not the way the Wardens had trusted him once—trust as a function, trust as rank and protocol—but the way you trust someone who has seen you at your worst and stayed anyway. She had watched him hold the choke point in the caverns while the dark came down on him like water, and she had carried him out, and she had never once said *you don't have to make up for anything*, which was the only kindness that would have worked, because any other kindness would have made him argue.

He looked at the window instead. Through the gap in the shutters, above the cloud sea that had swallowed the valleys whole, the Spire stood.

It was worse than the stories. Stories made things manageable; stories had edges. The Spire had none. It rose out of the clouds like the fingerbone of some god who had died mid-gesture, black stone streaked with ice, so tall that its crown bent the light around it into a faint wrongness, like heat-shimmer in winter. No banners. No windows lit. Just that impossible vertical line drawn against the sky, saying: *here. Here is where it ended. Here is where it begins again if you let it.*

Beneath it, wrapped around its root like a ring of knuckles, were the ruins of the old approach—the terraces, the gatehouse, the pilgrim stair cut into the living rock, nine hundred steps that Vessarine kings had once climbed barefoot in penance. Somewhere under all of it, deeper than any stair went, the Hollow Door waited. And Nia was walking toward it, small and silent and singing under her breath in a language that made the frost on the windows grow.

* * *

The first attack came at dusk, and it came exactly where Brann said it would.

He heard it before he saw it—the sound rolling up the mountain like distant thunder that had learned rhythm: fire-oil pots bursting on the lower terrace, the crackle of flame that burned too fast and too hungry in air that thin, shouts in Warden cadence, and then, cutting through everything, a sound he hadn't heard in years and had hoped never to hear again.

The horn. Three notes, rising. *Advance in force.*

"They're on the stair," Tomas breathed, mirror forgotten in his lap.

Brann hauled himself upright despite the leg, teeth locked against the white flash of pain, and got his eye to the shutter gap. Far below, tiny as chess pieces, torchlight was snaking up the pilgrim stair in disciplined columns. And above them, on the terraces, the defenders' answering lights moved—too few, too scattered, exactly as he'd feared. Ines had held the spur with two. She'd kept the rest for the stair, but the stair was nine hundred steps of open rock with no cover, and fire-oil flowed downhill.

"Signal," he rasped. "Tell them to give up the third terrace. Collapse the stair arch behind them—there's an arch, Tomas, at the fourth turn, it's in the old charts, it's a keystone design, bring it down and the column breaks in half."

"I can't read Vessarine engineering—"

"I can." Brann had burned villages for the Crown; he had also, in another life, spent three years mapping Warden supply routes across these same mountains, and the old Vessarine works had been part of every survey. "Fourth turn. Single keystone. Tell Ines: *the arch remembers how to fall.*"

Tomas stared at him. "That's not a signal code."

"It is now. She'll know. Her mentor's journal—she showed me the cipher once—it uses the same phrase for structural collapse." He was talking fast, faster than the pain allowed, riding the edge of it. "Go. Flash it. And then get down to the relay station, because when the arch goes, Vane's going to shift to night assault, and someone has to talk me through the signals while I watch the stair."

Tomas went. The mirror flashed. And Brann lay back against the wall, sweating, and watched the mountain burn in miniature, and understood with perfect clarity that he was doing the thing he had run from doing at the mill: choosing, in advance, which lives to spend and where.

At the mill he had refused to choose, and the refusing had cost him everything. Now the choosing cost nothing but sleep, and sleep wasn't coming anyway.

* * *

The arch fell at moonrise.

They heard it even up here—a deep, grinding boom, felt in the floorboards more than heard, followed by a silence with a specific quality to it, the silence of men realizing the path behind them no longer existed. Then the horn again, but different now. Two notes, falling. *Withdraw and regroup.*

Brann let out a breath he felt he'd been holding for eleven years.

It lasted until Tomas came pounding back up the stairs, gray-faced, chart tube still slung across his back like a sword he'd forgotten to draw.

"It worked," Tomas said. "The column's broken. Half of them are cut off on the lower steps with no way up and no way down except past our people. But—" He stopped. Brann watched him decide whether to say the next part, and watched him lose the argument with himself. "Ines signaled from the spur. They're pulling back to the gatehouse. She lost one of the villagers. And Brann—Vane's not regrouping. The main camp is moving. All of it. Torches, siege lines, the fire-oil wagons. Everything."

"To where?"

Tomas's throat worked. "Not the stair. Not the spur. Around. Through the cloud line, on the north face, where nobody's ever garrisoned because nobody thought it climbable." He unrolled a chart with shaking hands, and Brann saw it: the north face of the Spire's root, a sheer sweep of ice-sheathed rock that every mapmaker for two hundred years had marked with the same notation. *Impassable.*

"He's not going around the defenses," Brann said slowly. "He's going around the *mountain's opinion of itself*." And then, colder: "How long?"

"At that pace, with those numbers?" Tomas did the arithmetic with his eyes closed, the way he did when the answer was bad. "Dawn. They reach the upper terraces by dawn. And Brann—the cold's getting worse. The Door's waking up. Whatever happens at dawn, the mountain itself is starting to fight both sides."

Brann looked at the window. The frost had climbed past the shutter hinges now. Beyond it, the Spire stood against a sky going the color of a bruise, and high on its flank, impossibly, a light had appeared—not torchlight, nothing so warm. A seam of pale gold, thin as a knife cut, glowing in the black stone near the tower's base.

The Door. Showing itself. As if it knew the hour was coming, and wanted to watch.

He thought of Nia, somewhere between here and that light, a child in borrowed boots singing to the frost. He thought of Ines on the spur, spending herself like coin. He thought of Sefa, old and stubborn, standing wherever the thinnest point was, because that was where Sefa had always stood.

He thought of the mill, and the order he'd refused, and the village that burned anyway.

"No," he said aloud, to the room, to the mountain, to the eleven years and the broken leg and the man he'd been. "Not this time."

Tomas turned. "What?"

Brann reached for the crutch leaning against the bedframe. His leg screamed. He let it.

"Get my sword," he said. "And signal Ines: *hold until the leg arrives.*"

* * *

Below, in the dark, the Wardens began to climb the north face. Above, in the gold-lit dark beneath the Spire, something ancient turned its attention toward the sound of approaching boots—and Brann Thornbury, deserter, coward, cripple, started down the stairs to meet the dawn.
