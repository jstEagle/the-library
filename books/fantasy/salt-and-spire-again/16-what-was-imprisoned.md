## 16. What Was Imprisoned

Tomas had spent his whole life believing that maps were honest.

That was the family creed, anyway, drilled into him before he could hold a stylus: *a map is a promise made to a stranger*. The Thornburys had kept that promise for six generations—coasts charted true, rivers named right, borders drawn where the Crown said and nowhere else. He'd believed it until the day he'd found the sealed drawer in his grandfather's study, the one with the charts of a country that did not exist, and understood that his family's great work had not been cartography at all. It had been amnesia, practiced professionally.

Now he stood inside the last tower of that erased country, holding a lamp up to walls that remembered everything, and wished—not for the first time—that he had stayed drunk in Saltmere until the Wardens came for him.

"Careful where you step," Ines said ahead of him. Her voice carried strangely in the Spire, flattened by stone, as if the tower were listening and disapproved of echoes. "The floor's sound. It's the walls you don't trust."

He almost laughed. Almost. "The walls."

"You'll see."

They climbed. There was no other word for it—the stair wound upward through the tower's heart in a single unbroken spiral, worn smooth by feet two centuries dead, and every twenty steps a niche held a lamp bracket still black with old oil. Someone had maintained this place once. Someone had expected it to be needed again.

Nia rode on Sefa's back, arms looped around the old woman's neck, her bare toes—someone had finally found her boots, thank every salt-blessed saint—tucked against Sefa's spine. She hadn't spoken since the shrine. Since she'd told them, in her small careful voice, that she remembered the sealing. That she had been chosen. That she had been afraid for two hundred years.

Tomas kept glancing back at her and looking away just as fast. A child older than empires. His craft had no symbols for that. No legend, no scale bar. Some things you could not draw to proportion.

Brann wasn't with them. Brann was three hundred feet below and half a mile out, laid in a smuggler's cache with a wound Tomas refused to think about in detail, directing them by signal mirror flashes off the snowfield whenever Vane's pickets moved. Three flashes: clear. Two: hide. One: run. So far, all morning, three flashes. The mountain was holding its breath for them.

"Here," Ines said.

The stair ended at a landing no wider than a wagon bed, and the wall opposite was not stone.

Tomas raised the lamp and forgot how to breathe.

It was a door—of course it was a door, the Hollow Door, the whole reason they had crossed a kingdom's worth of frozen nothing—but calling it a door was like calling the sea water. It was a seam in the world, a vertical line of darkness so complete it drank the lamplight rather than reflecting it, running floor to ceiling in a frame of white stone carved with script. Vessarine script. He knew it now; he'd been reading Nia's birthmark for weeks, tracing its spirals against the pre-Erasure charts in his satchel until the letters surrendered their names.

The frame read: *HERE THE KING KEEPS HIS HOUSE.*

"No," Sefa said behind him. Quietly. With the flat certainty of a woman reading her own death warrant. "No, that's wrong. That's *wrong*."

"Sefa—"

"The guild records say the Door was sealed *against* the king. Against Vessar. That's the whole founding purpose, boy—we guard the knowledge so no fool ever opens it and lets the tyrant's line come back." Her voice cracked on the last words. "The Door isn't supposed to be *his*."

Ines had gone very still. "Then whose is it?"

Nobody answered. Nia slid down from Sefa's back, walked to the frame, and pressed her palm flat against the white stone. The spiral birthmark on her wrist—the royal mark, the mark that had made Tomas drop a bottle of gin in a Saltmere gutter four months ago—aligned with a matching spiral carved there, worn shallow by centuries.

She looked up at them and said, in her careful, rusted Common: "House. Mine."

* * *

The chamber beyond the landing took an hour to find, because it did not want to be found. Ines found it anyway—she had her mentor's instincts if not her mentor's journal for this part, and the journal had gone silent on the Spire's interior, its final pages devolving into a single repeated phrase: *read what the masons left, read what the masons left.*

What the masons had left was a room behind a wall that swung when Nia touched it, a room no larger than a chapel, and every surface of it was covered in relief carving.

Tomas had seen the great friezes. He'd apprenticed in the royal mapworks under men who'd studied the reliefs at Cael Amath, who spoke of them the way priests speak of scripture. Nothing had prepared him for this. The carvings here were older—cruder, more urgent, cut by hands working fast, by lamplight, maybe by fear—and they told a story in panels that wrapped the room like a belt.

He began at the first panel because beginning at the first panel was the only thing keeping his hands from shaking.

A city. Towers, harbors, roads radiating outward like spokes—a country at its height. He recognized the skyline from the sealed charts. Vessar, whole.

The second panel: the same city, but the towers now wore faces. Carved crowns above carved smiles, and the smiles were wrong, too wide, the chisel-work deliberately grotesque. Below them, tiny figures knelt in rows.

"A coronation," Ines said over his shoulder. "Or something wearing one."

The third panel stopped his heart.

It showed a procession—clearly a procession, banners, trumpeters, a figure borne aloft on a litter—and the figure on the litter had the same too-wide smile, and the crowd around it was not kneeling. The crowd was *leaning toward it*, mouths open, and from the open mouths of the crowd flowed carved lines, ribbons of stone, all converging into the smiling figure's mouth. Feeding it. Not worship. Consumption.

"It ate them," Sefa whispered. She had Nia's face pressed into her shoulder, turned away, one broad hand cupping the back of the child's skull. "Whatever that is. It ate them while they cheered."

Panel four: the smiling king, and around him now a border of smaller figures fleeing, and behind the fleeing figures the land itself unraveling—carved as literal threads pulling loose, fields fraying into nothing, rivers unwinding. Where the king's shadow fell, the stone showed blankness. Not darkness. Blankness. The sculptors had polished those regions smooth, as if absence required a different technique than presence.

Tomas thought of his grandfather's sealed drawer. Of the official histories: *Vessar fell to tyranny; the Crown sealed the kingdom as mercy and warning.* He had built his career on that sentence. He had erased coastlines on command because the sentence made it righteous.

The fifth panel showed the truth the sentence had been built to bury.

A circle of figures—twelve, thirteen, he counted twice—standing hand in hand around the smiling thing, and they were not soldiers. Their dress was plain. One held a shepherd's crook. One held a book. One held a child's hand. And from each of them ran a thread of carved light, inward, binding the smiling king at the center, and beneath the whole panel ran an inscription in deep-cut letters that Nia, without being asked, read aloud in a voice like a bell heard through water:

*"We cannot kill what wears our beloved faces. So we close the house around it, and we pay the keepers, and we forget it on purpose, so that no one ever loves it open again."*

The silence afterward lasted a long time.

"So it's not a prison," Ines said finally. Her voice was steady in the way Tomas had learned meant the opposite. "It's a—" She gestured at the panel, at the ring of plain people holding hands. "It's a knot. Tied by ordinary people. And we've been telling ourselves for two hundred years that the monster was the *kingdom*."

"That's why the guild guards the knowledge," Sefa said. She sounded ancient. She sounded like the walls. "Not to keep people from opening it. To keep people from *wanting* to. Because wanting is how it gets out. You'd have to love it open. You'd have to stand at that door and mean it, and it would wear your beloved faces while you did." She looked down at Nia. "And we brought it an heir. We walked the last heir of Vessar straight to its front door."

"We brought her," Ines said, "because she's the key to saving a country."

"And the country," Tomas heard himself say, and was surprised at how calm his voice came out, calm the way frozen things are calm, "and the country is the mouth."

* * *

He worked after that. It was the only thing he knew how to do with despair—put it in a drawer, label the drawer, keep his hands moving. He copied the panels. He sketched the doorframe script. He cross-referenced the inscription against the pre-Erasure charts in his satchel, hunting for the names of the thirteen binders, because names were anchors, names were the one tool he had.

He found one. In the margin of the oldest chart, in his great-great-grandmother's hand, in ink gone brown: a list of thirteen names, and beside the thirteenth, a spiral.

Oyelaran.

He stared at it for a long time. Then he folded the chart closed and did not say anything, because some drawers stay shut until the person they belong to is ready, and Ines had enough weight to carry tonight.

Below them, faint through miles of stone and snow, a signal mirror flashed. Two flashes. Hide.

They killed the lamps and sat in the dark of the mason-chapel, the four of them and the carvings, and Tomas listened to the wind trying the tower's seams and thought about the thing he now understood was true:

Every map he'd ever loved was a lie told kindly. Every road he'd ever drawn led somewhere, and one road—one road only—led to a smiling mouth that had eaten a kingdom while the kingdom sang its praises, and it was still hungry, and it had waited two hundred years behind a door that read *HERE THE KING KEEPS HIS HOUSE*, patient as hunger, for someone to love it open.

And tomorrow or the day after, Warden-Captain Aldric Vane would come up that mountain with fire-oil to break the door forever—which might hold the thing in, or might crack the knot the thirteen had tied, no one knew which—and on the other side of the arithmetic stood a child who could open it with a touch and a wish, because she was the heir, because the house was hers, because the thing inside had spent two centuries whispering through the seam to the one person alive who could hear it.

"Tomas." Ines's voice in the dark, barely a breath. "You went quiet. What did you find?"

He looked at the folded chart in his lap. At the spiral beside the thirteenth name.

"Ines," he said. "Who was your mentor's mentor?"

Above them, far above, through the roof of the world, something in the tower answered before she could—a sound like a lock turning over, deep in the stone, patient as hunger.

And below, faint and urgent, the mirror flashed once.

Run.
