## 17. Vane's Assault

Brann had held worse ground. He told himself that as he wedged the last barrel of salt-pork against the stairhead and checked the edge of his old Warden blade. He'd held a bridge at Corren's Mill with six men against forty. He'd held a burning granary. He'd held a line at the ford while Ines carried Nia out of the water, though he hadn't known until later that his hands had been shaking so badly he couldn't reload.

But he'd never held anything that mattered like this mattered.

The stair wound down from the Spire's broken crown in nine switchbacks, each landing barely wide enough for three men abreast. Below it, through the thin cold air, he could see the Warden camp: cookfires in a neat crescent, picket lines, the black-and-silver banner of the Crown snapping above a command tent. Vane ran a disciplined operation. Brann had served under him for eleven years. He knew exactly how disciplined.

"He'll come at first light," Brann said.

Sefa stood beside him at the parapet, her hands folded into her sleeves the way she did when she was thinking about something she refused to say aloud. The wind pulled at her grey braid. "You're certain?"

"I trained them. They don't storm in the dark—they lose too many men to footing they can't see. Vane will wait for the sun to clear the eastern ridge, come up the switchbacks in column with shields locked, and burn the Door before any of us can argue theology." He spat over the edge. "He brought fire-oil. I saw the wagons."

"Then we hold the stair," Sefa said simply.

"We hold the stair," Brann agreed.

It was a lie between friends, and they both knew it. Four of them—five counting Nia, who was not a fighter—against sixty Wardens. You didn't hold a stair against those odds. You made the stair cost something, and you prayed the something bought enough time.

Below, Tomas was doing what Tomas did best: lying with paper. He'd chalked false scorch marks along the lower landings, dragged brush to suggest abandoned positions, staked out decoy bedrolls where the third switchback bent out of sight. If even one Warden squad hesitated at a feint, that was seconds. Seconds were lives up here. Maybe more than lives.

Ines came up from below with Nia asleep against her shoulder, the girl's bare feet blue with cold despite the wool wraps Sefa had knitted. Something had changed in Ines since the shrine—since Nia had spoken, since the words *I remember being afraid* had come out of that small mouth in a voice two centuries deep. She moved differently now. Quieter. Like someone who had already decided what she would pay and was just waiting for the bill.

"You should sleep," Brann said to her.

"So should you."

"One of us has to be sentimental and useless. I've volunteered." He tried to grin. It came out crooked. Ines looked at him for a long moment, and he had the uncomfortable feeling she could read the whole ledger of him—the village he hadn't burned, the men he'd led to their deaths before that, the redemption he'd been chasing down this mountain like a dog chasing a cart it would never catch.

"Brann." Her voice was low. "Whatever happens tomorrow—"

"Don't." He held up a hand. "Don't do the speech. Save it for someone who's earned it."

She almost smiled. "Stubborn ox."

"Guild motto," he said. "I'm an honorary member. Sefa signed the book drunk."

That got a real smile, brief as a struck match, and then she carried Nia down into the Spire's heart, toward the Door none of them could look at for long without feeling their thoughts bend.

* * *

First light came up red behind the eastern ridge, and with it came the horns.

Brann heard them the way you hear weather coming—a pressure change, a wrongness in the air. Then the Warden drums took up the assault cadence, three beats and a rest, three beats and a rest, and the column began to move.

They came exactly as he'd predicted. Shields locked, torchbearers sheltered in the center, fire-oil casks hauled on hand-carts by teams of four. Sixty men moving like one animal up the first switchback. Brann counted them out of habit and felt the old sergeant's arithmetic settle over him like armor: *three landings wide, column of eight, ninety seconds per switchback under pressure.*

Tomas's decoys bought them nothing. Vane had burned villages; he wasn't fooled by chalk and brush. The column hit the second landing at a trot, and Brann stepped out from behind the fallen lintel and put his first arrow through the shield-bearer's throat.

The volley that answered drove him back off the parapet, splinters screaming off stone an inch from his skull. He rolled, came up, loosed again. Beside him Sefa worked her sling with the unhurried economy of a woman who had been killing wolves off her flock for fifty years. A Warden went down clutching his knee. Another took a stone across the visor and dropped his shield, and Brann's next arrow found the gap.

They fell back to the fourth landing. Then the sixth. The math was the math. Every retreat cost them ground and gained them minutes, and every minute, somewhere below, Ines and Tomas were doing whatever needed doing around the Door.

By the eighth landing Brann had taken a sword-cut across the forearm and Sefa was bleeding from a gash on her scalp that turned her collar dark. Three barrels of fire-oil had already gone over the edge, rolled down by Wardens to be ignited at the base—a signal, a threat, a promise. The Spire smelled of pitch and blood and cold stone.

"They're tired," Sefa panted. "Look at their footing. Green troops, half of them."

"They're tired because we've killed nine of them," Brann said. "There are fifty-one left. Do the sum, old woman."

"I did the sum years ago," Sefa said. "The sum never changes. We are the guild. We hold."

The ninth landing was the last. Beyond it, the stairhead opened onto the final chamber, and beyond that—

Vane came up himself.

Brann saw him at the head of the reserve, climbing unhurried, sword drawn but lowered, and something in his chest turned over. Eleven years he'd followed that man. He knew the set of those shoulders, the way Vane saved himself for the moment a fight was actually decided. Him coming up personally meant only one thing: he no longer trusted anyone else to finish it.

"Wardens!" Vane's voice carried up the stair, flat and carrying. "You have served with honor. Stand aside, and no charges follow. This is not your fight."

"It never was anybody's fight," Brann called back. "That's the whole damn problem."

Silence. Then, closer, quieter, meant only to carry to the stairhead: "Brann. Old friend. I watched that thing eat my brother. I am asking you, man to man, one last time."

"And I'm telling you, Captain—" Brann's voice cracked, and he let it. "You're going to burn down the house to kill the rat, and the house is full of people."

"The house is full of *her*, Brann. That's all it's ever been full of."

Brann closed his eyes for half a heartbeat. Then he notched his last twelve arrows in the snow beside him and said, "Come up, then."

* * *

They came up.

What happened on the ninth landing lasted, by Brann's later reckoning—and he had very little later—perhaps four minutes. The shields came in a wedge, torchbearers behind, and Brann and Sefa broke the wedge twice with arrows and sling-stones and sheer vicious positioning, using the narrowness of the stair so only three attackers could reach them at once. Brann fought with sword and axe both, having run out of arrows, and discovered that his body remembered eleven years of drill even as his mind stood apart and observed, calm as a clerk: *left guard is open, take his knee, pivot, the big one telegraphs his thrust.*

A spear took him through the meat of the thigh. He killed the spearman. An axe glanced off his collarbone and numbed his whole left side. He kept fighting right-handed. Somewhere in the press Sefa went down under two Wardens and came up again with blood sheeting down her face, still swinging, still cursing them in three languages, and Brann laughed—he actually laughed—because there she was, seventy years old, the stubbornest matriarch the Salt Road had ever produced, holding a stair against an empire.

Then the fire-oil came up.

They passed the casks hand to hand, shielded men rolling them to the stairhead, and Brann understood with terrible clarity what the plan was: flood the landing, flood the chamber beyond, and burn everything—Door, anchor, child, witnesses. Vane had stopped trying to be surgical. His brother's face had eaten whatever patience he had left.

"No!" Brann roared, and threw himself onto the first cask as the hatchet came down to breach it.

Oil sprayed across his chest, his arms, the stones. Cold, thank all the drowned gods—unlit, still cold. He wrestled the cask's weight against the press of bodies, buying seconds, whole eternities of seconds, and heard Sefa scream his name, and heard, beneath everything, from the chamber beyond, Nia begin to sing.

It was the lullaby from the valley. Of course it was. Two hundred years old and she sang it like it was new, and every man on that stair faltered—one step, half a step, a hitch in the drumbeat of shields—and in that hitch, Brann did the arithmetic one final time.

Twelve arrows' worth of dead. Four minutes. Not enough. Never going to be enough.

But oil was cold, and oil was on him, and there was a torch in the hand of the man directly in front of him.

"Oh," Brann said softly. "Oh, you stupid, beautiful—"

He grabbed the torch.

The world went orange.

He was aware of it strangely, in pieces: the heat arriving before the pain, the pain arriving and then deciding, after consideration, to be everywhere; the screams—not only his own; the wedge of shields breaking apart like ice in spring thaw, men falling back down the stairs to escape a burning man who would not stop advancing, who advanced *because* he was burning, arms spread wide, filling the width of the stair, an immovable door of flame. He heard Sefa shouting. He thought maybe she was telling him to stop, or maybe telling him she was proud, and he found he couldn't tell the difference anymore, and found that it didn't matter, because rage and love had long ago become the same fire in him, indistinguishable, one fuel.

*I don't deserve this,* he thought, with the last of the clarity. *I burned a granary in Aldermoor. I marched on Thornhollow. I—*

And then, underneath, gentler, like snow beginning:

*But Ines thinks I do. And Ines is never wrong about people.*

He planted his boots on the ninth landing and held.

* * *

Ines heard the screaming and knew whose it was.

There was no mistaking it. She had heard Brann laugh, curse, snore, weep once, drunk, over a letter from a woman in Corren's Mill—but she had never heard him scream like that, and she knew in her marrow that he was screaming on purpose, that he had chosen the fire the way he had once refused to choose it, redeeming a single refusal with a final acceptance.

"Tomas." Her voice came out level, which surprised her. "Is it done?"

"Sealed as well as chalk and prayer can seal it." Tomas was white-faced, charcoal-stained, the pre-Erasure charts spread around him like the wings of some enormous wounded bird. "It won't stop fire. Nothing stops fire. But the inner passage holds, and the Door itself—" He looked at her. "Ines, the Door doesn't burn. Whatever else happens, the Door doesn't burn."

"But we do," Ines said. "And Nia does. Get her into the passage."

Nia wouldn't go.

The child stood in front of the Hollow Door with her small fists clenched, singing, and the Door—Ines forced herself to look at it properly for the first time—was awake. The seam that had been a hairline crack in the world now glowed faintly along its length, and through the glow Ines could feel something listening. Something vast and patient and hungry, pressing against the other side the way a moth presses against glass, except moths were small and this was not, and the glass was very old.

"Ines." Nia's voice, in common speech, clear as a bell over the chaos. "It knows I'm here. It's been waiting for me to grow up."

"I know, sweetheart." Ines knelt, took the child's face in her hands. Behind her, through the chamber entrance, the light of the stairhead fire painted everything the color of Brann's sacrifice. "We're leaving now. All of us. Together."

"The man is dying for us."

"Yes."

"Will it work? The burning?" Nia's eyes were ancient and terrified and five years old, all at once. "Or will it wake it up?"

Ines opened her mouth and had no answer, because she didn't know, and because at that moment Sefa came through the entrance with a Warden's sword buried in her side.

The matriarch had taken the blow turning—not away from it, *into* it, angling her body so that the blade meant for Nia's back found her ribs instead. She walked three more steps, which was two more than any surgeon would have credited, and then her legs went and Ines caught her, and they went down together onto the cold stone in front of the Door.

"Sefa. Sefa, look at me."

"Bossy," Sefa whispered. Blood frothed at her lips with each word. "Always... bossy. Worst apprentice... I ever took."

"You only ever took me."

"Mm." Her hand found Ines's wrist and gripped with shocking strength. "Listen. Listen to me, girl. The journal. Your mentor's journal. Page... the page with the salt stain. There's a third option. There was always a third option. Why do you think... why do you think the guild guarded knowledge instead of guarding the Door..." Her eyes drifted, focused on something past Ines's shoulder, past the wall of the world. "Oh," she breathed. "Oh, it's beautiful. It's all still there. The gardens. The—"

Her grip loosened.

"No," Ines said. "No, no—you don't get to do this, you don't get to leave me the guild and the war and the *child*—" Her voice broke apart. She pressed her forehead to Sefa's brow, and the old woman's skin was already cooling, and somewhere beyond the entrance Brann was still burning, still holding, and the Door glowed, and the thing behind it pressed, and Nia began to cry, silently, the way children cry when they have cried for two hundred years and learned to do it quietly.

Ines lifted her head.

Grief came through her like a flood through a broken toll-house, and she let it, and then—because she was her mentor's apprentice, because she was Sefa's worst and only apprentice, because she was the Salt Road and the Salt Road always got through—she stood up out of the flood and dried her face on her sleeve and turned to the Door.

The salt stain. Page with the salt stain. She could see it in memory: the cramped cipher, the diagram she'd assumed was decorative, the words she'd translated once and refused to believe: *the gate must be held from either side; a willing anchor may stand within.*

"Get her back," Ines said to Tomas, without turning around. "Get Nia back. Whatever happens next, keep her back."

"Ines—" Tomas's voice, ragged. "Ines, what are you—"

Behind her, at the stairhead, the fire roared once, enormously, and then began, note by note, to gutter.

The hook of the next hour caught in her chest and pulled. She stepped toward the glowing seam, and the seam widened, gently, like an eye opening to recognize her—and inside, far down a street that could not exist, lamps were being lit in windows for someone expected home.

She went.
