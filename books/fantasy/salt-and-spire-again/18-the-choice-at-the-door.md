## 18. The Choice at the Door

The Door was smaller than Ines had imagined.

Two centuries of songs and smugglers' tales had built it into something vast—a gate of black iron tall as a cathedral, ringed in chains, breathing cold. What waited beneath the Spire instead was a seam. A crack in the world the height of a woman and half as wide, set into stone so old it had forgotten how to be rock. Frost feathered out from its edges in patterns like ferns, like feathers, like writing no one alive could read.

Nia stood before it with her small hand raised, and the frost reached for her fingers like a dog greeting its master.

Behind them, the stair groaned. Somewhere above, Vane's Wardens were pouring fire-oil down the spiral, and Brann—Brann who should have been lying flat on a cart with his ruined side stitched shut—was holding them off with a sword and a voice gone to gravel. Sefa was up there too. Ines refused to think past that fact. Sefa was up there, and she was still shouting orders, which meant she was still alive, which meant there was still time.

There was still time.

"Ines." Tomas's hand on her shoulder. His maps were spread on the stone at their feet, weighted with salt blocks, the pre-Erasure charts glowing faintly where candlelight caught the old silver ink. "The resonance is building. If she touches it—"

"I know."

"You don't." His voice cracked. He hadn't slept in three days; none of them had. "I've read the accounts of the sealing. When they closed it the first time, the shockwave killed every living thing within a league. The kingdom didn't just vanish, Ines. It was *folded*. And if Nia unfolds it wrong—"

"Then we make sure she doesn't." Ines crouched to the child's level. Nia turned, and her eyes were wrong again—not the dark, wondering eyes of a six-year-old girl who liked honey cakes and feared thunder, but something older looking out through them, patient as sediment. Two hundred years of watching from behind a child's face.

"Nia," Ines said gently. "Tell me what you see."

"The door remembers me," Nia said. Her common speech had come fully back over the last weeks, but now it slid sideways into Vessarine, both languages braided together, and Ines understood both, which frightened her more than anything else that day. "It has been waiting so long. It is *tired*, Ines. It wants to be finished."

"Finished how?"

Nia's small brow furrowed. "Opened. Or closed forever. Those are the only endings it knows."

*Those are the only endings it knows.* But Ines had read her mentor's journal cover to cover, had decoded the cipher in the margins twice more on the road, and Maera Voss had believed otherwise. Maera Voss, who had died on this mountain with frostbite-blackened fingers still clutching her notes, had written one sentence underlined three times:

*The Door does not need to open or close. It needs an anchor. A will strong enough to hold the seam from inside.*

And below that, in smaller script: *God forgive me for even writing this down.*

Ines rose. Behind her, the stair erupted—shouting, the wet roar of burning oil, then silence, then Brann's voice bellowing something wordless and defiant. Then a scream that was not Brann's.

Sefa's.

"No," Ines breathed. She took one step toward the stair and stopped herself with sheer physical effort, nails biting into her palms. *Not yet. Not until it's done. She'd flay you alive for abandoning this.* The matriarch's voice in her head, sharp as a whip-crack: *You finish the work, girl. Grief is for after. Grief is always for after.*

Tomas caught her arm anyway, steadying her, his own face gray. "She bought us the minutes. Don't waste them."

Ines turned back to the Door. To Nia.

"Listen to me," she said, kneeling again, taking the child's cold hands in hers. "Before you choose. There's something I have to tell you, and I need you to hear all of it."

Nia tilted her head. Waiting. She was very good at waiting. She'd had two hundred years of practice.

"When you open the Door, Vessar comes back. The streets, the towers, the people frozen mid-song—all of it, unfolded, alive again. That's true. But something else comes back too." Ines kept her voice steady. "The thing wearing the king's face. The reason your ancestors sealed everything in the first place. It's been down here in the dark with you, Nia. All this time. And the moment the seam opens wide enough for a kingdom, it opens wide enough for *that*."

Something flickered across the child's ancient eyes. Fear. Real fear, and suddenly she looked six years old again, barefoot in the snow outside a guildhouse door.

"It knocks sometimes," Nia whispered. "At night. It pretends to be my mother."

Ines's heart broke cleanly, like ice off a roofline. "I know, sweetheart. I know. And if you open the Door, it stops pretending."

"And if I close it forever—" Nia's chin trembled. "Vessar dies. Everyone in it. The baker who used to wave at me. The singing women. They die *again*, and this time no one remembers them at all. No maps. No songs. Nothing." Tears froze on her lashes. "I don't want to be the one who kills my whole country, Ines. I don't want to be the last thing that ever happens to it."

"I know." Ines pulled her close. The child was cold as riverbed stone. Over Nia's shoulder, Ines met Tomas's eyes, and saw her own despair reflected there. Open the Door and unleash the devourer. Close it and bury a nation alive. Two choices, both monstrous, and a six-year-old holding the knife.

That was when the footsteps came down the stair.

Heavy. Deliberate. Unhurried—the walk of a man who had decided he owned whatever he was walking toward. Ines was on her feet with her knife out before her mind caught up, pulling Nia behind her, but she knew the tread already. Every guide on the Salt Road knew it by now.

Aldric Vane came around the spiral's last turn with soot on his jaw and blood—not his own—on his gauntlets. His Wardens fanned out behind him, crossbows leveled, and behind Ines she heard Tomas step forward to put himself between the bolts and the child.

Vane didn't look at any of them. He looked at the Door.

For a long moment the Warden-Captain simply stood there, firelight from the torches guttering across his face, and Ines watched something happen in him that she had no name for. He stared at the seam in the world the way a man stares at a grave he dug himself.

"My brother stood there," Vane said quietly. "Twenty-two years ago. We were told it was a survey mission. Seismic instability, they said. The Crown wanted measurements." A short, terrible laugh. "He touched the edge. Just the edge. And it—" His jaw worked. "It didn't take him all at once. It took him the way water takes paper. Slowly. From the outside in. I held his hand and he was *there*, and then he was less there, and then he was looking through me at something behind me, and he said—" Vane stopped. Closed his eyes. "He said, *Aldric, it's so hungry.*"

No one moved. Even the crossbows wavered.

"So yes," Vane said, opening his eyes, and now he did look at Ines, and his gaze was not cruel—it was worse than cruel, it was *reasonable*. "I want this Door destroyed. Burned, collapsed, buried under a thousand tons of mountain. Not because I hate your guild or your smuggler roads or your little kingdom of ghosts. Because I have seen what lives at that threshold, and I will burn every village on this mountain before I let it take anyone else's brother." He extended one gauntleted hand toward Nia. "Come away from it, child. You don't owe these people your life. You don't owe a dead kingdom anything. Come with me, and you'll have a name, a home, a future. That's more than Vessar can offer you. Vessar can only offer you a choice between two kinds of dying."

"And you?" Ines stepped forward, keeping herself between his hand and the girl. "What do you offer yourself, Captain? Fire-oil and rubble? You collapse this chamber and the thing behind the seam doesn't die. It's not *in* the room, Vane. It's in the *seam*. In the space between. You can't burn a gap in the world."

"Then I'll spend my life trying."

"That's not duty. That's just grief with a uniform on."

His face went white, then dark. For a heartbeat she thought he'd strike her—or order the crossbows loosed—and she braced for it, arms spread, a wall of one woman and nothing else. But Vane only breathed, long and slow, the way men breathe when they're holding themselves together with discipline alone.

"You sound like him," he said finally. "My brother. He was always telling me the difference between duty and stubbornness." His hand dropped. "Give me another option, guide. You're famous for finding roads that don't exist. Find me one now."

And there it was. The opening she'd been praying for and dreading in equal measure.

Ines reached into her coat and drew out Maera Voss's journal. The leather was cracked, the pages soft as cloth from handling. She found the page—she knew exactly where it was, had dreamed of it for six nights running—and read aloud, her voice steady because it had to be, because Nia was listening, because Vane was listening, because the words were terrible and they were the only ones left.

"*The Door does not require opening or closing,*" she read. "*It requires holding. A seal is a wall, and walls fail. An anchor is a will, and a will endures as long as the one who bears it. Bind a living soul to the threshold—not as sacrifice, but as keeper—and the seam holds from within. The devourer cannot pass an anchored door. The kingdom cannot unfold. Both are preserved. Both are imprisoned. The anchor gives up the world in exchange for keeping it.*"

Silence. The frost on the Door's edges crept a finger's width further out.

"There's more," Tomas said hoarsely. He'd read the journal too; she saw him remembering. "Ines. The rest of the entry. Maera wrote that the binding is *permanent*. That the anchor exists between—neither inside nor outside. That no one who tried it ever came back, because there was no record of anyone ever trying. It's a theory. A dead woman's desperate theory."

"It's a third option," Ines said. "It's the only one where nobody dies and nobody's country gets murdered. It's the one where Nia grows up free, and Vessar stays sleeping but not dead, and the thing in the seam stays hungry behind a door that never opens." She looked at Vane. "Better than fire-oil?"

Vane stared at her. "You're talking about yourself."

"I'm talking about whoever stands there. And I'm standing there."

"No." Tomas grabbed her wrist. "Absolutely not. Ines—there has to be another way, we find another way, we—"

"Tomas." She covered his hand with hers. "You erased a kingdom off the maps because your family was ordered to, and you've spent your whole life trying to put it back. This is how it goes back. Not unfolded. *Kept*. Someone keeps it. Someone remembers the baker and the singing women, walks their streets, holds the door against the hungry thing. That's not dying. That's the guild's whole purpose, done properly for once." She smiled, and it hurt. "Last apprentice of the Salt Road Guides' Guild. Turns out I'm the last guide of a whole country."

"You're twenty-six years old."

"I'm older than that where it counts."

Nia tugged at her coat. Ines looked down. The child's face was wet, and her ancient eyes were gone entirely—just a little girl now, terrified, understanding far too much.

"If you go in there," Nia whispered, "who brings me salt?"

Ines laughed, or sobbed, or both. She knelt and gathered the child in, pressing her lips to the crown of her head, breathing in woodsmoke and snow and the strange deep cold of Vessarine magic.

"Every spring," she said into Nia's hair. "You bring it to me. An anchor needs anchoring too. That's the deal, little heir. You live your whole loud life out there, and every thaw you come to this door and tell me about it. Every song. Every caravan. Every friend you make. You keep me, and I keep your kingdom. That's the trade."

"That's a bad trade," Nia said, crying. "You're worth more than a whole country."

"Don't let the elders hear you say that."

Above them, the stair erupted again—shouting, steel, a Warden's cry cut short—and then a sound that stopped Ines's heart: Brann's voice, roaring her name down the spiral. *"INES! They're through! MOVE!"*

Vane straightened. Something had settled in his face, some final arithmetic completed. He looked at the Door, at his brother's grave-that-wasn't-a-grave, at the child, at Ines.

"How long do we have?" he asked.

"Minutes. Maybe less once the binding starts."

"Then we do it now." He turned and barked orders in the Warden tongue, and his soldiers—her enemies of a season, her persecutors, the closing fist of the Crown—lowered their crossbows and formed a ring facing the stair. Facing upward. Holding the line for her. "Wardens! Nothing comes down these steps that isn't invited!" He looked back at Ines one last time. "Tell your dead mentor she was smarter than my entire academy. And tell my brother—" His voice failed, recovered. "Tell him I finally understood what he was trying to say."

Ines walked to the Door.

Up close, the seam hummed, a note below hearing, felt in the teeth and the breastbone. The frost patterns writhed. And beneath it all, faintly, horribly, something on the other side pressed its face against the gap—as she had known it would—and she felt it notice her. Feel her measuring her. Tasting the shape of her soul through a crack two fingers wide.

*Little keeper,* it said, in a voice like her mother's, like Maera's, like Sefa's, like everyone she had ever loved. *Little morsel. Come hold the door. Hold it forever. Hold it until you forget why.*

"I'm not coming to feed you," Ines said aloud. "I'm coming to starve you."

She took Nia's hand one last time and pressed it flat against the Door's cold surface, beside her own.

"Nia Oyelaran-Vessarine," she said—giving the child her own name, because heirs needed houses and the girl had none, and Ines's was the only one on offer. "When I say *now*, you pull your hand back and you run to Tomas and you don't look back. Whatever you hear. Whatever I say. You run, and you live, and you come back every spring with salt. Promise me."

"I promise," Nia whispered. "Ines—I'm scared."

"Good. Scared means you plan to survive." Ines filled her lungs. Looked back once—at Tomas weeping openly into his charts, at Vane standing rigid as a spire himself, at the stair where firelight and steel flashed and Brann's silhouette held against the dark.

"Now," she said, and pushed her hand *through*.

The world folded.

Cold took her—not the cold of winter but the cold of *between*, the cold of a place that had been waiting two hundred years for someone to fill it. She felt the binding seize her, thread by thread, her name unraveling out of the world's ledger, her weight lifting off the stone, Nia screaming somewhere far away, Vane shouting an order, the devourer laughing—

And Ines Oyelaran, last apprentice of the Salt Road Guides' Guild, planted herself in the threshold like a salt post driven into mountain stone, and *held*.

The last thing she heard from the world she was leaving was Nia's voice, thin and furious and alive, calling a promise in perfect Vessarine:

*"Every spring! I swear it! Every spring, with salt!"*

The Door sealed around her like a hand closing in prayer.

And on the far side of the seam, in the dark, the hungry thing began to speak—but Ines was already turning to face it, and she had two hundred years of a country's memories to arm herself with, and she had made a promise of her own.

She was going to need every one of them.
