## 21. Spring Thaw

The snow on the Spire road broke first where the sun touched it, in long dark veins that ran down the mountain like the land itself waking. Nia counted them from the back of Marta's cart, one for each day they'd climbed, and by the time the valley fell away behind them she had lost count and stopped caring.

Tomas drove. He always drove now, hands steady on the reins, though his maps were long since burned or buried and there was nothing left to chart but the road everyone knew. The village had given them salt—two full sacks, coarse and grey-white, packed in oiled cloth—and a jar of honey, and a bundle of dried lavender that old Yeva swore kept bad dreams out of bedrolls. Nia carried the lavender herself, wrapped against her chest like something newborn.

"You don't have to talk to her," Tomas said, without looking back. "You know that. You can just sit by the door."

"I know."

"And you don't have to bring the salt every year. It's not a toll. It's not a debt. Sefa would've said—"

"Sefa's dead." Nia heard how it came out of her, flat and hard as river ice, and saw Tomas's shoulders tighten under his coat. She made herself softer. "I know what Sefa would say. She'd say an anchor needs anchoring too. She's the one who told me first."

Tomas was quiet for a while. The cart creaked. Somewhere below them a meltwater stream was finding its voice after months of silence, running loud and bright over stones.

"She'd be proud of you," he said finally. "Both of you. The way you two do this. I couldn't do it. I'd stand at that door and I'd weep like a child and I'd never come back down the mountain, and we both know it."

"That's why you drive the cart," Nia said.

That got a laugh out of him, rough and surprised, the kind he still seemed startled to find in himself. Two years ago he'd been a drunk sleeping off royal commissions in a ditch outside Thornfell. Now his hands were steady and his face had lines around the eyes from squinting at real horizons instead of drowned ones, and when people in the valley needed a boundary walked or a claim disputed, they came to him, because Tomas could read land the way priests read scripture.

The guildhouse had taught him that. What was left of it.

* * *

They reached the last toll house at dusk—the same one Ines had guided salt caravans through, once, before any of them knew what the world was actually made of. It had been rebuilt twice since the Long Winter. The Crown's Wardens had helped with the second rebuilding, which would have been unthinkable before, and which Brann would have found either hilarious or obscene, depending on which version of Brann you'd known.

Nia thought he'd have found it funny. Brann had died holding a stairway so a child could live free, and she refused to believe a man like that spent his last breaths on anything but a joke.

The toll keeper was a valley woman named Petra who had lost two sons to the winter and gained, somehow, an unshakable cheerfulness that Nia had never decided whether to admire or distrust. She fed them barley stew and asked about the roads south, and Tomas answered, and Nia sat by the fire turning her salt pouch over in her hands.

"Third year," Petra said. "You going up again."

"Every year."

"The Warden patrols used to stop anyone going up there. Captain Vane's orders, back when." Petra poked the fire. "He doesn't anymore. You know that? Since he burned his commission. There's no order against it. There's just the mountain."

"There's just the mountain," Nia agreed.

But that wasn't quite true, and she knew it, and lying—even small lies, even kind ones—sat differently in her mouth than it used to. There wasn't just the mountain. There was the Door, and behind the Door there was Ines, and inside Ines there was a war being fought so quietly that the whole world had already begun to forget it had ever been won.

She was twelve now. Old enough to remember everything clearly: the shrine where she'd spoken her first full sentences in two hundred years, the taste of terror that had been her only companion across centuries sealed in a child's body, the moment at the threshold when Ines Oyelaran had knelt down to her eye level and said, *I have an idea, little queen, and you're going to hate it.*

She hadn't hated it. That was the terrible thing. She had understood it instantly, the way you understand a lullaby, and she had let Ines take her place, and she had lived.

Every spring she climbed the mountain to say thank you. Every spring she wondered if thank you was a cruelty.

* * *

The Spire stood above the clouds the way it always had, a grey finger pointing at nothing, its lower courses furred with lichen and its upper reaches lost in weather. Vessar's last visible tower. Everything else of the kingdom was behind the Door beneath it—in the keeping of a guide who had traded her freedom for a child's future and never once, in three years, complained about the exchange rate.

The approach was clear of snow. That was new this year, and wrong. Nia noticed it while they were still a quarter mile out: a perfect circle of bare stone around the Spire's base, as if winter itself had been politely asked to wait elsewhere.

Tomas noticed too. He reined in the cart and looked at her, and she looked back, and neither of them said anything for a moment.

"It did that before," Nia said. "In the first year. When the Door was settling."

"It didn't do it last year."

"No."

"Well." Tomas climbed down and began unhitching the mule with more care than the task required. "Then let's not keep her waiting."

The Door was exactly where it had always been, in the deep vault beneath the Spire's root—a slab of black stone set into the living rock, seamless except for one thing. A seam. A hairline crack of pale light that ran from top to bottom, thin as a knife's edge, glowing faintly like dawn through a shuttered window.

It had been thinner, last year. Nia was almost sure it had been thinner.

She knelt in front of it and poured the salt out of its cloth onto the stone floor, spreading it in a line along the base of the Door, the way Ines had shown her in dreams during that first terrible winter—not dreams exactly, more like warmth at the edge of sleep, a presence saying *this is how, this is how, remember this is how.* Salt for the anchor. An anchor needs anchoring too.

Then she pressed her palm flat against the black stone.

It was warm. It was always warm, warmer than rock had any right to be, warm like skin, and beneath the warmth there was a pulse, slow and vast, like something enormous breathing in its sleep.

"Ines?" she said. "It's me. It's thaw. I brought salt."

For a long moment, nothing. The pulse went on beneath her hand. Then—

"Nia."

The voice came through the seam, thin as the light, worn as an old rope. But it was hers. It would always be hers. Nia felt her eyes sting and blinked hard, because Ines had told her once, in that first dream-warmth: *Don't cry at the door. It makes the stone damp and I can feel it, and then I get sentimental, and sentiment is a luxury I can't afford in here.*

"How are you?" Nia asked. "Really. Don't do the thing where you say fine."

A pause. A breath of laughter through the seam, dry as salt wind.

"You know me too well. I'm... holding. The country's quiet this year. Quieter than usual. The streets are—" Another pause, longer. "The streets are beautiful, Nia. Spring comes here too, in its way. The fountains run. Someone keeps planting flowers in the market square and I've never caught who does it."

"You could ask them."

"They don't talk to me much. The people here. They're not quite—they're memories wearing bodies, mostly. Kind ones. But there's one, an old man by the eastern gate, who bows to me every morning. Every single morning, three years. I've started bowing back."

Nia laughed, and the laugh cracked in the middle, and she let it.

"Ines. The snow. Around the Spire—it melted. All of it. In a circle. Is that you?"

Silence. Longer this time. When the voice came again it was smaller.

"Maybe."

"Ines."

"The devourer stirs sometimes," Ines said, very quietly. "It's supposed to. That's the design—I hold the Door, and when it pushes, I push back, and the pushing takes... shape. Heat, usually. This year it pushed harder than usual. I'm telling you because you'll notice anyway, you notice everything, you're your mentor's apprentice if you're nothing else. And I'm telling you because Sefa made me promise never to lie to you about the big things."

"What about the small things?"

"I lie about the small things constantly. Your hair looks fine. The stew in the valley is excellent. That sort of thing."

Nia pressed her forehead against the stone. Warm. Breathing.

"Come through," she whispered. "Just for a minute. Just to see the flowers."

"Nia—"

"Just for a minute. You could come through and look at the sky and go back. One minute. Nobody would even—"

"Little queen." The voice was gentle and absolutely immovable, the voice of someone who had spent three years becoming a hinge and knew exactly what hinges were for. "If I open this door one inch, everything behind me leans on that inch. Including the thing wearing the king's face. I made my trade. I'd make it again. Don't make me spend our minutes arguing."

Nia breathed. Counted, the way Yeva had taught her, four in and seven held. When she spoke again her voice was level.

"Then tell me what to watch for. If the pushing gets worse. Tell me what it looks like from out here, so I can be your eyes. You gave me your place. Let me give you something back."

Another silence—but a different quality of silence, the silence of someone recalculating.

"...The cold," Ines said at last. "When it pushes hard, cold leaks. Not natural cold—cold that arrives out of season and stays. If the passes freeze in high summer, if the rivers lock in June—if you see that, you climb this mountain immediately, and you don't come alone. Understood?"

"Understood."

"And Nia. One more thing." The voice dropped lower, and Nia leaned closer to the seam until the pale light lay across her cheek like a blade of morning. "There's writing on the inside of the Door. Old Vessarine, royal script. I've spent three years learning to read it upside down, which is exactly as miserable as it sounds. Most of it's the sealing litany. But there's a line near the bottom that isn't in any text Sefa ever showed me, and I think—I think it might be a warning, or an instruction, or possibly a grocery list, my grammar is still imperfect. I need you to copy it down. From memory. You wrote it, Nia. Two hundred years ago. Before you were chosen. Maybe you wrote it without knowing why."

Nia went very still.

"I don't remember writing anything."

"You remember the sealing. You remember being chosen. Dig deeper. There's a reason they chose a child who remembered—children forget, that's their whole genius, but you were meant to carry it forward. So somewhere in that stubborn old soul of yours is a line of script, and I need it."

Nia closed her eyes. Behind them, further back than memory should reach, something stirred—shapes, half-lit, a candlelit room and ink and her own small hand moving and moving—

"I'll try," she said.

"Good girl. Bad apprentice—you still haven't finished the salt properly, there's a gap near the left corner, honestly, three years and no discipline—"

Nia laughed through tears and crawled along the Door's base, patching the salt line, and Ines supervised, and for a few minutes it was almost ordinary, almost like a lesson in a guildhouse that smelled of woodsmoke and lamplight, almost like the world before.

When it was time to go, Nia put her hand on the stone one last time.

"Next thaw," she said.

"Next thaw," Ines answered. "Bring honey. The memories here have no bees."

* * *

Tomas was waiting outside with the mule, and he read her face the way he read all landscapes, quickly and completely.

"Heavier this year," he said. Not a question.

"Heavier this year." She climbed onto the cart. "Tomas. We need to go back through the valley and talk to the elders. All of them. And then we need to send word—to the other villages, the pass settlements, everyone the guild ever kept a ledger on."

"Why?"

"Because the guild isn't dead." She said it and heard, with a strange double hearing, both her own voice and the echo of Sefa's, and of Ines's, and of every matriarch back through the centuries who had kept a secret so heavy it bent them. "It was never dead. It was just small. And now it needs to be a guild again—guides, wardens of the roads, watchers of the seasons. Because Ines told me what to watch for, and I'm going to watch, and when the cold comes early—and it will come early—we're going to be ready, and we're going to climb this mountain together, and we are going to read that line on the Door."

Tomas looked at her for a long moment. Then he turned the cart downhill, toward the valley lights coming on one by one in the blue dusk.

"You sound like her," he said. "You know that. You sound exactly like her."

"I know," Nia said, and watched the Spire shrink behind them into the evening, one grey finger and one hairline of light, a kingdom and its keeper, both waiting for spring.
