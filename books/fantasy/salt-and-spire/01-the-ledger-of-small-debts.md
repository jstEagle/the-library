## 1. The Ledger of Small Debts

The ink was running low again, and so, Jonah Hartwell suspected, was the city.

They dipped the pen anyway. In the counting-house of the Guild of Impossible Things, you learned to make do. You learned that a half-empty inkpot and a half-starved city were both just problems of arithmetic—something could be stretched, something could be borrowed, and eventually someone would come to collect.

Entry four hundred and eleven. *One dream of flight, secondhand, sold to the widow Merren for three loaves and a promise.* Jonah wrote it in the trade column, then hesitated over the debt column. The widow had promised her late husband's name, which she no longer used, which meant the guild had taken payment in something that didn't exist anymore. That was either very clever or very sad, and Jonah had stopped being able to tell the difference around entry two hundred.

Outside the shuttered windows, the usurper's siege guns spoke their slow sentence against Vell's eastern wall. Boom. A pause long enough to pray in. Boom. The whole city had learned the rhythm the way you learn a heartbeat—not your own, but one you're trapped inside.

"Those ledgers won't balance themselves," said Master Corvane from the doorway, though they had been balancing themselves all morning under Jonah's pen, which was rather the point of Jonah.

He stood there in his grey guild robes, silver chain of office catching what little light the shutters allowed. Corvane had the kind of face that looked like it had been assembled by committee: nothing wrong with it, nothing memorable, everything agreed upon in advance. Forty years an elder of the guild, and he still talked about impossible things the way a tax collector talks about grain.

"They're balanced," Jonah said. "Mostly."

"Mostly." Corvane crossed the room and looked down at the open ledger without touching it. He never touched the ledgers. That was beneath him, apparently, though not beneath the youngest and least-valued member of the most feared guild in the free cities. "You write these as if they matter."

"They're trades. Trades matter."

"Trades matter when they're large." He tapped the air above entry four hundred and eleven, tapping nothing, touching nothing. "A secondhand dream. A borrowed shadow. Do you know what we are, Hartwell? We are bookkeepers. The magic is in the founding, in the charter, in the names on the spire door. Everything since is clerical work. You do the clerical work."

Jonah kept their eyes on the page. *One memory of rain, sold twice, both buyers satisfied.* They wrote it small and neat, because neatness was the only rebellion available at their pay grade. "Someone has to know what we owe."

"We owe nothing." Corvane said it the way other men said *the sun rises*. "That is the entire genius of the founder's design. The guild takes. It does not owe. Write your little numbers if it keeps you busy, but don't mistake them for weight."

He left. The door closed with the soft, expensive sound of good oak, and Jonah sat alone with the ledgers and the guns and the slow arithmetic of a dying city, and felt the old resentment settle into its usual seat behind their ribs. Quiet resentment. Patient resentment. The kind you feed on scraps and tell yourself is fine.

*We owe nothing.*

Then why, Jonah wondered, did the guild keep debt columns at all?

* * *

The courier came at dusk, between the fourth gun and the fifth.

Jonah heard him before they saw him—a wet, dragging sound in the outer hall, like a sack being pulled across stone. The counting-house porter had deserted two weeks ago, along with half the wall garrison, so there was no one to stop the man as he came through the door sideways, one shoulder leading, the rest of him following like an afterthought.

He was young. That was the first thing. Younger than Jonah, maybe twenty, with road dust ground so deep into his skin he looked carved from it, and a dark stain spreading down his left side that had soaked through cloak, coat, and whatever hope he'd started the journey with.

"You're the guild," he said. Not a question. His voice had gravel in it and blood underneath.

"I'm the ledger-keeper," Jonah said, rising. "If you have a trade to make—"

"I have a delivery." He took another step and his leg folded under him like wet paper. Jonah caught him—caught all of him, more weight than expected, the terrible looseness of a body already halfway to being cargo—and lowered him to the floor among the dust and the dropped quills.

Up close, the wound was bad. Worse than bad. Jonah had seen the siege wounded carried past the counting-house every evening; they made sounds, they begged, they held on. This man wasn't doing any of those things. He was simply leaving, steadily, the way water leaves a cracked cup.

"The map," he said.

"What map?"

His hand found Jonah's wrist with shocking strength. His fingers were cold and crusted with salt—actual salt, white crystals worked into the creases of his skin, in his hair, in the seams of his gloves, as if he'd swum the whole way here through some dead sea. He pressed something into Jonah's hands and folded their fingers closed over it.

It was stiff and crackling, oilcloth wrapped around something flat, and it was stained through with the same white salt, blooming across the fabric like frost.

"Salt-stained," the courier whispered, and laughed, or coughed, or both. "Of course it is. She always said it would be. Listen—" He pulled Jonah closer, close enough that Jonah could smell the blood and the brine on his breath. "Listen. The court beneath the mountain. There's a debt. An old one. A soul, pawned, still held. Still *held*—do you understand? It was never redeemed. It was never even *claimed*."

"I don't understand," Jonah said, which was true, and also not true, because some cold thing in their stomach had begun to understand perfectly.

"It's bound by bloodline." His eyes were fixed on Jonah's face with an urgency that had gone past fear into something almost gentle. "Bound to the line of Hartwell. Bound to—" His gaze flicked down to Jonah's hands, closed around the package, and something in his expression broke open. Relief, maybe. Or pity. "Oh. Oh, you don't know. You genuinely don't know what you are."

"What am I?" Jonah's voice came out smaller than intended.

"Collateral," he said. "You're the collateral. Four generations of collateral. And the brokers have finally found the ledger-page." He fumbled at his coat, brought out a folded paper, dark with blood, and pushed it after the map. "Names. Dates. The original contract's location. It's all—the founder sold it, the founder *sold*—"

The guns spoke again, closer this time, and dust sifted down from the rafters like grey snow.

"Who sent you?" Jonah asked. "Who's *she*?"

But the courier's eyes had drifted to the ceiling, or through it, and his grip on Jonah's wrist loosened by degrees, and then his hand simply fell, and the strange tension went out of him all at once, and Jonah was kneeling on the floor of the counting-house holding a dead man's message and a dead man's silence.

* * *

They should have called for help. That was the procedure. Death in the counting-house was to be reported to the elders, logged, entered in the mortality rolls, the body handled according to charter. Jonah knew the procedure. Jonah had written the procedure into the manual two winters ago, in their neat clerk's hand, because no one else could be bothered.

Instead Jonah knelt there for a long time, listening to the guns, holding the oilcloth package, feeling its weight like a stone in a river current.

Then they heard footsteps in the corridor. More than one set. Measured, unhurried, the footsteps of people who owned the building they were walking through.

"—found the horse at the Salt Gate," a voice was saying, low and professional. "Saddle branded for the Ondrel run. If the courier reached the city, he reached the guild."

"And the guild will cooperate," said a second voice, smoother, with the particular silkiness Jonah had learned to associate with the usurper's officials. "Free cities always cooperate, once the charter is explained to them. Fetch the elder. What was his name—Corvane."

Jonah moved without deciding to move. The oilcloth went inside their coat, against their chest, where it lay cold and heavy as a second heart. The bloodstained paper followed. Then Jonah stepped back into the shadows behind the ledger shelves, pulling a fallen drape half off its rail to cover the courier's body—or hide it, or pretend it hadn't happened—and stood in the dark between entries four hundred and eleven and four hundred and twelve, barely breathing.

The door opened.

Three figures entered. Two wore the grey-and-crimson of the usurper's service, and the third wore grey guild robes with a silver chain, and was Master Corvane, bowing.

Bowing. To them. Jonah watched, from the dark, as the youngest and least valued member of the Guild of Impossible Things watched the most senior elder of that guild bend his committee-assembled face into a smile and say, "Honored sirs. The Guild of Impossible Things extends every courtesy to the rightful authority of the usurper. How may our records serve you?"

*A courier may have entered the city,* said the silky voice. *He carried something belonging to the court beneath the mountain. A document. Your cooperation will be remembered when the charter is renewed.*

"You'll have every document we possess," Corvane said. "Every ledger. Every record. The guild holds nothing back from the new order."

*No,* said the silky voice, and there was something in it now, something hungry and amused. *Not every ledger. Only one. A debt outstanding. A soul, pawned, unredeemed. Bound by bloodline to a family called Hartwell.*

The pause that followed lasted three heartbeats. Jonah counted them, because counting was what they did, because the numbers were the only solid thing left in the world.

Then Corvane said, "There is no one of that name in the guild's employ."

And the silky voice laughed softly, and said, "Master Corvane. We saw him arrive. We have been watching this house since before your courier drew breath. Someone in this room is holding our property."

The lamps guttered. Dust fell. In the dark behind the shelves, Jonah Hartwell stood very still with a dead man's map pressed against their chest, and understood—with the perfect, vertiginous clarity of a figure in a ledger realizing it has been read—that the quiet resentment of a lifetime had curdled, all at once, into fear.

Somewhere beneath the mountain, something ancient had opened a page and found their name written in it.

And the men in the room were about to start turning over shelves.
