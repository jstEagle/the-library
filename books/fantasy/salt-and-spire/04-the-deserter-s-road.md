## 4. The Deserter's Road

Wren counted her father's death in days now, the way she'd once counted coins. Four since they'd fled Vell. Four since she'd watched a stranger with salt-stained hands carry the map her father died delivering, and decided—against every instinct that said walk away—that following him was better than following grief alone.

The grief was still there. It just had company now.

She walked at the rear of their small, ragged procession: Jonah Hartwell in front with the map wrapped in oilcloth against their chest like a second heart; behind them, Sable Ives, the disgraced archivist, who complained about her boots with the tireless precision of a woman cataloguing a collection. The road east out of Vell was a rutted scar through wheat fields gone unharvested, and the war sat on it like weather.

"Smoke," Sable said, for the third time that morning.

"It's a farmstead," Jonah said. "Farmsteads burn bread."

"Farmsteads don't burn *barns*."

Wren didn't say anything. She'd grown up reading smoke the way other children read letters—her father had carried news through three wars, and he'd taught her that smoke told you who'd won before any rider did. This smoke was fat and black and wrong. It meant soldiers who weren't in a hurry, which meant soldiers who felt safe, which meant the usurper's line ran closer than the refugees on the road believed.

"We should cut north," she said. "Through the hills."

"The hills are where the brokers' pickets sit," Jonah said. "Sable's contact in Ondrel said—"

"Sable's contact in Ondrel is selling you optimism at market rates." Wren adjusted the strap of her father's courier satchel across her chest. "I've been tracking this trade for six years. The brokers don't put pickets in the hills because they don't need to. They let people run for the hills. Hills are where things get quiet, and quiet is where they collect."

Jonah glanced back at her. There it was again—the look they kept giving her, half gratitude, half guilt, as if her father's death were a debt Jonah intended to repay and didn't know the currency. Wren was starting to suspect there wasn't one. That was the thing about being spent: no one ever paid you back. You just learned to stop waiting.

They cut north anyway, because Jonah was stubborn and Sable's boots were blisters now, and by late afternoon they stood at the mouth of a valley that smelled of pine and old iron, and Wren felt the particular prickle at her neck that six years of hunting soul-brokers had trained into her like a hound's sense for thunder.

"Stop," she said.

They stopped. The valley was empty. That was the problem. No birdsong, no goats, no woodsmoke from the shepherd hut rotting on the slope—a valley on a trade road should have had someone in it, even in wartime. Empty valleys meant someone had made them empty.

Then the voice came from the rocks above, low and unhurried:

"Drop the satchel and the map, and nobody gets appraised."

* * *

He came down the hillside the way water comes down—a deserter's descent, all angles chosen for cover, a soldier's rifle slung backward so the sun wouldn't flash on the barrel. He was maybe thirty, hollow-cheeked, with the gray uniform of the usurper's Seventh stripped of its insignia, the buttons torn off in a way that looked practiced. He held a knife instead of the rifle, which told Wren he was saving bullets for people who deserved them.

"I said drop it," he repeated, when Jonah only stared.

"You said *appraised*," Jonah said. "That's broker talk. You're not a broker. Brokers travel with ledgers and good boots."

Something flickered across the man's face—surprise, then something almost like respect. "You know the trade."

"I keep accounts," Jonah said. "It's my whole personality."

The man barked a laugh before he could stop himself, and the laugh seemed to cost him, like pulling a splinter. He lowered the knife an inch. "Tam," he said. "Seventh Irregulars, formerly. Eleven months gone."

"Deserters get shot," Sable said flatly. "Or worse. The usurper's quartermasters sell deserters' souls to fund the campaign. I've read the requisitions."

"I know what happens." Tam's voice went quiet and even in a way that made Wren's skin tighten. "I carried the crates. Do you know what a soul crate weighs? Nothing. That's the horror of it. Forty years of a man, and the crate weighs nothing, and the porter complains about his back anyway because the crates are stacked forty high and none of them weigh anything at all." His eyes moved over the three of them, cataloguing. "You're heading east. Toward Ondrel. Which means you're either fools or you're carrying something worth the road."

"And if we are?" Wren asked.

"Then you need someone who knows the passes, because the direct road has a toll station now, and the toll isn't coin." He sheathed the knife, slowly, showing his empty hands the way you'd show a frightened animal. "I'm offering passage. Not charity. Passage."

"In exchange for?" Jonah asked.

"Take me past the war. All the way past it. There's coast beyond Ondrel, ships, places where the brokers' charters run out. I get you through the mountains; when we reach saltwater, we're done with each other." He shrugged. "And in the meantime, you feed me, because I've eaten pine bark for two weeks and it tastes exactly as bad as it sounds."

"Why help us?" Wren said. "You don't know us. You don't know what we're carrying."

"I know what the toll stations do," Tam said. "I stood at one for six months and watched farmers come through with their families and leave with their families one soul lighter—the child's, usually, because children's souls fetch clean prices, no stains, no debts attached." He looked directly at Wren, and she hated how accurately he read her. "You lost someone to this. I can tell. You have the face of someone doing arithmetic about revenge."

"My father was a courier," Wren said. "The brokers killed him for a map."

"Then you understand me perfectly." Tam picked up his rifle. "I'm not asking you to trust me. Trust is for people with time. I'm asking you to make the same bargain everyone in this war makes: use me until I'm useful, and watch your backs while you do it. I'll do the same."

Silence. The valley held its breath around them.

"He knows the mountain passes," Sable said finally, in the tone of a woman filing a fact under a category she disliked. "The map leads under the mountain. We will eventually need exactly this kind of knowledge, and dead men are notoriously bad guides."

"You knew?" Jonah said. "About the mountain?"

"I deciphered the map, didn't I? I know more than I say. It's a professional habit." Sable rubbed her blistered heel and winced. "Also, I vote we take him. My feet have filed a formal petition."

Wren watched Tam while the others argued logistics. He stood the way her father used to stand after a long run—weight settled, ready to move in any direction, nothing wasted. A man who had spent eleven months being hunted learns to stand like that or stops standing altogether. And beneath the soldier's flatness, she saw the other thing, the thing he probably thought he'd buried: he wasn't running toward the coast. He was running away from those crates. From the memory of forty-high stacks of nothing weighing nothing at all.

She knew that shape of flight. She'd been wearing it for four days.

"Fine," she said. "But rules. One: you don't touch the map. Two: you don't lie to us about danger—if the road's bad, you say it plain. Three—" she stepped closer, and let him see her father's satchel, and the courier's knot she wore on the strap, the guild-mark of runners who die rather than deliver late, "—three, when this is over, you tell me everything you saw at those toll stations. Names. Faces. Everything. Someone's going to write it down someday, and I've decided it's going to be me."

Tam studied the knot for a long moment. When he spoke, some of the soldier's flatness had cracked, and underneath was just a tired man. "Your father teach you that knot?"

"He taught me everything."

"Then he raised you right." Tam shouldered his pack and turned up the valley, toward the dark teeth of the mountains. "Rule two, then, plain: the pass we want closes in nine days when the first snows come. After that, the only way under the mountain is the way the miners went, and the way the miners went is why nobody uses it anymore."

"Why not?" Jonah asked.

Tam glanced back, and in the last light his face was hard to read.

"Because the mine shafts go deep enough to reach the old courts," he said. "And the men who went down asking favors of what lives down there—" he tapped his own chest, twice, over the heart, "—came back with less inside than they left with. Some came back hollow. Some didn't come back at all."

He started walking. "Nine days," he called over his shoulder. "Keep up, keep quiet, and whatever knocks from below—we don't answer."

Wren fell into step behind him, one hand resting on her father's satchel, and did not tell the others what she had already guessed: that the thing knocking beneath the mountain had been knocking for a long time—and that somewhere in the dark ahead, it already knew her father's name.
