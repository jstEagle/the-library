## 5. The Market of Borrowed Breath

Ondrel smelled of rain on hot stone and, underneath it, something sweeter that Jonah couldn't name until they'd been inside the walls an hour. Then they could. It was the smell of a candle burning all the way down—wax and wick and the faint char of something being spent.

"Neutral ground," Tam said, pulling his hood lower. "The usurper's coin spends here. So does everyone else's. Nobody fights in Ondrel." He glanced at Wren. "Nobody's supposed to."

"And if they do?" Sable asked.

"Then they're fined," Tam said. "In advance. The city collects from both sides before the blood dries. That's the whole trick of neutrality—it's not peace. It's bookkeeping."

Jonah felt that word land somewhere under their ribs. Bookkeeping. They had been a bookkeeper for six years, balancing trades no one else in Vell would sign their name to: a memory pawned for a winter's grain, a shadow held against a debt, a promise of first laughter sold by a mother who needed medicine more than she needed joy. The Guild of Impossible Things called these entries *small debts*. Jonah had written them out in fair copperplate and told themselves the ledger was honest even when the trades were not.

Now they walked through a whole city built on the same arithmetic, and the ledger was the size of the world.

* * *

They found lodging above a dyer's shop in the Tanners' Quarter, where the vats made the air taste of iron and the landlord asked no questions because questions cost extra. Wren counted out the last of the courier's coin—the money her father had carried to his death—and it came to less than nothing. Enough for two nights. Not enough for food, passage east, or the climbing gear Tam said the mountain approaches would demand.

"So we trade," Sable said.

"We have nothing to trade," Wren said.

Sable looked at Jonah. Everyone looked at Jonah. Jonah looked down at the satchel where the salt-stained map slept against their hip like a second heartbeat.

"No," Jonah said.

"I didn't say what we'd trade," Sable said mildly. "I said we trade. There's a market under the Lantern Bridge. Everything moves through it eventually. We go, we look, we find something small—an impossible thing nobody will miss."

"Nothing in my head is small," Jonah said. "That's rather the point of me."

It was meant as a joke. It didn't come out as one.

* * *

The market under the Lantern Bridge opened at dusk, when the lamps were lit with oil that burned green and made every face below look drowned. Stalls lined both banks of the canal, and boats drifted between them, and the noise was the noise of any market anywhere—haggling, laughter, a child crying, someone singing badly for coins—except that here the goods were hung up like lanterns themselves.

Jonah saw a stall where a woman sold the sound of her own voice, one word at a time, speaking each into a glass vial while the buyer listened. She kept three words back, she told a customer, and those weren't for sale at any price. Jonah saw a man trading years off the end of his life in bundles of five, cheerful about it, spending himself like a drunkard spends wages. They passed a booth where dreams were decanted into stoppered bottles, labeled by feeling instead of content: *flying*, *falling*, *the house you grew up in*, *her*.

And then there was the long table at the canal's center, beneath the bridge's widest arch, where the brokers sat.

Jonah knew them without being told. They wore gray the way other people wore livery, and their scales were not brass but bone—small balances carved from finger-bones, or so the story went, though no one confirmed anything. A crowd stood around them in a loose half-circle, quiet in the way crowds are quiet at executions.

"They're auctioning tonight," Tam murmured. "We should go around."

But Wren had stopped walking, and Sable had stopped behind her, and Jonah understood that none of them were going around. You don't go around the thing you've come to see.

On the table lay a fragment. That was the only word for it—a piece of a person, glowing faintly, the way a coal glows after the fire's been raked over. The broker holding the balance was describing it in the flat tones of a fishmonger.

"—taken consensually, certified, from a debtor in good standing. A memory of safety. The subject recalls being held, aged four, during a storm. Complete, undamaged, single owner prior. Shall we begin at two crowns?"

Two crowns. Someone's entire childhood sense that the world would hold them, and it opened at two crowns.

A bidder took it at four. Another bought a fragment of courage from a soldier—Tam turned away at that one, jaw tight—for seven. Then a young man climbed onto the table himself, volunteering, selling a piece of his hope that he'd ever leave debt behind, and the broker weighed it, nodded, and paid him eleven crowns, and the man stepped down lighter, laughing, already counting the money, and Jonah watched the light go out of him by degrees, like watching a window darken room by room.

"That's how they get you," Tam said quietly. "First sale is always kind. The price is always fair. That's the bait. By the third sale you're not choosing anymore. You're just liquidating."

Jonah's hands had gone cold. Because here was the thing they couldn't say aloud, the thing curdling in their chest with every gavel-fall of the bone scale: *the market would price me kindly.*

They were a soul bound by contract, whole, unfragmented, provenance documented going back generations. In this place, Jonah Hartwell wasn't a person. Jonah Hartwell was a rare lot. Mint condition. Single owner prior—the founder of the Guild of Impossible Things, deceased, estate settled.

Someone in the crowd jostled them, and the map shifted against their hip, and Jonah's hand went to it without thinking, the way you touch a wound.

A broker looked up.

Not at Jonah—at the motion. Gray eyes, gray face, the stillness of a thing that weighs for a living. The broker's gaze traveled from Jonah's hand to their face, and stayed there, patient as a scale coming to rest.

"Friend," the broker called across the crowd, pleasantly. "You carry something of weight. Bring it to the table. We appraise freely."

* * *

Later, Jonah couldn't say why they went forward. Curiosity, maybe. Or the bookkeeper's sickness, the one Corvane had cultivated in them for six years: *know the value of everything, then you'll never be cheated.* Or maybe just the terror of refusing in front of a crowd, which is its own kind of currency, and the cheapest one spent.

They put the map on the table.

Not the whole way. Halfway. Their fingers still touching it, so that taking it would mean taking it *from* them, which was apparently a distinction the law of Ondrel respected.

The broker drew a lens from their coat and bent over the salt-stained parchment, and Jonah watched the gray face change—not much, a flicker, a hairline crack in stone—and knew in that instant that the broker knew exactly what this was.

"Old work," the broker said. "Mountain court. Pre-Usurpation." The lens clicked shut. "This isn't merchandise. This is a key."

"It's a map," Jonah said.

"It's a claim," the broker said, "on an asset held in trust beneath the Spire. And claims—" the gray smile widened by a degree, "—are transferable. Do you know what your friend here is carrying, ladies and gentlemen?" The broker raised their voice, and the crowd leaned in, and Jonah's stomach dropped through the floor of the world. "An instrument of recovery. One of perhaps three in existence. I'd open the bidding at—"

"No," Jonah said.

The word came out smaller than they wanted. They planted their hand flat on the map.

"It's not mine to sell," Jonah said, louder now. "That's the whole point of it. It's *mine*. Me. I'm the asset. And I'm not for sale."

Silence under the bridge. Somewhere a boat knocked against a piling. The broker studied Jonah with the unhurried interest of someone reading a ledger entry, and Jonah realized, with a clarity that felt like falling, that the broker was not disappointed. The broker was *delighted*. Refusal established provenance. Refusal proved the lot was contested, and contested lots appreciate.

"Then we'll note the asset declines," the broker said, making a mark in a gray book. "For the record. Assets always come to us eventually, friend. Debt finds its level. Like water."

"Like salt," Jonah said, before they could stop themselves. "It gets everywhere and ruins everything it touches."

Something moved in the crowd—Wren's hand closing on Jonah's shoulder, drawing them back; Tam materializing on the other side, big and deliberate, a wall wearing a deserter's coat. Sable lifted the map off the table with two fingers, courteous as a surgeon, and slipped it into her own coat, and the broker let it go, because letting it go was also part of the play.

"Come find us when you're ready to be reasonable," the broker called after them, pleasant as Sunday bells. "Everyone's reasonable eventually. It's the one price that never falls."

* * *

They didn't speak until they were three streets away and the green lamplight had given way to ordinary torch-gold. Then Wren rounded on Jonah in the mouth of an alley, and her voice shook.

"You put it on the *table*."

"I know."

"You put the only thing keeping you owned instead of erased on a *broker's table*, in a city full of the usurper's spies, because a stranger asked nicely."

"I *know*." Jonah's voice cracked on the second word. They pressed their back against the alley wall and slid down it a little, not all the way, just enough to feel the stone hold some of their weight. "I keep trying to tell myself I'm different from them. From Corvane. From the founder. From the people in that ledger I write up every night, the ones who sell a winter's warmth for flour. But I stood in front of that scale and my first thought—the actual first thought, before fear, before anything—was *I wonder what I'm worth.*"

The alley was quiet. Rain started somewhere above the rooftops, ticking on tiles.

"Eleven crowns for a man's hope," Jonah said. "What do you think I'd fetch? Whole? With papers?"

No one answered, which was answer enough.

Tam crouched down to Jonah's eye level. Up close, the deserter's face was older than his voice, scarred along the jaw like a ledger line drawn crooked. "My whole unit sold fragments to buy out of a bad posting," he said. "One at a time, fair prices, all voluntary. Six months later they marched us into the Fen Campaign anyway, because men missing pieces follow orders better. I ran with most of myself still aboard. That's the only difference between me and the ones who didn't run. Not virtue. Inventory."

He stood, and offered Jonah his hand, and pulled them upright.

"So here's what we learned tonight," Tam said. "The market knows what you are. Which means the usurper's brokers will know soon, if they don't already. Which means Ondrel has a clock on it now. We move tomorrow. Tonight we plan the mountain route, and nobody—" he looked at each of them in turn, "—nobody carries the map alone again."

Sable handed it back to Jonah anyway. All four of them standing close, her hand under Jonah's, the salt-stained parchment between their palms like a shared wound.

"Custody of the guild," she said quietly. "All of us. Witnessed." Her mouth twitched, almost a smile. "Consider it the first honest entry in a new ledger."

Jonah almost laughed. Almost.

Because that was when the dyer's boy came pelting down the alley, breathless, too scared to be polite, saying that there were men at the lodging house asking after travelers from Vell—gray men, with a bone scale—and that the landlord had told them everything, because the landlord's questions had finally been answered with coin.

Behind him, at the mouth of the alley, the rain fell silver through the torchlight, and in it, figures were already moving their way.
