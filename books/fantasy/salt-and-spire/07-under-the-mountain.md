## 7. Under the Mountain

Tam had led men through worse ground than this. He'd told himself that every hour since the pass, and it was starting to sound like a prayer he didn't believe in.

The mine-shafts below the Grayfell ridge were older than the war, older than the usurper, maybe older than the free cities themselves. The first diggers had followed a vein of something that wasn't quite silver and hadn't stopped until the mountain swallowed them. Their tunnels still held the shape of their greed: low ceilings where men had crawled on their bellies, sudden chambers where they'd blasted through rock in a hurry to get rich, and everywhere the marks of picks worn smooth by centuries of damp.

"Watch the third step," Tam said, holding the lantern high. "It's a lie."

Jonah tested it with one boot and found the stone give like wet bread. They climbed past it, breathing hard, Sable's satchel of maps and instruments clinking softly against her hip.

"You've been down here before," Wren said. It wasn't a question.

"I garrisoned at Grayfell for two years before I walked." Tam kept his voice level. "Deserters don't get pick of postings. We got the shafts. Patrol work in the dark, checking for smugglers." He paused at a junction where three tunnels met like the fingers of a buried hand. "And for people going where we're going."

"And what did you do," Sable said, "when you found them?"

Tam let the lantern light swing across her face. She didn't blink. He liked that about her, in the way you can like the edge of a knife.

"There was a man once," he said. "Came up out of the deep tunnels with salt on his boots and nothing else. No pack. No lamp. Just salt, from his knees down, like he'd waded through the sea." He turned down the left-hand passage. "The captain said take him to the brokers' wagon. I said yes, sir. And then I walked him the other way, into the hills, and told him never come back."

"Why?" Jonah asked quietly.

"Because he was crying." Tam shrugged, as if the answer embarrassed him. "Men don't cry like that unless something took everything. I figured whatever was left of him deserved better than a wagon."

Nobody spoke after that. The tunnel narrowed, and they went through it single file, shoulders brushing stone slick with mineral sweat.

* * *

They came up under the siege lines near dusk, or what passed for dusk underground—Tam kept time by a water-clock trick an old sergeant had taught him, counting drips into a tin cup. Above them, muffled by forty feet of rock, the usurper's army made its small noises: hammering, shouting, the groan of siege engines being winched into place. Vell was thirty miles west and starving, and here was the machine built to finish the job.

"The shaft opens behind their supply camp," Tam explained, crouching at a collapsed grate he'd pried loose years ago and never reported. "The sentries watch the road and the treeline. Nobody watches the mountain's back door because nobody believes there is one." He looked around at the three of them, lamplight carving hollows under their eyes. "We move at shift-change. Quarter hour when the guards are trading complaints instead of watching. We cross the ravine, we're in the roots of the Spire's mountain before full dark."

"And if we're seen?" Wren asked.

"Then we're dead, and the map goes to a broker, and your father died for nothing." He said it without cruelty, just the flat arithmetic of a soldier. "So don't be seen."

Sable snorted. "Charming company you keep, Hartwell."

"He keeps me alive," Jonah said. "I've decided not to hold the rest against him."

Something loosened in Tam's chest at that—small, unwelcome, quickly smothered. He'd spent two years learning not to belong to anyone. Belonging got men killed, or worse, got them *kept*. But the archivist was already repacking her satchel, and the courier's daughter was checking the edge of her knife with her thumb, and the little ledger-keeper was looking at him with those eyes that had seen too much too fast and somehow hadn't gone dull.

Shift-change came. They moved.

The ravine crossing was ninety seconds of pure animal terror—loose scree, a drop that ate sound, the distant voices of soldiers complaining about rations drifting close enough to touch. Tam went last, as he always did, because a good guide knows his job is to be the one who can stop and turn back. When he dropped into the shadow of the far wall, his hands were shaking, and no one mentioned it, which was its own kind of mercy.

Then the mountain closed over them, and the sounds of the war faded like a tide going out.

* * *

The air changed a mile in.

Tam noticed it first—he'd been trained to notice air, because bad air killed more miners than rockfalls ever did. This wasn't bad air. It was *old* air, stale and mineral, but threaded through it was something else: salt. Not sea salt. Something drier, sharper, like the smell of tears left to crust on a face.

"You smell that?" Wren whispered.

"I smell it," Sable said grimly. "I was hoping I wouldn't."

"What is it?"

"Promises," Sable said. "Old ones. The archives have accounts of this place going back four hundred years. Every account says the same thing. You smell salt before you hear anything, and you hear nothing, ever, and then you find the door." She adjusted her lantern. "The guild founder's own journal mentions it. He wrote that the mountain 'keeps its word,' and then he burned the next six pages."

"Burned them," Jonah repeated.

"That's what I said, isn't it."

They descended through workings that grew stranger the deeper they went. The pick-marks stopped. In their place, the walls bore long parallel gouges, like something enormous had dragged its fingers through the stone—not breaking it, just scoring it, the way a bored child scores a tabletop. Then even those stopped, and the passages became smooth, round, and wrong, curving in ways that no human engineer would choose, spiraling gently downward like the inside of a shell.

"We're not in the mines anymore," Tam said. His voice sounded flattened in the round tunnel, robbed of echo. "This is older."

"How much older?" Wren asked.

"Ask the map."

Jonah unrolled it—the salt-stained courier's map, edges soft as cloth now from handling—and held it to the light. The ink had shifted since Ondrel. Tam had watched it happen over the past days: roads redrawing themselves, distances shrinking as they approached, the whole document behaving less like a record and more like a creature coming home.

"It's close," Jonah murmured. "The door. Half a mile, maybe less."

"Good," Tam said, and meant it, and hated how much he meant it. Every instinct he owned screamed to turn around. Two years of desertion had taught him exactly one lesson: when a place wants something from you, leave before it learns your name. But there was nowhere left to go that wasn't owned by someone—the usurper, the brokers, the guild elders, the past. Down was the only direction left that belonged to them.

They walked on.

* * *

They heard the bones before they saw them.

Not a rattle—nothing so cheap. A whisper, almost, the faint gritting whisper of many small things shifting when the air moved. Tam raised the lantern and the light fell into a chamber where the round tunnel opened like a throat, and the floor was not a floor.

It was a slope of bone. Ribs, femurs, skulls in their hundreds, all of them arranged—or settled—in long slow rivers flowing toward the far wall. Toward the door.

"Saints," Wren breathed.

"No saints down here," Sable said. Her voice had gone thin. "Look at them. Look at *how* they're lying."

Tam looked. He'd seen battlefields. Battlefields were chaos—limbs flung, bodies stacked by men with shovels, crows doing the rest. This was different. These bones lay in currents, in drifts, all facing the same direction, as if hundreds of people had crawled toward that wall and simply stopped, mid-reach, and been taken apart by time. Some of the skeletons still stretched forward, finger-bones extended, touching the stone.

Touching the door.

Because there was a door. Set into the living rock, tall and narrow, black wood banded in metal gone green with age, and carved across its face in letters deep enough to catch the lantern light:

**ALL DEBTS COME HOME**

Below the words, a slot. A letter-slot, narrow as a hand's width, positioned exactly where a kneeling person's mouth would be.

"They knocked," Jonah whispered. "All of them. They came to knock."

"They came to *pay*," Sable corrected, and her voice cracked on the word. "Or to beg. Or to buy back what was sold. Look at the strata, Hartwell—these aren't one expedition. These are centuries. Generations of heirs, coming down here with their claims and their proofs, and—" she gestured at the drifts, helpless for maybe the first time since Tam had known her, "—and the mountain kept them."

Wren knelt at the edge of the bone-field. She picked up a skull, small, probably a woman's, and turned it in the light. Salt crusted the eye sockets, thick as frost.

"She got close," Wren said. "See? She's right at the wall. She reached the door." Her jaw tightened. "And it didn't matter."

"It mattered to her," Jonah said.

"Don't." Wren set the skull down with terrible gentleness. "Don't tell me it mattered. My father got close too, didn't he? That's why he was dying in your counting-house with salt on his map. He got close enough to learn the price, and he ran, and it caught him anyway." She stood. "I'm not crawling. Whatever happens at that door, I'm standing."

"Then we all stand," Tam said.

They looked at him. He realized he'd said it like an order, in the voice he'd used on green recruits, and that these three—none of whom he'd known a month ago—had simply accepted it.

"The bones crawled because they came alone," he went on, thinking it through as he spoke, the old sergeant's habit. "One heir at a time, one claim at a time, each of them certain their case was special. Alone, in the dark, in front of a door that eats people." He stepped onto the bone-slope, testing it; it shifted under his boot with that dry whisper, but held. "We don't knock alone. We don't kneel. And nobody talks to whatever answers until we've all agreed on what gets said. Understood?"

"Understood," Jonah said.

"Understood," Sable echoed, quieter.

Wren only nodded, but she drew her knife, and Tam understood that too.

They crossed the bone-field together, four abreast where the drifts allowed, shoulder to shoulder, the lantern held high between them. The bones whispered beneath their feet with every step, and Tam made himself look down once—just once—at the small skull Wren had set down, still reaching, still waiting four hundred years for a door to open.

It wouldn't wait much longer now.

Ten feet from the wall, the salt smell became overwhelming, thick enough to taste, and the lantern flame bent sideways though there was no wind. The door filled Tam's vision, black and patient, the carved words gleaming.

**ALL DEBTS COME HOME.**

Jonah stepped forward, alone after all, because some doors only open for the debtor. The ledger-keeper raised one hand—and paused.

"Tam," Jonah said, without turning around. "When I knock... if something answers in my voice—my *exact* voice—don't believe it."

The lantern flame stood perfectly still.

"I know," Tam said. "I've met the usurper's brokers. I know what borrowed voices are worth."

Jonah nodded once, squared their shoulders the way people do before a thing that cannot be unsaid, and knocked—

—and the door swung inward before knuckle touched wood, silent as a held breath, spilling candlelight and cold air and the smell of centuries across the bones.

From somewhere deep inside, unhurried, came footsteps. And a voice, dry as old paper, that said:

"At last. The Hartwell heir. We've been keeping your seat warm for a very long time."
