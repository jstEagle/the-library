## 10. A New Ledger

The first thing Jonah did with the guild's counting-house was open the windows.

It had taken three weeks to pry them loose. Corvane's locks, the shutters bolted from the inside like a man bracing against his own conscience, a decade of dust packed into the sills. Now morning came in off the harbor with the smell of tar and fish and, faintly, salt—though Jonah had learned that not all salt meant the same thing anymore.

Vell was breathing again. That was the phrase people used, half-laughing, as if they didn't quite trust it. The usurper's armies still sat beyond the eastern hills, but they sat like a dog that had forgotten why it was barking. The soul-brokers' tents had folded two days after Spirefall, when word spread through the camps that the mountain court was gone—that the ledgers beneath it had burned, and every debt they held had come due to no one. Soldiers deserted by the dozen. What is an army worth, Tam said, when its men stop believing they can be bought?

Jonah set the last ledger on the counter—the old one, the founder's own, its spine cracked and its pages stiff with generations of careful, dishonest arithmetic—and opened it to the first blank page.

Sable stood at the far shelf, reshelving. She'd been reshelving for an hour. It was how she thought; Jonah had learned that. "You could burn it," she said, without turning. "The old book. Some would say you should."

"Some would say I should burn the whole guild."

"Would you?"

Jonah considered it honestly, which was the only way they did anything now. The Guild of Impossible Things. A name that had always sounded like a boast and turned out to be a confession. For two hundred years it had traded in the impossible because the impossible was where the money hid—and when the money ran short, it had traded in people. One soul, pawned at the founding, compounding quietly down the bloodline until it landed, like a debt of ash, on the least valued member of the house.

"No," Jonah said. "Burning it would be easy. And we're done doing the easy thing, aren't we? We did the hard one under the mountain."

Sable made a sound that might have been a laugh. She crossed to the counter and laid her hand flat on the old ledger, over the founder's cramped signature. "I falsified entries in this book," she said. "Eleven years. I told myself it was mercy, smoothing the numbers so the desperate wouldn't look quite so desperate. But mercy that lies is just theft with better manners." She looked up. "So. New rules?"

"New rules." Jonah dipped the pen. "First rule: nothing traded that isn't freely given. Not coerced, not defaulted, not inherited. If both parties can't walk away, there's no trade. There's just theft wearing a contract."

"Corvane would call that the end of commerce."

"Corvane is in Tarrow, petitioning the merchant princes to charter him a new guild. Let him. He'll discover the impossible things are all spoken for."

The door opened, and Wren came in with the wind behind her, cheeks red from the harbor road, a canvas sack over her shoulder. She dropped it on the counter with a thud that rattled the inkwell. "Supplies," she announced. "And news. The brokers' auction-house in Ondrel has closed. Closed, Jonah. They hung a sign that says *under new assessment* and nobody inside will say what that means, but the whole city knows whose fault it is." She grinned, and for a moment she looked like her father must have, before the road wore him down to a map and a warning. "You broke their god."

"I broke a spire."

"You broke their god." She said it the way other people said prayers.

Tam followed her in, quieter, as he was always quieter. He'd taken a room above the chandlery and spent most mornings helping the harbormaster tally cargo, because, he said, a man who'd spent four years counting soldiers needed to count something harmless for a while. He nodded to Jonah, took up his usual post by the window—the seat facing the door, some habits never leaving—and said, "Eastern road's quiet. Saw refugees walking back toward Vell this time. Going the wrong way for fleeing." A pause. "Going home."

That word sat in the room with them a moment. Home. Jonah had lived in this building eleven years and had never once called it that. It had been a post. An obligation. A place where the least valued member kept the books and was thanked the way one thanks a lock—by using it.

* * *

They worked through the morning, and the work was strange and wonderful: people came.

Not clients, exactly—not yet. Neighbors. The baker whose ovens had gone cold during the siege and who wanted, of all things, a loan of yeast and good faith. A widow with a chest of her husband's tools, asking if the guild still bought impossible things, and Jonah explaining, gently, that the guild now sold something rarer: fair prices. Two children who pressed their faces to the open window until Wren shooed them off, then bribed them with dried plums to carry messages, freely given, paid in more plums.

By midday the counter was crowded with small honest transactions, and Sable had started a fresh ledger—a plain one, unbound, bought from the stationer for ordinary coin—and Jonah ruled its first page with a straightedge while the others watched.

"Read them out," Wren said. "Rules should be heard."

Jonah read. *Nothing traded that isn't freely given. No debt may pass to blood or heir. Every entry signed by both parties, in their own hand. Any soul named in any bargain voids the bargain entire.* And then, after a moment, added aloud: "*The guild serves the city, not itself.*"

"That last one wasn't in your draft," Sable said.

"No. It got written somewhere between the mine-shafts and the burning. I'm just copying it down late."

Tam said nothing, but he took the pen when Jonah offered it and added a line of his own, in a soldier's blunt hand: *No one is spent.* He handed the pen back as if embarrassed, and Wren kissed his cheek, and he pretended not to have noticed, and everyone let him pretend.

* * *

Evening came early that time of year, blue and cold off the water. They lit lamps, and Wren cooked—she'd claimed the counting-house's little back kitchen the way she claimed everything, by acting as though it had always been hers—and the four of them ate at the long table where Master Corvane had once held his grim councils of ledgers and dismissal.

Jonah found themselves watching the others and feeling something they had no clean entry for. Belonging, maybe. But belonging earned rather than inherited—which was to say, belonging that could be lost, which was the only kind worth having. Under the mountain, Jonah had been offered a choice dressed as arithmetic: pass the debt on, or stay owned. They had chosen a third thing, the thing no ledger had a column for—they had ended the account entirely—and the price had been everything the founder built, and what remained afterward was this: burnt stew, bad wine, three people who had carried each other out of a collapsing dark.

"You're doing it again," Wren said.

"Doing what?"

"The face. The one where you're writing us into a book in your head."

Jonah laughed. "Habit. I've spent my whole life recording trades. It's strange to sit inside one that isn't a trade."

"It's called friendship," Sable said dryly. "There are books about it."

* * *

Later, alone at the counter, Jonah opened the old ledger one last time.

The founder's pages were all before them now—two centuries of bargains, each one balanced, each one paid by someone who hadn't chosen to pay. Jonah read the final entry, the one that had started everything: *One soul, pledged, that the work might endure.* Beneath it, in the candlelight, they wrote the closing line:

*Account settled. Paid in full by J. Hartwell, freely, that no one else need ever pay.*

Then they closed the founder's book forever and drew the new one near—the plain unbound ledger, the first honest page—and wrote the opening entry in a clear hand:

*Entry the First. Lent: one loaf-starter, to Marta the baker. Terms: returned when she can spare it, or passed on to another who needs it. Given freely. Signed, Jonah Hartwell.*

Below it, a second signature appeared, unasked: *Wren Ashfall, witness.* Then Sable's precise script. Then Tam's blunt mark.

Four names on a loan of yeast. It was the smallest entry ever recorded in the Guild of Impossible Things, and Jonah felt prouder of it than of shattering a spire.

Outside, the harbor bells rang the hour, and somewhere east the usurper's army sat wondering what it was still doing there. Somewhere south, in Tarrow, Corvane was learning that charters could not be bought without collateral, and that all the old collateral was ash.

Jonah blew out the lamp, and in the dark of the counting-house, on the counter, the new ledger lay open to a page that was almost empty—

almost, but not quite. Because as Jonah's fingers brushed the cover one last time, they felt, faint and unmistakable, a warmth in the paper, like breath. And in the margin of Entry the First, in ink neither of them had used, in a hand none of them knew, someone—or something—had already begun to write:

*Entry the Second.*
