## 1. The Valley That Forgets

The dead of the Hollow kept better order than the living.

Vesper Ashgrove had learned that before she'd learned to read. Her grandmother had taught her the tending of graves the way other grandmothers taught bread or song: with patience, with repetition, with the quiet insistence that some things must be done properly even when no one watched. *The ground remembers care,* Elenna Ashgrove used to say, kneeling in the wet grass with her sleeves rolled to the elbow. *Even here. Even where nothing else does.*

Nothing else did. That was the truth at the bottom of the valley like a stone dropped down a well.

Vesper knelt now in the churchyard above the village of Lowmere, pulling chickweed from around a headstone so old its name had worn to a soft suggestion—*MART?N*—and worked the whetstone along the edge of the sword across her knees. The two tasks had nothing to do with one another and she performed them together every seventh morning anyway, because ritual was the only architecture her life had left.

The sword was called Merrow's Tooth, though no one alive knew who Merrow had been. It had belonged to her grandmother, and to her grandmother's mother, and back through eleven generations of women whose names were carved into the lintel of the Ashgrove house like a ledger of debts. The blade was good steel, honest and plain, and it had never once been drawn in Vesper's hand. She kept it sharp regardless. An oath, her grandmother said, is not a cage you lock once. It is a door you choose, every day, not to open.

*Never draw it,* Elenna had made her swear, both their hands pressed flat on the cold scabbard, on the night before she died. *Not for fear. Not for anger. Not for love. The words that live in that steel are older than our grief, and they do not care what we meant.*

Vesper had been nineteen. She was thirty-one now, and the oath sat in her chest like a second heartbeat, steady as the valley itself.

She turned the blade against the grey morning light. No gleam answered—the Hollow gave back light dully, the way a tired man returns a greeting. That was the other thing about the valley, the thing visitors noticed within an hour if they ever came, which they almost never did: the silence had texture. Prayers fell out of the mouth and dropped straight into the dirt. Wishes went nowhere. The little charms peddlers sold at the mountain passes—fever-knots, luck-threads, love-sigils—arrived in the Hollow already dead, dull as river pebbles. Children born here never saw their breath glow in winter the way travelers swore it did elsewhere. Nothing listened. Nothing answered. Nothing *watched.*

The elders called it sanctuary. The Hollow was a bowl of stillness ringed by ridgelines the fog never quite left, a place where the great powers of the world could not follow, could not see, could not reach. People had come here fleeing wars and plagues and gods alike, generations back, and found what they needed: a valley where magic could not enter, and so could not hurt them.

Vesper had believed in it the way she believed in rain. Simply. Without question.

She set the whetstone down, sheathed the sword, and laid her palm briefly on the worn headstone. "Morning, Martin," she said, because her grandmother had also taught her that the dead deserved greeting even if they couldn't hear it. Especially then.

Below her, Lowmere woke in its usual order: smoke from the baker's chimney first, then the clatter of the well-chain, then the dogs, then everything else. Forty roofs. Two hundred souls. A mill, a smithy, a shrine with no god in it that anyone could name—the statue on its plinth had been faceless longer than the village had records, worn smooth by weather or by something more deliberate. The children used to dare each other to touch it. Vesper had touched it once, at nine years old, and felt nothing at all, and had gone home strangely comforted.

That was the whole of her world. Graves and steel and a village that forgot to be afraid because there was nothing to be afraid of.

She should have known stillness that complete had to be built.

The stranger came up the pass road in the last hour of daylight, and Vesper saw him fall.

She had been walking the valley's edge out of habit rather than duty—there was no duty; the Hollow had no watch, no walls, no need—but the eastern ridge path was where she thought best, where the fog came through the saddle between the peaks in slow grey rivers and the air smelled of stone and cold pine. From the high shoulder of the path she could see the whole length of the pass road, and she had seen perhaps six travelers in twelve years. Merchants' factors who'd lost their way. One tax assessor from the lowlands who stayed two nights and left pale and quiet, saying the valley made his teeth ache. Once, a young woman heavy with child, fleeing something she wouldn't name, whom Maren had taken in until the birth and then gently pointed back down the road, because the Hollow did not keep what came from outside. It couldn't. Things brought in from beyond the ridges lost their edges here. Charms died. Blessings faded. And, the elders murmured when they thought no one listened, other things faded too.

The man was halfway up the switchbacks when his legs simply stopped being his legs. He pitched sideways into the gorse, and even at that distance Vesper heard the sound he made—not a cry, more a kind of folding, the noise a body makes when it gives up arguing.

She was moving before she decided to. That was the other inheritance the Ashgrove women carried, the one nobody had ever thought to make her swear away: when someone fell, you went to them.

He was older than she'd guessed from the ridge—fifty, sixty, hard to tell, because his face had been ruined in a way that wasn't age. A wound ran from his hairline down past his jaw, long since crusted, but wrong. Vesper had stitched enough farm accidents to know what blades and hooves and falling stones did to flesh. This looked like frostburn and lightning-scald had argued over the same territory and split it. His clothes had been fine once—a pilgrim's traveling coat, sun-faded, embroidered at the cuffs with symbols she didn't recognize and felt, obscurely, that she shouldn't.

"Hey." She knelt, got a hand behind his shoulders. His skin was fever-hot and dry as paper. "You're in the Hollow. You're safe. Can you hear me?"

His eyes opened. They were the color of weak tea, and they fixed on her with a sudden terrible focus, the focus of a man spending his last coins.

"Ashgrove," he said.

The name went through her like cold water. "How do you—"

"Heir." His hand came up—it took visible effort, trembling, fingers hooked like a bird's foot—and caught the front of her shirt. "Heir of the line. I looked for—" A cough tore through him, wet and deep. Blood showed at the corner of his mouth, too dark. "I looked for forty years."

"You're hurt. I need to get you down to the village, there's a healer—"

"No healer." He said it with absolute finality, the way men say things they've had a long time to decide. His grip tightened. "Listen. Listen while I can still—"

And then he began to pray.

Vesper had heard prayers before, of course. The Hollow said them at funerals, at births, at the turning of the seasons—words shaped like prayers, worn smooth as the faceless shrine, said to no one, answered by nothing. It was a form of speech, that was all. A way of talking to the dark and calling the talking comfort.

This was not that.

The man's voice changed. There was no other way to describe it. It deepened and widened at once, as though the words were coming up through him from somewhere far below, from under the ground, from under the *world*, and each syllable landed in the air with weight. Vesper felt the hairs rise on her arms. She felt her own heartbeat stumble.

And the valley—

The valley *shifted.*

There was no wind. But the fog on the ridgelines stopped moving, hung suspended like held breath. The gorse around them stilled though a moment ago it had been bending. And beneath her, through her knees, through the bones of her, Vesper felt something turn over in the deep rock of the Hollow—the sensation of a vast attention shifting in its sleep, of an ear pressing against the other side of a wall.

Something answered him.

She never heard the answer as sound. It arrived instead as certainty, blooming full-formed in her mind the way you sometimes wake knowing a thing without knowing how: *heard.* The prayer had been *heard.* Somewhere—or somewhen, or somehow—a door that had been shut for longer than the village had existed had opened the width of a knife-blade, and something on the other side had leaned close.

The pilgrim smiled. It was the worst smile Vesper had ever seen, bloodied and beatific.

"It hears," he whispered. "Even here. Even *here.* The seal is thin, Ashgrove. Your line thinned it, carrying it, generation on generation, and now—" Another cough, longer, wracking. When it passed he lay very still, and his tea-colored eyes had begun to glaze. "Take it. Take my burden. I carried it out of the burning house and across the salt waste and through the thorn country, and I am so tired, heir. I am so tired."

"Take what?" Vesper's voice sounded strange to her, flattened, distant. "What are you talking about?"

His hand fumbled at his own chest, at a cord around his neck, and drew out a sigil on a leather thong: a sliver of bone, yellowed, carved with lines so fine they might have been cracks until the failing light caught them and they seemed to move, slowly, like things written in water.

He pressed it into her palm and closed her fingers over it with both of his ruined hands.

"The covenant," he said. "Of the god your family killed. Kept alive by the family that killed him. Do you understand? They could not let him die *all* the way. They were too afraid of what would grow in the empty place. So they bound his death in an oath, and fed it, and hid it in the one place the world's powers could not look—" His breath hitched. "The Hollow was never sanctuary, girl. It is a *tomb.* And you are the gravedigger's daughter."

The bone sigil was warm. Warmer than his hands. Warm as a living thing, and against her skin, faint as a word spoken in another room, she felt it pulse.

Then the light in the pilgrim's eyes guttered, and he sagged back into the gorse, and whatever had been holding him upright—will, or purpose, or the answering presence itself—let go.

Vesper knelt there for a long time, holding a dead man's hands and a piece of carved bone that beat softly against her palm like a second heart. The fog began moving again on the ridges. The evening sounds of the valley resumed below—dogs, well-chains, a mother calling a child home—and none of it, none of it, sounded the same to her now. It sounded like a stage after the play has ended. It sounded like a held breath about to break.

Far off, at the crest of the eastern ridge, the fog parted for a moment, and Vesper would spend the rest of her life half-convinced she had imagined what stood silhouetted there: a figure, tall and still, watching the valley with the patience of something that had waited a very long time and could wait a very long time more.

Then the fog closed, and the figure was gone, and the sigil pulsed once against her skin—warm, insistent, patient as a seed.

*Heir,* it seemed to say, in a voice that knew her grandmother's name.

Vesper closed her fist around it, stood, and looked down at the lights of Lowmere beginning to kindle in the dusk—a valley that had forgotten how to be afraid, about to remember.
