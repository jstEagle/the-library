## 2. A Dying Man's Covenant

The stranger did not die quickly.

Vesper had carried him the last mile from the valley's edge to her cottage, his arm over her shoulders and his blood soaking through her sleeve in a warmth that frightened her more than any wound she had ever seen. She had bound wounds before—her own, a shepherd boy's gored leg, once the torn forearm of a man who'd fallen from the ridge path with a scythe still in his hand. Wounds had edges. Wounds had logic. You pressed cloth to them and they obeyed, slowly or not at all, but they obeyed the world's ordinary rules.

These did not.

She laid him on the pallet by her hearth and pulled back the ruined shirt, and for a long moment she simply looked, because looking was all she could do. Five marks crossed his chest and belly, deep as knife-work but wrong in every particular a wound could be wrong. They were too clean, each one precise as if drawn with a stylus rather than torn by anything with an edge. And they were black—not bruised black, not dried-blood black, but black like ink held under glass, like something poured into him from outside. The skin around each mark was pale and unbroken, unmarked even by inflammation, as though his body had given up arguing with them entirely.

"What did this?" she asked him, though she already knew he might not answer.

His eyes found hers. They were gray eyes, washed-out, the eyes of a man who had walked a very long way through weather. "The Court," he said. The word came out wet. "Thorn Court. I ran. I shouldn't have run. Running is—" He coughed, and the cough brought up something dark that steamed faintly in the firelight, and Vesper decided she would not look at it again.

"Hold still. I have willow bark. Yarrow. There's a poultice my grandmother—"

"No." His hand caught her wrist. His grip was astonishingly strong for a dying man, and cold, cold as river stones. "No medicines. Listen. There isn't time for you to be kind. There is only time for you to listen."

Outside, the Hollow went on being the Hollow. Wind moved through the pines along the valley walls with its usual dry whisper. Somewhere down-slope, old Hedda's dog barked twice at nothing and gave up. The light through the window was the flat silver of late afternoon, and nothing in it shimmered, and nothing in it listened, because nothing here ever did. That was the whole promise of this place. That was the covenant of silence her people had kept for longer than anyone could count: no voices from above, no power in the ground, no gods leaning close to hear what you wanted. Prayers fell like stones into deep water. Everyone said it was peace.

Vesper had built her entire life inside that peace, plank by plank, oath by oath.

And yesterday, at the valley's edge, a stranger had prayed aloud—and something had answered—and now he lay bleeding black on her floor while the wind kept whispering its ordinary whisper, and she could not stop feeling that the whisper had changed key.

"Your name," she said. "Tell me your name."

"Ilan." He said it like a man setting down a heavy pack after many miles. "Ilan Vosk. Pilgrim of the Third Road, which doesn't exist anymore, which is why I'm the last one walking it." A ghost of a smile. "You're the swordswoman."

"I'm not."

"You carry a sword."

"I carry a sword the way a farmer carries his father's plow. It's inheritance, not intention." She said it flatly, the way she said it to everyone who noticed the long shape wrapped in oilcloth above her door. *I swore never to draw it. I am not what you think.* "There are no swordspeakers anymore. Whatever you were told—"

"I was told you're the last," Ilan said. "Ashgrove. Heir of the Ashgrove line."

The name went through her like cold water through cloth.

Her grandmother had been dead eleven years, buried up on the south slope beneath a cairn Vesper rebuilt every spring when frost-heave knocked it loose. Ashgrove was a name for gravestones now. It was a name for an empty cottage and a wrapped sword and a woman who talked to graves because graves were the only things in the Hollow that never asked her for anything.

"You knew her," Vesper said quietly. Not a question. She could see it in the way his face changed—the softening, the grief arriving ahead of schedule, grief for someone already years dead.

"I served her," Ilan said. "Before she came here. Before she sealed herself away with the rest of you. I was young. I carried her standard at the Ford of Nine Sorrows, and I watched her cut down three of the Thorn Court's chosen in a single pass, and afterward she wept like a girl and told me she would spend the rest of her life making sure no one ever needed to do that again." His breath rattled. "She meant the valley. She meant all of this. She was the strongest of them, your grandmother. Strong enough to walk away."

"My grandmother was a herb-woman," Vesper said. "She grew valerian. She taught me which mushrooms kill and which ones only make you wish you were dead. She never—" But even as she said it, the memory rose unbidden: Sera Ashgrove standing at the grave of her own daughter, Vesper's mother, saying words over the cairn in a language Vesper had never heard before or since, words that made the air feel *thick*, and then catching Vesper watching and going white as bone and saying, *You saw nothing, little bird. In this place there is nothing to see.*

Vesper had been seven. She had believed, because believing was easier, and because the alternative—that her grandmother had been someone else entirely—was a door she did not have the strength to open.

"She never spoke of it," Vesper finished. "Whatever she was."

"That was the point of her," Ilan said. "Of all of you. The ones strong enough to carry a war inside them and choose silence instead." He tried to rise; his body refused; he settled for turning toward her, and the movement dragged a sound out of him that Vesper would hear in her sleep for weeks afterward. "Listen to me. The seal is failing. Do you understand? The quiet you've lived in—it was built, it was *made*, and everything made comes apart. Cracks first. Hairline. Then wider. Prayers slipping through. Things answering that should be sleeping. The Court felt it the way a spider feels a fly touch the web's outer ring, and they sent hunters, and I ran the wrong way, and now I'm here, and here is where I was always going to end up, because the road bends toward Ashgroves. It always has."

"You're raving."

"I'm dying," he corrected, almost gently. "Raving is faster. Dying gives me time to say this properly, so let me say it properly." He fumbled at his neck, fingers clumsy with cold, and drew out a cord she hadn't noticed beneath his ruined shirt. On it hung a sigil: bone, carved, small enough to hide in a closed fist. It was yellowed with age and darkened along one edge as though it had been held near fire, and the carving was of a tree—a rowan, maybe, or an ash—with roots that went down and down and became, if you looked too long, something else. Hands. Or claws. Or the fingers of someone reaching up from underneath.

He pressed it into her palm and folded her fingers over it with both of his hands, and his hands shook, and hers did not, and she hated herself for noticing that.

"Sera Ashgrove's heir," he said. "By blood and by burden. I don't know why it's you—I only know the sigil chose, and the sigil has never been wrong, and I have watched it lie cold in my hand for forty years waiting for the right palm." His gray eyes held hers. "Take it. Take it out of the Hollow. Take it anywhere but here, because here is where they'll come first, and what you carry must not fall into thorned hands."

"Ilan—"

"*Swear it,*" he said, and his voice cracked across the little room like a whip, and for half a heartbeat Vesper would have sworn the firelight bent toward him. "Swear it on your line. On her name. On the graves you tend—you think I didn't see you this morning, kneeling at the cairns with your whetstone and your flowers? I've been watching this valley for three days, girl. Watching you. You're her. Gods help us both, you are so much her."

*The graves you tend.* Heat crawled up Vesper's throat. Yes, she tended them. Every dawn, nearly. Her grandmother's, her mother's, the anonymous mounds from the sickness year when she'd been sixteen and the Hollow had buried thirty of its own and magic had been nowhere to be found, thank all the absent gods, nowhere at all. She sharpened the wrapped sword beside those graves because it was the only prayer she permitted herself: *not this, not again, not me.* She had thought it private. She had thought the Hollow kept her secrets the way it kept everything else—silently, permanently, dead.

"The sigil burns," she said. Because it did. It lay in her closed fist like a coal that had forgotten how to hurt her yet, warm past warmth, and beneath the heat she could feel—she searched for another word and failed—a *pulse*. Slow. Patient. Like something sleeping just below the surface of a lake, aware of the weight standing on the ice.

"It does that," Ilan whispered. "It knows you. Ask it—no. Don't ask it anything. Never ask it anything. Asking is how it gets in." His head lolled back against the pallet frame. Sweat stood on his brow despite the cold of his hands. "Gods. It's pulling. Can you feel it pulling? Forty years I carried it and it never pulled like—"

The sigil flared.

Not light, exactly. Warmth—heat flooding up her arm, through her elbow, into her shoulder, and with the heat a voice, and the voice did not come through her ears at all. It arrived the way a memory arrives, fully formed, from somewhere behind her own thoughts:

***Sera.***

One word. A woman's voice, or something wearing a woman's voice the way a thorn wears a rose. One word, and Vesper's knees nearly went, because the word was her grandmother's name spoken with such naked, ancient longing that it could only have been waiting decades to be said, hoarded like the last coin of a bankrupt kingdom.

Then the voice found *her*, and she felt it turn its attention on her the way a lighthouse turns, slow and immense and impersonal:

***Ah. The daughter-daughter. Little bird. You have her hands.***

Vesper wrenched her fist open and dropped the sigil on the floorboards and stepped back, heart slamming, and the sigil simply lay there, bone-yellow and small and utterly ordinary-looking, cooling in the dust like any carved trinket from any market stall.

But the room felt different. The room felt *occupied*.

"What," Vesper said, and her voice came out steadier than she deserved, "was that."

Ilan was laughing. It was a terrible sound, laughter from a man whose chest was full of black ink, thin and delighted and horrified all at once. "It spoke. It actually—forty years, and it never said a word to me, not one, and it speaks to you inside a minute." He coughed, wet and long. "It knows you. It's been waiting for you. Oh, Sera, you clever, stubborn ghost—you hid it in your own bloodline, didn't you? Made the covenant itself the heirloom."

"Stop talking in riddles and tell me what it is."

"It's the covenant," Ilan said simply. "What's left of it. What was left after the war, after the founding, after your ancestors did what needed doing and paid what needed paying. It's a god's last breath kept in bone. It's a promise and a prison and a key, depending on whose hand holds it." His eyes were losing focus, drifting toward the rafters, toward somewhere beyond rafters. "In the Hollow it sleeps. Outside—" he swallowed "—outside, it wakes. That's why it must leave. That's why it must never be found *here*, where the seal is thinnest, where one waking god could crack the whole valley open like an egg."

"And if I refuse?" Vesper's voice was low. "If I put it in the fire? If I bury it under the north scree where no one's walked in a hundred years?"

"You won't."

"Don't tell me what I won't do."

"You knelt at thirty graves this morning," Ilan said. "I counted. You straightened every stone. You are a woman who keeps faith with the dead, and the dead are asking." He smiled, and it was the saddest thing she had seen since her mother's funeral. "Besides. Burn it, and it burns you. Bury it, and it digs. It's patient, Ashgrove. It's had centuries of practice at patience. All you can choose is whether you meet it standing up."

The light through the window had gone from silver to copper. Evening coming on. Down in the village, lamps would be lit, bread set out, children called in from the fields—the whole drowsy machinery of a valley that believed itself safe, that had believed itself safe for so long that safety had become indistinguishable from the air they breathed. Vesper thought of Tomas Fenwick's boy, the shepherd child who waved at her every morning with both arms as though greeting a returning ship. She thought of Maren's hands, spotted and steady, sorting beans on her porch. She thought of the word Ilan had used. *Cracks.*

"How long," she asked, "has the seal been failing?"

"Years. Decades, maybe. Seals don't fail all at once—they leak. A dream here that's too vivid. A fever that breaks when someone prays over it, and everyone calls it luck." His breathing was shorter now, spaces between the words. "Yesterday, at your valley's edge, I prayed aloud. First time in forty years I dared. And something answered, Vesper Ashgrove. Something reached through a hairline crack in a hundred-year silence and touched a dying pilgrim, and that touch is the loudest bell that's rung in this world since your grandmother's time. They heard it. The Court heard it. That's why the hunters. That's why these—" he gestured vaguely at the black marks on his chest "—these are what happens when the Thorn Court catches a runner and decides to leave him alive long enough to deliver a message."

"A message," Vesper repeated. Dread arrived in her stomach like swallowed river water, cold and expanding. "You're the message."

"I'm the envelope," Ilan said, and laughed his terrible laugh again, weaker now. "The message is written in bramble somewhere on your ridge by tomorrow night. Something polite. Something like *return what you carry.* They're very courteous, the Court, right up until they aren't." He reached for her hand. She let him take it. His grip had gone from river-stone cold to something colder, and thinner, like holding winter itself. "So. Last ask. Last official act of the Third Road, pilgrim's privilege, older than your valley, older than their Court: carry it out. Carry it far. Find what it's for—or find what ends it—but don't let them crown themselves with it, and don't let it crown you either, because it will try, little bird, it will try so sweetly—"

"Ilan."

"—and tell Sera—" He stopped. His eyes cleared for one final moment, sharp and gray and unbearably kind. "No. She's gone. Tell the graves, then. You're good at telling the graves."

He died as the sun went down.

Not dramatically. The rattle in his chest slowed, spaced itself out like a tired man's footsteps, and stopped between one breath and the next, and the room was suddenly only a room, firelit and quiet, with a dead pilgrim on the pallet and a bone sigil on the floor and Vesper Ashgrove standing between them, unable to move, unable to look away from either.

She did what her hands knew how to do. She washed him—carefully, avoiding the black marks, which had not changed at all in death, which remained precise and glossy and wrong. She wrapped him in linen. She built up the fire. And when the work was done and there was nothing left between her and the thinking, she knelt and picked up the sigil.

Warm. Patient. Awake.

*I will not ask it anything,* she told herself, and understood even as she thought it that refusing to speak to a thing was itself a conversation, and that she had already lost the first exchange.

***

She buried Ilan at dawn on the south slope, two rows down from her grandmother's cairn, facing east the way pilgrims of whatever his Third Road had been presumably faced. The ground took him without complaint. The Hollow was good at taking things and keeping them silent.

Vesper stood over the fresh mound for a long time, the sigil in her pocket, weighing nothing and everything.

Then she climbed the extra ten yards to her grandmother's grave, knelt in the frost-wet grass, and pressed her palm flat against the cairn's top stone, the way she had since she was seven years old.

"He knew you," she said. Her breath fogged. "He served you, and he ran from them for forty years carrying a piece of bone, and he died on my floor calling me *little bird*—your word, your stupid secret word—and he says the seal is failing, Gran. He says the quiet is a *made* thing and it's coming apart, and he says you knew. You knew what this valley cost and what it was hiding and you planted valerian and taught me mushrooms and never said one single word."

The cairn said nothing. The Hollow said nothing. That was its whole nature; that was its sacred promise.

But in her pocket, against her hip, the sigil pulsed once—slow, warm, unmistakable—like a second heartbeat answering hers, and a voice that was not a voice murmured from somewhere behind her thoughts, gentle as frost forming:

***Ask me, little bird.***

Down in the valley, on the eastern ridge, black bramble had begun to grow where no bramble had ever grown, and it was growing fast, and it was spelling something.
