## 4. The First Answered Prayer

Tomas had counted the sheep twice and the stars once, and the numbers had not agreed with each other, which was the kind of thing that happened when you were eleven and the night was long and the hill was cold and your sister was dying three miles down in the village.

He said the number out loud—*forty-one*—and one of the ewes looked at him as if to say he'd miscounted, and he decided she was probably right, and then he sat down hard on the cold grass and did not think about sheep at all.

He thought about Wren.

She had been coughing since the first frost, a small dry sound like a stick breaking somewhere far away, and everyone had said *it will pass, it always passes*, and it hadn't passed. It had deepened. By yesterday her breath came in whistles, thin and reedy, like wind through a cracked shutter. By this morning she had not gotten out of bed at all. His mother had sat beside her pressing cool cloths to her forehead, and his father had gone for Old Saret, who was the closest thing the Hollow had to a healer, and Old Saret had come, and looked, and pressed her lips together in a way that told Tomas everything before she said anything at all.

"There are fevers that break," she'd said, "and fevers that don't," and then she'd mixed something bitter-smelling and left, and his father had stood in the doorway watching the road like it might bring him an answer if he stared hard enough.

The Hollow didn't have answers. That was the thing nobody said out loud but everybody knew, the way you know the weight of your own hands. The valley kept them safe—that was what the elders said on festival days, when they lit the lamps along the low wall and spoke of the founders' wisdom—but safe was a strange word for a place where nothing ever *happened*. No wonders. No terrors. The weather came mild and went mild. Crops grew the way crops grew, no better than they should, no worse. When people got sick, they either got better or they didn't, and there was nothing in between, nothing to bargain with, nothing to hold onto.

Tomas had never thought much about it until now. Now, sitting on a freezing hillside with forty-one—or forty-two—sheep around him and his sister's whistle-breath in his ears, he thought about it very hard indeed.

Because he had heard stories. Not from the elders—the elders didn't tell those kinds of stories—but from travelers, back when travelers still came through, before the roads south had gone quiet. Travelers talked about other places, places where you could *ask*. Where there were shrines at crossroads and little gods in little houses and sometimes, if you asked right, if you were desperate enough or good enough or just lucky enough, something *answered*.

His grandmother had believed in that. She used to leave a bowl of milk on the windowsill "for whoever's listening," even though his father laughed at her and said the only ones listening were cats. She died when Tomas was six. He still remembered how she'd held his face in her two dry hands and said, *There's more in this world than this valley lets in, boy. Remember that.*

He had forgotten. He was remembering now.

"Please," he said, out loud, to the dark, feeling immediately foolish.

The sheep didn't even look up.

"Please." Louder this time. His voice cracked in the middle of it, the way voices do when they're eleven and scared. "I don't—I don't know who does this. I don't know the words. Nobody taught me the words. But Wren is sick and she's only eight and she likes to catch frogs and she can't breathe right and—" His throat closed. He pushed through it. "I'll give anything. I mean it. Anything I have. Please. *Please.*"

The word went up into the black air and stopped, the way all words in the Hollow stopped, swallowed by the quiet that lived here like a stone lives in the earth.

Tomas put his face in his hands and cried a little, quietly, the way boys cry when there is no one to see, and the sheep breathed around him, and the stars burned indifferently overhead, and nothing happened.

Nothing happened for a long time.

And then something did.

It began—not as a sound, exactly. More as a *leaning*. As if the whole valley, the hills and the river and the sleeping village and the graves on the slopes, all of it tilted slightly toward him, the way wheat leans toward the sun. The cold on the back of his neck lifted. The darkness behind his eyelids turned warm gold, like sunlight through honey, though he knew, he *knew*, the moon was behind cloud and there was no light anywhere on that hill.

Something vast had turned its attention toward him.

It did not speak in words. Words would have been too small for it, the way a thimble is too small for a river. But meaning poured into him anyway, warm and enormous and impossibly gentle, and the meaning was:

*I hear you.*

Tomas stopped breathing. The sheep stopped grazing. Every animal on that hillside stood frozen with its head raised, ears pricked toward some point in the air above the boy's bowed head, and far off in the village, dogs began to howl.

*Your sister,* the warmth said, without saying. *Show me.*

"I—" His voice came out tiny, a mouse's squeak against the vastness. "She's down there. In the village. In our house by the mill. She's small and she has brown hair and she can't breathe."

The attention settled over him like a blanket, like a hand, like a whole sky lying down gently on top of a single blade of grass. And then it *moved*—he felt it go, felt it pour away downhill through the ground beneath him, through the roots and the stones and the sleeping soil of the Hollow, a tide of warmth draining toward the lights of the village, and where it passed, things woke.

Grass rippled in a wind that wasn't blowing. The river, three fields over, gave a single great splash, as if something enormous had surfaced and submerged again. In the graveyard on the near slope, every bird roosting among the headstones burst upward at once, a black explosion of wings against the stars.

And in the village, in the house by the mill, in the small room with the small bed—

Wren gasped.

Tomas felt it happen. He couldn't say how he knew; he simply knew, the way you know your own name. Felt the whistle in her lungs break open like ice on a spring river. Felt her draw a breath—a full, deep, easy breath, the first in nine days—and then another, and then sit bolt upright in bed, startling his mother half out of her wits, and say, clear as a bell across the whole distance of the dark:

"Tomas? Is Tomas okay?"

* * *

He ran home. He left the sheep—he left the crook, he left the lantern, he left everything—and he ran down the hill through the rippling grass with his heart slamming and tears freezing on his cheeks, and he burst through the door of the house by the mill still shouting her name.

Wren was sitting up in bed, pink-cheeked, wide-eyed, breathing. Breathing like it was the easiest thing in the world. Their mother stood beside her with the cloth fallen from her hand, staring at her daughter as if she were seeing a ghost wearing a familiar face.

"She just—" his mother started, and stopped, and started again. "She just sat up. Tomas, she just *sat up*. The fever broke all at once, like someone pulled a splinter out of the world—"

"Wren." He crossed the room in two strides and grabbed his sister by the shoulders, looking her over as if fever might be hiding somewhere, in her hair, behind her ears. "Are you okay? Are you really okay?"

"You were calling me," she said. "Really loud. From really far away. I heard you all the way in my dream." She frowned then, a small crease appearing between her brows, and touched the inside of her wrist, where the skin was pale. "There was something else too. Something big. It was like—" She searched for the word, eight years old and reaching for the biggest thing she'd ever felt. "Like being hugged by the whole sky."

Their mother made a sound that was half laugh and half sob and gathered them both in, and for a while there was nothing in the house by the mill but holding-on and crying and Wren demanding to know why everyone was acting so strange when she felt *fine*, better than fine, she felt wonderful, could she have some bread?

But Tomas, with his face buried in his mother's shoulder, saw what she could not see from where she stood.

On the inside of Wren's wrist, where she had touched, there was a mark. Small, no bigger than a thumbnail, pale as old scar tissue—but it wasn't a scar. It moved. Slowly, subtly, the way light moves on the bottom of a stream: a faint spiral, turning, turning, turning, like a tiny galaxy asleep under her skin.

He didn't say anything. He didn't know why he didn't say anything. Some instinct older than he was folded the knowledge away in a quiet place and told him: *not yet.*

Outside, the dogs were still barking. All of them. Every dog in the village, and beyond the village, faintly, from farms up-valley, more dogs, and somewhere a horse screaming in its stable, and geese rising off the millpond in a clatter of white panic.

The whole valley had felt it. Whatever had leaned down and listened and answered—it had shaken the Hollow the way a footstep shakes a spiderweb, and everything living on the web was trembling.

* * *

By morning, everyone was talking about the tremor.

That was the word they used, standing in knots in the lane, at the well, outside the smithy: *the tremor*. Because the ground had shuddered, hadn't it? Just before dawn. A single rolling shiver, like the valley itself had flinched in its sleep. Jars had rattled on shelves. The lamp hanging in the meeting hall had swung for a full minute. Old Ferro the miller swore the millstone had turned a quarter-turn on its own, with no water in the sluice, grinding nothing into nothing with a sound like a long sigh.

"No earthquake," said Doss the smith, arms crossed, jaw set. "I've felt earthquakes. This was different. This was—" He hunted for the word and didn't find it, and his silence frightened people more than any word would have.

"The seal," someone whispered, and then flinched, because that word wasn't supposed to be spoken, that word belonged to the elders' private ceremonies and the founding-day liturgies, and hearing it out loud in the open lane was like seeing someone undressed.

Tomas stood at the edge of the crowd with Wren's hand in his, and said nothing, and listened.

They were afraid. That was the thing that surprised him most, sitting in his chest like a swallowed stone. He had run home last night full of wonder, full of the sky-hug warmth, full of *it worked, it worked, somebody heard me*—and here was the whole village huddled together like sheep under a hawk's shadow, speaking in lowered voices about things their grandparents had sworn were only stories.

Because the stories *were* known. That was the other surprise. Tomas had grown up thinking the Hollow had no stories worth telling, but standing in that crowd he learned otherwise. They came out sideways, in fragments, in the particular tone people use for things they've been told never to repeat: that the founders had shut something out. Or shut something *in*. That the valley's peace was bought, not given. That the silence at the heart of the Hollow was not emptiness but a held breath, generations deep, and last night—for the first time in living memory—

The breath had hitched.

Old Saret appeared at the edge of the crowd, and the crowd parted for her the way water parts for a keel. She was ninety if she was a day, bent like a question mark, and she walked straight past everyone to where Tomas and Wren stood, and took Wren's wrist in her knotted fingers, and turned it over, and looked at the mark for a long, long time.

Her face did something complicated. Fear and grief and something that might, underneath everything, have been hope.

"Come with me," she said. "Both of you. Your mother too."

"Is Wren in trouble?" Tomas asked. His voice came out smaller than he wanted. "Did I do something wrong?"

Saret looked at him then, really looked, with eyes gone pale and sharp as winter ice, and whatever she saw in his face made her grip tighten on his arm.

"What did you *do*, boy?"

And Tomas, who had spent his whole life in a valley where nothing ever happened, opened his mouth to lie—and found, to his astonishment, that he couldn't. Not to her. Not after last night. The truth came out of him in a rush, quiet enough that only she could hear, his heart hammering against the swallowed stone:

"I prayed. Somebody answered."

Around them the crowd murmured on about earthquakes and omens and the elders' emergency gathering called for noon. Above the eastern ridge, where the morning sun was climbing, the light caught on something high up on the bare crest of Thornback Hill—something that glinted, briefly, like figures standing where no figures should be.

Nobody else looked up. Only Tomas, and Old Saret, whose grip on his arm went suddenly, terribly tight.

"Get inside," she said. "All of you. Now."
