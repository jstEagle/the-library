## 6. Oath and Iron

The tremor came at dusk on the third day, rolling through the Hollow like a stone dropped into still water, and every dog in the valley began to howl.

Vesper felt it in her teeth first. Then in the sword across her knees—the old blade, unnameable, that she had been oiling on her mother's porch step when the ground shivered beneath her. The steel sang. Not rang, not hummed, but *sang*, one clear note like a struck bell buried under wool, and she nearly dropped it in the dust.

"Stop that," she whispered to it, absurdly, and pressed her palm flat along the flat of the blade until the note died.

It did not die. It only went quiet, the way a held breath is quiet.

Above her, the sky over the Hollow was doing something it had never done in her lifetime: bruising. Not with storm-color but with something older, a violet-green shimmer at the northern ridge where the seal was thinnest, where Ilan had crawled through bleeding light. The elders called it weather. Vesper had watched weather for thirty years and knew this was not weather. This was a wound showing through cloth.

She wrapped the sword in its oiled cloth, bound it, and carried it inside—not because she meant to use it, but because leaving it outside felt suddenly like leaving a door open.

* * *

Tomas came for her before dawn, hammering on her door hard enough to crack the silence he'd been raised inside.

"Maren said—Maren said you'd know what to do." He was gray-faced, breathless, his shepherd's coat still buttoned wrong. "It's my sister. It's Lira."

"Your sister was fevered when I saw her two days past. Fevers break."

"Not fevers." His hands were shaking so badly he could barely hold the lantern steady, and its light caught the mark on the back of his wrist—a faint tracery, silver-pale, like frost ferns grown under the skin. She hadn't seen it before. He followed her gaze and hid it against his chest as though she'd accused him. "It started last night. After the—the shaking. She sat up out of her sleep and spoke words nobody knows, and now there's—" He swallowed. "There's light coming off her skin when the lamp's low. Just a little. Like she's lit from behind a curtain."

Vesper took her sword down from above the lintel.

She had sworn an oath over that sword at fourteen, hand on her grandmother's grave, the same oath every Ashgrove had taken since the sealing: *never drawn, never named, never answered*. She had kept it through her mother's death, through Ilan's dying, through three nights of watching the ridge where things walked that should not have legs. The oath had been easy. The oath had been the shape of her whole life, worn smooth as a river stone.

Now it sat against her spine like a coal.

"Show me," she said.

* * *

The Ferrow farmstead clung to the eastern slopes where the pasture gave up and the gorse began, a low stone house with smoke rising crooked from its chimney because the wind was behaving wrongly—coming down when it should come up, sliding along the ground in slow, deliberate ribbons. Vesper noticed that first. Wind did not deliberate.

Then she noticed the smell. Sweet, green, vegetal—the smell of a hothouse, of cut stems, of sap. Nothing on the eastern slope should smell like growing things in the dead month.

Tomas's mother met them in the yard, white-lipped, a carving knife held like she'd forgotten what it was for. "It came an hour ago," she said without greeting. "Out of the gorse. It's been standing at the fence line. It hasn't moved. It just—watches the house. Watches toward Lira's window." Her voice frayed. "Vesper, what is it? What's happened to my daughter?"

"I don't know yet." The words tasted like ash and cowardice. "Get inside. Bar the door. Take Lira to the root cellar—stone walls, iron hinges, nothing living in there. Go."

"What are you going to do?"

What she was going to do was stand between a family and a thing out of the stories the elders told children to make them grateful for the quiet. What she was going to do was draw a sword she had sworn never to draw. She said none of it.

"Go," she said again, and this time it came out in the voice her grandmother must have used, the Ashgrove voice, and the woman went.

* * *

She walked the fence line alone with the wrapped sword in both hands, the way one approaches a snake.

The thing stood forty paces off among the gorse, and her mind refused it twice before it would let her see it whole. It had the suggestion of a man the way a trellis has the suggestion of a tree—a framework, thorned and jointed, woven from black bramble and pale wood and something wet that gleamed. Where a face should be, brambles had knotted themselves into a rough mask, and set into the knots were seeds. Dozens of them, dark and glossy, and every seed turned slowly to track her as she moved, so that the creature's attention was a field of small black eyes.

It did not breathe. The wind slid around it and bent, deliberately, like water around a piling.

When she stopped ten paces away, it opened what might have been a mouth, and what came out was not sound so much as pressure, a vibration in her sternum, and beneath it words assembled themselves the way Ilan's covenant-voice had:

***THE CARRIER. THE HEIR. RETURN WHAT WAS TAKEN AND THE CHILD KEEPS ITS LIGHT. REFUSE AND WE PRUNE.***

"You'll prune nothing here." Her own voice sounded very small and very human. "This valley is sealed. Whatever you are, you have no purchase on this ground."

The thing's mask split into something like amusement. Bramble creaked. And it took a single step forward, and where its foot came down, the frost-blackened grass burst green, then flowered, then flowered again, too fast, flowers crowding flowers until they strangled each other into rot—the world around it unable to stop growing, growth becoming a kind of screaming.

***THE SEAL IS A DOOR WITH ITS HINGES RUSTED THROUGH,*** it said. ***WE DO NOT NEED PURCHASE. WE ONLY NEED THE CRACK.***

It stepped again. Closer. The seeds in its face all fixed on her, and she understood suddenly, with a lurch, that they were not looking at her. They were looking past her. At the house. At the small curtained window where a marked child slept.

"No," Vesper said.

***THE COURT THANKS YOU FOR YOUR CENTURIES OF SILENCE. IT MADE YOU SOFT. IT MADE YOU SLOW.***

It moved faster than anything woven from wood had a right to move—one fluid unfolding, thorn-limbs scything outward, crossing forty feet of darkness in the time it took her heart to beat once.

Her body decided before her mind did. Fourteen years of drills lived deeper than any oath: she dropped her shoulder, rolled inside the reach of the outer limbs, and drew.

Cloth tore. Steel cleared leather. And the world changed.

* * *

Later, she could never say whether she spoke the words or the words spoke through her. Both memories lived in her head, equally vivid, mutually exclusive, like two dreams of the same night.

In the first memory, she opened her mouth and language poured out of her that she had never learned—syllables like struck flint, like grinding gears, like the sound a sword makes being forged, and each syllable landed in the world and *did something*. In the second, the sword itself began to speak, using her throat, her tongue, her breath, patient as a tide coming in, and she stood behind her own eyes watching her mouth move.

Either way, the first word was **KEH**, and the air in front of her blade turned to a wall.

The thorn-creature's leading limb hit it and sheared away in a spray of black sap, and the thing recoiled with a shriek of splitting wood, and Vesper heard herself continue—**VAEL ORRUN KEH**—and the syllables were not sounds anymore but *edges*. Every word sharpened the world. The moonlight gained weight. The distance between her and the creature became a thing she could measure to the width of a hair, and the measure itself became a weapon.

**ORRUN.**

She advanced and the advance was perfect, footwork her grandmother had drilled into her at nine years old suddenly revealed as the skeleton of a sentence she'd been learning all her life without knowing it. Step, turn, cut—and the cut was a word, and the word was true, and truth cut through bramble and pale wood and the wet gleaming thing beneath like they were paper. Sap sprayed hot across her cheek. The creature staggered, mask splitting, seeds scattering across the churned earth where they immediately sprouted into small writhing plants that screamed silently and died.

**VAEL,** she said, and drove the point through the knot where its heart should be, and spoke the last word into it like driving a nail:

**SERRAH-KET.**

*Finished.*

The creature came apart. Not fell—*came apart*, unwove, every thorn and joint separating with a sound like a harp collapsing, until what lay steaming on the frozen grass was a drift of black splinters already graying into ordinary deadwood. The sweet hothouse smell guttered out. The wind remembered which way it was supposed to blow.

And the silence afterward—

Vesper stood in it, sword bare in her fist, chest heaving, sap cooling on her skin, and waited for the horror to arrive. It should have arrived. She had broken her life's central oath in a single motion; she had spoken forbidden words in a valley built on their prohibition; she had done the one thing every Ashgrove for six generations had died rather than do.

Instead what she felt, underneath the shaking, underneath the cold flood of fear, was *rightness*. Her body sang. The sword in her hand felt less like a tool than like a limb she'd been born missing, and every syllable she'd spoken had fit her mouth like her own name.

That was the terrifying part. Not the creature. Not the broken oath.

The joy.

"Oh no," she whispered. "Oh, no, no—"

The blade was warm under her grip. Warmer than her hand could make it. And faintly, at the very floor of her hearing, in the register where you feel music through floorboards, it was humming again. Contented. Like something fed.

Like something *awake*.

* * *

Tomas found her on her knees in the ruined garden, scrubbing black sap from her hands with frozen mud as if cleanliness could undo anything.

"It's dead?" he asked from the gate, not coming closer. Behind him, lamplight moved in the farmhouse windows. "Whatever it was—it's dead?"

"It's dead." Her voice came out scraped. "Your sister. Did you get her to the cellar?"

"She wouldn't go." He came through the gate slowly, and in the moonlight she could see the mark on his wrist had spread, delicate silver branches climbing toward his elbow. "She stood at the window the whole time. Watching you fight. And after—after the words—" He stopped. Started again, carefully, the way you speak to someone standing on a ledge. "After, she looked at me and said, *'Tell the swordspeaker the door is open now. Tell her it can't be closed by wishing.'*"

The words landed in Vesper's stomach like stones dropped down a well.

"She's seven," Tomas said. "She talks about chickens and she can't say *thistle* right. And she said that. In a voice like—" He shook his head. "Like a grown woman. Like a lot of grown women, all talking at once."

Vesper looked down at the sword. Bare. Unwrapped. Stained to the crossguard with sap that was already flaking away as if the steel refused to keep anything it touched.

Every story Maren had ever told, every warning, every grave-side lesson: the swordspeech was not learned, it was *inherited*, and inheritance was not a gift. It was a hook. The Ashgrove women did not carry power; power carried them, and it had simply been waiting, generation upon generation, for one weak enough or desperate enough or loving enough to give it a mouth.

She had drawn the sword to protect a child. And the sword had answered. And some traitor part of her wanted—gods help her—to draw it again.

"Tomas." She sheathed the blade. The click of it home sounded, in the new silence, like a lock turning—or a cage door closing, and she couldn't tell which way she stood relative to the bars. "Pack your family's things. All of you. Tomorrow you go to Maren's hall, where the stone is thickest, and you stay there."

"And you?"

She looked north, toward the ridge, toward the violet-green shimmer that had brightened while she fought—as if the killing of one emissary had been not a victory but a signal, a flare sent up to tell whatever waited beyond the hills exactly what had woken in the Hollow.

The sword hummed against her back, soft and pleased, and she understood with terrible clarity that it was not humming for her.

Something on the ridge had felt those words. Something old had heard its own language spoken after a hundred years of silence, and it was listening now. It knew precisely where she stood.

"Me," Vesper said, "I think I'm about to be visited."
