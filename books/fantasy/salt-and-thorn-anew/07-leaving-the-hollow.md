## 7. Leaving the Hollow

The seal broke on a Tuesday, though no one in the Hollow kept that calendar anymore. Vesper knew it by other signs: the well water tasting faintly of lightning, the way the sheep huddled at the valley's center as if the edges had grown teeth, and Maren standing in her doorway at dawn with a face like a woman who has finally admitted the roof is falling.

"It's failing," Maren said. "Faster now. Whatever you did when you drew that sword—whatever woke—it's pulling at the seams."

Vesper set down the cup she'd been holding. It was chipped clay, her grandmother's, and she found herself gripping it too hard, the way one grips a thing that might be taken. "I didn't wake anything. I defended a child."

"You spoke." Maren's voice cracked on the word. "Three hundred years, girl. Three hundred years of silence bought with blood and buried names, and you *spoke*, and the valley heard." She leaned on her cane, and Vesper saw how old she'd become overnight, how the weight of confession had aged her like weather ages stone. "The sigil has to go back in the ground. Or out of the valley. One or the other, and I know which one you're thinking of."

Vesper said nothing. Outside, the morning light came down strange and golden, gilding the terraced fields, the low stone houses, the graves she'd tended for fifteen years. It looked exactly like every other morning. That was the cruelty of it—catastrophe arriving dressed as an ordinary day.

"The Court is coming," Maren went on. "You saw what they left on the ridge. Bramble doesn't grow that fast. Bramble doesn't *carve*. They gave us until the moon turns, and the moon turns in four days."

"Then I'll be gone in three."

Maren closed her eyes. For a moment she looked like she wanted to argue, to beg again, to seize Vesper by the shoulders and shake some safer plan loose from her. Instead she just nodded, slow and terrible. "Your grandmother ran too," she said. "And look how that ended."

"My grandmother ran *from* something," Vesper said. "I'm running *toward* it. There's a difference, even if it's a small one."

She walked out into the gold light and began, methodically, to pack.

* * *

Packing for exile took less time than packing for market. That was the joke she didn't tell anyone: a life built on stillness weighs almost nothing. A whetstone. Dried meat and hard bread. Her grandmother's oilskin cloak, still smelling faintly of juniper smoke after all these years. The sword, wrapped in wool and strapped across her back where it sat like a sleeping animal she couldn't trust not to bite.

The sigil she carried against her sternum, bound in linen under her shirt. It had stopped burning after the first night. Now it was worse than burning—it was *warm*, patient, like a hand resting on her skin, waiting for her to notice it. Some nights she could feel it thinking. Not words. Not yet. Just a pressure of attention, vast and ancient and horribly gentle, the way a hawk attends a field.

On the second day, Tomas appeared at her door with a pack twice his size and his sister on his arm.

"No," Vesper said, before he'd finished drawing breath.

"You haven't heard me say anything."

"I don't need to. The answer is no. You stay here. You keep your family behind the warding stones, you keep Wren away from the ridge, and you forget you ever prayed."

Tomas set his jaw in the particular way of fourteen-year-olds confronting the stupidity of adults. "Wren can't come to the door anymore without the dogs whimpering. She sleeps twelve hours and wakes up talking in a language nobody knows. Yesterday she touched the hearthstone and it bloomed. *Bloomed*, Vesper. Moss and little white flowers, in our kitchen." His voice wobbled and steadied. "If the seal's dying anyway—if everything's leaking through—then she's already marked. And if the Thorn Court wants what she carries, then the safest place for her isn't here. It's wherever you are, because they want *you* more."

"That's exactly why you should stay away from me."

"And that's exactly why we can't." He lifted his chin. "You think I haven't noticed? Everything bad that's happened since Ilan died has happened near you, but nothing has *touched* you. The bramble wrote its threat on the ridge and left you alive. The thorn-creature came for Wren's family and you killed it. You're not just carrying that thing." He pointed at her chest, at the hidden sigil. "It's carrying you. And I'd rather my sister stand next to whatever that is than wait alone for it to arrive."

Behind him, Wren looked up at Vesper with eyes that were still, mostly, a child's eyes—but with a faint ring of silver around the iris now, like moonlight caught in water. She smiled, and the smile was sweet and wrong and heartbreaking, and when she spoke, her voice had harmonics in it, two voices braided together.

"You're sad," Wren said. "Why are you sad? The road is going to be so interesting."

Vesper's chest tightened. She looked at Tomas—at the fear he was holding upright through sheer stubbornness—and felt her resolve bend like green wood.

"I'll slow you down," she said finally, which was surrender wearing the clothes of an objection. "I travel fast and I sleep badly and I will leave you behind if you become a liability."

"I'm a shepherd," Tomas said. "I can walk forever and sleep anywhere. Ask anyone."

"Nobody asked," Vesper muttered, but she was already reaching for her grandmother's cloak, and they both knew what that meant.

* * *

They left before dawn on the third day, taking the western path—the Shepherd's Stair, a switchback trail cut into the valley wall generations ago, steep enough that horses were useless and carts impossible. The Hollow fell away beneath them in folds of grey-green field, the river a bright thread, the roofs of the village small and dark with pre-dawn woodsmoke. Vesper did not look back. Looking back was a luxury for people who expected to return.

Halfway up, the air changed.

It was subtle at first—a pressure in the ears, like descending into a cellar. Then the light thickened. By the fourth switchback, the dawn itself seemed to have texture, gold shot through with veins of violet, and the stones of the path glittered with frost that wasn't cold. Wren giggled and reached out to touch a boulder crusted with luminous lichen, and the lichen pulsed gently under her fingers like a heartbeat.

"The seal," Vesper said quietly. "We're passing through what's left of it."

Above them, hanging over the valley rim like a curtain of heat-shimmer, the last of the founders' work flickered—threads of pale light woven between the peaks, fraying visibly even as they watched, unraveling strand by strand like a garment coming apart at the shoulder. Through the gaps, Vesper could see the sky beyond the sky: a deeper blue, hung with stars that hadn't set, arranged in patterns no astronomer of the Hollow would recognize.

Constellations. Watching.

"Don't look at them," she said, and pulled Wren's hood up. "Don't name them. Don't wonder about them. Wonder is a door."

Tomas swallowed audibly but kept his eyes on his boots. Wren, mercifully, seemed more interested in the singing stones.

They passed through the final thread of the seal as the sun cleared the eastern ridge, and Vesper felt it go through her like a needle—the briefest sting, a moment of being *seen* by something enormous and indifferent, catalogued and weighed and found interesting. Then they were outside, on the high moor above the valley, and the world opened north and west in waves of heather and wind-scoured stone.

Vesper turned once, unable to help it, and looked down at the Hollow.

From here it was beautiful beyond argument: a green secret folded into the mountains, three hundred years of quiet lives, of lambs and weddings and grudges and harvests, all of it sheltered under a silence her ancestors had paid for with their gods' voices and, eventually, with their daughters' lives. Her whole life was down there. Every grave she'd tended. Every oath she'd kept.

"They'll be safe," Tomas said beside her. It wasn't quite a question.

"For a while. The Court wants the sigil and they want me. As long as both are walking away from the valley, the valley is just... a place." She paused. "It's the first gift I've ever been able to give them. Being worth hunting."

"That's a horrible gift."

"It's the only one I have." She settled the sword higher on her back and faced north. "Come on. There's a shrine town three days out called Bellhollow. If the world out here is half as awake as it looks, we need to learn what we're walking into before it learns us."

* * *

They made camp that first night in the lee of a standing stone so old its carvings had worn to suggestions—spirals, maybe, or eyes. Vesper built no fire. Fire drew attention, and attention, out here, was currency spent by things that weren't people.

Tomas slept with Wren tucked against him, his arm around her, his shepherd's crook within reach. He'd been brave all day, cheerful even, pointing out game trails and naming birds. But Vesper, keeping the middle watch, heard him talking in his sleep—not words, exactly. Pleas. The cadence of prayer, worn into him like a groove, surfacing even in dreams.

*Please. Please. Please.*

The sigil warmed against her chest in answer, and Vesper pressed her palm flat over it, hard, as if she could hold a door shut with her hand.

"Not this one," she whispered. "Not him. He's paid enough."

The warmth receded, sullen, obedient in the way of things that have agreed to wait. Above her, through the gap in the clouds, the unfamiliar constellations wheeled slowly, and one of them—low on the northern horizon, a cluster shaped vaguely like a crown—seemed, when she wasn't looking directly at it, to drift closer.

On the second day they met the first pilgrim.

He came up the moor road toward them, a gaunt man in travel-stained robes, walking with the stumbling urgency of someone following a voice only he could hear. When he saw Vesper he stopped dead, and his face did something complicated—hope and terror fighting for the same ground.

"You're going north," he said. It wasn't a question. "To the shrines. Everyone's going. They're *waking*, lady. The little gods in the roadside stones, the river-mothers, the ones under the hills—they're waking up hungry, and the ones who feed them get answered, and the ones who don't—" He laughed, high and thin. "Well. The ones who don't get visited instead."

"We're not going to any shrines," Vesper said.

"Everyone's going somewhere," the pilgrim said, and walked past them, south, toward the valley he couldn't see, toward the fading seam in the air that he somehow, impossibly, smelled. Ten steps later he paused, without turning. "There's thorn-brambles growing on the north road, lady. Grew up overnight, they say, in a single line, like a fence someone's building. Like a snare someone's setting." He glanced back, and his eyes were wet and wild. "Whatever you're carrying—they know which way you'll go. Everybody knows. It's written in the hedges."

Then he was gone, hurrying down toward the Hollow as if drawn on a string, and Vesper stood in the wind with her hand on her sword hilt and her heart going cold and steady.

North road. Brambles. A fence being built around her route before she'd even chosen it.

"Tomas," she said. "How do you feel about walking cross-country?"

He looked up from tightening Wren's bootlaces, read her face, and nodded grimly. "Sheep tracks," he said. "There's always another way. You just have to be willing to walk it."

So they left the road—Vesper, the boy, and the marked child humming a song in a language older than the valley—and cut west across the open moor, toward the distant smudge of hills where no fences had yet grown. Behind them, far to the north, along a road they would never take, something green and patient continued to build.

And against Vesper's chest, warm as a sleeping coal, the covenant of the dead god dreamed on—and for the first time, she thought she could feel it dreaming of her.
