## 9. The Godling's Bargain

The river they followed out of the hills had no name on any map Brey had ever drawn for her, and by the third day Vesper suspected it had no name at all—only a long gray memory of water that wound through country where every shrine they passed stood half-awake, like dogs lifting their heads in sleep.

She did not like it. The Hollow had taught her to read silence the way other people read weather, and this land was full of voices she could not see. A milepost carved with a saint whose face had been kissed smooth by centuries of pilgrims' thumbs. A roadside cairn with ribbons tied to it, each one faded to the color of old blood, each one a prayer someone had once believed was heard. At noon they passed a well where an old woman knelt, whispering into the dark water, and when she rose she looked directly at Vesper—looked at her the way people look at lightning, or plague—and crossed herself twice.

"They know," Tomas said quietly, when she'd passed.

"They guess."

"They guessed right."

Vesper said nothing. Her hand had been resting on the sword's hilt all morning without her noticing, the way a tongue returns to a broken tooth. She made herself let go.

They camped that night where the river bent against a stand of birches, white trunks pale as bones in the failing light. Tomas built a fire small enough to hide and cooked oats with the honey he refused to stop rationing for her. His sister's mark—a faint silver tracery below Wren's collarbone, like frost ferns grown under skin—had not spread since they'd left the valley, but he checked it anyway, every night, rolling up his own sleeve as if checking his arm would tell him anything about hers. It didn't. He did it anyway. Grief does that, Vesper thought. It makes you tend wounds on yourself because the real wound is somewhere you cannot reach.

She took first watch. The stars came out hard and close, more of them than the Hollow had ever shown—the valley's seal had dimmed even the sky, she realized now, or perhaps the sky itself had been part of what her ancestors paid away. Out here the heavens were a crowded room, and she could not shake the feeling that some of those lights were looking back.

"You should sleep," she told Tomas.

"I'm sleeping."

"Your eyes are open."

"My eyes are resting."

Despite everything, something in her chest loosened, just slightly. She let him win the argument and sat alone with the fire while the birches whispered overhead.

That was when the moths came.

Not two or three—a hundred, then a thousand, pouring down from the dark canopy in a slow spiral, wings dusted silver, circling above the flames until they formed a column of soft light and fluttering shadow. The fire did not gutter. The moths did not burn. They simply gathered, dense as smoke, and began—there was no other word for it—to *arrange* themselves.

Tomas woke with a knife already in his hand. Good boy, Vesper thought distantly. Bad odds.

"Don't," she said, to him and to whatever was assembling itself out of insects and starlight. "Watch. Don't run unless I say."

The moth-cloud contracted, tightened, took shape: a tall figure, slender, robed in what looked like woven dusk, its face a smooth oval of pale wings beating too fast to see individually. When it spoke, the voice came from everywhere the moths were—which was everywhere.

*Sister of the sealed valley. Keeper of the bone that burns.*

"I'm neither of those things," Vesper said.

*You are both, and more, which is why I have walked four nights to find you.* The figure inclined its head with a courtesy so precise it felt like mockery. *I am called Serevain. Once I was prayed to for safe crossings—forded rivers, unbroken axles, travelers who arrived before the snows. A small portfolio. Modest worship. Enough.* The wings rippled; the face seemed almost to smile. *Then your ancestors built their quiet little prison, and prayers stopped crossing, and gods like me began to starve.*

"We're sorry for your inconvenience," Vesper said flatly. "Is there a point?"

Beside her, Tomas made a small strangled sound—the sound of a shepherd boy watching someone be rude to a miracle. But the godling only laughed, a sound like dry leaves applauding.

*Oh, I like you. The Thorn Court will hate you. They despise anything they cannot flatter.* The moths shifted; the figure leaned closer, and the heat of its attention was physical, like standing near an opened oven. *Here is my offer, spoken plainly because plainness is a currency among the starving. The Court has your scent now. Nine of them walk your road behind you, and their Queen's roots go deep beneath the world. Alone, you are a candle in a gale. With me, you are a candle in a lantern. I know the hidden ways—the shrine-roads, the old crossings, the paths that remember being holy even though no one prays on them anymore. I can carry you past the Court's thorns to wherever you're running.*

"And in exchange."

*A taste.* The word dropped between them like a stone into still water. *One taste of what you carry. Not the sigil—I couldn't hold it, none of us minor things could, it would pour through me like light through cracked glass and leave nothing behind. Just... a sip. A single answered prayer, drawn from that covenant's power. Let me feel, one time, what it was to be fed properly. To feel a mortal kneel and *know*, absolutely know, that the vastness above them heard and loved and answered.* The wings stilled. For one moment the godling's voice lost all its silk. *Do you know what starvation is, swordspeaker? Not hunger. Hunger is a companion. Starvation is when the thing you are begins eating itself to keep the shape of itself, and one century you look inward and find only walls, and beyond the walls, nothing.*

Vesper sat very still. The fire crackled. Tomas's knife-hand trembled and steadied.

"No," she said.

The moths shivered, all together, like a field of wheat in wind.

*You haven't heard my terms fully—*

"There are no terms." She rose, drawing herself up to her full height, and was distantly surprised to find her voice level, because inside her chest everything was shaking. "I watched a godling in the capital buy a child's memory for the price of a healing. I've seen what 'one taste' becomes. You're starving, and I believe you, and I'm sorry for it—but I am not food, and neither is what I carry. Find another way to live. Or don't. But not through me."

Silence. The moth-figure hung motionless above the fire, and in that stillness Vesper saw—or thought she saw—the truth beneath the shape: something gaunt and desperate wearing beauty the way a beggar wears stolen clothes.

Then Serevain laughed again, softer now, and there was something in it that might have been respect.

*Refused by a mortal with a sword and a conscience. My, how the world turns.* The figure straightened. *Very well. No bargain. But hear me, Ashgrove—hear me freely given, because even starving things remember courtesy once. You believe the Thorn Court wants the sigil. You believe if you buried it in the sea they would lose your trail and turn to easier prey.*

"Don't they?"

*Foolish child.* There was no cruelty in it, which somehow made it worse. *The Court has dug relics out of the roots of mountains. If they wanted the bone, they would have taken it from your sleeping hand three nights ago—one of their number walked through your camp while you dreamed, close enough to touch your hair, and left you breathing. Ask yourself why.*

The cold that moved through Vesper then had nothing to do with the night air.

*They do not want the covenant,* Serevain said. *The covenant is dead meat, a husk, an empty throne. What they want is the one living soul whose blood can wake it. A vessel. An heir. You, Vesper Ashgrove—they want *you*. Your willing heart. Your poured-out blood. The last woman alive who can make a god breathe again. And they will follow you to the edge of the world and back for it, and they will be patient, because patience is the one prayer they've never needed help answering.*

"Why tell me this?" Vesper's throat was dry. "What do you gain?"

*Hope,* the godling said simply. *If you refuse everyone—if you break the game rather than play it—then perhaps the starving might finally be allowed to die in peace instead of being harvested forever by things with thrones. You are not a prize to me, swordspeaker. You are a possibility. I collect possibilities; they are nearly as nourishing as prayers.* The moth-shape began to loosen, to scatter upward, wings dissolving back into the ordinary dark. *Go east. There is a monastery in the cliffs above the river—ruined, roofless, but its library did not burn, because books are the one thing even gods fear to destroy. Your family's name is written there. Read what is written before the Court reaches you. Know what you are heir to. And when you understand it—*

The last of the moths drifted apart like ash on wind. The final words came from everywhere and nowhere, faint as breath:

*—remember that I warned you kindly.*

Then it was only the fire, and the birches, and Tomas staring at her across the flames with wide eyes.

"It talked to you," he whispered. "A god. It—it knew your name."

"A godling," Vesper corrected automatically, and then hated herself for it. "Minor. Local. Starving." She sat back down slowly, because her knees had opinions. "And it lied. Godlings lie."

"Did it?" Tomas poked the fire. Sparks climbed toward the scattered moths still settling in the branches above. "Which part?"

Vesper turned the question over, and found she could not answer it. The offer—yes, that had the ring of truth, the particular sweetness liars use. But the warning? *They want you, not the sigil?* That had been delivered like a gift, and gifts were the oldest bait in every story Maren had ever told.

Except.

Except that three nights ago she had woken at moonset with the certainty someone had stood over her, and dismissed it as a dream. Except that the thorn-creature at the ridge had struck at her sword-arm, not her heart—disabling, not killing. Except that the Court's message had said *return what you carry*, and what did a god need with returning something it could simply take?

Her grandmother's face rose in her memory, unbidden: the way Elira Ashgrove had gone silent whenever the subject of the family's duty came near, the way she'd held Vesper once, hard enough to hurt, and whispered *you'll never owe them anything, do you hear me, never*—

"She knew," Vesper murmured.

"What?"

"Nothing." She pulled her blanket around her shoulders. "Sleep, Tomas. We move at dawn. East."

"East to where?"

"To a ruin full of dead men's books." She stared into the fire and did not look at him. "Apparently my family left a confession."

Above them, the last few moths lifted off the birch branches and flew away east, all together, like a handful of thrown salt.

Tomas watched them go, then lay back with his knife under his hand, and neither of them slept much at all.

* * *

Two days later, from a ridge above the river, Vesper saw the monastery: a broken crown of gray stone on the cliff opposite, its tower snapped off like a finger, its chapel roof open to a sky that went on forever. Ivy had taken the walls. Wind had taken the rest.

And in the valley floor between them, half-hidden by morning mist, she saw the brambles.

They covered a mile of ground in a black tangle that no natural thornbush could form—geometric, deliberate, growing in spirals around a clearing at its center where the mist refused to enter. Even at this distance she could feel it, the way you feel a storm building behind hills: a pressure, a patience, a *waiting*.

At the clearing's edge, nine shapes stood in a row, motionless, facing east.

Facing the monastery.

Facing her road.

"They're not following us anymore," Tomas breathed beside her. "They're ahead of us."

"Yes." Vesper's hand found the sword hilt, and this time she let it stay. The blade hummed against her palm, so faintly it might have been imagination—the same hum she'd felt the moment she'd first spoken power, the sound of something vast turning over in its sleep and beginning, slowly, to wake.

Serevain had said *read what is written before the Court reaches you.*

Looking down at those waiting brambles, Vesper understood that "before" had already expired. The only question left was whether she reached the library with her mind still her own—or whether whatever was written there reached the Court first, through her, in the only coin they'd ever truly wanted.

Her blood.

"Come on," she said, and started down toward the ruins.
