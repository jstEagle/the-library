## 11. Marked

The trap did not look like a trap. That was the worst of it.

Tomas had imagined ambushes the way the shepherds of the Hollow imagined them—thorns bursting from the earth, a wall of bramble rising like a wave, something out of the old stories with too many teeth. What actually happened was a village.

It sat in a fold of hills two days east of the monastery ruins, smoke rising from its chimneys, bread-smell on the wind, a well at the crossroads with a bucket that creaked as it turned. Vesper stopped at the edge of it and stood very still for a long time, and Tomas watched her do the thing she always did: read a place like weather.

"Too quiet," she said finally.

"There's a dog barking."

"One dog." Her hand had drifted to her sword hilt without seeming to notice it. "Where are the other dogs?"

He wanted to laugh. He almost did. Then he saw what she was looking at—the way no one came to their doors despite the strangers on the road, the way the curtains hung perfectly still in every window, the way the bread-smell was real but the ovens beneath it were cold. The village was dressed up the way his mother used to dress the house when the priest came: everything arranged, nothing lived in.

"We go around," Vesper said.

They were already turning when the road turned with them.

Not literally—that would have been easier to understand. But the path they took bent, gently, the way water bends downhill, and the hills folded themselves around their route like patient hands, and an hour later Tomas looked back and saw the village behind them again, closer, though they had walked away from it the whole time. The third time it happened, Sela began to cry.

His sister was nine years old and had walked out of the Hollow on legs still weak from fever, and she had not complained once, because she was stubborn the way their whole family was stubborn, but now she cried without noise, tears sliding down her face while she kept walking, and the mark on her wrist—the faint silver tracery like frost on a windowpane—pulsed softly with each sob, brightening, dimming, brightening.

"It can feel me," she whispered. "The thing in the road. It can feel where I am."

Tomas knelt and gathered her onto his back, and he felt it too then—a pressure against his thoughts, vast and slow, like something breathing under a blanket. Not the warm presence that had answered his prayer in the Hollow. This was colder. Attentive. A listener rather than a giver.

"Vesper," he said. "How far to the next town?"

"Too far."

* * *

They made camp in the ruin of a shepherd's hut, walls waist-high, roof long gone, stars overhead sharp as nailheads. Vesper built no fire. She sat facing outward with her sword across her knees, and Tomas lay beside Sela with his arm around her, listening to her breath go shallow and even, and he thought about sleep the way a drowning man thinks about air.

He woke to thorns.

Not the crash of battle. Just thorns—rising silently from the packed earth around the hut's foundation, weaving themselves upward in the dark, braiding into a lattice that caught the starlight and held it. By the time Vesper shouted and her blade came free of its sheath, the lattice had become a dome, and beyond it the night had filled with figures.

They were beautiful. That was the horror of them. Tall and slender, crowned in living bramble that flowered white even in the cold, faces like carvings left half-finished—lovely from one angle, blank from another. They did not speak. They simply stood outside the thorn-dome in a ring, dozens of them, and waited, and the waiting itself was a kind of pressure, a thumb pressed slowly down on the world.

Vesper cut at the lattice. The swordspeech rang off her blade—a single word in a language that made Tomas's bones hum—and the thorns shuddered, blackened, parted. For a heartbeat there was a gap.

Then the gap closed, and the dome grew a second layer, and a voice said, from everywhere and nowhere, gentle as a hand on a child's head:

"Swordspeaker. You'll hurt yourself. And we've only come to talk."

* * *

They were taken, not dragged. That mattered somehow, though Tomas couldn't say why. Thorns lifted them—cradled them, almost—and carried them through the hills to a place that should not have existed: a hall grown rather than built, its pillars living trees fused at the crown, its floor a carpet of moss soft enough to swallow footsteps. Lanterns burned with light the color of dawn through leaves. It was beautiful. Everything the Thorn Court made was beautiful, and everything it made was also, he understood now, a mouth.

They set him and Sela in chairs of woven willow. They set Vesper apart, across the hall, in a chair of blackthorn that looked uncomfortably like a throne, and Tomas understood that too: this was not one conversation. This was two.

The Queen came last.

She wore the Court the way other people wore clothing—bramble and blossom flowing off her shoulders, her face the most finished of all the half-finished faces, so complete it seemed cruel, the way a portrait is crueler than a person because it cannot blink. She looked at Vesper for a long moment with an expression Tomas could not name, and then she looked at Sela, and her expression changed to something worse.

Delight.

"And here is the little vessel," the Queen said. Her voice was warm honey over cold stone. "Come here, child. Let me see the mark."

"No," Tomas said.

The word fell into the silence like a stone into a well. Every face in the hall turned toward him—dozens of beautiful blank faces—and he felt his courage shrivel and then, stubbornly, refuse to die. He stood up. His knees were shaking. He stood anyway.

"She's nine. She's sick—or she was. Whatever you want, you don't get it from her."

"Tomas." Vesper's voice, low, warning.

But the Queen smiled, and the smile was worse than any threat. "And who is this? The brother. The one who prayed." She drifted closer, blossoms shedding pale petals that never touched the ground. "Do you know what you did, little shepherd? In your valley of silence, you knocked on a door that had been sealed for three hundred years. And something answered. Do you know why?"

Tomas said nothing.

"Because it recognized the knock." The Queen circled him slowly. "The covenant knows its own. Your bloodline prayed at its shrines before your valley had a name. When you called, it reached for you—and found your sister instead, small and open and fevered, a door left unlatched." She tilted her head. "Marks are not given, boy. They are found. Like coins in riverbeds."

"You're not answering my question," Tomas said, and was surprised at how steady his voice came out. "What do you want with her?"

"What anyone wants with a key." The Queen glanced back at Vesper, and there it was again, that unnameable expression—hunger wearing patience like a mask. "Though I confess, the key interests us less than the lock."

* * *

They separated them after that. Guards of bramble walked Tomas and Sela down a corridor of flowering dark to a smaller room, round and soft-walled, lit by a lantern that gave warmth but no shadow. Sela curled against his side and slept, eventually, because children sleep anywhere, and Tomas sat in the green-lit quiet and hated himself with a thoroughness that surprised him.

Because he understood now. He understood what the Queen had been telling him between the lines, in the language of courtiers, which was the language of leverage.

Sela was not a prisoner. Sela was collateral. And the debt against her was his.

*You knocked on the door.* His prayer in the Hollow—his desperate, selfish, loving prayer—had cracked a seal his ancestors' ancestors had paid everything to forge. It had marked his sister. It had drawn the Court across the hills like wolves to a bleeding thing. And now Vesper—Vesper who sharpened a sword she'd sworn never to draw, Vesper who carried a dead god's covenant out of the valley so that people like his family could keep their peace—Vesper was in a throne of blackthorn being negotiated over, and every part of it traced back to him kneeling in a sheep-pasture asking the sky for help.

The door opened. No—it rippled open, the wall becoming a doorway the way water becomes a hole around a dropped stone. A courtier stood there, one of the half-finished ones, and beckoned.

"The Queen will hear the brother's bargain now."

"I didn't ask for a bargain."

"Everyone asks," the courtier said, with something that might have been pity on a more finished face. "Eventually."

* * *

The audience chamber had changed. Or perhaps Tomas had simply never seen it truly. The moss floor was the same, the pillar-trees the same, but the light had gone the color of dusk, and the courtiers had arranged themselves in tiers like a congregation, and Vesper stood—not sat, stood—in the center of the hall with her wrists bound in flowering vine, and her chin was up, and her eyes found Tomas's the moment he entered, and what passed between them in that glance was simple and enormous:

*I'm sorry you're here. Don't do anything brave.*

Too late for that, he thought.

"The swordspeaker has declined our hospitality," the Queen said, from her seat of braided bramble. There was no anger in it. That was the frightening thing; there was never anger. "She declines everything. She declined the godling Serevain's protection. She declines our invitation. She walks toward the capital carrying a covenant she refuses to open, like a woman hauling a well-cover across a desert and refusing to drink." The Queen leaned forward, and the bramble crown stirred. "So we will speak plainly, for once, among plain folk. Boy. Do you know what your swordspeaker carries?"

"A sigil. A dead god's covenant."

"Do you know what the covenant *is*?"

Tomas hesitated. "A promise. Between the Ashgroves and—" He stopped. He remembered the ruined library, the things Vesper had told him in a voice scraped raw. "Between the Ashgroves and a god they killed."

"Killed." The Queen laughed, and the sound was bells over a grave. "Gods cannot be killed, child. They can only be *unmade*—dismembered, scattered, buried in oaths and silence. Your valley is not a sanctuary. It is a tomb, and the Ashgrove women were its sextons, dying generation after generation to keep the lid down." She rose, and the whole court rose with her, a field of flowers turning toward the sun. "And now the seal is failing. Now the covenant leaks. Now the last Ashgrove walks the world with the god's heartbone in her pocket—and the Court has watched, and waited, and *counted*."

"Counted what?"

"Her blood, boy. Thirty years of it, coming due." The Queen descended the steps of her throne, slow and graceful, and stopped before him close enough that he could smell her—rain and roses and underneath both, faintly, iron. "A god unmade can be remade, if the vessel is willing and the lineage is true. One death, freely offered, from the proper vein—and the covenant crowns a new divinity. Whole. Hungry. *Ours.* We will feed it answered prayer until the world kneels to be fed, and we will sit at its right hand, and there will be no more silence anywhere, ever again."

The words landed in Tomas's chest like cold water. He looked past the Queen to Vesper, and Vesper had gone very still, the stillness of someone hearing their own death described as a coronation.

"That's why you want her," he said quietly. "Not the sigil. Her."

"The sigil is a seed," the Queen agreed. "She is the soil. And soil, little shepherd, can be *bargained for*."

Her eyes moved, deliberately, to Sela's sleeping weight against his side—to the silver tracery glowing faintly through his sister's sleeve.

* * *

They gave him a room to decide in. The same round green room, the same warm shadowless lantern. Sela woke halfway through his pacing and asked what was wrong, and he told her nothing was wrong, and she looked at him with nine years of younger-sister certainty and said, "You're lying with your whole face," and then asked to be told anyway, and he told her, because he had decided somewhere in the pacing that he was done lying to the people he loved.

"So give them me," Sela said.

"No."

"They want her blood because of the mark, right? Take the mark back. It's mine. I'm saying they can have it."

"It doesn't work like that."

"How do you know?"

He didn't. That was the truth. He knew nothing except what the Queen had let him know, and the shape of the trap was so elegant he could admire it even inside it: Vesper's life for Sela's freedom, or Sela's mark for Vesper's continued refusal, or everyone's ruin if nobody bargained. The Court did not need to threaten. It only needed to arrange the choices and wait.

He sat down beside his sister. He took her marked wrist in both hands, careful as holding a bird.

"When I prayed for you," he said, "in the Hollow. When the fever was bad. I didn't pray for the valley, or for Mam and Da, or for rain. Just you. And something answered, and it cost—I don't even know what it cost yet. Maybe nothing. Maybe everything." He swallowed. "I'm not going to pay that debt with you. Whatever else happens."

Sela studied him with their father's gray eyes. "Then pay it with something else."

"Like what?"

"I don't know." She shrugged, small and fierce. "You're good at talking. Talk."

* * *

When the wall rippled open again, Tomas was ready.

"I'll bargain," he said to the waiting courtier. "Take me to her."

The Queen received him standing, which he suspected was a concession, and Vesper stood bound at the hall's center, watching him come with dread written plain on her face. He stopped three paces from the throne, in the sight of the whole assembled Court, and he made his voice carry the way he did when calling sheep home across a storm-dark hillside.

"You said marks are found, not given. Coins in riverbeds." He raised Sela's wrist, and the silver tracery flared, responding to the attention of the entire Court like a struck bell. "This one's found. Here's my offer: you take it back. All of it. The mark, the leak it came through, whatever thread ties my sister to your god-to-be—you pull it out of her tonight, clean, and she and I walk away, and we tell no one what we've seen. In exchange, the swordspeaker keeps walking to the capital, and you keep hunting her, and none of it touches us again."

"Tomas, no—" Vesper's voice cracked across the hall.

"Because that's the trade you're actually offering, isn't it?" He kept his eyes on the Queen. "You don't need us. You need her *unprotected*. Every friend she sheds makes her easier to corner. So fine. Shed us. But you'll do it honestly—by taking the mark, not by keeping it hanging over us like a knife. That's the bargain. Mark for mark. My sister's freedom, bought with the piece of your god she's been carrying."

Silence. The whole Court stood in it, half-finished faces unreadable, and Tomas's heart slammed against his ribs, and he thought: *it isn't enough, it's a child's bargaining, pebbles against a fortress.*

Then the Queen smiled.

"Oh, I *like* him," she said, to the court, and the court murmured like wind through wheat. "Very well, little shepherd. A clean extraction. It will hurt her—marks root deep—but she'll wake tomorrow with nothing under her skin but herself." She gestured, languid, and the vines binding Vesper's wrists loosened and slid away. "And the swordspeaker goes free. As promised. Free to walk to the capital, free to be followed, free to discover what the covenant whispers to Ashgrove women in the dark." Her finished face bent close to Vesper, and though she spoke to her, the words were for the whole hall. "We do not need to take you, swordspeaker. We only need to wait. Thirty years comes for all your line. Ask your grandmother how escape went, the last time someone tried it."

Something crossed Vesper's face—grief and fury and, beneath them, a flicker of fear so naked it hurt to witness—and then it was gone, banked, buried.

"Take the mark," Vesper said hoarsely. "Let the children go. And when you come for me—come honestly. No more villages made of paper."

"Oh, we'll come honestly," the Queen said softly. "Honesty is so much more *persuasive*."

* * *

The extraction happened in a side chapel of living roots, and Tomas held his sister's hands while the Court's surgeons—things like tall pale priests with fingers of woven briar—drew the silver tracery out through her skin in threads of light, and Sela screamed, and he screamed with her, inwardly, where no one could count it. It took less than an hour. When it was done, her wrist bore only a pale scar shaped like a fading fern, and she was asleep, truly asleep, ordinary and heavy and hers.

They put the siblings out beyond the hills before dawn, on open moorland with the capital's distant smudge on the horizon. No escort. No farewell. Just released, like animals from a snare, and the snare's absence was its own kind of message: *run along now. The hunt doesn't concern you.*

Vesper stood looking at the horizon for a long time. The sunrise came up red behind her.

"You shouldn't have done that," she said at last, without turning. "What you traded. What you learned."

"You'd have done the same for us."

"That's not the point."

"Then what is?"

She turned. In the red light her face was exhausted and grim and, underneath everything, grateful in a way that clearly cost her something to show. "The point is you know now what they intend. And knowing makes you a loose end. And loose ends—" She stopped. Shook her head. "Stay away from me, Tomas. Take your sister and go home, or go south, or go anywhere. The road I'm on doesn't have room for people I—"

She didn't finish. He heard the sentence anyway.

"We'll travel as far as the capital," he said. "Together. Then we'll see."

"Tomas—"

"You said it yourself. Loose ends." He picked up Sela, still sleeping, and settled her against his back the way he'd carried her out of the Hollow. "You don't cut us loose. You keep us where you can see us. Come on. It's a long walk, and you talk in your sleep lately—I heard it last night, before the thorns came. Talking to your sword." He met her eyes. "What does it say back?"

For the first time since he'd known her, Vesper Ashgrove looked afraid of the answer.
