## 13. City of Small Gods

The capital announced itself long before we saw its walls.

We crested the last hill at dusk, and the sky above the city was wrong—not dark the way night should be, but layered, lit from below by a hundred small glows that had no business existing. Gold light bleeding up through cloud. A green shimmer like aurora gone rotten. Somewhere in the east, a column of white fire that burned without smoke and did not flicker when the wind moved.

Tomas stopped walking. His sister's mark—Sera had stayed behind in a river village with a midwife who owed Vesper an old debt, but her mark traveled with him in the stories he told, and sometimes, he swore, in the ache of his own palm—his hand drifted to his chest without his permission.

"It looks like the valley," he said quietly. "When the seal started failing. All those lights under the ground."

"This is what it looks like when there's no seal at all," Vesper said.

She had seen cities before. She had left the Hollow exactly twice as a girl, both times to trade at the market town on the far side of the pass, and she remembered Karset as a sprawl of gray roofs and river-smell and ordinary human noise. That city was gone. In its place stood something that had grown like a coral reef built by fever: spires where no spires had been, whole districts sheathed in living glass, streets that glowed faintly along their seams as if the stones themselves were remembering fire.

And above it all, everywhere, the shrines.

They lined the road for the last mile. Little alcoves of stacked stone, some no larger than cupped hands, each one occupied. Not by statues—by *presences*. A warmth in the air shaped like gratitude. A pressure behind the eyes shaped like attention. One shrine held nothing visible at all, only a smell of rain on hot stone so intense that Vesper's mouth watered and her eyes stung, and she understood that some minor power of weather had made this alcove its home and was drinking the offerings left there: dried flowers, buttons, a child's milk tooth on a saucer.

"They're *everywhere*," Tomas whispered.

"Every crack is a door," Vesper said, "and every door has something waiting behind it now."

The covenant rode against her ribs in its wrapping of linen and oilcloth, silent for three days, which she had learned to distrust more than its whispering. Silent meant patient. Patient meant it thought it was winning.

They went down into the city.

* * *

Karset had always been loud. Now it was loud in a language Vesper hadn't learned yet—the language of worship as commerce, as traffic, as argument.

They entered through the Tanner's Gate, and the first thing that struck her was the begging. Not the old kind, palms out and voices worn smooth with use. This begging was competitive. A woman knelt at the gate's inner corner with her hands raised, calling out not to passersby but upward: *"O Sallet-of-the-Doorways, I have kept your threshold clean seven years, seven years, look down, look down—"* And the air above her shimmered faintly, warm as breath on a winter pane, and coins appeared in her cup one at a time, materializing out of nothing with a sound like a struck bell.

Vesper watched a coin come from nowhere and felt her stomach tighten. Nothing came from nowhere. Every lesson of the Hollow said so. Stillness was the price of peace; silence was the price of sanity; everything taken was paid for somewhere, by someone.

"Where does it get the metal?" Tomas asked, hushed.

"Ask where it gets anything," Vesper said. "That's the question nobody here seems to be asking."

The streets beyond the gate were a bazaar of divinity. She counted eleven shrines between the gate and the fountain square, each with its attendant godling, each godling with its petitioners, and the godlings were—there was no kinder word for it—*small*. Not in their appetites, which seemed vast, but in their substance. She could feel them now; since drawing the sword at Tomas's family's farmstead, since the swordspeech had answered her unbidden, her senses had been opening like a wound deciding to become an eye. The godlings registered as heat-shimmers, as voices heard through walls, as the sensation of being watched by something that blinked too rarely.

One presided over a well and demanded a memory of clean water from every drinker. One haunted a bakery and blessed the loaves but soured any loaf sold to a rival street. One sat in a caged lantern outside a moneylender's shop and glowed brighter or dimmer depending on how much gold passed beneath it, and the moneylender charged extra for transactions conducted in its "favorable light."

"They're fighting," Tomas said, and he was right. She could hear it now that she listened—not with ears, but with whatever new sense the covenant had forced open in her. Underneath the street noise ran a constant friction, godling against godling, territory disputes conducted in pressures and chills. The well-godling and the bakery-thing hated each other with a hatred that made the air between their shrines taste like copper. When a drunk staggered from one territory into another, both powers yanked at him simultaneously, and the man vomited in the gutter and wept and didn't know why.

"Like dogs in a yard," Vesper said. "Except dogs don't make the yard smaller."

A boy of perhaps ten was selling candied nuts near the fountain, and his cry had adapted to the times: *"Blessed nuts! Sweetened under the Eye of Ammalet! Protection from the souring!"* Vesper bought a twist of paper just to watch him work, and the nuts tasted of sugar and, underneath, very faintly, of fear.

* * *

They found lodging in the Weft district, a tangle of weavers' lanes where the godlings were thinner on the ground—too poor a neighborhood, apparently, to support much divinity—and where an innkeeper named Odji took their coin without questions because questions cost extra everywhere in Karset now.

The room was narrow and smelled of dye-vat and tallow. Vesper unwrapped the sigil to check it, as she did each night, and for once it reacted: the bone warmed against her palm, and the voice came, soft as sediment settling.

*You see it now,* it said. *This is what waits when there are no keepers. Chaos wearing crowns. It need not be thus. There was order once. There can be again.*

"I've seen your order," Vesper said aloud. "It's in a library in the ruins of Vel Saroth, written in the blood of everyone who served it."

*Written by the victors.*

"Written by my grandmother." She wrapped the sigil away. "Your scribes are dead. Try someone else."

But lying awake after, listening to the city hum with its hundred small hungers, she found the sigil's argument lodging under her skin like a splinter. Because the chaos *was* real. She had walked through it all day. Whatever the covenant wanted from her, whatever sin the Ashgroves had committed to bind it, the world without governance of any kind was turning into this—a marketplace of petty tyrannies, each godling a landlord of awe, each miracle a transaction with hidden fees.

Maybe that was the trap, she thought, staring at the ceiling. Not the lie itself, but the truth arranged beside it like bait.

Tomas slept badly in the next bed, muttering. Since the Thorn Court had held them—since he had bargained with Sera's mark to buy their escape—he flinched at bramble shadows. He'd told Vesper what he'd heard while captive, the Court's plans spoken carelessly around him: they wanted her blood, her willing death, a war-god crowned and leashed. But he'd also told her, in a smaller voice, that one of the thorn-courtiers had looked at him almost kindly and said, *You'll understand when you're older. Everything costs. At least ours tell you the price.*

Vesper lay in the dark and listened to Karset pray, hundreds of voices rising through the floorboards like water through a hull, and wondered how many of them knew what they were paying.

* * *

She got her answer two days later, in the Plaza of the Nine Fountains.

They had come to the plaza because rumor said a cartographer worked there who still drew maps of the old roads, roads that predated the shrines, roads a person could walk without crossing a single territory line. What they found instead was a crowd.

It packed the plaza shoulder to shoulder, five deep around the central fountain, and the mood was not festival. It was hunger. Vesper worked herself and Tomas toward the front with an elbow and a lowered shoulder, and when she saw what the crowd had gathered around, her skin went cold.

A godling stood on the fountain's rim.

It wore a shape, which most of the small ones didn't bother with—a shape like a tall figure robed in woven light, faceless except where a face should have been there was a slow rotation of images: a wheat field, a cradle, a hearth fire, a grave. Comfortable images. Images chosen by something that had studied humans the way a merchant studies ledgers.

It was performing miracles.

Not grand ones. A farmer held up his twisted hand, and the godling's light poured over it, and the fingers straightened with a sound like green wood bending. A woman with clouded eyes received her sight back in a flood—Vesper saw her stagger, saw her sob, saw her press her face to the fountain rim in gratitude. Each miracle drew a roar from the crowd, and each roar fed the godling visibly; its robe of light grew brighter, denser, more real. It was farming them. It was *harvesting* them, awe by awe, and giving back just enough to keep the crop growing.

"Ammalet," a woman beside Vesper breathed. "The Eye of Ammalet. They say it came out of the eastern provinces after the Tremor. They say it's *generous*."

"Nothing is generous," Vesper said.

The woman glanced at her with the quick resentment of the faithful and turned away.

Then a man at the front lifted a child onto the fountain's edge.

The girl was maybe six. Fever-bright eyes, hair stuck to her skull with sweat, limbs limp as rope. The crowd's noise dropped to a murmur, then to nothing, the whole plaza leaning in. The father's voice cracked as he held her up: "Please. Please, lord of light, she burns, three days she burns, the physickers can do nothing—"

The godling turned its rotating face toward the child. Wheat field. Cradle. Hearth fire. Grave.

And Vesper, with her new-opened senses, felt the thing *consider*. Not compassion—consideration. She felt it weigh the child the way the moneylender's lantern-god weighed gold. And then she felt it reach out—not toward the girl, but toward the father, delicate as a pickpocket, sliding into him through the wide-open door of his desperation.

"No," Vesper said, though no one heard her.

The godling healed the child.

It took perhaps four seconds. Light flowed; the fever broke; the girl's eyes cleared and she laughed, startled, alive, and the plaza detonated with joy. Her father collapsed to his knees. People wept. Coins rained into the fountain. The godling blazed like a second sun, gorged, magnificent.

And the father—

The father stayed on his knees a moment too long. Vesper pushed forward, shoving through the dispersing edges of the crowd, and reached him as he stood, swaying, holding his daughter, smiling at her with an expression Vesper recognized and would remember until she died: love with a hole punched through the middle of it.

"What did it take?" she demanded, gripping his arm. "What did it take from you?"

He looked at her blankly. "It gave. It *gave*, woman. Look at her—"

"What did it take?"

"I don't—" He stopped. Something moved behind his eyes, a man patting his pockets and finding them lighter than expected. "Her name," he said slowly. "I was going to say her name just now, to you, and I—" His brow furrowed. "I know her name. Of course I know her name. It's—it's—"

He couldn't find it.

Vesper watched him search. Watched the panic bloom and then, horribly, fail to bloom—watched the godling's payment smooth the panic flat even as it happened, leaving him only a mild, foggy puzzlement, the emotional equivalent of a numb tooth. He knew he had lost something. He could no longer quite care that he had.

"Mama," the little girl said, tugging his sleeve. "Papa, I'm hungry."

"There's my girl," he said, and the words were right and warm and hollow, and he walked away with her, and he never once looked back at the godling that had eaten a piece of his heart out through the eye of his love.

Vesper stood in the emptying plaza shaking.

Tomas caught up with her. "Ves? Ves, what happened, what did you see—"

"It didn't take from the child." Her voice came out low and ragged. "Everyone will say it was merciful. It healed a dying girl in front of five hundred witnesses. And it paid for the miracle out of the father's *memory of her name*. Out of the first thing he ever knew about her. It took the smallest possible piece, from the person least able to notice, and it will do it a thousand times a day, and every one of those people will walk away grateful."

Tomas was quiet. Then: "The Court told me everything costs. I thought they were justifying themselves."

"They weren't justifying." Vesper stared at the fountain, where coins glittered on the bottom like scales shed by something enormous. "They were advertising honestly. That's worse."

She had grown up believing magic was the enemy at the gate, the thing the Hollow's silence kept out. Standing in the Plaza of the Nine Fountains, she understood at last that the enemy was not magic. Magic was weather. The enemy was *appetite wearing magic's face*—and it didn't live beyond the valley walls. It lived wherever worship met want. It might already be inside her, in the covenant's honeyed promises, in the seduction of the swordspeech, in the part of her that had felt the plaza's awe and known, shamefully, what it would be like to be the thing on the fountain's rim instead of the woman in the crowd.

Revulsion rose in her like floodwater—and then, instead of breaking against helplessness, it found a channel and became something harder. Purpose has a taste, she discovered. It tastes like cold iron and decided things.

She was done running from shrine to ruin to drunk innkeeper's rumor, collecting pieces of a history other people had buried. The godlings were multiplying because the seal was broken and the covenant was loose and every fledgling power in the world was feeding on the same desperate harvest. As long as the covenant existed—as long as divinity sat in the world like an open vein—there would be a thousand Ammalets, and then ten thousand, and eventually one of them would be strong enough and cruel enough and *patient* enough to become the war-god the Thorn Court dreamed of crowning.

It couldn't be negotiated with. She'd tried. It couldn't be buried; her ancestors had tried that for nine generations and the bill had simply come due in her lifetime.

It had to end. Not chained. Not fed. *Ended*—unbound entirely, dissolved, returned to mortals as memory rather than power. She had carried the idea for weeks like a coal in cupped hands, afraid of it. Now, watching the father who could not recall his daughter's name disappear into the crowd, she let it burn.

"Tomas," she said. "We're not going to Brey for answers anymore. We're going for weapons. Everything she knows about the covenant, about the binding, about how the Ashgroves executed a god—I need all of it. Because I'm going to kill this thing for good, and I intend to survive doing it."

Behind them, unnoticed, the godling Ammalet turned its rotating face—wheat field, cradle, hearth fire, grave—toward the two of them, and held it there a moment longer than chance required.

It had felt her purpose.

And somewhere across the city, in a court of living brambles, something ancient smiled and began, at last, to worry.
