## 15. The Court's Invitation

They did not take her by force. That was the first thing Vesper noticed, and the thing that frightened her most.

She woke in a garden.

Not the Hollow—she knew the Hollow's silences the way she knew her own heartbeat, and this was not that silence. This garden *breathed*. Bramble hedges rose on either side of a path of white stone, grown into arches and vaulted corridors, and every thorn on every stem caught light that came from no sun she could find. The sky above was the color of a bruise healing: violet fading to gold at the edges, beautiful in the way wounds are beautiful when they are nearly closed.

Her sword was still at her hip. The sigil was still against her sternum, wrapped in its binding cloth beneath her shirt, warm as a sleeping animal. Tomas—

"Tomas," she said aloud, and her voice fell flat among the roses.

"He is unharmed." The voice came from everywhere and nowhere, the way prayer sounds when it is answered. "Sleeping, in fact. He fought me with a shepherd's crook. It was charming."

Vesper turned, hand on her hilt, and found the Thorn Queen standing ten paces down the path as though she had always been there, as though Vesper had simply failed to notice her until now.

She was not what the stories promised. No crown of black iron, no robe of woven night. She wore a simple grey dress, travel-worn, hemmed with mud, and her hair was bound back with a strip of leather like any farmer's daughter going to market. Her face was ordinary—broad, sun-browned, lined at the eyes—and it was only when you looked at her eyes that you understood what stood before you. They were the color of deep water over deep stone, and they held the particular patience of something that had watched empires be born, beg, and die without once needing to hurry.

"You're smaller than I imagined," Vesper said.

The Queen smiled. "So are most gods, up close. We spend so much of ourselves being large that there's very little left over for being anything else." She gestured down the bramble path. "Walk with me. I've been wanting to meet an Ashgrove for two hundred years, and I would rather not do it shouting across a battlefield."

"I know what your Court does to people who walk with you."

"Do you?" The Queen tilted her head. "You know what my enemies say I do. There's a difference, and you of all people should appreciate it. Your whole valley was built on the difference between what the founders did and what they said they did."

Vesper's jaw tightened. She walked.

* * *

The garden went on longer than any garden should. They passed fountains where the water ran upward, silver threads climbing back into the air. They passed orchards whose fruit was small and pale and shaped like closed eyes. And they passed people.

That was how Vesper thought of them at first—*people*—gardeners and courtiers moving through the bramble halls with serene faces, pruning roses, carrying lanterns that burned without flame, laughing softly at some shared joke. A woman knelt by a flowerbed, turning the dark soil with bare hands, humming. A man sat on a stone bench reading a book with no words on its pages, turning them anyway, absorbed.

It took Vesper half the length of the path to see what was wrong with them.

They were too calm. Not peaceful—calm, the way a room is calm after everyone has left it. Their smiles reached their mouths but stopped somewhere short of their eyes, which were smooth and dark and empty as wells. The humming woman turned her soil with perfect, patient rhythm, and Vesper realized she had been watching her turn the same three feet of earth since they'd arrived, planting nothing, harvesting nothing, humming the same four notes over and over like a music box winding down.

"What did you do to them?" Vesper asked quietly.

"Nothing they didn't ask for," said the Queen. "Or pray for. Or wish for, once, desperately, in the dark, meaning it with everything they had." She paused beside the woman in the flowerbed. "This is Sela. She prayed for her village to be spared from the fever, forty years ago. It was. All of it. Every soul. Do you know what that costs, Vesper? Miracles aren't free. Someone pays. In the old days, the gods paid—or rather, mortals paid *to* the gods, tithes and blood and burnt offerings, and the gods grew fat and paid each other. I have tried to be more honest than that. My people pay with the parts of themselves they can spare."

"The parts they can spare," Vesper repeated.

"The fever memory. The grief. The year of hunger—they don't need it anymore, the hunger is over. Why should anyone carry a weight they've set down?" The Queen's voice was gentle, reasonable, terribly gentle. "Sela doesn't miss it. She doesn't remember there was ever anything to miss. She is happy here. Look at her."

Vesper looked. Sela smiled at the soil, hummed her four notes, and turned the earth that would grow nothing.

"She isn't happy," Vesper said. "She's *absent*. There's nothing left in her to be unhappy with."

"And yet she suffers less than anyone you have ever met." The Queen resumed walking. "You're going to tell me that suffering is sacred. That pain makes us human. I've heard the argument; I helped invent it, back when I was mortal enough to need consolations. But I watched a child burn alive in a plague-year, Vesper, praying to a god who was busy elsewhere, and I decided that if I ever had power, I would spend it making sure no one ever had to lose that argument with the universe again. Everything I have built grows from that root. You may judge the fruit. Judge the root too."

They came out onto a terrace overlooking a valley of the Queen's own—a green country stitched with villages, smoke rising gently from chimneys, children visible even from this height as bright specks running through wheat fields. It looked, Vesper thought with a lurch, like the Hollow. Like home, rendered by someone who had only ever heard it described.

"I could give you this," the Queen said.

* * *

They sat at a stone table under a trellis of white roses, and the Queen poured tea from a chipped clay pot, and that was somehow worse than any display of power could have been—the cracked glaze, the way she warmed her hands around the cup like any tired traveler.

"Here is what I know about you," the Queen began. "And do correct me where I'm wrong; I have been wrong about Ashgroves before, and it cost me a war. You were raised in a sealed valley by women who told you the world outside was a wound. You swore an oath never to draw your sword in anger, and you kept it for thirty years, and then you broke it in one afternoon to save a shepherd boy's family, and the swordspeech came out of you like water out of a cracked dam. You carry a dead god's covenant pressed to your chest. You have read your family's histories—you know now that the Ashgroves were jailers, not guardians, and that the covenant wants your death the way a lock wants a key." She sipped her tea. "You have spent the entire road telling yourself that if you can just get the sigil somewhere holy enough, or break it thoroughly enough, none of it will ever have to touch anyone. Am I close?"

"You've been watching us."

"I've been *listening*. There's a difference, and it matters." The Queen set down her cup. "Here is what I offer, and I will make it only once, and I will not dress it in thunder because you've had enough thunder to last several lifetimes.

"Give me the covenant. Not your life—I know what it asks of you, willing death, heir's blood, all the old machinery. I don't want your death. I want the *god*, or rather what's left of him, and I am the only power in this world strong enough to hold him without being consumed. Give him to me, and I will end the war that's coming. Not delay it. End it. The Thorn Court has spent centuries learning how to feed on answered prayer without devouring the answerers—we are not kind, but we are *careful*, and careful is more than the old gods ever managed. Under me, divinity becomes a managed thing. Prayers answered at cost, yes, but costs levied fairly, by law, by courts—" she smiled faintly at her own name "—with oversight. No more child burning while its god is busy elsewhere.

"And you—" the Queen leaned forward, and her deep-water eyes held Vesper's with a gravity that made the terrace seem to tilt "—you become what you were always meant to be. Not a jailer. Not a martyr. A goddess of oaths, Vesper. Of promises kept and vows broken justly. Every swordsworn soul in the world would kneel to you. Your valley opens its gates—not because the seal fails, but because you *unmake* it, freely, and the Hollow becomes a sanctuary instead of a prison. The boy keeps his sister. The sister keeps her mark, and learns to use it safely, under your protection. Brey gets her debts forgiven, though I suspect what she wants is punishment, and I can arrange that too, if mercy would insult her." The Queen spread her hands. "No one dies. No one is unmade. The world is remade *kindly*, which is the one way it has never once been remade in all its history. Wars are ended by victors, and victors are cruel. Let it end instead by invitation."

The tea steamed between them. Somewhere below, the bright specks of children ran through the wheat, and the humming woman hummed her four notes, and the man read his blank book.

Vesper found that her hands were shaking.

That was the horror of it—not the fear, but the *want*. Because the Queen had done her arithmetic correctly, and Vesper could find no error in it. Every alternative she had carried down the road like stones in her pack—break the covenant, shatter the sigil, let divinity scatter wild into the world—every one of those paths led through fire. She had already seen the first sparks: villages arming under resurgent shrines, old hatreds finding new voices, prayers being answered by things that had waited centuries in the dark and were *hungry*. If she shattered the heartstone, she would loose all of it at once, unmanaged, uncourted, unbound. The Queen was offering a dam where Vesper's plan was a flood.

And underneath the strategy, quieter and more shameful, was the other thing. The thing that sounded like her grandmother's voice and Maren's voice and her own voice at nineteen, swearing her oath with her hand on cold iron: *You were made for this. This is what the line was for.* Not the dying. The *reigning*. All those generations of Ashgrove women sharpening swords they never drew, memorizing words of power they never spoke—hadn't they been training her, all along, for exactly this? To stand where she could protect? To finally matter at the scale of her own strength?

"It's a good offer," Vesper said, and her voice came out rough. "That's what frightens me. If you'd offered me thrones and screaming crowds I'd have walked out already. But you offered me my valley with its gates open. You offered me the boy's sister safe. You're offering me the world the way I'd have built it if I'd been given the tools."

"Yes," the Queen said simply. "I know what you want. I've wanted it longer than you've been alive. That's why I'm dangerous, Vesper, and why you should be afraid—but you should be afraid of the right thing. Not of me. Of the *alternative*. Go ahead. Picture it. The heartstone broken, everything loose, every petty shrine-god and famine-saint and war-echo scrambling for worshipers, and no law over any of them but appetite. You've seen the capital. You've seen what fledgling godlings buy miracles with. Now imagine a hundred of them with the strength of the old pantheon and no Thorn Court to keep the ledger." She refilled Vesper's cup, unasked. "Refuse me, and that is the world you choose. Not freedom. *Weather*. Divinity as weather, raining on the just and unjust according to nothing but whim. You'll have kept your hands clean and your principles intact, and the wheat fields will burn anyway."

Vesper looked at her cup. The tea had gone cold without her noticing; time moved strangely here, or perhaps she did.

"If I say yes," she said slowly, "what happens to the people who pay the costs? Sela and the others. The ones who traded their memories."

"They live comfortably forever, lacking nothing but what they gave."

"That's not living. That's *preservation*." Vesper set the cup down with a click that sounded very loud. "I saw her. She plants nothing. She harvests nothing. She hums four notes. You didn't spare her the fever, you spared her everything, and there's nothing left under the smile. You call it payment, but you're the one who sets the price, and you've set it so high that what you're buying isn't service. It's *selves*. Your whole court is a granary of hollowed-out people, and you sit in the middle of it calling it kindness because you only take what they can spare—and you decide what they can spare."

Something moved behind the Queen's deep-water eyes. Not anger. Something older and wearier.

"Every system of justice takes something," she said. "Yours takes lives and calls it law. Mine takes memories and calls it mercy. Don't pretend yours is clean."

"No," Vesper agreed. "Mine isn't clean either. My family took a god's life and a valley's freedom and called it peace." She stood. Her legs felt strange, too heavy, as though the terrace itself were reluctant to let her rise—as though part of her, a part she hated, were still sitting in that chair being offered the world. "I need to think."

"Of course." The Queen rose too, graceful, unhurried, and Vesper understood suddenly that the offer had never been in danger of expiring, that the Queen could afford patience the way oceans afford rain. "But before you go—one honesty, from me to you, since I've asked you to consider mine." She stepped closer, and up close her ordinariness was its own kind of vastness, a plain so wide it looked like nothing. "You should know that whatever you choose, I will have the boy's sister. The mark on her is mine by right—it grew from a leak in *my* seal, fed by *my* domain, and marks come home. I would rather she came home to a friend's protection than to a summons. Think on that when you weigh your kindness against mine."

The bruise-colored sky seemed to darken a shade.

"Now," said the Thorn Queen, and smiled her terrible reasonable smile, "let me show you the rest of the garden. There's someone here I think you've been looking for. He arrived yesterday, asking after an innkeeper who owed his grandmother a debt—and he's been drinking my wine and refusing my hospitality ever since, which is more spine than most of my courtiers ever showed."

She led Vesper down the white path, past the humming woman, past the man with the blank book, toward a trellised arch where a broad-shouldered figure sat slung in a chair of living briar, wrists thorned loosely to the armrests, glaring at the roses with the magnificent, hopeless defiance of a man who had been drunk in better company.

Brey looked up. Her eyes, bleared and furious and unmistakably *full*—full of grief and guilt and sixty years of remembered fire—found Vesper's face.

"Well," Brey rasped. "Took you long enough, girl. And don't drink the wine. It costs more than you've got."
