## 16. What Tomas Prayed For

The mark on Lira's wrist had grown again.

Tomas noticed it the way you notice a storm building behind hills—not all at once, but in small wrongnesses that add up until the air itself feels like a held breath. Three days ago it had been a coin-sized shimmer beneath her skin, pale as moonlight through water. Now it spread from her wrist to her elbow in branching filaments, like frost climbing a windowpane, and when she slept, the branches moved.

She was asleep now, in the back room of the wayhouse at Fenmoss Cross, her breathing shallow and too quick. Vesper stood over her with a lamp, saying nothing, which was worse than anything she could have said. Vesper always had words—hard words, old words, words worn smooth by use—but tonight she only looked, and her silence had edges.

"It's spreading," Tomas said. He hadn't meant to say it aloud. It came out anyway, the way true things do.

"I can see that."

"Can you stop it?"

Vesper set the lamp down on the table beside Lira's bed. The light caught the scarred hilt of her sword where it leaned against the wall, and for a moment Tomas thought she wouldn't answer at all.

"No," she said finally. "I can slow what's feeding it. I can't stop what's already in her." She turned to look at him, and in the lamplight her eyes were the color of wet slate. "The Court marked her as a secondary vessel the night they took us. You bought our escape with her mark, Tomas. That was the price, and the price doesn't go away because we walked out of their hands."

He flinched. He'd known she would say it eventually—he'd been waiting three days for it, actually, the way you wait for a wound to start hurting properly—and hearing it still felt like a hand closing around his throat.

"I know what I did."

"Do you?" Vesper's voice wasn't cruel. That was the terrible thing about her; even her hardest truths came out level, patient, like a teacher explaining why the fire burns. "Because if you did, you'd understand that they didn't let us go because you bargained well. They let us go because a vessel walking free is worth more than a vessel in a cage. We're leading them somewhere, Tomas. Or they're letting us believe we are."

* * *

They'd been at Fenmoss Cross four days, holed up in a wayhouse that smelled of tallow and old smoke, waiting for Lira to be strong enough to travel and knowing, both of them, in the place where knowing lives, that she was never going to be strong enough to travel anywhere again unless something changed.

Tomas spent the days doing the things he knew how to do. He carried water. He split wood for the innkeeper, a broad silent woman named Hesk who asked no questions and charged them half rate and watched Lira's glowing arm with the wary look of someone who remembered her grandmother's stories about the time before the Hollow sealed. He fed the chickens. He sat by his sister's bed and told her stories about the valley—the ridge at dawn, the sheep with their foolish faces, the way the river sounded in spring when the melt came down from the high snows—because she was often too tired to talk and he couldn't bear the quiet.

And every day, the mark grew.

It wasn't painful, Lira insisted. That was somehow the worst part. "It feels warm," she said on the second night, turning her arm in the lamplight like a girl showing off a bracelet. "Like lying in the sun after swimming. Sometimes I hear... not voices. More like remembering something I never knew. Songs without words. Names of places that don't exist anymore." She smiled, and the smile scared him more than crying would have. "Tomas, it doesn't hurt. It feels like being *included*."

"That's how it gets you," he said.

Her smile faded. "You sound like her." She nodded toward the main room, where Vesper sat sharpening a sword she'd sworn never to draw, an oath broken and remade so many times now that Tomas wondered if the sword knew what it was anymore.

"She's usually right."

"She's usually afraid," Lira said, "and she's gotten very good at calling it right."

* * *

On the fourth night, the brambles came.

Tomas woke to a sound like green things growing fast—like a hedge sprinting—and was out of bed before his mind caught up with his body. The window of the room he shared with two traveling peddlers (who had fled down the road at first sight of Lira's arm) was black except for a faint thornlight glow, amber and wrong, seeping through the shutter seams.

He heard Vesper's door bang open across the hall. He heard Hesk scream once, briefly, and then go silent in a way that was worse than screaming.

By the time he reached the main room, the windows were gone. Not broken—*gone*, replaced by walls of living bramble woven tight as basketry, thorns as long as his thumb glistening with sap that caught the hearthlight. And standing in the middle of the room, where the hearth should have been, was one of the emissaries.

He'd seen them before, on the ridge above the Hollow, from a distance, at night: figures that walked like people but weren't, their outlines soft as heat-shimmer, their faces smooth as eggs. Up close was different. Up close, Tomas could see that the smoothness of its face wasn't absence—it was *potential*, the way a blank page is potential, and looking at it made his eyes water and his teeth ache.

"The Thorn Court acknowledges the bargain," it said. Its voice was pleasant. That was the worst thing. It sounded like a kind uncle, like a friendly neighbor, like every trustworthy voice Tomas had ever heard rolled into one. "The boy paid fairly. The girl is owed."

"She's not owed anything," Vesper said. She stood between the creature and the hallway to Lira's room, sword drawn, and the blade made a low sound Tomas had learned to dread—a hum, almost below hearing, like a struck bell heard through a wall. The swordspeech, waking. "She's a child."

"All vessels are children," the emissary said gently. "That is rather the point. The Court does not require her suffering, swordspeaker. Only her presence. Bring her to us willingly, and she will want for nothing. She will be *adored*. There are worse fates for a shepherd's daughter than godhood."

"There are no worse fates for anyone than godhood," Vesper said. "I've seen your capital. I've seen what you do to the ones who shine brightest."

The emissary's smooth face tilted. "Then you leave us no courtesy to extend." It raised one hand, and the bramble walls shivered, and every thorn pointed inward at once, at Vesper, at Tomas, at the hallway where Lira slept. "The Court will collect what it is owed. Resist, and collection becomes harvest. You have until the moon sets."

The brambles didn't open. They simply *unbecame*, unwinding into shadow and the smell of crushed green stems, and the windows were windows again, and the hearth was a hearth, and outside the night lay ordinary and star-scattered as if nothing had happened.

Hesk emerged from the pantry where she'd hidden, white-faced, holding a rolling pin like a warhammer. She looked at the intact windows, at Vesper's drawn sword, at Tomas, and said only: "Moon sets in three hours. I'll get your horses."

* * *

They didn't make it past the stable yard.

Not because of the Court. Because of Lira.

Tomas found her standing barefoot in the frost, wrapped in a blanket, staring east. Her arm blazed through the blanket's gap—not painfully bright, but bright, luminous as a lantern seen through skin, the branching filaments now reaching her shoulder and creeping up the side of her neck.

"Lira—" He grabbed her shoulder and she turned to him and her eyes were wrong. Not evil-wrong. *Lit*-wrong. Backlit, like windows of a house at dusk with someone home inside.

"They're singing," she said dreamily. "Did you know they sing? All the time. Under everything. The Court sings and the dead god dreams and there's a third song, Tomas, there's a third song nobody remembers, and I think—I think it used to be *ours*—"

"Lira. Look at me." He shook her, gently, then less gently. "Lira."

Her eyes focused. The light dimmed to its usual shimmer. She swayed, and he caught her, and she weighed almost nothing, and that frightened him more than the singing had. When had she gotten so thin? When had he stopped noticing?

"I'm sorry," she whispered. "It pulls. When they're close, it pulls. Like a hook behind my ribs." Her fingers found his sleeve and gripped. "Tomas, I don't want to be adored. I want to go home. I want to smell the sheep and complain about mucking the pen and I want Wren to stop stealing my boots. I want small things. Why does everyone keep offering me enormous things?"

He held her while she shook, there in the frozen yard under the setting moon, and something in him went very quiet and very clear, the way water goes still and deep over a stone.

Three hours. The Court would come at moonset, and Vesper was good—gods, she was good, the best he'd ever seen with a blade, and the swordspeech was waking in her whether she willed it or not—but he'd seen the Court's host on the ridge, and he knew arithmetic when he saw it. Three against a court of godlings was not arithmetic. It was a prayer.

A prayer.

He'd prayed once before, desperately, selfishly, kneeling in the dirt over his sister's fevered body, and something vast and warm had answered, and the answer had cracked the Hollow's seal and started all of this. Every road, every bramble, every branching light under his sister's skin traced back to that moment in the sheep pen when he'd opened his mouth and begged.

Everyone kept telling him prayer was dangerous. Prayer was power, and power charged a price, and the prices were memories and marks and wars.

But everyone kept praying anyway. That was the whole history of the world, as far as Tomas could tell: people paying prices for miracles because the alternative was watching the ones you loved be taken.

He laid Lira back in her bed. He waited until her breathing steadied. Then he went out to the stable yard alone, stood where the moon was laying down its last silver on the frost, and folded his hands the way the old woman in the valley had taught him, years ago, before anyone believed prayers worked.

He did not kneel. Kneeling felt like begging, and this wasn't begging. This was bargaining, and bargainers stand.

"Hello," he said to the sky. His breath smoked. "You answered me once. I don't know what you are—the dead god, or the covenant, or just... the world, listening. I don't care. I'm not asking you to save her. I'm asking you to *hide* her. Take the light out of reach. Make her plain. Make her a shepherd's girl with chapped hands and stolen boots and nothing shining anywhere in her. Let the Court look at her and see nothing worth collecting."

The night listened. He felt it listening, the way you feel someone standing behind you—not threatening, just present, vast, attentive. The stars seemed to lean closer.

"And I know the price," he said. His voice cracked and he let it. "I know what you charge. So take it. Take whatever it costs. Take my happiest memory—take all of them, take as many as it takes, I don't care, I'll grieve things I can't name and I'll think that's just how life is. Take it all. Just leave her small. Just leave her *hers*."

The last of the moon slid below the hills.

For a long moment, nothing. Frost creaked. Somewhere a dog barked, far off, at nothing.

Then the warmth came.

It rose out of the ground itself, out of the frozen mud and the stable stones, gentle as sun through a south window, and it wrapped around him and it was—there was no other word—*loving*. Not love like people had, complicated and conditional and tangled up with need. Love like gravity. Total, impartial, immense. In its warmth he felt every prayer ever prayed, every desperate voice in every dark century, all of them heard, all of them held, and he understood suddenly why people had worshipped, why they had built temples and burned offerings and given everything: not because they were fools, but because *this* existed, and having felt it, how could you bear the world without it?

The warmth said yes.

It didn't say it in words. It said it the way spring says it, the way dawn says it, and then it began to *take*, and Tomas understood in the last instant what he had not truly understood before: that you cannot choose which memory is your happiest. Only the taking knows.

It took the summer he was nine, the whole golden length of it, the summer Lira taught herself to whistle through a blade of grass and they lay in the hay loft for hours making terrible music and laughing until they ached, and the light came through the boards in dusty stripes and their father was still alive and still whole and called them in to supper in a voice like a hearth—

It took that, all of it, root and branch, and left behind a hole shaped exactly like joy.

Tomas came back to himself on his knees in the frost, though he didn't remember falling. The sky was graying toward false dawn. His face was wet. He touched his cheeks, surprised, and tried to remember why, and couldn't, and the *couldn't* was a smooth blank place in him like a tooth missing, and he probed it the way you probe a missing tooth, over and over, unable to stop.

Something had happened here. Something important. Something he had chosen.

He couldn't remember choosing it either.

* * *

Inside, Lira was sleeping deeply, and when Vesper pulled back her blanket to check her arm, they both saw it at once.

The light was gone.

Not dimmed—gone. The branching filaments had faded to the faintest ghost-lines, visible only when the lamp swung, like old scars or stretch marks, like something the body had carried and survived. Lira's skin was just skin. Her breathing was slow and even and utterly ordinary.

"What did you do?" Vesper asked quietly. She was looking at him with an expression he'd never seen on her—not suspicion, not gratitude. Something rawer. Almost grief.

"I don't know," Tomas said honestly. His voice sounded strange to himself, flattened, like a room with the furniture removed. "There was warmth. It said yes. And then—" He frowned, groping. "Then I came back inside. I think. Did I go somewhere?"

Vesper stared at him for a long moment. Then she crossed the room, took his face in her scarred hands, and looked into his eyes the way she looked at maps, reading terrain.

"Oh, you fool," she said softly, and her voice broke on it. "Oh, brave, stupid fool. What did it take?"

"I told it to take my happiest memory." He said it easily, factually, and watched her flinch as if he'd struck her. "I don't remember what it was. Isn't that strange? I know I had one. Everyone has one. Mine's just..." He searched for the word. "Missing. Like a coat I lent out."

Vesper's hands dropped. She turned away from him sharply, and her shoulders were shaking, and it took him a confused moment to realize that Vesper Ashgrove, the last oath-bound swordspeaker, the hardest woman he had ever known, was crying.

"You gave away your father," she said, when she could speak. "The summer in the hay loft. Your father alive, calling you to supper. That's what it took, Tomas. I've seen it in the covenant's visions. It takes the summers the dead were still alive."

He stood very still. The blank place in him ached now, ached terribly, though there was nothing in it to ache with. He could feel the shape of the loss without the content, grief with no photograph, mourning a room he'd been locked out of.

"Was it good?" he asked. "The memory. Was it a good one?"

Vesper wiped her face with the heel of her hand, and when she turned back, the tears were gone and something harder had replaced them—something that looked, Tomas realized, like resolve catching fire.

"It was the best one you had," she said. "Which means the covenant is awake, and listening, and willing to deal. Which means we're done running." She picked up her sword, and the blade hummed its low subterranean note, and outside the eastern horizon was going gold. "Get your sister. Get the horses. We're not going away from the Court anymore, Tomas. We're going to the heart of it. Because if that thing will bargain with a shepherd boy in a frozen yard, then it can be bargained with—and I am done letting it set the price."

Behind them, Lira slept on, small and plain and gloriously unmarked, dreaming of nothing at all.
